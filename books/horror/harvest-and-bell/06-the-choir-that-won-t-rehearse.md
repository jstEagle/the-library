## 6. The Choir That Won't Rehearse

Wren knew the stranger was coming to choir before he did, because Mrs. Aldery told her mother's cousin, who told the baker, who told everyone within an hour of the flour being milled. *The folklorist wants to hear us sing.* The words went through Harrow's Bell like a draught under a door, and every door in the village closed a little tighter behind them.

Wren did not close anything. Wren had been waiting eleven years for someone to ask.

She arrived at St. Brannoc's early, as she always did, because arriving early meant she could sit in the back pew and watch the light come through the east window without anyone asking why she wasn't helping set out the hymnals. The window showed a shepherd and his flock, but the glass was old and the colors had gone strange with age — the sheep were green, the shepherd's face was amber, and above them the sky had faded to a pale, watery grey that always made Wren think of the sky over the orchard in January. She liked that about it. Most things in Harrow's Bell pretended too hard. The window had given up pretending.

The church smelled of candle wax and cold stone and, faintly, of the cedar oil Reverend Crane rubbed into the pews every Advent. Outside, the frost had come down the hill from the orchard again — Wren had seen it on her walk over, silvering the hedgerows in patterns that made her fingers itch for her sketchbook — and inside, the stone held the cold like a held breath.

She took out her sketchbook and turned to the newest page. She'd drawn it that morning, kneeling on the frozen lane where the frost had climbed a gatepost: a spiral of small marks, hooked and barred, like a language trying to remember itself. She'd been drawing these since she was six years old. Nobody had ever believed they meant anything. Nobody had ever even looked properly, except once, when she was small, and old Mother Petch had gone white as whey, snatched the page, and burned it in the inn's kitchen stove while Wren watched, telling her that little girls shouldn't copy things off walls and windows and gates, that some writings weren't writings at all but *listening*, and you didn't want to be caught listening, did you, girl?

Wren hadn't stopped. She'd just gotten quieter about it.

The door opened at half past six, and the cold came in first, then the villagers, stamping and unwinding scarves, their breath hanging in the nave like ghosts that couldn't find their graves. And then, last, ducking slightly under the lintel as though the church might be smaller than he expected, came the stranger.

Xanthe Lockridge. Wren had seen him at the tavern two nights ago, when Tam Hollis sang the wassail and then left without paying, which nobody ever did, not Tam, who counted his coppers twice. She'd watched the folklorist's face when the room went cold — watched him look around at all those shut faces and see nothing but rudeness, or eccentricity, or whatever word a man like him would use. He hadn't understood. But he hadn't laughed either. That was something.

He found a pew near the back — not hers, but near enough — and set down a leather bag from which he drew a machine with two round ears of brushed metal. A microphone. He handled it the way other men handled a newborn lamb, careful and a little afraid.

Reverend Ilsa Crane stood at the front, arranging her music, and Wren saw her see the machine. Saw her go still, just for a moment, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"Mr. Lockridge," Crane said. Her voice carried in the way voices do in empty churches, flat and echoing. "You're welcome to listen. You're welcome to take notes." A pause, thin as ice on a puddle. "I'd rather you didn't record."

"It's a simple ambient capture," Xanthe said. "No names attached. It's how I work — I can't transcribe what I can't hear again."

"Can't you?" said Crane, and there was something in it that Wren caught and filed away, the way she caught everything: a question shaped like a statement, a warning shaped like a joke. "Very well. But if I ask you to stop, Mr. Lockridge, you'll stop."

He agreed. He smiled — a careful smile, professional, the kind Wren imagined he gave to archivists and landladies and everyone else whose cooperation he needed. But when he sat down and opened his notebook, Wren saw that his hands weren't quite steady. Good, she thought. He should be afraid. Fear meant he was paying attention.

* * *

They began, as they always began, with the processional carol, the one about the star that stood still over the orchard instead of over any stable. Wren sang alto, low in the row, where her voice could hide among the others. She had a good voice; everyone said so, though they said it quickly and changed the subject, as if praising her singing might invite something to praise itself.

The carol rose into the rafters. Twelve voices, thirteen with Crane conducting, and the sound filled the church the way warmth fills a room slowly, corner by corner. Wren loved this part — this was the one hour a week when Harrow's Bell sounded like a place where people lived instead of a place where people waited. She let herself lean into it, eyes half-closed, the harmony wrapping around her like a coat.

And then it happened.

It happened the way it always happened, so gradually that no one could have said afterward who started it. Old Sorrel Pike, who sang bass and had sung bass for sixty years, came around to the second verse for the second time — the repeat, the da capo, the thing every song in the world does without thinking — and beside him, his wife Maren, singing the same verse she'd already sung, drifted up a third into harmony. Not wrong. That was the terrible thing. It was beautiful. Their two lines braided together over the repeated verse, and for three whole seconds the carol opened like a door opening —

Crane's hand came down like an axe.

The chord died mid-breath. Thirteen people stood in the sudden silence, holding air in their lungs they suddenly didn't want.

"No," Crane said. Not loudly. She never said it loudly. That was somehow worse. "Again. From the top of the verse. Unison only."

"But we've sung it twice already tonight," Maren Pike said, bewildered. "First time through, and now the repeat—"

"The repeat is unison," Crane said. "As it has always been. As the book says."

There is no book, Wren thought. There had never been a book. She'd looked, years ago, in the vestry, in the archive, everywhere a child could reach and several places she'd reached by standing on chairs. The rule lived nowhere except in Crane's raised hand and in the flinch every villager performed at the second singing. It was a rule the way a limp is a way of walking: learned, permanent, and proof of an old injury nobody would name.

They sang it again, unison, flat and obedient, and the beauty was gone out of it, wrung out like water from cloth. Wren watched the stranger in the back pew. He wasn't writing. He was watching Crane's hands, and then he was watching the Pikes, and then — she felt it before she confirmed it, a prickle along her scalp like frost forming — he was watching *her*. No. Watching past her. Watching the sketchbook open on her knee, the spiral of hooked marks, the notation she'd copied off the orchard gate that morning.

She closed it. Too late. His eyes met hers, and he didn't look away the way adults did when children caught them looking. He tilted his head, very slightly. A question.

Wren's heart did something complicated, a stumble and a leap at once.

* * *

Rehearsal limped on for another forty minutes. Crane cut the descant from the second carol before anyone could sing it. When young Ned Tallow — home from market, cheerful, ignorant of everything — began humming the wassail tune between pieces, three people shushed him at once, and Crane said, "Not in here, Ned," in a voice that made the candles gutter, or seemed to. The folklorist wrote that down. Wren saw him write it.

At last Crane dismissed them, and the congregation-that-was-not-a-congregation broke apart into scarves and boots and murmured goodnights, everyone leaving fast, the way people leave a room where something embarrassing has happened. Xanthe packed his machine slowly, deliberately, a man making himself the last leaf on the branch.

Wren stayed too. She was good at staying. Orphans learn early that lingering is invisible — nobody expects the child with nowhere particular to be to have anywhere particular to be.

The last villager went out. The door boomed shut. Crane gathered her scores with quick, angry precision, and Xanthe approached the chancel steps with his notebook held like a passport.

"Reverend Crane. May I ask about the arrangement? The unison repeat — it's a fascinating local variant. In every other wassail tradition I've documented, the repeat invites—"

"You may not ask," Crane said.

"I only mean to understand the practice—"

"Do you?" Crane set down her scores. Up close, in the candlelight, Wren could see how tired she was, tired in the marrow, tired the way stone is tired of holding up a roof. "Mr. Lockridge. You are a scholar of songs. Tell me — in your scholarship, have you ever encountered a song that answers?"

Xanthe blinked. "Answers?"

"When sung twice. Have you ever recorded a song, played it back, and heard more than you sang?" Crane's voice dropped, and the church seemed to lean in, the stones themselves attentive. "Because I have. Once. Years ago. And I tore the answer out of the book it lived in, and I have spent every winter since making certain no one sings the question twice." She picked her scores back up. "You want to document our carols. Document them. First singings only. And when you leave Harrow's Bell — and you will leave, Mr. Lockridge, everyone leaves — you will leave your recordings here."

"That's not how scholarship works," Xanthe said quietly.

"No," Crane agreed. "It isn't. It's how survival works. Goodnight."

She swept up the aisle and was gone, and the cold she carried out with her was almost a relief.

* * *

The church settled around them, ticking as the stones cooled. Wren slid along the pew, closer, and spoke before her courage could reconsider.

"She's right, you know."

Xanthe turned. In the candlelight his face was younger than it had seemed at the tavern, and more frightened, though he was doing his best to fold the fright away like a map. "Right about what?"

"About the answering." Wren hugged her sketchbook to her chest. "You heard something. At the tavern. When Tam sang the wassail. You heard the room go quiet, and you thought it was superstition or bad manners. But it wasn't. It was listening. We were all listening. Because when a song gets sung twice here, sometimes—" She stopped. How to say it? How to say *sometimes the silence after has a shape, and the shape is a mouth*? "Sometimes there's a third voice. Underneath. Very far underneath. Like a song heard through a wall. Through a floor."

Xanthe studied her. She watched him decide whether to treat her as a child, watched him weigh it and — to his credit, and she would remember this — set the weight down. "How old are you?"

"Sixteen."

"And you believe this."

"I don't believe anything," Wren said. "Believing is for things you can't check. I've been checking since I was six." She opened the sketchbook, turned past years of pages — winters of pages, frost after frost after frost, spirals and bars and hooks rendered in pencil, in ink, once in berry juice — and laid it flat on the pew between them. "These are yours to look at. Nobody's ever looked. Mother Petch burned the first ones and told me listening was dangerous, and everyone else just says I've got a strange imagination, and maybe I do, but strange imaginations don't draw the same picture for ten years running, do they? Not unless the picture's *there*."

Xanthe bent over the pages. She watched his face change by lamplight — the professional detachment cracking, flaking away like paint, and beneath it something rawer and hungrier and much more honest. His finger hovered over a spiral without touching it.

"This is notation," he said. "Or — it's structured like notation. Pitch markers, duration bars. Where did you—"

"The orchard. The fences. The windows of the empty houses. The bark of the oldest trees, when the frost comes right." She swallowed. "And once — only once, the night my parents' house burned, when I was four, too young to hold a pencil — I heard it. Humming. Everyone says I dreamed it. Everyone says a lot of things."

The silence stretched. Somewhere overhead, in the belfry, a rope creaked against the beam, though there was no wind inside a church.

"My mother used to hum to me," Xanthe said at last, and his voice had gone somewhere Wren didn't think he'd meant it to go. "Before she died. Songs I've never found in any collection. I keep thinking — professionally, you understand — that if I could trace where they came from..." He stopped himself, jaw tightening, the scholar slamming the door on the orphan. Two of a kind, Wren thought. The village raises its children in common, and grief raises its children alone, and both kinds end up listening at walls.

"Reverend Crane tore pages out of a book," Wren said. "She said so just now. 'I tore the answer out of the book it lived in.' Did you hear her?"

"I heard her."

"There's a hymnal in the archive with its ending missing. I've known since I was nine. I could never reach the shelf it lives on, and Crane keeps the key on her belt, and I could never ask why without everyone deciding I was strange, which they'd decided anyway, but—" She ran out of breath and discovered she was gripping his sleeve. She let go. "You're going to look anyway. Aren't you. That's what you came for. And I'm asking — not telling, asking — to come with you. To help. Because I have ten years of drawings and no explanations, and you have explanations and no drawings, and neither of us believes the village's story, which is that there isn't one."

Xanthe looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the frost was climbing the churchyard wall; Wren could feel it the way she always felt it, a fine pressure behind the teeth, a tuning fork struck somewhere below hearing. Inside the church, the candles burned straight and slow.

"You understand," Xanthe said finally, "that if I write about your sketches, your name won't be in the book. Anonymity protects—"

"I don't care about your book." Wren closed the sketchbook and held it against her chest, over her heart, where the humming lived. "I care about the answer. Ten years, Mr. Lockridge. Everyone in this village knows half a story and guards it like a grave, and I have been the girl who asks questions until asking became a kind of silence. You're the first person in ten years who looked at my drawings and didn't look away." She stuck out her hand, formal as a treaty, the way the baker's wife shook hands at market. "Wren. They named me for the bird that sings in winter. I've always thought someone was being funny."

He took her hand. His grip was cold from the church, but steady.

"Xanthe," he said. "And I should warn you — I don't believe in any of it. Whatever any of it turns out to be. I believe in recordings and transcriptions and things that can be checked twice."

"Good," said Wren, and felt her grin split her face, sudden and unguarded, the first one in weeks. "Because I've got something you'll want to check twice. Tomorrow, when the frost comes down the hill — come to the orchard fence with me. Bring your machine. There's a stretch of gate rail where the pattern changes every morning, same as handwriting, same as a score being written out bar by bar." She paused, and lowered her voice, though the church was empty, though the world was empty, though it was only the two of them and the candles and the rope creaking in the belfry like something impatient to be rung.

"And here's the thing I've never told anyone, because there was never anyone to tell. This morning's pattern — the one I copied onto the page you just looked at — it wasn't new. I've seen it before. Drawn it myself, eight years ago, when I was eight." She met his eyes. "The frost is repeating itself, Mr. Lockridge. And in this village, everybody knows — you never, ever sing the same verse twice."

The nearest candle guttered, though no door had opened.

Above them, in the dark of the belfry, the bell — silent, unstruck, unmoving — gave the smallest sound. Not a ring. A hum. Like a throat clearing. Like something remembering it had a voice, and waiting, patient as frost, for someone to give it a reason to use it.
