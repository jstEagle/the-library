## 7. Wren's Questions

Wren had learned to count the village by its silences.

There was the silence of morning, when the frost lay thick on Harrow's Bell and even the crows kept their opinions to themselves. There was the silence after supper, when the tavern emptied and Bess Marrow turned the lock with a sound like a knuckle cracking. And there was the silence that followed any question beginning with *why* — a silence with weight to it, a silence that settled over a room like snowfall, soft and total and smothering.

Wren had been asking *why* since before she could tie her own boots. She'd been told, in the gentle collective voice of a dozen surrogate mothers and fathers, that some questions were like poking at a bruise: they didn't help, and they made everything ache worse. So she had learned to ask them quietly. To ask them alone. To draw what she couldn't say.

Which was why, on the fourth morning of the folklorist's stay, she found herself standing outside his door at the inn with her sketchbook clutched against her chest like a shield, listening to him move around inside, and hating herself for being afraid.

*He asks why too,* she reminded herself. *You heard him. In the choir loft, when Reverend Crane stopped the rehearsal — you saw his face. He wasn't frightened the way the others are frightened. He was hungry.*

She knocked before she could change her mind.

The door opened on Xanthe Lockridge looking rumpled and under-slept, wire-rimmed glasses slightly crooked, a pencil still tucked behind one ear as though it had grown there. Up close he smelled of cold air and machine oil and something faintly chemical — the tapes, she supposed, the reels he carried everywhere in their dented leather cases.

"Miss—" He paused, visibly searching. "Wren, isn't it? The alto."

"The alto who doesn't exist," Wren said. "Reverend Crane keeps me in the back row where I can't do damage."

"She keeps everyone somewhere," Xanthe said dryly, stepping back to let her in. "I'm beginning to suspect she keeps me in the village."

The room was small and had been transformed already into something between a study and a workshop. Reels of tape stood in ranks on the dresser like tin soldiers. His recorder sat open on the desk, its guts exposed, and beside it lay notebooks — dozens of them, filled edge to edge with his narrow, slanted handwriting. Wren's eyes went to them the way a thirsty woman's eyes go to a well.

"You write everything down," she said.

"Everything I can catch." He cleared a chair of tapes and offered it. "Most of it slips through my fingers."

"That's what it's like," Wren said, and then stopped, startled at herself.

Xanthe looked at her properly then. "What's what like?"

Wren sat down. Set the sketchbook on her knees. Her heart was going so hard she could feel it in her throat, in her wrists, in her teeth. Nine years she had waited to have this conversation, and now that it was here she found she didn't know how to begin it, because every way of beginning it sounded mad.

"When you record a song," she said carefully, "and then you play it back — is it exactly the same? The same song?"

"Nearly. Tape has its own grain. It breathes a little differently than a voice does. But yes — the same song."

"But not the same *singing.*"

"No." He tilted his head, interested. "No, the singing is gone. The singing only happens once."

Wren nodded slowly. "That's what I thought. That's what nobody here understands when I try to explain it. They think I'm talking about the rule — don't sing anything twice. But I'm not. I'm talking about something older than the rule." She took a breath. "Mr. Lockridge. Why did you really come to Harrow's Bell?"

"I told the tavern. To record the carols—"

"You told the tavern what gets strangers fed and bedded. I was born in this village. I know which words are doors and which are walls." She held his gaze. "Why did you come?"

For a long moment he said nothing. Outside, the wind worried at the shutters, and somewhere down the lane a shutter hinge squeaked its one note over and over, patient as a metronome. Then Xanthe sighed, and something in his face came down like a drawbridge.

"Because my mother sang me lullabies from a place she never named," he said quietly. "And when she died, I went looking for the place, and every road led nowhere except here — where nobody will sing anything twice, and the frost writes things on the trees that look like music." He laughed, short and without humor. "So. Scholarly curiosity. Professional detachment. All of it. And underneath all of it, a boy who wants his mother's voice back and will dress the wanting up in footnotes if he has to."

Wren felt something loosen in her chest — a knot she hadn't known was tied.

"My mother didn't sing to me," she said. "I never had one. Not one I remember. They found me on the church steps the winter I was born, wrapped in a blanket with no name sewn into it, and the village raised me between them — a month with the Cranes, a season with the Hollises, a year in Bess's attic. That's why they call me Wren. Small bird, no nest of its own, lives in everybody's hedge." She smiled, and knew it didn't reach her eyes. "But I'll tell you something strange, Mr. Lockridge. Something I've never told anyone, because the last time I came close, Tam Hollis went white as the orchard and wouldn't speak to me for a month."

"Tell me," Xanthe said.

Wren opened the sketchbook.

* * *

She had filled eleven of them since she was six years old. This was the twelfth, and it was more than half full, and every page held the same thing: patterns. Lines and branches and feathering whorls drawn in careful graphite, dense as ferns, ordered as nothing in nature is ordered. Some pages held single motifs, studied from four angles. Others held long sequences marching across the paper like processions.

"They come out of the frost," Wren said. "Or — no. That's not true either. That's what I told myself when I was little, that I was just copying what I saw on windows. But I drew them before I ever saw them. I drew them in summer. I drew them in the middle of church." She turned a page. "They arrive. That's the only word. They arrive in my hand, and my hand knows where to go, and afterward I look down and there they are, and I don't know what they are or where they came from or why they won't stop."

Xanthe had gone very still. It was the stillness of a man trying not to startle a wild thing — or perhaps of a man afraid the wild thing was himself.

"May I?" he said, and when she nodded, he bent close over the pages. His finger hovered above the graphite without touching, tracing the shapes through the air. "These aren't random. Look — repetition with variation. Call and answer. This figure here appears seven times, but each time altered by one element, like..." He stopped. Started again. "Like verses."

"I know," Wren whispered. "I've known since I was nine. I just didn't have the word for it until yesterday, when I saw the bark."

His head came up sharply. "You've been to the orchard."

"Last spring. Before you came, before anyone was watching the paths. I go sometimes. Nobody else goes, so it's the one place in Harrow's Bell where nobody asks me questions." Her mouth curved. "Ironic, isn't it. I went there to get away from questions and found the biggest one in the county growing on the trees."

"The notation on the bark."

"On the bark, in the ice on the pond, in the way the dead bracken lies flat against the ground. It's everywhere out there, if you look. And it's *this*." She turned to a page near the front of the book — one of the oldest, the graphite gone shiny with years of handling. "I drew this when I was eight. Under the north window at the Hollises' house, January, hard frost. And last spring I stood in front of the third tree past the well — the big apple, half fallen — and the pattern on its trunk was this. Exactly this. Same figures, same order, same alterations. Twenty years apart, Mr. Lockridge. Eight-year-old me and a hundred-year-old tree, writing the same sentence."

The room had grown cold around them, or perhaps Wren had simply stopped noticing the warmth. Xanthe pulled one of his notebooks toward him, flipped rapidly to a marked page, and laid it open beside her sketchbook.

Wren looked down and forgot how to breathe.

Her drawing. His transcription. Side by side. The same procession of figures, the same branching logic, the same seven-fold variation — his rendered in precise field-notes shorthand, hers in a child's careful hand refined across two decades into something almost calligraphic.

Neither of them spoke for a long time.

"It's the same," Xanthe said finally, and his voice had gone rough. "It's the same sequence. I recorded this off the bark two days ago and spent last night convincing myself I was seeing music in noise — pareidolia, apophenia, the mind's hunger for pattern. I wrote three pages debunking myself." He tapped the notebook. "And you drew it twenty years before I ever set foot in this valley."

"Before I could read," Wren said. "Before I could write my own name, I was writing *this.*"

"How?" The question burst out of him, and then he pressed his knuckles to his mouth, embarrassed. "Forgive me. That's not a scholarly question. That's a frightened one."

"Don't apologize." Wren closed her hands over the edges of both books, hers and his, feeling the strange communion of the paper. "Frightened questions are the only ones anybody means. I've spent my whole life being handed unmeant questions. *How are you settling in, Wren. Are you eating enough, Wren. Don't ask that, Wren.*" She looked up at him. "Nobody has ever asked me *how* before. Nobody has ever wanted the real answer, which is: I don't know. I don't know how. I don't know why me. I was left on the steps with no name and no mother and no history, and whatever put those patterns in my hand didn't leave a note."

The fire in the little grate popped. Downstairs, faintly, Bess Marrow was moving through the taproom, and the sound of her sweeping rose up through the floorboards like a slow heartbeat.

"There's one more thing," Wren said. Her pulse had climbed again, but differently now — not fear. Something brighter and more dangerous. "When I was drawing them, all those years. When they arrived in my hand. It never felt like nothing. It felt like... being spoken to. Like someone reading aloud very slowly to a child who can't understand the words yet, but who is expected to understand them someday." She met his eyes. "Do you know what that's like, Mr. Lockridge? Being read to by something you can't see?"

Xanthe was quiet for a moment. Then, reluctantly, as though the words cost him: "My recordings. At night. There's something under the wind on the tapes. A hum. And last night—" He stopped. Shook his head. "Last night it sounded less like a hum and more like a voice warming up. And I told myself it was interference. I'm very good at telling myself things."

"We should compare," Wren said. "Your tapes and my books. Your nights and my years. Because I think—" She hesitated, then committed, the way you commit to stepping onto ice you've tested with one heel. "I think we're both being taught. You came here an outsider with an ear, and I was born here with a hand, and whatever is doing the teaching has been working on us both. Separately. Patiently. For a very long time."

"That's not a comfortable theory."

"No," Wren agreed. "But comfort has never once answered any of my questions. Have you noticed that? Every comfortable answer in this village is a locked door. I'm done knocking on locked doors. I want the thing that's actually talking."

Xanthe looked at her for a long moment — and Wren watched something shift in his face, the professional distance thinning, the loneliness underneath showing through like light under a door. She recognized it. It was her own face, most mornings, in most mirrors.

"You were in the choir loft," he said. "When Crane stopped the rehearsal. You weren't frightened either."

"I was relieved," Wren said. "Ten years of singing one verse and stopping, one verse and stopping, like a heart that won't finish a beat. And then you asked *why*, out loud, in front of everyone, and the world didn't end, and I thought: *there. There's a person who asks the questions I ask.*" She smiled, and this time it reached her eyes. "I've been waiting for you, Mr. Lockridge. I just didn't know your name until Tuesday."

"Wren." He said it carefully, testing it. "If we do this — compare the tapes, decode the patterns, chase this thing wherever it leads — you understand the village won't thank us. Crane will bar the archive door. Bess will double the locks. Tam will drink himself brave and tell us both to stop. Whatever bargain this place struck, they've paid for it in silence, and they'll defend the silence like a wall defends a city."

"Then we knock the wall down gently," Wren said. "One question at a time. Questions are the only inheritance I've ever had. I'm not giving them up now that I've finally found someone who'll ask them with me."

Xanthe laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him — and pulled a fresh reel of tape from its case. "All right. Partner. Start from the beginning. The first pattern you ever drew."

Wren turned to the first page of the first sketchbook she'd brought — she'd brought three, hidden under her coat, because she had known, somehow, that she would not be going home with them — and smoothed it flat on the desk between them.

"This one," she said. "I was six. Midsummer. There wasn't a breath of frost anywhere in the valley, and I woke up with my hand already moving."

Xanthe leaned in, pencil ready, and began to transcribe.

Outside the window, unnoticed by either of them, the frost on the glass had begun, very quietly, to rearrange itself into lines and branches and feathering whorls — dense as ferns, ordered as nothing in nature is ordered — arranging itself, figure by patient figure, into the exact shape of the page they were studying together, as though something on the other side of the winter were taking notes of its own.
