## 6. Tunnel Music

There are three kinds of people who go into unknown tunnels at night. The brave, the stupid, and the ones who've run out of better options. I like to think I'm the first kind. The evidence suggests I'm all three, in rotation.

I waited until 11:40 p.m. to enter, which sounds dramatic but is actually just thermodynamics. Old tunnels hold the day's heat like a grudge, and the temperature differential makes my thermal camera useful instead of decorative. Also, Marrow Creek at night has the decency to pretend it isn't watching you. In daylight, the windows have opinions.

The access point was a storm grate two blocks off Main, behind what used to be Hollis's Hardware and is now a storefront with a FOR LEASE sign so sun-bleached it reads FOR LEASE in the way a tombstone reads a name—formally, without expectation. I'd found the grate on my second day of lidar work, sitting exactly where the 1972 municipal survey said there was solid earth. The survey, I'd come to understand, was less a record of the town than a costume it wore.

I went down the ladder rungs one at a time, counting them out of habit. Fourteen. My breath echoed back up at me a half-second late, like it was checking with someone before it committed to being mine.

* * *

The tunnel at the bottom ran wider than I expected—wide enough for two people abreast, which is a strange specification for a town of nine hundred. Brick barrel vaulting, the kind you see in nineteenth-century cisterns, except the bricks were laid in a pattern I didn't recognize. Not running bond. Not herringbone. Something spiral. I photographed it, because photographing things is how I keep the world from sliding sideways. The flash lit the spiral for a quarter second, and in that quarter second the pattern looked less like masonry and more like a fingerprint. Like the tunnel had been pressed into the earth by something very large and very patient.

I marked the junction on my tablet. The lidar overlay from the surface settled over my position with a soft chime, and there it was again: the discrepancy. Every foundation in Marrow Creek had a tunnel stub running out from beneath it, like roots. Every one. Including, I noted with a feeling I refused to name, the house I grew up in. Including my mother's porch, where a boy who hadn't aged was probably sitting right now, watching the street, waiting for me to come back and admit he was real.

"Not thinking about that," I told the tunnel.

The tunnel, politely, said nothing. But the acoustics in that place were wrong in a specific way—my voice came back to me too clean, without the dampening you'd expect from brick and standing water. It came back the way a recording sounds. As if something down there had a very good ear and was keeping copies.

I walked. The map grew under my stylus, line by line, and the more it grew, the less it looked like infrastructure and the more it looked like anatomy. Main trunk. Branches. Capillaries. All of it converging, somewhere south of the town square, on a node the lidar couldn't resolve—just a soft blur in the data, the way a camera smears when you point it at something that's moving too fast or too close.

The old plat books had called the node, in Abigail Crane's spidery hand, *the Throat.*

I'd been telling myself that was a metaphor.

* * *

I found the first handwriting forty minutes in.

It was on the eastern wall of a branch tunnel, low, at knee height, in pencil gone silver with age: a column of numbers. Dates, I realized. 1931. 1931. 1934. 1931 again. And next to each date, a tally mark. I photographed it, moved on, and found more—a name scratched into mortar: *CRANE.* A child's chalk drawing of a house with too many windows, faded to a ghost. A prayer in a hand I didn't know, in Latin I couldn't read, ending in a word I could: *dormi.* Sleep.

People had been coming down here for a hundred years and writing on the walls, and not one word of it had ever made it into an archive. It was like finding a second town beneath the first, one that kept no minutes and elected no officials, that met in the dark and left only these marginalia. I documented all of it. That's the pact I have with myself: if I record it, I don't have to believe it yet. The notebook holds the believing for me, in trust, until I'm ready.

I was maybe six hundred meters from the grate when I heard the singing.

* * *

It started as a hum I assumed was the water. There's a frequency that pipes make when they're carrying, a note just below hearing that you feel in your molars more than your ears. I stood still and let it come to me, professional habit, cataloguing: not pipes. Pipes don't breathe. This rose and fell. It had *phrasing.*

And then it did the thing I will be hearing in the back of my skull for the rest of my life, however long the rest of my life turns out to be.

It matched me.

I was breathing slow—the controlled four-count I do when I'm scared and don't want my body filing a complaint. Four in. Four out. The hum slid into the gaps of it, came in on my exhale like a second voice finding the harmony line, a third above, soft, patient, *practiced.* Not copying me. Harmonizing with me. There's a difference, and the difference is intention. A copy is a mirror. A harmony is a choice about what you'd sound like together.

I did what any sane person does. I changed my breathing. I went to a quick, shallow two-count, the way you breathe when you're about to run.

The singing adjusted in under a second. It found the new rhythm like a dancer catching a partner's hand, and it *smiled*—I don't know how a sound smiles, but this one did, some lift at the edge of the note, some warmth in the overtone that had no business being there. It was pleased. It had been waiting for me to pick a key.

"Okay," I whispered, and my voice shook, and the harmony took the shake and made it music.

I stood in that tunnel for—I don't know how long. A minute. Ten. Long enough for the cold to climb through my boots. And here is the part I keep circling, the part I've written three versions of in my field notes and deleted all three: it wasn't horrible. That's the horror of it. It was the most heard I have felt since my mother died. Some voice under Marrow Creek took the sound of my frightened breathing and made it beautiful, made it *belonged,* and some traitor chord in my chest wanted to stay down there and let it.

I turned off my headlamp.

Don't ask me why. I think I wanted to know if it would still sing in the dark, if it needed me to see. It sang. In the black, with my pulse hammering in my ears, the harmony held—wrapped around my breath like a hand around a match, sheltering it. And in the dark, with nothing to look at, I noticed something I'd missed with the light on.

The singing had words.

Not words in any language. Words the way a dream has words—meaning that arrives ahead of the sound, so you understand the sentence before you hear it, and what I understood, in the dark, in the harmonized breathing dark of a tunnel under my dead town, was:

*You came back. We kept your chair.*

* * *

I turned the lamp on and walked. I didn't run. Running is a decision you make with your body, and my body had gone over to the other side—I didn't trust it not to run *toward.* So I walked, fast, stylus out, mapping, because mapping is the rope I throw myself.

That's when I found her handwriting.

I knew it before I was close enough to read it. You know your mother's hand the way you know her footsteps on stairs—the particular lean of it, the way she crossed her sevens, the way her capital R's had a little kick like they were stepping over something. It was on the western wall, in ballpoint, in the dark zone past where any natural light had ever reached, and it said:

*Xanthe hates the dark. Leave a lamp for her.*

There was no lamp. There was a bracket on the wall, rusted, empty, exactly lamp-shaped, and the handwriting above it was my mother's, and my mother had never been in this tunnel.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the last tool I have left that still cuts. My mother was born in 1958 and died in March of this year in a hospital forty miles away, and in all the years between she left Marrow Creek exactly twice that I know of—once to move us out in 2004, and once, in 2011, for my father's memorial, which she attended the way you attend a sentencing. She was afraid of basements. She kept the cellar door locked with a padlock she wore the key to on a chain around her neck, and when I was nine I asked her what was down there and she said, "Nothing that needs us," in a voice I now understand was a locked door too.

She had never been down here. And her handwriting was on the wall, dated—I crouched, I shone the light, I made myself look—dated 1996. The year I was seven. The year Wren started following me around the yard.

The message continued, further along, in the same hand, smaller, as if the writer had been getting tired or getting scared:

*She asks about her father. Tell her he's working. Tell her anything but the truth until she's strong enough. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry it has to be a wall.*

And then, at the bottom of the wall, where the brick met the floor, in handwriting so faint I had to put my fingers on it to trace the ballpoint grooves:

*If you're reading this, Xanthe. If it's you, if you came back after all—*

The sentence stopped. Not faded. Stopped, mid-line, the pen lifted, the thought abandoned or interrupted or *taken,* and below it, in a different hand—a rounder, older hand, a hand I'd seen in Abigail Crane's journal entries in the historical society—a single line:

*The wall only holds what's given to it. Come below, and we will finish the letter.*

* * *

I sat down on the tunnel floor. I don't recommend the tunnel floor; it's cold and it's damp and it has opinions about your dignity. But my legs had resigned, and I figured if the singing wanted me, it had me either way, so I might as well be seated.

Here's what I knew, laid out in the dark like tools on a drop cloth. One: the tunnels under Marrow Creek were not infrastructure. Two: they had been used, for generations, by people who left messages for each other and for the future, and the town above had agreed, in the way towns agree to things without ever voting, to forget. Three: my mother had known. My mother had *written* down here—her hand, her words, her apologies—and had climbed the ladder and locked the cellar door and never told me, had spent her whole remaining life being afraid of basements and letting me think it was a quirk. Four: something down here had been waiting for me long enough to keep a chair.

And five—the one I said out loud, to the tunnel, to the harmony that had gone quiet and attentive, listening the way you listen to someone deciding:

"You knew I'd come back before I did."

The tunnel didn't answer. But down the southern branch, the one the lidar couldn't resolve, the one that ran toward the Throat, the singing started again—distant now, many-voiced, and underneath it, so faint I could have told myself I imagined it, a rhythm I recognized. A heartbeat's rhythm. Or a pickaxe's. Or a man working, deep under the town, at something he'd been working at for twenty years.

I got my mother's handwriting into the camera frame, all of it, every sorry. I got the empty lamp bracket. I got the Crane hand's invitation. Then I marked the southern branch on my map and did not go down it, because I am brave, stupid, and out of better options, but I am not *ready,* and there's a difference, and the difference is intention.

Climbing the ladder rungs, I counted them again. Fourteen. My breath came back to me at the top without the echo, without the harmony, just my own air in the open night, and it sounded lonelier than anything I'd heard all evening.

The FOR LEASE sign creaked in the wind. Down the block, on my mother's porch, a light was on that I had turned off before I left.

I stood on the sidewalk for a long time, deciding. Then I went up the porch steps, and the door was unlocked, and Wren was sitting in the kitchen at my mother's table with my tunnel map spread out in front of him—my map, the one on my tablet, the one that existed nowhere on paper—tracing the southern branch with one finger, and when he looked up at me he said, in my own voice, seven years old and certain:

*"It's not a tunnel. It's a throat. You said that. You said, 'It's not a tunnel, it's a throat,' and I said, 'Then what's it hungry for?' and you said—"*

He stopped. He waited. He wanted me to finish it.

I remembered. God help me, I remembered, and I said it, because some doors you have to open from your own side:

"I said, 'Me. Eventually. Everything comes back to where it started.'"

Wren nodded slowly, like a teacher confirming the right answer, and slid the map across the table to me, and pointed at the node the lidar couldn't resolve, and said:

"He's almost done digging."
