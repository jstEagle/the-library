## 7. Fenn's Confession

Deputy Fenn was waiting for me outside the courthouse when I came up from the basement with the covenant folded into my jacket like a confession of my own.

He was leaning against his cruiser with his hat in his hands, which is a posture I'd only ever seen men adopt at funerals and proposals, and given that Marrow Creek had recently provided me with one funeral and zero proposals, I assumed he was there about the other thing.

"Miss Reyes." He said it the way you'd say *ma'am* to a judge who already didn't like you.

"Deputy." I kept walking. He fell into step beside me anyway, boots crunching the gravel, and I noticed—because by now I noticed everything—that his shadow fell a half-second behind him. Or maybe that was the streetlight flickering. In Marrow Creek you learned to give the light the benefit of the doubt, because the alternative was worse.

"You've been in the archives," he said.

"I've been in a lot of places."

"Basement's flooded knee-deep most years. Nobody goes down there but rats and me." He turned the hat over in his hands. "And now you."

I stopped at the corner of Main and Hollow, under the dead traffic light that had been blinking yellow since before I was born—the town's one permanent instruction: *proceed with caution*. "If you're here to tell me to leave town, Deputy, you should know my mother just died and I'm the executor of an estate that consists of a house, a cat-shaped hole where a cat used to be, and approximately forty years of unopened mail. Leaving isn't on the menu."

"I'm not here to tell you to leave." He looked down the street toward the diner, where Mabel Cho's neon OPEN sign buzzed its lonely pink hum into the dusk. "I'm here because you found the covenant, and I need to know what you intend to do about it."

*How did he know.* The question arrived fully formed and then answered itself: he was the law in a town with no law left to keep. Of course he knew what was in his own basement. Probably he dusted it. Probably he visited it the way some men visit graves.

"That depends," I said carefully, "on whether it's real."

Fenn laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry as a well bottom, and it went on two beats too long. "Oh, it's real."

* * *

He took me to the station, which surprised me, because I'd expected a man with something to hide to hide it anywhere else. But the Marrow Creek sheriff's substation was the kind of place that hid things in plain sight out of sheer exhaustion—a double-wide trailer with a flagpole out front and a smell inside of burnt coffee and old paper and, faintly, underneath everything, water. Damp stone. Well water.

He made coffee he clearly didn't drink himself. Set the mug in front of me like an offering. Then he unlocked a filing cabinet with a key on a chain around his neck, the way other men wear dog tags or wedding rings, and began pulling folders.

"Missing persons," he said. "Marrow Creek, 1961 to present."

The stack he laid on the desk was not large. That was the first thing I noticed. Forty-some folders, thin ones, the kind that close without protest because almost nothing was ever written inside them. I opened the top one. A photograph of a man named Gerald Pruitt, 1974, last seen walking toward the reservoir. Below the photo, in handwriting I recognized with a lurch as Fenn's—or Fenn's predecessor's, the same hand passed down like the hat and the cruiser and the job—was a single line:

*Given.*

Not *missing*. Not *under investigation*. Not *case open/closed/pending*. Just: *Given.*

"What is this?" I asked, though my voice had already gone somewhere small and careful.

"Justice," Fenn said. He sat down across from me and put his hat on the desk between us like a third participant in the conversation. "You want to know what happened to your father? You want to know why this town has no crime? No meth labs, no domestic calls we can't talk down, no bodies in the creek even though every town this size drowns somebody once a decade?" He tapped the folder. "Because we have an arrangement."

"The well."

"The well." He said it without flinching, which frightened me more than flinching would have. "You read the covenant. Seven names, seven seals, every generation, or it refills. What the covenant doesn't spell out—because Abigail Crane figured decent folk could work that part out themselves—is that the ritual doesn't just *keep* the thing asleep. It feeds it. On a schedule. Small meals. And a town that feeds it on a schedule is a town it doesn't come hunting in."

My stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. "You've been giving it people."

"We've been giving it *the right people*," Fenn said, and the precision of the correction told me everything about how long he'd been rehearsing this conversation in the dark. "Gerald Pruitt beat his wife so badly she ate through a straw for a month. The Hollis boy in '89 burned down the Cho family's first diner with three people inside—he got off on a technicality, and then he walked out to the reservoir one night and never came home. Dale Merrick—" he pulled another folder, dropped it in front of me— "sold pills to teenagers. One of them, a girl named Cassie Boone, died in her bedroom while her parents watched television downstairs."

I stared at the photograph. Dale Merrick had a smile like a used car commercial. *Given*, said the line beneath him, dated eleven days after Cassie Boone's funeral.

"This isn't justice," I said. "This is a lynch mob with better bookkeeping."

"It's justice *outside any law*," Fenn said quietly. "That's what Sheriff Doane called it, back when I was a rookie and he was teaching me the cabinet. He said the law stops at the county line and the well doesn't. He said every town has evil in it, and most towns just have to live with it, and we were the lucky ones, because we had somewhere to *put* it." His hands were steady. His eyes were not. "I believed him. God help me, Xanthe, I believed him for thirty years."

"And now?"

"Now I drive past Mabel Cho's diner and I can't remember her husband's face. Now Pastor Ellery preaches to a congregation that's forgetting him mid-hymn. Now the folders—" he touched the stack, gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal you're afraid of— "the folders are getting thinner. We haven't *given* anyone since 2019. There hasn't been anyone worth giving. And the well has started taking instead of receiving."

*Already drinking.* The thought arrived with the certainty of a diagnosis. It wasn't waiting for the equinox. It was snacking.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "Really. Not the noble version."

Fenn looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the yellow light blinked its caution against the dark, and somewhere beneath our feet—I swear I felt it, or decided I felt it, and by then the difference had stopped mattering—something shifted its weight.

"Because you're going to finish the rite," he said. "I saw the list you're building. Names in your notebook. Crane. Reyes. Cho. You've got the hospice nurse in Ohio circled." My hand actually moved toward my jacket pocket before I caught myself, and he had the grace not to smile. "I need you to hear what finishing means. From someone who knows."

"Tell me."

"The rite isn't a lock," he said. "It's a trial. That's what Doane never understood, or understood and buried down in that basement with the rest of it. Every generation, the well wakes a little, and every generation, seven descendants stand over it and *judge*. They weigh the town. They weigh what's been fed to it, and what's been kept from it, and whether the keeping was mercy or murder." He spread his hands over the folders, forty years of *given*, and his voice finally cracked along a seam I suspected had been there since 2004. "And if they judge wrong—if they judge *honestly*—it takes them too. That's the price of a seal. That's why your mother ran. That's why everyone ran. Not because the ritual was hard. Because it was a mirror, and nobody wanted to see what the well sees when it looks at Marrow Creek."

The coffee had gone cold in front of me. I hadn't touched it. I don't think either of us had breathed much.

"So don't finish it," Fenn said, and now he leaned forward, and the hat slid forgotten to the edge of the desk, and the man underneath the badge was just a tired old deputy begging in a trailer that smelled of well water. "Let it refill. Let it take the whole rotten town. Ellery's right about that much—it's mercy, what it does. It takes the pain first. Mabel doesn't grieve her husband anymore. She doesn't remember him enough to grieve. Isn't that kinder than judgment? Isn't forgetting a mercy?"

"No," I said.

It came out flat and immediate and it surprised both of us.

"Mabel Cho made me a grilled cheese yesterday and asked about my mother's garden like my mother was still alive, because Mabel remembers my mother's garden and not my mother's death, and she smiled the whole time, and it was the worst thing I've seen since the funeral." I stood up. My knees were shaking and I let them. "Forgetting isn't mercy, Deputy. It's just the well's version of your filing cabinet. *Given.* Case closed. Nothing written inside."

Fenn sat very still. Then he gathered the folders back into their stack, squared their edges with a tenderness that broke my heart a little, and locked the cabinet.

"There's one more thing," he said, not looking at me. "Your father. 2004. There's a folder."

The room went narrow and quiet.

"He wasn't taken," Fenn said. "There's no *given* line. His file says—" and here the deputy of thirty years, keeper of the cabinet, wearer of the badge, finally looked up at me, and his face was the loneliest thing in Marrow Creek— "his file says *volunteered*. And Xanthe—there's a second page. I've never shown it to anyone. Your mother begged me not to."

He reached into his breast pocket and drew out a single folded sheet, soft with age, and held it out across the desk between us like a man handing over a live grenade with the pin already pulled.

Outside, the yellow light blinked. Beneath us, the water rose.
