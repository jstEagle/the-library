## 10. The Boy Under the Floorboards

The night my father went into the ground and didn't come back out of it, I was seven years old, and I was mostly concerned with whether the microwave would beep loud enough to wake my mother.

This is the thing nobody tells you about catastrophe: it arrives on a Tuesday. It doesn't check your schedule. It doesn't wait until you've finished your homework or apologized for breaking the good glass. My father kissed the top of my head at nine-forty in the evening—smelling like sawdust and the inside of the truck—and said, "Guard the house, little bird," which was what he always said, and I said, "Always," which was what I always said, and then he went down into the cellar with a lantern and a length of rope, and that was the last time anyone in Marrow Creek saw him walking around on the surface of the earth like a normal person.

I want to be clear about the microwave. My mother worked doubles at the clinic those months, and she'd left instructions: macaroni, three minutes, stand back when it beeps because the steam bites. So there I was at ten o'clock, standing on a kitchen chair in my dinosaur pajamas, guarding the house as instructed, listening to the turntable go around, when the floor under my feet made a sound.

Not a creak. Houses creak; it's their whole personality. This was more like a word. A long, low syllable spoken by the joists themselves, the way a dog speaks before it barks.

The microwave beeped. The steam bit me. I didn't cry about that, because I was busy being terrified of something much less explainable.

* * *

Here is what I understood at seven, and have spent twenty years trying to un-understand: our house had a boy in it who wasn't ours.

He lived under the floorboards. Not in the crawlspace—I checked the crawlspace once with a flashlight and my whole arm, and found only insulation and a dead mouse and the particular disappointment of an eight-year-old archaeologist. He lived *under* the floorboards, in the way that a thought lives under a silence. You couldn't point to where he was. But you could feel the weight of him, the way you can feel someone reading over your shoulder.

I'd heard him before that night, actually. Little sounds. A hum through the heating vents that matched whatever song was stuck in my head—not the song itself, but the shape of it, like a reflection in dark water. Once, when I dropped a marble, it rolled under the stove and came back out. Rolled *back*, uphill, against physics, and stopped against my sneaker like a cat bringing me a gift.

My mother's rule about the floorboards was simple and absolute: *If you hear him, don't answer.* She never said who *him* was. She never explained. She just said it the way you say *don't touch the stove*, with the tone of a woman who has already touched the stove and lived to regret the scar.

So that night, when the floor spoke its long low syllable beneath my dinosaur feet, I did what I was raised to do. I ignored it. I ate my macaroni standing up, which felt appropriately defiant, and I turned the television on loud, and I told myself the story seven-year-olds tell themselves, which is that everything is fine if you narrate it loudly enough.

Then the lights went out.

All of them—the kitchen, the porch, even the streetlight outside, which belonged to the county and had no business obeying our house. The dark came in like water filling a glass. And in that dark, from the space between the floorboards and the underneath, a voice said:

*"Xanthe. Xanthe Reyes. If a stranger offers you candy, what do you say?"*

That was my voice. That was *my* voice, from two years earlier, from the safety video they showed us in kindergarten, from me sitting cross-legged on a rug saying *no thank you* in the flat recitation of a child who has memorized the correct answers to a world she doesn't understand yet.

Something down there was playing me back to myself.

I should have run to my mother's room. I should have done any of the sensible things. Instead—and I have interrogated this moment for two decades and never gotten a satisfactory answer out of it—I knelt down, put my mouth near the cold gap where the baseboard didn't quite meet the floor, and whispered:

"Who are you?"

And the floor breathed. And the breathing became a boy.

* * *

He came up through the wall the way frost comes up through a window—gradually, then all at once, a pattern assembling out of nothing that insisted it had been there all along. He looked about my age, maybe a year older, with hair the color of creek water and eyes that did something wrong when the lightning flashed, some fraction-of-a-second delay, like he was being lit from slightly behind himself.

He was sitting cross-legged on the kitchen floor when the lights came back on, which was somehow worse than if he'd stayed in the dark. He looked up at me with enormous patience, the way adults look at you when you've asked a question they've been waiting your whole life for you to ask.

"You're not supposed to see me yet," he said. "But your dad said you would."

"My dad's in the cellar."

"No," the boy said, gently, the way you'd correct a small factual error. "Your dad's *down*. There's a difference tonight."

I remember hugging myself. I remember the macaroni going cold and gelatinous in its bowl. I remember thinking, with the strange administrative clarity children have in emergencies: *Mother will want to know about this.*

"What's your name?" I asked.

"Wren," he said. "Like the bird. Small brown one. Sings anyway."

"Are you a ghost?"

"I'm a *wren*," he said, offended. "Ghosts are people who used to be somewhere. I'm a person who's supposed to be somewhere and isn't yet."

Seven-year-old logic found this completely acceptable. Adult logic, I'll have you know, still hasn't recovered.

We played Go Fish. I'm not making that up, and I'm not softening it for effect. The night my father descended into the tunnels under Marrow Creek and did not come back, his daughter sat on the kitchen floor by lantern light—for the power stayed off, though the refrigerator hummed, which Wren said was "on loan"—and played cards with a boy who came out of the floorboards. He cheated. Badly. He had no idea how to hold cards and kept them face-up against his chest, and I won four hands in a row, and each time I won he laughed like the sound cost him something precious and he was glad to spend it.

It was during the fifth hand that the floor under the cellar door began to sing.

Low. Wordless. A chord held so long it stopped being music and became weather. Wren's head came up like a bird dog's. His face did something I had no vocabulary for then and only found the word for recently, writing this: *bereavement in advance.* Grief applied to a wound that hadn't happened yet, worn like a coat.

"Go to bed, Xanthe," he said, and it wasn't his voice anymore, or it was, but older, and sadder, and borrowed. "Go to bed and don't come down tomorrow. Whatever your mother tells you happened—let that be what happened."

"I don't understand."

"I know." He gathered the cards with both hands, clumsy, tender, like they were eggs. "You're not supposed to. That's the whole design. Nobody understands, and that's how it stays asleep."

"*What* stays asleep?"

The singing under the floor swelled, and every window in the kitchen fogged at once, though it was October and cold, and in the fog, on every pane, the same word appeared, written from the outside in a hand I almost recognized:

*STAY.*

Wren stood up. When he stood, he was taller than he'd been sitting, and taller than that a moment later, growing the way shadows grow when the sun goes down.

"Your dad loved you more than the leaving," he said. "Hold onto that part. Your mother will ask you to hold onto all of it, and you can't, nobody can, so hold onto *that* part and let her carry the rest."

And then the front door opened—by itself, politely, like a butler—and the cold came in, and the singing changed key, and somewhere below us a lantern went out.

* * *

My mother found me asleep at the kitchen table at dawn, the cards stacked in a tower too neat for a seven-year-old, the front door wide open, frost on the inside of the windows spelling a word I would not read again for twenty years.

She woke me by shaking my shoulders, and her face was the worst thing I saw that entire season, and I include what came after in that accounting. Her face was already doing the arithmetic of widowhood. Her face was already drafting the speech.

"Daddy went away," she said. "Last night. He had to go, and he can't come back, and it isn't anybody's fault."

"Down?" I asked. "Or away?"

Her hands stopped shaking me. For a moment—one long, terrible, honest moment—she looked at me the way you look at a witness, and I understood that she knew exactly which question I was asking, and exactly why, and that some part of her had been waiting since before I was born for her daughter to ask it.

"Xanthe," she said. "Listen to me. What did you see last night?"

"A boy," I said. "He came out of the floor. We played cards. He cheated."

I watched her decide. I watched it happen behind her eyes like weather moving across a valley—the impulse to believe me warring with the necessity of not believing me, the mother fighting the keeper, and the keeper winning, because the keeper had been winning in that family for two hundred years.

"There's no boy," she said. "There was no boy. You dreamed him, baby. You dreamed him because your mind needed somewhere safe to put a hard night, and that's *good*, that's what minds are for. But here's what you have to do now."

She knelt. She took my face in her cold hands. Outside, the town of Marrow Creek was waking up to a missing man, and dogs were already barking at the tree line, and Pastor Ellery's car was already turning onto our street, slow, like a hearse rehearsing.

"Promise me you'll forget him," my mother said. "The boy. Promise me you'll forget the floor, and the singing, and the word on the windows. Promise me you'll forget all of it except that your daddy loved you more than the leaving. Can you do that? Can you promise?"

"But he was real."

"He was *insurance*," she said, and then she heard herself say it, and I watched the word land on her like a stone, watched her try to take it back, and could not. "Forget, Xanthe. Forget, and live. That's the trade. It's the only trade this town has ever offered, and everyone who refuses it ends up—" She stopped. Swallowed the end of the sentence like a fishhook.

"Ends up where?"

"Forget," she said. "Promise me."

And I promised. Of course I promised. She was my mother, and the house was full of strangers' voices by then—Ellery praying in the doorway, Deputy Fenn on the radio saying words like *foul play* and *search grid*—and a promise was the one thing I had that was mine to give.

Here is what I learned about forgetting, though, and I offer it free of charge to anyone undertaking it: it doesn't work like erasing. It works like drowning. The memory doesn't leave; it just goes down where the light doesn't reach, and it keeps singing down there, and sometimes on quiet nights you hum along without knowing why, and wake with your cheeks wet, and call it allergies.

They searched for my father for eleven days. They found his lantern at the bottom of the old well on the Crane property, dry, burning, impossible. They found his rope coiled neatly beside it, tied in a knot I have never been able to identify. They found nothing else, and eventually Marrow Creek did what Marrow Creek does, which is fold the mystery into itself like dough and keep baking.

And I grew up. I forgot, mostly, professionally, with the diligence of a girl honoring a contract. I built a whole self out of the parts that didn't mention the floor. I became excellent at maps of places that exist.

But last week, standing in my mother's empty house with a lidar unit and a grief I hadn't unpacked, I watched the scan render the space beneath the kitchen floor—and there, in the void between joists where no survey shows anything, the machine drew me a small, patient, human-shaped absence.

And on the porch, when I stepped outside, someone was waiting who had not aged a day, holding a deck of cards fanned open, cheating already.

"You kept them," Wren said. "You kept the promise. Mostly. That counts, Xanthe. That counts for so much."

"It didn't keep *you*," I said. "You're still here. Twenty years, and you're still—"

"I'm still what I was made to be," he said, and dealt me in.
