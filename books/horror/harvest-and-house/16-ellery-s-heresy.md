## 16. Ellery's Heresy

The courthouse had been flooded for three days by the time we went back for the rite, and I want to tell you the water was the worst part. It wasn't. The worst part was that Pastor Ellery beat us there.

We came in through the east loading dock—Fenn's idea, because Fenn still thought like a cop even when he looked like a man whose face had been left out in weather. Dill Crane came with us in leg irons we'd cut off him at the county line, rubbing his wrists and saying grace under his breath, though I couldn't have told you which god he was addressing. The runaway—her name was Junie, she'd finally given us a name somewhere outside Dayton—carried the maps. I carried Abigail Crane's covenant in a ziplock bag like evidence, because documentation is my love language and my defense mechanism and possibly my whole personality.

The water inside stood ankle-deep on the marble floor of the rotunda, warm as bathwater, and it moved wrong. Not toward drains. Toward the basement. Toward the well. Every drop in Marrow Creek was walking home.

And standing in the middle of it, up to his shins in something that whispered names, was Pastor Ellery, holding a lantern like a man waiting for a train that only comes for one passenger.

"You shouldn't be here," he said. His voice was fine. His face was the problem. Half of it had gone smooth—not young, not old, just *unwritten*, like a page someone had erased to use again. The left side of him was still Ellery: sixty years of sermons and bad coffee and grief worn into grooves around his mouth. The right side was nobody at all.

"Pastor," Fenn said carefully. "You're forgetting."

"I'm *letting go*," Ellery said, and smiled with half a mouth. "There's a difference, Deputy. You'd know that if you'd ever let go of anything in your life."

* * *

I should back up. Three days earlier, Mabel Cho had smiled at her own diner like it was a photograph of strangers, and I had sat in my mother's kitchen making lists until four in the morning, because lists are how I pray. Six descendants. Six seals. One well rising faster than any hydrology could explain. And one problem I hadn't solved yet, which was that every hour we spent recruiting, driving, arguing, the town forgot a little more of itself, and the people it forgot didn't come back.

Ellery had been our near miss. We'd gone to him second, after Mabel, because a pastor knows where bodies are buried—literally, in Marrow Creek's case, since the churchyard sat over Tunnel C like a lid on a pot. He'd listened to me lay out the covenant, the seven families, the equinox deadline, with the patience of a man hearing a weather report about rain he'd already decided to stand in.

Then he'd said: "Or we don't."

That was the whole sermon, really. Everything after was elaboration.

Now, standing in the flooded rotunda with the water tugging at our ankles like small hands, I got the elaboration again, complete.

"Do you know what I did before I came here?" Ellery asked us. He wasn't looking at me. He was looking at Junie, who was seventeen and had run from three homes already and held her maps against her chest like a shield made of paper. "Forty years, girl. Forty years of funerals. I buried more people than this town ever produced. Do you know what they all had in common? They remembered. Right up to the end, they remembered everything, and it hurt them, and then they died anyway, and their remembering went into the ground and rotted."

"That's called being human," Dill said. "I've done time. I know what forgetting costs. You forget your daughter's laugh, you're not free. You're just empty with extra steps."

"Your daughter," Ellery said gently, horribly, "cried herself to sleep for a year after you went inside. Would you not spare her that? Spare yourself?" He turned, water swirling around his legs, and gestured at the drowned hall behind him—at the shapes.

I hadn't wanted to look at them. I looked now.

The forgotten townsfolk stood motionless in the flooded corridors, dozens of them, holding lanterns that burned underwater without flickering. Mabel Cho was among them. She stood at parade rest, smiling her blank smile, her lantern raised, and where her face should have been there was nothing—not wounds, not absence exactly, but a smoothness, an unwrittenness, skin waiting politely for features that were never coming back. They weren't suffering. That was the thing I couldn't stop circling. They looked like people mid-sentence in a dream, content, suspended, *held*. The well didn't torture what it took. It kept it. Like a child keeping fireflies in a jar, punching holes in the lid out of what it sincerely believed was kindness.

"They're at peace," Ellery said. "Look at them. When has this town ever given anyone peace? Marrow Creek ate its founders' children and lied on every survey and buried its sins under six feet of tunnel, and we stayed and called it home because leaving felt like betrayal. The well is offering us the one thing this place never managed." He spread his hands, and the right hand was beautiful, unlined, innocent. "Mercy. Oblivion as an act of grace. I've preached resurrection my whole life, Ms. Reyes. This is the only version that ever showed up on schedule."

* * *

"You're half-gone," I said. "You know that, right? Whatever's talking through the smooth side of your face isn't you. It's the well wearing your collar."

"And how would you know?" Ellery asked, mild as Sunday. "You've been gone twenty years. For all you know, the woman giving orders in this rotunda is also half-erased. Grief does that. Guilt does that. You came back for a funeral and stayed for a ritual, Xanthe. Which of those impulses do you trust?"

The awful thing was that it landed. It landed because I'd been asking myself versions of it since the porch light, since Wren, since the tunnels sang my own breathing back at me. I am a person who trusts documents over feelings, and the document here said: seven seals or the town drowns in warm water that loves you. But the feeling—the tired, human, forty-years-of-funerals feeling—said: maybe he's right, maybe ending the pain is the same as healing it, maybe I'm just another founder's descendant refusing to let the past stay buried because the past is all I have left.

Junie broke the spell, because seventeen-year-olds are immune to eloquence.

"So we just let everybody melt?" she said. "My mom forgot my birthday two years running and it wrecked me, and you're telling me forgetting everything is a *gift*? You're standing in a church basement full of faceless people calling it mercy. That's not mercy. That's a guy who's already drowning pulling everyone else into the water so he doesn't go down alone."

Ellery looked at her for a long moment. "You have your grandmother's mouth," he said. "She told me off in this same building in 1987. Over the rite. She wanted to let it refill too, you know. The Cranes always wavered."

"My—" Junie stopped. Adjusted her grip on the maps. "Don't do that. Don't make me matter to you. I'm not here to be your redemption arc."

Nobody spoke. The water whispered. If you listened too long—and I had, God help me, I had listened—you could pick names out of it. My name. My father's name. It pronounced them both the same way, tenderly, like a substitute teacher taking attendance for a class that no longer existed.

* * *

It was Fenn who ended it, and I want to be honest about what that means, because Fenn had spent thirty years feeding missing-persons reports into that well and calling it justice, and a man like that doesn't get to be the good guy just because he picks a side late.

He'd been quiet through all of it, standing at the loading dock door with his service weapon holstered and his hand nowhere near it, watching Ellery the way you watch a structure burn—professionally, sadly, calculating collapse zones. Now he stepped forward, boots splashing, and his voice came out flat and cop-flat, the voice he probably used on domestic calls.

"Pastor. Step away from the stairwell."

"The stairwell is public property, Deputy."

"The stairwell goes down to the well chamber, and these six people have a legal-moral—I don't know, a *covenantal*—right to be there, and you are obstructing them, and I am asking you once." Fenn's face, what remained of it, was doing something complicated. He'd known Ellery thirty years. Confessed to him, probably. Taken communion from his hands. "You're half-forgotten, Walt. You don't get to decide for the ones who still remember."

"Someone has to decide for them," Ellery said. "That's what a shepherd is."

"Yeah," Fenn said. "And when the shepherd decides the flock should walk into the pond, that's not shepherding anymore."

Ellery moved. I'll give him this—he wasn't fast, but he was committed, and commitment covers distance. He got between us and the descending stair, arms out, lantern high, and the light caught the smooth half of his face and threw his shadow huge and wrong across the flooded wall, a shadow with no face at all, and the water rose an inch while we stood there, I swear it, an inch in ten seconds, warm and eager, whispering *Xanthe, Xanthe* like it had missed me.

"Let it refill," Ellery said, and now he was pleading, and the pleading was the most himself he'd sounded all night. "Please. I have watched this town break every family it ever held. I have read the same funeral texts until I can recite them asleep. There is no version of saving Marrow Creek that doesn't cost a child, and I have buried enough children. Let it take the memory and keep the peace. Let it be finished with us. It's *kinder*, Xanthe. I promise you it's kinder. I remember what it feels like—that's the one thing I still remember—it feels like being forgiven for existing."

And God, the horrible thing, the thing I still can't write down without my hand shaking, is that for one full second I believed him. One second of standing in warm water that knew my name, wanting to put down the ziplock bag and the maps and the whole inherited weight of a town that had eaten my childhood, and just—stop. Stop carrying. Be held, even if holding is drowning.

Junie grabbed my sleeve. Dill said my name. And Fenn drew his gun.

* * *

Here is what happened next, as best as I can reconstruct it, because it took less time than a breath and I will be reconstructing it for the rest of my life.

Fenn didn't point the gun at us. I need that on the record, whatever record survives. He pointed it at the ceiling and fired once, and the report went through the rotunda like the building itself flinching, plaster raining into the warm water, and in the ringing aftermath he said, in a voice like a man reading charges:

"Walter Ellery, you are obstructing a lawful—no. No, that's not it." He lowered the gun. His remaining eyebrow was trembling. "Walt. I have fed eleven people into that well. Eleven reports I falsified, eleven names I gave it instead of due process, because it was easier than the law and I told myself it was justice. I know exactly what its mercy costs, because I've seen the ledger. It doesn't erase pain, Pastor. It erases the *person*, and keeps the pain, and wears it. Look at your hand."

Ellery looked at his hand. The smooth one. And in the lantern light we all saw it: beneath the unwritten skin, faint as a watermark, something moving. Not veins. Writing. Lines of text drifting under the surface like fish under ice, and I knew that handwriting. Everyone in that room knew it. It was the handwriting from the tunnel walls, from the courthouse covenant, from the margins of plat books a hundred years old. The well wrote in its captives eventually. It was writing in Ellery.

"That's not peace," Fenn said quietly. "That's dictation."

Ellery stared at his hand for a long, long moment. Then he laughed—a real laugh, cracked and human, from the written half of his face—and the sound of it nearly broke me worse than anything the well had done.

"Thirty-eight years of seminary questions," he said, "and the answer comes from Dale Fenn with a sidearm in a flooded courthouse." He looked at each of us. At Junie last. "I'm sorry, girl. Your grandmother was wrong and so am I, and being wrong kindly is still being wrong." He stepped aside from the stairwell. Water swirled into the space he'd occupied like the well objecting. "Go down and seal it. And when it asks you for something sweet—and it will ask, it always offers before it takes—remember that I warned you the offer is the trap."

He picked up his lantern. He walked into the eastern corridor, into the crowd of the faceless and their impossible flames, going to stand with his congregation, and none of us tried to stop him, because what do you say? What do you say to a man choosing the jar?

At the corridor's mouth he paused. "One more thing, Deputy. The gun. Keep it ready down there." He didn't turn around. "Not for me. For whoever comes up out of the water wearing a face you love. Check the eyes. Mine have started going smooth at the edges, and I can feel it, and it feels *fine*, and that's how you'll know."

Then the dark took him, and the water whispered his name without any tenderness at all.

* * *

We stood at the top of the stairwell, six descendants and a deputy, listening to the well rise.

Dill flexed his scarred wrists. "Man of God makes a hell of a doorstop," he said, but softly, without mockery, the way you speak at gravesides.

"He's still down there," Junie said. "Part of him. The part that apologized."

"Parts aren't enough," I said, and heard my mother in it, and hated that, and kept going. "That's the whole lesson of this town. Half-measures, half-rites, half-forgotten pastors. Down there we finish things or we don't come back up."

Fenn checked his weapon with hands that almost didn't shake, then holstered it, then unholstered it again and held it low against his thigh, and looked at me with the one eye that was still entirely his.

"Reyes. If it comes to the seventh seal and it asks for the kid—" he nodded at Junie, who stiffened— "or for you, or for any of them, and the math says one life buys the town—"

"It won't come to that," I said.

"You don't know that."

"No," I admitted. "But I know what I promised, and it wasn't math."

The stairwell breathed warm air up at us, air that smelled like lake bottom and my father's workshop, and far below, getting closer with every minute, the water sang the opening bars of a hymn I'd known all my life, pitched perfectly to harmonize with six heartbeats and one gun hammer easing back.

We went down.
