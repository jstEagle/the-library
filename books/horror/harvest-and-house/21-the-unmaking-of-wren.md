## 21. The Unmaking of Wren

The water took Fenn the way the town had always taken things—quietly, without asking, as if it had simply remembered he belonged to it.

One moment he was braced in the archway with his arms spread like a man holding open a door for company he didn't like, and the next the flood had him by the shoulders and pulled him under, and his lantern went spinning off across the black surface, still lit, still burning, a little orange eye drifting away from everything it had ever watched over.

I screamed his name. I'd like to tell you I said something profound. I said "FENN," the way you say a name when you want it to be a rope.

The water did not care. The water had never cared about names, not really—not until we taught it to, down here, in the circle, with our memories bleeding out of us one by one into the stone.

Six seals. Six of us kneeling at the points of Abigail Crane's star, ankle-deep and rising, our faces wet and not entirely from the flood. Dill Crane was sobbing without noise, the way men learn to in places where noise costs them. The hospice nurse—Ruthie Okafor, who had driven fourteen hours on four hours' sleep because her grandmother's name was in a plat book from 1891—was whispering something that might have been prayer or might have been inventory, counting what she had left to give. The runaway kid, Juniper, seventeen years old and furious about it, had both hands pressed flat to the seal-stone like she could hold the town down through sheer teenage stubbornness.

And Wren stood at the seventh point, where no descendant knelt, and looked at me with that face that hadn't changed in twenty years, and waited.

"No," I said. It came out cracked. Everything in me came out cracked; I'd given up my father's face an hour ago and I could feel the hole where it used to be, a socket of absence behind my eyes. "No. There has to be another way."

"There isn't." Wren's voice was my own voice, aged twenty years, played back to me—the voice I'd given it, all those nights on the porch and under the blankets, the night my father went below and never came up as himself again. "You know there isn't. You've known since the courthouse. You're just hoping I'll lie to you now, at the end, after everything."

"You lied to *me* for twenty years."

"I kept you," Wren said gently. "It's different. It felt different from the inside."

The water rose past our ankles. Past mid-calf. It was warm—that was the worst part, the warmth, the way it whispered names against your skin, offering itself like a bath, like a mother, like mercy. Ellery had believed in that warmth. Ellery was somewhere above us or below us or dissolved into it entirely, and I found I couldn't spare him any grief. Maybe that was the well working on me too. Maybe that was just Tuesday in Marrow Creek.

"Xanthe." Dill's voice, rough as gravel in a coffee can. "The seals are set. If we don't close the seventh, the other six don't hold. It's not a choice between Wren and nothing. It's a choice between Wren and everyone."

"I know the arithmetic," I snapped. "I've been doing the arithmetic since I was nine years old. My whole life is arithmetic somebody else did first."

Wren laughed—a small sound, startled out of it, and for a second it wasn't a guardian of anything, it was just my friend, sitting cross-legged on porch boards in the summer of 2004, telling me the well was just a hole and holes couldn't hurt you if you didn't look down.

"You finally remember," Wren said softly. "Don't you. What I am."

And God help me, I did. It had been coming back to me in pieces since the descent—since the singing tunnels, since the walls with my mother's handwriting, since the truth of what my father had chosen. He had gone down into the dark on purpose. He had become a keeper. And before he sealed himself into the stone forever, he had taken the last unspent thing he owned—

—and made it small, and warm, and shaped like a friend for a lonely girl on a porch.

*Wren was his parting gift.* Not an imaginary friend. A bequest.

"You're him," I whispered. "Or—you were his. You're what he had left."

"I'm what he had left," Wren agreed. "Which means I was never really mine to keep. And neither—" that gentle, terrible smile— "were you ever really mine to protect. But I did anyway. Because that was the job. Because that was the love. They were the same job, Xanthe. Nobody ever tells you that."

The water hit our knees. Juniper made a sound low in her throat. Ruthie grabbed her hand.

"The rite," Wren said. "Say it. Say my name."

"I don't—" I started, and then stopped, because I did. I had known it all along, the way you know a word that's been sitting on the tip of your tongue for two decades. It wasn't written anywhere. It wasn't in Abigail Crane's covenant or the flooded archive or the plat books that lied. It was in the place where my father had put it, wrapped around the gift like ribbon: the name he'd carried, the name he'd spent, the true name of the thing he'd made out of his own remaining self.

My father's name was Elias Reyes.

But Wren's name—the name underneath, the seed the gift grew from, the thing my father had whispered into the making of it—

*Samuel.*

His father's name. His grandfather's. The name that ran back through the Reyes line like a root, the name he'd wanted to hand down and never got the chance to hand to a son. So he'd handed it to a story instead. To a guardian. To the last thing he built with his hands before the dark closed over them.

"Samuel," I said.

The chamber changed.

I don't have better words than that. The water flinched. The lantern-light bent toward me. Every faceless drowned soul standing motionless in the flooded halls above turned its blank head at once, like a field of flowers finding the sun, and somewhere far below us—below even this—something enormous rolled over in its sleep and listened.

Wren closed its eyes. When they opened they were wet, and guardians aren't supposed to be able to do that, and I think that's how I knew it was real consent and not duty. Duty doesn't cry.

"That's me," Wren said wonderingly. "That's—I'd forgotten I had one. Twenty years without a name. Do you know what that's like? Being called only by what you're *for*?" A shaky breath. "Say it again."

"Samuel."

"*Again.*"

"Samuel. Samuel. Samuel." It became a rhythm, and the rhythm became the rite, and the rite became the seventh seal, and I knelt at the seventh point of the star with the water climbing my ribs and spoke the words Abigail Crane had left us—the old ones, the binding ones, the ones that meant *sleep, and stay slept, and take only what is freely given*—and I gave him.

Freely. Knowingly. With his hand in mine, which was smaller than I remembered, the way hands get when you finally really look at them.

The well drank.

I will not describe it fully. Some things belong to the six of us who were there, and to the stone, and to whatever ledger the dark keeps. But I will tell you that it did not feel like losing. That is the trap of it, the horror of it, the tenderness of it: it felt like being paid back. Like every debt Marrow Creek owed my family—my father, my mother, the years we fled, the funeral I'd come home to bury—was settled all at once, in full, in a currency of light.

Wren—Samuel—stood in the center of the circle as the water receded, drawn down, drawn home, and began to come apart the way morning fog comes apart: not violently, not tragically, just *honestly*, returning to the air it had always been borrowed from.

"Xanthe." The edges of it were already going soft. "Listen. I only get to say this once, so stop crying long enough to hear it."

"I'm not crying."

"You are absolutely crying. You cried exactly like this when you were nine and I told you the well was just a hole." The smile was fading with the rest of it, going gentle at the borders. "You finished it. Do you understand? Your father started something and your mother ran from it and you—you *came back*. Everyone else stayed or fled. You're the only one who ever chose. That's why it worked. That's why it finally, finally worked."

"Come with us," I said, stupidly, humanly, the way you say things you know are impossible because the alternative is swallowing them. "Upstairs. We'll get a house. We'll put a porch light—"

"You already are putting up a porch light." It nodded toward the tunnel mouth, upward, toward the town, toward a future I hadn't confessed to anyone yet. "I heard you planning it. In the courthouse. You talk to yourself exactly like you talked to me. Same cadence. It's very annoying. I loved it completely."

The water was calm now. Still. The whispers had stopped. Somewhere beneath us, the thing in the well turned its face to the wall of its long sleep, and the sleep held, because seven seals held, because one of them was named.

"Thank you," Samuel said.

"Don't thank me. Don't you dare—"

"For the name." Its voice was barely a voice now, more weather than words, more memory than weather. "Twenty years I was 'Wren,' and Wren was a job. You gave me back the person inside the job. Nobody's done that for a keeper in three hundred years, Xanthe. Maybe nobody ever thought to try." The last of it was nearly transparent, and through it I could almost see—just almost—another shape, taller, older, a man's outline folded inside the friend's outline like a letter inside an envelope, finally delivered. "Tell them I said hi. Tell them it didn't hurt. Tell them—"

The chamber was empty.

The water lay flat as glass. Six people knelt in a circle of stone, alive, sealed, whole, in a town that would keep breathing now, and the silence where a voice had been was the loudest thing I had ever heard.

Juniper got up first. She was crying too, furious about that as well, and she crossed the circle and put her arms around me, awkward and bone-hard and seventeen, and said, "We should go up. Someone should see the sunrise. Someone should—it feels wrong to let today end underground."

"Yeah," I said. "Yeah, okay."

Dill helped Ruthie stand. Ruthie helped Dill. That's how it works; that's the whole secret; that's the entire ritual, reduced to its honest parts. We gathered the lanterns—all but Fenn's, which was gone, which had drifted wherever drift goes—and we climbed.

* * *

We came up out of the tunnels through the courthouse cellar, into gray early light, into a town that was still standing, still smelling of rain and rust and Mabel Cho's coffee, which she was somehow already brewing in the diner at five in the morning despite remembering none of us and most of herself. She'd start over. They all would. The forgetting would stop now—the well was asleep—but the empty places would stay empty, and filling them was going to be work. Years of work. Our work.

That was the trade. Nobody had hidden it from us. Invisibility for belonging. The town would never make the maps again, never make the surveys, never appear in any record but ours—and ours would have to be enough. Ours would have to be everything.

On the courthouse steps, Ruthie asked the question I'd been avoiding.

"So what happens now? To us?"

I looked down Main Street. At the diner light coming on. At Pastor Ellery's church, dark and waiting for whoever would fill it. At the water tower, at the power lines, at the ordinary miracle of a town that had decided, against all odds and all sense, to keep existing.

"Now," I said, "we go home."

I didn't mean the house on Birch Street, though that too. I meant the word the way the founders had meant it, the way my father had meant it when he walked down instead of running away: home as a thing you keep, not a thing you have.

Behind us, in the cellar dark, the seals hummed their low note, patient as stone.

And three blocks away, on the porch of a house on Birch Street, a light was burning that I had not yet turned on.

I intend to find out why.

But first—coffee. Even the end of the world keeps better with coffee.
