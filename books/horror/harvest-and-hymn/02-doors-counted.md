## 2. Doors, Counted

The parsonage stood at the end of Chapel Lane, its garden gone to seed and its windows fogged with the breath of a hundred unused years. Vesper Sterling paid a week's rent in cash to a landlord who never looked up from his ledger, took the iron key without counting the change, and carried his cases inside one at a time, the way his mother had taught him.

*Never bring everything in at once,* she used to say, though he could no longer conjure her face when he said the words to himself, only the cadence of them, low and urgent, like a woman passing secrets through a wall. *If you bring everything in at once, it knows where all of you lives.*

He had been six, maybe seven. There had been a house then—a flat, really, above a laundrette in a town he had never been able to find again on any map—with a hallway that had five doors off it, and every night, before she would let him sleep, his mother had walked him down that hallway with her hand on his shoulder and made him say it aloud.

"One," she would whisper, tapping the bathroom door. "Two." The cupboard where the vacuum lived. "Three." Her bedroom. "Four." His. "Five." The door at the end, which was painted shut and never opened, which she dusted but never touched, and which he had privately believed for years was not a door at all but a painting of a door, so complete was its refusal to be anything else.

"Five doors," he would say. "All counted. All shut."

"Say the rest."

"Nothing comes in unless I let it."

"And the lights?"

"Left burning."

She had kept a lamp going in every room all night, every night, and when he'd asked her once, in the careless way children ask, *why*, she had gone very still, the way she went still whenever the question touched something below the waterline of her life, and said only: *Because a room in the dark is a room that's forgotten what it is. And a thing that's forgotten what it is will let anything in and call it itself.*

He had thought, for thirty years, that these were the eccentricities of a frightened woman. He had thought his counting was a habit, like biting his nails, like sleeping on the left side of the bed, something worn into him by repetition the way a stair is worn into a curve. He had never once thought of it as an inheritance.

That came later. That came at dusk, with the bell.

* * *

The parsonage had eleven doors, and he counted them twice before he allowed himself to unpack.

The front door, heavy oak, swollen in its frame. The back door, which opened onto a kitchen garden strangled by bindweed. The pantry door, the cellar door, the door under the stairs that opened on nothing but a shelf of dead wasps and a jar of preserves so old the fruit had turned the color of dark amber and floated like specimens. Four doors upstairs—his bedroom, the linen room, a child's room with a cot still in it and a window that looked out over the lane, and a fourth room at the end of the corridor whose keyhole had been filled with wax so old it had gone the color of bone.

Eleven. He walked it again. Eleven.

He lit the lamps. The parsonage had electricity—the landlord had mentioned it with the pride of a man describing a miracle—but Vesper had brought lamps, three of them, and oil, and he set one in the hall and one at the top of the stairs and one on the table by his bed, and lit them all, and felt the old familiar loosening in his chest, the ritual doing what ritual does: making a shape around the dark and daring it to be otherwise.

He told himself the comfort was irrational. He was a folklorist; irrationality was his trade. He had spent his career collecting the small desperate technologies people built against the dark—the horseshoes, the hawthorn sprigs, the bread and salt, the counted doors—and he had always believed, with the mild superiority of the scholar, that he himself stood outside all of it, taking notes.

He unpacked his recorder. He set out his notebooks, the good ones, sewn-spine, and sharpened six pencils because Edith Marsh's hands had shaken when she'd stopped mid-verse, and he suspected he would need to write fast and hold nothing fragile.

*You've already heard it once. That's how it starts.*

He had turned that sentence over perhaps a hundred times since leaving her cottage. It was nonsense, of course. He had never heard the Marrowfen harvest hymn before yesterday. He had grown up in towns whose names he couldn't find, among people who weren't family, and the only hymns he knew were the ones every child learns and forgets. And yet the melody she had sung—three lines of it, before the color drained out of her face and she pressed her knuckles to her mouth—had sat in him with the terrible familiarity of a word on the tip of the tongue. Not *I know this*. Something worse. *I have always known this, and I have been pretending not to.*

He wrote that down. He underlined it. He did not like his own handwriting.

* * *

Dusk came early to Marrowfen, or seemed to. The hollow gathered the dark the way a cupped hand gathers water, and by half past five the lane below the parsonage window was blue-grey and empty, the cottages hunched with their chimneys smoking, and the mist coming off the fen in slow, deliberate sheets, like something being laid down with care, layer by layer, for a purpose.

Vesper stood at the child's room window with his lamp behind him, watching the village put itself to bed.

That was when the bell rang.

One stroke. Deep, bronze, round—a sound with a body to it, a sound with weight and wear, the sound of a bell that had been rung by hands for generations and had absorbed all of them. It came from the direction of the church, down the lane, and it rolled up the hollow and broke against the parsonage like a slow wave.

He counted without meaning to. It was what he did. What he was *for*, apparently.

The bell tolled nine times, and stopped.

Vesper stood very still.

The church bell of Marrowfen had been melted down in 1911. He knew this the way he knew most things about the village: from the county records he'd read on the train, from the parish histories, from the one-line entry that had first snagged his attention and brought him here at all. *1911, bell removed and sold for metal, proceeds to the restoration fund.* A bell does not ring after it has been melted down. A bell does not ring after it has been, in all likelihood, beaten into shell casings and spent in a war, its bronze scattered across France in pieces too small to mourn.

And yet.

He went to the window and looked down the lane. The church stood black against the last of the light, and he could see the tower clearly, and the tower was empty—no bell, no frame, the belfry open to the sky like a mouth with the tooth gone.

The bell rang again. Nine strokes. He counted every one, and his lips moved, and he hated himself for it.

* * *

He did what his mother would have done. He did not go down.

He went through the parsonage instead, room by room, lamp by lamp, checking. The hall lamp burned. The stair lamp burned. He checked the pantry, the cellar—cellar door shut, and he put a chair under the knob, which he had not done since he was a child and which his hands did as if they had done it yesterday. He checked the eleven doors. Eleven. He checked the wax-filled keyhole at the end of the corridor and found, with a small cold shock, that the wax was warm.

Not warm the way a house is warm. Warm the way a hand is warm. As though something on the other side had been resting its forehead against the door, the way a child does, listening.

He stood in the corridor with his lamp shaking slightly in his grip and made himself breathe.

*Nothing comes in unless I let it.*

But the thought arrived then, uninvited, in his own voice but not his own cadence: *Where did you learn that? Who taught you the doors, Vesper? Who taught you the lamps?*

And the answer rose in him with the slow dread of water rising in a well: *She did. The woman whose face I cannot remember. The woman who moved us from town to town like a woman moving ahead of something. The woman who made me count five doors in a flat above a laundrette and never once told me why five, never once told me what she was counting against.*

He went back to the child's room window because it was the room with the best view of the lane, and because the cot in it disturbed him less than the waxed door did, and he stood there while full dark came down, and the bell tolled again—nine strokes, always nine, at intervals he could not predict and could not stop timing—and he watched the windows of the village.

One by one, as the bell rang, lights came on in the cottages below. Not all at once. One by one. And in each lit window, if he leaned close to the glass and breathed on it and wiped it with his sleeve, he could see a figure standing. Standing, not moving. Standing in the light of a lamp, the way a person stands when they are counting something.

*They're counting too,* he thought. *They all count. The whole village counts.*

And then the thought beneath the thought, the one that had been waiting for him all evening at the bottom of the dark like a stone at the bottom of a pool:

*And my mother counted. And my mother had no face in my memory, and no history, and no grave, and no family I ever met. And I have come to a village where the bell rings without a bell, and the first thing I did here was count the doors.*

He took out his notebook and wrote, in handwriting that had gone unsteady:

*The rituals are not mine. I thought I inherited a habit. I inherited a defense. She wasn't eccentric. She was garrisoned. And you do not garrison yourself against nothing—you garrison against a specific enemy, and you teach your child the layout of the fort.*

*Question: where did she learn it?*

He looked at the question for a long time. He already knew the answer was not *where*. He already knew the question was *when*, and the answer was a date, and the date was written somewhere in this village, in some ledger, in some hand, probably in Edith Marsh's careful schoolroom script.

The bell tolled nine. Below in the lane, a light came on in Edith Marsh's cottage, and her small shape appeared in the window, and she stood there, and she did not look out at the night.

She looked up. Directly up, at the child's room window of the parsonage, at him, across forty yards of dark, with the certainty of a woman who has known all evening exactly which window he would be standing in.

And she raised one hand—not in greeting—and pointed, once, at the door of his own house.

Then she turned away, and her lamp went out, and the bell tolled nine times more, and Vesper Sterling stood in a dead child's room with his mother's ritual failing around him like a wall in rain, and understood that tomorrow he would have to ask Edith Marsh what the counting was *for*—and that she would tell him, and that the telling would be worse than the bell.
