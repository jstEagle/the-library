## 5. Children of the Count

Mara Quill had taught in Marrowfen for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once had to fetch a child home from the road between the schoolhouse and the hollow. The village was small enough that every mother knew every other mother's step, and the children—well, the children were Marrowfen's one uncomplicated joy. They ran. They shouted. They came when called.

So when she found Tobias Wren standing barefoot in the churchyard at half past eleven on a Tuesday night, humming, she did not at first understand what she was seeing.

He was in his nightshirt. His feet were grey with dew and cold, and he did not shiver. His eyes were open—Mara would remember this later, would fix on it like a nail she could hang her sanity from—his eyes were open, but no one was home behind them. He stood facing the hollow, where the old well sat under its collapsed timber shelter, and he hummed a tune she did not know, low and steady, the way other children hummed lullabies.

"Tobias," she said.

He did not stop humming.

"Tobias Wren, you look at me."

His lips kept moving. The tune wound on, patient as water finding a crack. And then, because she could not think of anything else to do, Mara put her hands on his shoulders and turned him around, and walked him home through the wet grass, and the whole way he hummed, and the whole way the tune sat in her ears like a splinter she couldn't work loose.

His mother, Ivy, opened the door with her cardigan clutched shut and her face the colour of candle tallow. "Third night," she said, before Mara had said a word. "Third night he's got out. We bolt the door and he's still got out."

"Where's his father?"

"Asleep." Ivy said it flatly, and Mara understood. Half the village was sleeping like the dead these past weeks—sleeping through the bell, sleeping through their own children walking out of the house. Sleeping the way you sleep when something has decided you may sleep.

Mara got Tobias into bed. As she tucked the blanket under his chin, his humming stopped, mid-phrase, and his eyes came back—flickered into focus like a lamp turned up—and he looked at her with such ordinary, waking terror that her heart broke clean in half.

"Miss Quill," he whispered. "I keep dreaming about the water. And in the dream, I know all the words."

* * *

By Thursday there were nine of them.

Nine children out of twenty-two, found in the nights, standing in fields and lanes and doorways, all facing the hollow, all humming the same air. The Reverend's daughter, little Nell, had been found on the well's own timber shelter, sitting on the rotten planks with her legs dangling over the black water below, swinging her feet, humming, while her father slept three fields away. It was Edith Marsh who pulled her down—old Edith, who moved like a woman of sixty when there was need of it—and Edith who came to the schoolhouse the next morning with her jaw set like a door being locked.

"You'll keep them in at night," Edith said. It was not a request. "You've the big room. The one with the iron stove."

"It's a schoolhouse, Edith, not an orphanage."

"It's whatever keeps them off the water." Edith set both hands flat on Mara's desk, and Mara saw that they were shaking. "You think I don't know what I'm asking? I buried a husband in that ledger. I know what's owed in this village. But the children—" Her voice caught. "The children aren't in the ledger yet. Not properly. Not till they're sung. And I will not stand in a church and watch them be sung."

Mara looked past her, out the window, to where Vesper Sterling was crossing the yard with his recording boxes, that soft-footed outsider with his careful questions and his notebook full of other people's songs. She had not liked him when he came. She did not trust a man who arrived in a dying village asking for its voice; it was like a man arriving at a funeral asking for the rings. But he had gone pale at the right moments. He had not laughed at Edith. And last night, Ivy Wren said, he had sat up with Tobias until dawn with the lights all burning.

"Bring him," Mara said, surprising herself. "If we're doing this, we do it with everything we've got. And he counts things. I've seen it. He counts doors, windows, children. He counts the way I do."

Edith's eyebrows rose. "You count?"

"Every head, every night, since I started teaching. Twenty-two. You count your class or you lose one someday and don't know when." Mara heard her own voice go strange. "He counts like someone taught him to count. Like it's a prayer."

* * *

They convened that evening in the big room, and Mara watched Vesper take in the twenty-two small mattresses laid out in rows with an expression she recognized because she wore it herself: the arithmetic of dread. Twenty-two children. Nine already walking. Thirteen not yet.

"You've heard it," he said. It wasn't quite a question. "The hymn."

"Bits. Everyone's heard bits. It's a harvest song; we've all sung snatches of it at the turning of the year." She wrapped her arms around herself. "But we don't sing it whole. We never sing it whole, and we never sing the same verse twice. That's the rule. I thought it was superstition. A game, like not saying the name of the river in winter."

"And now the children hum it whole. In their sleep."

"Whole," Mara said. "In parts I've never heard any adult sing. Vesper—" She stopped, and made herself go on. "Tobias said he knows all the words. In his dream. He said the water taught him."

Vesper set down his notebook, and she saw that his hands were not steady. "The well has a version of the song," he said carefully, "that the living version leaves out. I've been transcribing what the villagers will give me, and the verses don't add up. There are gaps. Counted gaps. And the children are humming the gaps."

"You think it's calling them."

"I think it's *counting* them." He said it quietly, the way you say the worst things. "One verse, one soul. That's the structure. I can show you the arithmetic if you want it, though I'd rather not look at it again myself."

Mara had spent eleven years teaching children their numbers, and something in her went cold and certain. "Then we keep them where the water can't reach them, and we don't let a single one out of our sight until we understand what's owed."

They worked through the evening. The schoolhouse was stone below, timber above, and its doors were old but honest oak; Vesper went through the building like a man auditing it, and Mara followed, and together they did what neither had ever done with another living soul: they counted aloud, together, and it did not feel foolish. It felt like the first true thing either of them had said in years.

"Two doors. Front and back. Both barred."

"Six windows, ground floor. Shutters on four. We'll board the other two."

"Stove. Lamp oil for three nights. Water in the cistern—" He stopped at the cistern tap and did not drink, and Mara understood that too, and said nothing.

They carried the children in after supper, on the pretext of a harvest sleep-feast, blankets and bread and honey, and the children thought it a grand adventure. They laid the mattresses in rows and Mara stood at the door and counted them through, touching each head as they passed, an old habit, and Vesper stood beside her with his ledger of his own, marking, and their counts matched. Twenty-two.

The parents were the hard part. Some were grateful and wept; some were hollow-eyed and vague and had to be told twice; and three—Mara noted their names in the private ledger of her heart—three were angry, and asked why the schoolteacher thought she knew better than the church, and looked toward the hollow as they said it, and would not meet her eye. One of them, Tam Osgood, who had coached the boys at football every autumn for a decade, said, "It's only a song, Mara. Songs don't hurt anyone. Maybe it's time somebody sang it proper."

"Maybe it's time you went home, Tam," she said, and barred the door behind him.

* * *

The first night, nothing came.

The children slept, and the stove ticked, and Vesper sat by the window with his lamp turned low, transcribing, and Mara lay on her cot near the door with her boots on. At dusk the bell rang—the bell that had been melted down before her grandmother was born—and she heard Vesper's pen stop, and heard him breathe, and neither of them spoke of it. What was there to say? They had both stopped pretending, in that first hour of counting together, that the bell was a trick of the wind.

The second night, the children began to hum in their sleep.

It started with Tobias, a thread of sound in the dark, and then Nell caught it, and then a boy called Sam Osgood—Tam's own son, God forgive them all—and then it moved down the rows like fire through dry grass, until all nine of the marked children were humming in unison, eyes closed, faces slack, the tune rising and falling in the dark room like breathing. The other thirteen slept on, silent, and somehow that was worse.

Mara got up and stood in the middle of the room, and the humming did not falter. It was not a child's humming anymore. It was too steady, too matched, too old. She knew—she knew the way you know a face under a hood—that it was not the children making the sound. It was the sound passing through them, the way wind passes through a fence.

"Vesper."

He was already up. He had a candle lit and the notebook open, and his lips were moving as he listened, matching the hum to his transcriptions. "It's the gap verses," he whispered. "All of them. In order. They're—Mara, they're singing the missing part of the census."

"Make them stop."

"I don't think I can make them stop." He crouched by Tobias's mattress, and his face in the candlelight was grey. "I think the song is being sung *through* them, and I think—" He swallowed. "I think when the last verse is finished, the count is complete. Whatever it's counting toward."

Mara looked at the rows of small sleeping bodies, at the candle, at the barred doors, and felt the shape of the thing they were trapped in close around her like a hand. "How many verses are left?"

Vesper turned pages with a hand that shook, counting, and she watched him arrive at the number the way a man watches his own blood pool.

"One," he said.

Outside, far off across the dark fields, the bell began to ring—not the dusk toll, not the measured count, but fast and glad, like a wedding, like a homecoming—and beneath the bell, beneath the children's humming, Mara heard, or dreamed she heard, a great soft movement of water rising somewhere in the hollow, rising and rising, patient as a tide that has been walking toward the shore for a hundred years.

Vesper looked up from his notebook, and his face had gone the colour of the candle.

"It's my name," he said. "The last verse. It's my name."
