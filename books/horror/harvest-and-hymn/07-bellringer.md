## 7. Bellringer

The bell had not stopped ringing in three days.

Mara Quill heard it through the schoolhouse walls as she chalked the morning's arithmetic, the sound arriving not through her ears but through the floor, the desks, the bones of her own hands wrapped around a stick of chalk that trembled against the slate. It was not a loud ringing. That was the worst of it. A proper bell would have shattered glass and driven everyone mad with its clamor, and madness could at least be named. This was a sound like a spoon struck against the inside of a skull—patient, intimate, pitched so low it seemed to come from beneath the village rather than above it. The bell had been melted down in 1911. Everyone knew this. There was a photograph in the church vestry of men in flat caps standing around the crucible, proud as grooms. And yet each dusk it rang, and now it did not stop at all, and no one in Marrowfen said so out loud.

She finished the sum. *Seven children minus none,* she wrote, because that was how she thought of it now: seven children, minus none, and she intended to keep it that way.

"Miss Quill," said Tomasin Wren from the back row, "my da was singing last night."

Mara set the chalk down. "Was he."

"In the garden. Standing in the beans. He sang the one about the counting, the one Gran says we're never to sing twice." Tomasin was nine and had his father's long face and his mother's habit of watching everything. "He got the words wrong, though. He put our name where the Alderys go."

The room went very quiet. Outside, the bell tolled on, unhurried, like something patient with an enormous amount of time.

"Did he stop?" Mara asked.

"When Mum called him in. But he looked—" Tomasin turned his hands over, searching for the shape of it. "He looked like a house with nobody home. Like the windows were still lit but there wasn't anyone walking round inside."

* * *

By evening Mara understood that Tomasin had described half the village.

She walked home along the fen road past cottages whose windows she had known all her life—the Hollises' geraniums, the Petches' blue door, old Widow Crane's cat asleep on the sill since before Mara could read—and saw them lit from within by people standing motionless, mouths open, singing. Not together. Each alone, each in their own kitchen or doorway or garden row, singing different verses of the same hymn, and the verses braided in the air over the lane into something that made her teeth ache and her eyes water and her heart beat in a rhythm slightly wrong from her own.

She stopped at the Petches'. Ellen Petch had taught Mara her letters. Ellen had pulled her from the flooded beck when she was six, had held her head over a bucket when she drank bad well-water at eleven, had cried at Mara's mother's funeral and brought a ham she could ill afford. Ellen stood in her blue doorway now, singing verse four, the one that named the Hollis family, all nine of them, living and dead, and her eyes were open and dry and fixed on the middle distance where the bell must have been hanging in whatever air it hung in now.

"Ellen." Mara touched her arm. It was warm. That somehow frightened her more than cold would have. "Ellen, love, what are you doing?"

Ellen's mouth kept moving. Then, between lines, without turning her head, she said in her own voice—her ordinary, flour-dusted, gossiping voice: "It's not my turn yet, Mara. You have to understand that. It's not my turn."

"Whose turn is it?"

"The hymn knows." Ellen smiled then, and it was Ellen's smile, the one that had comforted a feverish child thirty years ago, and Mara felt something in her chest crack sideways. "I'm only helping it along. We all are. It goes faster if we help. And if it goes faster, mine comes sooner, and being sooner is better than being—" She stopped. Her face worked, briefly, like a woman swallowing a sob, and then smoothed again into that terrible mildness. "You should sing too, Mara. It's kinder to know your place in it."

Mara walked away fast, and did not run, because running felt like admitting the lane itself was listening.

* * *

In the churchyard she found the congregation.

Not for a service—there was no service, no priest, no order of any kind. They had simply gathered, forty or fifty of the adults of Marrowfen, standing among the leaning stones in the last grey light, faces lifted toward the empty belfry where no bell had hung for a hundred and thirteen years. And they were trading names.

That was the only way Mara could describe it. She stood behind the lych-gate and listened while Dan Hollis sang a verse naming the Petch family, and Ellen Petch answered with a verse naming the Cranes, and Widow Crane—who had buried a husband and two sons and had nothing left to lose—sang a verse naming the Fenns, who were not even present, who were perhaps asleep in their beds three fields away while strangers sold them aloud into the dark. Each singer's voice grew stronger as the verse went on. Each face eased, at the end, like a debt forgiven. And each listener flinched—not at the sound, but at the content, the way people flinch when a doctor reads another patient's chart and theirs waits unopened in the pile.

They were buying time off each other. Verse by verse, neighbor by neighbor, they were spending one another.

And the bell rang on overhead, though there was nothing up there but rooks and rot, and every toll seemed to Mara to be a tick of some ledger being balanced somewhere below the fen.

"You see it too."

Vesper Sterling came to stand beside her at the lych-gate. He looked worse than he had a week ago—grey under the eyes, his coat damp with fen mist, a notebook clutched against his chest like a breastplate. The folklorist. The outsider. She had distrusted him from the first day, the way Marrowfen distrusted anything that arrived by the low road, and she was not ready to stop, but terror makes strange pews.

"They're singing each other," she said. "Dan Hollis just gave the whole Petch line. Ellen gave the Cranes. Half these people I've known since I was in nappies, and they're—" Her voice failed her, and she hated it. "There's no saving them, is there. That's what you've come to tell me."

Vesper was quiet a moment. Below them the hymn rolled on, verse after verse, the congregation swaying gently now, all together, like wheat.

"I think," he said carefully, "that the hymn doesn't need their willingness. I think it takes it because willingness makes the count sweeter. But the count happens either way." He looked at her, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who had read his own name in a ledger written before he was born. "The children aren't singing. Have you noticed? The adults sing and the children sleepwalk. Two different harvests."

"I've barred the schoolhouse doors," Mara said. "Chained them. I sleep there now, on the floor, with a poker. Last night they all sat up at once at midnight and hummed until dawn. In unison. Faces toward the well side of the room." She wrapped her arms around herself. "I counted them eleven times. Eleven times, Mr. Sterling. Seven children, seven children, seven children. My mother used to count doors. I used to laugh at her for it."

"My mother counted too," Vesper said quietly.

The bell tolled. Below, the congregation changed key—a new verse, lower, slower—and Mara heard the first syllables of it and went cold from the scalp down, because she knew this verse. Everyone in Marrowfen knew this verse. It was the oldest one, the one Edith Marsh had gone white as bone trying not to finish, the one that named a family that had left the fen a generation ago and taken its name with it.

*Sterling.*

The congregation sang it willingly, gladly, forty voices offering up a man standing right beside her at the lych-gate, and not one of them turned to look at him. That was the detail that broke something loose in Mara's chest: they didn't look. He was already spent to them. Already paid. A name passed hand to hand like coin, and here he stood, holding his little notebook, listening to himself being given away.

"We leave tomorrow night," she said. It came out flat and certain, and she realized she had decided it somewhere between Ellen's doorway and this gate, maybe years ago, maybe her whole life. "The children and me. Out of the hollow, up the drover's road, anywhere that isn't here. You can come or you can stay and be sung, Mr. Sterling, but I am not going to stand at this gate watching Marrowfen eat its young."

"The roads won't let us," Vesper said. "I tried the low road Tuesday. It loops. I ended up at the well."

"Then we find the road that doesn't loop."

"There isn't one." He turned to face her fully, and in the dark his face was nearly gone, only the pale smudge of it and the sound of the bell moving through him. "There's one way out of Marrowfen, Miss Quill, and it's straight down. Through the water. And someone has to sing the hymn backward from inside it to hold the door open long enough for seven children to pass."

Below them, the last verse of the night rose and fell, and the congregation sighed as one body, satisfied, fed, and began drifting homeward through the stones like smoke finding its way back down a chimney.

Mara looked at the empty belfry, at the dark above it where a sound lived without a bell, and understood at last that there would be no rescue coming up the low road, no bishop, no constable, no morning when the ringing stopped on its own. Marrowfen was not drowning. Marrowfen had already drowned, years ago, and everything she loved was the echo.

"Then you'd better teach me the words backward," she said, "because I am not letting you do it alone."

Vesper opened his mouth to answer—and the bell, for the first time in three days, stopped.

In the silence that followed, from the direction of the hollow, faint and clear across the black water, came the sound of seven children humming in perfect unison, wide awake, standing at the lip of the well.
