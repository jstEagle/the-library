## 6. Tomas Hears It Too

*From the notebook of Freya Nystrom, entry dated the fourteenth of October:*

*Tomas Lindqvist stayed behind after class today. He is eleven, thin as a birch sapling, with the habit of watching doors as though he expects something to come through them. He asked me—very quietly, as though the walls had ears, which after everything I have documented in this notebook I am no longer prepared to rule out—whether I had heard it too.*

*I said I had heard the bell.*

*He shook his head. He said that wasn't what he meant.*

*What follows is his account, written down by me as he spoke, because he would not say it twice and because I have learned that in Vessing the only things that survive are the things written down.*

---

Tomas knew the sound of his sister's voice the way he knew the smell of their mother's bread—not as information but as a fact of his body, something he would recognize in the dark, underwater, in the last minute of the world. Astrid had a laugh that started in her nose before it got to her mouth. She was nine when she went into the fog on the fourth of November, four years ago, and she did not come out of it, and the searchers walked the fields for three days with lanterns, and then they stopped walking, and then nobody said her name again, not at school, not at church, not at the table where her bowl had been, which their mother put away in the same week and never took out again.

That was the part Tomas could never make fit. Not that Astrid was gone. That Astrid was *unmentioned*. It was like she had been taken out of the world the way you take a stitch out of a hem—carefully, so the fabric closes behind it and you'd never know anything had been sewn there at all.

Except the bell knew.

He heard it first on the second dawn, when the ringing started and the whole village stood in their doorways in their nightclothes, looking at the empty tower with faces like people reading a letter they already knew by heart. His father had said, *Old sound carrying over from Dalby, maybe*, and his mother had said, *Yes, probably Dalby*, and they had both gone inside and made coffee and nothing about the morning had been different from any other morning, except that the air had been full of ringing and the tower had been full of nothing.

But Tomas had stood in the yard with his face tipped up, and inside the bell-sound—not under it, not behind it, *inside* it, the way a yolk is inside an egg—someone was laughing. A laugh that started in the nose.

He hadn't told anyone. Telling was dangerous. He didn't know why he knew it was dangerous, but he knew it the way the other children knew it, the way they all seemed to know things without being told, the way you know not to step on ice that sings. The adults in Vessing had a way of going smooth when you asked about certain things. His father's face went smooth. The headmaster's face went smooth, and Headmaster Vey's face was already smooth to begin with, like a stone in a river.

So Tomas carried it alone for three weeks, the laugh inside the ringing, and then the bell began to say other things.

Not words. Not exactly. Words would have been easier, maybe, or maybe worse. It was more like—Tomas struggled for it in Miss Nystrom's classroom on the afternoon he finally stayed behind, his hands flat on the desk, his voice down at the bottom of his chest—more like the way you can hear a tune and know the words that go with it without anyone singing them. The ringing would come at dawn and fold itself around the house, and inside the ringing Astrid would be *there*, not saying anything, just being there the way warmth is there near a stove, and once, only once, he had heard her say his name. *Tomas.* The way she said it when she wanted the last of the bread. Two syllables with a little lift at the end, a little hook, like she was fishing for him.

He had run out into the yard in his socks. Frost on the ground. His feet burning. And the bell had rung and rung and rung, and Astrid had been in it, and the tower had been empty, and his mother had called from the door, *Tomas, come in, you'll catch your death,* and her voice had been so ordinary that for a moment he had hated her.

That was the worst part, he told Miss Nystrom. Not the bell. The hating.

---

*From the notebook, continued:*

*I asked him how long he had known that his sister was in the bell. He said he didn't know she was in the bell. He knew she was in the sound. He was very precise about this. I have found the children here are precise in ways the adults refuse to be.*

*I asked him if the other children heard it too. He looked at the door before he answered. Then he said: Petra hears it best. Petra doesn't say anything anymore, but she writes it down. He said it the way you'd describe a fact of geography. Petra writes it down.*

*I asked where.*

*He wouldn't tell me. Not wouldn't—couldn't, I think. I watched him try. His mouth opened and his jaw worked and nothing came out, and then he went pale and said, "I'm not allowed to say where," in a voice that was not his own certainty but something older, something borrowed, and I did not press him. There is a time to press and a time to wait, and I am learning—slowly, at cost—which is which in this village.*

*What I pressed instead was this: I asked him why he told me.*

*He thought about it for a long time. Outside the window the fog was doing its slow work on the far fields, erasing the world one fence post at a time. And then he said, "Because you write everything down. I've seen your notebook. You wrote down the thirteenth desk."*

*I confessed that I had.*

*"Nobody writes things down here," he said. "That's the whole trick of it. Nobody writes anything down, and then it's like it never was. But you wrote down the desk."*

*He said it without accusation. He said it the way you'd say the sun came up. And then he said the sentence I have not been able to stop hearing since, the sentence I am writing here in this notebook so that it will have existed, so that someone, somewhere, someday, will have to account for it:*

*"If you write her name down, does that mean she's real again?"*

---

Tomas walked home the long way, along the field edge where the frost had come early and lain down on the stubble like a second harvest, white and strange. The fog was lifting off the low ground in ropes. He liked this hour. It was the only hour when Vessing looked like a place instead of a performance.

He had done it. He had actually done it. For three weeks the secret had sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, and now it was out, and Miss Nystrom had written it down in her small quick handwriting, and the stone was still in his chest but it had turned over, it had shifted, and there was room around it now to breathe.

She hadn't laughed. That was the main thing. She hadn't done the smooth face. When he said *Astrid*, she had written the name down without hesitating, the letters coming out of her pen like they'd been waiting in there all along, and when she'd finished she had looked at the name for a moment and then said, "Astrid Lindqvist," out loud, reading it back to him, and Tomas had felt something happen in his chest that he had no word for. It was like hearing a door open in an empty house. It was like the hem closing back over the stitch—no. It was the opposite of that. It was someone unpicking the hem and finding the hole where the stitch had been and saying: *yes, something was here. Something was taken.*

Nobody had said that in four years. Nobody had said anything at all.

The bell rang while he was still in the fields. It was later than its dawn ringing—this was the new thing, the bell learning new hours, the bell getting hungry, though he didn't let himself think that word, he put the word down a well inside himself and dropped the cover over it. The sound came across the fields and folded around him, and Tomas stood still in the frost and let it.

*Astrid.*

Not his name this time. Hers. He was almost sure. The sound bent and swelled and inside it, in the yolk of it, something turned toward him, the way a sleeper turns toward a lamp being carried down a corridor. He couldn't hear words. He never could, quite. But he could hear *attention*, and the attention had a shape, and the shape was his sister standing in a doorway in a dress with daisies on it, the one from the photograph that wasn't in the photograph album anymore, the one that was just—gone, one day, along with the school picture and the drawing on the fridge and the hospital bracelet their mother kept in a tin, all of it gone, all of it unstitched.

"Tomas!"

His mother's voice, thin across the field. He turned. She stood at the garden gate with her arms crossed hard against the cold, and her face was doing the thing, the smooth thing, and behind her the house windows were gold with lamplight and everything about the picture of it was warm and wrong.

"Coming!" he called back.

But he didn't come. Not yet. He turned back to the fields, to the sound still dying away over the stubble, and he did something he had never done. He said it out loud, into the fog, into the ringing's last silver thread:

"Astrid. I'm going to get you out."

The words fell into the fog and did not echo. But the cold on the back of his neck changed, just slightly, the way air changes when a door opens somewhere in a house, and Tomas Lindqvist stood alone in the white field with his heart going like a hammer and knew—he knew the way he knew the ice that sings—that she had heard him.

*From the notebook, final entry for the fourteenth of October:*

*I have made a decision, and I record it here so that it is a fact and not a mood: I believe the children.*

*This should not be a decision. It should be a reflex. But I have spent three weeks in a village where the adults perform normalcy with such discipline that I had begun to doubt the evidence of my own notebook, and so I will write it plainly: I believe the children. They hear what I hear. They have always heard it. They are not less attuned to this place than their parents—they are more attuned, because no one has yet taught them how not to listen.*

*Tomas Lindqvist's sister Astrid vanished on the fourth of November, four years ago. I know this because Tomas told me, because his memory is intact and precise, and because he is eleven years old and has not yet learned to surrender his own record. There is no trace of her in the school register. There is no trace of her in the parish rolls. When I checked the census extracts in the vestry archive—carefully, with gloves, with the door barred—there was no trace of her in the census. A child who lived in this village for nine years has been removed from every document in the district, and the only remaining record of her existence is the memory of her brother and the sound inside a bell that was melted down in 1911.*

*Eleven missing students. I have been counting. Marta told me to stop counting. I understand now that the counting itself may be the danger—that in Vessing, arithmetic is a form of trespass.*

*I am going to keep counting.*

*Tomorrow I intend to speak with Petra. Tomas says she writes things down. If a child in this village has been keeping a record longer than mine, then I need to see it, and I need to see it soon, because the bell has begun ringing at hours that are not dawn, and the frost on my window this morning did not form in ferns or feathers.*

*It formed in letters. Rows of them. Small, careful, childlike.*

*I could not read them before the sun took them. But they were there. Someone—or something—is practicing its handwriting on my window, and I find that I am no longer afraid in the way I was.*

*I am afraid in the way that means I am finally paying attention.*
