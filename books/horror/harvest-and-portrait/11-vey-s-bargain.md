## 11. Vey's Bargain

Aldous Vey had not slept in the schoolhouse since the year his son stopped sleeping anywhere at all.

He kept the habit anyway. Every evening he climbed the narrow stair behind the headmaster's office to the little room where a cot still stood made up with a grey wool blanket, and every evening he sat on the edge of it in the dark and listened to the village settle around him like an old dog turning in circles before lying down. Doors latching. Kettles ticking. And now—this autumn, this bell-season—the bell. The bell that was not there, ringing in a tower that held nothing but pigeons and cold air, ringing closer each night, the way a tide comes closer to a wall it has already decided to take.

He had learned, over thirty-four years as headmaster of Vessing School, to hear the difference between the sounds a village makes when it is honest and the sounds it makes when it is performing. Vessing performed beautifully. It had been performing since 1788, when his ancestor Henrik Vey signed his name beneath the founders' covenant and discovered, as all the Vey men discovered in their turn, that a signature is a kind of door, and that once you have opened one for something, you become the keeper of the doorway whether you wish it or not.

The ledger lived in the floor beneath his bed.

Not under it—beneath it, in a shallow cavity cut into the original floorboards, sealed with a plank that had been replaced so many times it was newer than the rest of the room, newer by a century of fathers prying it up and pressing it down again. Aldous's father had shown it to him on his fourteenth birthday, in this same room, with this same lamplight, and had said the words Aldous had never managed to forget: *We do not own it, Aldous. We only keep it fed.*

He was thinking of his father's hands—dry, careful, trembling only at the very end of his life—when the knock came at the schoolhouse door at half past ten at night.

He knew who it was before he opened it. He had known for three days, since the moment he'd watched Freya Nystrom come out of the vestry with her coat collar turned up and her notebook pressed against her ribs like a shield, and he had seen the particular way she walked afterward: not faster, but closer to the ground, the way people walk when the world has tilted and they are trying to keep their footing on a slope that was flat yesterday. She had read the ledger. Or part of it. Enough.

He opened the door.

She stood in the fog with her notebook under her arm and frost in her hair, though the frost had not fallen yet that night—the frost simply *found* her now, gathered on her the way it gathered on the classroom windows in the shapes of names. Behind her, down the lane, the church stood black against the blacker sky, and though no bell was visible in its tower, the air around it had that faintly struck quality, that hum below hearing, like a tuning fork held against a tooth.

"Headmaster," she said. "I need to ask you about 1963."

"Come in," Aldous said, "before the village sees us talking."

* * *

He made tea because his father had made tea, because his grandfather had made tea, because the ritual of the cup gave the hands something to do while the mouth learned what it was going to say. She sat across the desk from him in the visitor's chair—the one reserved for parents, for inspectors, for the rare traveling authority who came and signed forms and left without ever quite looking at the thirteenth desk—and he watched her eyes go, as every visitor's eyes went, to the shelf behind him where the school trophies stood, and then, briefly, to the floorboard beneath his chair.

"You keep it here," she said.

Not a question. He had known she was intelligent from her first interview; he had thought, in those early hopeful weeks of September, that intelligence would save her, that a mind like hers would find the shape of the thing and simply decline it, the way one declines an invitation. He had not understood then that the covenant did not accept declines. It accepted completion or refusal, and refusal was only another word for feeding.

"My family has kept it since 1788," he said. "Henrik Vey was the schoolmaster then, too. It seemed—appropriate. The ledger and the register in the same house. The names and the names."

"Show me."

"Miss Nystrom—"

"Freya." She set her notebook on the desk, and he saw that its pages had gone soft at the edges, swollen with damp and handling, filled with her small upright handwriting until the handwriting had begun to lean, to crowd, to press. "You've watched me for two months. You've watched me find the gaps in the register, and you said nothing. You watched me copy the Portrait Day vellum—I know you knew; you were standing at the window when I did it—and you said nothing. You watched me go into the vestry archive and come out the way I came out, and you said nothing. So either you are the most cowardly man in Vessing, or you've been waiting for me to come to you." Her voice did not rise. That was the worst of it. It came at him level and cold as well-water. "I'm here. Show me."

He got down on his knees on the floor of his own office, in front of a teacher half his age, and lifted the plank.

The ledger came up wrapped in oilcloth, and even through the cloth he felt the weight of it settle against his palms—not the weight of paper and leather, which was considerable enough, but the other weight, the one he had never found words for, the weight of a thing that knows it is being carried. The room grew colder by degrees, the way it always did, and the lamp flame stood up straight and still, as if listening.

He unwrapped it on the desk between them and turned to the page.

She read in silence. He watched her read: the founders' hands giving way to his ancestors' hands, the covenant restated in each generation's ink, the harvest terms restated, the promise of the land's abundance restated, and then the list. Eleven names. Eleven entries spanning a hundred and sixty years, each in a different hand, each with its date, each with its small terrible annotation in the margin—*given*, *taken*, *given*, *taken*—and then the twelfth line, blank, waiting, ruled and ruled and ruled again by generations of Veys who had refreshed the empty line with a straightedge so that it would never fade, never blur, never be forgotten.

"Your son," she said quietly.

Aldous's hands went flat on the desk. He had rehearsed this. He had rehearsed it for thirty-six years, in this room, on that cot, in the dark, and still his voice came out wrong—too high, too old.

"Daniel. Born 1971. He was eighteen in 1989. He worked the late harvest that year, and the bell had begun to ring in September, and I—" He stopped. Started again. "The ledger requires a keeper's judgment. That was the arrangement. The Vey who keeps the ledger chooses when the season is ripe, and presents the vellum, and the covenant takes what the vellum offers. My father chose in 1963. He chose Marta Lindqvist's class, and Marta's mother scratched her daughter's name from the vellum, and the covenant—" he swallowed— "the covenant took the mother instead. It was permitted. It was *legal*, if you understand me. The terms allow a substitution freely made. My father told me that was mercy. That the mother's gift kept the arithmetic whole and spared the child. He told me that on his deathbed, as if it were a comfort he was leaving me."

"And in 1989?"

"I hesitated." The word came out of him like a tooth. "The bell rang for nine nights and I could not choose. I told myself I was being careful. I told myself I was weighing. What I was doing was hiding from the desk in this room, hiding from the ledger, hiding from the pen. And on the tenth night the covenant stopped waiting for my judgment." He looked at the blank twelfth line. "It took Daniel from the harvest field. No vellum. No name written. They found his coat folded on the fence rail, and his boots beside it, laced. He'd have never laced his boots to take them off. He just—stepped out of them. And the ledger opened itself to this page that night, and the ink was still wet on the eleventh entry, and the annotation in the margin was not in my hand and not in any hand I've ever known."

Freya leaned close. He watched her read the marginal word beside the eleventh name, the word he had read ten thousand times and never once said aloud.

"*Hesitated*," she said.

"The covenant keeps its own records," Aldous said. "It has been keeping mine longer than I have."

* * *

The tea had gone cold in both cups. Outside, the fog had thickened until the window showed nothing but the lamp's small yellow room of light, and beyond it, faintly, the tower's hum had climbed into audibility—not ringing yet, not tonight, but the way a throat clears before speech.

Freya had not moved from the ledger. Her finger rested beside the twelfth line without touching it, the way one's finger rests beside a wound.

"Tell me what you're offering," she said. "You didn't ask me here at midnight to confess."

"No," Aldous said. "I asked you here because I am a coward, and I have decided, after thirty-six years, to be a coward *efficiently*."

He turned the ledger toward her fully, so that the blank line faced her like an open mouth.

"You've read the terms. You know the covenant is unfinished. Eleven portraits given across a hundred and sixty years, and the twelfth line empty, and every bell-season since 1989 the land has thinned a little more—the wheat comes up grey at the roots, the wells taste of iron, the orchard on the north slope died in a single summer. The village tells itself it's the soil, the weather, the times. The village will tell itself anything. But you've seen the ledger, and you know: the covenant is starving, because it was built to be completed, and we have starved it for thirty-six years." He drew a breath that hurt. "And a starving covenant does not wait politely for a keeper's judgment. It takes. It took my son. And this season it will take from your classroom, Miss Nystrom. You've seen it already—Petra who has stopped speaking, the frost writing names on your windows, the bell coming closer every night. It is choosing. It is choosing *for* me, because I am the keeper who hesitated, and my judgment is forfeit."

"Then give it what it wants," Freya said, and her voice cracked for the first time, just slightly, just at the seam. "Give it a name. That's what you came to say. That's your midnight offer."

"Yes." He made himself hold her eyes. "Yes. Write a name on the twelfth line. Any name. It must be given freely—that's the one condition, the covenant has never been able to take what was not offered—but it must be a true name, a whole name, a living soul offered with open hand. And when the twelfth line is filled, the portrait is complete. The covenant is satisfied. The bell falls silent. The collections end. Forever." He heard the words leave him and was astonished, as he always was, at how reasonable they sounded, how almost kind. That was the ledger's oldest trick, he thought. It made its arithmetic sound like mercy. "Write a name, Freya, and your children live. Write your own, and you die. Or—" and here his voice did the thing he had sworn it would not do, the thing thirty-six years of rehearsal had not been able to prevent, the thing that made him, in that moment, in his own ears, his father's son entirely— "or write *any other name in the world*, and live, and teach, and grow old, and never tell a soul, as I never told a soul, as my father never told a soul."

The silence that followed was the longest of his life.

Freya looked at the blank line for a long time. Then she looked up at him, and her face had gone very still, the stillness of deep water, and when she spoke her voice had found its level again.

"You want me to murder someone."

"I want you to *choose*," Aldous said. "That's all the covenant has ever asked. That's all it has ever been, underneath the Latin and the vellum and the harvest—a machine for making someone choose. My family chose for a hundred and sixty years and called it stewardship. The village voted in October and called it prudence. Marta's mother chose and called it love. Everyone chooses, Miss Nystrom, and everyone calls it something gentler than what it is." He closed the ledger with both hands, gently, the way one closes the eyes of the dead. "I am offering you the only honest version anyone has ever offered: here is the pen, here is the line, here is the price of your students' lives, and no one will ever know but you and me and the dead."

"And you?" she asked. "What do you get?"

He almost smiled. "Sleep," he said. "I get to stop hearing the bell come closer. I get one night, before I die, of a village that isn't starving. And I get—" his voice failed and he let it— "I get to stop being the man who hesitated. Whatever you write on that line, Freya, I will carry it. That's the keeper's part. That's the only part left to me."

She stood. She took her notebook from the desk and held it against her ribs again, that shield, and at the door she paused with her hand on the latch, and the fog breathed in around her like something inhaling.

"You said the covenant can't take what isn't offered," she said. "Freely given. A true name, a whole name, a living soul."

"Yes."

"Then you should know," Freya Nystrom said, "that I spent last night at Marta's mother's grave. And I know what a scratched-out name is worth. And I know that your Daniel's coat was folded on that fence rail—which means he wasn't taken, Aldous. He was *collected*. There's a difference. And the difference is going to save us all."

The door closed behind her. The lamp flame bent toward it, as if following.

And down in the floor, beneath the plank Aldous had not yet replaced, the ledger lay open to the twelfth line—open, though he had closed it—waiting, patient, hungry, while somewhere out in the fog the bell that was not there began, very softly, to ring.
