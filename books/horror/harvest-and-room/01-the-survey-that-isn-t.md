## 1. The Survey That Isn't

The gravel road announced the house a full quarter mile before I saw it, my rental car's tires crunching out a rhythm like something counting. I know that's not what it was. I know that. But I've spent eleven years documenting abandoned places, and you develop a certain ear for rhythm — for the way spaces breathe when nobody's in them. The gravel counted, and then the house arrived at the end of the drive like a period at the end of a sentence I hadn't finished reading.

The Nystrom farmhouse sat where it had always sat, according to the photographs my mother kept in a shoebox: two stories, white clapboard gone gray at the weatherward corners, a porch that wrapped around the east side like an arm held out too long. It looked smaller than memory. They always do. What it didn't look like was dead. Abandoned houses have a particular slouch, a surrender in the roofline, and this one stood straight. The gutters were clear. Someone — something — had been maintaining it.

I parked behind the rusted Ford pickup still registered to a woman who'd been dead nine days, and I did what I always do. I photographed the approach.

This is the ritual, if I'm honest about it, and I try to be honest about it the way I'm honest about everything else: exhaustively, clinically, with timestamps. Urban explorers get romanticized as thrill-seekers, but the ones I respect are archivists. We photograph because a photograph is a fact. A fact can be filed, compared, cross-referenced. A fact doesn't shift when you look away from it. My camera has been my exoskeleton since I was nineteen and crawled through my first derelict textile mill, and I have never once entered a structure without documenting its exterior first. Sequence matters. Establish the baseline before you disturb the scene.

So: forty-one frames of the farmhouse from every cardinal direction. The porch boards, sound. The windows, unbroken, though three of them on the second floor had been painted over from the inside — a detail I noted and filed under *eccentric* without examining why an eighty-year-old woman would blind herself to her own view. The yard itself was mowed to within a week of the present, which meant either a neighbor with a sense of duty or a woman cutting grass she could barely walk across. The probate attorney had said Edith Nystrom died in her sleep, alone, no live-in care. The lawn said otherwise. I filed that too.

* * *

Inside smelled of lemon oil and dust in equal measure, which is to say it smelled maintained and unlived-in simultaneously, a contradiction I couldn't resolve and so photographed instead. The entryway. The staircase with its runner worn to the weave down the center. The kitchen, where a single coffee cup sat upside down on a drying rack, dry as bone, as though it had been washed and never used again — placed there deliberately, the way you'd set a table for someone expected.

I worked room by room, methodical, narrating aloud into my recorder because the house was quiet enough that my own voice seemed like a reasonable thing to add to it. "Kitchen, 10:42 a.m., northeast corner, water damage absent, plaster intact." The kind of litany that keeps a person company.

It was in the third bedroom — the one that must have been mine, once, during the summers my mother shipped me here while she sorted out whatever needed sorting — that I found the county plat map. Framed, actually. Hung at eye level beside the door like a certificate, which is exactly how my grandmother would have treated it. Edith Nystrom believed in documents. Deeds, surveys, titles, the whole paper skeleton of ownership. She'd told me once, when I was maybe ten and asked why she kept everything, that papers were how you proved a thing was yours and not the other way around.

The plat map was a 1987 survey, official seal, surveyor's signature in faded blue ink. And there was the farmhouse, drawn in its precise footprint, the barn, the well, the septic field rendered in careful hatching.

There was no cellar.

Not a small cellar. Not an omitted accessory structure. The map showed the foundation outline complete and closed, a rectangle with nothing beneath it but the notation *SLAB, GRADE* in the surveyor's tidy hand. According to Larch County, the house sat directly on the earth like any other building, hollow only above the floorboards.

I stood there holding my camera against my chest and felt the specific pleasure of a discrepancy. This is the part of the work I love, the part I've never admitted to anyone because it sounds wrong: the moment when the record and the reality refuse each other. Every explorer knows it. You find the bricked-up corridor that isn't on the blueprints. You find the thirteenth floor in a building whose elevator panel skips from twelve to fourteen. The world is full of rooms that paperwork denies, and finding one is like catching the world in a lie — briefly, thrillingly, you know something it doesn't want known.

Except this wasn't a derelict hospital in another state. This was my grandmother's house, and my grandmother had hung the lie on her wall where she could see it every day.

I went downstairs looking for the cellar stairs, telling myself it was curiosity, professional thoroughness, the same detachment I'd bring to any site. I knew even then that was a story I was telling myself, the way you tell yourself the noise upstairs is just the furnace. The truth was simpler and less comfortable: the map had said *no*, and something in me needed to hear the house say otherwise.

* * *

The cellar door was behind the pantry, which is a strange place for a cellar door, and it was locked, which is stranger still. Cellar doors don't lock from the outside in farmhouses; cellars hold preserves and potatoes and the kind of junk families accumulate and forget. This door was oak, older than the rest of the interior trim, fitted with a hasp and a padlock that looked newer than the door — brass, oiled, the keyhole clean. Maintained. Everything about this house was maintained, and I was beginning to understand that maintenance is a form of attention, and attention is a form of devotion, and I didn't yet want to ask devoted to what.

I knelt and put my eye to the gap beneath the door. Dark, obviously. But cold air moved against my face, steady, directional, like breath through slightly parted lips. Cellars breathe; temperature differentials make air move; I wrote all of this into my recorder in my flattest documentary voice, and my voice sounded fine, and my hands were not fine, and I documented neither.

Then I photographed the door. Fourteen frames. The padlock got its own close-up, and in the viewfinder I noticed the brass had been polished so often the maker's stamp was worn soft, and I thought: *nine days*. Nine days since she died, and the padlock on a sealed cellar door gleamed like it had been tended yesterday. Probate takes weeks. Nobody had keys but her. Unless the tending didn't require hands.

I stood up too fast and hit my head lightly on a shelf edge, and the jars on it rattled, and the sound went down through the floorboards and kept going, longer than jars should rattle, a low sympathetic hum from somewhere below that lasted maybe four seconds after everything else had gone still.

I recorded that too. On playback, the hum was there, faint, tonal, patient. Then, underneath it, so quiet I had to scrub the audio three times with headphones on, something else. Not a voice. A rhythm. Like tapping. Like counting.

I deleted nothing. I never delete anything. But I did stand in that pantry for a long moment with the camera hanging from its strap against my sternum, feeling its little weight like a heartbeat, and I made myself say out loud, in my professional voice, the sentence I'd been avoiding:

"The plat map shows no cellar."

The house said nothing back. Houses don't. That's the deal we have with them, the arrangement that lets us sleep in them: they keep our secrets in their walls, and we pretend their silences are empty.

Outside, the light had gone the color of old paper, and I realized I'd been inside for nearly three hours. I had four hundred and six photographs. I had a discrepancy between the county's earth and the actual earth. I had a locked door breathing cold air in a house that was supposed to be grieving.

I texted my sister Mara, because she was in the will too and because some things you don't carry alone even when you've gotten good at carrying things. *Here. House is intact. Found something odd.* Three dots appeared, vanished, appeared. Then: *Odd how?*

I looked at the pantry door. I took one more photograph — frame four hundred and seven, the last of the day — and it was only later, much later, reviewing the files by lamplight in the kitchen, that I saw what the frame held that my eye had missed: in the narrow gap beneath the cellar door, at the very bottom of the image, the darkness there wasn't flat black the way unlit space photographs. It had texture. Depth. The faint suggestion of a shape, standing very still, the way a person stands when they're listening to decide whether you've noticed them yet.

I zoomed in until the pixels broke apart and became meaningless, which is what pixels do when you ask them to prove something impossible.

"Odd," I typed back to Mara, and watched the cursor blink in time with something below the floor, "like the house is keeping a count."
