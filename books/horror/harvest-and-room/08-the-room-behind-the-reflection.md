## 8. The Room Behind the Reflection

The lag reached three seconds on a Tuesday, and Orsen knew it the way you know a tooth has gone bad — not by looking, but by the way the whole mouth of the house had changed around it.

They'd been timing the mirror every morning since the footage came back clean. That was the ritual now. Coffee first, then the bathroom, phone propped against the tile where the toothbrush cup used to sit, stopwatch app open, and Orsen standing in front of the glass doing the same small sequence they'd designed to be unambiguous: raise the right hand, touch the nose, turn the head left, say a word — any word, out loud, the date, usually, so the recording would timestamp itself.

*October fourteenth.*

The Orsen in the mirror said it three seconds late.

Orsen had started keeping a chart, because charts were the last language the world still spoke to them. A spiral notebook, one line per day, the lag written in the margin like a fever reading. One second for the first week. Two seconds by the second Sunday. And now, three, and the number sat on the page looking less like a measurement than a promise.

The house had counted another soul. Orsen didn't know whose. That was the part that made their hands shake as they poured the coffee — not the lag itself, which had become almost familiar, but the arithmetic underneath it. The lag grew by one second for each name the house took. Somewhere between two and three, between Sunday and Tuesday, someone had been *counted*. A stranger. A neighbor. Mr. Halvorsen down the road, maybe, with his careful old-man gait and his way of not looking at the farmhouse when he drove past.

Orsen had thought about walking down to Halvorsen's place and asking. They had not done it. Some questions you don't ask until you're ready to hear the answer, and Orsen was still at the stage of grief and dread where readiness was a country they could see from the border but hadn't entered.

Instead they did what they always did. They documented.

The morning light came through the kitchen window the way it had every morning since Orsen arrived — thin, gray, the light of a place that kept its weather close. Orsen photographed the kitchen. Photographed the hallway. Photographed the pantry door with its seam of newer mortar, and the hallway mirror at the end of it, the tall one in the dark walnut frame that grandmother had never let anyone move, according to the neighbor kids Orsen had interviewed on the drive in — no, that wasn't right, there had been no neighbor kids, there had been only the county records and the silence and the plat map with its blank square where the cellar should be.

Orsen was losing track of which memories were real. That was new. That was Tuesday's other discovery, though they didn't write it in the chart.

They stood at the end of the hallway with the camera raised and looked down the length of it — the runner rug, the doors, the mirror at the far end holding a smaller, dimmer hallway inside itself like a throat — and they took the photograph because taking the photograph was the prayer, the proof, the *I was here and it was like this*.

The shutter clicked.

And in the mirror, three seconds behind, the Orsen who lived in the glass raised their camera and clicked it too.

* * *

By afternoon Orsen had stopped photographing the house and started photographing the mirror, which was a different thing entirely, and they knew it was a different thing because their hands behaved differently. Photographing the house was habit. Photographing the mirror was surveillance. You surveil an adversary.

They set up the tripod in the hallway, framed the tall mirror dead center, and let the camera run while they sat on the stairs with the notebook and watched the little screen.

Here is what the screen showed, for the first hour: a hallway. A mirror. An Orsen on the stairs, occasionally turning a page. Nothing else. The lag didn't read on video — it never had, not since that first night in the bathroom when Orsen had filmed the one-second delay and played it back and found, frame by frame, a woman moving in perfect sync with herself, no gap, no ghost, no wrongness at all. The house did not permit itself to be caught. Whatever the mirror was doing, it did it only for eyes. Cameras saw a normal hallway. Orsen saw a wound.

But Orsen kept recording anyway, because there was a second reason, and the second reason was this: the lag was growing. One second, two, three. And at some point — Orsen had done the math at three a.m., the bad math of the sleepless — at some point the delay would be long enough that the reflected Orsen would be doing something *else*. Not a delayed copy. A divergent one. If the mirror was a window into a room that ran a beat behind, then every second of lag was another beat of that room living its own life, making its own small choices, drifting further from the original like a boat cut loose.

At one second, the reflection was an echo.

At three seconds, it was starting to be a stranger.

At ten — at the ten seconds the ledger's arithmetic seemed to be walking toward, 144 names counting down, each one a second purchased — at ten seconds, the person in the glass would have a full ten seconds of independent existence, every moment of it, and what would that person do with ten seconds? What had they *already* done, in the stretches when Orsen wasn't standing there watching?

Orsen looked up from the notebook.

On the little screen, the hallway was the same. On the actual hallway, the mirror held the same. But Orsen had the sudden, physical certainty — the kind that arrives in the sternum before the mind can file an objection — that the mirror was not showing the hallway at all. That it hadn't been, for a while. That it was showing a *recording* of the hallway, or a memory of one, or a room so committed to impersonating the hallway that the seams only showed when you knew where to press.

They got up. Walked down the runner. Stood in front of the glass.

The Orsen in the mirror arrived three seconds later and stood in front of their own glass, in their own hallway, and for a moment the two of them regarded each other across the lag like strangers at a train window.

*October fourteenth,* Orsen said.

The reflection's mouth moved. Three seconds. Orsen watched the words arrive on the other face like a letter from somewhere far away.

Then the reflection did something Orsen did not do.

It turned its head. Not toward the stairs. Toward the *pantry* — toward the sealed arch behind the brick, the one the camera had heard breathing through — and it held there, head turned, waiting, as if listening. Orsen's real body stood facing forward. Orsen's real head did not turn. There was no sound in the hallway except the refrigerator's hum from the kitchen, and underneath it, so faint it might have been the hum itself, that rhythm from behind the pantry wall. In. Out. Patient as tide.

The reflection turned back. Looked at Orsen. And — Orsen would spend the rest of the night failing to talk themselves out of this — the reflection *nodded*. Once. Slowly. The way you nod at someone you've been waiting for.

Then it dissolved into ordinary sync, three seconds behind, doing what Orsen did, and the hallway was just a hallway, and the mirror was just a mirror, and Orsen's heart was going like a fist on a door.

* * *

They did not sleep. They told themselves they were working.

The estate boxes were still stacked in the front parlor, grandmother's whole life in cardboard, and Orsen had been through the ledger and the Bible and the letters twice, but going through them a third time was better than being alone with the hallway, so they went through them a third time, and this time they noticed the photographs.

There were more of them than Orsen remembered. That was the first wrong thing. A shoebox of snapshots, farmhouse and field and family, and Orsen had catalogued forty-one of them in the first week — they had the catalogue, they had *inventoried the photographs*, documentation was the whole religion — and now there were fifty-three. Twelve photographs had been added to a sealed box in a locked house where Orsen lived alone.

They laid them out on the parlor floor in the lamplight, all fifty-three, hands steady in the way hands go steady when they've gone past shaking into something colder.

Twelve photographs of the hallway mirror.

Orsen had never taken them. Orsen had never *seen* them. They were old — the color palette of the seventies, the orange-shifted decay of old Kodachrome — and in each one the tall walnut-framed mirror stood at the end of the hallway, exactly where it stood now, reflecting the hallway back.

Except in each one, the hallway it reflected was *arranged differently*.

In the first, the runner rug was gone and the floorboards showed, worn pale down the middle as if something heavy had been dragged along them for years. In the second, the doors were wrong — six doors on the reflected side where the real hallway had four, the extra two narrow and windowless, painted a green Orsen had never seen in the house. In the third, the hallway in the glass was longer. Much longer. It receded past the point where the exterior wall should have stopped it, past the point where *anything* should have stopped it, door after door after door fading into a brown dark that the flash couldn't reach.

Orsen sat back on their heels.

The photographs were in date order. Grandmother had been an orderly woman; the ledger proved that, the Bible marginalia proved that, the whole terrible bookkeeping of the family proved that. The mirror photographs ran from 1968 to 1991, and across those twenty-three years the reflected hallway changed the way a face changes across a photo album — gradually, then suddenly. The rug vanished. The extra doors appeared. And in the last photograph, dated seven months before grandmother's death, the hallway in the mirror was not a hallway at all anymore.

It was a room.

A wide, low room, brick-walled, and the camera flash reached only partway into it, and at the edge of the flash's reach stood furniture — a table, a chair, shapes that might have been shelves — arranged with a terrible domesticity, like a parlor, like a *home*, built down where no survey had ever found a cellar. And on the near wall of the reflected room, catching the flash at an angle, there was writing. Rows of it. Columns. Too small and too blurred to read, but Orsen didn't need to read it. Orsen had seen the ledger. Orsen had seen the names in grandmother's handwriting, each one with its number, the count running down from 144 like a fuse.

The walls in the photograph were covered in names.

Orsen looked up from the floor of the parlor and down the hall at the mirror, and the mirror looked back, three seconds behind, innocent as glass, and Orsen understood — with the flat, unhurried certainty of a person reading their own diagnosis — that the mirror had never been showing them this hallway. It was showing them *that* room. It had always been showing them that room. The lag wasn't a delay.

It was the time it took the light to come back from somewhere the survey maps had never been.

* * *

Night came down over the fields like a lid.

Orsen left the parlor lights on and the hallway lights on and every light the old wiring would hold without tripping, and they told themselves they were not afraid of the dark, they were afraid of what the dark was *for*, and that the distinction mattered, and it did not matter at all.

They ended up — this was the part they would not write in the notebook, not in any notebook — standing in front of the mirror at two in the morning, on purpose, on *purpose*, because the divergent reflection had nodded at them and the photographs had shown them the room, and there is a kind of fear that curdles into appetite if you leave it sitting long enough. Urban explorers knew this. Orsen had built a whole life on this: the locked thing, the sealed thing, the sign that says DO NOT ENTER, and the small clear voice underneath that says *then it is real, then it is true, then it will finally be documented.*

The hallway was dark except for the bulb at the far end. The mirror held the hallway, and the hallway held Orsen, and the lag ran under it all like a current under ice.

*Show me,* Orsen said. Their voice sounded wrong in the dark — too flat, no echo, as if the hallway had already been soundproofed for something. *You've been showing me all week. Show me the rest.*

For three seconds, nothing. Orsen counted the nothing. It was the longest three seconds of their life, and they counted every part of it, and then—

The reflection moved.

Not the delayed copy. Not the echo. The reflection *moved first* — Orsen stood still and the figure in the glass turned away, turned its back, and walked. Down the reflected hallway, away from the glass, and the reflected hallway *received* it, the runner rug sliding away beneath its feet like something making room, and the doors it passed were the wrong doors, six of them, narrow and green, and the hallway in the mirror went on further than the hallway in the house had any right to go, and the figure walked down it without hurrying, the way you walk through your own house, the way you walk somewhere you have walked ten thousand times.

Orsen's breath fogged the glass. It was cold in the hallway. It had been getting cold all week and Orsen had blamed the furnace, the old furnace, the dead furnace, and now the cold had a direction and the direction was *out of the mirror*, a thin draft flowing from the glass like the house behind it was breathing out.

The figure stopped, far down the reflected hall, at a place where the real hallway had a window and the reflected hallway had none. It turned around. Even at that distance, even in the murk, Orsen knew the shape of the shoulders. The way the head tilted. The cardigan — God, the cardigan, the gray cardigan with the bone buttons that Orsen had buried their face in at the funeral while the pastor said words about rest.

*Grandmother?*

The figure in the mirror did not answer. It walked to the far end of the reflected hall — a door appeared there, or had always been there, a door Orsen's hallway did not have and could not have, a door that opened onto brick and lamplight and the low wide room from the photographs — and it stepped through, and before it went it did one thing more.

It held the door.

Held it open. Stood in the doorway of the room behind the reflection, one hand on the frame, and looked back down the long wrong hallway at Orsen standing at the mouth of it, and waited.

Not beckoning. Grandmother had never beckoned anyone in her life; she believed want was a weakness you kept private. She *waited*, the way she'd waited at the head of the table, the way she'd waited in the church pew, the way she had apparently waited for forty years in a room under her own house with a ledger grown into the wall — waited with the door held open, with the patience of a woman who had made a bargain and knew that bargains keep their own schedule, and who had left the door open for the one person in the family who could not, in the end, resist a sealed thing.

The lag was three seconds. The lag was growing. The count was coming due, and down at the end of the reflected hallway, in a chair Orsen could just see past the held-open door, something else was sitting — something that had been sitting there all along, in grandmother's place, keeping grandmother's seat warm — and it did not turn around, and it did not need to.

The mirror began, very softly, to fog from the inside.

Orsen's phone buzzed in their pocket. They flinched hard enough to crack an elbow against the wall, and stood there in the cold breathing, and looked at the screen.

**Mara.**

A text, the first in eleven months. No greeting, no preamble, just the kind of message you send when you've decided not to decide anymore:

*Tell me what's in that house, Orsen. June drew a picture today. It's a hallway with too many doors. She's never seen your house. I'm coming tomorrow. Don't argue.*

Orsen looked up.

The mirror was clear again. The hallway in it was just a hallway, four doors, one rug, one dim bulb. The far end held nothing but the ordinary dark of an ordinary dead-end hall.

But the door at the end of the *reflected* hallway — the one grandmother had held open — had not closed. Orsen could not see it anymore, could not see anything past the glass's new innocence, but they could feel it the way you feel a held breath: somewhere behind the reflection, a door was standing open, and something in a gray cardigan was waiting on one side of it, and something else was sitting in a chair on the other, and the count was one second away from four.

Orsen typed back with cold-stiff thumbs: *Come. Don't bring June. Don't let her near any mirror. I'll explain everything.*

They hit send, and in the glass, three seconds late, the Orsen in the mirror lowered their phone — and smiled.

Orsen had not smiled.
