## 11. Names in the Plaster

The tunnel breathed.

That was the only word for it. The air moved in long, slow cycles — out through the dark ahead of me, back against my face a few seconds later — as if the whole passage were a throat and I had been swallowed without the courtesy of being chewed. My headlamp cut a cone through dust that hung suspended, never settling, never drifting, just hanging there like it was waiting for permission to fall.

I had been walking for twenty minutes. Maybe forty. Time behaved strangely down here, stretching and pooling the way water does in a cracked dish. I'd marked my route with chalk arrows every fifty feet, an old urban-exploration habit, and when I turned around to check the last one I found it exactly where I'd left it — but the arrow pointed the wrong way. Not reversed. Rotated. Pointing deeper.

I hadn't touched it.

"Okay," I said aloud, because saying things aloud is what you do when the alternative is listening. "Okay."

The walls changed as I went. Near the arch behind me they'd been fieldstone, mortared with that same dense gray stuff that sealed the pantry door from the inside. Then brick. Then something older, something that wasn't stone at all but compacted earth, ribbed and smooth in places like the inside of a shell. And now, plaster. Actual plaster over lath, whitewashed once and gone yellow with age, running along both sides of the passage in panels separated by wooden strips — the way you'd finish a room. A hallway. Someone had built this stretch to be *interior*, which meant someone had expected people to stand here and look at the walls.

I looked at the walls.

The first name was small, scratched into the plaster with something sharp — a nail, a knife point, a key. **HALVORSEN, SIGRID**, and beneath it a date: 1911. A single line had been drawn through it, hard enough to gouge the lath underneath.

I stood there for a long moment doing nothing at all, because my body had decided that moving forward was a decision it wasn't authorized to make.

Then I raised my camera and photographed it, because that's who I am. That's who I've always been. The world goes wrong and I put it in a frame, and the frame makes it a thing I observed instead of a thing that happened to me. It has worked since I was nine years old and my father left, since I catalogued every item he took from the house in a spiral notebook so I could prove later that it had really happened. Documentation as a life raft. Click, and the horror becomes evidence. Evidence can be handled. Evidence can be filed.

The flash lit the panel white, then let it fall back into lamp-yellow.

There were more. Dozens more. I walked and they kept coming, one after another, spaced at intervals too regular to be accidental — arm's reach apart, roughly chest height, all of them scratched by different hands in different centuries of handwriting. Copperplate script beside a child's wobbling capitals beside something runic and old enough that the plaster around it had been patched twice.

**NYSTROM, ANDERS** — 1888 — scored through.

**NYSTROM, GRETA** — 1902 — scored through.

**LINDQVIST, JOHAN** — no Nystrom blood in that one, and the date matched nothing I knew — scored through.

**NYSTROM, ELSIE** — 1931 — scored through, though whoever scored it had pressed so hard the plaster had buckled outward around the line, like the wall flinched.

I stopped photographing every one somewhere past the twentieth. Not because there were too many. Because I recognized the pattern, and recognition is its own kind of horror — the specific kind where you understand something before you're willing to say it.

These were ledger entries. These were the ledger, written where the ledger lived. Every death in Grandmother's book, transcribed onto the body of the house itself, and each one struck through when the debt came due. Paid. Collected. Harvested.

The house didn't keep records. The house *was* the record.

* * *

I found the children's section about thirty feet further on, and I would give anything to unfind it.

The names were smaller there. Lower on the wall, at the height where a kneeling adult or a standing child would write. Some were scratched clumsily, letters backwards, the work of hands still learning their alphabet. Others were careful, lovingly formed, the way a parent writes a name when the parent is the one holding the tool.

**HALVORSEN, TOBIAS** — 1974 — scored through.

**HALVORSEN, ANNIKA** — 1974 — scored through.

Two lines, same year, same hand. Mr. Halvorsen's brother and sister, if his story held. Lost to a lagging mirror the summer I wasn't yet born, in a county two hours south of here, in a family that had nothing to do with mine except proximity and knowledge. He'd told us on his porch with his coffee going cold: *Your people were the keepers of the room.* I'd thought he meant custodians. Caretakers. People who swept the floors and kept the secret.

Standing in front of Tobias and Annika Halvorsen, struck through in a hand that shook, I understood that *keeper* meant something closer to *warden*. Or *paymaster*. Someone who delivered the count.

And some of these names weren't Nystroms at all. I counted as I walked — eleven Lindqvists, four Sandbergs, a Voss, a pair of Brandts, a Kowalczyk, names that scattered across decades like dropped coins. Strangers. Neighbors. Passers-through. People whose only crime had been living within whatever radius the house considered its hunting ground.

Grandmother's Bible marginalia had laid out the bargain plainly enough: prosperity traded for deaths on schedule, the contract renewed each generation by a keeper. I'd read it three times in the kitchen with Mara pacing behind me, and I'd understood it the way you understand weather — abstractly, at a distance, as a system.

The wall made it flesh. The wall had handwriting.

* * *

Somewhere in the walking I realized I was no longer afraid of the breathing.

Not because it had stopped. Because it had synchronized. Somewhere in the last hundred yards my own respiration had locked step with the tunnel's slow tidal cycle, inhale when it exhaled, and now we moved together, the passage and me, like two lungs in one chest. I noticed it the way you notice your own heartbeat — suddenly, and then unable to stop noticing — and I made myself break rhythm, breathe fast, shallow, wrong. The tunnel waited patiently for me to tire. Then it resumed. We synced again within a minute.

It wasn't hostile. That was the worst part. Hostility implies opposition, a contest of wills. This was accommodation. The house adjusted itself around me the way water adjusts around a stone, because the stone belongs at the bottom.

The plaster panels gave way to bare earth again, then to something even older — and I almost walked past the last section because it wasn't plaster at all. It was clay, packed hard and slick, and the names there weren't scratched. They were pressed in, thumb-deep, while the clay was wet. Whoever wrote them had done it before the clay set. Before the tunnel finished becoming a tunnel.

**NYSTROM, INGEBORG** — no date — unscored.

Ingeborg. The first name in Grandmother's ledger. The first entry, number 144, the top of the countdown. She'd died in 1867, according to a family Bible older than the county itself, and her name sat in the wet-pressed clay with no line through it, and I felt my stomach drop through the floor of the world, because I understood.

She was the first payment. But she was also the signature. The one who made the bargain. Her name couldn't be scored through because her name wasn't a debt — it was the *terms*. She'd pressed it into the wet clay herself, or something had pressed it for her, and everything after it — every Halvorsen child, every drifter, every Nystrom collected across a century and a half — had flowed from that one impression of five syllables in river mud.

I put my fingers near the letters. Not touching. Close enough to feel the cold coming off them, a cold that had no business surviving a hundred and fifty years in sealed earth.

"Don't," I said to myself, and pulled my hand back.

* * *

The final panel was plaster again, newer, whiter, and I knew whose hand I'd find there before my lamp found it.

I'd know that handwriting anywhere. I'd seen it on birthday cards for thirty years, on recipe cards, on the margins of the Bible, on the ledger itself — that upright, unhurried cursive, the penmanship of a woman who learned to write with a nib and never saw a reason to change.

**ASTRID NYSTROM.**

No date.

No line through it.

I stood in front of my grandmother's name for a long time. Long enough that the tunnel and I fell into sync again, breath for breath, and this time I let it happen because I needed something steady and it was the steadiest thing available.

Every other name on these walls carried its wound. Scored through, gouged, buckled, sometimes scratched so violently the writer had torn the plaster skin off the lath beneath. Payment left marks. Collection left scars. One hundred and forty-three entries in her ledger, and I had passed most of them, and every single one bore its strike like a healed fracture.

Astrid Nystrom's name was pristine.

She never paid.

The understanding arrived all at once, the way floodwater arrives — not gradually but *suddenly*, having traveled a long way in the dark. She never paid because she never intended to pay. Forty years she'd kept the books. Forty years she'd lived in that farmhouse with its fat soil and its never-failing well and its uncanny luck — the hailstorm that missed her fields, the bank error in her favor, the years the crops sold high while neighbors struggled. Prosperity, on schedule, uninterrupted. And the count coming due, entry after entry, deadline after deadline.

And Astrid Nystrom had done what our family does best.

She'd deferred.

Substitutes. That was the shape of it, though I wouldn't have the letter to confirm it until later — drifters, the missing, strangers funneled toward the house by a keeper's quiet arrangement, fed into the count like counterfeit bills passed to a patient creditor. The Lindqvists. The Voss. The Kowalczyk. Names with no blood relation, scattered through the ledger like seeds sown in someone else's field. She'd spent forty years paying the house with other people's children.

My grandmother, who smelled of cardamom and let me steer her truck on the gravel drive. Who taught me to document everything because "memory lies, Orsen, but paper doesn't." Who I loved without reservation and grieved without complication.

Who had murdered her way to a comfortable old age and then died owing.

The camera was up before I decided to raise it. Flash. The name burned white and settled back into the lamplight, unscored, immaculate, patient. I photographed it from three angles. I filmed ten seconds of footage. I did all the rituals, all the little ceremonies of control, and none of it helped, because you cannot file betrayal. There's no sleeve in the archive box for *the person you loved was a monster and the monster is your inheritance.*

"She cheated," I said. My voice went down the tunnel and did not echo. The tunnel ate it. "You know she cheated. That's why the lag is growing. That's why the count is accelerating. You're not collecting a debt. You're collecting *arrears*."

The breathing paused.

For the first time since the pantry door, the tunnel went silent — truly silent, no tidal cycle, no patient respiration — and the silence lasted three seconds, four, five, and I understood that I had said something true, and that truth was interesting to it.

Then the breathing resumed, slower now. Deeper. Satisfied, almost. The way a creditor breathes when the debtor finally demonstrates he understands the size of what he owes.

Interest. Restitution with interest. The count wasn't coming due — it had come due decades ago and been strung along, and now the house wanted everything at once, principal plus penalty, and the penalty was measured in seconds of lag and souls of Nystrom blood.

Mara. June.

I turned and ran.

* * *

I made it maybe sixty feet before the chalk arrows stopped making sense.

The first wrong arrow I'd noted on the way in had rotated. Now the next one had vanished entirely, its patch of wall smooth and unmarked, and the one after that pointed sideways into solid earth. I skidded to a stop, swung the lamp in a full circle, and got nothing but tunnel in every direction — the passage had become a loop, or had always been one, or had rearranged itself while I read the dead.

Behind me, far back in the dark, something scraped. Once. Deliberately. The sound of a fingernail — or a nail, or a knife point, or a key — beginning a fresh inscription in fresh plaster.

I didn't look. Looking costs time and time was the currency the house traded in; every second of lag was a soul, Halvorsen had said, or the ledger had implied it, or I'd dreamed it, and I could no longer reliably separate what I'd learned from what the house had shown me. I ran the other way, any other way, lamp beam juddering over names I'd already catalogued, Halvorsen children and stranger-children streaming past in the periphery like a congregation turning to watch me flee.

The tunnel opened. That was the mercy — sudden, unearned, a junction where the passage split into three branches, and the air from the left-hand branch moved differently. Cooler. Wetter. Carrying, faintly, impossibly, the smell of the farmhouse kitchen. Cardamom. Coffee. Yesterday.

I took the left branch because grief is a compass too.

Twenty steps in, the walls changed again — new plaster, bright, barely aged — and the names here were fewer, fresher, and I made the mistake of reading them.

**Nystrom, M.**

Unscored.

And below it, in letters smaller than any others on any wall in this whole buried archive, pressed shallow and uncertain, the way a child writes when someone is guiding the hand:

**June.**

No date. No line. Just the name, sitting in the plaster like a seed in soil, waiting for its season.

The tunnel breathed out against the back of my neck, warm now, almost gentle, and somewhere above me — through however many feet of earth and foundation and floorboard — I heard a child's voice, thin and clear, counting.

"One... two... three..."

Mara's voice answered it, tight with forced brightness: "Sweetheart, who are you talking to?"

"No one," June said. "Four... five..."

I ran toward the sound of my niece counting, and the tunnel let me, and that frightened me more than being lost ever had.
