## 1. The Appraisal

The Marchand house sat at the end of a road that the parish had apparently given up on sometime during the second Bush administration, and Odile Delacroix had been driving that road for eleven minutes, which felt like a personal affront. Their surveyor's GPS had lost the plot twice—first insisting they were in a sugarcane field, then insisting, with the serene confidence of software, that they had arrived at a destination that was clearly a drainage ditch. Odile had shut it off and navigated the old way, by the printed plat map folded on the passenger seat and the stubborn conviction that a house listed in the assessor's records since 1908 could not simply have evaporated.

It hadn't evaporated. It was worse than that. It was *waiting*.

It stood back from the road behind a collapse of fence, two stories of cypress clapboard gone the gray of old driftwood, its galleries sagging like a tired animal lowering itself to the ground. The roofline dipped in the middle where decades of rain had found a weakness and worried it. Paint had surrendered entirely on the south face, leaving bare wood furred with weather. Spanish moss hung from the live oaks flanking the drive in long gray curtains, motionless in the still air, and Odile noticed—filed away, did not interpret—that the moss hung still on every tree except the two flanking the drive, where it moved, gently, in a wind Odile could not feel through the truck's open window.

"Professional skepticism," Odile said aloud, to the cab of the truck, in the tone of a woman reminding herself of a password. They were thirty-seven years old, nonbinary in a parish that had barely mastered *ma'am* and *sir*, raised in seven foster placements across three parishes, and they had survived all of it by being the person in the room who measured things twice and believed the tape measure, not the feeling. Feelings were what other people used to move goalposts. Numbers didn't care if you were likable. Numbers were the only foster parent who never lied about loving you.

They parked, gathered the clipboard, the laser distance meter, the camera, and the good flashlight, and got out into air that hit like a wet cloth. July in Assumption Parish was not weather; it was a condition. Cicadas roared in the oaks, a sound like the whole treeline had been wired to a current. Somewhere far off a church bell rang eleven o'clock, and the sound seemed to arrive from the wrong direction, or from all directions, and Odile decided not to think about that either.

The plat map said 4.7 acres, parcel 14-033-B, structure circa 1890, last transfer of ownership 1981. The file was thin in the way that old rural files were thin—one owner's name smudged, a tax sale, a probate that had never quite closed. *Marchand, A.* And then nothing. Thirty years of nothing. Houses that nobody claimed were the ones that got assigned to Odile, because Odile was the one who took the assignments nobody wanted, and had built a whole career on being unbothered by rot, snakes, squatters, and the particular sadness of places where people had stopped.

The front steps bowed under their boots. Three of the balusters were gone; the fourth crumbled to the touch like a biscuit. Odile stood on the gallery, clipboard against their forearm, and did what they always did first: they looked up at the roofline, then down at the foundation piers, then let the house introduce itself.

It was, professionally speaking, a disaster. A beautiful disaster—somebody with money and taste had built this, once, with cypress milled fat and heart-pine floors laid tight—and it had been dying for a very long time, gracefully, the way old Southern houses died, by inches and by water. Odile photographed the gallery, the columns, the water staining fanning down the clapboard in dark blooms. The camera's shutter clicked, and in the pause between clicks, the cicadas stopped.

All of them. At once. The silence was so total Odile could hear their own blood.

Then, as one, the treeline started up again, and Odile stood on the rotten gallery with their heart going hard against their ribs and wrote in the margin of the survey sheet, in their small square handwriting: *ambient noise intermittent.* Which was the kind of sentence a person wrote when they were cataloguing evidence they refused to interpret.

* * *

The front door was locked, which was correct, and the lock was a skeleton key lock, which was a problem, until Odile tried the handle a second time and it turned with the soft oiled ease of a lock that had been waiting to be used. That was the first wrong thing that Odile wrote down and then underlined and then, on reflection, crossed out. Doors in abandoned houses stuck. Doors in abandoned houses were swollen, painted shut, or hanging off one hinge. Doors in abandoned houses did not open *smoothly*, and they certainly did not exhale a breath of cool dry air into a ninety-eight-degree morning, air that smelled not of mold and animal nests but of cedar and old paper, like the inside of a hope chest.

Odile stood in the doorway a long moment, letting the cool air move over their face, and told themselves: *cross-ventilation, a chimney draft, the house is shaded on this side.* The house had no chimney on this side. They went in anyway. They were being paid by the hour.

The foyer ran deep, twelve feet at a guess, with a staircase rising on the right, its banister dark with a century of hands. Dust lay over everything in a fine, even gray—on the newel post, on the floorboards, on the mirror in the hall that had gone cloudy with silvering loss so that a person's reflection came back soft and approximate, like a memory of themselves. Odile's reflection stood in it, clipboard and all, and Odile looked at it for one beat too long before turning away.

They worked the way they always worked: room by room, counterclockwise, laser meter and cloth tape in agreement, sketching the footprint on graph paper, noting condition, materials, anomalies. Parlor: 15 by 14, ceiling plaster down in sheets, fireplace mantel intact, black walnut, worth saving. Dining room: 16 by 15, chandelier gone, only the scar on the ceiling where it had hung. They measured and photographed and wrote, and the work did what work always did for Odile, which was hold the world still.

It was on the second pass—the return loop through the front rooms to check window counts—that the numbers stopped agreeing.

The parlor had been 15 by 14. Odile had it written down, in their own hand, twenty minutes old. The laser meter now read 17 by 16. Odile stood in the middle of the room with the device humming in their palm and remeasured, wall to wall, corner to corner, and got 17 by 16 again, and then got out the cloth tape, which did not run on batteries and could not be argued with, and the cloth tape agreed with the laser. The room was two feet longer in every direction than it had been when Odile measured it. The fireplace was still there. The windows were still there. The room had simply *grown*, the way a sleeping animal stretches, and settled back, and Odile stood very quietly in the middle of it, listening to the house make its small settling sounds, and wrote in the margin: *verify dims. instrument error suspected.*

They knew it wasn't instrument error. They had bought the meter themselves, calibrated, four hundred dollars, and it had never once lied to them. But *instrument error suspected* was a sentence that kept the world shaped like a world, and Odile had learned at six years old that you could survive almost anything if you kept the world shaped like a world.

The dining room was worse. It was smaller. It had given up a full foot on the north wall, and where that wall had been bare plaster on the first pass, it now held a door. A narrow door, painted the same white as the wall, with a porcelain knob gone the color of tea, in a wall that Odile had photographed twenty minutes earlier with no door in it. Odile found the photograph on the camera's little screen and looked at it for a long time: bare wall, water stain, no door. Then looked up: door.

They did not open it. They wrote *door, interior, condition N/A* and moved on with their pulse ticking in their ears, and behind them, as they crossed the hall, they heard—faint, unmistakable—the porcelain knob of that unopened door turn, once, slowly, all the way around, and stop.

Odile did not look back. Looking back was how you gave a thing permission.

* * *

By one o'clock the heat outside had become a physical weight and the cool inside the house had become its own kind of strange—too dry, too even, the temperature of a museum, of a place kept. Odile finished the ground floor, ate half a sandwich in the truck, and went back in to do the second story, because the report required the second story, and because a part of them they didn't examine too closely wanted to know if the stairs were still where they'd left them.

They were. Thirteen treads, and the thirteenth sang a low note under Odile's boot, a note like a finger run around a wet glass rim.

Upstairs the rooms were bedrooms, four of them, all stripped to the studs of anything personal except wallpaper—faded roses in one, a border of blue birds in another, and in the small room at the back of the hall, a nursery papered with a circus parade: elephants and a calliope wagon and a ringmaster whose face had faded to a smudge, so that all that remained of him was his smile, white and wide, floating on the wall. Odile photographed the wallpaper and did not linger.

It was coming back down the stairs, at ten minutes to two, that Odile saw the porch swing.

They had photographed it that morning from the front steps: a swing hanging from the gallery ceiling on two chains, its slats silvered, its seat furred with moss and a season of fallen leaves. It had been, in the photograph Odile could still call to mind, a ruin. Nobody had sat in that swing in decades. Nobody sat in swings in houses that had stood empty since 1981.

It was moving.

Not swinging—not the wide arc of a pushed swing—but swaying, gently, with the small residual motion of a thing recently vacated, chains creaking in a rhythm that was winding down, down, down, and would stop. And the leaves and the moss that had furred its seat that morning were gone. The slats were bare. Clean. And in the center of the seat, in the film of dust that had resettled since, there was a shallow depression the width of a person's hips, and beside it, smaller, at the outer edge, the faint oval press of a hand, as if someone had been sitting there, waiting, and had just—just now—pushed themselves up and stepped inside.

Odile stood at the bottom of the gallery steps with the clipboard pressed to their chest and made themselves do the thing they always did, the thing that had kept them alive and employed and alone for thirty years: they walked up the steps, they laid their palm flat on the swing's seat.

It was warm. Not sun-warm—the gallery was deep-shaded, and the morning had never touched it. Warm the way a chair is warm when someone has only just left it. Warm like a held hand.

The cicadas had stopped again. In the silence, from somewhere inside the house—upstairs, in the room with the circus parade, or in the hall behind the door that hadn't existed at noon—came a sound so small and so human that Odile's whole body turned toward it before their mind could stop it: a floorboard, taking a person's weight. Then another. Unhurried. Coming down the hall toward the top of the stairs.

Odile's hand found the swing chain and gripped it, and the metal was warm too, and in the margin of the survey sheet, without looking down, without any memory of doing it, they had written a single word in their own small square handwriting, a word they had not written, a word that was not on any form the parish had ever printed:

*Wren.*
