## 13. Countdown in Bronze

The morning after the quarry dive, I woke to find the kitchen window fogged from the inside.

That's not remarkable in itself. October in Wrenlow is a season of condensation; every pane in Hollow House weeps overnight, and El had taken to wiping the sills with a towel each morning like a nurse tending a feverish patient. What was remarkable was the shape the condensation had chosen to take.

Nine vertical strokes. Four crossed by a fifth, then four more beside them, neat as an abacus.

I stood in my dressing gown with tea going cold in my hand and looked at tally marks that had condensed out of the air inside a sealed double-glazed window, and I did what any trained folklorist would do. I made coffee instead of tea, because I was going to need it, and then I sat down at the kitchen table and started doing arithmetic.

* * *

Let me back up. Let me be methodical, because methodical is the last comfortable thing I have left, and I intend to wring it dry before I let it go.

On Tuesday — eight days ago now, though it feels like the kind of span you'd measure in geological epochs — Tam Osgood and I dove the flooded quarry and found the bell. The original bell, cast in 1843 or thereabouts, sunk deliberately in 1901 according to the parish records, because apparently when your church bell contains the compressed remains of a man your ancestors murdered to stop a plague, the sensible Victorian solution is to drop it in forty feet of water and pretend it's a landscaping feature.

The bronze skin had been covered in scratched tallies. Thousands of them. And they were wet, which is the detail I keep circling back to at three in the morning, because we were forty feet down and everything down there is wet, obviously, but these marks were wet the way a fresh wound is wet. Tam counted the newest cluster — nine marks, four-and-four with one apart — and said, in the flat voice of a man reading his own obituary: "That's us. It started counting us the day you came."

At the time I'd assumed the count referred to the people who'd heard Maren sing those four lines in the pub. Me, El, Wren, Tam himself, Maren, and the three patrons who'd walked out mid-recording without a word. That's seven. Add whoever else had been within earshot — old Hollis behind the bar, maybe, and his wife who never came out of the back room but always seemed to be listening — and you get nine. Nine hearers. Nine marks.

It had felt almost tidy. Almost manageable. A closed set, a finite list, like guests at a terrible wedding.

This morning's window suggested otherwise. Because this morning's window wasn't showing me how many of us there were. It was showing me how many days we had left.

* * *

"Wren's toast is burning," El said from the doorway, and I flinched hard enough to slosh coffee over my notes.

"Sorry. Yes. Toast." I dragged my sleeve across the window before Wren came downstairs. The condensation didn't want to wipe away — it smeared like grease rather than water, and for a moment the marks just went translucent, hanging there under the glass like things pressed in resin — but then they faded, and the window was only a window again, fogged and ordinary and dripping onto the sill El would wipe at noon.

El looked at me. I looked at El. We have been married long enough that entire conversations happen in the space between two raised eyebrows, and this one translated roughly as: *You saw it too, didn't you.*

"I'll tell her the window's cold today," El said quietly. "Sit on that side. Keep her facing the garden."

The garden faces the quarry. I didn't point this out. There are mercies even in a marriage under siege.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic, laid out as cleanly as I can manage, because if I don't lay it out cleanly I will start screaming, and Wren is at an age where she notices everything and files it away for later use against me.

Fact one: the tallies on the sunken bell numbered in the thousands, accumulated over a hundred and twenty years. The parish records show deaths in Wrenlow clustering in ways no epidemiologist would call random — one here, one there, always singly, always spaced, like a careful eater working through a plate.

Fact two: the newest cluster on the bell, as of Tuesday, was nine marks. Nine hearers of the song, beginning with me.

Fact three: this morning, the same nine marks appeared on our kitchen window, condensed from the inside of sealed glass.

Now. Two interpretations present themselves, and I've spent all day trying to decide which is worse.

Interpretation A: the window is simply another surface the Bell has learned to write on. The marks are a census, not a calendar. Nine hearers, listed and claimed, and the number will grow only if the song spreads further. Under this interpretation, we are already dead; we just haven't been informed through official channels.

Interpretation B: the marks are a countdown. Nine days. The Bell counts its listeners, yes — Maren said so, and Maren has had a hundred and sixty years of family memory to draw on — but counting isn't the same as claiming. It counts first. Then it takes. One per interval. One mark becoming one collection event, spaced evenly, the way the historical deaths were spaced. Evenly, Jude. Look at the ledger again. Evenly.

Under Interpretation B, someone in Wrenlow dies tomorrow. Or something happens to someone. And the day after, someone else. And nine days from now, the count reaches the Amberdale household, and the Bell comes for whichever of us it fancies, and I genuinely could not tell you whether it fancies the folklorist who came asking questions, or the spouse who filmed the mirror, or the child who hummed the whole cursed lullaby in her sleep.

Unless, of course, someone else hears the song. That's the variable that ruins everything. Every new ear adds a mark, and — here's the part I worked out over lunch while El watched me not eat it — every new ear doesn't add itself to the end of the queue. It resets nothing. It just makes the pool bigger. The Bell isn't running out of days; it's running out of *us*, and it will take its one per interval regardless, drawing from whatever the current total is. More hearers means more marks means more intervals, but it also means more names in the hat, and Wren's name has been in the hat since she hummed eight verses she couldn't possibly have known.

So the arithmetic is this: nine days, minus however many have already passed that I don't know about, divided by nothing, multiplied by everyone who might hum four bars of a tune they heard in a pub. Which is to say: the math is not on our side. The math has never once been on our side in this village.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing what I do, which is research, though it felt less like scholarship and more like a man counting the steps between himself and a cliff edge.

The vestry ledger gave me spacing. In 1843–44, the year after the sacrifice, the fever stopped — but other things started. The records are maddeningly euphemistic. *"Taken by the water." "A wasting." "Found quiet."* But the dates march. Roughly nine-day intervals, sometimes eight, sometimes ten, tapering off over months as the population either learned the rules or ran out of people who hadn't heard the bell ring. Because that's the thing I keep forgetting and keep relearning: the original sin wasn't the singing. It was the ringing. They rang the bell with Aldous Merrow inside it — I'm sure of the name now, sure in the way you're sure of a bone setting wrong — and every soul in Wrenlow heard it. Every soul was counted. And the village survived by learning to go silent, to let the count starve, to feed it only the occasional careless outsider or the occasional deliberate one, God help them, because some winters the alternative was worse.

Maren confirmed it that evening, when she came by with a casserole she clearly hoped would function as both sustenance and apology.

"You've seen the window," she said. Not a question. She'd brought her own dish towel, folded over her arm like a waitress, and she wiped our kitchen window with the brisk competence of a woman who has done this ritual in other houses, in other decades, for other families who thought they were visiting rather than arriving.

"How long?" I asked.

She wrung out the towel over the sink. The water that came off it was faintly brown, like steeped tea, and I decided not to think about that.

"If nobody sings, nobody new hears, and nobody goes near the water or the glass after dark — nine days. Give or take. It took my great-aunt's lodger on the eighth day once, and eleven once, when the winter was mild. It likes its rhythm but it's not a metronome."

"And if someone hears the song?"

"Then the count grows and the clock keeps its own time." She folded the towel. "Jude. Listen to me. You need to leave. Tonight. Take the car, take the girl, drive until the mirrors behave."

"We tried that logic already," I said. "We're past it. The lag followed us to Bristol for six hours in March, remember? Before we ever moved here. Before I ever heard Maren Cobb sing four lines and go white as milk." I hadn't told anyone about Bristol. It slipped out now, and I watched Maren absorb it, and I watched her shoulders drop half an inch, and I understood that she'd known. She'd known the lag preceded us. She'd sung anyway, or she'd sung because she thought it was already too late, and I still don't know which of those is kinder.

"The Bell isn't bound to Wrenlow," I said. "It's bound to the counted. We are counted. Running just changes the scenery."

Maren put the towel down very carefully, the way you put down something that might go off. "Then you'd best find out what it actually wants," she said, "before day nine. Because containment only works on things that can be contained. And that thing—" she nodded at the clean, blank window, which was already beginning, very faintly, to fog again at the corners— "has been waiting a hundred and eighty years for somebody to ask it a direct question."

* * *

Wren came home from school — the village school, twelve pupils, one teacher who has stopped meeting my eye — and announced over supper that her friend "the lady in the bathroom mirror" says hello.

We do not have a lady in the bathroom mirror. We have a lagging reflection that shows empty rooms and, lately, figures without faces. El and I exchanged the look again, the married-couple telegraph, and this time it spelled: *Not in front of her.* So I said, lightly, "What does the lady say?" and Wren said, "She's counting on her fingers. She does it slow. Like she wants me to check her work."

Nine fingers, presumably. I didn't ask. My voice wouldn't have held.

After she was asleep — door open a crack, nightlight on, monitor volume up — El and I sat at the kitchen table with the good whisky, the one we'd been saving for a celebration that now seemed to belong to a different marriage, a different life, a house where windows were just windows.

"Day nine," El said. "Or fewer."

"Or fewer."

"And you're not leaving."

"I'm not leaving."

"Because of the book." There was no accusation in it, which somehow made it worse. El has never accused me of anything louder than a fact.

"Because of the book," I admitted, "and because of the ledger, and because there's a name burned out of every page of that ledger and I think — El, I *think* — that the erasure is the engine. I think they didn't just kill him. They unmade him. And a thing that's been unmade doesn't want worship and doesn't want blood. It wants—" I gestured vaguely with the whisky glass, at the window, at the dark garden, at the drowned quarry beyond it. "It wants to be somebody again. And we've been giving it tallies instead."

El turned the glass slowly on the table, around and around, a small orbit. "And if you're wrong?"

"Then we've got eight days of being wrong left, and I'd rather spend them wrong in an interesting direction than right in a chair."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of El, which I counted as the day's single victory, and I went to bed and lay awake listening to the house tick and settle, and at some point — I know because I checked my phone, 3:14 a.m. — every clock in Hollow House chimed softly at once. None of them are wound. None of them have been wound since we arrived. Eight chimes, unhurried, like a man tapping a spoon on a glass to get a room's attention.

Eight.

I got up and wiped the kitchen window with my sleeve.

Eight marks.

One gone. One collected. Somewhere in Wrenlow, tonight, someone is missing, or changed, or quiet in the way the ledger means by *quiet*, and I don't know who, and I won't know until morning, and the not-knowing is its own kind of drowning.

Seven days.

I sat at the kitchen table until dawn with the ledger open under lamplight, raking the light low and sideways across the burn-scarred page where a name used to be, hunting the ghost of pressure where a pen once pressed too hard, because Maren was right about one thing: I need to ask the Bell a direct question. And to ask a man a question, you first have to know what he was called.

The graphite is out. The lamp is angled. And under the scorch, faint as breath on glass, something is beginning to rise to the surface — a letter, maybe two, the start of a word that the village burned and buried and sank in forty feet of water and prayed would stay forgotten.

Tomorrow I dive again. Tonight, I read the ashes.
