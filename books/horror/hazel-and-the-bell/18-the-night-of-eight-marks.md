## 18. The Night of Eight Marks

The eighth mark came at 3:14 in the morning, and it did not condense so much as *arrive*, the way a guest arrives who has stopped pretending to knock.

I know the time because every clock in Hollow House told me. That was the other thing that happened. None of them were wound — I had stopped winding them in week two, after the mantel clock began ticking in a rhythm slightly too slow, like a heart trying to remember what hearts do — and yet at 3:14 they all struck. The mantel clock. El's bedside alarm with its cheerful digital chirp. Wren's novelty clock shaped like a cartoon owl whose eyes had been watching us with an enthusiasm I'd come to find frankly sinister. Even my phone, dead for two days on the kitchen counter, lit up and produced a bell-tone from somewhere deep in its firmware where no bell-tone should live.

Eight strikes. Counted by Wren from her doorway in her pajamas, hair wild, voice flat as a child reciting times tables.

"Eight," she said. "It's nearly done, Dad."

Then she went back to bed, which was somehow worse than if she'd screamed.

* * *

Let me tell you what eight marks looks like on a window, because you will not find it in any folklore archive and I would spare you the discovery.

The tallies appear on the inside of the glass, in condensation, as though the house itself is breathing onto the panes. Four vertical strokes, then a diagonal slash through them — the way prisoners count days, the way shepherds once counted lambs. Except these marks are not scratched. They are *written*, in water, in a hand that trembles slightly, and when I press my finger against one the glass beneath is cold enough to burn, cold like quarry water in November, and my fingertip comes away smelling faintly of bronze.

Seven marks had stood on the kitchen window for two days. I had spent those two days doing arithmetic I hated, the way you hate a sum you already know the answer to. Nine marks. Nine days, or fewer. Tam Osgood's tally on the sunken bell matched ours stroke for stroke now; he'd rowed me out over the drowned church twice to check, standing hip-deep in his waders in a boat older than either of us, saying nothing, because there was nothing left to say that numbers weren't already saying better.

Eight marks meant one day left. Maybe less. The intervals had been shrinking — nine, eight, seven days between marks at first, then five, then three, then this: barely thirty hours since the seventh.

And El was half-gone already.

* * *

I found them in the bathroom at 4 a.m., sitting on the edge of the tub in the dark, fully dressed, staring at the mirror.

Our bathroom mirror had been lagging for weeks. By now the lag was not a lag at all — it was a delay with opinions. My reflection raised its hand when it felt like it. Sometimes it didn't raise it at all. Once, early in the month, I'd watched it yawn while I was wide awake and terrified, and I had decided then, in the rational part of myself that I have been feeding scraps to keep alive like a fire in wet weather, that sleep deprivation plus folkloric suggestion equals hallucination, QED, goodnight.

That rational part of me has been very quiet lately. It sits in the corner of my skull with its arms crossed, sulking, occasionally muttering something about sample sizes.

El's reflection was sitting on the edge of the tub too. But real El sat with their hands in their lap, and reflected El had one hand pressed flat against the inside of the glass.

"Jude," El said, without turning around. Their voice was wrong. Not foreign — worse than foreign. It was El's voice played at the correct pitch but the wrong cadence, sentences that ended where sentences shouldn't, pauses in the middle of words. Like someone doing an impression of El from a description written by someone who had never met them. "Come look. It wants to show you something."

"No," I said. "El. Look at me. Not the glass."

"It's been showing me things all night." They turned, finally, and in the dark their face was half-lit by the streetlamp through the frosted window, and I want to record here, precisely, what I saw, because precision is all I have left of my profession: El's face was El's face. Both eyes were theirs. And yet the expression sat on it the way an expression sits on a mask — correctly positioned, technically accurate, and hollow behind. "The children. You know about the children."

"Merrow's children," I said. "Yes. Wren told me. She sees them."

"They're not children anymore." El smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was warm, it was genuinely warm, and it belonged to no one. "They've been waiting so long. They want to introduce us. There's a door, Jude. Right there." They pointed at the mirror, and reflected El pointed back — simultaneously, perfectly, for the first time in weeks — "and it's not even locked. It never needed to be locked. We just have to stop being afraid of it."

"El." I knelt in front of them, took their hands. Their skin was cold in the specific way the window glass was cold. Quarry-cold. Bronze-cold. "Listen to my voice. Whatever is talking through you right now, whatever is wearing your mouth — Aldous Merrow or the fever or the hundred and eighty years of silence — I need you to fight it. Can you fight it? For Wren?"

Something moved behind El's eyes. A struggle, brief and distant, like watching someone drown far offshore — you can see it happening and you cannot reach it.

"They rang him inside the bell," El whispered, and this was El again, I was almost sure, the cadence breaking, the words coming out ragged and human. "They melted the fittings around his body, Jude. He was alive. He counted them while he died and he's been counting ever since and we let it happen, our village, my grandmother's grandmother signed the ledger, and now it's counting *us* and I can hear it, Jude, I can hear the number under everything, under the rain, under your voice—" Their grip crushed my fingers. "One more. One more mark and I go through the glass whether you open the door or not. Do you understand? I can feel which parts of me are still mine. There aren't many. There's maybe a day of me left."

"Then we have a day," I said. "Maren's teaching me the ninth verse tonight. Tam's getting rope and lights. We have a plan—"

"You don't have a plan. You have a *hypothesis*." El laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle into something that might have been sobbing, and I chose to let it be sobbing because I could bear that. "You're going to walk into a mirror with a name and a lullaby and hope a drowned man wants to be *witnessed*. That's not a plan, love. That's a paper read at a conference."

"Yes," I said. "It is. It's exactly that. And it's also the only idea anyone has had in a hundred and eighty years that isn't 'ring the bell' or 'don't say the name.' Everyone else chose silence, El. Silence is how it got this hungry. I'm choosing the other thing."

El looked at me for a long time. And here is the thing I will carry, the thing I already carry, the stone in the shoe of my soul: what was looking back at me was mostly not El. Mostly. Maybe sixty percent. And I loved the remaining forty percent so much it felt like being flayed.

"Then promise me something," they said quietly. "Before tomorrow. Before your mirror-walking heroics."

"Anything."

"If it doesn't work — if I start going through the glass, if I start *sounding* like this all the time — you let me go. Don't hold on. Don't do what the village does, don't keep a piece of me in a ledger and call it keeping me safe." Their thumb moved across my knuckles, an old gesture, ours, borrowed from ten thousand ordinary mornings. "Wren needs one parent who's entirely here. If you spend yourself trying to keep all of me, you'll lose the part of you she can't afford to lose. Let me go. Let me be a story you tell her kindly. Promise."

"I promise nothing," I said, "except that I will try everything first."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know what you asked." My voice did something embarrassing. I let it. "But I'm a folklorist, El. I don't accept endings before the fieldwork's done. Ask anyone. It's why nobody invites me to dinner."

El laughed — really laughed, El's laugh, the whole laugh, and for one second the mask fell away entirely and it was just my spouse on the edge of a bathtub at four in the morning, terrified, exhausted, holding my hands in a haunted house above a drowned quarry.

"There you are," I said.

"Don't jinx it," they said. "Go write down your verse. And Jude?" 

"Mm?"

"When you talk to him — Merrow — when you give him his name back." El's face did something complicated, grief and fury braided together, the founding lie of Wrenlow sitting in their chest like swallowed river-stone. "Tell him his children waited too. Tell him they never stopped waiting. Somebody in that village should finally tell him the truth."

* * *

I wrote until dawn. Maren had come at midnight, uninvited, unannounced, wrapped in a coat that smelled of woodsmoke and forty years of the same pub, and she had taught me the ninth verse in a whisper, against every vow she'd taken, her hands shaking so badly I had to hold the page steady for her while she spoke the words and I wrote them down.

She wouldn't stay past the last line. At the door she paused, and didn't look at me, and said: "My gran heard it through the wall in 1954 and never slept right again. She said it wasn't a song, Jude. She said it was a man talking to himself in the dark, trying to remember why he'd agreed." Then she walked off into the rain without her umbrella, because Maren Cobb has decided, apparently, that umbrellas are for people with fewer regrets.

So I sat at the kitchen table with the ninth verse in my handwriting and the eighth mark on the window and the clocks all lying silent again, and I did what I always do with material that frightens me: I transcribed it. Clean copy. Field notes. As if scholarship were a crucifix.

The verse is short. Eight lines. It isn't a lullaby at all — that's the horror of it, the plain human horror underneath the bronze and the mirrors. It's a letter. It's a man's voice, alone in the dark inside a bell, composing the things he wished he'd said: to his wife, to his apprentices, to the two small children whose names he'd been promised would be spared and who were taken anyway within the year, because a village that sacrifices one founder will sacrifice anything. He sang it to himself while the metal cooled around him. He has been singing it for a hundred and eighty years to an audience of tally marks.

He doesn't want worship. He doesn't even want revenge, though God knows he's earned it. He wants what every song I've ever collected wanted, underneath everything: *someone to listen to the end.*

Outside, the rain kept up its own percussion on the glass, and the quarry below the house lay black and patient, holding its bell, holding its count.

At dawn, Wren appeared at the table in her owl-print pajamas, climbed into the chair across from me, and looked at the window.

"Eight," she confirmed, unnecessarily.

"I know, sweetheart."

"The children say tomorrow night is the last night the door opens easy." She reached across and patted my hand, the way you comfort a grown-up, the way you comfort someone you've decided needs comforting. "They say to bring the verse. And they say—" she frowned, listening to something I couldn't hear, some frequency just under the rain, "—they say to tell you the mirror in the hall doesn't lag anymore. It opens straight through. Dad? Why are you crying? It's good news. They're being *helpful*."

I wiped my face with the heel of my hand and laughed, because the alternative was screaming, and screaming would use up air I might need tomorrow.

"Because they are, love," I said. "They're being very helpful. Go wake your other parent. We've got one day, and I intend to waste none of it."

On the window, the eighth mark glistened. And as I watched, the condensation around it began, slowly, deliberately, to form a ninth stroke — unfinished, waiting, patient as bronze —

— and stopped, mid-line, as if remembering that the last word of any count belongs to whoever is brave enough to interrupt it.
