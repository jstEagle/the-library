## 2. Twenty-Six Names

The register arrived on Freya's desk the first Monday of term, delivered by a boy so small she briefly wondered whether he was one of her pupils or simply a very committed messenger. He set it down with both hands, the way one might deliver a live thing, and fled without a word.

It was a handsome object, for all that. Leather-bound, corners worn soft as bread crusts, the school's crest stamped in gold that had gone the colour of old honey. HOLLOWMERE PRIMARY — ADMISSIONS & ATTENDANCE, said the cover, and beneath that, in someone's careful copperplate: *Property of the School. To be kept on premises.*

Freya ran her thumb over the spine and felt, absurdly, like a doctor receiving a stethoscope. Her register. Her class. Her twenty-six children.

She opened it at the first page and began to read the names aloud to herself, because there is no pleasure quite like a fresh class list, all possibility and no evidence yet. *Aldous, Bethan, Callum, Dree—* good grief, Dree, what a name, she'd have to ask about that one—*Edwin, Ffion, Greta, Harrow...*

She got to the end of the list and counted.

Twenty-six.

Then she counted again, because the first count had felt wrong in some way she couldn't articulate, the way a stair feels wrong when you expect one more step than there is.

Twenty-six. Definitely twenty-six. She checked the third time anyway, touching each name with her pencil tip like a woman counting coins she suspected of being short.

Twenty-six names. And yet when she had stood before her class on Friday morning — her first morning, her heart going like a kettle coming to boil — she had counted heads out of pure nervous habit and arrived at twenty-five. She'd assumed she'd miscounted. Children move; they blur; they are famously difficult to enumerate, like trying to count sparrows mid-flight. She'd put it down to nerves and taught her lesson.

Now, sitting in the empty classroom with autumn light slanting through windows that needed cleaning, she looked at the list again and noticed something she had missed.

The last name was smudged.

Not faded — smudged. As though someone had pressed a wet thumb against the ink and dragged it sideways, once, deliberately. The other twenty-five names sat crisp and black on the page, printed or written with the tidy confidence of an institution that had been doing this since before the war. But the twenty-sixth was a grey smear, a little cloud of ruined ink, roughly the shape of a name and bearing no letters at all.

"Hm," said Freya, to the empty room.

The room did not answer, which she would later come to appreciate as one of its better qualities.

* * *

She asked Mrs. Pell about it at lunchtime, finding the caretaker in the boiler room, which turned out to be less a room than a shrine to the god of Radiators, presided over by a small iron woman who bled heat and complained in gurgles.

"Register's got a smudge," Freya said. "Last name on the list. Ink's gone."

Mrs. Pell was elbow-deep in the boiler's innards. She did not turn around. "Has it," she said.

"It has. Twenty-five readable names, one blob. Do we have a master copy somewhere? The office, perhaps?"

"No."

"No master copy?"

"No." Mrs. Pell extracted herself from the boiler, wiped her hands on a rag that had clearly been through more than she had, and looked at Freya with an expression that might, in a warmer climate, have been mistaken for patience. "Paperwork goes missing in old schools. Damp. Mice. You'd be amazed what mice'll eat."

"Mice ate a child's name?"

"Mice aren't fussy." Mrs. Pell hung the rag on its nail with great precision. "You've got twenty-five pupils, Miss Amberdale. Teach them well and don't fret over arithmetic."

"But the register says twenty-six."

"The register says a lot of things." And Mrs. Pell picked up her toolbox and left, leaving Freya alone with the boiler, which gurgled after the departing caretaker like a disappointed audience member.

Freya stood a moment in the warmth. It occurred to her, distantly, that she had now been given two instructions since arriving in Hollowmere — *never count them aloud*, and *don't fret over arithmetic* — and that both concerned numbers, and that neither came with any explanation whatsoever.

She decided, in the optimistic way that had carried her through a difficult childhood and a worse teacher-training year, that country people were simply eccentric, and went back to her classroom to prepare a lesson on similes.

* * *

By Wednesday she had learned all twenty-five faces and could match them to their names, which she considered a personal triumph and celebrated with a custard cream from the staff tin. (There was only ever one biscuit left in the staff tin. She had begun to suspect Mrs. Pell of eating the rest, but the tin refilled itself mysteriously every few days, so perhaps it was the mice again. The mice of Hollowmere, she was beginning to understand, were extremely capable.)

Her class, she had decided, was a good one. Bethan Aldous read three years above her age and corrected Freya's chalkboard spelling with the delicacy of a bomb-disposal officer. Callum and Greta were engaged in a feud of such longevity that neither could remember its origin, conducted entirely through aggressively polite note-passing. Little Dree — whose full name, it transpired, was simply Dree, "it's just Dree, miss, me mum said the paperwork couldn't fit more" — had appointed herself unofficial class weather-service and announced each morning whether it would rain, based on methods she declined to disclose.

And there was Idris.

Idris Vane sat third from the window, a thin, watchful boy with ink permanently on his fingers and the expression of someone who had read ahead in the book of the world and wasn't sure he liked the next chapter. He was quiet in the way that quiet children are often the loudest things in a room. He handed in beautiful work. He never raised his hand first. And he watched Freya — not rudely, not challengingly, but with the steady attention of a boy keeping track.

On Thursday morning, taking the register properly for the first time — pen poised, ready to mark present or absent with the satisfying bureaucracy of it all — Freya reached the final line.

The smudge.

She looked at it. The class, settled into their morning murmur, hummed along pleasantly beneath her silence. She looked at the smudge a moment longer. Then, because she was a teacher, and teachers ask questions, she lifted her head.

"Class — I've a question about the register. There's a twenty-sixth name here, but it's been smudged past reading. Does anyone know who it belongs to?"

The murmur stopped.

Not faded, not dwindled — stopped, all at once, the way a clock stops: completely, and with a sense of something having been switched off. Twenty-four children became very still and very interested in their desks. Bethan Aldous, who would cheerfully correct an adult's spelling, developed a sudden fascination with her own fingernails.

And Idris flinched.

It was a small thing. His shoulder jumped, his pencil rolled off the desk, and he bent to retrieve it and stayed bent a half-second longer than the pencil required. When he sat up his face was composed again, carefully composed, the way a bed is made by someone hiding something under it.

"I'm sorry, miss," said Bethan, to her fingernails. "We don't know."

"We don't," agreed Callum, with unusual fervour.

"Nobody knows," said Greta, which had the ring of a rehearsed statement, possibly a legal one.

Freya looked around the room at twenty-four children performing innocence with the subtlety of a village pantomime, and felt the first cold thread of something that was not quite fear. Fear, she thought, requires a shape. This was more like the feeling of a door in your own house you'd never noticed before, suddenly visible, and closed.

"Well," she said lightly, "never mind. Perhaps the mice got it."

No one laughed. In her experience children laughed at even bad mouse jokes, out of politeness if nothing else. Twenty-four children regarded her with expressions of gentle, collective pity, as though she had suggested the moon was made of something other than cheese.

She took the register — twenty-five marks, twenty-five presents, the twenty-sixth line left blank as a pulled tooth leaves a gap — and began the lesson.

* * *

At playtime she found Idris alone by the low wall, which was apparently where he conducted his break, watching the others run and shout with the air of a naturalist observing a species he respected but did not wish to join.

Freya went and leaned on the wall beside him, at a respectful distance, the way you'd approach a cat.

"Good essay on the river," she said. "The bit where you called the current 'a rumour the water tells about itself' — I'm stealing that for my own writing."

Idris shrugged, pleased despite himself. She saw the pleased arrive and get quickly filed away behind the watchfulness.

"Idris," she said. "Can I ask you something? You don't have to answer."

He said nothing, which she took as permission of a guarded kind.

"The register. The smudged name. You knew something. Not what it says — I don't think anyone knows what it says. But you knew something *about* it."

For a long moment Idris studied the playground. Then he said, quietly, "Don't ask about it, miss."

"Why not?"

"Because they go funny when you do." He nodded at the playground, at the running, shrieking, ordinary children. "They're not being weird on purpose. It's just — nobody talks about it. Like how you don't talk about—" He stopped, hunting for the comparison, and landed on: "Like how you don't say the word for the thing that happens at funerals. Everyone knows the word. Nobody uses it."

"And this is like a funeral word?"

"It's like worse than a funeral word." He picked at the mortar between the bricks. "My gran says the school used to have twenty-seven on the roll. Before. And then it was twenty-six. And nobody remembers who the twenty-seventh was, and my gran gets angry if you push at it, like pushing a bruise." He glanced up at Freya then, and his eyes were far too old for his face, and far too kind. "You're new, miss. So you can still stop asking. That's the good thing about being new. You can just... teach us things instead. We'd like that better."

Freya crouched down to his level, which cost her knees she hadn't known were expensive.

"Idris. If something's frightening you — anything, in this school or out of it — you can tell me. That's rather the whole point of me."

He considered this with grave seriousness. Then he shook his head, once, and said the sentence that Freya would carry home that evening like a stone in her coat pocket:

"You can't tell me things I haven't lost yet."

* * *

That night, in her cottage on the lane, with the lamps lit — all of them, she'd taken to lighting all of them before dusk, though she'd have been hard-pressed to explain why, except that the house felt better lit, the way a boat feels better bailed — Freya sat at her kitchen table with the register open in front of her and a magnifying glass she'd bought for examining old manuscripts.

Under the glass, the smudge resolved into nothing. Just grey. Just ruin. But she noticed, at the very edge of it, where the drag of the thumb had thinned the ink almost to transparency, the ghost of a single letterform. A curve. Possibly an E. Possibly nothing at all — possibly her own hope of a curve, projected onto accident.

"E," she said aloud, and then felt foolish, and then felt foolish about feeling foolish, and wrote the letter in her notebook anyway, because a writer's notebook is where foolishness goes to be useful later.

Below it she wrote: *26 names. 25 children. 1 smudge. Nobody will discuss. Idris frightened — more than frightened, practised at being frightened. Village-wide silence. Gran's generation angriest.*

She sat back and looked at the list of facts, which looked, in her neat handwriting, exactly like the opening notes of a mystery. She had written three novels featuring plucky investigators and had killed off two of them for asking precisely this sort of question.

"Don't be absurd," she told herself. "It's a clerical error and a village that doesn't like gossip. Half of England runs on those."

The fire ticked. Outside, the lane lay dark and quiet, and the school stood at the end of it, and beneath the school, behind a door painted shut, was a cellar she had promised herself she wasn't thinking about.

She thought about it.

Then she closed the register — gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping person — and went to check that all the lamps were still burning.

They were. All six. She counted twice.

* * *

Friday morning brought the first frost of the season, early and unapologetic, silvering the playground grass while Dree announced, with grim satisfaction, "Told you. Knew it Tuesday." Freya took the register in the warm fug of the classroom, the radiators clanking their morning hymn, and everything was ordinary: Bethan reading under the desk, Callum passing Greta a note that said *truce?* and Greta passing back one that said *no*, the whole lovely machinery of a school day turning over.

She marked twenty-five names. Present, present, present. At the twenty-sixth line her pen hovered — a silly instinct, a tic, the urge to mark something, anything, in that blank space — and she made herself lift the pen away and carry on.

But as she set the register down on her desk, Idris was looking at it. Looking at the smudge. And his hand had gone, without his seeming to notice, to the collar of his shirt, and he was rubbing at it absently, brushing something away that wasn't there.

Later — much later, when she understood everything and wished she understood nothing — Freya would remember that gesture, and remember that the first frost had come overnight, weeks early, and that no one else in the room had brushed frost from their collars that morning.

Only Idris.

Only ever, from that day on, Idris.
