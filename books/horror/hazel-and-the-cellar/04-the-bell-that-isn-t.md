## 4. The Bell That Isn't

Idris Fenwick had three rules for walking home from Hollowmere Primary, and he had made them all himself, which made them better than rules adults gave you, because adults made rules for boring reasons like safety, whereas Idris made his for interesting reasons like not dying.

Rule one: never take the shortcut past the duck pond after four o'clock, because the ducks held grudges, and Idris had once thrown a biscuit at the wrong duck, and the wrong duck had told the other ducks, and now they all watched him. He could feel it. Forty-odd pairs of duck eyes, tracking him along the water like a jury.

Rule two: never say the word *cellar* out loud, not even in a whisper, not even in a joke. This rule had come to him fully formed on his first day at Hollowmere Primary, when Miss Amberdale had smiled at the class and said something about the school having "such lovely old bones," and every single child in the room had gone quiet in a way that wasn't the normal quiet of children who don't care about bones. It was the quiet of children who had agreed, long ago and without ever discussing it, that some words stayed indoors.

Rule three: walk past the church quickly, and don't look up at the belfry.

He was very good at rule three. He had been very good at it for his entire life, which was eleven years, which was long enough to know that the belfry of St. Maren's was empty and had been empty for longer than anyone's gran could remember. The bell had been melted down in 1911. This was a fact. It was in the parish records, and it was on the little laminated card inside the church porch that Mrs. Pell dusted every Thursday, and it was the sort of fact that Idris liked best: solid, laminated, finished. The bell was gone. It had been gone for a hundred and thirteen years. It had probably been turned into something sensible, like a church roof or a very large spoon.

So on the afternoon of the last Friday in September, when Idris came up the lane with his bag slung over one shoulder and a pear in his hand that he was eating in the methodical, slightly aggressive way he ate all pears, he did not look up at the belfry. He looked at the hedgerow. He looked at his shoes. He looked at a snail making its slow, doomed way across the lane, and he thought, with the casual cruelty of eleven-year-olds, *you and me both, mate.*

And then the bell rang.

One toll. Deep. Round. It didn't so much make a sound as *become* a sound, the way a stone becomes a splash when you drop it in water. It went through the lane, through the hedges, through Idris's pear, through Idris's chest, and it kept going, out over the rooftops of Hollowmere, and Idris stood absolutely still with half a pear in his mouth and listened to the end of it fade away into the hills like a word nobody wanted to finish.

He swallowed the pear. It went down like a swallowed stone.

"That," he said out loud, to the lane, to the snail, to nobody, "did not happen."

The lane did not agree with him. The lane just sat there being a lane, which was no help at all.

* * *

Here is the thing about Idris Fenwick: he was not a brave boy, but he was a *stubborn* one, and stubbornness looks a lot like bravery from the outside, and even from the inside it will do in a pinch. His mum said he got it from his dad, who had once argued with a parking warden for forty minutes over eleven inches of pavement, and won, and then felt so bad about it that he'd bought the warden a sausage roll. That was the Fenwick way: fight to the death, then be nice about it.

So instead of running home, which was what every sensible nerve in his body was screaming at him to do, Idris stood at the gate of St. Maren's churchyard and did the worst thing he could have done.

He looked up.

The belfry was exactly as it always was. Open arches on all four sides, the wooden louvres gone grey with weather, pigeons shuffling along the ledges with the smug air of pigeons everywhere. And in the middle of it — nothing. Just the empty oak beam where a bell would hang, if there were a bell, which there was not, because of 1911, because of the laminated card, because of *facts*.

The beam was bare. The beam was bare and dusty and there was no rope, no wheel, no bell, no nothing, and Idris looked at it for a long time, and the belfry looked back at him with the particular blankness of an empty stage after the play is over.

"Right," said Idris, in the voice he used when he was lying to himself, which was the same voice he used when he was telling his mum he'd definitely done his homework. "Right. Wind. It was the wind."

It was not the wind. Idris knew it was not the wind the way you know your own name. Wind doesn't toll. Wind whistles, and rattles, and once, memorably, had blown the school bins over in a way that had made the whole class laugh until Miss Amberdale appeared. Wind does not produce a single, perfect, deliberate note, the kind of note that means *someone pulled a rope*, except there was no rope, and there was no bell, and there was no one up there except the pigeons, and the pigeons hadn't even flapped. That was the detail that got him, later, lying in bed staring at the ceiling. The pigeons hadn't moved. A sound that big should have sent every bird in the county up into the sky like confetti. Instead they'd just shuffled their feet, like they'd heard it all before.

He turned up his collar against a chill that had come from nowhere — the afternoon had been warm, properly warm, one of those golden September afternoons that Hollowmere did so well — and he walked home, and he did not run, because rule three had a sub-rule, which was: *never let the church think it's got to you.*

* * *

His mum was in the kitchen when he got in, doing the crossword and burning something, which was her usual evening arrangement.

"All right, love?" she said, not looking up. "How was school?"

"Fine," said Idris, which was rule two applied to everything, not just cellars. "Miss Amberdale's reading us a book about a lighthouse."

"Oh, lovely. Anything happen on the way home?"

And here Idris paused, pear juice still sticky on his fingers, and considered telling her. He considered it properly. His mum was a good listener. His mum believed in things — not ghosts, exactly, but in the general principle that the world was bigger than the telly made it look. She'd once told him, very seriously, that her own nan had heard her late grandad whistling in the shed for a whole year after he died, and that she'd never once been frightened of it, because "some sounds are just love with nowhere to go."

But this sound had not been love with nowhere to go. This sound had gone through him like a nail through a board. And besides — and this was the thought that stopped him, the thought he'd keep coming back to for weeks — if he told her, she'd tell someone, and someone would tell Mrs. Pell, and Mrs. Pell would look at him with those grey eyes of hers that seemed to have seen everything and forgiven almost none of it, and she'd say something quiet and terrible like *you heard it, then,* and then Idris would be a boy who had heard it, and he didn't want to be that boy. He wanted to be a boy who did his homework and fed the ducks out of spite and got a B-plus in spelling.

"No," he said. "Nothing. Ducks were out, though."

"Still holding the grudge?"

"Life sentence, Mum. Ducks don't parole."

She laughed, and the kitchen was warm and smelled of burnt something and crossword ink, and Idris decided, firmly, that the toll had been the wind, or a lorry, or a very large spoon falling off the back of a very large truck, and that was the end of it.

He went up to his room and did not think about it for exactly four hours, which was a personal record.

* * *

It was when he was getting his pyjamas on that he found the frost.

His collar — the collar of his school shirt, which he'd slung over the back of his chair — was white. Not dirty-white, not paint, not bird muck. *Frost.* Proper frost, the spiky kind, the kind that forms on windows in January and that you draw smiley faces in with your thumbnail. It was laid across the collar of his shirt in a neat crescent, like a hand had rested there. Like something cold had leaned against the back of his neck, all the way home, and he'd thought it was just the wind.

It was the twenty-eighth of September.

Idris stood in his bedroom in his pants holding a frosted shirt collar in a warm house on a warm late-September evening, and every single explanation he offered himself — the freezer, a spilled drink, science generally — lined up, looked at the evidence, and resigned.

He touched it. It was cold. It was so cold it stung, the way metal stings in winter, and where his fingertip touched it, the frost melted into a little clear drop that ran down the fabric like the collar was crying.

"Right," said Idris, to his bedroom, in his smallest voice. "Right."

He put the shirt in the laundry basket face-down, as if that would help, and got into bed, and lay there in the dark doing the thing his dad called *having a good think*, which was what the Fenwicks called lying awake in terror.

Because here was the thing he kept circling back to, the thing that wouldn't let go of him like the ducks wouldn't let go of the biscuit incident: the toll hadn't sounded *wrong*. That was the horrible part. It had sounded exactly like a church bell. Warm and round and old and certain, the sound of a hundred years of Sundays, the sound his nan used to hum along to. It had sounded like the most *right* thing in the world. And it had come from a place where there was nothing to make it.

It was like hearing your own voice call your name from an empty room.

He pulled the duvet up to his chin. Outside his window, Hollowmere lay quiet under the last of the daylight, chimney smoke going up straight, the hills going purple, the church a small dark shape at the end of the lane with its open, empty belfry like a mouth that had finished saying something and was waiting to see if anyone would answer.

And Idris, staring at the ceiling, thought about the laminated card in the porch. *The great bell of St. Maren's was melted down in 1911, its metal given to the war effort.* He'd read it a hundred times. He'd read it the way you read anything in a church porch, which is to say, with your hands in your pockets, waiting for the rain to stop.

But he'd also — and this surfaced now, from wherever he'd buried it, from the place where children keep the things adults say that they're not supposed to hear — he'd also once heard old Mr. Tredwell down the post office say, to another old man, in the tone people use for weather: *they never should have melted her. A bell's not metal, boy. A bell's a voice. You can melt the metal, but the voice goes on the parish.*

At the time Idris had assumed this was just old-man talk, the same genre as *they don't make bread like they used to* and *the hill's moved, you know, it's three feet further east than when I was a lad.*

Lying in the dark, he revised his opinion.

* * *

He didn't sleep much, and when he did, he dreamed of a classroom.

It wasn't his classroom. It was like his classroom the way a reflection is like your face — everything in the right place and all of it wrong. Rows of desks going back further than the room should have allowed, the way a corridor in a dream goes on longer than the building. Chalk dust hanging in the air without falling. A blackboard with writing on it that he couldn't read, not because it was blurry, but because his eyes slid off it every time, the way your foot slides off the third-from-bottom stair when you expect one more step than there is.

And the desks. He counted them in the dream, because counting is what Idris did when he was frightened — he counted stairs, he counted ducks, he counted the seconds between lightning and thunder. Twenty-six desks. He was sure of it. Twenty-six, in a school where Miss Amberdale's register had twenty-six names and only twenty-five children ever sat down, in a school where the smudged name at the bottom of the register made Idris flinch every single morning like a tooth finding something cold.

In the dream, all twenty-six desks were empty.

Except they weren't, quite. The way things in dreams aren't quite empty. The way you know, without looking, that the room behind you isn't quite empty either.

He woke on Saturday morning with the duvet kicked to the floor and his breath fogging in front of his face, a small grey ghost of breath in a warm bedroom on the last Saturday of September, and on the windowpane, on the *inside* of the glass, someone had drawn a smiley face in the frost.

Idris lay there looking at it for a long time.

It wasn't scary, exactly. That was the strangest thing. It was almost — friendly. Like a joke. Like something saying *I'm here, I've been here a while, don't make a fuss.*

His nan's whistling grandad, he thought. Love with nowhere to go.

Except his nan's grandad had been *dead*, and dead things had earned their sounds. This was different. This was a bell with no bell, a voice with no throat, frost in September, and a smiley face on the inside of the glass, and Idris Fenwick, who was not brave but was extremely stubborn, sat up in bed and made a decision the way his dad made decisions about parking wardens: completely, and slightly against his own interests.

He was going to find out what the bell wanted.

He just had to work out how to do it without saying the word *cellar*, without looking up at the belfry, without telling his mum, without telling Mrs. Pell, and without being a boy who had heard it. Which left, he realized as he got dressed and pulled on a jumper even though it wasn't cold — well. It left the library. It left the parish records. It left the laminated card and the year 1911 and whatever had happened in Hollowmere that everyone was so careful never to say.

It left Miss Amberdale, actually. Because Miss Amberdale had said, on Friday, bright as anything, that next week was Local History Week, and they'd all be looking at old photographs and parish records and *the story of our village*, and she'd said it with that smile of hers that made you want to do your best work, and nobody — not one of the twenty-five — had told her that Hollowmere's story had a hole in it the exact size and shape of a melted bell.

Idris looked at the smiley face on the window, which was already fading in the morning sun, drooping at the edges like it was tired.

"Local History Week," he said out loud, testing it. It felt like picking up a stone to see what was underneath. "Right. Fine. Good."

Underneath the stone, he was fairly sure, would be the thing that tolled.

And somewhere down the lane, faint as a memory, so faint he'd spend the whole weekend wondering if he'd imagined it — he hadn't, and he knew he hadn't, and that was the whole problem — the bell rang once more. Soft, this time. Almost polite. Like something clearing its throat before class.
