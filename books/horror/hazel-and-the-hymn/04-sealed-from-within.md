## 4. Sealed from Within

*Isandro*

Corwin Holloway made the call to the excavation crew at seven in the morning, standing in the gravel drive in a dressing gown worth more than my car, and by eight the men were back with their shovels and their concrete truck like nothing had ever been found at all.

"Fill it," he said. "All of it. And if anyone asks, we hit bedrock and changed our minds about the cellar."

The foreman, a broad man named Dill who chewed his toothpick like it owed him money, looked at the door one more time before the first load of fill went in. I watched him look. He didn't touch it. Nobody had touched it since they'd broken through — not with bare hands, anyway. The crew had gone quiet around it the way people go quiet in churches, or around open caskets.

"You want to tell me what that is?" Dill asked Corwin.

"A door," Corwin said pleasantly. "Old houses have them."

"Sealed from the *inside*, though." Dill spat his toothpick into the dirt. "That's the part I don't care for."

"Then you'll be glad when it's buried again."

I stood on the terrace steps with Ambrose's morning pills in my pocket and watched them pour concrete over a thing older than the county, and I want to be honest here: part of me wanted to shout stop. Part of me wanted to walk down there and put my palm flat against that stone and see what it felt like, whether it was cold the way stone is cold or cold the way something else is cold.

But I'm a nurse. My whole career is built on knowing which doors you don't open until it's time, and more importantly, knowing that time always comes whether you knock or not.

So I let them bury it.

Ambrose slept through the entire operation. His count had slowed overnight — I'd checked him at three a.m., as I'd been doing, and heard it faint under his breath: *fourteen… thirteen… twelve…* A number so small it should have frightened me, except that he'd been counting small numbers for two days now without ever reaching the end of them, like a man pacing the last mile of a marathon and refusing to cross the line.

I sat with him after the trucks left. The house above us felt different with the door entombed — heavier, somehow, like a held breath finally exhaled wrong.

"You heard it," Ambrose said. His eyes were open. When had his eyes been open?

"Heard what?"

"The digging." He smiled at the ceiling. "It's been waiting for someone to dig. A hundred years of patience, and then some fool wants a wine cellar."

"Ambrose—"

"Hush now, nurse. I'm counting." He closed his eyes. "Eleven," he said, contented as a man naming grandchildren. "Ten."

And I got out my notebook, because that's what I do, because ritual is the only prayer I've ever trusted, and I wrote down the numbers as they came, and they came slow, and they came kind, and somewhere around *seven* I understood that he wasn't going to die today either. He was going to hover at the bottom of the arithmetic like a stone at the lip of a well.

Unless something pushed.

* * *

*Corwin*

My father was dying. This was, on paper, the most normal thing that had happened to our family in a decade, and I resented how much comfort I took in its normalcy.

People die. Fathers especially. It's practically traditional. What was not traditional was the way he did it — the numbers, the serenity, the way Vera had gone white as bone the first night she heard him counting and had looked at me across the kitchen table with an expression I recognized but had never once seen directed at me: *you knew.*

I hadn't known. Not exactly. I'd known there were things in this family you didn't discuss, the way every old family has things, except ours came with a maintenance schedule.

"It's fine," I told her. "He's ninety-one. People count down at the end. It's poetic."

"It's *scheduled,* Corwin."

"People say that about hospice all the time."

Vera set down her coffee with the deliberate care of a woman deciding not to throw it. "Your grandfather counted. You know that. Your great-aunt found the diary pages. Don't stand in my kitchen and tell me this is poetry."

I told her what I'd been raised to tell anyone who asked, the sentence my father had fed me with my first taste of scotch: *the Holloways are long-lived, and long-lived families attract superstition.* It's a good sentence. It has gotten us through doctors, journalists, one very persistent genealogist, and two marriages. But saying it to Vera felt like handing her a receipt for something she'd already paid for.

She left the kitchen. Tennyson was at the window watching the concrete trucks pull away, and when I put a hand on his shoulder he flinched, which he never used to do, and asked me the question children ask precisely once:

"Daddy, is Grandpa going to zero?"

I want it noted, for whatever record survives me, that I did not lie to my son. I said, "Everyone goes to zero eventually, Ten."

"That's not what I asked."

No. It wasn't.

* * *

*Isandro*

They finished the burial by noon. By two, Hollis the groundskeeper was up on a ladder outside the east wing, trimming the ivy off the brickwork, whistling something I couldn't place — a hymn, maybe, or half of one, the way groundskeepers whistle things, fragments worn smooth by repetition.

By four, Ambrose's count had stopped slowing down.

I was in the room when it happened, and I will describe it plainly because plainness is all I have. He opened his eyes at 4:17 in the afternoon and said, "Six," and then, after a pause no longer than a breath, "Five," and the numbers began coming faster, not panicked, not labored — *eager* was the word that came to me and I hated it — four, three, two, each one arriving like a train pulling into a station on time, and I took his hand and he squeezed mine and said, "One," and smiled.

Not grimaced. Smiled. Wide and warm and grateful, the smile of a man being handed back something he'd lent out long ago.

"One," he said again, softer. "There you are."

And then he died, between one and nothing, the way a candle dies when you cup your hand around it — not blown out, just *finished*, and the room went very quiet, and I sat there holding the hand of a man who had just smiled at whatever came next, and I did the only professional thing left to do.

I checked my watch. I wrote the time in the ledger. Under cause of death, after a long moment, I wrote: *zero.*

Then I went downstairs to make the calls, and that's when I passed the front hall and heard whistling.

Hollis was still on his ladder outside, visible through the glass, still trimming ivy. Except now the whistle had words under it, barely, a murmur threaded through the tune, and I stepped onto the terrace to hear better because eleven years of listening to dying people has ruined me for minding my own business.

"*Fourteen,*" Hollis murmured to the ivy. "*Thirteen.*"

I stood very still. The evening was warm. Somewhere a bird was making an idiot of itself.

Hollis climbed down the ladder, saw me, and tipped an imaginary hat. "Evening, miss. Sad news upstairs?"

"Ambrose passed about an hour ago."

"Mm." He nodded, unsurprised, unsurprised in the specific way of a man reading a schedule. He gathered his shears and his gloves, and as he turned to go he said, conversationally, almost kindly:

"He was a good number. Long one. Mine's shorter, but I don't mind telling you, miss — short numbers go easier." He patted his chest, right over the heart, the way you'd pat a coat pocket to confirm your keys were there. "You can hear it if you like. I know you can. You're the one who writes 'em down."

I didn't answer. I don't remember walking back inside. I remember sitting in my room with the ledger open to Ambrose's page and writing beneath *cause of death: zero* a second line, my handwriting less steady than I'd have liked:

*Transferred. Groundskeeper, H. Hollis. Begins at fourteen.*

Eleven years I'd believed the counts belonged to the dying — private mercies, sealed correspondence between a person and whatever waited for them. I had built my whole strange devotion on the idea that these numbers were intimate. That they stayed where they started.

They moved. They moved like debt moves. Like a name on a bill that gets passed down when the signer dies.

And Hollis had known. Had *thanked* me, nearly, for witnessing. As if being counted down in front of the Recorder was a courtesy the house extended, and he was gracious enough to accept it.

Outside my window, faintly, from the direction of the gardener's cottage, I could hear him whistling.

*Twelve,* said the space between the notes.

* * *

*Corwin*

I found Isandro in the library at midnight, sitting in my father's chair with her notebook, and I'll admit my first instinct was irritation. Grief makes you territorial. Also she was in my father's chair.

"You should be asleep," I said.

"So should you."

"My father died today."

"I know. I was there." She didn't close the notebook. "Corwin, Hollis is counting."

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass. I kept my face still, because I've had practice, but inside me something old and cellular leaned forward and listened.

"Counting what?"

"Backwards. From fourteen. He started the hour Ambrose died. He knows what it is. He called me — " she paused, and I watched her decide whether to give me the exact words, and decided to, because she's the sort of person who believes precision is mercy — "he called me *the one who writes them down.*"

I sat down across from her. The fire had burned to that red stage where it looks like it's thinking.

"There are things in this family," I began, and she cut me off, which nobody does.

"I've been a hospice nurse for eleven years. I have sat with two hundred and six dying people, Mr. Holloway, and I have written down every number they gave me, and until this week every single one of those people was a stranger whose blood I never traced and whose house I never entered." She turned the notebook around and pushed it across the table toward me. On the page, in her small even hand: *Transferred. Groundskeeper, H. Hollis. Begins at fourteen.* "Your father hovered at single digits for two days. Do you know why? Because the door was open. Something was deciding. Then you buried it, and within four hours the decision was made and the count moved downhill to the nearest man who works for you."

"The door was a hole in the ground."

"The door was *sealed from the inside,* Corwin. Somebody stood where you're standing a hundred years ago and chose to stay behind that stone, and locked it after themselves, and your family has spent a century pretending that doesn't mean anything." Her voice didn't rise. That was the worst part. It stayed level and tired and precise, a nurse's voice, the voice of a woman who has held too many hands to be impressed by anything except patterns. "I bury people for a living. I know what it looks like when a death is natural and I know what it looks like when a death is *on time.* Your father died on time. Hollis is going to die on time. And somebody in this house knows the timetable."

I could have lied. I want to be clear that lying was available to me; it usually is. I'm good at it. It's practically the family trade — we deal in plausible deniability the way other estates deal in timber.

But she was looking at me with my father's numbers still wet on the page, and something in me — something I'd have sworn under oath I didn't have — flinched away from adding her to the list of people I'd managed.

"I need you to keep doing your job," I said instead. "Care for Hollis. Write down whatever you write down. And Isandro — don't go near the cellar again."

"It's full of concrete."

"Good." I stood. At the doorway I paused, because fair is fair, and said: "My father smiled at the end. Did you write that down too?"

Her jaw tightened. "Yes."

"Then you already understand more than you're letting on." I left her there with the ledger and the dying fire, and I climbed the stairs past my son's dark bedroom, past my wife's closed door, and I stood in the hallway listening to the house settle around its hundred-year-old secret, and I told myself the oldest lie in the family inventory:

*This generation, it stops with the help. It never touches us.*

Downstairs, faint as a pulse through a wall, I could almost hear Hollis counting.

*Ten.*

*Nine.*

And underneath it, so faint I might have invented it, something else — not a voice, not quite. More like the house itself, patient as arithmetic, keeping its own books.

Waiting to see who paid, and who merely signed.
