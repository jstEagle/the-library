## 8. The Creditor

The box arrived at 11:47 p.m., forty minutes before airtime, and it smelled like a courtroom.

Orsen noticed the smell before they noticed anything else—old paper and dust and something sharper underneath, something like iron gall ink, the kind they used to make from wasp galls and rust. It came up out of the cardboard in a slow exhale, as if the box had been holding its breath for sixty years and had finally decided to let go.

The return address was typed: **ESTATE OF A. WREN — HOLD FOR THE CURRENT RESIDENT.** No street number. No stamp. Just a strip of brown paper tape across the flap, gone amber with age, that someone had licked shut sometime before Orsen was born.

They should have waited. They knew that even then, standing in the station's mail room with the fluorescent tube buzzing overhead and eleven doors between them and the parking lot—one more than there should have been—they knew that opening a package addressed to *the current resident* by an estate that had been settled decades ago was exactly the kind of thing you did not do alone at night in this building.

But it was addressed to them. Specifically, impossibly, to them. And Orsen Fairweather had spent three weeks being told things by dead people over the phone lines, and somewhere in those three weeks a line had worn through, the way a rope wears through on a sharp edge, and on the other side of that line was a simple, terrible belief: *the house is talking to me, so I might as well listen.*

They cut the tape with a letter opener shaped like a sword. The blade went through the old adhesive with a sound like a small sigh.

Inside, wrapped in newsprint yellowed to the color of weak tea, was a ledger. Leather covers, cracked at the spine, corners worn round and soft as river stones. The word **DIARY** was too small for it—it was a day book, a working thing, the kind a man carries in his coat pocket and fills with appointments and figures and grievances. Stamped into the cover in faded gilt: **A.W.**

And beneath the ledger, folded into quarters, a single sheet of modern typing paper. Someone—or something—had added a note recently. The ink was still glossy.

*He wanted it read aloud. That's why he wrote it down. You know how testimony works by now.*

Orsen stood very still in the mail room. The fluorescents buzzed. Somewhere deep in the building, in a wing where nobody had worked since the renovation, a door closed—not slammed, just closed, politely, the way a butler leaves a room.

"Okay," Orsen said to the empty air, because talking to the empty air had become a professional skill lately. "Okay. Fine."

They carried the diary to the studio.

* * *

The ON AIR sign glowed its familiar red. 12:00:00 a.m. The board lit up green. Orsen leaned into the microphone, and their voice did what it always did—dropped half a register, found the warm place, the place that made insomniacs feel less alone in the dark.

"You're listening to *Nightwatch*, channel nine, broadcasting from the old Wren house on Calder Street. It's midnight, friends. Coffee's hot, lines are open." A beat. "I want to tell you about something I got in the mail tonight."

They unwrapped the ledger on the desk, in view of the studio camera that fed the low-band stream—the one Pip watched on a cracked phone propped against a cereal bowl. Orsen had learned, these past weeks, that saying things plainly was better than saying them cleverly. The dead didn't appreciate cleverness. Neither, apparently, did whatever else was listening.

"This is a diary. Judge Aldous Wren's diary. For the new listeners—and I know we've gotten some new listeners, hello, I see you in the chat—Judge Wren owned this house before it was a radio station. He lived here his whole life. And on the night of March the ninth, 1961, he—" Orsen paused. The station's official history said *died suddenly*. The town's unofficial history said other things, quieter things, said in hardware stores and funeral parlors with a glance toward the door. "He died here. The night before he was scheduled to sign away the town's water rights. This is his handwriting. I'm going to read some of it to you."

They opened to the first marked page—a ribbon bookmark, water-stained, already laid where someone wanted it laid.

*"March 2nd, 1961. Mr. Vane called again today. He does not use a telephone. He simply appears in my study at four o'clock, which is when my father used to appear, and sits in my father's chair, and waits for me to speak first. I am beginning to understand that this is the whole of his method. He never speaks first. He lets you hang yourself with your own words, and then he offers you scissors."*

Orsen read slowly. On the board, line one blinked. Then line two. Then line three, four, five—the switchboard waking up the way it did now, all at once, like birds startled off a wire.

"I'm going to keep going," Orsen told the blinking lights. "You can call in after. I know some of you have been trying to reach me all week. I hear you. But tonight I think you need to hear him first."

*"March 3rd. He came again. I asked him—I made myself ask him—what he looked like. He said, 'What would be useful?' I said, 'That isn't an answer.' He said, 'It is the only honest one. Faces are for people who can be refused to their face.' I looked at where his face should be and I saw the room behind him, perfectly, as though he were a window dressed in a suit. I have not slept since Tuesday."*

In the chat window, Pip's handle appeared: **PIPWIRE: orsen. orsen is that real. orsen blink twice.**

"Blinking twice, Pip," Orsen said, and heard their own voice shake slightly, and let it shake, because the audience of the living deserved honesty and the audience of the dead, apparently, fed on something else entirely.

*"March 5th. The offer, at last, stated plainly. He says every debt in this town can be forgiven tonight. Every mortgage, every lien, every medical bill, every IOU scrawled on a napkin at the Elks lodge. He has them all. He showed me a book—he showed me a BOOK, pages of names and sums, going back generations, sums no living accountant could have tallied. He calls himself a creditor. I asked him who owed him. He said, 'Everyone who ever wanted something they couldn't afford. Which is everyone. Which is why I am patient.'"*

Orsen turned the page. The handwriting changed here—looser, the letters leaning like drunks supporting each other. Three days of no sleep lived in that slant.

*"March 6th. The price. One signature. Mine. Not the town's—the papers for the water rights were only ever the visible part, the part the bank could understand. What he actually wants is my name on HIS document, and with it, he says, the authority of this house passes to him, and everything the house holds—its records, its claims, its people—passes with it. I asked him what happens to the people. He smiled. I want to write that he smiled, though I could not see a mouth, and I will spend the rest of my life trying to un-see it. He said, 'Nothing happens TO them. They simply owe me. As they always have. I am merely collecting what is already mine.'"*

Line seven on the switchboard. Line eight. The chat scrolling too fast to read. Marisol's handle surfaced once—**NIGHTSHIFT_M: keep reading**—and vanished back under the flood.

*"March 7th. I consulted the statutes. I have been a judge for nineteen years; I know what a contract looks like, and his document is beautiful, I will grant him that. Consideration recited, parties named, terms plain. Except for one thing. There is a blank line where the grantor signs, and above it, in letters so small you'd need a jeweler's glass, a condition: THE AUTHORITY OF THIS INSTRUMENT DERIVES FROM ITS EXECUTION. Meaning: none of it is real until someone signs. Meaning: the entire edifice—his book of debts, his patience, his sixty years of showing up at four o'clock—is a structure built on the hope that a man will pick up a pen."*

Orsen stopped. In the soundproofed room, the silence had texture, like felt. The refrigerator hum in the break room two doors down seemed to lean closer, listening.

"He figured it out," Orsen said quietly, to the microphone, to the dark, to whoever needed it said. "In 1961, sitting right where I'm sitting, he figured out that the whole thing was hollow. That the emperor didn't just have no clothes. He had no *body*."

They turned the page.

*"March 8th. He knows I know. He sat in my father's chair for two hours this afternoon and said nothing at all, and the nothing was worse than the offers. When he left—he does not leave, he becomes absent, the way a tooth becomes absent from your mouth—you notice the gap, the tongue keeps going to it—he left the document on my desk. I have put it in the drawer. I have locked the drawer. I know this is like locking a drawer against weather."*

The next entry was dated March 9th. The last day.

Orsen's hand hovered over the page. On the switchboard, every line was lit now. All eight. Plus the ninth line that wasn't supposed to exist, glowing a soft, patient amber, the color of lamplight through curtains.

*"March 9th. Tomorrow morning I was to sign the water rights over to Harrow County Development, and the money would have cleared the town's debts to the bank, and everyone would have called it a good day's work, and he would have been inside the signature like a splinter inside a glove. So instead I have spent today writing. Not a confession. A finding. If anyone ever reads this: I have entered a judgment. The claim is void. It was void before I ever met him, void ab initio, because a debt that cannot show its origin cannot be collected, and his book has no first page. I have written it all down in proper form, sworn and dated, and I have hidden it where he will not look, because he does not read, friends—he only counts signatures.*

*I have also written to my sister in Cedar Falls, and enclosed—*

The entry stopped. Mid-sentence. The ink trailed off in a long thinning stroke, the pen lifting, or the hand failing, or—

Orsen stared at the unfinished line. The ribbon bookmark lay across the gutter like a hairline fracture.

"That's where it ends," they said. Their voice sounded far away to themselves, broadcast-delayed, a stranger's voice coming back over a satellite hop. "That's the last entry. March ninth, 1961. The coroner's report called it—" They swallowed. "They found him in the morning. Upstairs. The papers for the water rights were on his desk, unsigned. Whatever he hid, wherever he hid the judgment he wrote—" 

They looked up, and the studio camera's tally light burned red, steady, faithful.

"—nobody ever found it. Sixty years. And the thing he wrote about, the creditor, the collector, whatever we want to call the polite man with no face who shows up at four o'clock and lets you talk yourself into the noose—" Orsen's jaw tightened. "It's still waiting for that signature. It's been waiting in this house for sixty years. And three weeks ago, somebody moved in who has authority over the building. Somebody whose name is on the lease."

The chat had gone almost silent. One message, pinned by Pip, repeated over and over:

**PIPWIRE: ORSEN GET OUT OF THE HOUSE. ORSEN. GET OUT.**

"I can't, Pip," Orsen said, and was surprised to find they meant it—not as despair, but as fact, the way you can't leave a courtroom in the middle of a verdict. "Because here's the thing I keep coming back to. He wrote a judgment. A real one, in proper form. Void ab initio. And it was never delivered, never filed, never read into any record—because filing required a signature, and he wouldn't give one, and so his own weapon stayed sheathed. Sixty years, that judgment has been sitting in this house like a loaded gun in a wall."

Orsen looked at the ninth line on the switchboard. Amber. Patient. It had been lit for six minutes without ringing.

"So tonight I'm making a promise on air, to everyone listening, living or otherwise. I'm going to find what Aldous Wren hid. And when I find it, I'm going to read every word of it into this microphone, into the public record, into whatever passes for a record at four in the morning—and we're going to finish what he started."

The ninth line rang.

Once. Soft. Like a bell in another room.

Orsen looked at it for a long moment. Then, deliberately, they reached over and flipped the toggle that routed line nine straight to voicemail.

"Not tonight," they said. "Tonight we read."

Behind them, reflected faintly in the dark glass of the control room window, the studio door—door number eleven, the one that hadn't existed last month—stood very slightly ajar.

And from the hallway beyond it, unhurried, came the sound of footsteps practicing someone's walk.
