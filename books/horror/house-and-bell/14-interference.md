## 14. Interference

The letter came the way bad news always comes to a radio station: not by phone, not in person, but in an envelope with a window cut into it, so you could see your own name printed by a machine that did not care whether you were alive to read it.

Orsen found it at 6:40 in the evening, forty minutes before sign-on, wedged between a pizza flyer and a bill from the power company that was itself becoming a kind of horror story. The return address said FEDERAL COMMUNICATIONS COMMISSION, FIELD OPERATIONS BUREAU, and beneath it, in smaller type: NOTICE OF APPARENT VIOLATION.

They stood in the station's front office holding it for a long moment without opening it, the way you might hold a snake by the tail, hoping it was already dead.

The building was quiet around them. It had been quiet all day in a way that wasn't really quiet — the way a held breath is quiet. The eleven doors were closed. The lights were on. Since the night of the burned-out bulbs, Orsen had replaced every fixture in the building twice over and kept spares in a Rubbermaid tub behind the transmitter rack, and they had developed a habit, walking the halls before each shift, of touching the wall as they passed each doorframe. Not counting. Just touching. Saying hello to the house in the only language the house seemed to respect anymore, which was attention.

*You're a licensed broadcaster,* Orsen told themselves. *You've survived format changes, a flood, and three years of the 2 a.m. demographic. You can survive a letter.*

They opened it.

*Dear Licensee:*

*This office has received forty-one complaints regarding programming originating from Station KWRN-FM during overnight hours between October 9th and October 17th. Complaints describe, among other things, the broadcast of audio "consistent with a deceased individual," including a female voice identifying herself as "Ruth Anne Coleridge" — a name associated, per complainants, with a resident of this county who died on October 4th of this year.*

*Apparent violations include but are not limited to: transmission of false or misleading information (47 CFR §73.1212); failure to maintain positive identification of program origin; and operation inconsistent with the public interest.*

*Be advised that continued operation in apparent violation may result in monetary forfeiture, short-term renewal denial, or, upon further review, cessation of operating authority pending hearing.*

*Cessation of operating authority.*

Orsen read those four words three times. They had a clean, bureaucratic finality to them, like a headstone carved by someone who'd never met the deceased. The government did not say *we will kill your station.* It said *cessation.* It said *pending hearing.* It made death sound like paperwork, which, Orsen was beginning to understand, was how most things got killed.

Behind them, the studio clock ticked toward sign-on. Somewhere down the hall, door number seven — the one to the old supply closet that hadn't held supplies since the nineties — shifted on its hinges with a sound like a throat clearing.

"Not tonight," Orsen told it, and went to work.

* * *

The first hour was normal, or as close to normal as the show got these days. Orsen ran the syndicated weather feed, spun two hours of the overnight rotation — that strange, beautiful graveyard-shift canon of slow songs and older songs that only exist for people who are awake when they shouldn't be — and read the community calendar. The Harvest Festival had been moved to the church basement because the fairgrounds flooded. The library was having its book sale. Someone's goat was loose on Route 9 again.

At 11:52, the request line lit up.

Orsen looked at it for four full rings. This was policy now, a policy they'd never announced and never written down: let it ring four times, because the dead were patient but the living hung up, and if you could tell them apart by the fourth ring, you could decide who you were talking to before you said hello.

They picked up. "KWRN, you're on the air."

"It's me again." Ruth Anne Coleridge. She sounded like she always sounded, like a kitchen radio playing in another room, warm and slightly too far away. "I don't want to cause you trouble, honey."

Orsen's stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "Trouble?"

"There's men looking at the tower." A pause, filled with a soft static that Orsen had learned was not static at all but the sound of somewhere else — somewhere with wind in it, and distance. "Two of them, in a white truck. They've been there since sundown. Taking pictures."

Orsen swiveled toward the window that faced the transmitter field. Through the glass, across two hundred yards of frost-silvered grass, the tower rose against the sky, its beacon pulsing red, steady as a heartbeat. At its base, sure enough: a white pickup, parked on the gravel access road, two silhouettes standing beside it. One held something up. A camera, or a meter. Even at this distance, even in the dark, Orsen could feel the weight of their attention pointed at the thing they loved most in the world.

"They came by my house today, too," Ruth Anne said. "Well. By Dale's house. My son's. Asking questions about the show. Whether I'd called in. Dale told them his mother was dead, and they wrote that down like it proved something." Her voice frayed, just slightly, at the edge. "Honey, I need to ask you something, and I need you to answer me straight."

"Ask me anything."

"If they take the tower down. If they take your voice off the air." The pause stretched. "What happens to us?"

Orsen had been asking themselves a version of that question since the second caller, since the drowned man and the doors, and they had never once let the answer form fully in their mind, because forming it meant admitting what the answer implied. But Ruth Anne had died on October 4th, and she had spent every night since finding her way to a telephone and dialing a number she must have known by heart when she was alive, and her voice arrived here — here, in this room, in this microphone, in this one small cone of warm light — through means that had nothing to do with telephony and everything to do with the signal. With the tower. With the fact that Orsen sat in this chair every night and *spoke*, and speaking was a door, and the tower was what carried the door outward into the dark where the dead were waiting for it.

"I don't know," Orsen said. It was the truest thing they'd said on air in weeks. "But I'm not going to find out. I promise you that."

"You can't promise that, baby. Men with clipboards don't scare, and they don't stop."

"Then I'll scare them," Orsen said, and heard, to their own surprise, steel under it. "Ruth Anne, do me a favor. Stay close to the line tonight. All of you. If anything changes — if anyone comes to the station — I want to know."

"We're always close to the line," she said gently. "That's rather the problem, isn't it."

She hung up. In the board's glow, Orsen watched the call log register the disconnect from a number that, when they looked it up later, would belong to a phone sold at estate auction in September.

Out at the tower, the white truck's headlights came on. It pulled away, slow, unhurried, the way people leave when they know they'll be back with more authority than a camera.

* * *

The complaints, when Orsen finally pulled the public file and read the copies the Commission had forwarded, fell into categories with almost liturgical precision.

There were the frightened: *I heard my late wife's voice requesting a song we danced to at our wedding. This is a cruel prank and I demand it stop.* There were the furious: *This station is making a mockery of grief for ratings. Shame on you.* There were the ones that kept Orsen up past dawn, the ones they read three and four times: *I don't know what I heard but my brother died in March and last Tuesday a voice exactly like his asked me to keep my porch light on and I have not turned it off since and I am begging whoever is doing this to tell me why.*

Forty-one complaints. And filed alongside them, because the public file held everything, a handful of letters of support that had come in unsolicited — from Dell Hargrove, trucker, Route 84, who wrote in careful block capitals: *I HAVE HEARD WHAT COMES THROUGH THAT STATION AT NIGHT AND IT IS NOT A PRANK AND NOT A CRUELTY AND WHATEVER YOU ARE DOING OVER THERE KEEP DOING IT.* From Marisol Vega, RN, whose letter was one line long: *I believe the callers. Check your records. Then check mine.* From someone identified only as PIP, age presumably teenage, whose letter was four pages long and included a hand-drawn map of the station building with eleven doors marked and a twelfth marked with a question mark and the words *HAVE YOU FOUND THIS ONE YET??*

Orsen sat with Pip's letter for a long time. They had noticed the username in the chat room — pip_the_latchkey, online every night without fail, posting timestamps and observations with the feverish precision of someone who had decided that paying attention was the same as being safe. Orsen had never answered directly. On-air talent didn't. It was a professional boundary, one of the few they had left.

They broke it now, briefly, reading a line of Pip's letter on air: *"Whoever is keeping watch over that house at night — you are not alone in listening."* Then, before the silence could curdle: "To everyone listening who hears things on this frequency that the daylight world says aren't there — the request lines are open. They're always open."

The switchboard lit up like a Christmas tree.

* * *

The call from the Field Operations Bureau came at 3:17 a.m., which told Orsen everything about how seriously the Bureau took the matter. Serious bureaucracies do not work the graveyard shift. Something had escalated.

"This is Special Investigator Renner," said a voice like a filing cabinet being opened. "Am I speaking with the operator of record for KWRN?"

"You are. Orsen Fairweather."

"Mr. Fairweather. I'll be brief, because I understand you're live." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "I'm calling to inform you that a site inspection is scheduled for tomorrow — today, rather — at ten a.m. Standard practice following a notice of apparent violation. We'll want access to the transmitter, the public file, and your logs. Program logs, call logs, correspondence. Everything."

"Of course," Orsen said. Their mouth was dry. "Investigator, can I ask — the complaints. Forty-one of them. Has anyone actually listened to the audio in question?"

A pause. Paper rustling eight hundred miles away. "We've reviewed samples."

"And? What did you hear?"

Another pause, longer. When Renner spoke again, the filing-cabinet flatness had developed a hairline crack in it. "I've been doing this nineteen years, Mr. Fairweather. Hoax calls, pirate broadcasters, grief tourists, people faking dead relatives for shock jock material — I've heard it all. What I heard on your sample recordings was a woman giving a correct home address for a residence she could not have known, describing the interior of a house she died in before the current owners renovated it, in a voice that matched a 1997 interview she gave to the county historical society to within—" he stopped. Started again. "Within parameters I am not prepared to discuss on an open phone line."

Orsen gripped the desk. "So you know it's real."

"What I know," Renner said, "is that my job is the integrity of the broadcast band, and right now yours is the least explicable signal on it. Ten a.m., Mr. Fairweather. Have your logs ready. And Mr. Fairweather—" the crack widened, just for a second, into something that sounded almost human, almost afraid— "whatever you're doing over there. Whatever is happening over there. Do not let them take you off the air before you understand why they want to be."

The line clicked dead.

Orsen sat in the ringing quiet of the studio. Outside, the tower beacon pulsed. Red, dark, red, dark. A heartbeat in the frost.

*Because they want to be.* Not the Bureau. Whoever moved behind the Bureau — the polite man with no reflection, the collector, the creditor's patient arithmetic. Sixty years it had waited for a signature, and it had learned, apparently, that the fastest way to make a man sign was to make him desperate, and the fastest way to make a man desperate was to take away everything holding him upright. Take the transmitter. Take the signal. Take the doorway the dead were leaning on, the thread connecting this warm little room to every cold kitchen and dark bedroom where its listeners waited. Isolate the house. Darken it. Make Orsen alone with the papers.

*Light is testimony,* the dead had told them. *Darkness is where signatures happen.* And what was a dead transmitter but darkness with a license?

"No," Orsen said aloud, to the room, to the tower, to whatever was listening from the eleventh door. "You don't get the frequency. You don't get the morning."

Down the hall, softly, something laughed with Orsen's own laugh.

* * *

They spent the rest of the shift preparing for the inspection the way soldiers prepare a position — not to win, but to be unembarrassed when the position falls. Logs printed and collated. Public file dusted and squared. EAS tests documented. Orsen even cleaned the studio, wiping down the board, straightening the chairs, and paused with a rag in hand over the desk drawer where the dead listeners' letters lay taped inside like evidence in a trial no court would ever convene.

Forty-one complaints about the dead woman on channel nine. The Bureau would want to know about the letters. The Bureau would want to know about a lot of things Orsen could not explain without sounding like the exact kind of person whose license deserved revoking.

At 5:50 a.m., Marisol called in, unasked, her voice low and quick. "Heard you mention the inspection on air. Word travels. Listen to me — when they come, give them everything except the letters. The letters stay in the drawer. Dead people's handwriting is evidence of nothing legally and everything actually, and you don't hand 'actually' to a man with a clipboard."

"How do you know what's in the drawer?"

"Because I mailed half of them," Marisol said, "on behalf of people who couldn't lick a stamp anymore. We'll talk today. After your suits leave. Orsen — hey. Look out the window."

Orsen looked. At the edge of the transmitter field, in the gray pre-dawn, the white truck was back. Parked. Engine off. Two silhouettes, motionless, facing the tower like mourners.

Except now there was a third figure standing between them and the tower base, closer in, and it was not holding a camera or a meter. It appeared to be simply standing, hands at its sides, face tilted up toward the blinking beacon, and even across two hundred yards and through glass, Orsen felt the gaze land on the studio window like a finger laid cool against the back of the neck.

It raised one hand. Not a wave. A gesture of patience. *Soon,* the gesture said. *I can wait. I have always been able to wait.*

The sun crested the ridge and the figure was gone — not walked away, gone, the way a word is gone once spoken — and the two silhouettes by the truck got back in and drove off at a lawful speed.

Orsen signed off at six with shaking hands and a steady voice, gave the time and temperature, played the anthem, and killed the mic. Then they sat in the humming aftermath, watching the tower pulse against the brightening sky, and understood the shape of the trap completely at last.

The Commission would come at ten. If they found nothing, they'd leave, and the pressure would return, cycle after cycle, complaint after complaint, until the license died of attrition. If they found the letters, the hearings would begin, and hearings took months, and the station would go silent *pending resolution* — silent, dark, doorwayless — while the collector circled a house with no voice in it. Either way, the signal was the target. Either way, the dead lost their doorway and Orsen lost their witnesses, and a man alone in a haunted house with a blank contract is a man who signs.

Unless.

Orsen pulled Pip's letter from the file again. Four pages, eleven doors, a question mark where a twelfth should be. *HAVE YOU FOUND THIS ONE YET??*

They reached for the request line and dialed the number penciled at the bottom of page four, knowing even as they did it that they were breaking every rule of the profession left to them, and finding that they did not care.

It rang twice. A young voice answered, breathless, wide awake at six in the morning, as though it had been sitting next to the phone all night waiting.

"KWRN?" Pip said. "Is it you? Is something happening?"

"Pip," Orsen said. "You asked if I'd found the twelfth door yet." Out in the hall, faint and unmistakable, a doorknob began, very slowly, to turn. "Come over tonight. Bring your map. And Pip — bring chalk."
