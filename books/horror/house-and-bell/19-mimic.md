## 19. Mimic

Pip had been told, in no uncertain terms, to stay home.

*Stay home,* Orsen had said, hand on Pip's shoulder in the studio's yellow light, the word *home* doing something strange in their mouth, like they were trying it on. *You did your part. You counted the doors. Now let the adults handle the haunted radio station.*

Which was a joke, probably. The kind of joke people made when they were terrified and didn't want to say so. Pip had nodded and biked back through the wet dark at three in the morning with the wind pushing at their back like a hand between the shoulder blades, and they had made it inside, and they had locked the door, and they had lain on top of their blankets fully dressed listening to the broadcast on the little transistor radio their grandmother left them, because the house phone was a thing they no longer trusted.

The show sounded wrong tonight. Not bad-wrong. Not dead-air wrong. It sounded like someone reading a letter they'd memorized instead of one they'd written. Orsen was playing requests — old stuff, stuff from before Pip was born, songs about highways and heartbreak — and between the songs Orsen talked, and the talking was smooth and warm and completely empty, like a mannequin wearing Orsen's voice the way you'd wear a coat.

Pip sat up.

They knew Orsen's rhythms by now. Three nights of listening will do that; three nights of a voice coming through the dark into your bedroom will do that faster than years of knowing someone in daylight. Real Orsen hesitated before saying anything true. Real Orsen filled silences with small sounds — the click of the fader, the tap of a pen, a low hum that wasn't quite a tune. Real Orsen said *you* like it meant somebody specific.

This voice said *you* like it meant everybody, which meant nobody.

"Okay," Pip whispered to the empty room. "Okay, okay, okay."

They grabbed the transistor and their phone and their shoes, and then stood in the middle of the floor not putting the shoes on, because Dell had texted the group chat forty minutes ago: *STAY WHERE YOU ARE. Something's walking around the station wearing his walk. Marisol's on her way. DO NOT COME DOWN HERE.*

And now the voice on the radio was smooth and warm and empty, and either Orsen had finally cracked under it — which, honestly, fair — or the thing in the dark wings of the house had gotten better at its homework.

Pip put the shoes on anyway. They told themselves it was just in case. They told themselves a lot of things just in case.

* * *

At 3:47 a.m., the radio went quiet mid-song.

Not static. Not dead air. Quiet — the specific, held-breath quiet of a room where someone has stopped talking because someone else has entered it. Then Orsen's voice came back, close to the mic, intimate, the volume turned down like a secret:

"Pip. I know you're listening. You always are."

Pip's whole body went cold and hot at once.

"You left your notebook," the voice said gently. "Blue cover, coffee ring on the corner. You left it on the desk by the board. Come get it, kiddo. Door eleven's sticking again — I can't reach the latch from here. Just open it for me and we'll both go home."

Door eleven.

Pip's hands were shaking so hard they nearly dropped the phone. Because here was the thing, the terrible arithmetic of it: the notebook was real. Blue cover, coffee ring, left on the desk — all real, all details only Orsen could know, unless the thing had learned to read while it practiced walking. Every fact it offered was true, and every fact made it worse, because it proved how much it had watched, how long it had studied, how carefully it had catalogued the small furniture of their lives.

That was the trick of it, Pip understood suddenly, sitting on the edge of their bed in the dark. It didn't lie. Lying was crude. It just arranged the truth into a shape you'd walk toward.

The phone buzzed. A call. **ORSEN** on the screen, because of course it was, because the thing had had all night to learn what names looked like when they lit up a screen.

Pip stared at it ringing. Six rings. Seven. On eight, some animal part of them answered, because if there was even a chance it was really Orsen —

"Pip." The voice was perfect. That was the horror of it — not rough, not distorted, not buzzing with wrongness the way movie monsters sounded. It was *perfect*, warm and tired and fond, Orsen's exact cadence, the little upward lilt they got when they were worried about you. "Hey. Thanks for picking up. Listen, I hate to ask—"

"Where are you?" Pip managed.

"The hallway outside the studio. Power flickered and the door swung shut behind me and the latch is being temperamental. I'd knock but my hands are full — I've got the work lights." A soft laugh, so exactly right that Pip felt tears start stupidly behind their eyes. "Eleven's the problem. It sticks. You know how it sticks. Just come open it, kiddo, and we'll laugh about this over pancakes."

Kiddo. Orsen called them kiddo. Once, on night two, half-asleep on air, and had apologized for it immediately, ears going red on camera-less camera, and Pip had secretly loved it and never said so.

It knew that too. Of course it knew that too. It had been listening to everything, all of it, every frequency, every frequency of them.

"I can't," Pip said. "I'm — I'm supposed to stay home."

A pause. Half a second too long. When the voice came back it was still warm, but underneath the warmth something shifted its weight, like a large animal redistributing itself.

"Home." It tasted the word. "Pip, sweetheart, it's cold out there in the dark by yourself. And I'm right here. Eleven's the last door between us. One latch. That's all. That's such a small thing to ask."

"That's what you said to him," Pip whispered. "To Judge Wren. Tea. One cup. Such a small thing to ask."

Silence on the line. Long enough that Pip checked whether the call had dropped.

When the voice returned, it hadn't changed at all, and that was the most frightening thing yet. No anger. No mask slipping. Just patience, vast and courteous, the patience of an entity that had waited sixty years and could wait sixty more.

"Clever Pip," it said fondly. "Reading the diary. Doing the homework. He said no too, you know. At first. They all say no at first. And then morning comes, and morning is such a long way off, and everyone gets tired of being brave eventually. Even judges. Especially judges." A gentle sigh. "Your brother wouldn't have wanted you alone in the dark, Pip."

The world went white at the edges.

Because that — that wasn't in any diary. That wasn't in the chat logs or the letters or the show. Pip had mentioned Danny exactly once, three weeks ago, calling in to request a song, voice cracking on the third word, and Orsen had played it without comment and said afterward, off-air, *for whoever Danny was, he had good taste in little siblings.* That was all. One sentence, in a soundproofed room, to one person.

One person who was currently standing in a hallway asking Pip to open a door.

Or one person-shaped thing that had eaten the person and was wearing what was left.

"No," Pip said. Their voice came out tiny. They made it bigger. "No. I'm not opening anything. And Orsen would never say that about Danny. Never. So whoever — whatever you are —"

"But I'm right here," the voice said, wounded, reasonable. "Pip. Look outside your window."

Pip looked. They hated themselves for looking. Across the street, at the corner where the streetlight had been dead since Tuesday, something stood under the broken lamp. Tall. Patient. In the starless light it had the outline of a person the way a glove has the outline of a hand — filled, but wrong, the proportions slightly generous, as if drawn by someone working from a description of a human rather than a human itself.

It raised one arm. Slowly. Courteously. And waved.

Pip slammed the curtain shut and backed away and their heel caught the transistor radio cord and yanked it from the wall, and in the sudden silence of the room they heard — faintly, impossibly, from the dead radio, from the unplugged dead radio — Orsen's voice, thin as thread:

"—kiddo? Kiddo, if you can hear me, it's not me. Whatever's talking to you, it's not me. Don't open eleven. Say the line. Say the opening line, say it back to me, that's how you'll know—"

Static swallowed it.

*Say the opening line.*

Pip stood in the middle of their dark room shaking, and thought: *the opening line.* Forty years of broadcasts. Orsen opened every single show the same way, every night, the one ritual they'd never broken, the thing regulars set their clocks by, the thing that had made Pip feel, the first time they heard it, like being let in somewhere warm.

The phone rang again. **ORSEN** glowing in the dark.

Pip answered. Didn't speak. Held the phone like a live grenade and waited.

"Come on now," the voice said, honey-smooth, a thread of strain running through it now, fine as wire. "Enough games. Open door eleven, and I'll bring your brother's voice back to you every night. Isn't that what you want? Someone to say goodnight?"

And Pip — crying now, furious, fourteen years old and holding the line against something older than the town — asked it the only question that mattered:

"What does he say? Every night. What's the first thing out of the speakers?"

A pause. The pause stretched. Somewhere in it, Pip heard the thing searching — riffling through everything it had gathered, every broadcast, every call, every recorded hour, looking for a sentence that had never once been spoken on air, never written down, never said anywhere except—

Except to the four people in that studio. Except in the room itself. Off the air. Unrecorded. Ungathered.

"Open the door," the voice said, "and I'll tell you."

"There is no opening line," Pip said. "That's the answer. There isn't one. He starts every show with silence — ten seconds of dead air, on purpose, so the town knows the mic's live and nobody's hiding. He told us that Tuesday night, in the studio, off-air. *Ten seconds of nothing, so they know it's really me.*"

The silence on the other end changed quality. It went from patient to hungry to, very briefly, almost respectful.

Then, softly, in a voice that was no longer pretending to be anyone's: "Clever Pip."

The call ended.

* * *

Pip didn't remember deciding to go. That was the honest truth they'd carry afterward — that there was no decision, just a body in motion, shoes already tied, bike already out of the shed, the wet night swallowing them whole. Dell had said stay away. Marisol had said stay away. Orsen's stolen mouth had said stay away, which should have counted double.

But the real Orsen was in there. The real Orsen had found a way to push a message through a dead radio, which meant the real Orsen was alive and improvising and losing ground, and Pip had spent their whole life being the kid everyone protected, the kid whose grief was handled for them, the kid kept out of rooms. Danny used to do it too. *Stay here, Pip. Watch my bike.*

Danny had stayed watching the bike.

Pip pedaled through the sleeping streets with the rain starting, past the dead streetlight where the thing had stood — the corner was empty now, mercifully, or worse than empty, hard to say — and up the hill to the station, where every window was black except the studio's, one square of gold burning in the dark house like a lit porthole on a sinking ship.

The front door was unlocked. That was wrong. Orsen locked everything now; locking things had become a religion. Pip stood on the threshold breathing rain-smell and heard, from deep inside the house, footsteps. Steady. Unhurried. Practiced.

Practiced. That was the word their brain supplied and refused to take back. These were footsteps that had been rehearsed.

"Pip?" Orsen's voice floated down the main hall, warm, relieved, perfect. "Thank God. Get in, get in — quick, the hall door, number eleven, it's open, I'm right through it—"

Pip stepped inside. Closed the front door. Leaned against it.

Down the length of the hallway, at the far end, door eleven stood ajar. Through the gap: darkness, and a smell of ink and rain, and a shape standing just inside the blackness with its arms open the way you'd open your arms for someone you loved.

"Pip," the shape said kindly, in Orsen's voice, from Orsen's throat, with Orsen's tired warmth. "You came. Good. Good kiddo. Just one door now. One little latch."

Pip's hand was already on the banister. Their feet had carried them three steps down the hall before their mind caught up, and they stopped, and they stood in the dark hallway shaking, fourteen years old, soaked in rain, staring at the open arms of the thing that wore their friend.

And from behind the closed door of the studio — the gold-lit door, the real door — muffled, urgent, unmistakably hoarse from shouting, came a voice that was *not* perfect, that cracked in the middle, that had gravel and panic and humanity in it:

"*Pip, don't you dare — ten seconds of dead air, kiddo, tell it why — TELL IT WHY—*"

Pip started to cry. And laughed. And cried harder.

"Because," they said out loud, to the hallway, to the shape, to the whole listening dark, "the show starts with ten seconds of nothing. So everyone knows the mic is live. So everyone knows nobody's hiding." They wiped their face with a soaked sleeve. "He told us that in the studio. Off-air. You weren't there. You'll *never* be there, because you don't get let in."

The shape in the doorway went very still.

And then, from everywhere in the house at once — from the walls, the stairs, the pipes, the space behind the wallpaper — came a sound like a page turning. Like a vast ledger being consulted. Like something checking its figures and finding, for the first time in sixty years, an entry it could not collect.

The shape's arms lowered slowly. Its head tilted — too far, much too far, the tilt of something consulting a different anatomy — and when it spoke again it used no one's voice at all, just a dry rustle, a courtesy stripped down to its bolts:

"Noted," it said. "Filed."

Door eleven swung shut on its own, soft as a book closing.

And behind Pip, from the studio, the real lock — the one Orsen had installed themselves, the one with the key on a chain around their neck — clicked open, and Orsen stood in the gold light with a fire axe in one hand and tears on their face, and said, wrecked and laughing, "You absolute idiot. You beautiful idiot. Get in here."

Pip got in there.

The lock clicked shut behind them, and for one whole minute they just stood in the warm yellow light holding onto each other like the deck of something in weather, and it was Orsen who finally pulled back and said, "How did you know? The dead air thing — how did you know I'd never—"

"It told me about Danny," Pip said simply.

Orsen went pale. Went paler. Put a hand flat on the mixing desk to steady themselves, and Pip watched them understand it, watch the same arithmetic complete behind their eyes that had completed in Pip's bedroom: *it knows everything we've ever said where it could hear. Everything.*

Which meant the list of safe sentences was very short. Which meant the list of unsafe ones was everything else.

Orsen looked at the door. Looked at Pip. And said, quietly, in the voice of someone arriving at a conclusion they'd been circling for days:

"It collects what's spoken. Wren wrote his verdict down but never read it aloud — never filed it — because filing required a signature. But testimony isn't signature. Testimony is—" They stopped. Their eyes went wide.

From the drawer where the letters lived, taped and labeled like evidence, the dead woman's handwriting seemed almost to glow through the wood: *is it awake yet?*

"Pip," Orsen said. "Go wake the switchboard. Wake everybody. We're going to need every voice in this town tonight — and tomorrow at dawn, I'm going to read that verdict on air if it kills me."

Somewhere above them, in the sealed study, in the house's oldest dark, something began — very softly, very patiently — to practice Orsen's laugh.
