## 23. Confession Broadcast

The pen had been on the desk for eleven minutes.

Orsen knew because they'd been watching it the way you watch a spider on the far wall—not moving, not moving, not moving—and the clock above the board said 3:47, and the pen had appeared at 3:36, sliding out of the dark of the hallway through the open studio door as if carried on a tray by an invisible waiter. It was a good pen. Heavy barrel, brass fittings, the kind that cost more than a month of transmitter rent. It had rolled to a stop against the fader for Channel One and sat there, patient as a debt.

The second voice had stopped speaking. That was worse. For six hours it had read Orsen's life back to them in that borrowed cadence—the cadence that was almost Orsen's own, a half-step flat, like a recording of a recording—but now the house had gone quiet in the specific way a courtroom goes quiet before a verdict. The work lights Marisol had rigged buzzed overhead. The generator thumped in the basement, steady as a pulse. Somewhere in the wings of the house, in the dark past the eleven doors and the twelfth, something was waiting to see what Orsen Fairweather would do with the pen.

*You're live,* Orsen reminded themself. *You're always live. That's the whole point of you.*

They keyed the mic.

"This is Channel Nine, the night watch, and it's—" a glance at the clock, because even now, especially now, the show went on the clock— "3:48 in the morning. If you're just joining us: the lights are on, the doors are counted, and we are not signing anything."

A breath. In the headphones, the dead air breathed back.

"I want to tell you a story," Orsen said. "It's not about the judge. I've told you about the judge. It's about me. And I'm going to tell it wrong, probably, the way you tell things wrong when you're scared, but I'm going to tell all of it, and I'm not going to stop, and if the thing in my hallway wants to interrupt me it can do it on its own frequency, because this one's mine."

The pen did not move. Orsen did not touch it.

"Six years ago I came to this town because nobody here knew me. That's the truth of it. I wasn't chasing some dream of late-night radio. I was running. I'd been fired from a station in the city for—well. We'll get there. And this place had a little AM signal and a house that came with the job, and I thought: perfect. Nobody to disappoint. Nobody who knew enough about me to be disappointed *with*. I could be a new person in a new town, and the new person would be the guy on the radio who talks to insomniacs, and that guy would be *good*, and the old guy—the one who—" 

Orsen's hand was shaking. They put it flat on the desk, under the board, where the microphone couldn't see it. A lie of framing. They'd been framing lies for six years. It was practically a skill.

"The old guy walked out on his mother."

The words came out smaller than Orsen intended. On air, small is fine. Small is intimate. But this wasn't craft anymore, and Orsen knew the difference, and the knowing made it worse.

"She got sick. Not the dramatic kind. The slow kind, the kind that eats a person a spoonful at a time, and I was her only—there wasn't anyone else. And there was a night, about four months in, when she didn't know who I was, and she was scared of me, and I sat in her kitchen at two in the morning—two in the morning, isn't that funny, I've always been a two-in-the-morning person—and I thought: I cannot do this for five more years. I thought it so loud it felt like saying it. And then I did say it. To her. I said, *I can't do this*, and she looked at me with those eyes that didn't know me and said, *that's all right, honey, nobody can*—"

Orsen stopped. The board hummed. In the headphones, faintly, the sound of a line opening—Channel Four, the one the dead callers had been using all week—and then a hush, like a room full of people leaning in.

"She died eleven months later," Orsen said. "I was in another state. I'd taken a shift I didn't need because a shift is a reason to not be somewhere, and the home called, and I let it go to voicemail, and I heard it the next morning in a parking lot, and I sat in my car and I thought: *well. Now you never have to be that scared again.* And do you know what I felt, for about four seconds, before the grief came? Relief. Four seconds of relief. And I have never said that out loud, not to a priest, not to a therapist, not to the woman I left in Portland who figured it out before I did and did the leaving for me. Four seconds. And then six years of talking to strangers in the dark, which is a way of talking to one stranger in the dark, which is a way of not talking at all."

The lights flickered. Orsen's stomach dropped—*it's working, it's feeding*—but the flicker wasn't the brown-out sag of the last three nights, the lights being drunk down like a candle in a draft. It was something else. A shimmer. A steadying. The work lamps burned a shade whiter, and the shadows in the hallway—the ones past the studio door, the ones that had been *thick* all night, thick as wet wool—seemed to flinch.

Orsen kept going, because when you find a door that opens you walk through it.

"Here's the other one. Here's the one I've been circling all week like a moth. The dead woman who called on the first night—Mrs. Halloran, who wanted the song for her husband—I knew her. Not from her funeral. From *before*. She used to call the show every Thursday, and three months before she died she told me her husband was gone and she didn't see the point, and I did the thing. You know the thing. The radio thing. I said *hang in there, friend, and here's a song*, and I put a song on, and I went to break, and I thought about her for maybe ninety seconds, and then I thought about what I was going to have for dinner. And when she called me last week from the other side of whatever that is, and she said *you sound tired, dear*, I heard it. Underneath it. The question she was really asking, then and now: *did you hear me? Did any of it land? Was I a person to you, or was I content?*"

Orsen's eyes were burning. The board was a blur of little colored lights.

"I'm a professional," Orsen said. "That's what I've been telling myself all week, locking doors, counting doors, keeping the lights on, doing the show. Professional. But *professional* is just a word for the walls I built so nobody would ever hear the four seconds. So nobody would ever hear the kitchen. And this thing in my house—this collector, this faceless clerk of whatever hell holds the paper—it figured that out. That's what it's been reading me all night. My debts. My lies. Every time I said *we'll be right back* and didn't come back. Every time a caller said *I'm not doing so good* and I gave them a song instead of a question. It thinks those are the debts it can collect on. It thinks shame is a currency, and it's been running my tab all night, and I'll tell you what—"

Orsen leaned into the mic, and the old radio instinct, the true one, the one that wasn't a wall, rose up clean.

"—it's right about the debts. I owe all of them. Every one. But it doesn't get to collect. Because a debt you confess isn't a debt you hide, and it can't buy what I've already given away. So here it is. All of it. Free of charge. To everyone listening, living and otherwise: I failed my mother. I failed Mrs. Halloran. I failed every one of you who called in the dark needing more than a song, and I knew it, and I did it anyway, because it was easier, because I was scared, because the walls were load-bearing. That's the confession. That's the whole confession. No signature required."

The studio lights flared—and held.

Not the guttering, not the brown sag. They came up like a struck match coming up *sure*, and the shadows in the hallway pulled back a full foot, and the generator in the basement changed pitch, smoothed out, like an engine finding its gear. Orsen felt it in the headphones before they understood it in the room: the dead air had changed texture. It was full. It was *populated*. The way a church is full before anyone has spoken.

On Channel Four, a voice. Mrs. Halloran. Thin, warm, unmistakable.

"*I heard you,*" she said.

Orsen put a hand over their mouth. Then, because the show must go on, because the show was no longer a wall but a window, they took the hand away and said, "Hi, Mrs. Halloran."

"*You heard me too, dear. Ninety seconds is ninety seconds. I counted.*" A sound that was almost a laugh. "*And I'll tell you something for free, since you're in a confessing mood: the four seconds. Everyone has four seconds. My four seconds were at the funeral, looking at the flowers, thinking about the cost. The collector doesn't know what to do with a person who says it out loud. It's been trying to buy your shame all night, and you've gone and spent it yourself. There's nothing left on the table for it to buy.*"

The line opened again. A man's voice, waterlogged and gentle—Dell's drowned man from week one, the one who'd asked for the door count. "*I never told anyone I was tired that day,*" he said. "*The day the river took me. Everyone said it was the current. It was tiredness. I was so tired, and I didn't say it, and now I'm saying it.*"

Another line. A woman Orsen didn't recognize, dead three years, confessing a letter never mailed. Another. A boy, nineteen, confessing that the last thing he'd said to his father was a lie. They came one after another, not the strange requests of the first nights—*count the doors, keep the lights on*—but plain, human, unburdening, the switchboard becoming what it had always secretly been: a place where the dark could put down what it was carrying.

And with every voice, the lights burned steadier.

Orsen watched it happen and understood, with the clarity that only comes at four in the morning at the far end of a siege, what was occurring. *Testimony is also consent—to truth.* The collector fed on consent, the dead had said, starved on refusal. But there was more than one kind of consent. The collector wanted a signature: consent given in secret, in fear, in the small print, consent that could be *held over* you. This was the other kind. Consent given in the open. Consent that, once given, belonged to the giver no longer and to the collector not at all. You could not leverage a confession. You could not foreclose on the truth.

The pen on the desk had not moved. But it looked different now. Smaller. Brass fittings gone dull, like a prop after the play.

"Keep talking," Orsen said to the open lines, and their voice cracked and they let it crack, on air, unprofessional, un-wall-ed. "All night if you have to. I'm not going anywhere. None of us are."

"*Orsen.*"

The second voice. Back. But changed—it came flat now, stripped of its borrowed cadence, stripped of everything borrowed, and what was underneath was not a voice at all but the sound a contract makes when it's being read aloud by something that has never once been lied to and is only now learning the sensation.

"*You understand that confession changes nothing. The claim stands. The line remains blank.*"

"Yeah," Orsen said, and picked up the pen—held it up to the work lights so the whole dark house could see it, so the thing in the hallway could see it—and set it down again on the far side of the desk, out of reach, out of ritual, out of *play*. "But you've been listening, so you already know: I'm not the one who's changed nothing. You came here for a secret. I just spent the last twenty minutes giving mine away for free, to witnesses. You can't collect a debt that's been forgiven, and you can't buy a shame that's been *shared*. Ask the judge. He's been in this house sixty years, and the one thing he never did was say it out loud."

The silence that followed was the silence of a machine recalculating.

And then, from the twelfth door, from behind the sealed study at the end of the hall, faint but unmistakable, carried on the generator's hum like a voice carried on a wire:

The sound of a man clearing his throat.

* * *

Orsen froze, hand on the fader, eyes on the hallway. The studio lights burned white and steady. On Channel Four, Mrs. Halloran whispered, "*Oh,*" in a tone Orsen had never heard from the dead—something like hope.

The throat-clearing came again. Closer. Or a door closer. Footsteps now—slow, formal, the tread of a man who had spent a lifetime walking into courtrooms—and they were coming down the hallway, past door one, past door four, past the pen lying dull on the desk's far edge, and Orsen reached for the mic because that was the only weapon they had ever owned, and said, voice steady, voice *live*:

"Channel Nine. Whoever you are—come into the light. We're taking testimony."

The footsteps stopped at the edge of the studio doorway, where the work light's reach ended and the dark began, and stood there, at the border, waiting—sixty years of waiting condensed into a single held breath—for permission to step through.
