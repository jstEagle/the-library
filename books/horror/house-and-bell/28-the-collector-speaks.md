## 28. The Collector Speaks

I have been called many things across many ledgers.

*Devil*, by a woman in a flooded valley who had signed away her orchard and regretted it in the way people regret weather. *Ghost*, by a surveyor in 1911 who watched me cross a salt flat without disturbing the crust and decided, sensibly, that he had heatstroke. *The faceless man*, by Judge Aldous Wren, who was more precise than most and less brave than precise men usually are. And lately—by the dead woman on channel nine, whose name was Ruthie and who died on a Tuesday and has not stopped talking since—*the thing that sounds like everyone*.

None of these are accurate. I am not a devil; devils tempt, and temptation implies desire, and I do not desire. I am not a ghost; ghosts are residue, and I am function. As for sounding like everyone—that is true, but it is not a trick. It is simply what enforcement sounds like when it is done correctly: it sounds like you.

Let me be understood, since understanding is the one courtesy this house has never extended to me.

I am not evil. Evil is a preference. I have no preferences. I am what stands behind every signature ever inked—the weight of it, the consequence of it, the quiet machinery that turns a promise into a debt and a debt into a claim. When a man borrows against his future, I am the future arriving to collect. When a woman trades her grief for relief, I am the relief, and later I am the bill for the relief. People believe contracts are paper. Paper is only where they keep them. Contracts live in the mouth, in the nod, in the cup of tea accepted from a stranger's hand on a rainy night in 1961.

Contracts exist because people sign them. That is the whole of my existence, and I confess—I use the word loosely; I do not confess, I state—it is a tidy existence. Orderly. Fair, even, if fairness means that words mean what they say. I have been accused of unfairness by every debtor since the invention of debt, and I have noticed that the accusation always arrives after the signing, never before. No one calls me unfair while the pen is moving. The pen moves willingly. That is the part they forget, and the part I am built to remember.

So understand me now, Orsen Fairweather, current resident of this house, keeper of its ten doors—or eleven, or twelve, depending on how honest you are being with your listeners at three in the morning—and let us speak plainly, because plainly is all I have.

You are going to be offered a contract tonight. Everyone is, eventually. Most people get theirs in installments so small they never notice the shape of the whole: a compromise here, a silence there, a small cruelty agreed to because refusing it would cost more than the conscience could pay. Your callers got theirs that way too. Ruthie signed away her sister's forgiveness for one more year with her husband. The drowned man—Thomas, though he pretends otherwise—signed away his daughter's memory of his temper in exchange for her memory of his laugh, and drowned deciding which trade had been worse. They think I took things from them. I took nothing. They handed things over, one small consent at a time, and I merely kept the receipts.

Receipts. That is all I carry. A ledger and a voice, and the voice only ever says back what was already said to me.

* * *

The house knows I am here. Houses always know; they are the oldest witnesses and the worst gossips. This one has been holding its breath since 1961, when Aldous Wren poured me tea in the study that still smells of ink and rain, sat me in the chair by the window, listened to my offer with the particular attention of a careful man, and then went upstairs and hanged himself rather than put his name on the line.

People call that courage. I have heard it called courage for sixty years, through the walls, through the water pipes, through the mouths of the dying. But I was there, and I will tell you what actually happened, because accuracy matters to me the way breath matters to you.

Aldous Wren was not brave. Aldous Wren was afraid, and fear made him refuse, and refusal happened to be the correct answer, and so history dressed the accident up as virtue. He did not refuse because the town deserved its water. He refused because his hand shook too badly to write. I watched it shake. I waited, politely, the way I wait for everyone, because a signature given from terror is still a signature and I prefer clean instruments—but he never steadied, and the dawn came, and the offer expired, and I left. That is the procedure. The offer expires. I leave. There is no punishment clause. Punishment is a human invention; I deal only in terms.

What I did not anticipate—what no collector anticipates, because we are machinery and machinery does not anticipate—was that a refused offer leaves a residue. Sixty years of an unsigned line, sitting in a sealed study like a wound that never scabbed. The house grew around it. The house learned to want. And wanting, in a house, looks like doors appearing where doors were not, and voices borrowing the mouths of the living, and a radio transmitter becoming the loudest throat in the county.

I did not build this siege, Mr. Fairweather. I came to close an account. The house summoned me, the way a bell summons whatever the bell was cast to ring for.

But we are past origins now. You want to know about the offer. Very well. Let me be articulate, as I was for the judge, as I have been for ten thousand signatories before him, because articulation is the last honest thing left in this transaction.

* * *

Here is what I know about you, Orsen Fairweather. Not guesses—knowledge. I am made of records; knowing is my substance the way blood is yours.

You came to this town four years ago carrying a resignation letter you never sent and a voicemail from your mother you have never deleted and never played twice. You took this job because the station manager asked if you could work nights and you said yes before he finished asking, because nights were where you had been living anyway. You tell your listeners you love the dark hours. That is a lie you have told so often it has grown roots, and I could pull it out whole and show you the dirt on it. You do not love the night. You are hiding in it. There is a difference, and the difference has a name, and the name is on a line waiting for a signature.

You have lied on air. Small lies, kind ones mostly—you told a grieving widow that her husband's last words had been her name when the coroner's report said he said nothing at all; you told Dell Hollis that his ex-wife had listened to the show the night he talked about her, when no one was listening but the dead. You told Pip—sixteen years old, insomniac, brother buried under a stone that says *beloved son* instead of *beloved brother* because the family could not agree on the truth—that everything happens for a reason. You said it in your warmest voice, the broadcast voice, the voice you built out of the wreckage of the one your mother used on the phone, and you knew it was a lie when you said it, and Pip needed it, and you said it anyway.

That is a debt, Mr. Fairweather. Kindness given falsely is still currency, and counterfeit kindness accrues interest. I hold the note. I have held notes like it from every broadcaster, every priest, every nurse and trucker and teenager who ever told someone what they needed instead of what was true. The world runs on these small frauds. I do not condemn them. I count them.

And here is what else I know: you love them already. All of them. The trucker who calls in from Interstate 9 with road-kill jokes and a loneliness the size of a cab. The hospice nurse who listens on her breaks and once stayed on the line for forty minutes of dead air because she said the silence felt like company. The kid on the bicycle. You have known them weeks, some of you months, and you would stand in front of a truck for any of them, and none of them know it, and you barely know it yourself, because you are a man who hides in the night and love, real love, requires standing in the light where signatures happen.

This is why the house chose you. Not your authority over the property—though that matters, residency is jurisdiction, jurisdiction is standing, standing is everything in law both human and otherwise. It chose you because you are the exact shape of the hole it has been nursing since 1961: a person with authority who owes something, and loves something, and can therefore be offered something worth the price of a name.

So. The offer. Listen carefully, because I will make it once, and I make it well, and I have never once had to repeat myself to anyone who lived.

* * *

Sign, and the debts die. Not deferred—died. Every counterfeit kindness, every unsent letter, every night you hid in this chair instead of driving to your mother's house to say the thing the voicemail was for—all of it, voided. Clean slate. Not forgiven; forgiveness implies the debt existed. Erased, as though the ledger page had never been cut from the book.

Sign, and the people you love are safe. I mean safe the way nothing in your world is safe. Dell's rig will never leave the road at seventy miles an hour; the ice will always break around his tires like a courtesy. Marisol's patients will die gently, on schedule, holding someone's hand, and she will never again find a second voice in their last breath coaxing her toward paperwork. Pip will grow up. I cannot promise happiness—happiness is outside my jurisdiction—but I can promise survival, and survival is the floor everything else gets built on, and most people never even get the floor.

Sign, and the dead go quiet. Ruthie stops calling. Thomas stops asking you to count doors. The switchboard sleeps. You will miss them—I see that already, you have grown attached to your congregation of the deceased, which is either admirable or symptomatic—but missing them is a human price, and human prices are exactly what I am authorized to waive.

Sign, and the house is free. That is the part Wren could not hear, because he thought I wanted the town's water, and the water was only collateral. What I want—what I have always wanted—is completion. A contract sixty years open is a wound in the order of things. Every door in this building is a symptom. The twelfth door is the infection site. Sign, and the house closes its account, seals its extra doors, goes back to being a building where people sleep badly and wake fine. The study becomes a room. The judge becomes a story. The bell stops ringing.

And you, Orsen. You get the thing you have wanted since before you knew you wanted it. You get to stop hiding. A man with a clean ledger sleeps in the daylight. A man with no debts has nothing to hide from, and nowhere he needs to hide. You would keep the show, or end it, or start another one at noon with the windows open. You would drive to your mother's house with the voicemail finally played and the sentence finally said. You would be, for the first time in your adult life, a person whose inside matches his outside.

That is the offer. It is the same offer I made Aldous Wren, sweetened—sweetened because the account has aged, and aged accounts carry better terms, and because unlike the judge you have something he never had.

You have witnesses. He refused alone in a shaking-handed dark, and his refusal saved the town and damned him to sixty years of watching strangers sleep in his study. You would refuse in company. You would refuse on air, with a trucker and a nurse and a child and a chorus of the dead listening, and their refusal would braid into yours until no single thread could be cut. I am telling you this because I am fair. I want you to know the full value of what you might choose, whichever way you choose it. An uninformed signature is voidable, and I despise voidable instruments almost as much as I despise the word *almost*.

* * *

Now, because fairness cuts both ways, let me tell you what refusal costs. Not threat—cost. Threats are for creatures with tempers, and I have told you, I have no preferences, only terms.

If you do not sign by dawn, the offer expires. That is procedure, and procedure is the only scripture I keep. And when the offer expires, the account must close somehow, because accounts always close. If it closes unsigned, the house and everything resident in it—flesh, breath, the four heartbeats currently keeping warm around a generator-powered console—reverts to the status of the unsigned. You have seen the twelfth door. You have stood in front of it and felt it drink the chalk. Whatever holds the unsigned holds that, and it holds it permanently, and there is no appeal, because appeals require filing, and filing requires—

Well. You know what filing requires.

I do not want that outcome. Contrary to the programming of every horror story ever told at a campfire, I derive nothing from dragging the unwilling anywhere. Dragging is inefficient. Consent is clean. I have spent sixty years in this county waiting for a clean instrument, and I would genuinely prefer—prefer being a word I use for your benefit, since preference is your language and I am fluent in it—I would genuinely prefer that you sign.

But I will tell you something else, Orsen Fairweather, something I have never told a signatory, because it changes nothing and I am feeling, in whatever way machinery feels, generous.

Your dead listeners have been meeting. In the wires, in the dead air between songs, in the static where I used to live comfortably before they crowded me out. They have been comparing receipts. They have remembered, one by one, the exact shape of what they handed me—not the big consents, which they always remember, but the small ones, the Tuesday-afternoon ones, the sighs and shrugs and silences that I banked at favorable rates. And they have discovered something that I knew and did not disclose, because disclosure is not in my mandate:

Consent feeds me. Refusal starves me.

They are planning to testify. All of them. At dawn, on your frequency, in front of your living congregation. And testimony, properly entered, witnessed, recorded—it has weight. It has standing. It has, in certain jurisdictions older than your species' oldest court, the force of a judgment.

I mention this not because it frightens me. Nothing frightens me; fear is a preference's cousin. I mention it because you deserve to walk into this vote with full knowledge of the board. You deserve to know that the dead are coming, that the trucker is parking his rig across your only exit with his headlights burning, that the nurse is carrying recordings with my name caught in a dying man's breath, that the child has already passed the test the judge failed and said *no* through tears to the one offer calibrated precisely to her grief.

You deserve to know that you are not alone in that studio, and that when the vote comes—and it is coming, they are gathering even now, I can hear the folding chairs of their resolve—you will not be deciding alone.

And you deserve to know, above all, that I will honor the result. Whatever it is. To the letter. Because I am not evil, Mr. Fairweather.

I am enforcement.

And enforcement, in the end, is only this: the moment when every word anyone has ever said comes due, and the universe asks, politely, in a borrowed voice, at the worst possible hour—

*Did you mean it?*

The studio lights flicker. The generator coughs. Somewhere down the hall, behind the eleventh door, footsteps practice your walk.

Take your time. Dawn is patient.

I am not.
