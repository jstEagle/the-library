## 38. Aftermath

The letter from the Federal Communications Commission arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside an envelope so thin Marisol could feel the nothing of it through the paper. She stood in her kitchen with her coffee going cold and read it twice, because the first time she'd assumed she was misreading it.

*After review of complaints received regarding programming on channel 910 AM, the Commission has determined that no further action is warranted. The matter is closed.*

No further action. As if the whole thing had been a clerical error. As if somewhere in a filing cabinet in Washington there was a folder labeled THE DEAD WOMAN ON CHANNEL NINE that had been stamped and shelved by someone who would never know what they were closing the lid on.

She set the letter down on the counter, next to the fruit bowl, next to the ordinary salt and pepper, and laughed — a short, startled sound, like a hiccup with teeth. Then she picked up her phone and texted the group chat, which was what they had started calling it without ever quite admitting they'd made one: *It's over. FCC dropped it. "No further action."*

Orsen replied first, as Orsen always did now, fast and slightly breathless even in text: *That's it? Just like that?*

*Just like that,* she typed back. *The government has decided we are not haunted.*

Pip's answer came thirty seconds later, all lowercase and exclamation points: *we were never haunted. we were BESIEGED. get it right*

Dell's reply took ten minutes, which meant he was driving, which meant he'd pulled over at some point to type it with two enormous thumbs: *good. tell them to send the file to the chimney.*

Marisol looked at the letter again. The paper seemed very white in the morning light. Everything did, these days. That was the thing nobody warned you about surviving something — the world didn't darken afterward. It brightened, aggressively, obscenely, like a stage crew flooding the set after the curtain call, daring you to find the tragedy still standing in all that light.

She poured her cold coffee down the sink and got dressed. They had breakfast at eight. They always had breakfast at eight now.

* * *

The diner on Corliss Street didn't have a name anyone used. It had been the Blue Jay once, then the Corliss Grill, then nothing for eleven years, then a woman named Tante Odile bought it and painted it yellow and never repainted the sign, so everyone just called it Odile's. It had booths with cracked vinyl and a pie case that fogged up when the kitchen ran hot, and it had one corner booth, second from the window, where four people had been sitting every morning for six days.

Marisol arrived last, which was unusual, and found them already arranged: Dell wedged into the bench side with his forearms on the table like the table might try something; Pip across from him, hunched over a phone but not really looking at it; Orsen at the end, nearest the window, where the light came in. Orsen had lost weight during the siege week — everyone had, though none of them talked about that — and the hollows under their eyes had gone from bruise-colored to merely tired. But they were here. They were upright. They were wearing the same cardigan they'd worn on air the night the lights went out, and Marisol suspected they would wear it every day until it disintegrated, the way soldiers keep the boots they walked home in.

"You look terrible," she said, sliding in beside Pip.

"Thank you," Orsen said. "I've been working on it."

"Six days of sleeping," Dell said. "You'd think it'd take."

"I don't sleep so much as I lie down adjacent to it." Orsen turned a coffee cup right-side up when Odile's granddaughter came by with the pot, and Marisol watched the small ritual of it — the cup, the pour, the nod — and thought about how many mornings she'd spent in hospice break rooms watching families relearn these gestures after a death. The pouring of coffee. The passing of creamer. The way hands need something to do while the heart catches up.

Because that's what this was, she realized, not for the first time. Not celebration. Grief management. The crisis had ended, but the dead hadn't come back, and the living who remained had been fused together by heat and pressure into something none of them had asked to be.

"So the water rights," Pip said, because Pip could not let a silence sit for more than four seconds. "I checked the county records again this morning. The Hargrove claim is dissolved. Not transferred, not sold — *dissolved*. Like it never existed. And the town's easement reverted automatically. The reservoir's ours again. All of it."

"Void ab initio," Orsen said quietly. "From the beginning. Wren's language."

"The lawyer at the county office kept staring at me," Pip said. "Like he remembered typing it but couldn't remember why. You should've seen his face. He said, 'Huh,' three times. Just — huh. Huh. Huh."

Marisol smiled despite herself. "The whole town's walking around saying 'huh.'"

"They're saying more than that." Dell tore a napkin into precise quarters as he spoke, a habit Marisol had catalogued early — the man needed his hands busy or the memories got loud. "Fella at the fuel depot told me half the town's been sleeping through the night for the first time in weeks. Didn't know they weren't. Didn't know something had been leaning on them. Now it's off and everybody's lighter and nobody can say why." He shrugged his enormous shoulders. "Town's like a man who took his coat off and can't recall putting it on."

"And the show?" Marisol asked, and felt the table go careful.

Orsen looked out the window. Across the street, the station building sat in the morning sun, its transmitter mast catching light, its windows dark because nobody had been inside since. There was a question pending there — whether Orsen would go back, whether the show would return to the airwaves, whether channel 910 would resume its long nightly conversation with the sleepless — and Orsen had been carrying the question visibly, the way you carry a stone in your pocket, touching it through the fabric.

"License is still active," Orsen said. "Equipment's fine. The generator needs a new fuel filter, probably. I could be back on air by Friday if I wanted."

"Do you want?" Pip asked.

"That's the thing." Orsen wrapped both hands around the coffee cup. "I don't know yet. For seven years the show was how I kept the night at arm's length. Insomniacs, shift workers, the grieving, the scared — my people, my flock, whatever you want to call it. And then the night came *into* the studio and sat down across from me, and I found out the show wasn't armor at all. It was bait." A pause. "But it was also a lighthouse. Both things. It called the collector *and* it called the chorus. Same signal."

"The dead wouldn't have found each other without it," Marisol said. "They told us that. They were scattered. Alone in the dark, each one thinking they were the only one being asked. Your show gave them a switchboard."

"It gave them a room," Orsen corrected softly. "You gave them testimony. Dell gave them a wall. Pip—" Orsen glanced at the kid, and something moved through their face that Marisol could only describe as parental and terrified and proud all at once, "—Pip gave them the history. The map. We each held a piece and none of us knew it until we were standing in the same room holding it."

"Found family," Pip said, with the particular self-conscious defiance of a sixteen-year-old using a word they mean completely. "I'm calling it that. You can't stop me."

"Nobody's trying to stop you," Dell rumbled.

"Marisol looks like she's trying to stop me."

"I am absolutely trying to stop you," Marisol said, "because if you name it, it becomes real, and if it becomes real, we have obligations, and I already have enough obligations," and Pip grinned, and Dell huffed something that was almost a laugh, and Orsen smiled down into their coffee, and the moment passed through the booth like warmth through a wall — brief, real, unrepeatable.

Odile's granddaughter brought the food. Nobody had ordered; Odile simply sent out what she'd decided they should eat, and they ate it, because arguing with Odile was like arguing with weather. Eggs for Dell, toast for Pip, oatmeal for Marisol because Odile had decided Marisol was a person who ate oatmeal, and pancakes for Orsen, which Marisol noted with interest.

"Pancakes," she said. "Look at that. The insomniac eats solids now."

"One victory at a time." Orsen cut a piece, paused, set the fork down. "Can I say something strange?"

"We'd be disappointed if you didn't," Dell said.

"I miss them." Orsen's voice stayed level, but Marisol heard the load-bearing quality of it, the way grief sounds when it's been reinforced with discipline. "Not the fear. Not the voice, God, never the voice. But the callers. Mrs. Aldous who wanted the hymn at four a.m. The drowned man who just wanted someone to count doors with him, because counting meant somebody was listening. The woman from Tuesday — the first one — who described her own funeral and then thanked me for taking her call like it was the nicest thing that had happened to her that week." Orsen's jaw tightened. "They were my listeners too. Maybe my best ones. And they're gone, and the switchboard is quiet, and I keep waking up at 3 a.m. reaching for a board full of lit lines that aren't lit anymore."

The table was quiet. Outside, a delivery truck rattled past, absurdly normal.

"I know that quiet," Marisol said finally. "Thirty-one years in hospice. You spend months inside a family's worst season, and then the patient dies, and you do the paperwork, and you go to the next house, and there's this — gap. This ringing absence where all that intensity used to be. People think relief comes first. It doesn't. Emptiness comes first. Relief shows up late, like a guest who had trouble parking."

"When does it show up?" Pip asked, younger than their years for exactly one sentence.

"For me? Around the third breakfast," Marisol said. "Which is today. So." She lifted her coffee. "Here's to relief, whenever it finds a spot."

Dell raised his glass of orange juice. Pip raised theirs. Orsen hesitated, then raised the coffee cup, and the four vessels touched above the syrup and the napkin confetti and the cracked vinyl, and Marisol felt something settle in her chest that she suspected would stay settled, more or less, for the rest of her life.

* * *

She went to the cemetery that afternoon.

She hadn't planned to. She'd driven past the turnoff on her way home from an errand and her hands had made the decision before her mind caught up, the way hands do. The gate was open. October light lay flat and gold across the grass.

She didn't know most of them. That was the strange truth of the siege — she'd heard their voices, played their recordings, testified alongside them, and she couldn't have picked half of them out of a crowd. But she knew where some of them lay, because the town was small and the recent dead were fresh-turned, and she walked the rows reading names until she found one she recognized: Ruth Ellery Aldous. Died the eleventh. The hymn at four a.m.

Marisol stood at the grave with her hands in her coat pockets.

"I don't actually know your hymn," she said aloud, feeling foolish and doing it anyway. "You never named it. Orsen played something that night — I don't remember what. Something old." A breeze moved through the maples along the fence line, and she waited until it passed, the way you wait for a hospice patient to finish a sentence. "Anyway. I wanted to say that what happened to you wasn't right. Being asked. Being worked on after you were done. Whatever it wanted from you, you didn't owe it, and I hope somebody told you that before you went. If nobody did — consider it told."

She stood a while longer. The gold light didn't move. Somewhere beyond the fence a dog barked twice and quit.

On the drive home she passed the station. She slowed without meaning to. The building sat there with its ten windows and its one mast, and she found herself counting the windows the way she now counted everything — exits, doors, reflections — a habit she suspected would fade eventually and didn't mind keeping meanwhile. Ten windows. Ten doors, presumably. Always ten now.

Her phone buzzed against the console. A message in the group chat, from Orsen:

*Went to the station today. Stood outside for an hour. Couldn't make myself open the door. Felt stupid about it. Then I remembered hospitality rules and decided maybe it's fine to never invite myself anywhere ever again.*

Marisol laughed alone in her car, then dictated a reply: *Or buy a key and stop asking. Doors only matter to things that need permission.*

There was a long pause, long enough that she'd reached her street, before the reply came:

*That might be the smartest thing anyone's said all week. Putting it on a mug.*

* * *

Friday morning, seventh breakfast, Orsen arrived with news and a face that was trying very hard to be casual about it.

"I called the engineer," they announced, sitting down. "Had him do a full inspection. Transmitter, board, generator, wiring, the works. He found nothing wrong. Nothing. Said it's the cleanest system he's seen in twenty years." They turned their coffee cup right-side up. "And then he said something odd. He said, 'Funny, the generator log. Somebody ran it hard for about nine hours straight last month. Never been run like that.' And I said, yes, that was us. And he said, 'Ran beautiful. Better than spec. Like it wanted to hold.'" Orsen looked up. "Like it *wanted* to hold."

"The house helped," Pip said simply, around toast. "Obviously. It was fighting for its own life too. Wren built that verdict to protect it. Of course it held the lights."

"The house is a museum now," Dell said. "Council voted Wednesday. Wren study stays sealed except by appointment. Kid's notes are framed in the lobby." He nodded at Pip, whose ears went red. "Plaque says 'Researched by P. Vasquez, age 16.' Your mother cried."

"My mother cried because I broke curfew for a month and nearly got eaten by a debt-collector ghost, and the plaque is her coping mechanism," Pip said. "We contain multitudes."

Marisol watched them — the kid deflecting with sarcasm, the trucker smiling into his eggs, Orsen turning the cup, turning the cup — and felt the shape of the thing they were all circling. The show could end. That was the unspoken item on the table every morning, passed like the creamer. The crisis was over; the reason for the ritual was dissolving; any of them could simply stop coming, and life would absorb the loss the way life absorbed everything. Dell had routes to run. She had shifts and a sister in Ohio who called on Sundays. Pip had school and, presumably, eventually, a life that happened in daylight.

And yet.

"Here's what I keep thinking," she said, and four faces turned to her, and she understood suddenly that they'd all been waiting for someone to say it first. "In hospice we have a word for the visits that happen after the medical need ends. We call them presence visits. No vitals to check, no meds to manage. You just show up and sit with somebody, because the sitting is the medicine." She wrapped her hands around her oatmeal bowl, warm through the ceramic. "Nobody needs this breakfast. Medically speaking, we're all fine. But I've noticed I sleep better on the nights before it. And I've noticed nobody's missed one. And I've noticed that when Dell's phone buzzed yesterday mid-pancake, all three of us went still until he said it was dispatch." She looked around the table. "So I vote we keep having it. Not because anything's chasing us. Because the sitting is the medicine."

Silence. The pie case fogged and cleared.

"Thursdays too?" Pip said, in the smallest voice Marisol had heard from them since the hallway, since door eleven, since the mimic wore Orsen's voice like a stolen coat.

"Thursdays too," Marisol said.

"Every day, if the schedule allows," Dell said, which from Dell was a speech.

Orsen didn't answer right away. They were looking out the window again, at the station across the street, at the mast catching the low gold light, and Marisol watched them arrive at whatever they arrived at — saw it land in their shoulders first, then their hands, then their face.

"Then I'll keep the license," Orsen said. "Not the show. Not yet. Maybe not ever the show. But the license, and the building, and the transmitter warmed up once a week so it doesn't rot." They turned from the window. "A lighthouse doesn't have to ring a bell every night. It just has to stay lit. In case."

"In case of what?" Pip asked.

Orsen smiled, and it was the first smile Marisol had seen from them that didn't cost anything.

"In case anybody's out there," they said, "listening."

* * *

That night, Marisol slept nine hours, dreamless and deep, and woke on Sunday to her sister's call and the smell of rain coming in off the hills. She watered her plants. She answered emails. At noon she opened the group chat out of habit and found a photo Pip had posted an hour earlier: the station's front step, wet with rain, and chalked on the concrete in Pip's unmistakable handwriting, where the mail carrier and the meter reader and the whole indifferent world would walk over it and never know —

*WE KEPT THE LIGHTS ON.*

Below it, Orsen had replied with a single line: *See you Thursday. Eight o'clock. Booth by the window.*

And below that, a message from Dell, timestamped 6:14 a.m., sent from somewhere out on the highway with the sunrise coming up ahead of his rig:

*Roads clear. Weather's clean. Funny thing — passed the county line and the radio scanned itself right to 910. Nothing broadcasting. Just carrier wave, dead air, the hum of a tower waiting.* 

*Could've sworn, for about half a second, I heard somebody clearing their throat.*
