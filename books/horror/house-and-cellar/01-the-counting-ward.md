## 1. The Counting Ward

The ward smelled of lavender and something underneath it, older — the sweetish rot of bodies forgetting how to be bodies. Edmund Dunmore had worked the night shift at St. Brannoc's long enough that she no longer noticed either smell, the way a fisherman stops hearing the sea. What she noticed was the counting.

It started, as it always did, quietly.

Room four. Mr. Albert Voss, seventy-eight years old, pancreas gone to ruin, three days from the end by every chart and every instinct she possessed. He had been lucid all evening, complaining about the television, asking after his granddaughter with the fondness of a man arranging his affairs in order. Then, at 11:42, he had turned his face to the window — where there was nothing to see but the car park and the sodium-orange smear of the streetlight — and begun to count.

"Thirty," he said.

Edmund stood in the corridor outside his door, her hand resting on the frame, and told herself what she had told herself for twelve years: *it's coincidence.*

"Twenty-nine."

The morphine did this sometimes. She knew that. She had read the studies, or skimmed them, in the early years when she still needed the studies to hold between herself and the thing she kept witnessing. Terminal lucidity, they called one version of it — the strange final clarity of the dying. And delirium could take any shape. Numbers were as good as anything else. A mind letting go of its grip might slide down the banister of arithmetic all the way into the dark.

"Twenty-eight. Twenty-seven."

She pushed the door open and went in.

The room was dim. She had left the lamp on low because Mr. Voss disliked full dark, and now the light lay across him like a sheet pulled up to the chin. He was a big man, or had been; illness had deflated him, left him looking like a coat someone had stepped out of. His hands lay on the blanket, and his eyes were open, fixed on the ceiling, moving slightly as though he were reading something written up there in very small print.

"Twenty-six," he said. "Twenty-five."

"Mr. Voss." She kept her voice soft, pitched the way you pitch your voice to horses, to frightened children, to the dying. "You're all right. I'm here."

He didn't look at her. That was the first wrongness, though she would not let herself name it wrongness. The dying looked at you. Even deep in delirium they reached for you, grasped at your sleeve, mistook you for their mothers, their dead wives, the nurses of forty years ago. They reached for the living. Mr. Voss did not reach. He counted.

"Twenty-four. Twenty-three."

She checked his pulse anyway — professional habit, and also an excuse to touch him, to anchor him to a warm human hand. His skin was cool and dry. His pulse was slow but steady, not the frantic flutter she'd have expected from a man in terminal agitation. If anything, he seemed calm. Calm as deep water.

"Twenty-two. Twenty-one. Twenty."

His lips moved around each number with a strange precision, the way a man says words in a language he is only pretending to know. Not like counting down at all, Edmund thought, and then made herself stop thinking it. Like reciting. Like keeping an appointment.

*Coincidence,* she told herself. *Morphine. Delirium. Coincidence.*

She had said those words so many times over twelve years that they had worn smooth as river stones, and she handled them now without feeling them, the way you handle rosary beads when the prayer has gone out of you.

Because here was the thing she did not say, even to herself, even in the privacy of her own skull at three in the morning: they all did it. Not every patient. But enough. Enough that she had stopped being surprised and started keeping a private tally in the back of her mind, the way other people kept grudges or debts. Mrs. Halloran, eleven years ago, counting backwards from twenty with her eyes shut and a smile on her face. The young man with the glioma who had counted from fifty, so slowly it took him two days, and died on zero exactly as the consultants predicted but somehow that felt different from prediction. Old Tom Ellery, who had counted in a language nobody in the ward recognized, and whose daughter swore afterward it was Cornish, though the family hadn't a drop of Cornish blood in five generations.

They counted backwards. Always backwards. Never up. And always toward zero.

"Nineteen," said Mr. Voss. "Eighteen."

Edmund sat down in the chair beside the bed. There was nothing else to do. You couldn't medicate a man out of his own dying, and the on-call doctor would only sigh and adjust a drip and leave her alone with it again. So she sat, and she held his hand, and she listened, because listening was the job. Listening was most of the job, if she was honest. Twelve years of sitting with the dying had taught her that medicine handled the body and left her to handle everything the body left behind.

"Seventeen. Sixteen. Fifteen."

Outside, the hospital settled into its nighttime register — the hum of the vending machines, a trolley squeaking somewhere on the second floor, the particular silence of a building full of people who are mostly asleep or mostly leaving. Edmund found herself matching her breathing to his counting, slowing herself down number by number, as though she could pace him toward some gentler arrival.

"Fourteen. Thirteen."

Her grandmother had died counting too. That was where it had started, though she rarely let the memory surface whole. She was nineteen, and she had sat by Nana's bed in the little back room of the house in Marrow Fen while the old woman's breath went thin as paper, and near the end Nana had opened her eyes — clear eyes, younger than her ninety-one years — and said, "Thirty," and begun to count.

And Edmund, nineteen years old and desperate, had counted with her. All the way down. Because it seemed kind. Because it seemed like holding her hand in another way.

Nana had stopped at zero, drawn one long breath like a woman surfacing from deep water, and smiled.

Not a peaceful smile. Edmund had spent years sanding that smile down in her memory into something bearable — relief, maybe, or recognition, seeing someone waiting on the far side. But she knew what it had been. She had seen it since, again and again, on face after face in ward after ward, and she knew. It was terror. Terror shaped like joy. The smile of a person who has just understood something enormous and unbearable and cannot, will not, look away from it.

"Twelve," said Mr. Voss. "Eleven. Ten."

His grip tightened on her hand. For the first time, his eyes moved — slid off the ceiling and came around to her, and for one moment he looked directly at her, and his mouth kept counting while his eyes did something else entirely. They pleaded. Or they pitied. She couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing was worse.

"Nine. Eight."

"Albert," she said, using his first name because the moment demanded it. "Albert, it's all right. Whatever you're seeing, you don't have to be afraid."

But he wasn't afraid. That was the horror of it, the part she never managed to explain to herself on the nights she came closest to admitting there was something to explain. He wasn't afraid of what was coming. He was afraid the way you're afraid on a rollercoaster — strapped in, delighted, appalled, unable to get off. Terror and appetite in the same face.

"Seven. Six. Five."

His breathing didn't change. That was wrong too. A dying man counting down should have been going out with the numbers, each one fainter, each breath shallower. But his breath stayed level, steady as a metronome, as though the counting weren't coming out of his dying but through it, as though he were a doorway and something on the other side were speaking politely through him.

*Stop it,* she told herself. *He's a man. He's dying. People do strange things at the end. The brain fires its last fireworks and calls them visions. It means nothing.*

"Four. Three."

The lamp flickered. Just once — a stutter of brownout, the filament browning and recovering — and Edmund glanced up at it, and when she looked back at Mr. Voss his smile had already begun.

It came up slowly, like dawn coming up over something that should never see daylight. The corners of his mouth lifted. The lines around his eyes rearranged themselves into something that on anyone else, in any other context, would have been delight.

"Two," he said, smiling. "One."

And then, softly, with the satisfaction of a man setting down a heavy load after carrying it a very long way:

"Zero."

He smiled with pure terror, and he died.

There was no drama to it. No rattle, no last gasp. His chest simply declined to rise again, the way a clock declines to tick once the spring has run out. His hand went loose in hers. The smile stayed. She had to close his jaw herself, gently, the way you close a book you've finished reading, and even then the shape of it lingered underneath her fingers, and she washed her hands afterward longer than she needed to.

* * *

The paperwork took twenty minutes. Time of death, 11:58. She wrote it in her careful nurse's hand and noted *no distress observed*, which was technically true and a lie in every way that mattered, and signed her name, and sat for a moment in the nurses' station with the fluorescent light buzzing above her like a trapped insect.

Twelve years.

Twelve years of the counting. She had never spoken of it — not to colleagues, not to the chaplain, not to the one boyfriend who'd lasted eight months before her silences wore him down. What would she even say? *The dying count backwards and stop at zero and smile like they've seen the bottom of everything?* They'd refer her to counseling. They'd be right to. Grief does strange things to the people who marinate in it; she knew the literature; she'd used the literature as a wall for twelve years, brick by peer-reviewed brick.

And yet.

She got up and walked the ward, because walking was what she did instead of thinking. Past room four, door closed now, the bed stripped and waiting for the next occupant. Past room six, where Mrs. Adeyemi slept the drugged sleep of the comfortably medicated, no numbers anywhere in her. Past the window at the end of the corridor, where the night pressed its face against the glass.

Down in the car park, under the orange light, a man was standing beside a parked car, just standing, not getting in, not going anywhere. As she watched, he turned his head and looked up — directly up, directly at her window, four floors up, behind glass, in the dark — and even at that distance she had the absurd certainty that he was smiling.

She stepped back from the window. Her heart was doing something unprofessional.

*Coincidence,* she said to the empty corridor. *A mourner. Grief makes people strange.*

The corridor said nothing back. Corridors never did. But she had the sudden, unshakeable sense of having been counted herself — tallied, entered into some ledger she couldn't see, a small neat mark placed against her name. The feeling passed in a few seconds, the way a shadow passes over water, and she made herself breathe and finish her rounds and tell herself the story she had been telling herself for twelve years:

People die. Dying minds do strange things. Numbers are nothing. Zero is nothing. It's coincidence, all of it, coincidence all the way down.

In the morning she would drive to Marrow Fen, to her mother's house, to pack up a dead woman's life into boxes. She had put the trip off for four months. She told herself it was the logistics — the leave to arrange, the estate paperwork, the sheer labor of it.

Standing at the ward window, watching the man below finally get into his car and drive away, Edmund admitted, in the place where she kept the things she refused to know, that she had delayed because Marrow Fen was where Nana had counted down to zero. Because going home meant going back to the beginning of the arithmetic.

She told herself it was coincidence, and began to pack her bag for morning, and did not sleep.
