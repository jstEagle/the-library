## 6. The Bargain of 1911

Reverend Nathaniel Sloane had been awake for three hours when the bell began to ring, and he would be awake for a great many hours after it stopped, because sleep had abandoned him the way a debtor abandons a creditor — quietly, in the dark, without leaving an address.

He sat in the vestry of St. Brannoc's-in-the-Fen with the parish register open on his knees and his predecessor's diary beneath it, both of them older than any living thing in Marrow Fen except, perhaps, the fen itself. The candle he'd lit guttered in a draught that came from nowhere, from under the floor, from the direction of the old rectory two fields away. He had not been able to make himself pray. The words rose in him like water into a drowned lung and would go no further.

The bell rang thirteen times.

It should not have rung at all. The bell of St. Brannoc's had been melted down in 1911 — he had held the receipt for its sale to a foundry in Norwich, had read the ledger entry in his predecessor's careful hand: *One bell, bronze, cracked at the crown, sold for scrap, £4 10s.* And yet the sound came through the walls as though the tower had never been empty, as though the metal remembered itself, and each stroke fell on the village like a footfall on a chest.

Sloane counted them because counting was all that was left to do. Thirteen. Not twelve, as a clock would have it. Thirteen, as the diary said it would be.

*It has come for us,* he thought, and the thought was almost a relief, the way dread is a relief when it finally arrives after years of being merely expected.

* * *

He had found the diary eleven years ago, the week he was instituted to the living, tucked behind the vestry panelling where the wood had swelled around it like flesh healing over a splinter. Its author was the Reverend Ezekiel Marsh, vicar of Marrow Fen from 1889 until his death in 1919, and its early pages were what one would expect: sermons sketched, harvests noted, the price of coal, the names of children baptised. Marsh had a neat, upright hand and a habit of underlining words twice when they displeased him.

The winter of 1911 changed the hand. Sloane had watched it happen across forty pages — the uprightness sagging, the ink thinning, then thickening, pressed so hard the nib tore through in places, as if the man writing were no longer holding a pen but gripping a rope.

*January 3rd.* The flour is gone. The potatoes are gone. Mrs. Tregale boiled the last of the hides yesterday and called it soup. I buried two children this week and could not dig the graves deep enough for the frost, so I covered them with stones and prayed the Lord would consider the intention. I have prayed every day since November and the Lord has not answered, or His answer is the silence between the wind and the reeds, which sounds very much like nothing at all.

*January 9th.* Old Mother Quill says there is a door under the rectory cellar. She says her grandmother knew of it, and her grandmother's grandmother before that, back to the days when the fen took a tithe of every third child and the village learned to live small so as not to be noticed by what lived beneath. She says the door opens inward, and that it is warm behind it, warm as a beast's belly, and that in the hungry years of 1743 the village went down to it and came back fed.

I told her she spoke superstition. She looked at me with such pity that I have not slept since.

*January 14th.* I went down.

I will set down plainly what I saw, though I know how it will read, and I do not care. There are nine of us left who can still walk to the church. The rest lie in their beds waiting to stop. If God will not feed us, then I will knock at whatever door will open.

The rectory has stood empty since the fever of '09. Its cellar stairs go down further than the house stands tall — I counted thirty-one steps and the house above cannot be more than fourteen feet from floor to eaves. At the bottom there is a wall that is not a wall. It gave when I pushed, the way a cheek gives. Behind it there was warmth and a smell of loam and candlewax, and a light that did not flicker, and something sitting in that light that had been waiting a long time to be asked.

I did not see its face. I want to write that I did not see its face, but the truth is worse: I saw every face. It wore them the way a man wears coats, one over another, and underneath them all there was only patience. It spoke — or I understood it; I cannot say which — and it said: *You are hungry. Hunger is a debt. All debts can be settled.*

I asked it what it wanted. It said: *Remembrance. The dead owe their memories to no one. Bring me theirs, and I will keep your living alive.*

*January 15th.* We met in the church, the nine of us, and I told them, and not one of them laughed. That is the measure of our desperation — that not one of us laughed.

We argued till dawn about whose dead we would give. Widow Petch refused to surrender her husband's memory of their wedding day; she wept and struck at Josiah Crane with her stick. But by morning even she had gone quiet, and she said, in a voice I shall hear until my own death: "Better they forget me than the children starve forgetting everything."

So we carried them down. Not their bodies — their bodies stayed in the churchyard, frozen past digging. We carried their names, spoken aloud at the bottom of those stairs, and the thing below took the memories out of the ground itself, out of the graves, and we felt them go. I cannot describe it better than that. A coldness passed through the fen from east to west, like a tide drawing back, and where it had been there was a strange lightness, as of a room after furniture is removed.

And the fish came back to the dykes. And the turnips broke the frozen ground green. And Mrs. Tregale's son, who had not risen in six days, sat up and asked for bread.

God forgive me. I have read Job backwards and forwards all my life and never understood until now that the devil also walks to and fro in the earth. We did not sell our souls. We sold something we told ourselves mattered less. I underline the word *less* twice, and my pen goes through the page.

*February 2nd.* The prosperity holds. But today Widow Petch asked me why she keeps a wedding ring on a chain at her throat when she cannot remember whose hand it was measured for. She smiled as she asked it. That smile was the most terrible thing I have seen in this fen, and I have seen the thing below.

*March 30th.* It has made terms plain. What it gives, it gives for a season — a generation, it says, in its accounting, which is longer than ours. When the term ends, the bell will ring thirteen, and payment falls due again: more memories, or the lives themselves, returned with interest. It used the word *interest*. I have been a clergyman thirty years and I have preached on usury, and now I find myself the broker of it.

I have hidden this book where my successor may find it, if the fen suffers him to live long enough to look. To him I say: I am sorry. I was hungry, and I was proud, and I believed myself the shepherd when in truth I was only the first through the gate.

The flock follows whoever opens the gate. Remember that. Remember it when they thank you.

* * *

Sloane closed the diary and sat in the dark of the vestry with his hands flat on its cover, feeling the grain of the leather the way a man feels a pulse.

Eleven years he had carried this knowledge. Eleven years of christenings and funerals and harvest festivals, of standing in a pulpit under a bell-less tower, preaching a God whose silence he had priced and purchased around. And in all that time he had told himself the story the way his predecessors must have told it: that it was necessity, that it was mercy, that the alternative was graves full of children. He had even, in his weaker moments, called it stewardship.

That was the word that had corrupted them all, he thought. Not greed. Greed was crude and recognisable. It was *stewardship* — the decent man's word for keeping the machine running — that had turned nine starving villagers into a hundred complicit ones, generation after generation, each inheriting the debt and the vocabulary to disguise it.

His own turn had come twenty years ago. He had gone down the thirty-one steps with a candle and a list of names — the recently dead of Marrow Fen, their memories weighed like coin — and he had paid them out to buy his daughter Grace another winter, and then another, and the thing below had been courteous, had thanked him, had spoken of *renewal* and *good standing* as though they were neighbours discussing a hedgerow. He had come up the stairs lighter, and told himself, kneeling in his study with his face in his hands, that any father would have done the same.

Any father would have. That was the horror of it. The bargain did not need monsters. It needed only love, and hunger, and someone willing to spend other people's pasts.

And tonight the term had ended.

* * *

He heard the cars before he understood what he was hearing — engines running, unattended, idling in lanes all across the fen, a low communal murmur like the village breathing in its sleep. Through the vestry window he could see the Hendersons' Land Rover stopped crooked in the middle of Church Lane, headlights burning, doors open, empty. Down at the Green, lights burned in windows behind curtains that no one drew.

Every soul in Marrow Fen asleep, mid-motion, mid-meal, mid-life. Every soul except him.

Except him — and, he would learn within the hour, except one other: a woman he did not yet know, driving a car that circled the same crossroads again and again, unable to leave. He did not know her name yet. But he knew, with the certainty of a man reading the last page of a ledger, that the bell had rung thirteen times for a reason, and that the reason was walking toward the rectory.

Sloane took up the candle and the diary and went out into the street.

The cold was extraordinary — not the cold of January but of something older, a cold with intent in it, flowing low along the ground from the direction of the old rectory, from the cellar that went down farther than the house stood tall. And on the air, faint as a hymn heard through a wall, came a sound that stopped him at his own gate:

Counting.

A woman's voice, somewhere in the sleeping village, counting backwards from thirty. Then another voice joining it. Then another. The whole village murmuring numbers in its sleep, descending together toward zero, like a congregation reciting a creed written by something else entirely.

Sloane stood in the lane with his candle shaking in his hand and understood, at last, the shape of the debt. It was not memories it had come to collect tonight. It was the principal. A century of borrowed life, called in all at once, and the sleepers were the ledger closing line by line.

"Lord," he said, and the word cracked in the middle. "Lord, show me what to do."

What answered was not the Lord. Down the lane, a door opened. A figure stepped out of a house with its eyes shut and began to walk toward the rectory, and behind it another, and another, the whole village rising from its beds and tables to file silently through the frost, drawn home to the thing that owned them.

And among the sleepers, clear and terrible, Sloane heard a voice he knew — a voice that had sung in his congregation, that had argued with him about flower rota, that had baked him bread at Christmas —

His own dead, walking. His whole parish, marching. And the night was long, and dawn was four hours away, and the clock in the vestry, when he turned to look at it, was running backwards.
