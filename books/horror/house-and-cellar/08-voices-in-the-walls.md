## 8. Voices in the Walls

For a long time after Sloane spoke those three words — *It rang for you* — Edmund stood in the vicarage doorway and let the cold of the night press against her like a hand testing fruit at market.

Then she went home.

Not because home was safe. Because it was the only direction her body remembered how to take. Her feet found the lane by instinct, past the sleeping post office with its letters still in the slot, past the green where the maypole leaned like a man too tired to stand, past the war memorial that listed no names after 1911 because there had been no one left to carve them. The moon sat low and swollen over the fen, the color of an old pearl, and beneath her shoes the road was soft as flesh.

Her mother's house rose out of the dark the way it always had: narrow, brick-shouldered, its chimney like a raised finger. Edmund let herself in with the key she had carried for eleven years without once using it, and the smell of the hallway undid her a little — lavender polish, old paper, the ghost of toast. Home smells. She had forgotten she knew them.

She did not turn on the lights. Some animal part of her understood that light would be a declaration, and she was not ready to declare anything to whatever was listening.

In the kitchen she lit instead a single candle she found in the drawer where her mother had kept string and batteries and the small useless hoard of a careful woman. The flame stood perfectly still, as if afraid to move. Edmund sat at the table with her hands wrapped around a mug of water she had no intention of drinking, and she listened to the silence of Marrow Fen.

It was not silent.

* * *

At first she thought it was the wind moving through the hedges, that long fenland soughing that had sung her to sleep as a child. But wind does not keep time. Wind does not shuffle. What she heard was footsteps — dozens of them, hundreds, uneven and unhurried, dragging through grass and gravel and mud, all of them moving in the same direction, all of them converging on the rectory lane the way streams converge on a sinkhole.

Edmund went to the window and pulled back the curtain an inch.

They came up the lane from the village like a congregation after a midnight service that had never been announced. Mrs. Achebe first, still in her dressing gown, her slippers black with dew, her arms hanging loose as cut marionette's arms. Behind her the Henderson twins, who ran the farm shop, walking shoulder to shoulder with their eyes closed and their mouths slightly open, breathing in unison. Behind them more — the whole village, it seemed, every soul Sloane had said was asleep, now awake in the way that furniture is awake when something else moves it. Their faces were slack and peaceful and utterly vacant, and they murmured as they walked.

That was the worst of it. They murmured.

The sounds came across the night in fragments, half-swallowed, like words remembered underwater. A woman's voice saying *the blue dress, I wore the blue dress and he never even looked*. A man's voice repeating *fourteen pounds ten, fourteen pounds ten, that's what the cow made at market* over and over with the tenderness of a prayer. A child's voice — a child's voice, God help her, though every child in Marrow Fen was grown and grey — singing four bars of a skipping song and then losing the tune and starting again, losing it, starting again, a music box winding down forever.

They were reciting their memories. Or what was left of them. Scraps and leavings, the things the thing below hadn't wanted or hadn't finished taking, spilling out of their mouths as they walked because there was nothing left inside to hold them down.

Edmund's breath fogged the glass. She wiped it with her sleeve and watched, and told herself — the old litany, twelve years of it — *coincidence, sleep disorder, mass hysteria, there is always an explanation if you look hard enough.* But the litany had gone thin as paper, and behind it she could see the truth standing there plain as a figure at a window: these people were being collected. Their memories had been spent a century ago, spent on harvests and health and luck, and tonight the debt had come due, and the debt was walking itself to the collector's door.

She should have felt horror. She did feel horror. But underneath the horror, shamefully, she felt something older and more familiar: recognition. Twelve years in the counting ward had taught her this rhythm — the murmuring of the dying, the fragments, the backwards numbers. She had always thought she was witnessing death. Now she understood she had been witnessing collection all along. She simply hadn't known whose ledger it fed.

* * *

She saw her mother third from the end of the line.

Edmund knew her before her mind agreed to know her — by the walk, the particular roll of the shoulders, the way her head tipped slightly left as if listening to someone just behind her. Margaret Dunmore had been dead five weeks. Edmund had signed the certificate herself, had watched the coffin go down into ground that was soft and black and hungry-looking, had thrown the handful of earth and felt it land heavier than earth should.

And here she came up the lane in her burial dress, dew on her shoulders, eyes closed, mouth moving.

Edmund did not remember crossing the kitchen. She was outside before she knew she'd moved, barefoot on the frozen mud, candlelight nowhere, moonlight everywhere, and the sleepers parted around her without pausing, without noticing, flowing past like water around a stone. She caught her mother's arm.

It was warm. That was the detail that broke her. Not cold like the dead, not clammy — warm, alive-warm, the warmth of a woman walking in her sleep, and under Edmund's fingers the pulse beat steady and patient as a metronome.

"Mum," she said. "Mum, wake up. It's me. It's Eddie."

Her mother's face did not change. The slack peace held, serene as a portrait. But her mouth kept moving, and now, this close, Edmund could hear what she was saying, and the world tilted sideways and poured her out onto the ice of it.

"Thirty," her mother whispered. "Twenty-nine. Twenty-eight."

Counting. Backwards. The way they all counted, the dying in her ward, twelve years of them, thirty to zero, always thirty to zero, and Edmund had built a career on pretending it was coincidence and now her dead mother stood in the moonlight counting toward zero with a smile of terrible contentment curdling at the corners of her mouth.

"No." Edmund shook her. Gently, then not gently. "No, Mum, you don't do this, you don't get to do this—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the little girl sound of it. "You knew. You knew about all of this, didn't you? You stayed here your whole life, you never left, you never told me—"

"Twenty-four," said her mother.

"Tell me what to do!" Edmund screamed it into the sleeping face, into the whole shuffling congregation, into the fen. "Somebody tell me what to do!"

Nobody answered. The sleepers walked on. And her mother, patient as tide, disengaged her arm from her daughter's grip with a smooth mechanical courtesy that was worse than any struggle, and continued up the lane toward the rectory, counting.

"Twenty-three. Twenty-two."

Edmund stood in the middle of the road and wept, briefly, furiously, the way you bleed — because the body insists. Then she wiped her face with both hands and followed.

Because the counting was familiar. Because she had spent twelve years learning its grammar. And because somewhere in the arithmetic of it, some nurse's instinct she couldn't switch off was already doing the math: her mother had started at thirty. Everyone in the ward started at thirty. Thirty counts, however slow the tongue, however broken the breath. When they reached zero—

She didn't let herself finish the thought. But her feet quickened.

* * *

The rectory stood at the end of the lane where the fen began, a long low house the color of wet ash, and it had been empty for as long as Edmund could remember — boarded, condemned, the sort of place children dared each other to touch. Its windows were boarded still. But light leaked from under the front door, a warm yellow glow like candlelight, like lamplight, like the light of a hundred years of hearths burning all at once behind one thin door.

The villagers gathered before it in the field, not pressing, not crowding — arranging themselves. They settled into rows, into something horribly like pews, facing the door, and they knelt, all of them together, a thousand joints cracking in chorus, and they bowed their heads. Mrs. Achebe's lips moved silently. The Henderson twins held hands. And Margaret Dunmore knelt among them in her burial dress with her daughter's name caught between her teeth and swallowed, counting.

"Nineteen," she said. "Eighteen."

Edmund crouched at the edge of the field, hidden by a drystone wall furred with moss, and pressed her fist against her mouth.

The murmuring of the crowd had changed. It had been fragments before — scraps of lives, blue dresses and cattle prices and skipping songs. Now the fragments were braiding together. Voices overlapped and harmonized, and out of the overlap came sentences none of them could have spoken alone:

*We sold the winter and bought the spring.*

*We sold our mothers' faces and bought our children's bread.*

*We sold remembering and bought remaining.*

*One hundred years. One hundred years. One hundred years.*

A century of voices speaking in unison, and under it, threading through it, individual threads still unraveling — a man whispering *I never told her I was sorry, I never*, a woman humming a lullaby with three notes missing, an old soldier reciting a name that might have been his brother's, over and over, *Tommy, Tommy, Tommy,* each repetition fainter than the last, like a coin wearing thin.

This was what a memory sounded like when it was spent. This was the small change of a hundred years of luck. Every prosperous harvest, every fever that broke, every child that lived past its fifth birthday in Marrow Fen since 1911 — paid for with this. With blue dresses and apologies never made and lullabies missing their middles.

And her mother's voice rising clear above it all, steady, unhurried, a professional to the last:

"Seventeen. Sixteen."

Edmund realized she was gripping the wall so hard the moss had come away in her fists. She made herself breathe. In through the nose, the way she taught the families of the dying. Out through the mouth. You cannot save them, Edmund. You have never once been able to save them. All you can do is be present. All you can do is witness.

But witnessing had never meant anything before. Witnessing had been the lie she told herself so the counting wouldn't mean anything. Tonight it meant everything. Tonight the counting was her mother, kneeling in the frost, spending down the last of whatever was left of her, and dawn was hours away, and zero was coming for all of them at the speed of a whispered number.

"Fifteen," said Margaret Dunmore.

And then two things happened at once.

The light under the rectory door went out — not dimmed, but drawn inward, sucked away like breath, leaving the door a black seam in the world. And every sleeper in the field turned their head. Together. Smoothly. Faces still slack, eyes still closed, but heads rotating toward the wall where Edmund hid, toward the exact spot where she crouched, with the precision of a hundred compass needles finding north.

The murmuring stopped.

In the silence Edmund could hear her own heart, and beneath it, faint and patient, rising out of the ground itself, out of the cellar beneath the ash-colored house, a voice made of every voice she had ever nursed — Mr. Pryce from bed four, the young mother in the corner room, all of them, all the counters, layered into one — and it said, kindly, in the tone of a hospice nurse waking a patient gently:

"You can come in now, Edmund. Your mother saved you a seat."

In the field, her mother smiled — wide, warm, wrong — and said:

"Fourteen."
