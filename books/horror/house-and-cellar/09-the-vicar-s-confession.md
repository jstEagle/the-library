## 9. The Vicar's Confession

The rectory kitchen had not changed in twenty years, and that was the first thing that shamed him. Thomas Sloane stood at the same counter where he had once measured out tea for a congregation of the living, and now he measured out silence for a congregation of the sleeping, and his hands shook in exactly the same way they had shaken the night he signed his name in the ledger.

Edmund Dunmore sat across from him at the scrubbed pine table, her mother's papers spread between them like a spread of tarot cards, and she was looking at him with her mother's eyes. That was the cruelty of it. He had buried Margaret Dunmore's secrets along with Margaret herself — or he had believed he had — and now here came the daughter, carrying the same steady gaze, the same refusal to look away, the same terrible patience of a woman who has spent her life watching people die and refuses to flinch from it.

"You knew," Edmund said. It was not an accusation. That was worse. Accusations could be answered.

"I knew," Sloane said.

The kettle on the stove had gone cold an hour ago, along with everything else in Marrow Fen. The clock above the door had stopped at 12:13, its second hand trembling against the glass like an insect trapped under ice. Outside the window, the village lay still as a photograph. He could see the Hendersons' porch light burning with no one home to see it. He could see the shape of old Mrs. Achebe's cat, frozen mid-leap from a garden wall, and it hurt him to look at, the way a dislocated joint hurts — everything correct and everything wrong at once.

"Tell me," Edmund said. "All of it. My mother's notes stop in 1998. They stop in the middle of a sentence."

He knew the sentence. He had read that diary entry a hundred times in the dark, the way a man reads the confession he has never managed to speak aloud. *Sloane is going to do it. He is going to do it and I cannot stop him and God forgive me I understand why—*

"Her name was Grace," he said.

* * *

She had been born in the spring of 1979, in the back bedroom of the old vicarage, and she had been the easiest thing in his life. That was how he thought of her even then, though he would never have said it to anyone: easy. His faith had been a labor — sermons wrung out of him like water from a stone, prayers recited into a silence that never once answered — but Grace had come into the world already laughing, already reaching for things, already certain the world was made of light. He had loved her the way you love a window in a dark house.

She was nineteen when the tiredness started. Twenty when the doctors at the county hospital used the word *leukaemia* in the gentle voices doctors use, the voices that are worse than shouting. Twenty-one when the treatments stopped working and the hospital sent her home with a bag of medication and a pamphlet about hospice care, and Sloane had sat in the parking lot and read that pamphlet three times and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the Church had nothing. That he had spent thirty years serving a God who did not pick up, and his daughter was going to die in the back bedroom where she had been born, and there was nothing above that could help him.

But there was something below.

"I'd known since I was a curate," he said. "Reverend Marsh showed me the ledger my first month in the parish. I think he wanted a witness. I think it's the kind of thing you can't carry alone." He turned his teacup in its saucer, around and around, though there was no tea in it and had not been for an hour. "The bargain of 1911. The winter the whole village died. Marsh's predecessor — Reverend Cobb — he wrote it all down in the parish diary, and the diary passes to whoever holds the living. It's in the cellar of this house. Or it was. I burned it, after."

"You burned the record."

"I burned my record." He looked up at her. "The village's history is in your mother's papers. Mine is in my chest, and I would give a great deal to cut it out."

* * *

The night Grace's fever spiked to 104 and the doctor said *days, Reverend, I'm sorry, days*, Sloane had gone down the cellar stairs of the rectory carrying a hurricane lamp and the parish ledger, and he had spoken aloud to the darkness the way Cobb's diary instructed, and the darkness had answered.

He had never described it to anyone, because there had never been anyone to describe it to. Margaret Dunmore had guessed — she had guessed everything, that sharp, sorrowful woman — but she had never asked him directly, and he had never volunteered, and between them had grown a silence as thick as the fen fog. Now the silence had to break, and he found it broke easily, like ice in spring, like a scab, like all the things you think will hold forever and hold for nothing at all.

"It doesn't look like anything," he told Edmund. "That's the first thing you have to understand. I went down those stairs expecting — I don't know. A demon. Horns. Something out of the woodcuts in the old Bibles. And there was a chair. A plain wooden chair at a plain wooden table, and a candle burning, and the candle had been burning so long the wax had built up around it like a small white mountain. And a voice. It used — " His throat closed. He made himself go on. "It used Grace's voice. Not from the room above. From years ago. From when she was small, calling down the stairs that dinner was ready. *Daddy, it's ready.* It knew I would follow a voice like that anywhere."

Edmund's face did not change, but her hands, flat on the table, went very still.

"It was courteous. That's the thing no one believes. It asked me to sit. It asked after Grace — asked after her *by name*, and it knew her blood count, knew the name of the consultant, knew the exact words the doctor had used in the parking lot. It had been listening. It had always been listening. And then it explained the arrangement, the way a bank manager explains an overdraft. The village owed it. Had owed it since 1911, when Cobb and the others came down and traded. And the debt had been allowed to run, generation after generation, because each vicar in turn had come down and made a small withdrawal against the principal. Marsh took twenty years for his wife's arthritis. The one before him took a son out of the war. And each withdrawal was paid for — " He stopped.

"Paid for with what?" Edmund asked, though he could see she already knew. Her mother's papers were full of it. The parish registers with their impossible gaps. The harvest records. The census returns that made no sense.

"Memories," Sloane said. "Other people's memories. That's the currency. It doesn't want your money — what would it do with money? It wants the interior lives of the people above. A wedding day. A mother's face. The smell of a childhood kitchen. It takes them out of the villagers like coins from a purse, and the villagers wake the next morning with a small hole in them they can't find, and the village goes on being lucky. The crops come in. The sickness passes over. The young people stay, because Marrow Fen feels like somewhere you belong, and belonging — " he laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, "— belonging is the product, Edmund. That's what it sells. And everyone in this village has been paying for it for a century without knowing they were paying."

"And Grace," Edmund said quietly. "What did you pay for Grace?"

* * *

The candle had burned down to the table by the time the terms were settled. He remembered that detail with horrible precision — the wax mountain flattened, the flame guttering in a draught that came from nowhere, the voice patient and warm as a hand on his shoulder.

Grace would live. Not a miracle cure — the thing below did not do miracles, it did *accounting* — but a remission, a real one, the disease driven down and down until the consultants used the word *dormant* and shook their heads at their own charts. Twenty years, the voice had said. Perhaps more. It had never lied to him. That was the other thing no one believed. In twenty years it had never once lied.

The price: one hundred memories. Drawn from the village, at its own choosing, over the course of a year. Small ones, mostly — it preferred small ones, it said; the large ones were fatty, overworked, full of other people's contamination — but a hundred of them, from a hundred of his parishioners. The first kiss of a girl on Church Lane, taken the night before her wedding, so that she walked up the aisle feeling fondness where the love had been and never knew why the marriage felt like a house with one window bricked over. The tune old Achebe had hummed to each of his children, gone from his mouth one morning, so that he whistled nothing at all and his children grew up unhummed-to. The face of a dead mother, fading from a grieving son like a photograph left in the sun.

And one large one. The voice had asked for one large one, and it had named it, and Sloane had said yes, because Grace was dying, and there is no word for what a man becomes when his child is dying. There is no word because there is no man left to name.

"It took Margaret's memory of the day her son was born," he said.

Edmund's breath went out of her slowly. "My brother."

"She came to me a week later. She stood where you're sitting and she said, *Thomas, I've lost something and I don't know what it is, and it's the worst thing that's ever happened to me, and I can't even grieve it properly because I can't find its shape.*" His voice cracked on the last word. "She knew. She worked it out within the year — she was the only one who ever did. And she never told the village, and she never told you, and she never forgave me, and she stayed. She stayed and she watched and she wrote everything down and she kept Grace alive too, in her way, because Grace needed a village that didn't ask questions. Your mother carried this parish's sin on her back for twenty years so that my daughter could live in it comfortably. And I let her. God help me, I let her, because the alternative was a grave."

* * *

Grace had lived.

She had lived, and the remission had held, and she had married a soft-spoken surveyor from the next county and moved into the cottage on Fen Edge Lane, and Sloane had walked her up the aisle feeling like a man walking on the roof of a burning building. Every year on her birthday he had counted. Every year the voice below had been silent, the ledger balanced, the debt running quietly under the village like a drain. Twenty years. The thing below had said *perhaps more*, and it had not lied, but it had not told him everything either, because on the night of Grace's fortieth birthday the tiredness had come back.

Not the leukaemia. Something else. A sleeping sickness, the doctors called it, though they had never seen its like — she had gone to bed on a Tuesday in October and had not woken, and the consultants had gathered at her bedside with their scans and their shrugs, and Sloane had sat in the corridor and understood, with the same falling clarity as before, that the account had come due. Twenty years, the voice had said. It had never lied. It had simply never said what happened *after* the twenty years.

He had gone down the cellar stairs again in October, three months ago, and the chair had been waiting, and the candle had been lit, and the voice had used Grace's voice again — her adult voice this time, the voice from the phone calls, *Daddy, I'll ring you Sunday* — and it had told him, gently, that the principal was called in now. A century of interest on the bargain of 1911. The whole village, or a settlement. And it had named its settlement, and the settlement had been Edmund Dunmore, and Sloane had said no.

He had said no, and the voice had thanked him for his honesty, and had told him the bell would ring when it was ready, and had asked him — courteously, terribly courteously — to keep the matter private until then.

"So you knew it would ring for her," Edmund said. "You knew, and you said nothing. You let her drive into this."

"I tried to warn her. I telephoned. She didn't answer — I think her mother's letters frightened her off my calls. I stood at her gate on Tuesday and I couldn't make myself knock." He pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. "Twenty years ago I sold a hundred of my parishioners to save one girl, and I told myself it was love. And then I stood at your gate and couldn't knock, and I understood that it was never love. Love would have knocked. It was fear wearing love's coat, the same as it's always been. The same as it was for Cobb in 1911, and Marsh, and every vicar in between. We are not the keepers of this village, Edmund. We are its bookkeepers, and the books have come due, and I am the last of a long line of men who signed their names."

* * *

Edmund was quiet for a long time. Through the window, the fen lay silver-grey under a sky that refused to lighten. Four hours to dawn, the kitchen clock said, though the clock had stopped and the hour meant nothing now; he had the growing, sickening sense that time in the fen had come loose from its moorings, that the night was going to be as long as the thing below required it to be.

"Grace," Edmund said at last. "Your daughter. Where is she now?"

The question he had been dreading. He stood, because he could not answer it sitting down, and walked to the window, and looked out at the sleeping village, at the cottage on Fen Edge Lane just visible past the churchyard wall.

"She's asleep," he said. "Like the others. She was at home with her husband when the bell rang — he's asleep too, sitting up at the kitchen table, his tea going cold in front of him. I went to her this morning, before I came to find you. I sat on her bed and I held her hand and I talked to her, the way they tell you to talk to people in comas. And she opened her eyes." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "She looked straight through me. Not unconscious — *asleep*, but walking, the way they all walk. She got up and stood at the window and she was saying something under her breath, over and over, and I got close enough to hear it, and Edmund — she was counting. Backwards. From a hundred. And when I said her name she didn't turn, and when I took her hand she let me hold it the way you'd let a stranger hold it at a bus stop, politely, waiting for the bus."

He turned back to the table. Edmund had gone very pale.

"It doesn't know me anymore," he said. "I spent its currency to buy her life, and now the account is closed, and it has taken her back along with everything else — and the first thing it took was me. Twenty years of Sundays. Twenty years of birthday calls. All of it, gone out of her like coins from a purse. I am a stranger standing in my daughter's bedroom, and I did that. Me. With the best intentions in the world, which I now understand are the most expensive intentions there are."

The words hung in the cold kitchen. Somewhere far off — too far off to be outside, too close to be imagined — came a sound like a great slow breath, or a bell that had learned to whisper.

And then, beneath it, faint and rising, from every sleeping house in Marrow Fen, from every open window and every dreaming mouth: the murmuring began. Hundreds of voices, half-voiced, sleep-soft, all saying numbers.

Edmund was already on her feet. "What is that? What are they doing?"

Sloane looked out at the village and felt his heart go cold, because the sleepers were not in their beds anymore. They were in their gardens. Their lanes. Their doorways. Moving — slowly, all together, in the same direction — toward the rectory. And the numbers were falling as they walked.

"They're counting down," he said. "And they're coming here."
