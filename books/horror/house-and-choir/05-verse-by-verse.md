## 5. Verse by Verse

The first verse came to her in a dream of the orchard, which was strange, because the orchard had been cut down before Beatrix was born. She knew it only from a photograph in the front hall: twelve gnarled apple trees in a row, and her grandmother standing among them with her hands folded like a woman at prayer. In the dream the trees were alive, heavy with fruit that gleamed too red, and someone was walking the row behind her, singing.

The melody was simple. That was the horror of it, though she didn't understand that yet — she only understood that it was simple, the way a lullaby is simple, the way a thing designed to be carried in the mouth of a child must be simple. Six notes, rising, then a fall like a sigh. Words in a language she half-recognized, the old Danish her grandmother had spoken to the walls when she thought no one listened. *Husets hjerte, husets hånd* — the house's heart, the house's hand. Beatrix woke with the syllables sitting in her mouth like smooth stones, and she lay in the gray light of dawn with her lips pressed hard together, refusing, absolutely refusing, to let them out.

She got up. She found the ledger where she had left it, open on the desk in her sister's old room, and she wrote the verse down. Not in notation — she had never learned notation — but in phonetics, the way you might write down a word in a language you intended to betray. She looked at it on the page and felt nothing. That was the trick, she thought. On paper it was only sounds. On paper it could not reach her.

Downstairs, the smell of coffee. Mrs. Aldous — the housekeeper who had served the Eriksens since before Beatrix's father was born, whose hands had shaken for the last decade — was standing at the stove with her back straight as a girl's, humming.

Beatrix stopped in the doorway.

Mrs. Aldous turned, and her face was serene in a way that Beatrix had no word for yet. Not happy. Serene the way a pond is serene, the way a held breath is serene. "Good morning, Miss Beatrix," she said. "Sleep well?"

"Mrs. Aldous. Are you — you're humming."

"Am I?" The old woman smiled and poured the coffee, and her hands did not shake at all. "I suppose I am. It's a fine morning for it."

* * *

The second verse came the next night, and this time the dream was the house itself — Eriksen House as it must have been a hundred years ago, every window lit, the halls full of people. Servants in dark dresses and white aprons, footmen, a governess, a cook with flour to her elbows, and every one of them singing as they worked, the way people sing without knowing they sing. The second verse braided itself into the first, a harmony line that answered the melody the way an echo answers a shout, and Beatrix walked through the dream-house listening, and it was — she could not deny it, even alone in her own skull — it was beautiful.

She woke with tears on her face and did not know when they had started.

She wrote the second verse beneath the first. The phonetics came easier this time, as though her hand had learned the shape of the song overnight, as though the song had been practicing her hand. She sat back and looked at the two verses together and saw the structure the way she had once seen the structure of her sister's stories — where it was going, what it was building toward, the shape of the room it intended to become. Two verses. There would be more. And somewhere at the end of it, a space with her name on it.

At breakfast there was no Mrs. Aldous. There was a note on the kitchen table in the housekeeper's careful copperplate: *Gone to my sister's in Aalborg. Don't trouble yourself, Miss Beatrix. I am where I'm meant to be.* The coffee had been made anyway, and it was perfect, and beside the pot the bread had been set out with butter and jam as though the kitchen itself had learned her habits in one night.

Beatrix stood in the empty kitchen for a long time. Then she ate, because her body was a traitor and the bread was good, and she hated herself a little for how good it was, for how the house fed her and kept her and made itself easy to love.

* * *

The third verse arrived on the third night, and the dream changed. No orchard now, no bright old house. She was in the chapel beneath the floors — she knew it was the chapel, though she had never seen it — and the choir was arranged in rows on stone benches, and they were singing the three verses together, and in the second row, third from the left, was her sister.

Not dead. Not decayed. Astrid stood with her hands folded, her chipped canine visible when her mouth opened on the syllables, and her eyes were closed, and her face was the face of someone deeply, perfectly asleep. When the verse ended the choir breathed as one body, and Astrid breathed with them, and Beatrix tried to run to her and the dream would not let her legs obey, and Astrid's eyes opened — just her sister's eyes, just for a moment, finding Beatrix across the stone room with an expression Beatrix would spend the rest of her life trying to forget. Not pleading. *Warning.*

Then the third verse began, and it was the most beautiful thing Beatrix had ever heard, and she woke with her own throat working silently around the melody, shaping it, and she had to press her face into the pillow and scream without sound until the wanting went out of her muscles.

She wrote it down. Her hand was steady. She was almost proud of her hand.

* * *

The days developed a rhythm, and the rhythm was the trap. Mornings: the house quieter by one person, or stranger by one person. The gardener, Halvorsen, who had grumbled his way through forty years of Eriksen winters, was found on the fourth morning sitting in the ruined greenhouse with dirt under his nails and a smile on his face, planting things in the dead beds. "They'll come up," he told Beatrix cheerfully, patting soil over seeds she was fairly sure were stones. "Everything comes up here, miss. You'll see." The cook left no note at all; her room was simply empty, her shoes gone, her coat gone, and the front door — which Beatrix had begun locking at night out of a superstition she refused to examine — was unlocked, and no one had heard it open.

The nights: the song, one verse longer each time, and each verse more beautiful than the last, the way a staircase is more beautiful the higher it takes you, the way you stop noticing you are climbing. By the sixth night there were six verses and the dream-choir had grown and Beatrix had begun to recognize faces in it. The governess from the photograph in the hall. A footman she'd never seen but somehow knew was called Anders. Her grandmother, hands folded, singing with her eyes closed and her mouth soft. And always Astrid, second row, third from the left, always asleep, always warning.

Beatrix transcribed everything. The ledger's blank back pages filled with her phonetic script, verse under verse, and she studied them in daylight the way a chess player studies a position — not to play, but to see the whole board. And the more she saw, the more she understood, and the understanding was its own kind of seduction. The song was not random. It was architecture. Each verse added a load-bearing element; each melody answered the last with a logic so clean it felt less like music than like mathematics, or like law. Six verses built something. Seven, she was certain, would complete it. And the seventh would need a voice that was not yet in the choir, because the song was not a relic, it was a machine, and machines require fuel, and the fuel was always a new voice, freely given.

*Freely given.* She remembered the phrase from the ledger, from the wages paid in verse-counts, from the entries that ended when the name was struck through. No one had ever been forced. That was the thing Aunt Maren would not say and the house did not need to. The house did not drag. The house *invited*, and its invitation was a melody you could not stop hearing, and its price was a belonging so warm and total that everything outside it — grief, doubt, the cold gray world of mortgages and funerals and wrong bodies in coffins — came to feel like the dream.

On the seventh morning Beatrix caught herself humming.

She was in the pantry, looking for tea, and the first verse came out of her before she knew it was there — six notes, rising, the fall like a sigh — and the pantry went utterly, terribly still. The jars on the shelves seemed to lean in. Somewhere below the floor, very far down, something paused to listen.

Beatrix stood with her hand over her own mouth, heart slamming, and did not finish the phrase. She did not let the seventh note fall. She backed out of the pantry and did not take the tea.

That night she tied a scarf around her jaw before she slept, a childish, desperate precaution, and the dream came anyway, and the seventh verse was there, waiting for her, and it was so beautiful that she woke weeping with her teeth clenched through the wool, and the melody sat behind her eyes all morning like a guest who would not leave.

She wrote it down. Seven verses on the page. She looked at them and understood that she now knew the entire song — that the house had given it to her, all of it, because the house believed the giving was the same as the taking. That it was only a matter of time. That a voice will always, eventually, do what a voice knows.

She was wrong about one thing, and she would not learn it until it was nearly too late: she thought the seventh verse was the end of the song. It was not. It was the door.

But that morning, the morning after the seventh verse, Beatrix came downstairs to find the front hall full of light and the sound of singing — real singing, awake singing, thin and cracked and unmistakable — coming from the direction of the cellar stairs.

Aunt Maren was standing at the bottom of the steps, in her nightgown, in the cold, with the old iron door standing open before her, and she was singing the first verse in a voice that had not cracked in sixty years.
