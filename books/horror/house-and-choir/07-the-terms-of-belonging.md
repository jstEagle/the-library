## 7. The Terms of Belonging

Beatrix did not sleep. She sat in the study with the ledger open before her, the lamp turned high, and she watched the ink.

It had been writing since midnight.

She had found it by accident — or by invitation, which was perhaps the same thing in this house. She had gone looking for the page that held her sister's name, the one struck through in the old hand, and she had found instead a fresh entry, the ink still wet enough to shine, in a script that was not her aunt's and not her own and not any hand she had ever seen, and yet was all of them at once: the looping E of Great-Grandmother Astrid, the cramped severity of the stewards who had kept the books through the war years, the slanting, hurried letters of a scullery maid from some century she couldn't name. Every hand the house had ever held, braided into one.

*The twin below is well,* the ledger had written. *She asks after you.*

Beatrix had not touched the book for an hour after that. She had stood at the window with her arms wrapped around herself, watching the excavation pit below the east lawn, where the tarps over the broken stairwell stirred in the wind like something breathing under a sheet. Then she had come back to the desk, because there was nowhere else in the world to go, and because the alternative to knowing was imagining, and imagining was worse.

*Ask your question,* the ledger wrote now, unprompted, the pen lying untouched beside it, the ink rising out of the page the way water rises through cloth.

"You're the house," Beatrix said. Her voice sounded strange in the study, flattened by the dark, by the portraits, by the weight of two hundred years of Eriksen faces watching from the walls. "The house itself. Or you speak for it."

*The house is a throat,* the ledger wrote. *I am what passes through it. Ask your question.*

Beatrix pulled the chair closer. The legs shrieked on the floorboards and somewhere above her, in the empty servants' corridor, something shifted its weight — patiently, the way an audience settles.

"Is my sister alive?"

*She is held. She is sung. She is more alive than you are, in the manner that matters to us. Her heart beats in time with the verse. It has not missed a measure in nine days.*

The words should have been a relief. They were not. They were the relief of a man told the hostage is being fed.

"Why her? Why not me? I'm the twin. I'm the one who came back."

*Because she answered,* the ledger wrote, and the ink bloomed a little, as though the page itself found this amusing. *On the third night, when the house called her name in her mother's voice, she answered. You did not. You were taught better. Your aunt saw to that, in her way, before her way failed her.*

Beatrix thought of Maren — of what was left of Maren, which was nothing, which was a young woman's face on an old woman's body, empty as a rung bell, walking down the stairwell two nights ago with her hymn trailing behind her like a bridal veil. She had not been able to stop her. She had not even tried hard enough, and she knew, in the honest hour of her own heart, that some part of her had been afraid to touch her aunt, as though refusal were contagious and she could not afford to catch it.

"The song wants a final verse," Beatrix said. "That's what the dreams are. That's what you're teaching me."

*Yes.*

"And if I never sing it? If I burn this house down and salt the ground?"

The ink paused. She had the distinct sensation, absurd and total, of being measured — of the house turning her over like a jeweler turning a stone to the light, checking for flaws, checking for fire.

*Then your sister remains as she is,* the ledger wrote at last. *Held. Sung. Anchored to an unfinished song, for as long as the song remains unfinished. The choir does not age. It does not hunger. It does not end. It waits, and she waits with it, and her voice grows more beautiful every year, and every year she will be exactly as she was the night she answered — which was frightened, and calling for you, and reaching for a door that closed.*

Beatrix's hands had gone cold on the desk. "You're telling me she's a hostage."

*We are telling you she is a guest,* the ledger wrote, and beneath it, smaller, in a hand she recognized with a lurch as her mother's — her mother, who had died eleven years ago and whose handwriting Beatrix knew better than her own face — *guests who wish to leave may say so. None have wished to. Ask her yourself.*

"I can't ask her anything. She's below a locked door."

*The door opens,* the ledger wrote. *It has always opened. It is sealed from the inside because that is how the choir keeps the world out, not how the world keeps the choir in. Your sister could walk up those stairs tomorrow. She will not. You would not, either, if you had heard what she has heard. But you may ask. You may go down, and open it, and speak her name, and ask her if she wishes to leave. We will not stop you.*

*We will not need to.*

* * *

She went down at dawn, or what the house offered as dawn — a grey seeping through the high windows of the stairwell, the excavation pit's broken earth still dark around her, the tarps pulled back now because there was no longer any pretense of hiding. The claw marks on the door's inner face were worse up close: not the scratches of an animal but of fingers, of many fingers, over years, over generations, gouged into the stone by people who had wanted out and then, apparently, stopped wanting it.

She put her hand flat against the door. The wood was warm.

"Vera," she said. Her sister's name came out cracked. "Vera. It's Bee. I'm going to open this. I'm going to ask you something, and I need you to answer me honestly. Can you hear me?"

The singing on the other side did not stop. It never stopped; she understood that now. But it thinned, parted, the way water parts around a stone, and through the gap in it came her sister's voice — clear, close, so close it might have been her lips at Beatrix's ear through the keyhole, except there was no keyhole, and the voice was coming from everywhere, from the stone itself.

"Bee." A pause, and then a small, wondering laugh that broke Beatrix's heart in half, because it was the laugh from their childhood, from the attic, from the summer the lightning struck the oak. "Bee, it's so *beautiful* down here. Why did you never tell me? Why did we never come?"

"Vera. Listen to me. Do you want to come home?"

The silence on the other side of the door lasted four seconds. She counted them. They were the longest four seconds of her life, and she understood, in the third one, exactly what the ledger had meant by *we will not need to.*

"I *am* home," Vera said, gently, the way one speaks to a child who has not yet learned the shape of the world. "Oh, Bee. You'll understand when you sing it. You'll understand everything."

The singing closed over her voice like water over a dropped coin.

Beatrix stood with her forehead against the warm wood for a long time. She did not cry. She had the sense that the house was listening for exactly that, and she denied it, and the denial felt like the first thing she had won in nine days.

* * *

The ledger was waiting for her on the desk when she came back up, open to a fresh page, the morning light lying across it like a hand.

*You went down,* it wrote. *You asked. You have your answer.*

"She's not a hostage. She's a believer. You made her a believer."

*We made her nothing. We offered, and she accepted, and the offering was sweet, and that is the whole of our art.* The ink flowed on, unhurried, patient as erosion. *You believe we are a trap. A trap closes on the unwilling. We are a door, and doors only work from both sides. Your sister walked through hers with her eyes open. Your aunt walked through hers after sixty years of standing in the frame. Even your mother —*

The writing stopped. A long, deliberate blankness, like a breath held.

*Even your mother,* the ledger began again, in a different hand, older, the letters pressed so hard into the paper they had torn through, *asked us, at the end, whether there was room for one more.*

Beatrix sat down slowly. "She's in there. My mother is in the choir."

*Your mother is in the choir. Your grandmother. Your great-uncle Halvard, who drowned and did not drown. Eleven Eriksens. Forty-one servants. The count is kept. The count is always kept.* The page turned itself. *And the song wants its final verse, Beatrix Eriksen, and it has chosen you, and we will tell you the terms plainly, because the house has never once lied to its own.*

She read.

*The verse requires a twin's voice. Not a sister's — a twin's. Two lives cast from one mold, one given to the choir, one remaining above: this is the old arithmetic, and it does not bend. Vera is the anchor. You are the voice. The verse must be given freely — sung with a whole heart, wanting it, meaning it — or it will not bind, and the house will fall silent, and everything the song has built for two hundred years will rot with the rest of the world. We do not take the verse. We cannot. It must be a gift.*

*In exchange: your sister walks out of the ground whole. Your mother's face is the last thing you will never have to mourn. The house prospers, and you prosper, and the fields come in gold, and the money never runs out, and no one you love will ever be taken from you again by anything so ordinary as death. This is what the song has bought this family since before the maps had names for this valley. This is what your father spent his whole life pretending he hadn't inherited.*

*You have until the verse completes itself in your dreams. It is at six verses now. There are seven.*

Beatrix looked up at the portraits. Great-Grandmother Astrid, in her high collar, with her mouth like a closed purse. Her father, painted the year before the drinking took him, wearing the family's smile like a coat that didn't fit. All of them prosperous. All of them comfortable. All of them, she understood now, *paid.*

"That's the corruption of it," she said quietly, to the room, to the book, to whatever was reading her thoughts with such terrible patience. "You don't force anyone. You just make refusing cost more than anyone can bear. That's how you've lasted two hundred years."

The ledger wrote nothing for a long moment. Then:

*We prefer the word* belonging, *it wrote. *But yes.*

Beatrix closed the book. She pressed her palm flat against the cover and felt, faintly, through the leather and the board and the two hundred years of paper beneath, a pulse — her sister's pulse, keeping time, keeping measure, six verses long and waiting for the seventh.

That night, the dream began at the first verse, as it always did. But this time, at the end of the sixth, the choir did not fall silent and release her.

They held the last note open. And in the space where the seventh verse should have been, her sister's voice — alone now, tender, unbearably familiar — began to teach her the melody.

And Beatrix, dreaming, opened her mouth — and sang it wrong.
