## 7. Rooms That Recount

Ansel didn't sleep so much as hold still for six hours, the way a man holds still in a room with a wasp.

The cot they'd brought—the county issued them to surveyors on multi-day rural jobs—stood in what Ansel had designated *Bedroom 2, Northeast*, based on the floorplan sketched that first afternoon. The sketch was in the truck. The laser measure was in the truck. Everything measurable was in the truck, and the truck was in the dooryard, and between the cot and the dooryard stood forty feet of hallway that had been thirty-five feet when Ansel walked it at dusk.

They'd measured it twice. Thirty-four point seven feet, door casing to stairwell newel. A number with no poetry in it. Ansel had written it in the margin of the site map and underlined it, because underlining made it official, and official things could be trusted.

At three in the morning, needing water from the cooler in the cab, Ansel walked the hallway without counting steps, which was the mistake. Halfway down, some animal part of the brain flagged the discrepancy before the conscious mind caught up: the walk was taking too long. The baseboards were passing by too many. Ansel stopped, turned on the phone's flashlight, and looked back toward the bedroom door.

It was still visible. It was just *farther*.

Not far. Not absurdly far. Maybe four extra feet of hallway, maybe five, the kind of difference you'd blame on your own tired legs if you were a person who blamed things on tired legs. Ansel Thornbury did not. Ansel Thornbury walked back to the bedroom door, laid a palm flat against the jamb, and stood there breathing until the wasp-brain quieted.

Then they went out to the truck, got the laser measure, and shot the hallway again.

Forty-one point two feet.

Ansel stood in the dark hallway holding a device that cost eight hundred dollars and had never once lied, and felt the specific vertigo of a man whose religion has developed a stutter. Six and a half feet of new hallway. Built while they slept. Built out of nothing, or built out of something being moved, or—

Or the house was counting wrong on purpose.

"Okay," Ansel said aloud, to nobody, in the professional tone used for difficult clients. "Okay."

Nobody answered. That was somehow worse than Wren answering.

* * *

Dawn came up gray through the east windows, and Ansel did what Ansel always did when the world misbehaved: documented it.

The ritual helped. Tripod out of the case. Camera mounted, bubble leveled. Wide shots of every elevation, then interiors, room by room, shutter clicks steady as a pulse. Photographs were testimony. Photographs were admissible. Whatever was happening in this house, it would happen on the record.

Ansel started outside—the barn, the collapsed well house, the foundation line where the original 1911 footprint met the 1948 addition—and worked inward. Kitchen. Pantry. Parlor. The photographs came off the card clean and ordinary, and Ansel reviewed each one on the camera's little screen with the satisfaction of a doctor reading a healthy chart.

Then, in the parlor shot, there was Wren.

Standing in the corner behind the defunct radiator, mud-splattered, hands at her sides, looking directly into the lens with an expression of perfect patience. Like she'd been waiting all morning for the shutter. Like she'd known exactly when it would fire.

Ansel checked the frame before it. Kitchen. Empty. Checked the frame after. Hallway. Empty. Went back to the parlor frame. There she was, small and solid and *there*, rendered in twelve megapixels of forensic evidence, and Ansel felt the floor tilt under a different kind of measurement entirely.

Because here was the thing Ansel's mind seized on, the thing it worried like a loose tooth: the photograph didn't show a smudge. It didn't show an artifact, a flare, a double exposure. It showed a child in focus, lit correctly, casting—Ansel zoomed until the pixels broke apart—a faint shadow along the floorboards.

Photographs don't lie. That was the whole point of them. That was why Ansel took them instead of believing things.

Ansel deleted the file.

Then sat on the parlor floor holding the camera, understanding that deleting evidence was not something a surveyor did, and hating the small cowardly relief of it anyway.

* * *

By nine, Ansel had re-surveyed the entire interior, and the numbers had gone from wrong to *narrative*.

The hallway: forty-one point two feet, confirmed in daylight.

The pantry, which yesterday had run six feet deep against the kitchen's north wall, now ran nine. The extra depth wasn't empty—it held shelving that hadn't existed, old shelving, sagging under jars of something dark that Ansel declined to examine closely. The shelf wood matched the house. Not similar to. Matched, grain for grain, as if the boards had always been there and only the wall around them had gotten out of the way.

And on the west side of the parlor, where yesterday's floorplan showed solid exterior wall with a window over it, there was now a door.

A plain door, painted the same exhausted white as everything else, with a glass knob gone cloudy with age. It opened onto a short corridor Ansel had no record of, which turned once and ended at another door, this one older, iron-hinged, cold to the touch. Through its seams came a smell Ansel knew from childhood visits to farms—the deep mineral smell of well water, of stone that stays wet year-round.

Ansel put the laser measure against the new corridor and let it speak.

*11.6 m.*

Eleven point six meters. Thirty-eight feet. Of corridor inside a house whose exterior walls—Ansel checked, walking the perimeter in the yard, checking the window count, counting siding courses—had not changed by a single inch. The outside of the house was the same size it had always been. The inside was getting bigger, the way a lung gets bigger. The way something gets bigger when it's *growing toward* something.

Ansel sat down on the porch steps with the site map spread across both knees and drew the changes in red pencil, because red was for revisions, and revisions implied that a correct version existed somewhere underneath.

New hallway length. New pantry depth. New corridor bearing west-by-northwest.

When the lines were drawn, Ansel stared at them and felt the dread arrive not as a feeling but as a fact, which was the only way dread ever got past the door.

Every addition pointed the same direction. Every new foot of hallway, every reclaimed inch of wall, every impossible corridor—they all trended northwest, toward the collapsed well house at the property's back corner. The house wasn't rearranging itself. It wasn't haunted in any way the word covered. It was *extending*. Room by room, board by board, reaching across its own cellar like a root system toward the one thing on the property it needed and didn't have.

"It's growing toward the well," Ansel said.

"You're getting faster at that."

Wren stood at the bottom of the porch steps, in the same clothes, with the same mud dried on her shins, and her voice had the mild tone of a teacher watching a student finally show their work.

"How long has it been doing this?" Ansel asked.

Wren considered. "Since it got hungry enough to try. Rooms are how it reaches. Doors are how it asks." She nodded at the map. "You should mark the doors especially. It doesn't make a door unless it wants something to come through it."

"The iron one."

"That one most." Wren climbed the steps and sat on the swing, which creaked under her weight, which was itself a wrongness Ansel catalogued and refused—an imaginary friend should weigh nothing, and yet the chains complained like they'd borne her for years. "You slept in Bedroom Two."

"I did."

"It moved you closer in the night. Four feet. You didn't wake up." She said it without accusation, almost gently. "It counts what comes in. You're the biggest thing that's come in fifty years."

"I'm a person, not a delivery."

"To you." Wren swung her heels against the boards, once, twice. "To the register, you're a name with a balance outstanding."

Ansel looked at the red lines on the map, at the little arrow of corridors all pointing northwest, and made the decision the way they made all decisions: by writing it down. At the top of the site map, above the north arrow, Ansel printed in block capitals:

*STRUCTURE EXHIBITS UNDOCUMENTED EXPANSION. ALL ADDITIONS BEAR NW TOWARD WELL STRUCTURE. RECOMMEND IMMEDIATE RE-SURVEY AT 24-HR INTERVALS.*

There. On paper. In ink. If the house wanted to grow, it would grow into the record, and the record would be waiting.

Wren read it upside down. Something crossed her face—not amusement, exactly. Older than amusement. Pity, maybe, wearing amusement's hand-me-down coat.

"You think the paper protects you," she said.

"I think paper is real."

"So am I," Wren said, "according to your camera. Where'd that picture go?"

Ansel's jaw tightened. "Artifacts happen."

"Mm." She rocked once in the swing. "Take more tonight. Take one every hour. I want to see what you do when they stop looking like artifacts."

"Why would you want—"

"Because I need you to believe me before Thursday," Wren said, "and I'm running out of ways to say it kindly."

She didn't say what Thursday was. When Ansel asked, she only looked northwest, toward the well house, with an expression Ansel couldn't file under anything, and swung her heels, and waited for evening the way the house seemed to wait for everything: patiently, and with an appetite.

* * *

Night fell early behind the treeline, and Ansel kept the bargain with the camera because a bargain kept was a method maintained.

Hourly frames. Living room, hallway, kitchen, the new corridor with its iron door. Ansel set a timer, worked the tripod, logged each shot in the field book with time stamps, and told herself the exercise was documentation and not prayer.

Frame one, 8:04 p.m.: hallway, empty.

Frame two, 9:00 p.m.: hallway, empty.

Frame three, 10:02 p.m.: Wren, ten feet down the hall, facing the camera.

Frame four, 11:00 p.m.: Wren, six feet.

Frame five, midnight: Wren, close enough that her mud-flecked hand filled the lower left of the frame, fingers slightly curled, as though she'd been about to knock on the lens.

Ansel reviewed them in order and watched the approach happen with the flat, rising horror of a man reading his own autopsy scheduled in advance. No blur. No drift. Each image sharp as a mugshot. She was getting closer every hour, and she was doing it *between* the exposures, politely, punctually, like something that respected the interval because the interval was a form of counting, and counting was the house's whole language.

Frame six, 1:00 a.m., was taken with shaking hands.

Wren stood immediately before the camera. Close enough that Ansel could see, in the screen's cold light, that her eyes weren't right—not wrong in a monstrous way, but wrong in a *young* way, wide and wet and ancient all at once, the eyes of a child who has been waiting so long that waiting has become the shape of her face. Behind her, at the far end of the hallway, the iron door stood open.

It had never been open.

Through it, black and vertical, a shaft went down.

Wren, in the photograph, was smiling. And the smile was not the smile of a threat. It was the smile of someone standing aside. Of someone saying, with her whole patient face: *it's open now; it's been asking for you; please come see.*

Ansel lowered the camera.

The hallway was empty. The iron door, when Ansel walked to it on legs that felt borrowed, was shut, cold, seamless, exactly as it had been all day.

But on the floor beneath it, in the dust that no broom had touched in decades, there were small footprints. Bare feet. Child-sized. Leading away from the door, down the impossible corridor, toward the room where a surveyor had slept.

And beside the cot, on the field book where Ansel had left it open, in handwriting that was not Ansel's but was horribly, precisely neat—the careful print of a child taught penmanship by someone long dead—one line had been added beneath the day's notes:

*You're eleven rooms closer than you were yesterday.*
