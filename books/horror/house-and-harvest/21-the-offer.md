## 21. The Offer

I have been counting the days wrong, I think. Or maybe I've been counting them right and they just don't add up to anything anymore. Nine days until the equinox, and the house has started whispering numbers in its sleep—numbers it took from people who are still walking around somewhere with holes in them where the numbers used to be—and I sit on the porch swing at three in the morning because I can't be inside the house and I can't leave it either, and Ansel is in there, in the parlor, sleeping badly on a mattress that wasn't there yesterday, and I am trying to do arithmetic that a child shouldn't have to do.

Except I'm not a child. That's the whole problem. I look like one. Mud-splattered knees, cowlick, the gap where my left canine never came in. But I'm ninety-three years old, or I was seven once and then I stopped, and the years since then haven't been years so much as a long held breath. And a held breath can turn into something else if you hold it long enough. It can turn into hunger wearing your face.

The house taught me the offer tonight.

That's how I have to say it, even though saying it makes my chest hurt worse than anything the house has ever done to me. The house taught me. I didn't think of it myself. I want to be honest about that, at least once, before whatever happens happens, because honesty is about to become the rarest thing on this property and someone ought to plant some.

It came to me while I was sitting here, the way the house gives me things—no words, just knowing, the way you know a room is empty before you open the door. *There is a way,* the house said, in the voice of every wall at once, which sounds like wind through a keyhole if you're lucky and like your mother calling you home if you're not. *A name can be carried by another. A debt can be worn like a coat.*

And I understood it immediately, the way you understand a game's rules the first time someone shows you, because I have been a name for fifty years. Wren. Not Harrow-Wren, not the girl who fell, just Wren, the shape children give me when they need someone to play with and no one real will come. Names are portable. I know this better than anyone alive or otherwise. I have been carrying someone else's daughter around inside myself since 1931, and she fits so well that sometimes I forget which of us is the coat.

So here is what I know, laid out plain, the way I'd lay out marbles before a trade:

Ansel Thornbury is the last name on the register. The house has been very clear about this. Every night the walls recite it a little louder: *Thornbury, last of the line, due at equinox.* The house doesn't hate Ansel. Houses can't hate. The house is just hungry the way winter is hungry—it doesn't want anything, it just takes everything that hasn't been put away. And Edith Thornbury put away something that belonged to the house, spent it, ate it down to the wrapper, and the house has been starving ever since, and starving houses collect.

But a name, the house says. A name could go instead.

If I take Ansel's name onto myself—if I stand at the well mouth on the equinox and say *I am Ansel Thornbury* and mean it, mean it down to the marrow the house counts—the register would close over me instead. The house would take what it's owed from whoever is holding the name. And I am, when you get right down to it, mostly made of borrowed things already. My shape is borrowed from children's wanting. My voice is borrowed from the house. My patience is borrowed from a dead girl's grief. What's one more loan? What's one more coat?

I could do it. I know I could do it. That's the terrible part—not that I'm afraid I couldn't, but that I'm certain I could, and part of me wants to, and I can't tell anymore which part is me.

* * *

Ansel comes out at dawn.

I hear the screen door before I see them—Ansel moves carefully now, has learned the house's moods the way you learn a dog's, testing each floorboard like it might be lying. Which it might. Everything here lies a little. Even me. Especially me, lately.

They sit down on the other end of the swing without asking, and the chains creak, and for a moment we just watch the light come up over the Hollis corn, which is the only honest green thing for two miles in any direction.

"You were talking in your sleep," Ansel says. "Or the house was. Hard to tell."

"It was both of us. We share a lot these days." I kick my feet. They don't reach the porch boards. They've never reached the porch boards. "Ansel. I have to tell you something, and you have to let me finish, because if you interrupt me I'll lose my nerve, and I've only got the one nerve left. The house has been eating the others."

Ansel looks at me. Thirty-six years old, eyes like someone doing sums under stress, jaw set the way their grandmother's jaw must have been set in 1974 when she walked out of this house alone. I love Ansel. I want to say that first, before anything else, so it's on the record. I loved them at six years old when they invented me out of loneliness and a bad summer, and I loved them at twelve when they decided I wasn't real and stopped inviting me, and I loved them through thirty years of being unthought, which is a kind of cold nothing I wouldn't wish on the Hollises' worst enemy. Love isn't the complication here. Love is the only clean thing I'm bringing to this conversation.

"I found more of the ledger," Ansel says. "Last night. Pages behind the attic insulation. Edith's handwriting gets worse toward the end. Like she was—" They stop. Recalculate. "Like she was running out of ink and time at the same rate."

"She was running out of herself. That's what spending does." I grip the swing chain. It's cold. Everything metal here is cold, even in August, even at noon. "Ansel. There's a way for you to live."

Their head turns slowly. I watch hope arrive on their face like weather—unwelcome, unasked-for, impossible to ignore. I hate that I'm the one bringing it. I hate more that I'm going to keep talking anyway.

"The register wants a Thornbury. It says so every night; you've heard it. But a name isn't blood. A name is—" I search for the words, and they come too easily, which frightens me, because easy words are usually the house's words. "A name is a coat. And coats can change hands."

"No," Ansel says.

"You don't even know what I'm—"

"Yes I do." Their voice has gone flat and careful, the surveyor's voice, the voice they use when the numbers are coming out wrong and they need to believe the instrument and not the room. "You're offering to take my name. To stand in for me. To let it collect you instead." They laugh, one dry note with no humor in it. "Wren, you're an imaginary friend. You're the least substantial thing on this property, and I include the dust."

"That's exactly why it works." I lean forward. The swing groans. "Nothing here is what it looks like, Ansel. You've seen that. The rooms recount themselves. The road loops. The house appraises everything that comes through its door. So why should I be the exception? Why should the mud-splattered little girl on the porch be the one true thing?" I make myself smile, and it costs me. "I'm mostly absence wearing a shape. Absence can hold a name just fine. Maybe better than flesh. Flesh argues."

"Absolutely not."

"You didn't let me finish."

"There's no version of finishing that ends somewhere I agree to."

I expected this. I rehearsed it all night, all nine nights, all fifty years. But rehearsal doesn't help, because Ansel's refusal isn't really refusal—it's fear wearing stubbornness, and I know that costume intimately, I invented half my wardrobe in it.

"Then listen to the rest," I say, "because the rest is the part you need."

They fold their arms. Waiting. Suspicious. Good. Suspicion is the correct response to everything I'm about to say.

"The house taught me this," I say. "Not me. I want that written down somewhere, in your ledger, in your instruments, wherever you keep the true things now. The house put the offer in my head the way it puts numbers in the drifter's mouth. It showed me how it would work. It showed me the wording. It was patient about it." My hands are shaking, and I look at them shaking, small hands, child's hands, and I make myself keep going. "Because the house doesn't care which of us it takes, Ansel. It cares that it takes. A debt collected is a debt collected. And it knows—I know—that if I offer this, you'll be tempted. Not because you want to live. Because you'll want to save me. Because that's what you do. You counted the surveyor's tally marks with him for six hours so he wouldn't unravel. You photographed every room so the house couldn't deny you'd been there. You are constitutionally incapable of letting someone pay your bill."

"So the offer is bait."

"The offer is real *and* bait. Both. That's the worst part." I swallow. My throat clicks, dry as the well. "Here's what I can't promise you. Here's the confession, the whole ugly thing, so you can weigh it honestly. If I take your name—if I stand at the well and say the words and let the register close over me—I don't know what comes out the other side. If anything does. The house taught me the trick, and the house is not a teacher, Ansel, it's a fisherman. Teachers want you to learn. Fishermen want you to bite. And I cannot promise that what stands up after the house collects me will still be—"

My voice goes. Just for a second. Long enough that I have to start the sentence again, smaller.

"—will still be me. Will still be Wren. It might be. It might be the house wearing my shape with your name stitched inside it, standing up out of that water smiling at you, and you'd never know, because I don't even know where I end and the house begins anymore. Fifty years, Ansel. It lent me strength to wait and the loan is being called in and I am fraying at every edge. Look at me. Really look."

They look. I let them. I let them see what I've been hiding under the cowlick and the gap-toothed grin: the places where my edges have gone soft and gray, like paper left in rain. The place on my left arm where you can almost see through me to the swing slats behind. The way my shadow has started arriving late.

"Oh, Wren," Ansel says, and it's the soft voice, the six-year-old voice, the one they used when I told them I was scared of the dark under the bed, and I nearly break apart right there on the swing, because that voice was mine first. They gave it to me. And now I'm asking to give everything back.

"Don't," I say. "Don't use that voice. Use the surveyor voice. Use the voice that says the numbers are wrong. Because I need you to refuse me for the right reasons, and the right reasons are these: one, accepting would be theft. One more Thornbury taking something from this house—my self, my whatever-I-am—to buy their way out. Your family has spent fifty years paying debts with other people's property. Don't finish the pattern with me. Two, I can't promise I survive it, and you know it, and taking the deal anyway would just be you choosing a comfortable lie over an honest horror, and you didn't drive four hours with a laser measure to do that. And three—"

"Three?"

"Three." I take a breath I don't technically need, for the dramatic effect I absolutely do need. "Three is that I might be lying. Right now. The house taught me the offer, and the house might have taught me the whole speech too, the trembling and the arm and the shadow arriving late. Maybe none of it is fraying. Maybe it's theater, and the exit is through you, and I'm the trapdoor." I meet their eyes. "I can't prove I'm not. That's the gift the house gave me tonight. Doubt. Free of charge."

The silence stretches. Down the road, a crow lands on the fence post and does not caw, just watches us with the professional interest of something that has seen this conversation before, in other years, on other porches, with other names.

Ansel's jaw works. Their hands—long, precise, ink-stained hands—are very still on their knees, and I know that stillness. It's the stillness of someone holding an instrument level, waiting for the bubble to settle, refusing to nudge it toward the answer they want.

"If I said yes," they say finally, "if I let you do it—what would you be afterward? Honestly. Best case."

"Best case?" I consider it. The honest answer is a wound, so I give it gently. "Best case, the house takes the name and the debt and lets the rest of me go. And the rest of me is a dead girl's grief and thirty years of being imagined and a shape that fits nobody anymore. Best case, I stop. The way a song stops. Not tragically. Just—over. And you walk away and live a long life and every year on the equinox you feel a little cold and don't know why, and that's me, checking on you. Best case is a good ending, Ansel. For you."

"And worst case?"

"Worst case, I'm the house's new mouth, and I come find you in thirty years wearing your childhood nickname, and you let me in, because you'll be old and lonely and it'll sound exactly like me." I smile, and this one is real, which makes it worse. "Worst case, the house finally learns to lie beautifully. With your face's favorite memories."

Ansel closes their eyes.

I watch them decide. I actually watch it happen—the way their breathing changes, the way their hands unclench, the way something settles in their shoulders like a level finding true. And what scares me, in the last moment before they speak, is that I genuinely cannot read which way they've gone. I used to be able to read Ansel like a picture book. Now they're a ledger in a language I only half remember.

"No," they say.

Just that. Quiet. Final. The surveyor's voice, reading the number off the instrument even though the number ruins the budget.

"No," they say again, and open their eyes, and they are wet but steady. "You beautiful, borrowed, impossible—no. I'm not letting this house teach you to spend yourself. That's all it's ever known. Taking. Trading. Collecting. And I'll be damned if the last thing it teaches the girl it broke is how to break herself on purpose." They stand up. The swing rocks empty beside me. "You told me yourself you can't promise what you'd be. Then I can't accept. That's not heroism, Wren. That's just arithmetic. The risk column doesn't balance."

"It doesn't have to balance," I whisper. "Love isn't a ledger."

"Everything here is a ledger," Ansel says. "That's the whole disease. Including me, apparently, because I'm about to go do something stupid and honest instead." They pause at the screen door, hand on the frame, and look back at me, and for one second I see the six-year-old superimposed on the adult, both of them looking at me with the same expression: *you were real to me first.* "Thank you for offering. I mean that. It's the most real thing anyone's done on this property in fifty years. And that's exactly why I won't take it."

The door bangs shut behind them.

I stay on the swing. The crow leaves. The sun comes up the rest of the way, indifferent, golden, counting nothing.

And underneath me, under the porch boards, under the parlor floor, under everything—the well shifts in its sleep. I feel it the way you feel a dog turn over at the foot of the bed. It heard. Of course it heard. The house hears everything said on its own skin.

And it is not pleased that its fisherman's trick failed.

The walls begin, very softly, to count.
