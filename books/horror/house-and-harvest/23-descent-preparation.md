## 23. Descent Preparation

Ruth Hollis arrived at the Harrow place an hour before sundown, driving a truck older than I was, its bed loaded with things that looked like they'd been waiting in a barn for exactly this day: a lantern of blackened iron, a coil of rope gone stiff with age, a canvas satchel that clinked when she set it down on the porch boards.

She didn't knock. She didn't need to. The house knew her truck—I'd watched it through the parlor window as she came up the drive, and every board in the floor had shifted under my feet at once, like a dog lifting its head.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've been better."

"You've been worse too. I heard about the road." She said it flatly, no question in it. News traveled in Vell County the way water travels through limestone—slowly, invisibly, and arriving everywhere at once. "You tried to leave."

"Twice."

"And?"

"The county has apparently rerouted me through my own front yard."

Ruth grunted and started unloading the truck. "That's it counting you. Once it's got your number, you don't walk out. You get paid out or you get paid in." She hefted the iron lantern onto the porch swing—Wren's swing, though Wren hadn't shown herself since the night she'd made her offer and I'd refused it, and I was trying not to read anything into that absence, and failing. "My grandmother kept this ready for fifty years. Iron holds a flame that doesn't belong to the house. Everything else down there—" she nodded at the walls, "—burns on loan."

I picked up the lantern. It was heavier than it looked, cold in a way the evening air couldn't account for, and etched around its base with a ring of small marks. Tally marks. Dozens of them, scratched deep.

"What are these?"

"Hollis hands," Ruth said. "Every generation, somebody scratches their count into the lantern before they carry it. So if the house takes them, there's proof they were counted by somebody who loved them first." She looked at me. "You'll want to add yours before we go down. Not after. After doesn't work."

I didn't ask how she knew that. There was a whole category of questions I'd stopped asking since the drifter, since the ledger, since my mother's voice coming through a dead phone line saying *the debt runs down the bloodline* in a tone I'd never heard her use—not even the day my father left.

* * *

We worked by the last of the light, because Ruth refused to open the satchel indoors until we were ready to use it.

Edith's ledger went into a oilskin sleeve Ruth had sewn herself, the stitching uneven but dense. I'd spent two days reassembling it—the torn pages recovered from the well shaft's upper reaches, the ghost impressions under the parlor floorboards pressed back into legibility with graphite and patience—and what emerged wasn't a survey at all. It was a map drawn from memory of a place Edith had only seen once, in 1974, by lantern light, while something below her held very still and let her look.

The pantry. That was the way in. The pantry floor, third board from the north wall, which had opened for me once already in a dream I'd stopped calling a dream. Below the pantry, the shaft. Below the shaft, the water. And below the water—

"There's nothing below the water," Ruth said when I said this aloud. "There's just what the water keeps. You go down, you say what has to be said, and you come up. That's the whole rite. My family's version has more singing in it, but the Hollises always did love to overcomplicate a thing."

"And if it wants more than words?"

Her jaw worked. "In '89, when they took my brother Dale, my father wanted to go down after him. Grandmother wouldn't let him. She said the well doesn't trade, Ansel. It doesn't barter and it doesn't bargain, no matter what voices come up claiming otherwise." She fixed me with a look that had probably ended arguments for sixty years. "Whatever you hear down there—and you will hear things—you speak your piece and you keep your hands in your pockets. Nothing goes down that isn't already owed, and nothing comes up that isn't already yours. Can you do that?"

I thought about Wren's hand in mine in the collapsing dark of a hallway that no longer existed. *You gave it truth. Let me give it the rest.*

"I can try," I said.

Ruth snorted. "Thornburys," she said, and it sounded like a diagnosis.

* * *

The chalk came out of the satchel last: a tin of fat white surveyor's crayons, the kind used for marking stone, the kind the previous surveyor had used to line his own descent with numbers. I'd found his marks on my one trip to the well house—*412, 411, 410* descending into the dark, his handwriting steady all the way down, steadier than his mind had been by the end.

"He gave these to my mother in '09," Ruth said. "Came to the farm half-mad, handed them over the fence, said, *Counting is the only prayer the house respects.* Then he walked back toward the Harrow place and we never saw him again. Until you did." She pushed the tin across the porch rail to me. "He's still down there, isn't he. In the well house."

"He's alive. I wouldn't call the rest of it living."

"You going to bring him up?"

"If I can."

"Don't promise him that." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the word *promise*. "Dale's been down there fifty years because somebody promised."

I put the tin in my jacket pocket, next to the folded page of the ledger that named me, and felt the weight of both like a stone sewn into the lining.

* * *

The house began fighting at dusk.

It didn't announce itself. It never did. It started small—a door I'd propped open with a chair found shut when I came back for the rope, the chair standing neatly on the far side of it as if the house had tidied. Then the stairs.

I'd measured the main staircase my first day, back when I still trusted my instruments: fourteen risers, total rise eight foot six. Tonight, carrying the ledger up to the attic for the oilskin sleeve, I counted twenty-two steps and arrived at a landing I'd never seen, facing a wall where the attic door should have been.

I stood there breathing while the wall breathed back.

Below me, Ruth's voice, calm as a woman calling hogs: "Ansel. It's doing the stairs. Come down the way you came up and don't touch the new ones."

"How do you know it's the stairs?"

"Because it's always the stairs. It grew them in '74 too, the night Edith came out. A house that's starving gets long in the throat." A pause. "Come *down*, boy."

I came down. Fourteen steps, each one real under my boot, each one creaking in a register I now recognized as speech. By the time I reached the bottom, the extra eight had closed behind me like a mouth finishing a sentence.

* * *

By full dark, the doors had begun to seal.

Not lock—seal. I watched it happen to the kitchen door: the gap beneath it narrowing by fractions, the wood swelling without moisture, until the door sat in its frame the way a tooth sits in a gum. Ruth went through the ground floor with a hammer and a pocketful of iron nails from the satchel, driving one into the jamb of every door we needed open, and where the nails went in, the swelling stopped—but only there. The house simply moved its attention elsewhere, patient as arithmetic.

"It's not trying to trap us," I said, watching a window dim as though a membrane had grown across it. "It could have done this days ago. It's trying to make us *late*."

"That's worse," Ruth said. "Trapping's anger. Stalling's strategy." She hammered another nail home. "Equinox is tomorrow at 6:49 in the morning. We go down tonight so we're standing at the water when it opens, not knocking on a sealed floor when it does."

"And if we're late?"

"Then it refills with whatever it can catch, and the county loses a property surveyor, and I lose the only Thornbury I ever liked." She said it without looking at me, which somehow made it worse. Or better. With Ruth Hollis it was hard to tell.

* * *

At eleven o'clock, the walls began reciting.

I was in the parlor, prying the last nails from the floorboard above the hiding place—we'd need the space clear, Ruth said, because the pantry and the parlor shared a wall and sound traveled between them like gossip—when the plaster started.

It came low, under the threshold of hearing, the way a refrigerator hums in an empty house. Then it climbed. Names. That was the first thing I understood: they were names, dozens of them, hundreds, spoken in overlapping voices—some old, some young, some so worn the syllables had eroded into pure sound. Hollis names. Harrow names. Names I didn't know and names I did, and threaded among them, unmistakable, my grandmother's voice saying *Edith Marion Thornbury* in the flat cadence of someone reading a list.

Then, after hers, in a child's voice I knew better than my own:

"*Ansel James Thornbury.*"

I dropped the pry bar.

Wren stood in the parlor doorway. She'd come back—or she'd never left; with her the distinction had stopped mattering weeks ago. Mud-splattered, unchanged, thirty years old and six years old and older than the county itself. But something in her face had frayed since the night of her offer. The edges of her weren't holding their edges. When she moved, she moved slightly *after* herself, like a figure on a lagging film.

"You shouldn't listen to that part," she said.

"Which part?"

"The part with your name in it." She came into the room, and the walls' recitation bent around her, the voices dipping reverent, the way a congregation dips when the censer passes. "The house says the names it's keeping. Yours isn't kept yet. That's the whole fight, Ansel. Tomorrow morning, either your name comes off the list or you come onto it."

"Then help me take it off."

"I'm trying." Something crossed her face—grief, or guilt, or the place where those two had grown together like roots of the same tree. "The house knows I'm helping you. It's been docking me for it." She held up her hands. In the lamplight I could see through them now, faintly, the way you can see through a finger pressed to a flashlight. "Every hour I spend on your side, it takes back a little of what it lent me. By sunrise I'll be mostly suggestion." She smiled, and the smile was the same smile from the porch swing, thirty years intact, and that was the worst thing in the room. "So I'm spending everything tonight."

"Wren—"

"Don't." Gentle. Final. "You refused my offer. It was a good refusal. It was the first right thing a Thornbury ever did in this house, and I will not let you ruin it by being sorry." She turned to the wall where the reciting voices lived and laid her palm flat against the plaster. "Now get Ruth. Get the lantern. Get the book. The house is going to scream all night, and we're going to work through it, and tomorrow at 6:49 you're going to stand at the water and say the true thing. That's the plan. That's the whole plan."

Through her hand, the wall shuddered. The voices rose—not louder, but *closer*, as if the plaster had thinned to skin.

And then, beneath the hundred borrowed voices, beneath Edith's flat recitation and the eroded names of the collected dead, the house found a new register. Deeper. Older. The foundation itself, speaking up through the soles of my boots:

"*One Thornbury owed. One Thornbury due. One Thornbury walking down the throat.*"

Ruth appeared in the doorway with the iron lantern lit, its flame burning a color fire has no business burning—white at the heart, steady as a star. She looked at the wall. She looked at Wren's fading hand against it. She looked at me.

"Well," she said. "It knows we're coming. Good. I hate sneaking."

I took the ledger off the table. I added my tally mark to the lantern's base—shallow, crooked, mine—and Ruth watched me do it and nodded once, the nod you give a thing that's finished.

The house screamed all night.

We worked through it.
