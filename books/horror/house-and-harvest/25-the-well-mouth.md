## 25. The Well Mouth

The pantry was the last room the house let us have.

Ruth Hollis stood in its doorway with her lantern held high, iron-framed, the glass smoked yellow with fifty years of use, and she said what she'd been saying for an hour: "It opens when it's ready. You can't pry it. Edith tried prying it."

"I know," I said. "I read her ledger."

"You read half of it." Ruth set the lantern on a shelf between jars of nothing—dust and glass and the ghosts of preserves some grandmother had put up before either of us was born. "The other half is down there."

The pantry was small, which felt like a joke. A house that had grown wings and swallowed hallways whole, that had added rooms overnight like a body adding bone, had kept this one room honest: six feet by eight, shelves on two walls, a window too small to climb through, and a floor of wide pine boards gone silver with age. Ordinary. Almost aggressively ordinary. The house had left it plain the way a liar leaves one sentence true.

Wren sat on the floor with her back against the shelving, knees drawn up, mud-splattered shins exactly as they'd been when I was six years old and drew her that way in crayon. She hadn't spoken since we came downstairs. She watched me the way you watch someone carry something breakable across ice.

"Tell me again how you know it's here," I said to Ruth.

"Because my brother helped dig the footing for the well house in '62, before your grandmother ever—" Ruth stopped herself. Recalibrated. "Because the Harrow well was dug under the house. Not beside it. Under. The old-timers said the Harrows wanted their water where they could keep an eye on it." Her mouth did something bitter. "They should have wanted it farther away."

I knelt on the boards. They were cold through my jeans, colder than floorboards had any right to be in September, and when I pressed my palm flat against them I could feel—not sound, not vibration, but *attention*—rising up through the wood like heat off summer asphalt. The house wasn't hiding the well anymore. It was presenting it.

"It knows today's the day," Wren said quietly. First words in an hour. "It's been counting down since the equinox eve. It counts better than I do."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not supposed to be."

Ruth handed me the surveyor's chalk—the dead man's chalk, the one we'd taken from the well house, worn to a stub and soft as butter from his grip. "Marks on the way down," she said. "Every ten feet. You mark so the shaft remembers you passed. And so you remember which direction is up, because down there, up is a rumor."

"And if I don't come back up?"

"Then I burn the house myself and answer to God about it later." She said it flat, no drama, a woman quoting a family policy older than the county. Then, softer, the first crack in her all morning: "You come back up, Ansel. Your grandmother didn't. Not really. Whatever walked out of this house in '74 wore her coat for forty years and never once called me by my name again."

I didn't have anything to say to that, so I nodded, and Ruth took it, and stepped back out of the pantry doorway to give the room—and me—room to do whatever rooms and people do at moments like this.

The boards were nailed down. I knew that because I'd pried up the parlor floor three days ago and found Edith's note instead of the bundle, and I'd come into this pantry after and knocked on every board like a doctor checking lungs, and they had all answered with the dull knock of wood over joist. All except one. There had always been one board near the north wall that answered differently—a hollowness, a depth—and I had told myself it was a root cellar access, a crawl space, a gap over bedrock. I had told myself a great many things in this house. That was practically my profession now.

I got the flat bar out of my kit. My kit. Three weeks ago it had been a laser measure, a tape, a clipboard, a phone full of apps that turned buildings into numbers. Now it was a crowbar, a coil of rope, Edith's ledger wrapped in plastic against the damp, a stub of chalk, and a flashlight whose batteries I'd triple-checked because I did not trust this house to leave the dark alone.

The board came up easier than it should have. Nails sliding out of old wood make a particular scream; these made none, just slid free like the house unclenching one tooth. Beneath it: darkness, and a smell that hit me like a hand—wet earth, mineral cold, and underneath it something sweeter and worse, the smell of water that has been sitting still for a very long time in a place no light has reached.

Not a root cellar. Not a crawl space.

A shaft. Round, lined with fieldstone for the first few feet and then raw packed earth below that, dropping straight down into black. The mouth of it was maybe four feet across. I lay on my stomach at the edge—it seemed important not to stand—and shone the flashlight down.

The beam went maybe fifteen feet before the dark ate it. In those fifteen feet I could see the stones, green-slicked, and scratched into the mortar between them, in handwriting I recognized from a clipboard in an attic:

**390 — 380 — 370 — 360**

Counting down. He'd marked his own descent the way he'd marked everything else, keeping himself counted so he'd stay real, and the marks were still here, thirty years fresh, waiting for him to come back and finish the sequence.

"He went down," I said. My voice sounded wrong in the pantry, small and flattened, like the room was pressing on it. "He went all the way down."

"He went down," Wren agreed. "And then he stopped being able to be down, and started only being able to be in the well house, counting. The house doesn't like people leaving once they've seen the bottom. It thinks they've seen inventory." She unfolded herself and stood, and looked down the shaft with an expression I couldn't read—homesickness, maybe, or hunger, or both wearing each other's clothes. "You're going to see the bottom, Ansel."

"Yeah."

"And then it will try to keep you."

"I know."

"Do you?" She looked at me then, and her face was six years old and also not, and behind her eyes was something that had waited fifty years in the dark and another thirty on a porch swing, and I understood that whatever tenderness was in her was real and whatever else was in her was also real, and neither canceled the other out. "Because I need you to actually know. Not bravado-know. The house can tell the difference. It appraises everything, including courage."

"Then it'll find mine wanting," I said. "And I'm going down anyway."

Something moved across her face that might have been pride, or grief, or the place where those two things share a border.

* * *

Ruth rigged the rope around the pantry doorframe and tested it with her whole weight, twice, swearing at it in Dutch the whole time, words I suspected her own mother would have boxed her ears for. The rope was good hemp, old but sound, and I chose not to think about how many hands had used it for exactly this purpose before mine.

"The ledger says the bottom is ninety feet," Ruth said. "Edith measured it in '74. Before." She wouldn't say *before what*. Nobody in Vell County said *before what*; they said *before*, the way you say *the war*. "Ninety feet down, and the water stands at sixty. You'll hear it before you see it. When you hear it, stop and mark your chalk and say the names."

"Whose names?"

"All of them. Every name you know that the house took. Names are ballast going down and buoyancy coming up. Don't ask me why. Ask the house, if it'll talk to you, though I'd sooner you didn't encourage it." She gripped my shoulder, hard, her fingers blunt and farm-strong. "Ansel Thornbury. Fifty years this county has waited for a Thornbury to walk down that hole on purpose instead of being dragged. Don't make us wait fifty more."

"No pressure," I said.

"Pressure is what keeps the water down," she said, entirely seriously, and let go.

Wren didn't hug me. I think she wanted to—I saw her hands open and close at her sides—but she kept her distance the way you keep distance from someone standing at the edge of ice, as if proximity itself might tip them. Instead she crouched at the mouth of the shaft and said, in the small clear voice I had invented for her when I was six and needed a friend who never shouted:

"I'll be right above you. The whole way. Every foot. You won't be able to see me but I'll be talking, and if the talking stops, climb."

"What if I can't climb?"

"Then I'll come down and get you, and believe me, Ansel, you do not want that. I'm heavier down there than I look."

I almost laughed. It came out as something else, a short ragged breath, and then I swung my legs over the mouth of the well and the cold came up around them like water already, and I thought: *ninety feet.* I thought: *my grandmother stood here.* I thought: *she was twenty-six years old and drowning in debt and desperate, and the thing she found at the bottom of this hole offered her a deal, and she took it, and everyone I love has been paying the interest ever since.*

I thought: *measure it anyway.*

That was the creed, the old one, the one I'd carried into this county like luggage: nothing real unless it can be measured. And the truth was I was about to descend into the one place in the world where measurement was the native language—the house counted, the well registered, the ledger tallied, the mad surveyor chalked his numbers into stone—and every single measurement down there was a lie about something true. But you work with the instruments you have.

I went over the edge.

* * *

The first ten feet were stone, and I descended by the iron rungs someone had driven into the mortar a century ago—Harrow hands, probably, the father who wanted his water where he could watch it. The rungs were slick and cold and rang faintly when my boots touched them, a low bell-note that fell down the shaft ahead of me like a warning given to whoever lived at the bottom.

At ten feet the stone ended and the earth began.

Packed clay, hard as pottery, sweating cold. The flashlight showed me walls that were not empty. That was the thing I had not prepared for, though I should have—should have known, after the buttons in the parlor dust, after the teeth in the wall cavity, after everything this house had shown me about how it kept its accounts. The walls of the shaft were studded with objects, set into the clay like fossils in shale. Small ones. Domestic ones.

Buttons. Dozens of buttons, horn and bone and brass, pressed into the earth at eye level, at knee level, everywhere. A hairpin. A thimble. A doll's porcelain arm, no bigger than my thumb, reaching out of the clay at a angle, fingers curled around nothing.

And lower—when I'd dropped past the last rung and was descending on the rope, boot-braced against the wall, lowering myself hand over hand into the throat of the earth—teeth. Human teeth, set in rows, molars and incisors, dozens of them, embedded in the clay like a mosaic done by something that had learned about mosaics from a description. I made myself look at them. I made myself count them—forty-one, forty-two—because counting was armor, counting was the one prayer I knew.

At the thirty-foot mark I stopped and marked the wall. The chalk went on smooth, obedient, and I wrote **60** beneath the dead man's fading **60**, and my hand shook so badly the 6 came out wrong and I wrote it again.

Below the teeth, the objects changed character. Older. A clay marble, cat's-eye glass gone cloudy. A rusted tin whistle. And then—

A shoe.

A child's shoe, leather, laced, set into the wall sole-outward as if the foot that wore it had simply walked into the earth and kept going, leaving the shoe behind as a signature. It was small. Smaller than my palm. The stitching along the toe was fine, careful, handmade or repaired by hands that loved it.

1931 was carved into the clay beside it, in a child's uneven letters: **WREN HARROW — AGE 7**.

I hung on the rope in the dark and looked at it for a long time.

Above me, faintly, filtering down the shaft, I heard Wren's voice. She was singing. Something tuneless and patient, a child's made-up song, the kind you sing on porches in summer to fill the silence. She'd promised she'd talk the whole way down. This was her talking. I closed my eyes for one second—one—and let the song reach me, and then I kept going.

Forty feet. Fifty. The air grew colder and wetter and thicker, and the objects in the walls grew stranger: a wedding ring fused to the clay by rust. A pair of spectacles, folded. A milk tooth, then another, then a whole child's smile of them. At fifty-five feet I passed a horseshoe, and below it a pocket watch whose hands had stopped at some hour I couldn't read and didn't want to.

At sixty feet I heard the water.

Not a sound, exactly. A presence. The dark below me changed texture—from the dry black of earth to the absolute black of still water—and the temperature dropped through my boots into my bones, and the smell rose up sweet and mineral and old. I stopped. Marked the wall: **30**. Said the names, as Ruth had told me, my voice falling down the shaft and coming back changed:

"Edith Thornbury. Wren Harrow. The surveyor—God, I never got his name—*the surveyor.* The drifter from the barn. Ruth's brother. Hollis. All of them. Everyone. Counted."

The water didn't answer. But the shaft did—a long slow exhalation of cold air rising past me, carrying the smell up toward the light, and somewhere far above, the house groaned once, deep in its frame, like a sleeper turning over.

Seventy feet. Eighty. My arms burned and my shoulders screamed and I stopped trusting the rope and started trusting the rungs again—they reappeared below sixty, iron driven into clay now instead of stone, and I don't know who drove them there or when, and I have decided never to wonder about it again. Eighty-five. Eighty-eight. The flashlight beam finally found it:

Water. Black, flat, perfect. A circle of it perhaps twelve feet across, filling the bottom of the shaft like a pupil filling an eye. Still. So still it looked solid, looked like a floor, looked like a door lying flat.

I came off the last rung and stood in the mud at the edge of it, ankle-deep in cold, and raised the light.

The walls of the bottom chamber were covered in tally marks. Thousands of them. Groups of five, four strokes and a slash, four strokes and a slash, climbing the walls in columns, covering every inch of clay from the waterline to the ceiling of the chamber, and among them, in the deteriorating handwriting I knew from the attic clipboard, over and over, getting smaller and smaller and finally collapsing into pure stroke:

*I am counted. I am counted. I am counted. I am counted.*

He had stood here. He had stood exactly here, in this mud, in this light, and looked at what I was looking at, and he had tried to survive it the only way he knew.

And the water—

The water was moving.

Not rippling. *Reflecting.* Faces were surfacing in it the way bubbles surface, slow and silent, one after another, breaking the black skin of it without disturbing it: faces looking up at me from underneath, eyes open, mouths closed, patient as photographs. A woman in a housedress. A man in a feed cap. Children. Old men. A young woman with Edith Thornbury's jaw and someone else's terrified eyes.

And nearest the edge, closest to my boot, treading the still water without making a single ripple:

A girl. Seven years old. Dark hair floating around her face like ink. Looking up at me with an expression of unbearable, ordinary hope.

She raised one small hand out of the water, dripping, and pointed past me—at the wall behind my shoulder, at a section of clay I hadn't turned the light on yet.

Her lips moved. No sound came up the shaft. But I read them the way you read a child's whisper across a church pew, and what she said was:

*The pages are here. Bring the light.*
