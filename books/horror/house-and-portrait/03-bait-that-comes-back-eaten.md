## 3. Bait That Comes Back Eaten

Wendell Marchetti had a ritual for everything, and the ritual was the point.

He'd learned it from his father, who'd learned it from a man in Scranton whose name nobody remembered anymore: *a pest controller who doesn't document is just a man chasing shadows with a flashlight.* So Wendell documented. He photographed the droppings before he swept them. He measured the gnaw marks with a machinist's caliper and logged the readings to the tenth of a millimeter. He kept his traps numbered — not one through twelve like a normal man, but with a letter-and-number system he'd devised himself, cross-referenced against a spreadsheet he updated every evening in a green ledger book he'd bought in 2011 and had never once let run out of pages.

His sister Rosa called it his rosary. She wasn't wrong, and Wendell didn't mind. A man needed something to hold onto in the dark, and if what he held was a pen and a book of columns, well. Plenty of people held worse.

By seven that evening he had twelve traps set in the Holloway farmhouse, and he knew each one like a godparent knows a godchild.

A-1 through A-4 went along the kitchen's baseboards, snap traps, baited with smooth peanut butter — never crunchy; the crunch gave them something to grip and carry off, and Wendell had opinions about peanut butter the way other men had opinions about baseball teams. B-1 through B-3 went in the pantry, live-catch box traps, because Mrs. Holloway — the grandmother, the bedridden one Wendell had heard moving above him in a wheelchair that sounded like a drawer opening and closing — had said, in a voice like dry leaves, *nothing dies in this house that doesn't have to.* He'd found that an odd thing for a woman with a rodent problem to say, but he'd written it down in the margin of his work order, because odd things were data too. C-1 through C-5 went down the cellar stairs and along the cellar walls, heavy-duty traps, the kind rated for rats the size of small dogs.

He photographed the whole arrangement from four angles. He logged the bait weight of each trap — he weighed the peanut butter with a jeweler's scale, 2.3 grams apiece, because variation was the enemy of pattern. He noted ambient temperature (61°F in the kitchen, 54°F in the cellar, and he wrote *cellar cold for October, cold like a hole in the ground, not like a room*) and humidity and the direction of the prevailing draft, which came, oddly, *upward* — out of the cellar and into the kitchen, faint and steady, like the house breathing out.

Then he sat at the kitchen table with his thermos of coffee and his green book and he waited, because the first four hours of a trap line told you more than the next four days.

Maren Holloway came down once, around nine, to say goodnight, and he noticed — because Wendell noticed everything, it was the whole architecture of his mind — that she said goodnight to *him* and not to the house, and that she stood with her back to the cellar door the entire time, the way you'd stand with your back to a door if you didn't want what was behind it to see your face.

"Mr. Marchetti. You'll want to be gone by midnight."

"I've done overnight stakeouts before, Ms. Holloway. It's no trouble."

"It's not a request." She said it gently, which somehow made it worse. She was wringing a dish towel the way you'd wring a neck. "The house is — it doesn't like being watched after midnight. By people. It gets confused about who's watching."

"Confused."

"Confused," she agreed, and looked at the floor, and Wendell understood that the conversation was a door closing, and that pressing on it would get him nothing but a bruised nose. He was a patient man. Patience was the other half of documentation. You didn't get the pattern by kicking the wall; you got it by watching the wall long enough to learn when it breathed.

"Midnight," he said. "I'll be at the motel by twelve-thirty."

She thanked him. She did not look at the cellar door. She left the kitchen walking backward until she reached the hallway, and Wendell wrote that down too, in his small neat hand: *client exits room in reverse. Avoids sightline to cellar. Consistent w/ prior visit. Not superstition — habit. Habits take years to build. Ask how old.*

At eleven-forty he packed his gear, did a final walk-through, photographed the trap line one last time — all twelve traps pristine, bait intact, mechanisms armed, each one logged with its exact position relative to the baseboard — and let himself out into the cold. The night smelled like woodsmoke and turned earth. Behind him the farmhouse sat in its yard like a thing holding its breath, every window dark except the grandmother's, where a lamp burned the way lamps burn in houses where somebody is keeping a vigil and won't say for what.

He drove to the motel in Harlow Falls, showered, and was asleep by twelve-twenty, which he also wrote down, because Wendell Marchetti wrote everything down, and it had never once occurred to him that a man's records could become a debt.

* * *

He was back at seven the next morning, thermos in hand, and he knew something was wrong before he was out of the truck.

Not wrong the way a broken window is wrong. Wrong the way a room is wrong when someone has been in it and put everything back *almost* right. The porch boards were wet. Not dew-wet — the night had been dry, he'd checked the forecast, he always checked the forecast — wet in a line, as if something heavy had been dragged across them and then carefully, thoughtfully, dried.

The kitchen door was unlocked. He'd locked it. He had the key on his own ring, and the key was still on his own ring, and the lock showed no scratching, and Wendell stood in the doorway for a long moment with his hand on the frame, feeling the upward draft from the cellar move across his knuckles, cool and patient, like a clerk waiting for him to present his papers.

"Okay," he said out loud, to nobody, because saying things out loud kept the animal brain fed and quiet. "Okay. Let's see what we've got."

What he had, first of all, was the smell. Peanut butter. Everywhere. Not rancid, not scattered — *arranged*. The kitchen smelled like a bakery run by something with a very precise idea of how much peanut butter a room should contain.

Then he saw the traps.

A-1 through A-4 were lined up on the kitchen table. Not on the floor where he'd set them, along the baseboards, spaced at his calibrated intervals — on the *table*, in the center, in a row, nose to tail, facing the cellar door. B-1 through B-3 were beside them. C-1 through C-5 were beside *those*, and Wendell felt the hair on his arms rise as he counted them, because the row was ordered A, then B, then C — his own classification system, his own lettering, *respected*. Something had gone through the house, found all twelve traps in three separate rooms, and reassembled them in the kitchen in taxonomic order, facing the cellar door like an audience facing a stage.

Every trap was sprung. No — not sprung. Wendell got down close, got his calipers out, got his loupe, because if he was going to lose his mind he was going to lose it *on the record*. The mechanisms were untouched. The springs held full tension. The kill-bars sat in the armed position, and when he tested A-1 with a pencil eraser it snapped with a clean, healthy crack, fully functional, never fired.

The bait was gone. All of it. Every trap, 2.3 grams of smooth peanut butter, and the pans where the bait had sat were not licked, not gnawed, not smeared with rodent spoor. They were *clean*. Cleaned the way you'd clean a plate at a dinner where you'd been raised to have manners. He weighed the empty pans on the jeweler's scale out of pure reflex: 0.0 grams of residue. Not 0.1. Not a smear. Zero.

He photographed all of it. Four angles per trap, wide shots of the row, a shot of the row *from the cellar door's perspective*, which was a thing his hands did before his brain approved it, and when he saw the photo on the little screen — twelve traps in a line, and beyond them the cellar door, and the composition so deliberate it looked staged for a textbook — he had to put the camera down and stand in the corner of the kitchen for a minute, breathing, being a professional, being a man with a system.

Then he opened the green book and he worked, because the work was the rope and he was going to climb it.

*7:12 a.m. — all 12 traps recovered from kitchen table, arranged in single row, ordered A1–A5 (sic, B and C included), facing cellar door. Bait absent, all 12. Mechanisms unfired, full spring tension. No residue in pans. No droppings. No tracks in flour* — and there his pen stopped, because he'd laid down a monitoring dusting of flour along the baseboards the evening before, standard practice, a fine white snowfall to record whatever crossed it.

The flour was pristine. Not a print in it. Twelve traps had been lifted off the floor, carried — *carried*, through the kitchen, past the flour lines, without disturbing a single grain — and set on the table, and the flour had been left perfect. Either the thing could fly, or it knew where the flour was, and avoided it, which meant it had *seen him lay the flour*, which meant it had been in the house while he worked, watching him work, learning his methods the way he was learning its patterns.

Wendell sat down at the kitchen table, across from the row of empty traps, and poured his coffee, and had a conversation with himself that went like this:

*It's a hoax. The family's doing it. Some farm-kid prank, moving traps around, eating the bait off a spoon.*

Except the pans were clean. A spoon left a smear. And the traps had been moved without touching the flour, which meant the hoaxer knew about the flour, and Maren Holloway didn't know about the flour — he'd laid it after she went up, after the lamp in the grandmother's window had gone out.

*It's raccoons. Raccoons move things, raccoons are clever.*

Raccoons left prints. Raccoons left scat, hair, grease marks, chaos. Raccoons did not arrange twelve traps in taxonomic order facing a door. Raccoons were clever the way a burglar is clever. Whatever had done this was clever the way a *butler* is clever.

*It's* — and here his mind offered him the fingerprints from the flour, the ones from his first visit, the handprints too large with too many joints, the ones he'd photographed and catalogued and privately filed under *structural settling of an old house, some kind of impression artifact, dust physics* — and his mind offered them up now with a little note attached, the note reading: *you knew. You knew the first day and you documented it anyway because documenting felt safer than believing.*

Wendell drank his coffee. It had gone cold. He drank it anyway.

Because here was the thing, and the thing was this: he wasn't afraid. He kept waiting to be afraid — he checked, the way you probe a tooth — and what he found instead, rising in his chest like warmth off a stove, was *fascination*. Twenty-two years in this trade and he had never once, not once, met an animal that was *courteous*. He'd met clever rats and bold coons and one possum in Vestal County with a genius-level grasp of crawlspaces, but everything alive was greedy and sloppy and left a mess, because life was greedy and sloppy, that was the whole signature of life.

This thing cleaned its plate. This thing respected his classifications. This thing had eaten twelve servings of peanut butter — 27.6 grams, he did the math in the margin — and had left the kitchen cleaner than it found it, and had arranged the evidence *to be found*, facing the cellar door, and Wendell had been in this business long enough to know what an arrangement like that was.

It was a message. And messages meant a mind. And a mind meant you could talk to it.

He was so absorbed — logging, measuring, photographing the flour lines at raking light with his flashlight held low, hunting for the single grain out of place — that he didn't hear Maren come down. He became aware of her the way you become aware of weather: a change in the pressure of the room. She stood in the hallway with her arms crossed tight, looking at the row of traps on the table, and her face did something Wendell had seen on people standing at open caskets — the flinch of confirmation.

"It did this," she said. Not a question.

"It did this," Wendell said. "Ms. Holloway, I need to ask you some questions, and I need you to answer them straight, because I'm going to be honest with you — I have never in twenty-two years seen anything like this, and I have seen a *lot*."

"Mr. Marchetti—"

"It ate the bait and cleaned the pans. It moved twelve traps through a dusted room without leaving a print. It *filed them by my own labeling system*, which means it read my labels, which means it can read, or it can recognize symbols, or it has been watching me long enough to learn my shorthand, and every one of those possibilities is a problem I am not equipped for, and I'd like to know what I'm actually being paid to deal with here."

She looked at the traps. She looked at the cellar door. And Wendell watched her make a decision — watched it happen, actually watched it, the way you watch a scale tip — and the decision, whatever it was, went the wrong way, because she said, in a voice sanded down to nothing:

"You should check your truck, Mr. Marchetti. You should always check your truck. It likes vehicles. It doesn't understand them, and things that don't understand things *touch* them."

And she turned and went back up the stairs, and Wendell stood in the kitchen with his cold coffee and his green book full of beautiful useless columns, and he thought: *that's not a woman describing a pest. That's a woman describing a neighbor.*

He wrote one more line before he went out to the truck. He wrote it slowly, and he underlined it, and later — much later, when everything was ash — this would be the line he'd remember, the line where the fascination was still winning, where he still thought he was documenting an animal instead of an account:

*Subject is intelligent, deliberate, and courteous. Subject returns what is offered in better order than received. Working hypothesis: we are not dealing with an infestation. We are dealing with a resident. Recommend further observation. Recommend* — and here his pen had hesitated, and he'd written the word and hated it and kept it — *recommend courtesy.*

Then he walked out to the truck, and the morning was bright and ordinary, and the driver's side window was covered in fingerprints.

Dozens of them. Overlapping. Pressed into the dew from the *outside*, at a height no man's hand would reach comfortably, each one too large, each one with a joint too many, and they were not smeared or random or animal.

They were placed.

Wendell stood in the wet grass with his camera and his shaking, competent hands, and he photographed every single one, and he did not run, because running was not in his system. But in the green book, under *recommend courtesy*, he added a final line, and his handwriting, for the first time in twenty-two years, was not neat:

*It counted my traps. It is going to count something else. Find out what.*
