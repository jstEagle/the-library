## 13. The Well Refills

The rain started on a Tuesday and did not stop until it had opinions about everything.

Wendell Marchetti stood at the kitchen window of the Holloway farmhouse with his coffee going cold in one hand and his journal open on the sill beneath the other, and he watched the back field turn into something that was no longer a field. It was becoming a lake with ambitions. The surveyors' stakes from the excavation — Dale Pruitt's crew, before they'd broken the door and then scattered like startled birds — stood in six inches of standing water, their orange flags drooping like men who'd given up.

He wrote: *Day 4 of rain. Field flooded to the fence line. Well water at 11 feet below grade (measured by line and weight, 6:40 a.m.).*

Then, because the journal deserved honesty even when he didn't feel honest: *I have started measuring a well every morning at dawn like a man taking his own pulse. I am aware of how this looks. I do not care. The numbers are the only thing in this house that behaves.*

Behind him, Maren Holloway set another log on the stove without being asked. She'd learned his rhythms the way he'd learned hers — which was to say quickly, quietly, and with a tenderness that neither of them had named out loud yet because naming things in this house felt dangerous. Names went into ledgers here. Names got counted.

"Eleven feet?" she asked.

"Eleven feet."

"It was thirteen on Sunday."

"I know."

She nodded and didn't say anything else, which was its own kind of fluency. Wendell had spent thirty years reading houses for a living — reading droppings and grease trails and gnaw-marks, the whole honest language of things that lived where they weren't wanted — and Maren Holloway was the first person who'd ever read him back. It was disconcerting. It was also, he was beginning to suspect, the best thing that had ever happened to him, which said something grim about the competition.

* * *

Here is what Wendell knew, arranged in the order he'd learned it, which was not the order anyone would have chosen:

He knew the thing under the Holloway farm had been fed for a hundred and seventy years, on a schedule, by a family that kept a ledger of it the way other families kept recipe books. He knew Elias — Maren's brother, gentle Elias, who counted under his breath the way other men whistled — had been taken below eleven days ago, marked *pending* in wet ink by no hand anyone could see. He knew the count ran to seven, and that six names could stop it if they were freely given, and that the grandmother, Ada, had written her own name in the ledger four nights ago and died in her sleep with an expression of such profound relief that Wendell had briefly envied her.

He knew the well had been rising since.

Not like a well rises after rain. He'd checked. He'd driven three miles to the Kesterson place and asked old Harlan Kesterson, over coffee, whether his well had come up any. Harlan said two feet, maybe, and complained about his sump pump, and Wendell had smiled and agreed and driven home doing arithmetic in his head that made him pull over twice.

The Holloway well had come up nine feet in four days. The rain was an excuse. The rain was cover. Whatever lived down there had decided, in the way of all patient things, that the season was favorable, and it was climbing.

So Wendell did the only thing he knew how to do with a problem too large to solve: he measured it.

Every morning, six-forty, before coffee, before conversation, he dropped a weighted fishing line down the well shaft in the cellar — the old well, the original one, bricked over in 1911 except nobody had told the water — and he marked the depth in his journal. Then he walked the perimeter of the house and noted the sounds. Then he wrote it all down in columns so neat that Maren once picked up the journal, looked at it, and said, "You know this is the most romantic thing you've ever done," and he'd said, "That's genuinely alarming," and she'd kissed him anyway, which had made the whole enterprise worth it.

The columns looked like this:

*Oct. 14 — Well: 13 ft. Sounds: none. Scratches at tree line: no.*
*Oct. 15 — Well: 12 ft. Sounds: dripping, irregular, from NE corner of cellar. Scratches: no.*
*Oct. 16 — Well: 11 ft. Sounds: dripping regular now, like a clock. Scratches: yes, fence post, north side.*
*Oct. 17 — Well: 10 ft. Sounds: breathing? (see note). Scratches: porch rail.*

The note on Oct. 17 read: *I don't want to write "breathing." I have listened to that sound for six hours through the floorboards and I cannot make it be anything else. It is slow. It is patient. It is the sound of something conserving itself. I have decided to call it "the tide" because "breathing" makes me want to leave and I can't leave.*

Grim competence as coping. His father had called it something less kind — *stubbornness dressed up as a system* — but his father had also died believing pest control was a calling, so the family bar for self-deception was high.

* * *

On the fifth morning the line came up at eight feet, and Wendell stood in the cellar holding the wet rope and understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only when you've run out of ways to be wrong, that the thing wasn't rising with the water.

It was raising the water.

"You're sure?" Maren asked. They were both in the cellar, which had become the least frightening room in the house, which was a sentence Wendell never expected to think. The scratches had moved past the porch now. Last night they'd been on the kitchen window, outside the glass, five-fingered and too long-jointed, pressed there gently, almost politely, the way you'd knock if you didn't believe in knocking.

"The aquifer doesn't work like this," Wendell said. "Rain recharges groundwater over weeks, months. It doesn't lift one specific column of water nine feet in five days while every other well in the county goes up two." He held up the rope. "This is a bucket brigade. Something down there is hauling water up and stacking it under itself like—" He stopped.

"Like a man standing on crates to reach a window," Maren finished.

They looked at each other. Neither of them said *to reach us.* Some sentences you keep in your pocket.

Elias had explained it once, before — the logic of the ledger, the terrible bookkeeping of the place. *It doesn't hate us,* Elias had said, in the tone of a boy describing weather. *It counts us. That's worse, probably. Hate gets tired. Counting doesn't.* And now the count was short one name — six given, seven owed — and the thing below had apparently concluded that if the payment wouldn't come down the stairs, it would bring the stairs up.

Wendell wrote it all down. *Conclusion: the rise is active, not passive. Rate increasing ~1.5 ft/day. At current rate, surface water in the shaft by Oct. 22. At current rate, whatever is riding the water reaches the house by—*

He did the math twice. He didn't like either answer.

* * *

Ada Holloway's room was at the back of the house, and since her death nobody had slept in it, and since her death the floorboards in its northeast corner had begun, very softly, to talk.

Not scratch. Talk. A low rhythmic creak, like a rocking chair with no one in it, keeping time with nothing audible. Wendell timed it against his watch. Forty-one cycles per minute. He timed his own resting heart rate out of professional thoroughness and got sixty-two, and then sat on the edge of the guest bed feeling foolish and relieved and foolish again.

Maren found him there at midnight, sitting in the dark with his journal on his knees.

"You're mapping it," she said. Not a question. She'd seen the journal. Tide tables, she'd called the pages, and she hadn't meant it as a compliment, exactly, but she'd touched the back of his neck while she said it, so it hadn't been an insult either.

"Somebody has to know where it is," Wendell said. "That's the whole job. Thirty years of it. You find out where the thing lives, what route it takes, what time it moves. Then you know where to put the trap." He laughed, short and dry. "Except I can't bait this one. Believe me, I tried. It ate everything and left the mechanisms untouched and arranged them facing the cellar door, and I logged it, and somewhere in my files there is a photograph of a row of empty snap traps presented like an offering, and I keep thinking — that was the polite phase. We were in the polite phase."

"And now?"

"Now we're in the arithmetic phase." He turned the journal toward her, to the page where he'd drawn the house in plan view, top-down, and marked the progress of the scratches night by night in red pencil. Fence line. Garden. Porch. Kitchen wall. Each mark dated. Each mark closer. It looked like a military map. It looked like a storm track. It looked, most of all, like a countdown, and they both knew it, and he showed her anyway, because the alternative was protecting her from information, and he had decided — possibly wrongly, possibly fatally — that the Holloways had been protected from information for a hundred and seventy years and look how that had gone.

Maren studied the map for a long moment. Her finger hovered over the kitchen wall marks.

"How many nights?" she asked.

"At this rate? Three. Maybe four."

"And then what?"

"And then I don't know, because every record in this family ends right around there." He closed the journal. "Which is why tomorrow I'm driving to the county archive. There's a deed from 1849 I haven't seen. Original paperwork. If this thing has terms, the terms are written down somewhere, and I want them."

Maren sat down next to him on the dead woman's bed. Outside, the rain kept up its work, and beneath the floorboards the rocking-creak kept its own count, forty-one to the minute, unhurried, certain.

"Wendell," she said. "When you find the terms. Read them before you tell me what they are. Just — once. Just for yourself. So that when you tell me, your face already knows."

He promised he would. It was the easiest promise he ever broke.

* * *

The sixth morning brought two developments, and Wendell recorded them in order of measurability, because measurability was the raft he was clinging to.

First: the well stood at six feet, and the water in it was no longer still. He lowered the line and watched it drift sideways in the shaft, pulled gently toward the bottom, as though something far below were drinking through its teeth.

Second: the mail came.

The mailbox was at the road, a quarter mile down the drive, and Wendell walked it because walking the drive had become part of the ritual too — checking the fence posts for new marks, noting the water level in the flooded field, logging the whole drowned geography of the place. The marks had stopped appearing on the fence posts two nights ago. He'd written *scratches: none* with a relief that lasted approximately four hours, until he realized why they'd stopped.

Nothing leaves marks on things it has already passed.

In the box: a flyer for gutter cleaning, a bill from the co-op, and a padded envelope addressed to DALE PRUITT, C/O W. MARCHETTI, in handwriting that shook like a seismograph.

Wendell stood in the rain looking at it for a long time. He knew what it was before he opened the truck door. He knew the way you know a house has mice before you see a single dropping — some knowledge arrives through the skin.

Inside was a cassette tape, a single sheet of paper, and a photograph.

The paper said: *I'm sorry. I should have listened to the lady. This is what I saw. Play it somewhere with good light. — D.P.*

The photograph was of the chamber behind the stone door, taken on a flash camera, and Wendell had to sit down on the truck's running board in the rain to look at it properly, because it was not a lair. It was not a den. It was a *room*. Furnished. A chair worn smooth by decades of sitting. Shelves. A cot. A child's drawing pinned to the stone wall, faded to ghosts. Generations of habitation, ending in dust — and on the inner face of the sealed door, visible in the flash glare, the finger-smeared mortar, dragged in long desperate strokes.

Sealed from the inside. By something that had lived there. In a chair.

Wendell put the tape in his jacket, inside the plastic bag he now carried everywhere for documents, and walked back up the drive through the rain, and at the tree line he stopped, because the tree line had changed overnight. The standing water reached it now. And at the edge of the water, among the trunks, the light fell wrong — a tall absence, patient, upright, watching the house with the stillness of something that had watched it for a hundred and seventy years and considered itself, in some ruined way, a resident.

Wendell did not run. Running was data he refused to provide.

He raised his journal instead, and wrote, standing in the rain, hand steady through sheer bureaucratic spite: *Oct. 19. Subject visible at tree line, 0900 hrs. First direct observation. Estimated height 7 ft. Did not approach. Appears to be waiting. Note: it is not between us and the road. It is between us and the well. It is guarding its own exit. Conclusion: it wants us to stay more than it wants anything else.*

Then, underneath, smaller: *Three nights.*

And underneath that, because his father had taught him that a good log always separates observation from interpretation: *Interpretation: we are running out of mornings to measure.*

He walked inside. Maren met him in the kitchen, took one look at his face, and put the kettle on, and while it heated he laid the cassette on the table between them like a card he didn't want to play.

"Pruitt mailed me what he saw," Wendell said. "Before he—" He stopped. The paper hadn't said. But men who mailed apology tapes did not usually follow up by phone.

Maren looked at the tape. Then at him. Then, together, they turned toward the parlor, where the old reel-to-reel recorder sat on a shelf — Ada's, inherited with the house, along with the ledger and the debt.

From below the floorboards, soft and steady, forty-one times a minute, the creaking kept its count. And for the first time since he'd arrived at this house, Wendell Marchetti noticed that the rhythm had changed.

He timed it against his watch, hands not quite steady, and wrote the number down before he let himself understand it.

Thirty-eight.

It was slowing down. Like something settling in. Like something making itself comfortable for a long stay — or like a clock winding down toward zero.

"Play the tape," Maren whispered.
