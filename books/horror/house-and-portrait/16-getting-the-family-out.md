## 16. Getting the Family Out

Wendell Marchetti had once extracted a colony of forty-seven bats from a church attic in Kettle Falls without waking the priest sleeping in the rectory below, and he had done it with a flashlight held in his teeth, a bedsheet, and a plan he'd drawn on the back of an invoice. He liked to think of that night as his masterpiece. It was the story he told at parties, back when he went to parties, back before he'd learned that the word "infestation" could mean things with too many knuckles in their hands.

The Holloway extraction, he decided, spreading his journal flat on the kitchen table at eleven at night, would make the bats look like a warm-up act.

"Walk me through it again," Maren said. She was sitting across from him with a mug of tea she hadn't touched in an hour, which was how he knew she was scared. When Maren was calm she drank tea like it was a competitive sport. When she was frightened the mug just sat there, going cold, a little monument to everything she couldn't hold onto.

"Three phases," Wendell said. He'd drawn a map of the property in ballpoint, and he'd drawn it well, because drawing things well was the one prayer he still believed in. "Phase one: the grandmother. Phase two: you. Phase three: the ledger."

"You put the ledger after me."

"The ledger is the heaviest and the most dangerous to carry."

"I'm not talking about weight." She turned the mug a quarter turn on the table, a nervous habit he'd catalogued weeks ago, entry forty-something, *subject rotates objects when distressed, clockwise only.* "It goes in my hands or it doesn't go. That thing down there has been fed by that book for a hundred and seventy years. I'm not handing my family's whole rotten history to you like you're a moving company."

Wendell considered arguing. He was, by nature and by trade, an arguer — he'd once spent forty minutes explaining to a farmer why his "mouse problem" was actually a mink problem and the farmer had thanked him at the end, which Wendell counted among his life's genuine triumphs. But he looked at Maren's face, at the exhaustion and the iron in it, and he folded.

"Fine. Phase three is you and the ledger together. But you hold my arm on the fence line. The ground's uneven past the oak and if you go down with that book under your arm you'll break a wrist, and then I'll have to carry both of you, and I skipped arm day for the last forty years."

That got the ghost of a smile out of her. He wrote it down later, in his head, where he kept the entries that didn't belong in the journal: *Made her smile. 11:40 p.m. Method: self-deprecation. Efficacy: moderate. Recommend continued use.*

* * *

The plan, laid out in full, looked like this.

The truck would stay in the barn, engine cold, because the thing below had learned the truck. It had pressed its prints into the frost on the windshield twice now, and Wendell did not believe in coincidence, he believed in pattern, and the pattern said: *it knows the shape of your leaving.* So the truck would sit there all night like a decoy duck while the three of them — four, counting the ledger, which Maren insisted on counting — went out through the back pasture on foot, a mile and a half of open ground to the county road, where Dale Pruitt's second-in-command, a guilt-racked kid named Tommy Oakes, would be waiting in a flatbed with the tarp off and the engine warm.

They'd cross before four. The well had been rising all week — Wendell had mapped it in his journal like tide tables, because grim competence was the only life raft he had left to cling to — and the water's nightly peak came around four-thirty, when the scratching under the floorboards got closest to the kitchen. Whatever window the house gave them, it gave them then.

"The grandmother," Maren said.

And there it was. The problem he'd been circling all evening like a mink around a henhouse.

Edith Holloway was eighty-nine years old, bedridden for two years, sharp as a tack that had been left out in the rain and somehow gotten sharper. Wendell had met her exactly four times, and each time she had looked at him the way you look at a bill you intend to dispute. She weighed perhaps ninety pounds. She could not walk. She could, however, refuse, and she had the refined, lifelong expertise in refusal that only very old women and federal agencies possess.

"I'll carry her," Wendell said. "Fireman's carry won't work with her hips. I'll do a cradle carry. I used to haul fifty-pound bags of bait compound all day. She's got twenty on that, easy."

"She won't weigh twenty pounds more than bait," Maren said, and then her face did something complicated. "That came out wrong."

"No it didn't. It came out exactly right, and I'm choosing to be flattered." He capped his pen. "The real question isn't the carry. It's whether she'll consent to be cargo."

"She'll refuse."

"She'll refuse," Wendell agreed. "So we don't ask her permission. We ask her advice."

Maren frowned at him. "That's either very clever or very dishonest."

"Those are the same thing when done well."

* * *

They went up to Edith's room at midnight, and Wendell understood immediately that his plan had a flaw, and the flaw was that Edith Holloway had been running cons since before he was born.

The room smelled of camphor and lavender and, faintly, of the well — that wet mineral breath that had been seeping up through the house for a week now, stronger every night, so that the whole farmhouse smelled like the inside of the earth holding its breath. Edith lay propped against her pillows, small and white-haired and utterly unfooled, and she watched Wendell come through the door with the expression of a woman who has already read the last page of the book he was carrying.

"Well," she said. "The exterminator's come to move the furniture."

"Mrs. Holloway—"

"Edith. You've been in my house six weeks, Wendell Marchetti, and you still knock like a bill collector. Edith." She patted the quilt beside her, an invitation aimed at Maren, and Maren went and sat and took her grandmother's hand, and Wendell stood by the door feeling like a salesman in a cathedral.

"We're leaving tonight," Maren said. "All of us. Before dawn. There's a truck on the county road and a plan and—" her voice caught, and Wendell watched her rebuild it, brick by brick, "—and it has to be all of us, Edith. The count's at six. If it gets to seven with a Holloway still in the house—"

"Then the count finishes and the rest of you live," Edith said. "That's how it's always worked. That's how it worked for your great-great-grandmother, who stayed. That's how it worked for my mother, who stayed. That's how it worked for me, God help me, who stayed when your grandfather begged me to run, because *somebody has to keep the house.*" She said it without drama, the way you'd recite a phone number you'd dialed ten thousand times. "The house needs a keeper, girl. That's not sentiment. That's maintenance."

"The house," Wendell said, before he could stop himself, "is going to be on fire by tomorrow night."

Both women looked at him.

He hadn't meant to say it. It was phase four of the plan, the phase he hadn't told Maren about yet, the phase that lived in the back pages of his journal in pencil because some things you don't ink until you're sure. But there it was, out in the camphor air, and he found that he didn't want to take it back.

"You're going to burn it," Edith said. Not a question. And then, to his lasting astonishment, she smiled — a dry, cracked, genuinely delighted smile, like a woman hearing that a hated neighbor's fence had blown down. "Good. I've wanted somebody to burn this house since 1962. Never had the nerve. Never had the *right.*" The smile folded away. "But I'm not going with you, and we both know it, so let's skip the part where you two pretend I'm cargo."

"Edith—" Maren started.

"I'm eighty-nine. I can't walk, I can't be carried across a frozen pasture without my heart giving out at the fence line, and you'd be burying me in a ditch by six a.m. instead of letting me die in my own bed like a civilized woman." She said it flatly, factually, the way Wendell himself reported trap counts, and he recognized the technique because it was *his* technique — the professional calm laid over the abyss. She'd been doing documentation-as-prayer her whole life too. Hers just took the form of refusing to flinch.

"There's another way," Wendell said quietly. "The ledger. It takes names. Your grandmother wrote hers in 1911. It bought the house sixty years."

Edith's eyes came around to him, slow and sharp. "I know what the ledger takes. I helped my mother write in it. I know exactly what it takes." She was quiet a moment. The well-breath moved through the room, and somewhere below them — below the floor, below the cellar, below the sealed door and the stairwell and the rising black water — something shifted its weight, patient as arithmetic.

"Here's my advice," Edith said, "since you came all this way to ask for it. You take my granddaughter across that field, and you take that book, and you burn this house down to the footer stones, and you don't you *dare* write my name in anything." Her hand found Maren's and gripped it with a strength that surprised them both. "Six generations of Holloways have paid that thing in blood so the rest could run. I'll pay the old way. In place. It's what the house needs. And Wendell Marchetti—" her voice dropped, and for a second the iron in it trembled, just once, like a wire in wind, "—you take care of my girl. That's not advice. That's the price of my blessing, and my blessing is the only thing you're getting out of me tonight."

Wendell stood there with his plan in his pocket and his throat full of gravel, and he understood that he had been outmaneuvered by a woman who hadn't left her bed in two years, and that there was nothing to do but salute.

"Yes, ma'am," he said.

* * *

They spent the small hours preparing, and it was strange, Wendell thought, how tenderness hides inside logistics. He packed the truck's decoy cab with junk — old traps, a spare coat, his hat — so it would read, from below, like a man making ready. He checked the pasture fence line by flashlight and found the loose rail by the oak and wired it shut so it wouldn't betray Maren in the dark. He filled a thermos with coffee and a second one with the sweet milky tea Edith liked, because even a woman refusing rescue deserved her provisions, and because his hands needed tasks or they'd start shaking.

Maren came out to the barn at two in the morning and found him kneeling in the straw, checking the latches on a wooden crate he'd built for the ledger.

"It has a crate," she said.

"The ledger has a crate."

"It's a book, Wendell."

"It's a hundred and seventy years of my girlfriend's family being held hostage, and it's traveling in a crate with foam lining, and if you have a problem with that you can take it up with my supervisor, who is also me, and who is very firm on this point."

She laughed — a real laugh, brief and startled out of her, the first one in days — and then she crossed the barn and put her arms around him from behind, her cheek against his back, and neither of them said anything for a while. The house stood dark behind them. Under the ground, the water rose. Somewhere in the walls of the world, something counted.

"Three-forty," Wendell said finally. "We move at three-forty. You, me, the crate. Edith stays. Tommy's flatbed at the county road. And then—" he turned in her arms, and took her face in his hands, and said the phase four out loud, because she deserved to hear it from him and not from the smoke, "—then I come back with kerosene and a match, and we end it."

"You're coming back to me," Maren said. It wasn't a question either. It was an instruction, delivered in her grandmother's exact tone, and Wendell Marchetti, who had faced down mink and raccoons and one deeply personal encounter with a snapping turtle, found that this was the order he was most afraid to disobey.

"I'm coming back to you," he said.

They were in the kitchen at three-thirty, coats on, the crate by the door, when the scratching under the floorboards stopped — all of it, at once, the way a clock stops — and the ledger, shut in its crate across the room, opened itself with a sound like a sigh.

Wendell looked down at the page it had turned to.

Wet ink was already drying on the seventh line.
