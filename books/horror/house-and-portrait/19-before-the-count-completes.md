## 19. Before the Count Completes

### Wendell

The county archive closed at five, and Wendell Marchetti had been the last one out since 1998.

He'd spent four hours in the deed room with cotton gloves and a magnifying lamp, photographing every page of the original 1849 conveyance, and now the photographs sat in his breast pocket like a stone he'd swallowed. He didn't need to look at them again. He knew what they said. He'd read them eleven times, and each time the words had rearranged themselves into a slightly worse shape, the way a bad dream does when you try to hold it still.

*The town of Harrow's Bend, being indebted to the party of the first part for considerations rendered during the Fever Winter, hereby discharges said debt by grant of the following parcel, together with its appurtenances, obligations, and resident.*

*Resident.* Singular. Like it was a tenant. Like it paid rent.

Wendell drove out past the tree line with his headlights off for the last half mile, which was stupid, and he knew it was stupid, and he did it anyway because some habits of caution are just prayers you make with your hands. The truck rolled to a stop in the dead grass where the excavation crew had left their scars — survey stakes like little white teeth, orange flagging tape fluttering on nothing. He'd documented all of this once. Stake locations. Tape condition. Soil displacement in cubic yards. There was a version of himself, three weeks younger, who believed that if you wrote down enough of a thing, you could hold it. Put it in a box. Label the box.

That version of himself had watched Sheriff Boone feed a manila folder into a sink full of burning solvent and stand there warming his hands over it like it was a campfire.

So no more documentation. Tonight there was only the ledger, wrapped in a flour sack on the passenger seat, and the pen in his shirt pocket, and the name he intended to write in it.

His own.

He sat in the cab for a while and let himself do the thing he never let himself do, which was think about Maren Holloway without an agenda. The way she held her coffee cup with both hands even in summer. The way she'd laughed — actually laughed, one sharp surprised bark of it — when he'd told her about the time he'd been called to a bakery infestation and the "rats" turned out to be the baker's son eating the bait cheese at 3 a.m. for a dare. The way she looked at Elias's empty chair now, at dinner, and then made herself look away, because looking was a kind of counting too.

If he wrote his name, the count stopped. That was the arithmetic of it. Six names taken or given; the seventh completed the tally and freed the thing below to start fresh, hungry, unbound. But a keeper — a willing keeper, written in living ink by a hand that chose it — reset the whole ledger. Another generation bought. His grandmother's people had come from a village in Calabria that had similar arrangements with similar things, though theirs had lived under a fig tree and preferred goats. His nonna used to say: *the debt is never the animal's fault, Wendellino. It is always the men who signed.*

Well. Here he was. A man, signing.

He got out of the truck. The night smelled like rain that hadn't arrived yet — that green metallic promise hanging over everything. The house stood against the sky with its windows dark except one, and he felt the old familiar pull in his chest, the one that had started somewhere around chapter — around week two, when she'd told him about the well and her mother's hands teaching hers the difference between hunger-sounds and patience-sounds.

He'd promised her he wouldn't do this. He'd promised her with actual words, out loud, which for Wendell was roughly equivalent to notarization.

"Sorry," he said to the dashboard, and took the ledger, and walked toward the house.

The cellar door wasn't locked anymore. Nothing needed locking now; locks were a courtesy between parties who hadn't yet agreed on terms. He went down the stairs with his penlight between his teeth, past the kitchen where the floorboards had finally gone quiet, past the shelf where the traps still stood in their neat row facing the inner door — emptied, courteous, intelligent. He knelt at the stone door with its mortar finger-smeared from the inside, and he laid the ledger open on the cold floor, and he uncapped his pen.

"Okay," he said quietly, to whatever was listening. "Let's talk terms."

And then the penlight caught movement behind him, and he knew — before he turned, before he saw her standing at the top of the stairs in her coat over her nightgown, hair loose, breathing hard — exactly how much trouble he was in.

"Maren."

"You left a note," she said.

"I left a *list*. Of emergency contacts. That's different."

"It said 'if I'm not back by dawn, tell the sheriff everything, including the parts he burned.'" She came down the stairs slowly, each step deliberate, like she was walking toward him through deep water. "You wrote *including the parts he burned*, Wendell. In your handwriting. On my refrigerator. With a magnet shaped like a strawberry."

"I contain multitudes," Wendell said. "Also I panicked about the note. It was either that or 'gone fishing,' and I've never fished in my life, and you'd have known it was a lie."

"Damn right I would." She reached the bottom step. Her eyes were wet and furious and lit up green in the penlight, and God help him, even now, some traitorous corner of his brain was cataloguing it. *Subject: M.H. Condition: incandescent.* "Give me the pen."

"No."

"Wendell—"

"It has to be me." He kept his voice low, steady, the voice he used on frightened homeowners. *Ma'am, the wasps won't hurt you if you don't swat.* "It was never supposed to be your family. I read the deed, Mare. Your great-great-whatever didn't owe this thing anything. The *town* owed it. They handed your family the bill and called it inheritance. Two hundred years of Holloways paying a debt they never signed for. Well, I'm signing. I'm the first volunteer in the whole sorry history of this arrangement, and I think—" his voice cracked, and he hated it, and pushed through, "—I think that matters. To it. I think choice is the one currency it's never been paid in."

Maren stared at him for a long moment. Then she did something he didn't expect. She sat down on the cold cellar floor across from him, cross-legged, coat pooling around her, and folded her hands.

"Fine," she said. "Then we negotiate together. Because I have read that ledger my entire life, Wendell Marchetti, and you have read it for three weeks, and I am telling you — it doesn't want a name. It wants a *keeper*. And keepers don't get to be strangers. Keepers get fed to it eventually, same as everyone else, just slower. My mother taught me the sounds. You can't learn the sounds in one night."

"I'm a fast study."

"You alphabetize your spice rack."

"That's not relevant."

"It's extremely relevant. It tells me you think order is a shield." Her voice gentled, which was worse than the fury. "It isn't. I watched it not be a shield for Elias."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Above them, faintly, the house ticked and settled. Below them — beneath the stone, beneath the stairwell, down where the well water had risen forty feet in nine days — something breathed. Or something that wanted them to think it breathed. Wendell had stopped being sure there was a difference.

"The count completes at seven," Maren said. "Or a keeper binds it. Those are the only two doors. But there's a third thing, Wendell. A thing my grandmother told me once, drunk on communion wine at Easter, laughing like it was the funniest joke in Christendom." She leaned forward. "She said: *it can't take what's freely given twice over. One giver binds it. Two givers confuse it.* Confuse it, she said. Like you'd confuse a dog. Like you'd confuse a child."

"Two names?"

"Two names. Written together. By two people who mean it." She held out her hand for the pen. "You want to sacrifice yourself alone? That's not love, that's accounting. I do the accounting in this family. Have for years. You want to give? Give *with* me, or go home."

### Maren

She had followed him because of the strawberry magnet, yes, but also because she'd woken at midnight with the certain knowledge of it — the way you wake knowing someone has died, or will call, or has left the stove on. Twenty-nine years of sleeping in this house teaches you its weather. And tonight the house had been holding its breath.

Now she sat on the cellar floor with her palms flat on the cold stone and watched Wendell wrestle with the idea, his face doing that thing it did — the slow tectonic shift from *no* to *maybe* to *I'm going to lose this argument and pretend I won*. She'd catalogued his face too. Fair's fair.

"My grandmother tried to warn me off you," she said, mostly to fill the silence while he thought. "First week. She said, 'that pest man looks at you like you're a problem he intends to solve.' I told her that was romantic. She threw a spoon at me."

"He's not wrong, historically," Wendell admitted. "I did intend to solve you. Briefly. Week one."

"How'd that work out?"

"I have a file cabinet full of evidence that a sheriff burned, a ledger on my lap, and a woman I can't solve sitting on a floor that scratches back. So. Poorly."

Despite everything — despite the well rising, despite Elias's name sitting in that ledger marked *pending* in wet ink that never dried — Maren laughed. One bark of it, echoing up the stairwell, and both of them flinched, because sound traveled down here, and things below listened. But the laugh had happened, and she found she didn't regret it. If the end of the world was coming, it could wait thirty seconds while she remembered what joy sounded like in her own throat.

"Wendell." She took his hand. His fingers were ink-stained and cold and trembling very slightly, and she loved them, and she was done pretending otherwise, even internally, even as a private filing system. "Listen to me. My mother starved beside that thing so I wouldn't have to. My grandmother wrote her own name six days ago so we'd have time to run. Every Holloway for six generations has paid so the next one could walk away, and none of us ever walked. We just ran slower and slower until running became staying." She squeezed his hand. "I am not writing my name to bind it. I'm writing it because if you go down those stairs alone, I will spend the rest of my life in some clean apartment in some clean city hearing hunger-sounds in the pipes. There's no version of tomorrow where I let you do this solo. There's only versions where I'm with you, or versions where I hate myself. I've met the second version. She's unbearable."

Wendell looked at her for a long time. The penlight lay between them on the stone, throwing their shadows huge up the wall, two shapes leaning toward each other like a house and its portrait.

"When you say it that way," he said finally, hoarsely, "it sounds almost reasonable, and I resent that deeply, because I had a whole speech prepared."

"You always have a speech prepared."

"It was a good speech. There were bullet points."

"Save it. Read it to me at breakfast. If there's a breakfast." She took the pen from his pocket herself, gently, giving him every chance to stop her, and he didn't, and that was its own answer. "Together. On three."

They opened the ledger to the current page. Six names in the column, the last one her grandmother's, the ink still glossy after six days, refusing to dry, refusing to be finished. Below it, blank lines waited with the terrible patience of ruled paper.

And below the ledger, below the stone, below everything — the breathing changed. Slowed. Attended.

"One," Maren whispered.

"Wendell," Wendell said.

"What?"

"If this works — if it binds, if we buy another generation — I'm taking you to the coast. Actual ocean. You've never seen it. I checked your file cabinet."

"You went through my file cabinet?"

"There's a folder labeled 'M.H.' with a question mark. I have questions, Marchetti."

"Two," she said, laughing and crying at once, pen touching paper, his hand over hers, both their names beginning under the same stroke of ink—

—and the stone door shuddered.

Not opened. Not broken. *Shuddered*, the way a dog shudders off water, the mortar dust sifting down like flour, like ash, and from the stairwell below came a sound neither of them had ever heard in all the generations of sounds their families had learned to read.

It was knocking.

Three knocks. Polite. Almost shy. And then a voice — thin, layered, wearing every keeper's throat at once like coats — said, in a tone of genuine bewilderment:

"*No one has ever asked to stay.*"

The door began, slowly, grinding, finger-width by finger-width, to open from the inside.

Maren's hand tightened around Wendell's. The pen was still in it. The ledger lay open between them, two half-written names gleaming wet.

"Well," Wendell whispered. "I guess we're doing this face to face after all."
