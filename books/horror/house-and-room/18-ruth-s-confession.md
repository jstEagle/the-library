## 18. Ruth's Confession

Ruth Halloway had kept the ledger under her bed for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had never once slept on the side of the mattress that hung above it.

It wasn't superstition, exactly. Or rather, it was, but it was also arithmetic. The ledger was a heavy book, bound in cracked black leather gone soft as old ear at the corners, and it weighed more than a book its size had any right to weigh. She had weighed it once, out of a curiosity she immediately regretted, on the kitchen scales her mother had used for flour and sugar. Nine pounds, four ounces. A newborn's weight. Her mother had laughed when Ruth told her — a short, hard laugh with no floor beneath it — and said, *Of course it does. It's been fed.*

Ruth lay awake now in the blue hour before dawn of the second day, listening to the house breathe. It did breathe. She had known that since she was six years old, when she'd pressed her ear to the cellar door during a game of hide-and-seek and heard, instead of silence, a long tidal sound, in and out, patient as digestion. Her mother had found her there, crouched on the cold flags with her eye to the keyhole, and had not scolded her. That was the thing people got wrong about her mother, and about her grandmother, and about every Halloway back to the ones whose names were written in the first pages in ink gone brown as dried blood. They were not cruel. They were *careful*. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole of Marrow Fen.

Below her, in the guest room at the front of the house, Quentin Galloway was either sleeping or pretending to. Ruth could tell the difference through two floors of old pine; she always could. Sleepers breathed like the house did, slow and tidal. Pretenders held their breath in little rationed sips, counting toward something.

The girl was counting. Good. Counting would keep her alive another night, if she counted correctly, if she never counted the ninth, if she did everything Ruth told her and nothing Ruth couldn't stop her from doing.

Ruth turned her face to the wall and made herself review the facts, the way she did every morning, the way her mother had done, the way it had apparently always been done in this bed in this room since 1911, a litany passed down like a recipe:

The bell was sold. The bell was not gone. The fen below the vicarage had swallowed the bell pit whole and grown a hunger around the shape of what it lost, the way a mouth grows around a missing tooth, tongue worrying the gap forever. When the bell rang — and it rang, though there was no bell, and that was the first fact you had to make your peace with or go mad — someone had to hold the vigil. Someone had to keep the door. And someone had to be given.

That was the word her great-grandmother had used, according to the earliest entries, in handwriting so fine and upright it looked engraved: *given.* Not taken. Not sacrificed. Given. As if the distinction mattered to the thing below. As if it mattered to anyone but the givers.

Ruth had fed it eleven names.

She lay still and let them come, because she made herself let them come, every one, in order, the way you are supposed to let yourself feel grief in full measure or it curdles into something worse.

Walter Petch, 1979, a surveyor who came to map the drainage ditches and stayed three nights too many. He had a wife in Norwich. Ruth's mother had put his name in the book on the third night, and by morning Walter Petch was walking out along the fen road with his coat over his arm, whistling, and he was never seen again, and the water in the dykes stood unusually still for a week, like something listening.

Marion Tebbit, 1984, who had come to photograph the roofless church. Marion had been kind to Ruth, had shown her how the light meter worked, had bought her an orange squash at the Bell Inn and called her *petal.* Ruth had been fourteen. Her mother had written the name while Marion slept, and Ruth had known, and had said nothing, and had hated herself in the specific way that a person hates themselves when they have discovered that love and survival can be paid for out of the same purse.

And so on. Eleven names in all, each one an outsider, each one chosen with a care that Ruth had come to understand, in her fifties, was the true horror of the arrangement — not the feeding itself but the *selecting*, the years of quiet attention a Halloway paid to strangers passing through, weighing them, asking after their families, noting whether anyone would come looking. You learned to smile at travelers and think like an undertaker. You learned to ask *where are you headed next* in a voice of warm interest and mean *how far from you is anyone who loves you.*

Her mother had chosen well. Her grandmother had chosen well. Ruth liked to believe she had chosen well too, though there were nights — most nights — when the word *well* tasted like pond water.

And then there was Quentin Galloway.

Ruth had not chosen her. That was the terrible joke of it, the thing she would have to say aloud today, soon, before the girl found the ledger on her own, because she was a broadcaster's granddaughter and a broadcaster herself and she would find it, Ruth had watched her eyes go to the floorboards, to the seams in things, all yesterday. Ruth had not chosen Quentin Galloway. The book had.

It had happened in 1998, the year of Ruth's mother's last illness, when the keeping of the ledger was passing to Ruth the way a hot stone passes hand to hand. She had opened it to enter the year's accounting — there was always an accounting, even in quiet years, even when no bell rang, a line to say the vigil was kept, the door held, nothing given — and she had found a name already there, in her mother's handwriting, dated nineteen years earlier.

*Quentin Mary Galloway. Chosen. Held.*

Ruth had sat on the edge of this very bed with the book open on her knees for the better part of an hour. Then she had gone downstairs, where her mother sat wrapped in a blanket by the dead fire even though it was June, and asked her what it meant, and her mother had looked at her with an expression Ruth had never seen on her before and never saw again — an expression like a woman caught mid-prayer by someone she'd hoped wouldn't notice her praying.

"It means we don't get to choose everything," her mother said. "Sometimes it chooses ahead. Sometimes it gets a taste of a family and wants the whole line." She had pulled the blanket tighter. "Agnes gave it a Galloway's bargain, petal. It's been owed a Galloway ever since. I wrote the name down the day I read about the girl in the paper — the radio girl, Agnes's great-granddaughter, up in Norwich with her late-night show. I wrote it down and the book took it. You'll know what that means when your turn comes."

Ruth had known already. The book taking a name meant the name ripened. Meant that somewhere down the years, the circumstances would arrange themselves — a death here, a curiosity there, a road that bent toward Marrow Fen — until the named one walked through the vicarage door of her own free will, believing it was her own idea. Free will was the cruelest instrument in the kit. Ruth had watched it work now for three days: Quentin arriving with her notebook and her sensible questions, telling herself she was investigating, researching Edith Marsh, chasing a story. Believing the door had opened outward, onto a mystery, when of course it had only ever opened inward.

And the show. God, the show. Ruth had listened to it sometimes over the years, late at night, the way half the county did — the lonely, the sleepless, the grieving, all calling in to Quentin Galloway's warm low voice at 2 a.m. Ruth had listened with the particular attention of her trade, and years ago she had understood what her mother must have understood in 1979, reading that small newspaper item: the girl had built, without knowing it, a standing open line between the living and whatever else was out there in the dark, listening. Ten years of open lines. Ten years of the thing below the vicarage turning its attention, night after night, toward that frequency like a plant toward a window. No wonder the book had wanted her. No wonder the bell had started ringing again after forty quiet years, the summer Quentin's show went county-wide. Ruth had done the arithmetic a hundred times and it always came out the same. They hadn't summoned her. They had tuned her, decade by decade, and she had tuned herself, gladly, generously, giving her voice away every night to whoever needed it — and every gift, Ruth knew, every single one, was a debt recorded somewhere below the floorboards.

* * *

Ruth rose before the girl did. This was ritual too: the kettle, the salt pinch stirred into the caddy the night before, the doors checked in their order — back door before front, front before cellar, cellar last and locked twice, because the cellar was the closest room to the throat of the house and you did not leave the throat unattended. She moved through the dim corridors doing these small offices and felt, as she always felt, the house attend to her. Not warmly. Attentively. The way an old dog watches a hand that has fed it for years, wondering, always, whether today the hand holds food or a knife.

In the kitchen she set out two cups, then stood with her hands flat on the table and made herself rehearse the confession.

She had rehearsed it, in fragments, for three days. Ever since the first toll, ever since she'd watched Quentin stand in the churchyard at dawn among the gathered villagers, mud to her knees, her face wearing the expression Ruth had seen on eleven other faces right before the end — the expression of a person realizing the world has a second set of books. But rehearsing and saying were different animals, and Ruth was seventy-one years old and had spent her whole life being the one who explained the rules to newcomers, never the one who explained the *reasons*, because the reasons were shameful, and shame, her mother had taught her, was a luxury the vigilant could not afford.

But the girl deserved it. That was the other arithmetic, the one Ruth had added late, against her mother's teaching: the arithmetic of what you owe the people you've spent years preparing to spend. You couldn't call it kindness. Kindness would have been leaving the ledger shut and the girl ignorant and the machine running smooth. But there was a thing sterner than kindness and truer, and it demanded the truth be spoken by the person who had done the wrong, out loud, in daylight, to the wronged one's face.

Besides — and here Ruth allowed herself the smallest, bitterest honesty — besides, the plan required it. The book had chosen Quentin, but the book did not choose *everything*, and Ruth had learned in thirty-one years of keeping that there were narrow channels of choice left open even inside a trap, and one of them was this: the named one, warned, armed with the whole ugly truth, was harder to take. Fear made them run and running fed the road-loops. Ignorance made them wander and wandering fed the tower. But a person who knew exactly what she was and what she was wanted for — that person could stand still. Could hold. Could, if she was the right sort of person, do what no Halloway had managed in a hundred and thirteen years, which was to change the terms.

Ruth didn't know if Quentin was the right sort. She only knew the alternatives, having buried them, and they were all worse.

Footsteps overhead. The girl was up. Of course she was up; nobody truly slept in this house on a tolling night.

* * *

Quentin came down dressed, hair tied back, carrying her recorder the way some people carried a crucifix. She stopped in the kitchen doorway and looked at the two cups and then at Ruth, and Ruth saw her arrive at the understanding that this was not breakfast, this was a hearing.

"You've decided to tell me something," Quentin said. Not a question. Three days in the house had taught her that much.

"Sit down, love."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down anyway. What I have to say is easier sitting. For me, I mean. I'm the one saying it."

Quentin sat. Ruth poured the tea — salt and all; the girl had learned to drink it without grimacing, which Ruth noted with something that in a better life might have been pride — and then she went upstairs and fetched the ledger, because she had decided in the night that she would not tell it secondhand. The book would tell it with her. Let the girl see its weight. Let her feel what nine pounds, four ounces meant.

She laid it on the kitchen table between the teacups, and Quentin looked at it the way you look at a snake that hasn't moved yet.

"What is it?"

"The parish register says our bell was melted down in 1911," Ruth said. "Sold for scrap to pay off the restoration debt. That's the story in every record, and it's even true, mostly. What the records don't say is why the village was so eager. Why they sold their bell — their *voice*, Quentin, a village's bell is its voice — for less than it was worth, in a hurry, in winter." She put her hand flat on the cover. The leather was warm. It was always warm; she had stopped finding that remarkable decades ago. "Because my great-great-aunt Agnes made a bargain. And the bargain needed a bell-shaped hole to live in."

She told it all. She told it steadily, in the voice she used for explaining rules to newcomers, and she watched Quentin's face as the story landed piece by piece: Agnes Galloway, the matriarch, the dying child, the thing in the bell pit that had whispered up through the earth that it could give breath back for a price; the melting of the bell as payment-in-kind, silencing the village's voice so the thing's voice would be the only one; the contract, unwritten anywhere but in the fen's memory, that a Galloway would keep the vigil whenever the bell rang again — and it would ring again, oh yes, because a hunger doesn't accept annulment, it accepts installments.

"And when there were no Galloways left willing," Ruth said, "or none left near enough, my family filled the gap. We kept the vigil. We held the door. And we—" Here she stopped, because here was the pit of it, and she made herself look directly at Quentin as she said it, because the girl deserved to be lied to by no one ever again, least of all with averted eyes. "We gave it names. Outsiders. Travelers, photographers, surveyors. People passing through with no one waiting for them. My mother chose them. Her mother chose them. I chose them. Eleven names in this book, and every one of them walked out along the fen road and didn't come back, and the village lived, and the village never asked where they went, because deep down the village knew, and deep down the village was grateful, and gratitude like that rots a place from the inside. That's what Marrow Fen is, Quentin. Not cursed. Fed. Fed so long it forgot it was eating."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere below the floor, faint as a pulse taken through a wrist, the house breathed.

Quentin's hands were around her cup, knuckles white, but her voice, when it came, was level — broadcaster's level, Ruth recognized it, the professional calm that is really just fear wearing its good coat. "Edith Marsh. Tom Verrity. Were those names yours?"

"No." And Ruth felt the truth of it land in her own chest like cold water. "No, love, and that's what I've been lying awake trying to understand. Those aren't feedings. Those are *callers*. The dead phoning your show — that's not the old pattern. The old pattern is quiet. One name, one night, one empty road. This is loud. This is the bell inviting a whole congregation home. Something's changed down there. The account's come due, or close to it, and it's stopped picking off strays and started setting a table." She turned the ledger around and opened it to the page, and showed her. Showed her the entry in her mother's fine upright hand, dated 1979, twenty years before Ruth had found it.

*Quentin Mary Galloway. Chosen. Held.*

Quentin read it twice. Ruth watched her read it twice. Watched the girl's lips move slightly on the second pass, the way people's lips move when they're checking a sum.

"Nineteen seventy-nine," Quentin said. "I was four years old."

"Aye."

"My mother cut my hair short that summer. We moved twice that year. I always thought—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "You're telling me this book chose me when I was four. That everything since — the show, the callers, coming here — that I've been walking down a corridor someone marked out before I could read."

"I'm telling you the corridor exists," Ruth said. "I'm not telling you you've been pushed along it. That's the one thing I've learned in thirty-one years of keeping this bloody book, and it cost me eleven names to learn it. The thing below can bend roads and ring bells and put words in dead mouths. What it cannot do — what it has *never once* managed — is make a person choose. It waits at the end of every corridor hoping you'll walk. It needs your feet, your voice, your yes. Agnes gave it a yes in 1911 and it's been living off the interest ever since." She closed the ledger, gently, the way you close a coffin lid. "So here's what I'm telling you, and then I'll tell you the rest, the part about Daniel, and the part about what the villagers are starting to whisper, and you'll wish I hadn't, but you'll hear it. You were chosen. You were lured. Every comfort I've shown you these three days was real AND it was bait, both at once, and I won't insult you by sorting which was which, because I don't fully know myself anymore. But the choosing isn't finished. Nothing is finished until you say yes. And I am asking you — I, Ruth Halloway, who wrote eleven names into this book and salted my tea and smiled at strangers like an undertaker — I am asking you to never, never say yes."

Quentin was silent a long moment. Outside, the fen light was coming up gray and thin, and the church tower stood against it, roofless, empty, listening.

"Why tell me now?" the girl said finally. "What changed?"

Ruth looked at the window. Down the lane, at the gate, Daniel was standing in the early mist, watching the house. He had been standing there a while; she'd seen his shape from the stairs. He wasn't looking at the tower. He was looking at Quentin's window.

"Because my son has stopped believing in holding the door," Ruth said, "and started believing in ending the war. And last night I heard him on the phone to the Hollis brothers, talking about rope."
