## 19. Run or Ring

The decision came to her somewhere between the third cup of tea and the second night's falling dark, and it arrived with the terrible clarity of a thing she should have known all along: she was not going to survive this by staying still.

Ruth had gone to bed — or to whatever Halloways did instead of sleeping, some ancestral crouch behind the ledger — and Daniel had vanished into the lanes with a torch and a face like a man rehearsing an argument. The vicarage sat around Quentin in its salt-and-shadow quiet, doors closed in their proper order, and she stood in the hall with her car keys biting into her palm and understood that the house was not holding her. It was simply waiting to see what she was made of.

She packed nothing. Packing would have been an admission that she believed she'd get where she was going, and some animal part of her mind had already begun rationing belief like wartime sugar. She took her coat, her keys, her phone at eleven percent, and she let herself out through the kitchen door because it was the one closest to the yard, and because closing the front door behind her felt too much like being swallowed politely.

The night air of Marrow Fen smelled of cold water and cut peat and something faintly sweet underneath, like flowers rotting in a vase nobody had emptied. Her breath hung in front of her. Overhead the sky was the colour of wet slate, starless, low enough to touch if you were tall enough and foolish enough.

Her car started on the first try. That frightened her more than if it hadn't.

* * *

She drove east first, toward the coast road, because east meant away from the fen and the tower and the drowned choir she had not yet seen but already dreamed about twice. The lane unspooled ahead of her headlights, hedges pressing close, and for four minutes — she checked, four minutes exactly — everything was ordinary. A pheasant exploded from the verge. A sign said NARROW BRIDGE, and there was a narrow bridge, and she crossed it, and the tyres sang on wet stone.

Then the bridge came again.

Same stone, same singing, same rusted iron rail with a strip of orange plastic bag snagged on it, fluttering. She had passed it before; now she passed it again, and the plastic bag turned in the windless air as though waving.

Quentin pulled over. Her hands were steady on the wheel, which struck her as a betrayal by her own body — her heart hammering while her hands stayed calm, as if they'd been briefed separately.

"Right," she said aloud, to the car, to herself. "Right."

She turned around and drove west instead, back through the village, past the roofless church where the empty tower stood against the sky like a black tooth in a blacker gum. She didn't look at it. She looked at it anyway, the way you look at a wound. Then on past the last house, up the hill road toward the moor, which led, eventually, according to every map she'd ever consulted, to the A-road, to cities, to anywhere that was not here.

The hill road climbed. The engine laboured. The fen fell away below her, a vast darkness that might have been water or land or something that had never bothered learning the difference.

And then the road crested, and began to descend, and descended too long, and curved, and there was the churchyard wall sliding past her window, lichened and crooked, and the lychgate with its leaning posts, and the yew tree that had been old when the bell was young.

She stopped the car in the middle of the lane and sat with both hands on the wheel and let the engine idle and did not scream, though the scream was right there, fully formed, sitting behind her teeth like a coin someone had slipped her.

Three directions. Three roads. One destination.

She thought, absurdly, of her show. Of callers who rang in at two in the morning convinced the world had narrowed to the size of their bedrooms, that there was no way out of the small hours, that dawn was a rumour spread by people who slept well. *I know,* she'd told them, night after night, warm-voiced, professional. *I know it feels endless. But it isn't. Hold on till sunrise.*

She wondered, now, whether any of them had ever driven this road.

* * *

She tried the south lane next, the one that ran between hedgerows so high they met overhead and made a green-black tunnel even in daylight. In daylight it was pretty. Now it was a throat.

Halfway down it, the radio came on.

Not switched — *came*, the way a voice comes to you across a room, the way a memory comes. The dial glowed amber without her touching it. Static first, thick and granular, the sound of a sea made of white noise. Then, beneath it, a hiss-and-click she knew better than her own heartbeat: the sound of a reel-to-reel machine starting up.

Her grandmother's voice filled the car.

"—fourteenth of March, 1974, continuing the Marrow Fen recordings. Present: myself, Eileen Galloway, and Mrs. Ada Verrity of Church Lane, who has asked me to note that she is speaking freely and under no—"

The tape cut off. Not faded — cut, mid-word, the way it always cut, the way every recording in the attic box cut, at the same instant, with that sound underneath like a struck throat. Quentin had listened to that truncation forty times. It had become almost familiar, a full stop she knew by shape.

But this time the tape kept going.

"—under no duress," her grandmother finished, and Quentin's blood went cold and stayed there. "Ada, perhaps you could tell me again what you saw on the anniversary."

"I saw the bell," said an old woman's voice, thin and papery and patient. "Or I heard it, which is worse. Seeing can be doubted. Hearing gets inside you. It rang at midnight and my husband got up and dressed and walked out and I never saw him again, and the vicar told everyone he'd gone to his brother's in Norwich, and I let them say it, because what was the alternative? That I'd let him answer?"

"Did you want to answer, Ada?"

A long pause on the tape. Long enough that Quentin found herself leaning toward the dashboard, toward the little amber eye of the dial, as if closeness could help her understand.

"Every year," Ada Verrity said. "Every year since. You'll think me wicked."

"No," said Eileen Galloway, and her voice was gentle in a way Quentin had never heard it, gentle in a way that made her realize how much of her grandmother's tenderness had been spent here, in this village, on these tapes, and how little had been left over for the granddaughter. "I don't think you wicked. I think you called."

"And what are you, then?" Ada asked, and there was something new in the old woman's voice, something that made the hairs on Quentin's arms rise beneath her coat sleeves. "You've come asking after the bell, Miss Galloway. Asking after the vigil. Nobody comes asking unless they've been asked. What did it say to you?"

Another pause. Longer. And then her grandmother said, in a voice stripped of all its warmth:

"It said, *we are missing one.*"

The tape ran on into silence — real silence, tape-hiss silence, the machine turning and turning — and then, very softly, underneath the hiss, a sound that was not hiss at all. A slow, wet, rhythmic sound. Like breathing. Like something enormous drawing breath through fathoms of peat and water, patient as geology.

The radio clicked off.

Quentin sat in the hedge-tunnel with the engine idling and her grandmother's last recorded sentence ringing in the architecture of her skull. *We are missing one.*

She put the car in gear and drove on south, because stopping had become unbearable and movement at least had the dignity of effort.

* * *

The tunnel of hedges ended. The lane opened onto the crossroads by the war memorial, and she turned left, and drove, and drove, and the memorial slid past her window again, facing her, its bronze soldier looking not at the horizon but slightly downward, slightly inward, at the road itself, as though he'd been cast watching for exactly this.

She tried north. The north lane ran along the top of the fen drain, straight as a ruled line, and she drove it at sixty with the windows shut and the heater on and her jaw clenched, and at the end of the straight the road bent gently, reasonably, inevitably, and delivered her past the lychgate, past the yew, past the leaning wall, into the churchyard lane.

On the fourth circuit she stopped counting circuits. On the fifth she noticed that the mud on the churchyard road was printed with footprints — bare footprints, dozens of them, all pointing toward the tower door, none pointing away. On the sixth she realized the sky had not changed at all since she'd left the vicarage. No drift in the cloud. No shift in the dark. The night had stopped spending itself. Time in Marrow Fen had begun to pool, like water finding a hollow.

She parked outside the lychgate and gripped the wheel and spoke to the car, to the night, to whatever was listening — and something was listening; she could feel it the way you feel a stare on the back of your neck, vast and unhurried and located somewhere below.

"You want me here," she said. "Fine. I'm here. But I choose it. Do you hear me? Whatever happens next, I choose it. You don't get to drive me."

The fen said nothing, in a way that felt like agreement, which was worse than refusal.

* * *

It was when she turned the key to drive the seventh pointless circuit that the radio came back — and this time it wasn't 1974.

"This is Quentin Galloway, it's ten past three, and you're listening to Night Lines." Her own voice, warm and rounded, broadcast-perfect, filling the car. "We've got open lines all night, so if you're out there in the dark, ring in. Everyone who calls deserves to finish their sentence."

She stared at the dial. This was her show. This was her voice, from years ago, from some archive night she barely remembered — except she remembered it now with horrible precision, because she remembered ending that show, remembered signing off at four, and the voice on the tape sailed smoothly past four o'clock and kept going.

"—next caller, you're on Night Lines. Go ahead."

Static. Then a voice she knew. Edith's voice, the drowned woman's voice, polite and dripping and terribly reasonable.

"Hello, Quentin. I'm nearly done. I just need a minute more. Is that all right? I only ever needed a minute more."

"You've got all the time you need," said Quentin-on-tape, warmly. "Take your time."

And Edith began to speak — and Quentin, live and present and trapped in a parked car at the edge of a hungry fen, leaned forward and turned the volume *up*, because that was the horror of it, that was the whole trap of her entire life distilled into one gesture: she wanted to hear. Some unfinished sentence was being spoken and every instinct she owned, every year of open lines and held silences and *go ahead, I'm listening*, demanded she listen to the end.

The tape-Quentin said: "Go on, Edith. Finish."

And Edith said: "*Marrow Fen, we are missing—*"

The radio died. All of it — the dial light, the clock, the whole dashboard going dark as though the car itself had been unplugged from the world.

In the sudden silence, Quentin became aware of the bell.

Not ringing. Not yet. But present — a pressure in the air above the village, a held note waiting for permission, the way an orchestra holds before the downbeat. She could feel it in her fillings, in the fluid of her inner ear, in the soft floor of her mouth.

She got out of the car. There seemed no reason to stay in it; the car had stopped being a vehicle and become merely a room, and rooms, she was learning, belonged to the house, and the house belonged to the fen, and the fen was missing one.

The churchyard gate stood open. It had been closed. She was almost sure it had been closed.

Barefoot prints in the mud led toward the tower — hers, she realized with a lurch, hers, from last night, from the walking she didn't remember — and beside them now, fresh, deep, deliberate, a second set. Larger. Older-looking, somehow, though the mud was the same mud. They came from the direction of the lychgate and went to the tower door and stopped there, side by side, toes pointed at the threshold, as if someone had stood a long time deciding.

At the top of the empty tower, against the starless sky, something moved. A rope, swinging gently in no wind at all. Back and forth. Back and forth. Patient as a pendulum. Patient as an invitation.

Quentin stood at the gate with the fen breathing behind her and her grandmother's voice in her ears — *we are missing one* — and understood, finally, with the clarity that only arrives when every other option has been driven off the map:

There was no run. There was only ring, or refuse.

And the rope was waiting to know which.
