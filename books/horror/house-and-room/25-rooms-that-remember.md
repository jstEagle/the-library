## 25. Rooms That Remember

The corridor should not have been there.

Quentin knew the vicarage the way you come to know a body in three nights of fever — every creak, every cold joint, the particular way the landing light bled under doors that had to be closed in order. She knew that the east passage ended at Ruth's linen cupboard and that beyond it was nothing but brick and ivy and the long drop into the bell pit. So when she opened her door at four in the afternoon, into a darkness that had arrived hours early and wrong, and found a corridor stretching where no corridor had been — longer than the house, wider than the house, lit by a soft amber glow that came from nowhere and everywhere — she did what she had learned to do.

She counted.

One breath. Two. The corridor did not blink out. The gaslight — and it was gaslight, she could see now, little glass-fitted flames behind soot-browned mantles, each one breathing with its own small pulse — did not gutter or apologize. It simply waited for her, the way the dead callers waited on the line, patient as held breath.

"Ruth," she said aloud, because saying names was a rule, and rules were armor. "Daniel. Anyone."

The house answered with a sound from below, deep and muscular, like a stomach turning over after a long fast. Somewhere beneath the floorboards, the fen shifted its weight.

* * *

She went left, because the right-hand passage sloped downward and everything in her that still worked told her never to go down. Left took her along a wall hung with paper — not wallpaper exactly, but something older, a pattern of willow branches printed in faded sepia, each tree weeping its leaves toward the floor. Her fingertips came away dusted grey. Behind the paper, faintly, she felt movement. Not rats. Rats moved in starts and stops. This was slow, continuous, deliberate — the motion of something swimming just beneath the skin of the house.

The first door she passed stood ajar, and through the gap came lamplight and the smell of beeswax and camphor and, underneath it all, fen water.

She should have kept walking. She knew that too. Rule three: never cross thresholds uninvited. But the door was already open, which meant the room had already invited someone, and Quentin Galloway had spent ten years answering phones at two in the morning on the theory that an open line is an obligation. She looked.

A woman sat at a dressing table with her back to the door, dressed in black bombazine gone greenish with age, her hair pinned up in a style Quentin had only ever seen in photographs — her grandmother's photographs, the stiff ones, the ones where nobody smiled because smiles cost money. On the table before her lay a ledger, open, and a pen, and a small dish of salt, and a candle burned down to a thumb's width.

"Close the door kindly," the woman said, without turning. Her voice was dry paper, and it was also — and this landed in Quentin's chest like a stone dropped down a well — familiar. It was her grandmother's voice. It was her own voice, aged sixty years past its time. "Or leave it open forever. Those are your choices tonight, I'm afraid. They were always the choices. Nobody tells you until you're sitting in the chair."

"I'm looking for Ruth Halloway," Quentin said.

"Mm." The woman dipped the pen. Wrote. The scratch of it was the loudest thing in the world. "You'll be one of mine, then. You have the look. We keep the look, my lot. Long chins and listening faces." She turned then, and Quentin's whole body went cold and then hot and then cold again, because the face was Agnes Galloway's face — she'd seen it once, a daguerreotype in her grandmother's attic, propped behind the reel-to-reel machine like a saint's icon — but the eyes were wet and living and terribly tired, and they fixed on Quentin with recognition rather than surprise.

"You're not real," Quentin said. "This is 1911. You've been dead a hundred years."

"Yes," Agnes agreed pleasantly, and returned to her ledger. "And yet here we both are, keeping vigil. That's rather the point of the house, dear. It doesn't remember the past. It *is* the past. The rooms go round and round like the bells, and some nights — bad nights, early nights, nights when the dark comes at four o'clock — the rounds catch up with themselves, and anyone standing in the corridor gets caught in the turn." She gestured with the pen at the window, where black pressed against the glass like a face. "It came at four today. Did you notice? Four o'clock, and full night. It's hungry. It hasn't been fed properly since 1974, and before that since 1911, and it is *tired*, and tired things stop being polite."

"What happens if it isn't fed?"

Agnes smiled, and it was the worst thing Quentin had seen in three nights, because it was kind. "Then it feeds itself. Close the door kindly, dear. Or don't. But choose. Standing in doorways is how they get your shape."

Quentin closed the door. Kindly. Slowly, with both hands, the way you'd close a coffin on someone you loved. Through the wood she heard the pen resume its scratching, and then, faintly, a woman beginning to weep, and then the sound of the sea, though there was no sea within forty miles of Marrow Fen.

* * *

The corridor had changed while the door was shut.

That was the thing she would try, later, to explain to herself and fail: the house didn't move like a haunted place in films, no slamming and rattling. It moved like a dream of a house, like the house in your childhood that was somehow bigger inside than out, except this was inverted — the vicarage was shrinking inward, folding its modern rooms away like letters, and unfolding older ones in their place. The electric sockets were gone from the skirting boards. In their place, brass gas taps. The carpet under her feet thinned to bare boards, then thickened to a runner worn pale down its center by a century of feet going back and forth, back and forth, between the guest rooms and the stairs and the tower door.

Back and forth. Vigil feet.

She passed a window and stopped, because the view was wrong twice over. First, outside there was no fen and no village — only the churchyard, closer than it should be, the yew trees pressing against the glass like curious cattle. Second, in the churchyard, people were walking. Mourning clothes. Black armbands. A whole village moving slowly among the graves in the blue-grey light of a 1911 afternoon, and every single one of them, as they walked, turned their heads — not quickly, not horribly, but with the weary inevitability of sunflowers finding the sun — to look at the vicarage. At her window. At her.

Among them, small and straight-backed, a girl of perhaps ten carried a wreath of white flowers too heavy for her arms.

"That's the funeral," said a voice beside her, and Quentin did not jump, because some part of her had known the boy was there, the way you know weather coming. Daniel stood in the corridor in his ordinary clothes, but the gaslight made him look sepia too, drained him toward the color of old photograph. His eyes were red-rimmed. He had been crying recently and recently again. "The bell's last ringing. They melted it the week after. My great-great-whatever sold it. Everyone walked past here on the way to the pit to watch them break it up." He swallowed. "They all look at the house because the house is why. Because someone in the house traded the bell away, and everyone knew, and nobody said."

"Daniel." Quentin reached for him and he stepped back, not unkindly, the way you step back from a flame you're fond of. "Your mother said the Halloways chose me. Years ago. Is that true?"

He looked at his shoes. "Mum keeps the ledger. Somebody has to. If nobody writes the names down, the thing below doesn't know who's promised, and then it takes whoever it likes, and it likes children, it always liked—" He stopped himself. Pressed his knuckles to his mouth. When he spoke again his voice was flat and careful, a man walking on ice he already knew was cracking. "I shouldn't be talking to you. Mum says the corridors listen on nights like this. Everything you say in here, it keeps. It plays it back later, in voices you love."

As if summoned — as if *invited* — the radio in Quentin's room, three doors and fifty years away, clicked softly on.

* * *

She heard Tom Verrity's voice through the walls, and it was worse than it had been on the air, because here it wasn't asking forgiveness anymore. Here it was reciting. Names. Dates. *Galloway, Agnes, keeper, failed. Galloway, Margaret, keeper, failed. Galloway, Edith—*

"My grandmother's name wasn't Edith," Quentin whispered.

"It is tonight," Daniel said miserably. "It's whatever name fits the room you're standing in. Go forward, Quentin. You can't stay in the turn. The turn eats the ones who stand still — that's what happened to the 1911 one, that's what happened to Gran in '74. They stopped walking. The house gave them a chair and a ledger and a candle and they sat down in it, and sitting down is agreeing, and agreeing is—" He made a sound that might have been a laugh. "Go forward. Find her. Find the bargain. It's the only room in this house that tells the truth, because she paid for the truth with everything."

"The bargain. Agnes's bargain."

"You know some of it already. Your gran's tapes cut off before the middle of it. Everyone's tapes cut off before the middle of it." He touched her elbow, briefly, a ghost of a push. "Forward. And Quentin — when you find her, and she asks you to understand her — don't. Understanding is how it gets in. Pity is the open window. Just *listen*. Listening is safe. It's the only thing you do that's safe."

He turned and walked away down the corridor, and as he walked the gaslight behind him dimmed lamp by lamp, and by the time he reached the corner he was wearing mourning clothes, and by the time he rounded it he was someone else entirely, some Halloway of 1911 with Daniel's walk and Daniel's grief, and Quentin understood that the house was showing her its filing system. Every generation shelved in its proper corridor. Every failure catalogued.

She walked forward.

* * *

The rooms came at her like stations of a story she almost knew.

A nursery, blue-painted, a cot with its rail down, and the smell of fever — camphor and hot wool and something sweeter underneath, the smell of a child burning up. On the cot's coverlet lay a christening gown folded with terrible neatness, the folds sharp as origami, prepared for a body too soon. She did not go in. From the doorway she heard breathing that was not quite breathing, a small wet rhythm, and she counted her own breaths against it until it stopped, and when it stopped the room exhaled and was only a room, and the wallpaper was willow trees again, weeping.

A kitchen, copper pans, a table scrubbed white, and salt — salt in lines across every sill, every lintel, poured fresh, the crystals still glittering, and a woman in an apron standing guard over them with a carving knife held wrong, held like a torch, like faith. The woman saw Quentin and nodded once, soldier to soldier, and said nothing, and Quentin understood that this was courtesy, this was the old politeness, and she nodded back and walked on, and behind her she heard the woman begin to hum — a tune with no fourth note in it, a tune that circled and circled and never resolved, and she realized with a lurch that she knew the tune. It was the one the drowned choir sang at the fen's edge. It had been a lullaby first. Everything here had been something gentler first.

A study. A desk. A contract.

She stopped. The study was the vicarage's own study, she was nearly sure — same bay window, same view of the tower — but the desk was oak and dark with polish, and on it lay papers weighted with a bell-shaped piece of scrap metal, blackened, slagged, a fragment of the melted bell itself used as a paperweight, and the cruelty of that was so precise it could only be intentional. The topmost page was written in a hand she recognized from the parish records: the buyer's agent, the scrap merchant's man, the one whose signature had turned a village's voice into coin.

But beneath the contract, half-hidden, was another page. Smaller. Private paper, not legal. Written in a woman's hand, hurried, the ink blotted where tears or rain had fallen on it.

*For the breath of my child I give the voice of the bell,* it read. *Let the fen take the ringing and spare the breathing. Let it be fed what rings and not what lives. I invite it. I invite it. God forgive me, I invite it.*

Below the words, a signature: *Agnes Galloway.*

And below the signature, in different ink, added later, in a hand gone shaky and old:

*It has been eighty years and it is still hungry. I thought I was selling the bell. I was teaching it to expect us. Every Galloway after me must know: I did this. Not the devil. Not the fen. Me, at this desk, in this room, loving my daughter more than my neighbors' children. If you are reading this, you are reading it in the house I built the hunger into. Do not pity me. Pity is the open window.*

Quentin stood in the doorway a long time. She did not go in. She listened — to the paper, to the silence around it, to the house holding its breath around her like a fist around a moth — and then she said, quietly, to the room, to the woman who had written it and the woman who had been the dying child and the hundred years of keepers stacked behind them like plates:

"I hear you."

The gaslight in the study steadied. Somewhere far below, the fen turned over, restless, unsatisfied — but quieter. Listening is safe. Listening was the work. Ten years of open lines at two in the morning, and she had never once understood what the show was *for* until this corridor, this hour, this house teaching her its oldest lesson: that attention is a food, and the question is only ever who is feeding whom.

* * *

At the end of the corridor — the true end, the end that had been waiting for her since four o'clock — stood a door unlike the others. No paneling. No paint. Just raw planks, silvered with age, and carved deep into them a face: eyes shut, mouth open wide, wide, impossibly wide, the yawning face from the foundation stone in the cellar, brought up into the light and given a door to be.

From behind it came a sound she knew better than any voice on any frequency she had ever broadcast. It was the sound from her grandmother's tapes — the sound that had cut off every interview mid-sentence, the struck-throat sound, the sound of a voice being taken rather than given.

Except now, for the first time, she could hear what it was underneath.

It was a bell. Melted down in 1911, broken into scrap, sold for thirty pounds and the safety of a village — and still ringing. Ringing inside the metal that no longer existed, ringing in the memory of the metal, ringing the way the dead called her show: unfinished, unfinished, unfinished, a sentence a century long and no one willing to let it finish.

The mouth in the door opened. Not the carving — the door itself, the planks parting like lips, and beyond it not a room but a corridor going down, gaslit, willow-papered, and standing at the top of the steps with her hand on the newel post, in black bombazine, looking up at her with her own long chin and listening face, was Agnes Galloway.

"You're the one sitting in the chair in 1974," Quentin said. "You're the one on the tapes."

"And you're the one on the wireless," Agnes said. "Every generation gets the instrument it deserves. Mine was a pen. My granddaughter's was a reel of tape. Yours is a transmitter with an open line and ten years of lonely people on the other end of it." She smiled her kind, terrible smile. "Come down, dear. There's something I've been trying to finish saying for a hundred and fourteen years, and the house has finally, finally let me get to the middle of it."

Behind Quentin, at the far end of the corridor, the front door of the vicarage opened onto ordinary night — the real night, the fen-smelling night — and Ruth Halloway stood silhouetted in it, holding a lantern, holding out her hand, and shouting something Quentin couldn't hear over the bell that wasn't there.

Down, or out. The middle of the sentence, or the door.

The house waited, mouth open, for her choice.
