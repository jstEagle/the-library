## 26. The Great-Grandmother's Bargain

The corridor had stopped pretending to be a corridor.

Quentin felt it first as a change in the air — the gaslight smell thickening, coal smoke and beeswax and something medicinal, laudanum perhaps, or the camphor they used to keep moths from wool. The wallpaper under her fingertips was no longer Ruth Halloway's sensible flocked paper but something older, darker, a pattern of pears and vines printed in a green that had faded toward grey. Her hand, when she lifted it to her face, was not her hand. It was thinner. The knuckles were swollen. There was a wedding ring sunk into the flesh of the fourth finger so deeply it looked grown there.

She did not scream. She understood, in the way one understands things in dreams — completely and uselessly — that she had walked backward into 1911, or 1911 had walked forward into her, and either way the house had made room.

The room she stood in was a bedroom. A child's bedroom, though no child slept in it now. A cot stood against the wall with its rail down, and the sheets were thrown back as if someone had risen in haste or been lifted out. On the washstand, a basin of water had frozen over, though it was only October. In the grate, a fire burned without warmth; Quentin put her palm toward it and felt nothing at all, as though the fire were a picture of a fire, painted by someone who had never been cold.

And in the chair beside the cot sat a woman.

She was small. That was Quentin's first thought, absurdly: *she's so small.* All her life she had imagined her great-grandmother Agnes Galloway as a monument, because monuments were what families built out of women they'd never met. Here was the woman herself, five feet of her at most, wrapped in a shawl the colour of wet slate, her hair coiled and pinned and coming loose at the temples in soft grey wings. Her hands lay folded in her lap like two sleeping birds. Her face was tired the way stone is tired — worn smooth, all the sharpness long since weathered off.

She was looking directly at Quentin. She had been, Quentin realized, since the moment she arrived.

"You're late," Agnes said. Her voice was low and unhurried, an East Anglian flatness underneath the gentleness. "Sixty-three years, give or take. I did wonder."

"I don't—" Quentin began, and heard, with a lurch, that the voice coming out of her mouth was not quite hers either. Older. Accented. "I don't know why I'm here."

"Yes, you do." Agnes unfolded one of the birds from her lap and gestured to the chair opposite, a plain wooden thing drawn up close to the cold fire. "Sit down, girl. We've a long night and I've a confession forty years old and nobody living to make it to. You'll do. You're the only one who ever comes."

Quentin sat. The chair received her weight with a creak that sounded almost like relief.

Outside the window, the fen lay black under a moon the colour of a fingernail paring. And somewhere out there — not ringing yet, but *waiting* to ring, the way a held breath waits — was the bell.

* * *

"My daughter was called Miriam," Agnes said, without preamble, the way people speak when they have rehearsed a thing too long and can no longer bear to approach it sideways. "Born in 'ninety-eight. Thirteen years old in the autumn of 1911, and dying of it. The consumption. You'd know it as something else." She glanced up. "You'd know everything as something else. That's the trouble with your generation. You've got better words for it and worse nerve."

Quentin said nothing. It seemed important not to interrupt. It seemed, more than that, dangerous — as if this room were a held note and any sound she made might break it.

"The doctors came from Norwich twice," Agnes went on. "The second time they didn't come into the room. They stood on the landing and took their hats off, which is how doctors tell you, when they're too polite to say it. Three weeks, they reckoned. Perhaps four." Her thumb moved over the back of her other hand, back and forth, a small mechanical motion, a woman worrying a rosary that wasn't there. "I had buried a husband already. I had buried a son before him, drownded in the fen at nine years old, which I'll come to, because it matters, though you'll wish it didn't. And I could not — do you understand me? — I could *not* sit in that chair again and watch another little bed empty out. I'd done the arithmetic. I knew what was left in me after. Nothing. They'd have carried me out feet first within the month, and who'd have kept the farm, and who'd have kept—"

She stopped. Her jaw worked.

"Who'd have kept *them* fed," she said.

The fire painted itself burning. Neither of them looked at it.

"There was a man came round that October," Agnes said. "Or I say a man. He wore a man's shape the way a scarecrow wears a coat — well enough at a distance, and wrong at the gate. He'd been seen before, on and off, for as long as anyone could remember. Old Mother Pettifer used to leave him milk. Said he kept the fen quiet. Said every village on this coast has one, if it knows its business, and Marrow Fen had known its business once and let the knowing slip." She looked at Quentin then, fully, and Quentin saw that her eyes were the exact grey-green of the faded wallpaper, as if she had been standing in this room so long she was becoming part of it. "He knocked at my door on the ninth of October, 1911, and I let him in. That's the whole of it, girl. Everything else is consequence."

"What did he offer?" Quentin asked. Her borrowed voice came out rough.

"He offered a trade." Agnes smiled, and it was the worst thing Quentin had yet seen in that house, worse than the drowned callers, worse than the yawning stone in the cellar — because it was tender. It was the smile of a mother. "Not for money. He had no use for money. He said: your bell rings, madam, and every soul in earshot sleeps easy, because a bell is a voice and a voice tells the dark where the edges are. Give me the voice. Melt the bell. Let the fen have its silence back — silence is food, you understand, and it's been fasting on scraps since the church was raised. In exchange, your daughter breathes till spring. And past spring. And past that, if you keep the terms."

"And the terms?"

"To hold the door whenever it knocks. To keep vigil whenever the silence gets hungry enough to remember having a voice. One night here and there. One Galloway, always — because the bargain ran in blood, not in ink. Blood remembers longer than paper." Agnes's folded hands tightened. "I said yes before he'd finished saying it. God forgive me, I said yes while his mouth was still moving. I'd have said yes to anything. I'd have said yes to the devil himself if he'd promised me one more winter of that child's cough getting better instead of worse."

* * *

The room shifted around them — not violently, but the way a sleeper turns. The cot was made up now, blankets tucked, and in it lay a girl of thirteen with dark hair plaited over one shoulder and a book open face-down on her chest. Her breathing was the laboured, whistling breathing Quentin had heard through her own childhood radio headset a thousand times, from callers phoning in at three in the morning about lungs and hearts and the fear that comes with both.

"That's her?" Quentin whispered.

"That's her. Miriam." Agnes did not look at the cot. She looked, deliberately, at the window, at the tower beyond it, roofless even then — no, not roofless then; the roof was there, the slates whole, but the tower stood empty, and would stand empty forever after. "She lived. That's the part nobody believes, when I tell it. She lived to be seventy-one. Married a Cooper boy from the next parish, had four children, scandalized the vicar, died in her sleep in a house with the sea in view. The bargain *kept*, Quentin. That's what I need you to understand, sitting there with your grandmother's chin and your great-great-grandfather's stubborn mouth. It wasn't a cheat. It wasn't a trick. It was a trade, honestly struck, and it bought sixty years of my daughter's breath. I never regretted it. Do you hear me? Forty years I've been dead and I *still* don't regret it."

"But the bell—"

"We melted it that November. My idea, if you can believe it — I pushed the parish into it, I stood up at the meeting and spoke for it, because I wanted the debt begun and paid and done with, and I thought a trade was a trade. You give the voice, you get the breath, everyone shakes hands." Her laugh was dry as seed husks. "But the fen doesn't shake hands. The fen takes what it's given and holds the rest in trust, and trust compounds, girl. Like interest. Every year the silence sat there, hungrier. Every year the hunger needed feeding a little more often, a little more formally. First it was just me, keeping my nights, sitting up in this chair. Then it wanted a Galloway on the anniversary. Then it wanted the ledger kept, the names written, the vigils scheduled like church. Then it started calling people who weren't party to anything at all — Ediths, Toms, poor souls who'd never signed a paper in their lives — because a debt that size stops caring whose pocket it digs in."

Agnes rose at last. Up close she smelled of woodsmoke and camphor and, faintly, underneath it, of fen water — the same smell that had soaked into Quentin's own hem the night she'd woken barefoot in the churchyard.

"I tried to end it," she said. "1919. Went out to the pit myself with a lantern and told it we were square, told it the bell was gone and the voice was gone and it should move along. You know what it said to me?" She turned. In the firelight her face was suddenly very old, older than death, older than the wallpaper. "It said: *you gave me a taste, Mrs. Galloway. Nobody gives a starving thing a taste and calls the account settled.*"

The words landed in Quentin's chest like stones dropped down a well. She thought of the ledger in Ruth's parlour, the columns of names in a dozen different hands across a century. She thought of Ruth's face when she'd confessed — *we chose outsiders so the village could live* — and understood, finally, that Ruth had not been the architect of anything. Ruth was a clerk. The architecture was older, and it was standing right in front of her, wearing a shawl.

"It was never going to stop," Quentin said. "No matter what anyone did."

"No." Agnes said it simply, without drama, the way you confirm the time of a train. "Bargains like this don't end. They only change hands. That's why I'm telling you, and telling you now, while the house still lets me — because tonight the account comes due in full, and you'll be asked to pay it, and I want you to walk into that asking with your eyes open. Not innocent. Innocence is what got us here. I was innocent. I loved my daughter. And I built this." She spread her small hands at the room, the house, the fen, the century. "Every toll you've counted. Every name in the ledger. Every caller on your wireless who couldn't finish their sentence. Mine. All mine. I rang the first change, and everyone after has just been keeping time."

* * *

The fire guttered — actually guttered now, Quentin felt cold air move across her knuckles, real cold, the room beginning to remember it was a memory. The girl in the cot stirred and murmured something in her sleep, and Agnes crossed to her and drew the blanket up with a motion so practiced, so worn into the body, that Quentin's throat closed. A hundred nights of that. A thousand. This woman had sat in this chair through the whole of a dying and then through the whole of a miracle, and had paid for the miracle in instalments until the day she dropped, and had come back after dropping to keep paying.

"Why tell me it can't be ended?" Quentin asked. "If it's hopeless, why warn me?"

Agnes paused at the cot, her hand resting on the rail.

"Because I lied to the ones before you," she said quietly. "Not on purpose. But I told your grandmother it was duty, and duty makes people obedient, and obedient people feed it on schedule forever. I told your mother nothing at all, which is its own kind of lie. You're the first one I'm telling the truth to: it cannot be *ended*. But it can be *answered*." She looked back over her shoulder, and her grey-green eyes caught the last of the firelight. "A debt is a hunger, girl. Hunger isn't cured by starving it and it isn't cured by feeding it forever. It's cured by finding out what it's actually hungry *for*. It's been eating names and vigils and silence for a hundred years and it's still starving. Ask yourself why. Ask yourself what a voice is, that a thing in a hole would trade a whole village's safety for one. And when it comes asking tonight — and it will come, and it will wear faces you love — don't pull the rope and don't run the road. *Listen.* For once in this family's history, listen all the way to the bottom of what's being said."

The room was thinning. The wallpaper was fading back toward flocked grey, the gaslight toward bulb-light, the cot toward shadow. Agnes was fading fastest, her outline going soft as breath on glass.

"Wait," Quentin said, rising. "Wait — what do I do? What's the answer?"

"Ask it," Agnes said, and her voice was coming from further away now, from deeper in the house, from under the floor, from the pit itself. "Ask it what it lost. Nobody's ever asked it what it *lost*."

The last thing to fade was her hand on the cot rail, and the last thing she said was not to Quentin at all. She bent over the sleeping girl and murmured, in the tone mothers use across centuries:

"*Breathe, my love. Breathe. I bought you the morning.*"

* * *

Quentin came back to herself in the upstairs corridor of the present-day vicarage, on her knees on the modern carpet, her own hands — her own chapped, ink-stained, thirty-four-year-old hands — pressed flat against the floorboards. Below her, through the joists, through the boards, through the foundation stone with its yawning carved face, she could feel the house listening. Not hungrily. Not yet.

Expectantly.

Somewhere below, deep in the cellar dark, something turned over in its sleep like a patient waiting for a name to be called.

And somewhere above her — upstairs, in the direction of Daniel Halloway's room — a floorboard creaked, once, deliberately, followed by the soft unmistakable sound of a key being tested in a lock.

Quentin stayed very still, and counted the bells that hadn't rung yet, and waited to find out which of them the night intended to spend.
