## 27. Daniel's Betrayal

Daniel Halloway had spent his whole life learning how to hold a door.

It was the first thing his mother taught him, before he could read, before he understood that the lessons had teeth in them. *When the bell rings, you hold the door, Danny. You don't open it, and you don't let it slam, and you don't leave your post no matter what comes to the other side of it asking in your father's voice.* He'd been six years old the first time he stood vigil, small hands flat against the vicarage's front door while something outside breathed like a tide going out. His mother had sat behind him with her ledger on her knees, writing names, and she had told him he was brave, and he had believed her for thirty years.

He did not feel brave now. He felt like a man holding a door shut from the inside, knowing what he was about to do to the person on this side of it.

The tower stairs were narrow and smelled of pigeon lime and cold stone, and Quentin Galloway climbed ahead of him with her torch beam jittering against the walls, still talking — she never stopped talking, that was the thing about her, the radio voice ran even when the radio was off — about the tapes, about the road that looped, about the way her car radio had played her grandmother's voice saying things no dead woman should know. Daniel climbed behind her and said nothing, because everything he might say would be either a lie or an apology, and he hadn't decided which one he could live inside.

*One clean sacrifice,* his mother had said, two nights ago, her hands steady on the ledger the way they were steady on nothing else anymore. *A hundred years of vigils, Danny. A hundred years of half-memories walking around this village like amputees. My mother forgot her wedding day. Your grandfather forgot his brother's face. And every generation we feed it a stranger's name and call it mercy.* She had looked at him then, and her eyes had been wet and hard at once, like ice over a well. *She's already chosen. She's been chosen since before she was born. All we do is stop pretending.*

He had said no. He remembered saying no. He could almost feel the shape of the word in his mouth, though it seemed very far away now, a word spoken by a man who still believed there were doors that stayed shut.

* * *

At the top of the stairs the belfry opened up into darkness, and Quentin's torch found the rope first.

It hung down through the trapdoor in the bell chamber above, thick as a wrist, honey-dark with a century of hands, and it swayed very slightly in air that wasn't moving. Daniel watched her look at it. Watched her take a step toward it and then stop herself, physically stop, one hand coming up like a woman warding off a blow.

"It's not even attached to anything," she said. "I checked yesterday. I went up there and looked. The headstock's rotted through. There's no bell. There hasn't been a bell since 1911." Her laugh came out wrong, frayed at the edges. "So what does it even *do*, if I pull it? What's the mechanism?"

"You are," Daniel said.

The words came out flatter than he meant them to, and he saw her turn, saw the torch swing and catch his face, and he knew what she saw: a big man in a waxed jacket standing between her and the only way down, blocking the stairwell with his shoulders, his hands hanging at his sides like they belonged to someone calmer.

"Daniel." Her voice changed. The radio warmth drained out of it and left something younger, something that sounded like a woman alone in a strange house at night, which was exactly what she was. "Daniel, move away from the stairs."

"I can't."

"Yes, you can. You're a grown man, you can stand anywhere you like. Move."

"The third night started early," he said. "You noticed. Four o'clock, and the dark came down like a lid. It's never done that. Not once in the records. Mum says —" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, because he'd rehearsed this, God help him, he'd rehearsed it in the kitchen mirror that wasn't there anymore, and none of the rehearsal survived contact with her actual face. "Mum says the thing below has stopped being patient. It's stopped taking what it's given and started reaching for what it wants. And what it wants is a Galloway. It's always wanted a Galloway. Agnes made sure of that when she made her bargain, and every year since, it's come back to collect, and every year we've paid it in strangers instead, and every year the interest gets worse."

"So you give it me." She said it without drama, which was worse. She said it the way you'd read out a sum. "That's the plan. That's Ruth's plan. Lock me in the tower with the rope and let me decide to be brave."

"Mum doesn't know I'm doing this."

The confession fell out of him before he could stop it, and he hated it, hated the crack it put in the thing he'd built, but it was true and the truth was all he had left that wasn't rotten. "Mum wanted to wait. Mum wanted to talk to you, explain, give you the choice, all the things we've never given anyone. But the dark came at four o'clock, Quentin. We don't have time for choices anymore. The bells are coming out of order. Two, five, seven. The house is moving its rooms around. Last night my kitchen was where the pantry used to be and the pantry window looked out on 1911. I watched a woman in mourning clothes carry a dead child across the churchyard, and she looked at me through the glass, and she *nodded*, like she knew me, like she'd been waiting for me to get on with it."

"That was Agnes," Quentin said quietly. "I met her too. In the corridor. She told me what she traded." Her torch hand was trembling; he could see the light shivering on the wall. "Your great-grandmother sold this village's soul for her daughter's breath, and a hundred years later you're standing there telling me the debt's come due and I'm the payment. Daniel, I didn't sign anything. I didn't agree to anything. Whatever your family decided —"

"I know."

"Then move."

"I know," he said again, louder, and his voice broke on it, and that was the moment he understood he was going to weep through the whole of it, that his body had already decided and was simply waiting for the rest of him to catch up. "I know you didn't. That's the whole bloody horror of it, don't you see? Nobody ever agrees. That's what a debt is. Agnes agreed, and everyone since has just *inherited*, and I have inherited right along with you, every vigil, every ledger page, every name Mum wrote down and fed to the dark so the rest of us could keep our memories another year. I've held the door since I was six. I've heard it ask for my dad in my dad's voice. I've heard it ask for *me*. And I thought that was the worst thing there was, until this week, until you turned up with your grandmother's tapes and your ten years of open lines, and I realised there was a version of this where it ends. Where it actually ends, tonight, and nobody has to stand a door again, ever, and the children born next spring grow up in a village where midnight is just midnight."

"And the price is me."

"The price is one person who was always going to be taken anyway," he said, and the cruelty of it landed in his own chest a half-second after it left his mouth, and he wished — he would wish this for the rest of his life, however long that was — he wished he could have swallowed it. "No. No, that's not — I'm sorry. I'm sorry. That's Mum's arithmetic, not mine. I don't know what the price is. I just know it's smaller than forever."

* * *

She tried to reason with him then, and it was the hardest part, harder than the crying, because she was good at it. Ten years of talking lonely people down off their worst nights had made her good at it, and she aimed all of it at him like a lamp.

She told him about Edith, about letting her finish her sentence on air, about the fen receding an inch — *an inch, Daniel, kindness moved it, actual measurable kindness* — and he nodded because it was true and because he'd felt the ground ease under his feet that morning and hated how much hope it gave him. She told him about Tom Verrity's widow standing at the vicarage gate saying *forgiven* into the dark and the whole village hearing it, feeling it go through the ground like warm water. She told him about the transmitter in her car, about broadcasting from the tower, about opening the lines to the dead and letting them finish, all of them, every unfinished sentence in a century of ledgers.

"It's working," she said. "Whatever your family's been doing with locks and salt and silence — mine works too. Better. Let me get to the transmitter. Let me try it properly, tonight, before the ninth bell. If I'm wrong, you can lock me in tomorrow."

"And if you're wrong tonight," Daniel said, "there's no tomorrow. The dark came at four o'clock. Tomorrow it'll come at noon. Then dawn. Then it won't leave at all, and there'll be no more vigils because there'll be no more village, just a mouth with a fen in it." He shook his head, slow, miserable. "I can't gamble the whole parish on a radio show. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. Hold the door, Quentin. That's all any of us have ever been able to do. Tonight I'm holding it for you."

She rushed him.

He'd known she would — she was cornered and clever and far braver than his mother had predicted — and he was bigger and he'd grown up on these stairs and he caught her wrists and turned her, gently, terribly gently, the way you'd handle something you'd already broken, and walked her back into the belfry. She fought the whole way. She was stronger than she looked, and she got a heel into his shin hard enough to make him gasp, and at some point her torch went spinning off into the dark and rolled in a circle of dying light on the floor like a lighthouse drowning.

"I'm sorry," he kept saying, into her hair, into the dark, an idiot litany, useless as prayer. "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry."

He got the door shut. It was an old door, oak, iron-banded, older than the church around it, and it had waited a long time for this particular use. The lock was a bar, not a key — his grandfather had installed it in 1962 for reasons the family had never written down — and Daniel dropped it into its brackets with a sound like a bone setting.

Then he stood there with his forehead against the wood, on the outside, listening to her.

She didn't scream. He'd braced for screaming. Instead she talked, because of course she did, because the radio voice ran even now: calm at first, reasoning, then pleading, then furious, cursing him with a fluency that would have made him laugh on any other night of his life, and then — worst of all — quiet. Quiet for a long time. And then, through four inches of oak that his family had seasoned for exactly this:

"Daniel. Your mum's ledger. Page one. Read me the first name."

He knew it. He'd known it since he was nine years old and had sneaked the book down from its shelf and read the whole terrible genealogy of payment. But he said it anyway, because she asked, and because not answering her felt like the last cruelty available to him and he refused it.

"Margaret Galloway," he said. "1911. Agnes's daughter. The one who died anyway."

"The one who died anyway," Quentin repeated, and he heard her sit down on the other side of the door, her back against his back through the wood, close as a conversation. "Agnes traded the bell for her child's breath, and the child died, and the debt didn't care. A hundred years of strangers, Daniel, and it never brought Margaret back, and it never will. You can't pay a hunger into being full. You should know that better than anyone. You've been holding the door since you were six. Has it *ever* once been satisfied?"

He didn't answer. The answer was no, and they both knew it, and the no filled the stairwell like water filling a lung.

"Let me out," she said, softer now. "Not for me. For you. Because in twenty years you'll be your mum, sitting up with a ledger full of names, and you'll remember the night you locked a woman in a tower to save a village that doesn't want saving, it wants *finishing*, and you'll hear my voice every time you switch on a radio. Let me out and I swear to you — I swear on my grandmother, on the tapes, on every dead caller I've ever cut off mid-sentence — I'll ring the ninth bell myself if it comes to that. On my terms. With my voice. But let me choose it, Daniel. That's all. That's the whole thing. Let it be a choice."

His hand was on the bar. He didn't remember putting it there. It lay on the cold iron the way it had lain on a hundred doors, and it was shaking, and somewhere below them in the dark the bell began to toll — out of sequence, three strokes, then eight, then a pause stretched taut as wire — and the sound came up through the stone and through his boots and through his teeth, and beneath it, fainter, from the fen, the drowned choir had started singing harmonies to a bell that wasn't there.

And Quentin, on the other side of the door, began to hum along.

He snatched his hand back from the bar. Because that was the trick, wasn't it — that was the oldest trick in his family's unwritten book — the voices never asked you to do the wrong thing, they asked you to do the *right* thing, kindly, reasonably, at exactly the moment the dark needed a door held open. His mother had warned him. *The night it sounds most like mercy, Danny, that's the night you bolt the bar.*

But she had also said: *she's already chosen.*

He pressed his forehead to the oak one last time. "I'm sorry," he said, and meant it past all meaning, and turned, and went down the stairs into the dark, leaving her alone above the village with the rope hanging in reach and the eighth toll fading and the ninth waiting, patient as hunger, for her hand.

Behind him, muffled through the oak, he thought he heard her start to speak — not to him, not to the dead, but in her broadcast voice, warm and level and carrying, as if somewhere in that stone room she'd found a microphone after all —

— and the tower door swung shut on the rest of the sentence.
