## 35. What Belongs to Whom

The attic of the vicarage smelled of dust and old cedar and, faintly, of salt — the salt Ruth had kept in the tea caddy for forty years, which Quentin had not yet decided whether to keep or to scatter. Morning came through the round window in a soft grey wash, the kind of light that made everything look like it was already a memory.

She had been up here three days now, since the funeral that wasn't quite a funeral — since they'd stood at the fen's edge and said Ruth Halloway's name into the wind because there was nothing left to bury but the ledger's ashes, and even those had gone into the water. Three days of climbing the ladder with a thermos of tea and coming down with her arms full of her grandmother's life, boxed and labelled in handwriting Quentin had almost forgotten: *Radio. 1974–79. Do not play after dark.* The instruction was in her grandmother's hand, and it had been followed so faithfully that the tape at the bottom of the box still held its original factory seal, unbroken for fifty years.

Quentin sat cross-legged on the floorboards with the reel-to-reel machine between her knees, its case scarred and travel-worn, and she sorted.

It was slow work, and she wanted it to be. There were thirty-one tapes in the first box alone, each one labelled with a date and a name. Edith Marsh. Tom Verrity's grandfather, as it turned out — a Verrity had been hearing voices on the wire since before there was a wire, if his entry was anything to go by. A woman called Sorrel Pike who had apparently spent an entire afternoon in 1976 explaining, in exhaustive detail, how to close a door kindly. Quentin had listened to four minutes of that one on the first morning and had to stop, because Sorrel Pike's voice was so patient and so certain that Quentin had found herself getting up to check whether the vicarage doors were all shut in their proper order, and then standing in the hallway feeling foolish, and then not feeling foolish at all.

That was the thing about the tapes. They were instructions. Her grandmother had understood that before Quentin did — had understood that the village's survival lived not in the ledger or the contract or the vigil itself, but in the small courtesies, passed mouth to mouth, generation to generation, wearing the disguise of superstition. Close the doors in order. Salt in the caddy. Count the bells, never the ninth. Answer your people.

*Answer your people.* Quentin turned that one over now, holding the tape labelled **M. GALLOWAY — PERSONAL** — the only tape in the collection marked personal rather than dated — and thought about what answering had cost, and what it had bought.

She had let them finish. That was the whole of it, in the end. Ten years of open lines at two in the morning, and she had never once known why it mattered until the night the dead started calling, and by then it was too late to do anything but what she'd always done: listen, and make room, and let the sentence end. Edith's daughter had wept into the phone while half the county listened, and the fen had receded an inch, and Quentin had learned in that moment something no contract could have taught her — that the hunger below was not fed by silence. It was starved by it. Silence was the thing's native tongue. Every unfinished sentence was a coin dropped down a well, and the well grew deeper and hungrier with each one, and her family had been dropping coins into it for a hundred years while calling it duty.

Her grandmother had known. Quentin was sure of it now. She threaded the personal tape onto the spools — daylight outside, mid-morning, the safest hour there was — and pressed play.

Static, then the click of a switch, then her grandmother's voice, younger than Quentin remembered it, rougher, the voice of a woman talking to herself because there was no one else in the room.

*"Testing. It's the fourth of November, 1979, and I'm going to say this once, on tape, where nobody has to hear it but me."*

A long pause. In the pause Quentin could hear the vicarage around her grandmother — the same house, the same groan in its bones, though back then it must have been a different groan, a younger stomach turning over.

*"Agnes traded our child's breath for a bell's voice, and I have spent my whole life paying the interest. Mother never said sorry. She said necessary. She said someone must hold the door. And I held it, God help me, I held it every year, and I raised my girl to hold it, and I never once asked her whether she wanted to."*

Another pause, longer. When the voice came back it was quieter, closer to the microphone, as if the room had leaned in.

*"But I'll tell you what I learned, holding that door. The thing under the fen doesn't want obedience. Obedience is just silence wearing its Sunday clothes. It wants what everyone wants. It wants to be answered. And the day somebody answers it honestly — not with a rope, not with a name read off a ledger, but with a voice that means it — that's the day the account closes. I won't live to see it. But whoever finds this tape: you don't inherit belonging. You say it out loud or you don't have it at all."*

The tape ran on into hiss. Quentin let it run. Outside the round window, the fen lay flat and silver under a high thin sun, merely wet, merely water, and the roofless tower stood against the sky like a cupped hand, open, empty, listening to nothing.

She stopped the tape. Rewound it. Set it, deliberately, in the pile marked KEEP.

* * *

The piles grew through the afternoon. KEEP was the smaller one, and it surprised her how quickly it stopped surprising her. The interviews with villagers about the bell — those went to KEEP, every one, because they were the village remembering itself, and a village that forgets its own rules is a village waiting to be reminded by something worse. The recordings of vigils, of tollings, of the struck-throat sound that cut her grandmother off mid-sentence in 1974 — those went to the other pile, the one she hadn't named yet, because she kept hoping a reason would arrive to save them and none ever did.

There was one tape she handled differently. **1911 RECONSTRUCTION — A.G.** — Agnes Galloway, her great-grandmother, recorded not in 1911 but decades later, dictating from memory what the melting of the bell had sounded like, what the buyer's agent had said, what the bargain had cost. Quentin had listened to it twice already, once in the tower and once here, and both times she had come away with the same cold understanding: Agnes had not been wicked. She had been a mother with a dying child and a voice in the dark offering a trade, and she had taken it, and the interest compounded for a century, and Ruth Halloway had spent her whole life enforcing the payments because someone had to and because the alternative was unthinkable, and Ruth had chosen outsiders, always outsiders, people with no roots to tear — until she chose Quentin, who had roots after all, deeper than anyone knew, and who had refused to pay in the only currency the thing accepted.

Ruth had burned the ledger instead. Quentin still woke some nights seeing her at the altar steps, feeding page after page into the flame, unhurried, thorough, the firelight making her face look young. *The house screams,* Quentin thought. *Let it. It's digesting.*

She set the 1911 tape in the second pile. Not because it was worthless — it was the truest thing in the attic — but because some truths are meant to be given back to the ground that made them. Her grandmother had buried nothing; she had boxed everything, labelled everything, kept the past in mint condition like evidence for a trial that never came. Quentin was beginning to understand that this, too, had been a form of silence. You can keep a truth so carefully that it never does any work. You can inherit a house and never once tell it what you want from it.

The sun moved. She made more tea, carried the pot up, and worked until the light went amber and long across the floorboards, and the piles were finished, and there was nothing left in the boxes but packing straw and the smell of fifty years.

Thirty-one tapes to keep. Nineteen to bury.

She counted them twice, and then a third time, and then laughed at herself — quietly, alone, the way you laugh when you catch yourself performing an old ritual without believing in it — because counting was counting, and the number didn't matter, and the ninth had never come again and never would.

* * *

They dug the hole on Saturday morning, and half the village came.

Not because anyone had organised it. Daniel put the word around — Daniel, hollowed and quieter now, moving through Marrow Fen like a man learning to walk in a house whose rooms had all shifted — and people simply arrived, with spades, with thermoses, with children who were told to stay back from the edge and immediately didn't. The bell pit lay behind the church ruins, a shallow depression in the turf where the old casting pit had been, and the ground there had always grown grass a little too green, a little too eager, and everyone knew why and no one had ever said so aloud.

They said so aloud now. That was the strange grace of the morning. Old Mr. Tredgett, who had not spoken more than ten words to Quentin in his life, stood at the pit's edge and told the story of the melting in a clear carrying voice, the way you'd tell it to children, because children were present and needed to hear it: how in 1911 the parish sold its bell to buy its safety, and how safety bought that way is a loan, not a gift, and how the interest came due every year for a hundred years until a woman with a radio refused the terms.

"It weren't refusing," said Mrs. Pike — Sorrel Pike's granddaughter, with the same patient certainty in her face. "It were answering different. My gran always said the thing just wanted finishing, same as anyone."

"Well," said Tredgett, "either way. It's done."

It was done. Quentin knelt at the edge of the hole they'd dug together — Daniel had done most of the digging, silently, methodically, as if the work were a penance he'd assigned himself and intended to complete — and she lowered the nineteen tapes in, one at a time, saying each label aloud as it went down. Not a rite. Just reading. Just letting the names exist in open air one last time before the earth took them.

"Agnes Galloway, reconstruction, dictated 1963."

"Vigil recording, November 1958."

"The struck-throat sound, 1974, cut mid-sentence."

At that one, several of the older villagers crossed themselves, and Quentin understood, and did it anyway, because the sound deserved the gesture even if the fear no longer did. The tape that held the noise that had silenced her grandmother went into the ground, and the ground received it, and nothing rose, and nothing rang, and the morning went on being a morning.

When the last tape was down, Daniel filled the hole, and the children helped, shovelling with the seriousness of the very young, and someone brought out cake, because in Marrow Fen every gathering eventually became a wake and every wake eventually became a party, sometimes within the same hour. Quentin ate her slice sitting on the church wall with her boots in the nettles, and looked at the roofless tower against the blue, and felt — not peace exactly, not yet, but the space where peace would go, cleared and swept and waiting.

"You'll want to see this," Daniel said, appearing beside her. He handed her his phone. On it was a photograph, taken yesterday, of the inside of the tower: the walls, the empty belfry frame, the sky through the missing roof. And on the wall, low down where a child would reach, someone had chalked a picture. A bell. With a smile drawn on it. And underneath, in a child's lurching capitals: **IT IS SLEEPING NOT DEAD AND THAT IS BETTER.**

"That's the Pike girl," Daniel said. "Six years old. Her mum asked her why she drew it and she said—" he paused, and Quentin watched him decide whether to say the next part, and watched him choose honesty, which was new for Daniel and cost him visibly, "—she said, *so it knows we're not scared of it, just polite.*"

Quentin gave him back the phone. "Out of the mouths," she said.

"Yeah." He looked at the tower a while. "My mum used to say the house was a mouth. She meant it as a warning." He shrugged, and it was almost the old shrug, almost light. "Maybe mouths are just for talking, if you feed them right."

* * *

That evening, Quentin climbed to the attic one last time with the KEEP box under her arm, and gave the room away.

Not literally — the vicarage was hers now, papers signed, keys on her own ring, the deed bearing her name in ink that had dried without incident or omen. But she stood in the middle of the attic, in the last of the light, and spoke aloud to the empty space, because her grandmother had taught her the lesson posthumously and she intended to prove she'd learned it: things belong to whom they're given to, out loud, in words, witnessed.

"This room was yours," she said to her grandmother, to the dust, to whatever portion of either was still listening, which she suspected was the same portion that listens to everything, which is to say none, which is to say the part of us that keeps the habit after the need is gone. "You kept it dark and sealed and safe, and I'm going to put a window in it. A real one. And shelves. And I'm going to leave these tapes where anyone can find them, with a card in the box that says what they are and says it plainly. No warnings after dark. Just: *these are ours, and we survived, and here's how.*"

The house settled around the words. She felt it the way you feel a sleeping animal shift — not menace, not hunger, just weight redistributing itself, a body finding a comfortable position at last. Somewhere below, a door stood open to the evening air, and nothing required it shut, and it stayed open anyway, breathing.

She carried the box downstairs and set it in the front room, on the shelf beside the mirror she'd hung there in the first week — the first mirror the guest rooms had seen in sixty years, and the house had taken it without complaint, and her own reflection had looked, if anything, relieved.

On top of the tapes she placed the card, written that afternoon in her best hand:

*Marrow Fen sound archive. Interviews, vigils, and testimony, 1911–1979. Kept by choice, given freely. If you live here, this belongs to you. Say so, and it will.*

Then she sat down at the little table by the window with her notebook, because the show was over but the habit of writing remained, and she wrote the sentence she'd been circling for three days, the one that had finally landed:

*Belonging is not an inheritance. It is a broadcast. It only exists while someone is saying it, and it only reaches as far as someone is willing to hear.*

She underlined it. Then, because the light was going and the tea was cold and the fen outside the window had turned to hammered pewter, she closed the notebook — and froze.

From the front room, faint but unmistakable, the telephone rang.

Once.

Only once.

Quentin sat very still. The house held its breath around her, or she imagined it did, or there was no difference anymore and never had been. The phone did not ring again. It simply waited, the way the tower waited, the way the fen waited, the way every open line waits for the voice brave enough to answer.

She looked at the clock. Two minutes past two in the morning.

She got up, and went to answer it.
