## 37. New Neighbors

The first of the new families arrived on a Tuesday in late September, when the fen had gone the color of old pewter and the reeds were shedding their heads like grey hair. Daniel Halloway watched from the vicarage gate as a removal van took the bend at the old toll bridge too fast, its trailer swaying, and then slowed — the way everything slowed, eventually, once it crossed into Marrow Fen — and crept the last half mile along the lane as if the village itself had put out a hand and said, gently, *no hurry. You're nearly here.*

He knew the van's contents before it parked. He'd read the ledger for too many years not to know the weight of a household. Beds, a dresser, boxes marked KITCHEN in a woman's careful capitals. A child's bicycle strapped upright against the tailgate, one wheel still turning, ticking over and over like a small clock that hadn't learned yet what the village did to clocks.

*The Pryces,* the letter had said, when it came in August. *Referred by the county resettlement scheme. A couple and one daughter, nine. The husband's work is drainage engineering — he says he's not superstitious.*

Daniel had almost laughed at that. *Not superstitious.* Nobody was superstitious in August. August was for optimism. The village had voted in July — the first full vote anyone could remember in living memory, held in the church hall with the windows open because nobody trusted closed windows anymore, or perhaps because they finally did — and the vote had been to take new families in. To fill the empty cottages along the fen road. To let the village become a village again instead of a vigil.

Quentin had argued for it. Daniel had argued against it, and then, when the vote went against him, had gone home and sat in his mother's kitchen — his mother's, though she was eleven months gone now, burned on the altar steps with the ledger, and the ash of that fire was still worked into the grain of the altar wood no matter how the new vicar scrubbed — and had admitted, in the silence where her voice used to be, that he'd argued against it for the wrong reason.

Not because it was dangerous. Because he couldn't bear to watch strangers move into houses where grief still hung in the plaster like damp.

* * *

The Pryce woman got out of the van first. She was tall and angular, with the kind of face that had decided early to stop performing pleasantness, and she looked at the village the way people look at a hotel they've booked blind — assessing, withholding judgment. Her husband came around the back and began untying the bicycle without being asked, and the girl slid out last, nine years old, with her mother's angularity softened into something still unfinished, still deciding what shape it would take.

"Mr. Halloway?" the woman said. "Marian Pryce. You wrote about the house."

"I did." He'd written four drafts. The first had been a lie — *a quiet village, friendly neighbors, good schools in Thetford* — and he'd burned it in the sink. The last had been the truth, or as much of the truth as a letter could carry: *The houses are sound. The people are kind, and they are kind in a particular way you'll want to learn. There are courtesies here. They cost nothing, and they matter. I'll teach them to anyone who asks.*

"About the courtesies," Marian Pryce said, and Daniel felt something in his chest turn over, the way a key turns in a lock that hasn't been used in a while.

"You'd like to hear them."

"I'd like to hear them *before* we unpack," she said. "My grandmother was from somewhere like this. Norfolk. She had rules she never explained and got very upset when you broke them. I've decided I'd rather know the reasons this time, if there are reasons. And if there aren't reasons, I'd rather know that too."

There were reasons. God, there were reasons. There were reasons stacked in the cellar of the vicarage like cordwood, reasons buried in the bell pit, reasons drowned waist-deep in the black water of the fen with their mouths still open on unfinished sentences. Daniel looked at this woman with her unperforming face and her careful capitals and thought: *my mother would have chosen you. That's the worst part. She'd have read that letter and put a star by your name.*

"Come in," he said. "The kettle's on. There's salt in the tea caddy. I'll explain that first, actually, because otherwise you'll think I've lost my mind, and I'd rather you reach that conclusion in an orderly fashion."

* * *

He told them at the kitchen table, with the girl — Nell, her name was Nell — drawing on the back of his shopping list with the fierce concentration of children who have learned that adults talk longer when you're quiet and busy.

He told it plainly, because Quentin had taught him that. *Plain words carry further,* she'd said, in the weeks after, when the two of them had sat up night after night in the vicarage kitchen going through what happened, what had always happened, what would need to be said aloud now that the ledger was ash and the contract with it. *The old families hid it in rules because they were ashamed of the reason. Shame is how it stayed fed. You want to kill a thing like that for good, you say its name in daylight.*

So he said its name in daylight. He told them about the bell, melted in 1911 and ringing anyway. About the house built as a mouth. About his mother, Ruth Halloway, who had kept a ledger for thirty years and fed it names, and who had burned it on the altar steps with her own hands and not stopped burning, and who was buried now under the rowan at the vicarage's east corner because she'd left instructions and nobody dared disobey them, even posthumously. Especially posthumously.

He told it plainly and he watched their faces, and what he saw was not what he'd braced for. He'd braced for fear, or the polite blankness of people deciding how soon they could leave. What he saw was Marian Pryce's hand finding her husband's on the tabletop, and her husband — Gareth, the drainage engineer, not superstitious — going grey and then, slowly, going *steady*, the way men do when a thing they've half-suspected about the world is confirmed and turns out to be survivable. And what he saw in Nell was nothing at all, because Nell was nine, and nine-year-olds file horror under *later*, and draw their pictures.

She was drawing the church tower. The roofless one. She'd gotten the proportions right, which Daniel tried not to notice.

"So the rules," Marian said, when he'd finished and the kitchen had gone quiet enough to hear the fen breathing, that low tidal hush it made even in still weather. "The courties. Give them to me properly. I'll write them down."

"There's no writing them down," Daniel said. "That's — " he stopped, and started again, because the old rule had been *never write it*, and the ledger was ash, and some habits deserved to die with their keeper. "No. Write them down if you want. My family kept them unwritten for a hundred years because written rules can be found, and found rules can be enforced, and enforced rules become a ledger. Say them out loud instead. Say them to each other. That's the whole difference, really. That's the whole difference between a courtesy and a cage."

He held up one hand, and began to count.

* * *

"One," he said. "Close doors kindly."

Gareth frowned. "Kindly?"

"Don't slam them, don't let them swing, and — this is the part that sounds mad — say something as you close them. Goodnight, or thank you, or just the person's name. The house — the houses — they notice. They spent a long time being something other than houses, and they remember being listened to. A door closed with a word is a door the house knows is closed on purpose. A door slammed is a door it wonders about."

Marian wrote: *Close doors kindly. Say goodnight.*

"It sounds mad," Daniel agreed. "I'm aware. My mother used to stand in the hall at night and say goodnight to every door in the vicarage. I used to think she was mad too. Then I spent one night in a house where nobody had said goodnight to the doors in forty years, and I stopped thinking that."

"Which house?" Marian asked.

"Mine," Daniel said, "before I came back to hers."

"Two," he went on. "Answer your people. If someone calls your name — your actual name, the one your mother used — you answer. Even if it's late. Even if you're tired. Even if you're *certain* you're alone. Especially then." He looked at Nell, who had stopped drawing, though she was still looking down at the page. "That one's for you most of all. If you hear your mum calling from another room, you call back *I'm here*, and you go to her, and if it isn't her — you'll know, it's like a coat that fits wrong — you don't run. You say *not today*, polite as anything, and you walk to where the light is. Things that borrow voices can't follow you into a room where someone's expecting you."

"Things that borrow voices," Gareth repeated. His voice was flat, but his hand hadn't left his wife's.

"The fen's full of them," Daniel said. "Or it was. Or — " and here he had to do the thing he still hadn't gotten good at, the thing that caught in his chest every time, " — it is, a bit, still. Fewer now. Most of them finished. Quentin let them finish. That's a long story and it's hers to tell, and she tells it better than I do, and she'll be round on Thursday with a casserole, because that's another courtesy: you feed the new people, it's how the village says *you belong here*, and Quentin Galloway has strong opinions about belonging. But some of them didn't finish. Some of them just went quiet instead. So you answer your people, and you're polite to everything else, and you don't linger at the fen's edge after dark, and that's not a rule, that's just sense, the way you don't linger on railway tracks."

Marian wrote: *Answer your people. Not today, polite as anything.*

"Three," Daniel said. "Count the bells. Just in case."

"There's no bell," Gareth said. "I looked. The tower's empty. Roofless. The estate agent's photographs — "

"The tower's empty," Daniel agreed. "That's the point of it. We voted to leave it that way. A skylight, Quentin calls it. An open throat, my mother would have called it, and then she'd have laughed at herself for calling it that." He turned his cup, which was empty, which was a habit he was trying to break, because an empty cup turned is a door slammed, sort of, if you squint, and he was trying to teach himself which of the old rules were load-bearing and which were just scar tissue. "There's no bell. There hasn't been a bell since 1911. But if you ever hear one — felt in the teeth before it's heard, that's how you know it's the real kind — you count. You count out loud, you count with someone, you hold hands and count. And if it gets to eight and then stops, you don't fill the silence. You don't say *nine*. You don't think *nine* in a shouting kind of way. You wait for sunrise, together, and you let the silence stay empty, because an empty silence is just a silence, but a silence you've filled with a number — "

He stopped. Nell was looking at him now, fully, her pencil down, her chin on her fists.

"What happens if you say nine?" she asked.

And Daniel Halloway, who had locked a woman in a bell tower eighteen months ago because he loved his village more than he loved her, and who had wept while he did it, and who had spent every day since learning the difference between love and hunger, said:

"Nothing. Now. It would have done something once. Now it would just be rude." He smiled at her, and found, to his surprise, that the smile was real. "But you count anyway. Just in case. That's what a courtesy is, Nell. It's a thing you do when it's not needed, so that it's already done if it ever is."

* * *

They moved in that weekend. The whole village turned out, which surprised the Pryces and didn't surprise Daniel at all, because Marrow Fen had spent a century learning exactly one skill above all others, and that skill was *gathering*. They had gathered at midnight when the bell called. They had gathered at dawn, weeping and serene, when it finished. Now they gathered with a removal van and a tea urn and Mrs. Tredgett's seed cakes, and it looked, from the outside, like any village welcome in any county in England, and it was, and it wasn't, and both of those were true, and Daniel was learning to let both be true.

He watched old Wren — ninety-one, one of the serene ones from the first dawn, who had lost the memory of his wedding day to the tolling and gotten, in exchange, a calm that never left him — teach Nell to skip stones off the footbridge, and heard him say, casually, in the way of a man passing on something he didn't know he was passing on: "Always let the last stone be hers, mind. Never throw the last one for them. They've got to finish."

*Just in case,* Daniel thought. *Just in case.*

Quentin came Thursday, with the casserole, as promised. She looked better than she had in the spring — the hollows under her eyes filling in, the radio voice settling back into her body for everyday use. The final show had been six months ago now. *Signing off the frequency forever,* she'd said, and then she'd sat in silence for a full minute of dead air before the switch, and Daniel had listened to that minute in his kitchen, and it had been the fullest silence of his life.

She and Marian Pryce got on immediately, in the way of two women who had both decided long ago that pleasantness was a tool and not a personality. Daniel left them at the kitchen table with the casserole and went out to check the rowan, which was what he did now instead of praying. His mother's tree. The leaves were turning. In another month they'd go the deep arterial red that rowans go, and the village children would call it the blood tree, and he'd let them, because his mother would have loved being a blood tree. She'd have considered it a promotion.

When he came back inside, Nell was at the window, and Quentin was crouched beside her, and they were looking out across the gardens toward the fen, where the evening light was going long and amber and entirely, perfectly ordinary.

"What are you two looking at?"

"The heron," Nell said. "It's standing on one leg. Quentin says it's counting."

"Counting what?" Daniel asked.

"Reeds," Quentin said. "Or bells. Or nothing. Herons count. It's how they know the day's done." She stood, and looked at Daniel, and something passed between them that had no words in it yet but was circling, the way the heron circled its own reflection. "Marian asked me if it's over," she said quietly. "I told her the truth. I said: the hunger's fed, not gone. Fed things sleep. And the best way to keep a thing asleep is to live loudly and kindly on top of it, and never, ever let it get lonely."

"That's not a rule," Daniel said.

"No," Quentin agreed. "It's a courtesy."

* * *

That night, walking home past the churchyard, Daniel heard the girl's voice from the Pryces' new cottage — one of the old fen-road cottages, empty for eleven years, its doors unsaid-to for eleven years — clear across the garden in the dark.

"Goodnight, door," Nell Pryce was saying, with the solemnity of a child performing a sacrament she half-believed in. "Goodnight, other door."

And then, from inside, her mother's voice, warm and tired and alive: "Goodnight, Nell. I'm here."

"I'm here too," the girl said.

Daniel stood in the lane a moment longer than he needed to. The fen breathed. The roofless tower stood open to the stars like a skylight, like an open throat that had learned, at last, to sing instead of swallow. Nothing rang. Nothing called his name. It was, he thought, the kind of night his mother would have ruined by checking the ledger — and the ledger was ash, and the ash was in the altar wood, and the wood was in the church, and the church was just a church, roofless and ordinary and done.

He said goodnight to his own front door, out loud, because it cost nothing.

Inside, on the kitchen table, he found an envelope that hadn't been there when he'd left that morning. His name on it in his mother's handwriting — her real handwriting, the small sharp hand from the ledger's margins, the hand of a dead woman — and beneath the name, a single line:

*You counted well, my darling. But there's one courtesy I never taught you, and you'll want it before the winter. Ask Quentin about the eleventh bell.*

Daniel stood very still in his kitchen, in the house that had been a mouth, in the village that had learned to sing.

Outside, far off across the dark water, a heron called once — a sound like a struck throat — and fell silent.
