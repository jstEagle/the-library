## 40. Sign-Off

The new studio was smaller than the old one, and that was the point.

Quentin had chosen it herself — a converted chapel on the edge of Marrow Fen, deconsecrated twice over, once by the diocese in 1963 and once by a failed craft-brewery venture in 2019 — because it had good bones and bad acoustics and one window that looked east across the water. The desk was her grandmother's kitchen table, sanded down and rewired with a mixing board set into its grain. The walls were bare except for a single framed photograph: the tower at St. Brannoc's, roofless, open to the sky like a cupped hand.

And above the door, where a church would have kept its bell, hung the ON AIR sign. Red glass, salvaged from the original transmitter room, glowing now the way it had glowed for ten years of nights she could not fully account for and no longer tried to.

It was 1:40 in the morning. In twenty minutes, *The Quiet Hours* would go live for the first time without Quentin Galloway's voice on it.

She watched Nell Farrow arrange herself at the microphone the way you'd watch someone learn to hold a bird — too carefully at first, then finding the grip that let it stay. Nell was twenty-six, came up from Norwich, had a voice like low tide and a habit of listening with her whole body tilted forward, which was either a professional gift or a warning sign, and Quentin had decided it didn't matter which. Both kinds of people ended up behind this desk eventually. She'd been both herself.

"Say something," Quentin said. "Anything. Into the mic."

"Testing." Nell winced at her own voice in the headphones. "God, I sound—"

"You sound exactly like you. That's the whole job." Quentin turned a dial a quarter-inch, just to feel the fader under her fingers one more time. "Most people spend their whole lives editing themselves down to something acceptable. Your job is to make a stranger feel safe enough to stop doing that. For three minutes. Maybe four."

"And then what?"

"Then you let them finish their sentence."

Nell looked at her. "You've said that before. Like it's a rule."

"It's *the* rule. Everything else is furniture." Quentin settled into the chair by the window — she'd claimed it months ago, the listener's seat, the producer's chair, whatever she was calling herself these days. Consultant. Trainer. Ghost who haunted the studio until the new blood stopped flinching at the creaks. "You can break every broadcast convention there is. Dead air, long silences, callers who ramble, callers who cry, callers who call back eleven times in one night. None of it matters. But if you cut someone off mid-sentence—" she paused, and outside the window the fen lay flat and silver under a half-moon, "—you'll find out why that's a rule the hard way. And I'd rather you never found out the hard way."

"Because of Marrow Fen," Nell said. Not a question. Everyone who took this job knew the stories by now; Quentin had made sure the job listing mentioned the village by name, so that anyone who arrived had already decided to.

"Because of Marrow Fen," Quentin agreed. "But also because it's just decent. The other reason is backup."

* * *

They ran the pre-flight together one last time. Transmitter check — the old unit from the first studio, the one Quentin had hotwired in a bell tower while the world came apart around her, rebuilt now, clean, humming at 104.7 FM into a county of maybe four thousand insomniacs, night drivers, and people who simply couldn't be alone with their own thoughts unaccompanied. The frequency had been hers for fifteen years. It would be Nell's now.

"First caller's usually Edith's daughter," Quentin said. "Maren. She rings in around two-ten most nights, doesn't say much, just likes to hear the show start. Treat her like a regular, because she is."

"Maren Verrity-Cole. I remember from the notes." Nell had read the notes. All of them. Quentin had written three hundred pages of them over the years — caller histories, village histories, the rules softened into courtesies, the courtesies hardened back into rules when the moon was wrong. Close doors kindly. Answer your people. Count the bells, just in case. Never assume silence means nothing is listening; sometimes silence means everything is being patient.

"And if someone calls who sounds—" Nell hesitated, hunting for the word the notes used.

"Wet," Quentin said. "The word is wet. Voices carry water differently. You'll know. Don't panic, don't hang up, don't cut them off. Ask them what they were saying. Ask them to finish. Nine times out of ten, that's all they ever wanted, and then they're gone, and the fen stays a fen, and everyone gets to keep their memories."

"And the tenth time?"

Quentin looked out the window. The church tower stood against the sky, roofless as it had been since the night Ruth Halloway burned the ledger and the ninth toll came home. Empty but no longer listening — that was how the parish newsletter put it, every year, in the anniversary notice, half joke and half prayer. Children played up there now. Someone had strung bunting between the stonework last summer for the fete. Nothing rang.

"The tenth time," Quentin said, "you ring me. Any hour. I still sleep light."

Nell nodded and wrote it down anyway, though it was already in the notes, in Quentin's handwriting, underlined twice. Some things you write down so your hands have something to do while your heart catches up.

At 1:58, Quentin did the thing she'd done ten thousand times and would never do again professionally: she leaned to the mic and spoke the station ident. "*You're listening to The Quiet Hours, broadcasting from the edge of the fen.*" Her voice sounded older than she felt. Everything did, lately. "Nell. It's yours."

She slid the fader down on her own channel and watched the red light burn steady above the door, and Nell Farrow took a breath the whole county couldn't see, and began.

"Good morning. It's two o'clock, and if you're awake, you're in the right place..."

* * *

Quentin stayed for the whole first hour. She'd promised herself she wouldn't hover, and she hovered anyway, but quietly, from the window chair, with tea — salt in the caddy, out of respect rather than fear, the way she kept it at the vicarage too, because some habits are how a house says thank you.

Nell was good. Better than good. At 2:11 Maren called in and said her four sentences about the weather turning and her son starting school and how she still couldn't swim in open water but had started going back to the shore, just to stand there, just to look — and Nell listened with her whole body tilted forward and said, "That sounds like grief learning to stand up," and Maren laughed, wet-eyed, and stayed on through the weather report.

At 2:40 a man called about his brother, dead eight years, whom he'd never told he forgave. Nell didn't flinch. She gave him all the time in the world, and when he finally finished his sentence — *I forgive you, you great idiot, I always did* — spoken aloud into a microphone at three in the morning to a county of strangers, the studio felt, briefly, like the inside of a held breath releasing.

Quentin got up and walked out into the cold so no one would see her face.

Outside, the fen breathed. That was the only word for it anymore — the slow tidal shimmer of the reeds, the black water gone merely wet years ago, the smell of mud and mint and distance. She followed the path to the shore without deciding to; her feet knew it the way they'd once known the way to the churchyard at midnight, except this pull was gentle, and went nowhere she didn't want to go.

She stood where the drowned choir had stood. Waist-deep, they'd been, singing harmonies to a bell that wasn't there, and among them a woman with Quentin's face and her grandmother's hands. Some mornings, even now, she woke with the memory of those hands — not grasping, never grasping. Waiting. The way you wait at a departure gate.

"Hello, Gran," she said to the water, because it was nearly the anniversary, and because the living are allowed their rituals too.

The reeds moved. A moorhen crossed the shallows, absurd and alive, leaving a wake like a signature. Somewhere behind her, faint on the night air, the transmitter hummed its steady note, and Nell's voice carried across the water saying someone's name kindly, the way you'd hand someone back something they'd dropped.

Nothing answered. Nothing needed to. That was the peace of it — not silence, exactly, but silence that had finished its sentence.

* * *

She went back inside at four. The show was in its long middle stretch, music and murmurs, and Nell waved without taking off the headphones. Quentin wrote a note and left it on the desk:

*You did the thing. The real thing. Go home at six, sleep till noon, and don't listen back to your own tape for a week — you'll only hear your mistakes, and they weren't mistakes.*

Then, after a moment, she added:

*The kettle sticks. Hit it on the left. Welcome to the Quiet Hours.*

She gathered her coat, her bag, the small box of things she'd brought for luck — her grandmother's reel-to-reel spool, labeled in faded pencil, the tape inside long ago recorded over with birdsong, because some voices deserved rest more than preservation — and let herself out.

The door closed kindly behind her. She checked, the way she always would, and smiled at herself for checking.

The walk home took twelve minutes along the fen road. The vicarage windows glowed ahead of her, every mirror in every room catching the lamplight and giving it back doubled, and the house settled around her when she stepped inside with the deep contented sigh of a body digesting at last. She put the salt caddy straight. She wound the clock. And before bed, out of a habit she had never once regretted keeping, she switched on the little radio in the kitchen, dialed to 104.7, and left it playing low.

Nell's voice, warm and unhurried, filling the dark house with the sound of strangers finishing their sentences.

Over the quiet fen, seen through the window as Quentin climbed the stairs, the transmitter light glowed red and steady on the chapel roof — a small constant star where a bell used to hang — burning on, and on, and on, watching over everything that slept.
