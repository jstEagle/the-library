## 14. The Town That Sang

The list started with Mrs. Okonkwo and grew the way rot grows—quietly, then all at once.

Quentin sat at his kitchen table with a legal pad, a pot of coffee going cold beside him, and wrote down every name she'd given him. She'd talked for two hours on his sofa the night before, hands folded in her lap like a woman testifying, and when he'd finally asked her why now, after forty years of silence, she had looked at him with an expression he was still trying to file somewhere.

"Because you asked," she said. "Nobody ever asked. We all waited our whole lives for somebody to ask."

So he asked.

He made calls first, because that was easier than doors. He told people he was doing family history, settling his grandmother's estate, and most of them bought it or pretended to. Halvorsen, who ran the hardware store until it closed in '09. The Pruitt sisters, ninety-one and eighty-eight, still living together in the house they'd been born in. A man named Deke Ferris who answered on the fourth ring and said, before Quentin got three words out, "You're Ruth Iversen's grandson."

Not a question.

"How'd you know?"

"Because you're the third one to call me this month." A pause long enough to hear a clock ticking somewhere behind Deke's voice. "Except the other two hung up when I picked up. Like they wanted to but couldn't go through with it. You want to come by? I don't say this kind of thing over the phone."

* * *

Deke Ferris lived in a ranch house at the end of Corliss Road with a flagpole out front and no flag. He was maybe seventy-five, built like a barrel that had been left out through too many winters, and he shook Quentin's hand like he was checking it for something.

He led him into a den paneled in dark wood, offered him a beer at ten in the morning, took one himself, and then pointed at the corner of the room without looking at it.

"You see that?"

Quentin looked. Rocking chair. Old, oak, the varnish worn down to bare wood along the arms. Nothing about it said anything except *old*, except that was exactly what everything in this town said, so he looked harder. It sat at an angle to the wall, facing into the room. Facing the recliner where Deke sat.

"Rocking chair," Quentin said.

"That's what my wife called it too, God rest her. Bought it at an estate sale in '81, she said. Said we never owned one before that day, but she remembered buying it, clear as anything. Remembered carrying it in." Deke drank. His hand wasn't quite steady. "I've been in this house fifty years, Mr. Iversen. I know every stick of furniture I own. And I'm telling you we did not own that chair before August of 1981, and I am telling you my wife did not buy it, because I asked her sister, and her sister says they never went to any estate sale that summer. But Marlene remembered it. Remembered it like it happened. That's the thing you have to understand." He finally turned and looked at the chair straight on, the way you look at a dog that bit someone once. "It doesn't just show up. It brings the memory with it. It makes you remember owning it. And then you can't trust your own head about anything else."

"What happened in August of '81?" Quentin asked, though his stomach already knew there would be an answer.

Deke set the bottle down. Took his time.

"I was a volunteer fireman back then. There was a fire out on the Kettleman place—a chimney fire, spread into the walls. Kettleman had a hired man living in the back room, old fella named Amos Teague. Everybody got out except Amos. And here's the thing—" Deke's voice dropped, not for drama, Quentin realized, but because it had been held down so long it only knew how to move quietly. "Here's the thing is, the fire didn't kill him. Fire marshal said smoke inhalation, but I was there. I went in. Amos was in the back room and the door was shut from the outside. Wedged. And the Kettlemans were standing on the lawn, all four of them, and none of them were burned, none of them were hurt, and none of them would look at me. And nobody asked questions. Not the marshal, not the sheriff, not the paper. Chimney fire, sad loss, move along." He rubbed his face. "Amos used to sing. Had a voice like gravel in a bucket, sang while he worked. And the week before the fire, Marlene said she heard singing coming off that property every night. Real pretty, harmonized. Four-part. The Kettlemans had four kids."

Quentin's coffee-less hand had gone cold around nothing. "Singing what?"

"Never could make out the words. Marlene said it sounded like a lullaby." Deke looked at him. "You know it, don't you. I can see it land on you."

"I think I might."

"Then you know more than I do, and I've been sitting with that chair for forty years." He drained the beer. "Go see the Pruitt girls. And go see the widow Marsh. And when you're done, you come back and tell me what it is, because I'm too old to chase it myself and too stubborn to die not knowing."

* * *

The Pruitt sisters received him in a parlor that smelled of lavender and dust, and their chair was a wingback.

It stood by the window, upholstered in a green so faded it had gone gray, and when Quentin's eyes swept past it, Edith—the elder, the ninety-one—said, "Don't mind that one. It came with the house."

"The house was built in 1911," her sister Lorraine said immediately, automatically, the way you recite a catechism. "That chair has always been here."

They said it in unison. Or nearly—Edith half a beat behind, correcting herself mid-word, and the correction was worse than the lie. Quentin watched them both look at the chair and then away from it, and he thought: *they rehearse this. They've been rehearsing this for decades.*

He sat where they pointed and asked gentle questions about his grandmother, and they gave him gentle answers, and it was all pleasant and useless until he mentioned Elias Vann's name.

Lorraine stopped knitting. Edith's teacup paused halfway to her mouth.

Then, as if on cue, both women began talking at once about the weather, about the road construction in '88, about whether Quentin had eaten, and the wall of chatter was so sudden and so coordinated that it could only be practiced. He let it run for a full minute. Then he said, quietly:

"There's a chair in every house on this street. I've seen three today. You know what that means better than I do."

The silence afterward had texture. Edith set her cup down with a small click.

"He was sick," Lorraine said finally, to her knitting, to her lap, to no one. "Elias Vann. Consumption of the lungs, or near enough. The doctor wouldn't come out anymore—not for a boarder, not for a colored man, and he was both. So Ruth kept him in the back room and the children sang to him. All the neighborhood children. It was considered a kindness." Her needles had stopped moving entirely. "We were told it helped him sleep. We were children. We believed what we were told."

"And when he died?"

Neither woman spoke. Outside, a car passed, ordinary as anything.

"We were told he went to his sister's in Ohio," Edith said at last. "There was no sister. I checked, thirty years ago. There was never any sister." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and terribly old. "Mr. Iversen, I will tell you what I have never told anyone. In October of 1974, my father dug a hole in the back garden. My mother stood at the door singing to keep us inside. And the next spring the lilacs came up thicker than anywhere else on this street, and my father never ate from that bed again, and neither did we, and we all pretended the lilacs were just lilacs."

She folded her hands.

"Every house on this street has a chair. Did Okonkwo tell you that? Go count them. Go ask. Every single one. Because it wasn't just the Iversens. The doctor who wouldn't come. The sheriff who didn't look. The neighbors who heard the singing and knew what singing meant and stayed indoors. The whole street sang, or stayed silent, or dug, or looked away. The whole street." Her voice frayed at the edges. "And now every house keeps its seat."

* * *

He counted.

He drove Corliss Road end to end, then Birch, then the loop around the old mill pond, and he knocked on doors with a clipboard he didn't need, asking about water pressure, about property lines, about anything, and getting invited onto porches where he could see into parlors and hallways and front rooms.

Nine houses. Nine chairs.

A ladder-back in the Aldermans' kitchen, facing the stove. A piano bench in the widow Marsh's front room that nobody played piano on anymore. A child's high chair, of all things, in the Hollis home—and Mrs. Hollis, seventy and sweet-faced, had gone white when Quentin's eyes touched it and said, "That was Danny's. Danny's high chair," and her husband had put his hand over hers and said, "Yes, dear," in a voice that meant *no, it isn't,* and Quentin had excused himself fast because he could feel the shape of the wrong underneath it, some other buried thing, some other hole in some other garden, and he understood suddenly that the chairs weren't all Elias's.

That was the part that rearranged him.

He sat in his van outside the Hollis place with the engine running and the clipboard on his knees and let it settle over him like cold water. The chair in his grandmother's photographs—that was Elias. That was the lodger in the back room, the man the family sang quiet. But the high chair, the rocking chair, the wingback, the bench—those belonged to other silences. Other names nobody had spoken in fifty years. The town hadn't been cursed by the Iversens' sin. The town had been *keeping books*. Every household its own entry. Every chair its own unpaid debt, sitting in the parlor, warm, patient, facing whatever direction guilt faced.

It wasn't a haunting. Hauntings were random, hauntings were hunger. This was different. This was orderly. This was a ledger, and the ledger balanced itself, and it had been balancing for fifty years, waiting for someone to read it aloud.

His phone buzzed against the console. Dana.

He grabbed it fast—she'd been strange since the attic night, since the humming, since the nosebleed—but the screen showed a text, just three words:

**found more photos**

And then, a second later, before he could even thumb a reply:

**quentin there are eleven chairs in the church directory**

Eleven. He stared at it. The church directory—every family in the congregation, posed on the church steps, 1974 edition, the one framed in the vestibule. Eleven households. Eleven chairs scattered through the group photo like punctuation marks, and he'd walked past that frame his whole childhood without seeing a single one.

He typed back: *coming over. dont hum anything*

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared again.

Then the reply, and it dropped the temperature of the van ten degrees:

**too late. i cant stop. and quentin—i found out what happens to the verse if nobody sings it. grandma underlined it in the hymnal. it doesnt fade. it waits. and dana's handwriting stops there, mid-sentence, and i dont remember writing that last part**

He read it twice. Read it a third time. The van idled. Somewhere down the block, behind a picture window, a rocking chair sat facing an old man's recliner, patient as interest accruing.

The ledger wasn't finished. It was waiting for a reader.

He put the van in gear and drove toward Dana's with the windows down, because the whole town smelled faintly of lilacs, and it was October, and lilacs bloomed in May.
