## 15. Dana Disappears

The first call went to voicemail at nine in the morning. The second at noon. The third at three, and by then Quentin wasn't leaving messages anymore—he was just listening to the ring tone stretch out and die, that flat little melody of nothing on the other end, and hearing in it the same quality as the silence in his grandmother's hallway.

Dana didn't miss calls. Dana called *him* twice a day minimum, usually with some estate-sale grievance—the auctioneer wanted to split the china, a cousin wanted the good lamp, someone had left a sticky note on the piano reading THIS STAYS IN THE FAMILY without signing it. She was the family's switchboard. She had been since they were kids. If Dana went quiet, it meant one of two things: she was furious at him, or something had her.

He ran through the last conversation in his head while he drove. Tuesday night, eleven days ago now. She'd sounded tired, thin, like the signal between them was degrading even though both bars were full.

"You keep humming," he'd said. "When you think I'm not listening."

"I'm not humming."

"Dana."

A pause long enough to drive a truck through. "It's just stuck," she said finally. "That song. It's been stuck since I was five years old and I never knew where it came from until you played me that recording." Another pause. "Quentin, I can't get it out. It's like—like a hook behind my teeth. Like when you were a kid and you swallowed a cherry pit and Mom told you a tree would grow in your stomach, and you lay awake every night feeling for roots."

"Come stay at my place."

"Why? So it can follow me there too?" And then, quieter, almost gentle, which was worse: "You know it's already there. You told me about your window."

He hadn't told her about the window. He'd told her about the phone. The window he'd kept, because saying it out loud made it furniture instead of fear, and he'd spent forty-six years believing that things you don't say have no weight.

Now he drove out Route 9 with the windows up and the radio off, because the radio had started doing something he refused to describe even to himself—a faint under-singing beneath whatever station he chose, like a choir rehearsing one floor down.

* * *

Her car was in the driveway.

That was the first wrong thing. Not just parked—*positioned*, nose toward the house, centered in the gravel with a precision nobody achieves by accident. The driver's door stood open. Her purse sat on the seat, upright, straps arranged. Her keys were in the ignition.

The house looked exactly as it always did: gray clapboard gone soft as old chalk, shutters hanging at the angles of a shrug, the porch roof sagging over the front steps like a brow drawn down in thought. It had looked abandoned for thirty years and somehow still managed to look occupied. That was the trick of it, Quentin thought. Houses like this one never really empty. They just change what fills them.

"Dana!" His voice hit the porch and died there. No echo. Sound didn't bounce off this place; it was absorbed, filed away.

The front door was unlocked. Of course it was. Nothing here needed locking anymore. He stepped into the hall and the smell reached him first—dust and old varnish and underneath it something green and wet, like a terrarium, like the inside of a throat.

The chair was in the middle of the corridor.

He'd seen it there before, weeks ago, warm as a sleeping animal, facing grandma's bedroom door. Now it faced the other way. Down the hall. Toward the parlor. Toward the room where the wallpaper climbed the walls in its endless climbing pattern of vines and small pale flowers, the pattern Dana had once traced with her finger and said, *this used to move, didn't it, when we were small, we just forgot how to see it.*

The chair was waiting for him to walk past it.

Quentin stood in the entryway for a long moment, breathing through his mouth, and made himself do the thing his whole life had trained him to do: treat it as evidence. A missing person's vehicle. An open door. A chair is a chair is a chair. He logged it all in the flat voice he used for work reports—vehicle present, door ajar, no signs of struggle—and hated how much comfort the flatness gave him, and hated more that he needed it.

Then he heard the humming.

* * *

It came from the parlor, thin and steady, a thread of sound pulled through a needle's eye. He knew the tune the way you know your own pulse. Two verses' worth of melody, looping, patient, the lullaby from the hymnal with its third verse torn out—except Dana was humming past where the sheet music ended, humming into the gap, filling the torn space with notes that weren't written anywhere but fit anyway, the way water fits a hole.

His grandmother had done that too, he realized. On the recording. In the walls. The song didn't end; it just waited for someone to carry it across the missing part.

He walked down the hall. The chair sat at the edge of his vision the whole way, and he could feel it—not watching, chairs don't watch, but *attending*. Taking minutes.

The parlor door was open.

Dana sat in the corner where the light came thinnest through the dirty window, in a straight-backed wooden chair that had not been in the room an hour ago—he was sure of it, sure the way you're sure of your own name—and her hands rested in her lap and her eyes were open and she was humming.

She was also, in the way that matters most and words fail worst, half gone.

The wallpaper behind her was a repeating pattern: climbing vines, small pale flowers, and between them, if you let your eye soften, faces. Everyone saw different faces in wallpaper; that was the game children played. But these faces were specific. These faces were *local*. There was Mrs. Okonkwo's brother, maybe—there was certainly Elias Vann, blurred and smiling, the same blurred smile from every photograph since 1974. And now, spreading outward from where Dana's shoulders met the wall, the pattern was changing. New vines grew around her. New flowers opened along the line of her arm. The paper had taken her outline the way frost takes a windowpane, and inside the outline she was going translucent at the edges, her cardigan's sleeve dissolving into vine-work, the curls at her temple becoming small pale blooms.

She was being filed. That was the only word Quentin's mind would give him. The house kept its records in wallpaper, and Dana was being entered into the ledger.

"Dana." He crossed the room in four strides and grabbed her shoulder.

Solid. Warm. Real. She turned her head toward him with a smooth unhurried motion, like something on a swivel, and smiled, and the smile was wrong—not because it wasn't hers but because it was *too* hers, the exact smile from her driver's license photo, held at perfect fixed intensity, a smile that had stopped being an expression and become a feature.

"Hey, Quent," she said. Her voice was fine. That was the horror of it. Everything about her was fine except the parts of her that were wallpaper. "You came."

"You didn't answer your phone. Three days, Dana. Three days."

"Was I gone three days?" She considered this with mild interest, the way you'd consider weather in another country. "I've been sitting here. It's nice here. You should sit down." Her hand patted the arm of a second chair he had not noticed, positioned beside hers, angled companionably, waiting. "There's room. There's always room. That's the thing nobody tells you—it's not crowded in here at all. It's just quiet, and everyone's so *glad* to see you."

"Up. We're leaving. Right now." He took her wrist and pulled.

She rose—she let herself rise, he understood that much—with that same swivel smoothness, and for one step she came with him. Then the humming resumed, mid-phrase, exactly where it had paused, as if the song had simply been holding her place in line, and her feet slowed, and the vine-pattern on the wall behind her stretched, reaching, and he felt the pull through her wrist like a current, gentle as tide.

"Quentin." She said his name kindly. That was the worst part. She wasn't drugged or entranced or possessed in any way he could fight. She was *content*. "I remember now. All of it. Grandma in the doorway with her arms folded, and Tommy crying, and us singing so he'd sleep, so *Elias* would sleep, all week, every night, and the last night he sang too, did you know that? At the end he sang along with us. Through the door. Weak as he was." Her translucent fingers tightened on Quentin's hand with surprising strength. "He harmonized, Quent. He forgave us while we did it. Isn't that the most terrible thing you ever heard?"

"It's terrible. Come outside and tell me the rest."

"He doesn't want revenge. That's why the chairs. He wants—" she frowned, searching, and the frown rippled the wallpaper around her head like wind on water, "—he wants to be *counted*. That's all. We counted everything else in this town. The hogs and the harvest and who paid what at church. Nobody counted him."

"The song counts him. Verse three. We find the verse, we sing it, we name him, it's over. That's the plan, Dana. That's been the plan for two weeks."

"And whoever sings it joins him." She recited it without fear, without anything, a fact among facts. "Mrs. Okonkwo's brother. 1980. There's a chair in her hallway, Quent, I bet you didn't ask about the chair." Her eyes came back into focus then—actually focused, actually found him, and for a moment the real Dana surfaced through the pattern like a swimmer breaking water, and her grip on his hand turned desperate. "*Go.* Get out. I'm mostly in already, I can feel it, it doesn't hurt, it's like falling asleep in the car when you're little and Dad carries you in—but you're still all edges, you can still leave, and you have to finish it, you have to be the one who finishes it because I can't hum anymore without wanting to harmonize—"

The surface closed over her. The contentment poured back in like warm wax. She smiled her license-photo smile and began, gently, to sit back down, drawing him with her, and the second chair scraped invitingly across the floorboards by itself.

Quentin bit the inside of his cheek until he tasted copper and pulled.

* * *

He got her to the porch. He got her to the yard. He did not get her to the car.

At the property line—the ditch, actually, the shallow drainage ditch along the road that he'd jumped a thousand times as a kid—she stopped as if she'd hit glass. Her toes touched the gravel on the far shoulder and her whole body shuddered, and the humming broke off, and she looked back at the house with naked longing, and then at Quentin with the last of herself.

"She'll take me back tonight," Dana said. Her voice was fraying now, going papery. "Or tomorrow. It doesn't matter how far you drive me. I'll wake up in the chair. I already know the way home better than I know my own address." She laughed, a small broken sound. "You know what the funny thing is? When we were kids, I thought the house loved us. All those summers. I thought we were its favorite."

"It doesn't love anybody. It keeps accounts."

"Same thing, in this family." She squeezed his hand—her hand felt lighter than it should, less dense, like fruit beginning to dry. "Find the verse. Don't sing it alone. And Quent—if it comes down to you or me—"

"Don't."

"—pick the verse." She smiled, and this time it was entirely her own, crooked and tired and brave. "Grandma wrote something before she died. I know she did. She was tidy that way. She settled her accounts." Her eyes drifted past his shoulder, to the house, and softened. "Oh," she said quietly. "He's at the window. He's been at the window the whole time. He looks so *tired*, Quent."

Quentin turned. The upstairs window, the lodger's old room, stood dark. For half a heartbeat he saw it—or thought he saw it, or let himself see it: a shape, thin, patient, one palm flat against the glass. Not reaching out. Just showing that the glass was there, and that he was on the inside of it, and had been for fifty years.

When Quentin turned back, Dana was walking up the lawn toward the porch, unhurried, humming again, and he understood with cold clarity that if he grabbed her she would come, and if he let go she would return, and that he could spend the rest of his life playing tug-of-war with a house and lose it one inch of rope at a time.

So he did the hardest thing his hands had ever done, which was nothing.

He watched her go in. He watched the door close—not slam, not creak, just close, with the soft finality of a book shutting. He stood in the ditch until the light went long and orange, and then he walked back to his truck and sat in it and shook for ten full minutes, and then he stopped shaking, because there was work to do.

Grandma had settled her accounts. Grandma had been tidy that way.

A sealed letter. A lawyer. Instructions never to open it unless someone sat in the chair.

Someone had.

Quentin started the engine. Behind him, in the upstairs window, the shape was gone, but the glass held the fading warmth of a handprint, and as he pulled onto Route 9 he caught it in the rearview—one frame, half a second—a wooden chair riding in the back seat, patient as luggage.

He didn't look again. He drove toward town, toward the lawyer's office on Delancey Street, faster than the law allowed, while somewhere behind him, in a parlor going gold with evening, his cousin hummed the lullaby into its missing verse—and this time, faintly, hoarsely, gladly, something in the walls hummed harmony.
