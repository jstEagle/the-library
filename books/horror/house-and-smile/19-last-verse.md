## 19. Last Verse

The letter lay open on the kitchen table of a house that had no right to still be standing.

Quentin had read it four times. He'd read it in the car with the dome light on, read it under the streetlamp at the end of the Iversens' drive, read it standing in the doorway while the hallway breathed around him like something sleeping too close to the surface. Now he read it a fifth time by flashlight, because the house had eaten its own electricity sometime after eleven, and because he wanted to feel the paper under his thumb one more time before midnight made it irrelevant.

*The last verse names the dead,* his grandmother had written, in the same careful schoolteacher's hand that had written *we sang* in the margin of a newspaper clipping forty years ago. *It does not forgive. It does not forget. It only says the name out loud where the dark can hear it, and the dark has been waiting to hear it since 1974.*

Below that, in ink gone brown as old blood, the verse itself.

He didn't read it aloud. Not yet. He'd learned that much about this house — you didn't feed it early. You didn't give it a taste and hope it would wait politely for the meal. The walls were already sweating. He could see it in the flashlight beam: beads of moisture crawling down the wallpaper, tracking through the faded pattern of vines and small smiling faces that Dana had once told him she'd never noticed until she was grown, and then couldn't stop noticing.

Somewhere upstairs, Dana was humming.

That was the part that kept his hands steady, if he was honest. Not courage. Not justice. Just the sound of his cousin's voice drifting down through the floorboards, thin and contented, singing a lullaby she should not have known the words to anymore, because he had dragged her out of 1974 three days ago and she hadn't recognized him since.

The doctors called it dissociative amnesia. Mrs. Okonkwo, when he'd shown her the photograph of Dana seated in the chair with her eyes open and her mouth moving, had called it what it was.

"She's still sitting there," the old woman said. "Part of her. The part that sang. You can pull a body out of that house, Quentin. The song doesn't leave with it."

So: midnight. The house's hour, the hour the ledger balanced, the hour Elias Vann had stopped being a lodger and started being a silence everyone agreed to keep.

Quentin folded the letter along its original creases and put it in his jacket pocket, over his heart, which was beating like something trying to get out of a locked room. Then he picked up the flashlight, and the folding chair he'd brought from his truck — because if the house wanted an audience, it could damn well have one that chose to be there — and he walked into the hallway.

* * *

The chair was waiting for him.

Of course it was. It had been waiting since 1974, probably; it had simply gotten better at traveling. It sat at the end of the corridor outside his grandmother's bedroom door, oak and spindle-backed, older than any furniture the family had ever owned, and it was warm. It was always warm. He'd pressed his palm against it in chapter after chapter of his own disbelief, and it held heat like a living thing, like a lap someone had just left.

Tonight he didn't touch it. Tonight he set his folding chair down six feet away, facing it, the way you'd sit across from a man you'd come to interrogate.

"Okay," he said, to the house, to the chair, to the wet whispering walls. "I know how this works. Somebody sits, somebody sings, somebody stays. That's the deal you've been running for fifty years."

The house said nothing, which was its loudest voice.

"But here's what your letter didn't count on." He tapped his pocket. "Grandma wrote instructions. She wrote them like a trap, because everything she ever did was a trap — that's what you do when you're guilty, you build cages and call them rules. But traps only work on people who follow the whole song. And I've had three days to learn the tune, and I only ever learned it halfway. On purpose."

Upstairs, Dana's humming faltered. Stopped. Started again, a half-tone lower, like the house was tuning her.

Quentin's throat tightened. He let it. Fear was fine. Fear was fuel. What he refused — what he stood in that cold hallway refusing with every stubborn cell in his pest-controller's body — was the idea that fear got a vote.

"I believe in what I can spray," he said quietly. It was the joke he'd made his whole life, the armor, the creed. "I don't believe in ghosts. I don't believe in songs that eat people. I believe in raccoons in rafters and termites in sills and grease on handprints." His voice cracked and he let that happen too. "But I believe in Elias Vann. That's the thing, isn't it? He's the only one in this whole story who was real all the way through. Sick man. Locked room. Family singing over him so the neighbors heard music instead of a man dying. Fifty years, and nobody's said his name out loud in this town except into pillows and graves."

The walls shivered. Moisture ran faster. Somewhere below the floorboards, beneath the crawlspace, beneath the foundation itself, something turned over — slow, vast, attentive — the way a sleeper turns toward a voice saying their name.

"Yeah," Quentin said. "You heard me."

He took the letter out. Unfolded it. Held it up in the flashlight beam, though he no longer needed the light; the words had burned themselves into the backs of his eyes somewhere around the fourth reading, and they glowed there now like a brand.

*Elias Vann, born March 1911, died the second week of October 1974, in the north bedroom, alone, while the children sang.*

The first two verses were mercy. That was the trick of it, the genius of the trap. A lullaby is a kindness; a lullaby is how you tell a frightened thing the world is soft. Verse one promised sleep. Verse two promised morning. Any child could sing them without knowing what they were for, which was exactly why Grandma Iversen had taught them to five-year-olds and set them outside a dying man's door. The song wasn't a weapon. It was an alibi with a melody.

The third verse was different. The third verse was a name spoken into the dark — and the dark, having waited fifty years, would come to collect whoever spoke it. That was the rule Mrs. Okonkwo's brother had died proving. Sing the name, join the named. The house didn't want justice. The house wanted company. It always had. Every extra chair in every photograph in every home on that street was just recruitment.

Quentin had spent three days turning that arithmetic over, and somewhere around dawn on the second day he'd found the flaw, and the flaw was so simple it had made him laugh, alone in his apartment, in front of a window that reflected a chair that wasn't there.

The verse ended on invitation. *Come and rest, come and stay.* That was the cadence Grandma had preserved, the cadence the house enforced, the hook set into the melody itself. But songs were just air shaped by throats, and throats belonged to the living, and the living got to choose where the air went.

You could sing the name without singing the welcome.

You could end on accusation instead.

"Here's my terms," Quentin told the house. His breath fogged now, though it was June. "I say his name. Full name, loud, at midnight, in the room where it happened. That's the debt. That's fifty years of interest paid in full. But I don't take the seat. I don't take the verse's ending. I change the last line, and you're going to hate it, and it's going to work anyway, because a rule built on guilt was never strong enough to survive being argued with."

The humming upstairs stopped.

The silence that followed had texture — dense, fibrous, like felt packed into the ears. Then, from everywhere at once, from the walls and the floor and the warm oak of the chair, the house began to sing.

It used his grandmother's voice. Of course it did. It had worn that voice for decades the way a squatter wears a coat. *Hush now, hush now, the day is done* — verse one, sweet and low, seeping down the stairwell like gas. And underneath it, hoarse and ruined and fifty years deep, the fourth voice he'd recorded a week ago, the one that asked to be named, rising up through the boards like water through a cracked foundation.

*Say it,* the hoarse voice begged. *Say it, say it, please—*

"I'm going to," Quentin said. "Not for you. For him."

He checked his phone. 11:58. He thought about calling Dana one more time and knew she wouldn't know who was calling. He thought about Mrs. Okonkwo's brother, and the chair in her hallway, and the way she'd said *there's a price* without looking at him. He thought about his own reflection, which had started lagging behind him by a quarter-second this week, like a man walking a pace back from his own life.

Whatever happened tonight, he suspected he'd already started paying. Fine. Debts were debts. He'd spent his whole career believing only in what he could spray, and the truth was he'd never believed in anything at all, and believing in nothing was just another kind of empty chair.

11:59.

He walked past the folding chair. Past the warm oak chair. Up the stairs, each tread groaning like it was being asked to remember, down the hall to the north bedroom door, which stood open onto a darkness that was breathing.

Inside, the room was 1974. Not metaphor — the wallpaper was bright again, the window unboarded, a cot against the wall with a blanket folded at its foot, and the smell of camphor and sickness and October. And on the cot, or in the shape of the cot, or woven through the room the way a smell stays in a coat, was the thinness of a man who had died listening to children sing.

Quentin stood in the doorway. He did not go to the bed. He understood, finally, that the bed was the trap's center, and that Elias Vann deserved better than to be approached like a job site.

"Midnight," he said. "October, 1974. North bedroom, 14 Larkspur Street. Present: Elias Vann, aged sixty-three. Also present: everyone who wasn't."

The house screamed.

Not the walls — the house, the whole structure, timbers twisting, nails shrieking out of joists, the scream of a building being forced to hold a memory it had spent fifty years digesting. The wallpaper writhed. The little smiling faces opened their mouths and joined the chorus, and the chorus was no longer a lullaby, it was the sound of a family trying to sing louder than a dying man's breathing, and Quentin stood inside it and planted his feet like a man in a flood and began, in a clear and terrible voice, to sing.

* * *

His voice was bad. That helped. A good singer might have drifted into the melody's gravity and been pulled under; Quentin's flat, cracked, determined baritone fought the tune the whole way, dragging it where he wanted it like a dog on a short leash.

Verse one. *Sleep now, sleep now, the day is done.* He gave it grudgingly, honestly, because the dead man had earned a lullaby even if he'd never gotten a true one.

Verse two. *Morning comes to everyone.* And here Quentin bent the line, leaned on it, made it mean what it had never meant in that house: *everyone*, you bastards. Even him. Even the ones who kept the ledgers.

Then the third verse, the torn page, the scorched fragment, the sealed letter — and the house threw everything it had at him. Cold poured down the stairwell like water finding a hole. The door tried to swing shut. Dana's voice joined the chorus from upstairs, sweet and lost, singing the invitation cadence, trying to drag him into harmony, and Quentin bit the inside of his cheek until his mouth filled with copper and sang through the blood:

*Elias Vann.*

The name hit the room like a dropped chandelier.

*Elias Vann, who was sick and not wicked. Elias Vann, who asked for a doctor and got a locked door. Elias Vann, who died to the sound of his landlords' children singing —*

The cot was emptying. Or filling. The thin shape was standing up out of fifty years of silence, and it was looking at him, and under the blur Quentin could see a face — tired, ordinary, human, astonished —

*— and the ones who sang knew, and the ones who knew stayed, and the town that stayed quiet bought its peace with him, and every chair in every photograph is a receipt —*

The house was screaming again but the scream had changed key, and Quentin heard it, heard the exact moment the melody's hook snapped, because the last line was coming and the last line was his:

*Come and rest, come and stay* — the house hurled the old cadence at him, grandma's voice, Dana's voice, fifty years of voices, *come and rest, come and STAY —*

And Quentin Iversen, who believed only in what he could spray, looked straight into the blur where a man's face was remembering itself, and changed the ending:

"*Stay named, Elias Vann. Stay seen. And every soul who sang you quiet — stay accused.*"

Silence.

Total, structural, load-bearing silence — the silence of a house whose foundation has just been pulled out from under it.

Then the north bedroom exhaled. The 1974 wallpaper bleached and curled. The cot collapsed into dust and plain modern carpet, and the blur — the man — Elias Vann — looked at Quentin one last time with an expression that was not gratitude, exactly. It was simpler than gratitude. It was the look of a man who has finally been introduced at a party he attended fifty years ago.

And then he was weather, lifting. Gone the way fog goes when the sun decides.

The house came apart around the absence. Walls sweated rivers. Floorboards buckled. From every room at once came the sound of chairs — dozens of them, hundreds, phantom oak scraping phantom floors — toppling, dissolving, going out like lights down a long street. Quentin felt the pull on himself, the invitation's broken hook snagging at his own edges, taking its toll anyway, because the verse had been sung and sung verses are never free. Something in him loosened. Something in him blurred.

He didn't care. He walked out of the north bedroom, down the screaming hall, past the spot where the warm chair had been — bare floor now, cold as honesty — and up the stairs to where Dana sat on the landing, humming faintly, eyes open, eyes clearing, eyes focusing on his face for the first time in three days.

"...Quentin?" she said. Her voice was her own. Her nose was bleeding, but her voice was her own. "Why are you — Q, why are you blurry?"

He smiled. It cost more than the singing had.

"Long story," he said. "Grab your shoes. We're burning this place down properly."

Behind them, in the ruins of the house, in the last photograph anyone would ever take there — though no one would take one for years, and no one would know who took it — a figure stood in the doorway, smiling, a little out of focus, keeping watch.
