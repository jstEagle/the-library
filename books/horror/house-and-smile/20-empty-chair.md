## 20. Empty Chair

Dana woke on the floor of the hallway with her cheek pressed against floorboards that were warm as skin.

For a long moment she didn't move. She lay there listening to the house breathe around her—the tick of cooling pipes, the settle of old wood, a sound like wind moving through rooms that had no windows open—and she waited for the humming to come back. It lived in her chest now, or it had. She could feel the space where it used to be, the way you feel a tooth socket with your tongue.

The humming was gone.

She sat up slowly. Her whole body ached, deep and symmetrical, like she'd been carried somewhere and set down wrong. The hallway stretched out in front of her, dim with afternoon light coming through the plastic sheeting over the porch window, and at the end of it stood Quentin.

He was holding his phone in one hand and looking at it like it had said something terrible.

"Quentin." Her voice came out rusted. She tried again. "Quentin."

He looked up. And for one second—one full, unmistakable second—he didn't know her.

She saw it happen. Saw his eyes go through her the way you look through a stranger at a bus stop, polite and blank, already deciding she wasn't his problem. Then something behind his face caught up, some deeper ledger than memory, and he blinked hard and said, "Dana. God. Dana." He crossed the hall in three steps and crouched down and took her shoulders like he needed to confirm she had weight. "You're here. You're—okay. Say something."

"You didn't know me," she said.

"I knew you." But he said it too fast, and they both heard it.

* * *

They sat on the porch steps because neither of them could stand to be inside anymore, even with the door open, even with the light coming in honest and yellow and ordinary. The house behind them felt emptied out—not peaceful, exactly. More like a room after the furniture's been hauled away, when the walls still hold the shapes of things.

"I was in the chair," Dana said. "I remember it. I remember all of it, which is—I don't know if that's better or worse." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "I was sitting there and I was singing and I was so happy, Quentin. That's the part I can't get past. Whatever it was doing to me, it felt like being forgiven. Like someone finally saying, you were five, you didn't know, come sit down, we'll take care of it now. And I wanted that so badly I would have let the wallpaper finish."

Quentin was quiet for a while. Down the street, Mrs. Okonkwo's sprinkler chattered across her lawn, the most mundane sound in the world, and Dana held onto it.

"What happened?" she asked. "In there. At the end."

"I sang," he said.

"You sang the verse."

"The last verse. Yeah." He turned his phone over in his hands. "It names the dead. All of them. Elias first. Then—" He stopped. "Then everyone who let him die. Grandma. The neighbors who heard the singing and closed their windows. The town. Me, if I'd sung it right."

"If you'd sung it right?"

"There's an invitation built into the last line. Whoever speaks the names joins them. That's the price. That's what Okonkwo told us—that's why nobody ever finished it." He laughed, short and without any humor in it. "So I changed the ending. Flipped the cadence. Made it an accusation instead of an invitation. Named Elias, named the crime, and then just—stopped. Left myself out of it."

"And the house screamed."

"The house screamed." He said it flatly, like reading off an invoice. "And then it was done. Whatever it was holding, it let go. You came back. Elias went—" He searched for the word. "Lifted. Like weather clearing."

Dana looked back through the doorway into the hall. From here she could see the spot where the chair had stood for forty years of photographs, warm wood and patient grain, and the floorboards there were pale. Not worn. Pale. As if something had been lifted off them that had been pressing down so long the wood had forgotten its own color.

"It's gone," she said.

"It's gone."

She should have felt relief. She did feel relief—it moved through her in a slow wave, loosening muscles she hadn't known she'd been clenching since she was five years old and learning a lullaby she couldn't remember learning. But underneath the relief, in the space where the humming used to live, something else was settling in. Something quieter and much worse.

Because Quentin was still turning his phone over in his hands. And he wouldn't look at the screen.

* * *

They found out about the photographs that evening, at Mrs. Okonkwo's kitchen table, over tea nobody drank.

The old woman had come out onto her porch when they walked past—come out fast, for a woman her age, one hand pressed flat against her sternum—and stared at Dana like a resurrection. Then she'd fed them without asking, because that was what you did, and only afterward, with the kettle cooling between them, did she take out her own phone with trembling fingers.

"My grandson set this up for me," she said. "Pictures of my Harold. Of my brother, before. I look at them every night."

She scrolled. Dana watched over her shoulder.

Every photograph was clean.

Not restored—there was nothing to restore, no chair to remove, because the chairs had simply never been there. The birthday pictures from 1961 showed just the family, just the cake, just the yard. The funeral photo of her brother showed mourners and flowers and no extra seating. Forty years of haunted negatives, wiped in an afternoon.

"It's gone," Mrs. Okonkwo whispered. She kept scrolling, faster, as if she expected the chairs to leap back in. "Gone from all of them. Even the ones from before he—" Her voice caught. "Even Harold's. There was never a chair in Harold's pictures. There was never—" She set the phone down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off. "Fifty years I've had a chair in my hallway. Fifty years. This morning it was just a chair. Wood. Cold. A thing you sit on." She looked up at them, and her eyes were wet and fierce. "What did you do?"

"He sang the last verse," Dana said. "And changed the ending."

Mrs. Okonkwo was silent for a long time. Then she reached across the table and took Quentin's hand, and held it, and said nothing at all, which was somehow worse than anything she could have said.

It was Dana who noticed. Later—when the tea had gone cold and Mrs. Okonkwo had gone to lie down, overcome—Dana picked up Quentin's phone where he'd left it on the table, meaning only to check the time.

The screen lit up with his photos.

She scrolled without thinking, back through the last few weeks. The house. The attic. The corridor. Herself, in the car outside, taken the day everything ended. And in every single photograph—

She stopped scrolling.

"Quentin," she said. "Come here."

He came. He looked. And Dana watched him go very still, in the particular way of a man who has been waiting for bad news and has finally received it.

In every photograph, where Quentin stood, there was a blur.

Not the chair-blur. Nothing so defined. Just a softness, a smearing, as if the camera couldn't quite decide where he ended—as if he were moving during every exposure, or as if the light were passing through him and only mostly stopping. In the oldest photos, from weeks ago, the blur was faint. A halo of unsharpness. In the ones from yesterday, standing in the corridor with the chair behind him, he was barely more than a suggestion. A warmth in the air shaped like a man.

And the newest photo—taken that morning, on the porch, by Mrs. Okonkwo, to prove to herself that they were real—showed the steps, and Dana sitting on them with her arms around her knees, and beside her a slight brightening of the empty air, and nothing else at all.

"That's not how phones work," Quentin said.

"No," Dana agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

He tested it that night, methodically, because he was Quentin and methodical was how he grieved. He took selfies. He set the phone on a shelf and photographed himself walking past. He stood in front of the bathroom mirror and filmed thirty seconds of video, watching his own reflection with the cold attention of a man inspecting a pest report.

The mirror lagged.

It was subtle. If you weren't looking for it, you'd never see it—a half-beat delay, his reflection arriving a fraction late to every movement, like a video call with bad connection. When he raised his hand, the mirror raised its hand a moment after. When he stopped smiling, the mirror went on smiling briefly, alone, in the empty bathroom.

Dana stood in the doorway and watched him watch himself, and felt the humming-space in her chest ache.

"How long?" she asked.

"I don't know." He lowered the phone. In the mirror behind him, his reflection lowered its phone a beat later. "Okonkwo's brother tried to sing it in 1980. He didn't finish—he stopped, couldn't do it, couldn't pay the price. She said he lived another twenty years. Said toward the end he'd walk into a room and people would keep talking like he wasn't there. Not rudeness. They genuinely didn't see him." He turned from the mirror. "But he didn't finish the verse. I did. I paid something. I just don't know how much."

"You left yourself out of the song."

"I left myself out of the song." He smiled, and it was almost his old smile, dry and armored. "Turns out the song noticed."

* * *

Over the following week, the town healed in ways that made the local paper run a baffled editorial about community spirit.

People Dana had known her whole life called her, hesitantly, to ask strange questions. Had she ever noticed anything odd about the old Iversen place? Because Harlan Pruitt, who never talked about 1974, had gotten drunk at the Legion and told the whole bar about the week the Vann man died, and then cried, and then gone home and slept twelve hours and woken up feeling, in his words, fifty pounds lighter. The church was talking about a memorial service—for Elias Vann, by name, forty-nine years late. Somebody had found the plot in the pauper's field where they'd put him, unmarked, and there was talk of a stone.

The chairs were gone from every attic and hallway and spare room in town. People kept mentioning it the way you mention good weather—strange, isn't it, thought I'd have sworn we had a chair there, must have sold it years ago—and then moving on, because whatever else the lifting had done, it had also sanded the edges off the remembering. Only the ones who'd been closest, only Dana and Quentin and Mrs. Okonkwo, seemed able to hold the whole shape of it in their heads.

Elias was gone. Truly gone. Dana could feel it when she visited the house—which she did, once, in daylight, needing to see it empty. No hum in the walls. No warmth in the hallway. Just a dead house with bad wiring and forty years of dust, waiting for the bulldozer.

But Quentin.

Quentin was fading in small ways, and only she seemed to be counting.

The waitress at the diner forgot his order twice in one visit, and got visibly flustered, and comped the meal. His landlord called Dana instead of him about a lease renewal—called Dana, whose number he shouldn't have had, and said, "Sorry to bother you, hon, is your friend around? I've knocked twice." The bank teller squinted at Quentin's ID for a long moment, then at Quentin, then back, and processed the deposit anyway, but slowly, like a person doing arithmetic in an unfamiliar currency.

And his photographs got worse. By Thursday, the blur had become a rule: any image containing Quentin also contained a soft bright absence where he stood. By Friday, the mirror lag had grown to a full second. He'd stand at the sink and watch his reflection finish drying its hands after he'd already stopped.

On Saturday morning, Dana found him in his apartment packing a duffel bag.

"We're burning the house down," he said, before she could ask. "Properly. Permits, demolition contractor, the works—or not the works, actually, because I'm going to do it myself, tonight, and leave the permits on the seat of my truck. And then—" He zipped the bag. "There's a box of documents. The clipping, the sheet music, the letter from the lawyer, my notes, Okonkwo's statement, everything. I'm donating it to the county historical society. Public archive. Unsealed. So nobody gets to bury him twice."

"Quentin—"

"After that," he said, and his voice did something careful, "I'm going to need to figure out how much of me is left. Whether it keeps going. Whether I end up like Okonkwo's brother, or whether I end up—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. They both knew the word was *gone*, and they both knew the other word was *chair*.

"You could fight it," Dana said. "Find another way. There has to be—"

"Dana." He looked at her then, fully, and his eyes were clear and steady and entirely present, and she understood that whatever was draining out of him, this wasn't. "Elias Vann spent the last week of his life locked in a bedroom listening to children sing over him so he'd sleep through his own murder. For forty-nine years, nobody said his name out loud. Not one person. I said it. I said it loud enough to bring down a house." He shrugged. "That trade doesn't feel like a loss to me. It feels like the first fair deal anyone's cut in this town since 1974."

She hugged him then, hard, and felt him hug her back, real and warm and solid, and refused—absolutely refused—to think about how many seconds she'd counted the hug lasting, as if checking.

* * *

They burned the house that night.

Dana stood with him at the property line while the fire took the roof, and the flames lit both their faces orange, and the heat pushed against them like a living thing. The house burned fast and clean, dry as a matchbox after all these years shut up, and it made very little sound. Just fire, and the pop of old nails, and once—only once—a long exhalation that might have been wind through broken windows, or might have been something forty-nine years held finally setting itself down.

Quentin watched until the roof beams fell. Then he put the permit papers on the truck seat, exactly as promised, and drove Dana home, and stood in her driveway under the porch light.

"Get some sleep," he said. "You've earned about a decade of it."

"Call me tomorrow."

"First thing."

He got back in the truck. She watched the taillights go down the street, and she raised her hand to wave, and she would swear forever afterward that the porch light flickered as he passed through it—a stutter, a blink, the light losing track of him for a quarter of a second before finding him again.

She told herself it was the bulb.

* * *

He called the next morning. First thing, as promised. His voice sounded normal. Better than normal—light, unburdened, a man with a finished job and a clean conscience.

"Archive took everything," he said. "The archivist, this kid named Pham, got really excited about the 1974 file. Says they're doing an exhibit. Says they want to interview people." A pause. "I gave him your number for that. Hope that's okay. He tried to write mine down and—" Another pause, longer. "Anyway. Your number's on the form."

"Quentin."

"I'm fine, Dana."

"Look at a photograph of yourself. Right now. Tell me what you see."

Silence on the line. Long enough that she could hear him breathing, and hear, beneath it, the faint domestic sounds of wherever he was staying. When he spoke again, his voice was gentle, and that gentleness frightened her more than anything the house had ever done.

"I see a guy who did the right thing," he said. "Go have your life, Dana. Sing something nice for once."

He hung up, laughing a little, and the laugh was warm and real and hers to keep.

But that evening, sorting through her own camera roll—looking for a picture of the two of them from the porch, wanting to frame it, wanting proof—she found that the photos from the fire were wrong. Not blurred. Worse than blurred. In every shot of that night, the flames reflected in the truck's windshield, and the streetlight glowed, and she stood at the property line with her arms crossed against the heat.

And beside her, at the edge of the frame, the driver's side door of the truck stood open, and the cab light was on, and no one was getting out.

She zoomed in. She zoomed until the pixels broke apart. There was nothing there—just the open door, the lit cab, the shape of the night beyond it.

Except that she remembered him getting out. She remembered it clearly: the crunch of gravel, the slam of the door, him standing beside her, close enough that she'd felt the heat of the fire on one cheek and the warmth of his shoulder on the other.

She sat on her couch with the phone in her lap for a long time, in her new apartment, in her new life, in the silence where the humming used to be.

Then, from the hallway—her hallway, the one she'd personally measured for furniture, the one with no room for anything extra—came the small, distinct sound of wood settling.

She didn't get up to look.

She was afraid of what wouldn't be there.
