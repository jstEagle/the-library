## 1. The Commission

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Jude Vane would later think of as the last ordinary day of his life, though at the time it was simply a Tuesday like any other: grey rain against the office window, the radiator ticking in the corner, and forty-one unread emails he had no intention of answering before noon.

He almost didn't open it. The envelope was heavy cream stock, hand-addressed in ink so dark it looked wet, and Jude had learned long ago that hand-addressed envelopes meant one of two things—weddings or trouble. He was invited to neither. But the postmark said Ashcombe Vale, and Ashcombe Vale meant old money, and old money meant fees that didn't require three rounds of invoicing and a polite threat.

*Ms. M. Ashe, Hollowmere House.*

He slit it open with the bone-handled knife he kept for exactly this purpose—a gift from a father he barely remembered—and read it twice standing up, the way he read anything that might change his week.

*Dear Mr. Vane,*

*I am given to understand you are thorough. I require an appraisal of my property, complete and unsparing, for the purposes of sale. My solicitor will forward particulars. You will find my terms unusual. I trust you are a man who does not frighten easily, and if you are not, I trust you are a man who does not ask questions.*

*I must insist the survey be conducted in full, on-site, between dusk and dawn. No interim visits. No partial reports. The appraisal is only valid if completed in a single night, concluded at first light, when the papers may be signed.*

*I apologize for the strangeness of this request. I am very old and very tired, and I have found that strange requests are the only ones people honour properly.*

*Yours faithfully,*
*M. Ashe*

Jude set the letter down on his desk, next to the dying fern he kept as a reminder that some things could not be saved by attention alone, and did what he always did with the inexplicable: he filed it under *business*.

Because here was the thing about Jude Vane, the thing his colleagues would have told you if any of them knew him well enough to say it, which none of them did: he was very good at not asking questions. It was not a flaw. It was a discipline, honed over thirty-four years the way other men honed golf swings or grudge-holding. When the pipes in his flat knocked three times at 2 a.m., he did not lie awake wondering; he noted *settling, thermal expansion* in the mental ledger where he kept all such entries, and slept. When a client's house smelled of something sweet and wrong beneath the fresh paint, he opened a window and moved on. When his mother vanished from his life at nine years old—not died, *vanished*, a word the adults used with careful vagueness before they stopped using any word at all—he did not ask why. Asking, he had learned early, was how you got answers, and answers were doors, and doors, once opened, stayed open.

So he read Marguerite Ashe's letter again, and instead of asking *why dusk to dawn*, he asked the only question that mattered to him, which was *how much*.

His solicitor contact at Hargreaves & Pell answered within the hour, which was itself unusual; Gerald Pell treated correspondence like a tax. The figures came through in a PDF with no cover note. Jude read them, sat back, and read them again.

It was four times his standard rate. Five, if you counted the expenses clause, which was generous to the point of absurdity—travel, accommodation, "any incidental costs arising from delay." Delay. As if delay were the anticipated condition rather than the risk.

He called Gerald. "These numbers are wrong."

"They're not wrong."

"Nobody pays this for a rural appraisal. Not for a listed property, not for a contested estate, not even for—" He stopped himself before saying *not even for something they're afraid of*, because that wasn't a sentence a professional man said out loud.

Gerald's voice dropped into the register he used for things he wished he weren't discussing. "Miss Ashe is specific about her requirements, Mr. Vane. She asked for you by name."

That gave Jude pause. He'd never heard of Marguerite Ashe. His name appeared in trade journals occasionally, in the small print of disputed-valuation cases where his reports had held up in court. He was known the way a good lock is known—to the people who need one.

"She asked for me specifically?"

"By name. And she was particular about your—background." A pause with weight in it. "Your childhood residence. She mentioned it."

The radiator ticked. Rain ran down the glass in long fingers.

"I don't see how my childhood is relevant to a valuation," Jude said, evenly, because his voice never did anything but stay even; that too was discipline.

"No," Gerald agreed. "Nor do I. Will you take it?"

Jude looked at the letter. *I trust you are a man who does not ask questions.* He thought about the number in the PDF. He thought about the six months of retainer payments he'd been carrying for the Fenwick account, dead now, and the overdraft facility he'd been quietly nursing since spring, and the fact that his savings—what remained after the last bad year—would cover eleven weeks of his life if he were careful, and he was always careful, carefulness being the other half of his discipline, the twin of silence.

"I'll take it," he said.

* * *

The particulars arrived by courier that evening, in a leather folder that smelled of cedar and, faintly, of something else—something like the air inside an old church, stone and candle-smoke and time. Inside: title deeds going back further than Jude had ever seen for a private dwelling, a surveyor's plat from 1893 drawn in beautiful fading iron-gall ink, and a single sheet of instructions in Marguerite Ashe's own hand.

The handwriting was extraordinary. Not beautiful—*controlled*. Every letter stood at attention, as though the woman writing it had spent decades forcing her hand to behave.

*Mr. Vane. Some notes, so that we do not waste each other's time.*

*1. Arrive at dusk. Not before. The house does not like to be observed arriving.*

*2. You will survey every room. Every room. Do not skip the ones that seem unimportant. The house knows which rooms you skip.*

*3. There is one mirror in each room. They are covered. Leave them covered until you have reason otherwise.*

*4. I will be present. I will not interfere. I am dying, Mr. Vane; it is slow work, and I intend to see it finished properly, which is why you are here.*

*5. The papers sign at dawn. Only at dawn. If you attempt to leave before the survey is complete, you will find the door disagreeable. This is not a threat. It is a description.*

*6. Whatever you notice, finish noticing it. Curiosity abandoned halfway is worse than curiosity never begun.*

*7. Fee as agreed, payable on signature. If you do not sign, you will still be paid. I want honesty, not obedience. Though I confess I would prefer both.*

Jude read the sheet three times. Then he did what he always did: he made a list.

*Unusual client terms,* he wrote in his neat surveyor's hand. *Single-night site attendance. Dusk-to-dawn completion clause. Restricted access to certain fixtures (mirrors). Client present throughout. Penalty-free withdrawal.* He stared at item five—the door, *disagreeable*—and then, with the deliberate motion of a man closing a drawer, he wrote beneath it: *Terms accepted without material objection.*

Without material objection. As if the objections were merely immaterial, rather than simply unasked.

* * *

He spent the following week preparing the way he prepared for everything: exhaustively, and as a form of armour. He pulled every record on Hollowmere House that existed in the public domain, which turned out to be almost nothing, which was itself a kind of information. The house appeared in the county records in 1893, built—or rebuilt; the phrasing was odd, *rebuilt upon the former foundations*—for one Edmund Ashe and his wife. It passed through the family like a sentence being handed down. In 1974 there was a fire insurance claim, withdrawn. In 1988 a planning application for electric lighting in the east wing, approved and apparently never executed. And then nothing. Thirty years of nothing. Houses that generated no paperwork were houses nobody visited, and houses nobody visited were either ruins or secrets, and the photographs in the folder showed no ruin.

There was one photograph, actually, tucked behind the deeds. Sepia, cracked along one edge. A large grey house with too many windows, all of them reflecting a sky that looked, even in the faded print, darker than the sky above it. On the steps stood a woman in Victorian dress, her face blurred—not by the camera's slowness, Jude decided, but by something else, as though she had been moving when everything around her was still, or still when everything around her was moving. Beside her, a man held his hat to his chest. Neither of them smiled. On the reverse, in pencil: *Hollowmere, completion. E.A. & wife. At last it is full enough.*

*Full enough.* Full of what? A house was full of rooms. That was what a house was.

Jude put the photograph back and did not ask the question. He packed instead: laser measure, disto, moisture meter, thermal camera, notebooks, two pens (one always fails), torches, spare batteries, a flask of coffee, sandwiches he would not eat because he never ate on site, and a folded page of standard valuation templates. Professional detachment, he had found, was mostly luggage. You carried enough of the right equipment and there was no room left for wondering.

On Thursday night he laid everything out on his kitchen table and checked it twice, and it was while checking it the second time that he noticed the envelope again—the original cream one, which he'd kept because he kept everything—and saw, for the first time, that the address was written in mirror script. Backwards. Every letter reversed, elegant and unhurried, legible only if you held it up to the window glass, where the night street outside corrected it into sense.

He held it up. The words righted themselves against the dark:

*Mr. Jude Vane. He will know the way in.*

*He*, not *she*. Not Miss Ashe's controlled hand at all, or—if it was hers—hers written the other way round, like a person signing their name in reflection.

Jude lowered the envelope. The radiator ticked. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocked once, softly, the way it sometimes did, and he entered it in the mental ledger—*settling, thermal expansion*—and closed the drawer, and did not ask.

He needed the money. That was the whole of it, he told himself, and it was nearly true, and nearly true was the only kind of true he had ever required. Four times his fee. Expenses. A dying woman who wanted her house counted honestly before the end. There were worse ways to spend a night than walking room to room with a tape measure, and he had spent his whole life in worse rooms than any he was likely to find in the country.

Still, when he switched off the kitchen light, he paused a moment in the dark doorway, listening to his own flat settle around him—the familiar knocks, the tick of cooling pipes, the small sounds he had spent three decades translating into harmlessness—and he had the sudden, unaccountable certainty that somewhere far away, a house with too many windows had just heard his name spoken aloud, and had begun, patiently, to learn it.

He went to bed. He did not dream, or if he did, he did not ask.

Dusk was Saturday.
