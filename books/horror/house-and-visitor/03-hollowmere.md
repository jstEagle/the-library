## 3. Hollowmere

The taxi would go no further than the gate.

Jude stood in the falling light with his survey case in one hand and the printed commission in the other, and watched the car's taillights shrink down the lane with a speed that had nothing to do with professional courtesy. The driver had been cheerful enough on the way out—chatting about football, about the price of diesel—but when Jude had given the address, Hollowmere House, the man's chatter had thinned like broth stretched too far, and by the last mile he'd been silent in a way that felt less like politeness and more like listening.

Now the lane was empty and the dusk was coming down blue and cold, and the gate stood open.

That was the first wrong thing, though Jude didn't catalogue it yet. He was still in arrival-mode, still running the checklist that kept him functional: gate, drive, approach, elevation. He noted that the gate was wrought iron, Victorian, its hinges recently oiled—no squeal, no rust, moving under no hand at all as far as he could see. He noted that the gravel of the drive had been raked. Not swept—raked, in long parallel lines, like a Zen garden or a grave.

The house sat at the end of it the way a face sits at the end of a held breath.

It was larger than the photographs had suggested, three storeys of grey local stone with a deep porch and tall sash windows, Georgian bones under Victorian skin. It should have been beautiful. It was, in the way that a well-preserved animal skull is beautiful. Every window glowed faintly amber against the darkening sky—not lamplight exactly, or not only lamplight, but something warmer, more interior, as if the house were lit from within its walls rather than within its rooms. The chimneys stood straight. The slate roof showed not one slipped tile. And there was no ivy, no moss, no lichen anywhere on the stone, which for a house of this age, in this damp country, was less like maintenance and more like refusal.

Jude had surveyed four hundred and eleven properties in nineteen years. He had a private taxonomy of dereliction: the sag of abandoned eaves, the blind stare of boarded windows, the way empty houses seemed to lean away from you, embarrassed to be seen undressed. Hollowmere leaned toward him.

He checked his watch. 6:47 p.m. Sunset at 7:12. The contract specified he begin the interior survey at dusk and complete it by sunrise—a fourteen-hour window, billed at triple rate, plus a completion bonus that made his eyes water even now, even here, even with the gravel raked and the windows glowing. Fourteen hours. No surveyor worth his salt needed fourteen hours for a house this size. He'd assumed the owner was simply old, and old people paid for company as readily as for competence.

He walked up the drive. His footsteps sounded wrong on the gravel—too crisp, each footfall distinct, as if the ground were amplifying them, announcing him. *Visitor approaching,* said the gravel. *Visitor approaching.* By the time he reached the porch he had stopped trying to make his steps quiet, because they wouldn't be.

The front door opened before he knocked.

* * *

"You're punctual," said the woman in the doorway. "I'd half hoped you wouldn't be."

She was small and folded into herself like a letter someone had tried to unread, propped in a wheelchair with a blanket over her knees despite the mildness of the evening. Her face was a network of fine lines, deeply lived-in, but her eyes were startlingly clear—pale grey, almost colourless, and fixed on him with an attention that made him feel inventoried. He knew that look. He gave that look. It was the look of someone assessing what a thing was worth.

"Mr. Vane," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Ms. Ashe. Marguerite Ashe?"

"I'm she. What's left of her." She rolled back from the threshold to let him pass. "Come in, then. Mind the step. It's the only thing in this house that will admit to being imperfect."

The hall swallowed him.

It was vast—the kind of entrance hall built by people who wanted arriving to be an event—and it was warm. That registered second, after the size, and then kept registering, insistently, because the warmth was wrong. Not stuffy, not stale, not the trapped heat of a sealed house. Warm the way a hand is warm. Warm the way breath is warm through cupped fingers. The air itself seemed to have a pulse to it, faint and slow, and Jude caught himself holding his own breathing near it, matching it without meaning to.

And it was clean. Immaculate. He ran his eye along the dado rail, the panelling, the cornice work eighteen feet up, and found no dust, no cobweb, no dead fly on any sill. In nineteen years he had never entered a house of this age that did not carry some sediment of its own living. Houses shed. Houses sloughed skin. This one had none. It was like meeting a person of seventy with no lines on their hands.

"Your cleaner must be devoted," he said, because saying things was how he kept the floor of himself solid.

"My cleaner died in 1998." Marguerite's voice was dry as paper. "The house keeps itself. You'll find that convenient for your report."

He turned. She was watching him with that pale attention, and something else now, something almost gentle, the way you watch a man walk toward a hole he hasn't seen.

"Tea first," she said. "Surveyors who skip tea miss things. Through here."

* * *

The drawing room was the same story told again in better furniture. Fire lit—though he'd seen no chimney smoke from the drive—carpets thick enough to swallow footsteps, and every surface polished to a depth that made the room feel less maintained than *inhabited by something very careful*. On the mantelpiece stood a row of photographs, all of them facing the wall.

He noticed that. He filed it. He did not ask.

Marguerite poured tea from a pot that should have been too heavy for her wrists, and her hands barely trembled, and he understood suddenly that the frailty was real but selective—that she spent her strength the way misers spend coin, only where it counted.

"Terms," she said, before he'd taken his first sip. "You'll survey every room. Every room, Mr. Vane—all of them, top to bottom, including the ones that don't look like they matter. You'll note defects honestly; I want a true valuation, not a flattering one. And you'll stay until sunrise. You don't leave before dawn, and you don't finish before dawn. If you're done at three in the morning, you'll sit somewhere and review your notes until the light comes."

"The contract says as much," Jude said. "But if I may ask—why the timing? A survey takes six hours at most."

"Because the house is only honest at night." She said it the way another woman might say *the doctor is only in on Tuesdays*. "In daylight it performs. At night it stops bothering. You want to value what's actually here, yes? Then you'll value it in the dark, when it thinks no one's pricing it."

"That's—" He searched for the professional word. "Unusual."

"Everything about this arrangement is unusual. That's why I'm paying you what I'm paying you." She set down her cup. Her hands did tremble now, just slightly, and he saw that it wasn't weakness. It was the tremor of someone holding still next to something loud. "One more term. Unwritten, because no solicitor would draft it."

He waited.

"Don't mind the mirrors."

* * *

Later—he would think of it as later, though it was only minutes later, standing in the hall with his case open and his torch clipped to his breast pocket—later, the phrase would come back to him and he would take it apart like a lock. *Don't mind the mirrors.* Not *don't touch*. Not *don't uncover them*, though he'd already glimpsed that every mirror in the house wore a sheet or a shawl or a dust cover, draped shapes standing along the walls like furniture in mourning. Not *don't look*. *Don't mind.*

As if the mirrors might do something that required minding. As if they were guests whose manners couldn't quite be guaranteed.

"There are eleven mirrors in the house," Marguerite said from her chair, following his gaze to a tall shape under linen near the stairs. "Or twelve. The count changes. Don't let that trouble your inventory—just record what you find when you find it."

"The count changes," Jude repeated.

"Houses settle," she said. "Ours settles outward." And then, catching whatever moved across his face: "I'm teasing you, Mr. Vane. Partly. Old women are allowed. Write it down as twelve and let God sort the arithmetic."

He wrote it down as twelve.

She watched him write it. He felt the watching the way you feel sun through glass—no weight, but heat, definitely heat. And beneath the heat, threaded through the whole warm immaculate silence of the house, he became aware of a third presence in the room that was neither of them. Not a sound. Not a movement. More like the quality of attention a room has when it is listening to itself. He had been in thousands of houses. Houses had atmospheres; he was the first to admit it, professionally, in his reports—*the property has a neglected atmosphere, a gloomy aspect, an unwelcoming aspect.* But those were echoes of people, residue, the shape left behind by living. This was not residue. This was present tense.

This house was not remembering being occupied.

It was waiting to be.

"Your torch," Marguerite said quietly, "will behave strangely after midnight. Don't replace the batteries. It isn't the batteries."

"Then what is it?"

"It's the house reading over your shoulder." She smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was kind. "You're a thorough man—I can tell. Thorough men hate loose ends. So I'll give you this one free: everything in this house works perfectly except the mirrors, and the mirrors aren't broken. Remember that distinction. It matters more than anything you'll put in your report."

"Not broken," Jude said slowly. "Then what are they?"

Marguerite Ashe looked at him for a long moment, and in the firelight her colourless eyes went briefly, terribly young—as if something behind them had leaned forward to see through them.

"They're learning," she said.

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, in a room he hadn't surveyed yet, a floorboard settled—or accepted weight—or practised accepting weight. And down the hall, behind its sheet of linen, the twelfth mirror stood very still, the way anything stands still when it is concentrating.

Jude clicked on his torch, opened his survey ledger to the first clean page, and began, in his small precise hand, at the top:

*Hollowmere House. Interior survey commenced 19:04. Client: M. Ashe. Condition of property: excellent.*

His pen hovered. He crossed out *excellent*.

*Condition of property:* — and he sat there, in the warm attentive silence, unable for the first time in nineteen years to find the word.
