## 8. What Was Invited

The study was the last room on the ground floor, and Jude had been avoiding it.

He told himself it was scheduling. A proper survey ran in an order—exterior first, then the service rooms, then the principal rooms in descending order of complexity—and the study, with its built-in shelving and its probable wainscoting and its inevitable tangle of wiring behind plaster that had been cut and recut for a century of electrical updates, was the most complex room in the house. It deserved the end of the day, the full remaining light, his best attention.

The truth was simpler and he knew it: the study was where the papers would be.

He had found the first of them by accident, three hours earlier, when he'd pulled the radiator cover in the morning room to check the pipework. Wedged behind the cover, flat as a pressed flower, had been a single sheet of cream laid paper, yellowed at the edges, typed on a machine that had long since gone to the museum of dead technology. An appraisal summary. Dated 1931. A surveyor named A. Pemberton. At the bottom, in ink gone the color of weak tea, a signature—and struck through it, one clean diagonal line, as if someone had begun to cross the name out and then thought better of it, or worse, hadn't needed to finish.

Jude had photographed it with his phone, because that was what a professional did with anomalies, and then he had stood in the morning room holding the paper and listening to the house breathe its warm, dustless silence around him, and he had put it back exactly where he found it.

Not because he was afraid of it. Because he was afraid of what he'd do if there were more.

There were more. Of course there were more. He'd known since the moment he'd slid the radiator cover home and heard, from somewhere in the walls, a sound like a page turning.

* * *

By seven o'clock the light through the study's tall window had gone the color of brass, and Jude could postpone no longer without it becoming obvious that he was postponing, and he had spent twelve years learning never to be obvious.

The study smelled of cold paper and, beneath it, something faintly sweet, like the ghost of cut flowers. Bookshelves lined two walls, floor to ceiling, and the books were real—leather spines, gilt lettering, the works of people dead so long their names had softened into furniture. A writing desk stood beneath the window, mahogany, its green leather inlay worn pale in the center where generations of elbows had rested. The room's single mirror hung above the fireplace, and it was covered, like all the others, in a sheet of white linen so clean it looked less like a covering than a bandage.

Jude did not uncover it. He wrote *mirror, wall-mounted, approx. 1.1m × 0.8m, gilt frame, condition concealed* in his survey book, and his hand was steady, and he was proud of how steady it was, and he hated that he was proud of it.

The desk's drawers were locked. He was a surveyor, not a burglar, and he noted *desk, lock engaged, contents unexamined* with the particular satisfaction of a man drawing a boundary he intends to keep.

Then he opened the rolltop.

It wasn't locked. It wasn't even latched. It rose under his hands with the smoothness of something that had been waiting to be opened, and the sound it made was not the groan of old wood but something softer, something almost like an exhale, and the smell that came out of it was the smell of the whole house concentrated: warm, clean, expectant.

Inside, in neat stacks, were the papers.

Jude counted them before he read them, because counting was a thing he did, a thing his mother had taught him without meaning to—*count the noises, love, name them and they're only noises*—and counting made the stack finite, and finite things could not be bottomless.

Eleven appraisal documents. The oldest was dated the 14th of June, 1893, on paper so brittle it crackled when he breathed near it. The newest was Marguerite's own commission, the one that had summoned him, still bearing the creases of its journey. Between them, one for roughly every twenty-five years: 1893, 1918, 1931, 1946, 1959, 1972, 1984, 1997, 2009, 2021. Each one a full survey of Hollowmere House. Each one signed at the bottom in a different hand—spidery, bold, cramped, elegant—and each signature, without exception, struck through.

Not crossed out carelessly. Struck through with a single deliberate line, ruler-straight, the kind of line you draw when you are removing a name from a list rather than a name from a page. Jude laid them side by side on the desk and looked at the eleven dead straight lines and thought, with a clarity that felt like cold water: *someone kept these. Someone kept every one of them, in order, in this drawer, for a hundred and thirty years.*

And beneath the stack, at the bottom of the drawer, wrapped in oilcloth tied with string gone gray as cobweb, was something thicker.

He untied it because his hands were already moving. Later, he would notice that. Later, in the small hours, he would lie awake replaying the moment and understanding that he had not decided to untie it, that the decision had been made somewhere below thought, the way a hand pulls back from a flame or reaches toward a door handle. But now, with the brass light dying on the wall and the covered mirror at his back like a closed eye, he simply untied the string and unfolded the oilcloth and found a letter.

The envelope was addressed to no one. Inside was a single sheet, written in ink, in a woman's hand that started out careful and ended up ragged, the letters leaning like people in a crowd pushing toward something.

Jude read it standing up. He didn't remember deciding to stand.

* * *

*Hollowmere, the 9th of March, 1893.*

*I write this so that whoever comes after me will not have to learn it the way I learned it, which was too late and all at once, the way one learns anything true.*

*My name is Edith Hollowmere. You will know the house bears my husband's name, and that my husband has been dead eleven months, and that our sons died before him, one in the winter and one in the sea, and that I have lived here alone through a year in which the silence of this house grew so loud that I could hear it through my hands pressed over my ears. I do not write this to be pitied. I write it so you will understand what I did, because understanding is the only inheritance I have left to give, and because you will need to understand it quickly, and no one living can tell you.*

*There is a thing that comes when a house is empty enough for long enough. I did not summon it. I want that written plainly, because the vicar would say I summoned it, and the vicar says a great many things about houses and grief that he learned from other vicars and not from houses. I did not call it. I only grieved, thoroughly, in a large empty house, for a very long time, and grief, it turns out, is a kind of calling. It carries. It goes out through the walls and into the ground the way heat goes out through a window, and somewhere, in whatever lies next to the world, something felt it the way a cold man feels a lit window, and it came.*

*I did not see it arrive. I saw the house change. The rooms began to feel attended. You will know what I mean if you have ever come home to a house where someone is waiting—the quality of the silence is different, warmer, as though the air itself has been arranged. That was how Hollowmere began to feel. I thought at first I had gone mad, and I was almost glad, because madness would have been mine, a thing of my own making, explicable.*

*Then I saw it in the mirror.*

*It stood in the hall behind me, where I had been standing a moment before, and it was wearing my posture the way one wears a borrowed coat—not yet fitted, not yet mine, but patient about it. It did not move as I moved. It moved a moment after I moved, and every day the moment grew shorter, and I understood, in the slow way one understands a tide, that it was learning me. That the lag was not a defect. It was study.*

*I could not leave. I tried. I got as far as the gate and found myself walking back up the drive with no memory of turning, and when I reached the hall my reflection was already in the mirror, waiting for me, and it smiled a half-second before I did.*

*So I did what one does. I bargained.*

*It does not want much, or it did not want much then. It wants to be in the world, and it cannot enter on its own; it can only be admitted, the way damp is admitted, through a crack, and the crack is consent. A signature. That is the whole of it, and the whole of the horror of it: the entire apparatus of the thing, the learning and the lagging and the long rehearsal of your life in glass, exists to obtain a signature. It needs a keeper. Not a victim—a keeper. Someone bound to the house by ink, whose name on paper makes the arrangement lawful in whatever court such things answer to. I signed in my husband's name at first, because I could not bear to spend my own, and it knew, and it made me sign again, properly, and the moment the ink dried I felt the house close around me like a hand around a candle flame.*

*In exchange, I am kept. The house keeps me as I keep it. I do not age as I should. I am warm in winter. And the visitor fills the rooms, which was, I am ashamed down to the floorboards of my soul to write, what I wanted. I wanted the house full. I wanted to hear, in the next room, the sound of someone. I have it now. I hear it every night, walking the corridors one second behind itself, learning to be a person, and some nights the loneliness is so much quieter that I almost forgive myself.*

*Almost.*

*Here is what you must know, and I will write it as plainly as I can, though my hand is failing and the lamp is low: the signature must be renewed each generation, because the visitor wears out its keeper the way a fire wears out its grate. It feeds, though not on flesh. It feeds on the kept thing—on memory, on the warm interior life of the one who signed. Each year I remember less of my boys. I have their photographs and I know their names and I can no longer call their faces up, and this morning I looked at the sea-room where Thomas slept and felt nothing at all, a room with nothing in it, and I understood that the visitor is not filling the house with itself.*

*It is filling the house with me.*

*Every room it furnishes in the glass, it furnishes with something taken out of my head. I have seen my mother's kitchen in the east mirror. I have seen the beach at Whitby where the boys dug holes to Australia. It is building a copy of my life out of the pieces, and when the copy is finished it will need no keeper at all, and I do not know what happens to the keeper on that day, and I am afraid that I will find out before I find anyone to sign after me.*

*The surveyors come every generation because the house arranges it—the estate solicitor receives instructions he does not question, and a surveyor comes, and the surveyor signs, because the papers are drawn so that signing is the natural end of the work, the last line of the last page, and what tired man at the end of a long job does not sign?*

*Mr. Pemberton signed in 1931—no. Forgive me. The dates. I have lost the trick of them. The first surveyor signed in the spring of '94, a year after me, a kind man named Aldous Crane, and he is still here. You may see him in the mirrors. He dusts the long gallery. He has been dusting it for a very long time. When the signature is struck through, the world strikes the man through with it, and the house keeps what the world releases.*

*Do not let them strike your name through. I do not know how to prevent it. I only know that it happens, and that Crane's face in the glass is very calm, and that calm, in that place, is the most frightening thing I have ever seen.*

*If you are reading this, you have found the drawer, which means the house wanted you to find it, which means it is time, which means I am nearly empty and the house is nearly full, and I am sorry. I am sorry the way one is sorry for the weather. I did not make the storm. I only stood in the window too long, wanting.*

*Grief is the door. That is all I have learned, and it is not enough, and I give it to you anyway. It came because I was empty. It will stay as long as there is someone to empty. Whatever you do—sign or burn the house or run to the ends of the county—do not grieve it here. Do not stand in its windows wanting. It learns wanting faster than anything.*

*—Edith Hollowmere, who was Edith Crane before, and whose mother's face she can no longer remember, though she is looking at it now, in the glass, in the kitchen that is no longer hers, and it is smiling at her with her own smile, one second late.*

* * *

The lamp in the study had come on without Jude touching it. He stood looking at it for a long moment, the small yellow globe of it, and decided—not decided, filed, the way he filed everything, in the drawer of things that would be dealt with later—that the wiring must be on some old timer, some early automatic system, the kind of thing a house like this would have.

His hands were cold. He noticed this the way he noticed everything now, from a slight distance, a man observing a man.

He read the letter twice more. The second time, he read it slowly, the way he read contracts, which was the only way he knew how to read anything that could take something from him. And certain sentences began to arrange themselves in his head like furniture being carried into a room:

*It feeds on the kept thing.*

*The surveyor signs because signing is the natural end of the work.*

*When the signature is struck through, the world strikes the man through with it.*

Eleven documents. Eleven straight lines. He thought of the half-struck signature on the 1931 page—Pemberton, the line beginning at the P and stopping—and he thought: *someone interrupted it.* Someone had pulled the pen away, or pulled the man away, or pulled something away, and Pemberton had—what? Escaped? Been kept anyway? Was there a Pemberton somewhere in the glass, dusting a room forever, his erasure only half-finished, like a word smudged but legible?

And beneath all of it, quiet and cold and certain, the thought he had been not-thinking since the radiator cover:

*My mother sang to drown out the noises. And one night she stopped singing. And I never asked why.*

The house around him was warm. It was always warm. It had been warm since the moment he arrived, dustless and lit and expectant, a house where someone was waiting, and Jude stood in Edith Hollowmere's study with a hundred-and-thirty-year-old letter shaking very slightly in his hands and understood that he knew, now, the exact quality of that warmth, because he had grown up inside it.

*It is filling the house with me.*

He looked up at the covered mirror above the fireplace. The linen sheet hung perfectly still. Nothing in the room was moving, nothing in the house was moving, and yet Jude had the absolute, bodily conviction—as clear as cold water, as close as breath on his neck—that somewhere on the other side of that white cloth, something had just finished reading over his shoulder.

And that it had learned, from watching him, exactly how a man's hands shake when he is afraid.

From the hall below, faint and patient, came the sound of the front door unlocking itself.
