## 12. The Price Itemized

The contract lay on the study desk like something recently dead.

Jude had seen hundreds of documents in his career—deeds, easements, title transfers, the dry liturgy of property law—and he had learned to read them the way a butcher reads an animal: quickly, professionally, noting only what mattered. He picked up Marguerite's papers expecting more of the same. A standard sale agreement. A surveyor's certification page. Perhaps some unusual clause about the mirrors, some lawyer's euphemism for the house's strangeness.

He should have known better by now. Nothing at Hollowmere was ever quite what it appeared to be, and nothing here was ever *only* what it appeared to be.

The paper was heavy stock, cream-colored, older than it had any right to be. The topmost sheet was typed on a machine that predated Jude's career by decades, the letters slightly uneven, the ink gone brown at the edges. But it was the second sheet that stopped his breath—the one beneath, visible through the thinness of the first, its lines showing through like bones under skin.

He lifted the top page away.

The document underneath was handwritten. Not typed. Written in a spidery, patient hand that Jude recognized from the ledger in the study—from the appraisal records going back to 1893. The same hand had written every entry, every signature line, every struck-through name. It was as if the contract itself had been waiting, unchanged, while generations of typewriters came and went around it.

*Terms of Keeping*, the heading read.

Jude sat down. His chair did not scrape; the carpet swallowed the sound, and he had the sudden, irrational certainty that the house preferred him seated, preferred him still, preferred him reading.

*The Keeper shall hold the House entire,* the first clause began, *together with all grounds, rooms, corridors, and reflections appurtenant thereto.*

*Reflections appurtenant.* He read the phrase three times. In ordinary conveyancing, *appurtenances* meant the fixtures that ran with a property—the doorframes, the mantels, the things too much a part of the house to be separated from it. Whoever drafted this had classified the mirrors' contents as part of the real estate. As if what lived behind the glass were a fixture. As if it could be conveyed, transferred, owned.

*The Keeper shall dwell within the House and shall suffer no harm therein,* the second clause continued, *for so long as the Keeping is maintained.*

*So long as.* Jude's eyes narrowed. Every generous sentence in this document contained a hinge, and every hinge opened onto darkness.

*In consideration of the foregoing,* said the third clause, and here the handwriting seemed to press harder into the paper, the ink thicker, almost wet-looking despite its age, *the Keeper shall render unto the Visitor one memory of the Keeper's own life upon each anniversary of the signing.*

One memory. Per year.

Jude sat back. Forty years, he thought. Marguerite had said she'd kept the visitor fed for forty years. Forty memories. It sounded survivable—a tax, not a slaughter. Who would miss forty scattered moments out of a lifetime? A bad holiday. A dull afternoon. Some forgotten Tuesday.

Then he read the next line.

*The memories rendered shall be selected by the Visitor, and the Visitor shall select according to its nature, which is hunger, and hunger takes first what is most held.*

Hunger takes first what is most held.

Jude read it again, and felt the floor tilt gently beneath his chair—not the house moving, but his understanding of it. The visitor didn't take random memories. It took the ones you loved most. The ones you returned to. The ones you carried close and warmed yourself against in the cold hours. Those were the fattest memories, the richest ones, the ones worth eating first.

Marguerite no longer remembered her husband's face.

Not because forty years had worn it away. Because the visitor had taken it—perhaps in the very first year, perhaps the very first month—because it was the memory she held most dear, and therefore the first course.

And then it had taken the next most held. And the next. And the next. Forty years of Marguerite's love, eaten in order of devotion, until what remained was a woman who could remember the terms of a property contract perfectly but could not summon her own wedding day.

Jude looked at the final clause, and his mouth had gone dry before he reached it.

*Upon the exhaustion of the Keeper's held memories, the Keeping shall pass to the Keeper's reflection, and the Keeper shall be received into the House as staff.*

Staff. The struck-through names. The figures he'd seen moving in the mirrors, dusting rooms that existed only behind glass, polishing banisters in a hallway that was one second late. They hadn't died. They hadn't fled. They had simply been emptied—eaten down to the rind—and when there was nothing left to take, the house had found a use for what remained.

*What remains after everything loved is taken,* Jude thought, *is staff.*

* * *

He set the pages down carefully, aligning their edges, because his hands wanted something precise to do and precision was the last armor he had left.

Outside the study window—or where the study window should have been—he could see evening light lying across the lawn. Or what he told himself was evening light. He had stopped checking whether the windows showed the night. Checking cost something, and he was learning to ration himself.

Forty memories a keeper. He turned the arithmetic over in his mind, looking for a loophole, because loopholes were how he had survived his childhood—if the noise was pipes, then pipes were safe; if the footsteps upstairs were settling wood, then wood was safe; if his mother's silences were tiredness, then tiredness was safe. Find the rational explanation and you could go on living inside the irrational one.

But the arithmetic offered no mercy. Even if a person surrendered only one memory a year—even if the visitor were patient, even if it grazed rather than gorged—it would take a lifetime to exhaust a life. That was the design. That was the elegance of it. The contract didn't kill its keepers. It *outlasted* them. It let them live whole decades in the house, comfortable, unharmed, *suffering no harm therein*, while it ate them from the inside out in order of love, saving the deepest meals for last, savoring.

And Marguerite had signed this willingly. Had begged him, tonight, with her papery hands and her pitying eyes, to sign it after her.

Because the alternative—the thing Jude had glimpsed at midnight when the front door opened onto the same hallway behind him—was worse. The alternative was running, and being followed home through his own reflection, or staying and refusing, and becoming staff ahead of schedule.

There was no clause for walking away. He'd looked. He'd read the document twice now, front and back, holding the pages up to the lamp, and the contract acknowledged only two states of being: keeper, and kept.

* * *

His mother's face rose in him unbidden, and for once he did not push it down.

She had been beautiful in the way of women who are tired most of the time—lovely in flashes, between yawns, in the lamplight of their small kitchen when she forgot herself and laughed. He remembered—no. He *held* that memory. Held it the way the contract meant the word. He returned to it. He warmed himself against it. On the worst nights of his adult life, in cheap flats with thin walls, he had lain awake and played it back: the two of them singing in the kitchen to drown out the noises in the walls, her voice deliberately off-key so his would sound better, her hand keeping time on the counter, flour dust hanging in the yellow light like something holy.

That was the memory the visitor would take first. If he signed. If he became keeper. It wouldn't start with his mortgage paperwork or his university exams or the name of his dentist. Hunger takes first what is most held. It would open with his mother's voice, off-key and warm, and it would swallow that, and then it would come for whatever he loved next, and next, and next, working inward like frost through a wall, until one day he stood in this study knowing that he had once loved something desperately and could no longer remember what it was.

He thought about Marguerite saying *I no longer remember my husband's face*, and how she had said it without horror. That was the cruelest part. Not the losing—the not-minding. She had been eaten so thoroughly that even her grief had been digested. She pitied *him*. She thought she was offering him a bargain.

And the worst question, the question the contract forced into the open like a splinter working toward the skin: what *would* he pay to keep existing?

Because that was the actual transaction. Not money. Not the fee sitting in escrow, absurdly large, the commission that had lured him here. The currency was himself—the specific, irreplaceable accumulation of a life. And the contract made the price look gentle. One memory a year. You'd barely notice, at first. You'd have decades before the hunger reached anything vital. You could tell yourself, every anniversary, *this one doesn't matter, I have others*—right up until the year you reached for your mother's kitchen and found the wall bare.

He had spent his whole life surviving by not asking questions. Don't ask about the noises. Don't ask where his mother went that last night, why the song stopped mid-verse, why the house afterward felt emptier in a way that had nothing to do with furniture. Not asking had kept him alive. Not asking had been his roof and his coat.

But the contract was asking. The contract demanded, in its brown old ink, that he ask himself the only question that mattered: how much of Jude Vane was Jude Vane willing to lose in order for Jude Vane to continue?

And beneath it, quieter, a second question he almost couldn't bear: had his mother signed something like this? Was that what the silence mid-song had been—not a trade she'd made for herself, but a *keeping*? Had she fed something, year after year, so that the noises in the walls would stay in the walls, so that her son could sleep? Had he grown up inside someone else's payments, a child sheltered by his mother's slowly emptying self?

He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw constellations.

* * *

When he lowered his hands, the lamp had dimmed—or the room had moved closer to the dark—and the great mirror on the study's far wall had uncovered itself. He didn't remember it being exposed. He didn't remember the study having a mirror at all, though of course it did; every room in Hollowmere held exactly one, each covered, each waiting.

In the glass, the study reflected faithfully: the desk, the lamp, the contract spread open, the man sitting motionless with his hands flat on the paper as if taking its pulse.

Except that in the reflection, the contract's pages were turning. Slowly. By themselves. The reflected Jude sat with his hands in his lap, watching, while the reflected document leafed through itself like something browsing a menu.

Jude looked down at the real pages. Still. Face-up. Untouched.

He looked back at the mirror. The reflected pages had stopped at the third clause—the consideration—and the reflected ink of that clause seemed darker than the rest, wetter, as though the glass were showing him not the document but the document's appetite.

*Hunger takes first what is most held.*

It wasn't warning him. He understood that with a cold, clarifying certainty. It was *itemizing*. Somewhere behind the glass, something was going through his life the way he went through a property on survey day—room by room, assessing value, flagging the assets. His mother's kitchen. Her off-key voice. Whatever else he loved and had never said aloud, because saying it aloud would make it easier to find.

The lag was closing. He knew that too. This morning the mirror had run a full second behind him; tonight, in this glass, the reflected Jude's chest rose and fell in time with his own. Perfectly synchronized. No delay at all.

The copy was nearly finished rehearsing him. Which meant the house was nearly finished pricing him.

Jude stood, and the reflected Jude stood with him, exactly, instantly, and for the first time the synchronization felt less like fluency than like a hand closing around his wrist.

He gathered the contract pages together, squared them, and set them in the center of the desk beneath the lamp—where they would be ready at dawn, because some traitorous practical part of him insisted on readiness even now.

Then he took out his notebook, clicked his pen, and wrote, in his small surveyor's hand, the only honest entry the night had produced so far:

*Item: one (1) soul, occupied. Condition: fair. Encumbrances: total.*

He stared at the words. Then, below them, without deciding to, he added:

*Mother — kitchen — song. Do not let it be first.*

The pen trembled on the final stroke. Behind him, in the mirror, the reflected Jude was also writing—but the reflected notebook showed different words entirely, a single line in that old spidery hand, and though the glass was too far away to read it clearly, Jude already knew what it said.

It said: *Received with thanks.*
