## 20. Appraisal Complete

The morning after Hollowmere let him go, Jude Vane sat at his kitchen table with a thermos of coffee going cold beside him and a blank appraisal form laid out flat, and found that his hands would not stop shaking.

Not from fear. He had expected fear. He had budgeted for it the way he budgeted for everything—two parts dread, one part denial, a contingency reserve of whisky for when the other two ran dry. But what he felt instead was something closer to the stillness after a long illness breaks: an exhaustion so complete it had passed through pain and come out the other side as clarity.

He had signed the papers at first light. He had written his name backwards, letter by letter, in a hand that did not look like his own and yet was more his own than anything he'd ever written—the hand of a man who had spent one long night learning, finally, to look directly at himself. The contract had taken it. He had felt it take it, the way you feel a key turn in a lock behind you: a small, definite click, and then a silence where a hunger used to be.

And Marguerite Ashe had died smiling on the divan in the east parlour, her hands folded, her face turned toward the window where the sun was coming up over the drowned garden, and she had said—or he had read it in the settling of her features, because her voice was gone by then—*Thomas. You kept your hat on all through the ceremony. I never told you I noticed.*

He had covered her gently with the dust sheet from the piano. It seemed right. Houses should learn that much, at least, from the people who keep them.

Now there was work to do. There was always work to do; that was the one lesson of his childhood that had proved durable and even, in its way, kind. When the noises came, you catalogued. When the questions rose in your throat like water, you measured something instead. His mother had taught him without meaning to, and the house had finished the lesson, and for thirty-eight years the habit had kept him small and safe and unseen.

But invisibility, he had learned at Hollowmere, was not the same as safety. It was only the same as being last.

So this morning he would do the opposite of what every surveyor before him had done. He would look at everything. He would write down everything. And he would make sure that no one—not a bank, not a buyer, not some grieving widow fifty years from now with an empty house and a desperate heart—would ever be given a reason to walk through that front door.

* * *

The drive back out to Hollowmere took forty minutes along roads that seemed ordinary in daylight, which was its own kind of obscenity. Hedgerows. A tractor. A school bus idling at a junction, children's faces pressed to the glass. The world going about its business a few miles from a house where mirrors had learned to breathe.

Jude parked at the end of the drive rather than up by the door. Some instinct told him not to bring the car any closer than necessary, and he had decided, somewhere in the small hours, to start trusting those instincts instead of explaining them away. That was the whole of the new discipline, really. Not courage. Just attention, honestly rendered.

The house stood in the flat grey-gold light looking—smaller. That was the first thing. All night it had seemed to expand around him, corridors lengthening to accommodate mirrors that hadn't existed at dusk, staircases multiplying like roots. Now, in the sun, it was only a large Victorian pile gone slightly green at the sills, its slate roof patched in three places, its chimneys leaning a degree or two off plumb. A house. An old house with old problems.

Except for the windows. Every pane of glass in the façade reflected nothing at all—not sky, not trees, not his own approaching figure. They were dark the way deep water is dark, and when he moved along the front of the building, they did not track him. They simply were, the way stones are.

He stood on the gravel for a full minute, clipboard under his arm, and let himself feel it. Grief, mostly. That surprised him. He had known the house for less than two days and hated most of it, and still some part of him mourned—because whatever else Hollowmere had been, it had been *attentive*. For one night, something in the universe had looked at Jude Vane completely, had studied him with a patience no living person had ever spared him, and even though the attention had been the attention of a thing deciding how best to wear him, there had been a terrible comfort in being seen. He understood now why Marguerite had stayed forty years. Why they all had.

Then he climbed the steps, let himself in with the key she'd left him—she'd left it under the third flowerpot from the left, an arrangement so mundane it nearly made him laugh—and began the survey.

* * *

He started, as procedure demanded, at the exterior, and worked clockwise. He measured the elevations. He photographed the subsidence crack running diagonally from the northeast corner window, wide enough at its worst point to admit two fingers. He noted the slipped roof tiles, the failed flashing around the chimney stacks, the rainwater goods corroded through at four separate joints, the damp course bridged by soil on the west elevation, the timber lintel over the scullery door soft with rot—a penknife went into it half an inch without resistance.

All true. All ordinary. And he wrote each defect down in his careful surveyor's hand with a gratitude that would have looked, to anyone watching, like devotion. Because every flaw was a nail in the coffin. Every rotted joist and buckled gutter was a line in a report that would make a lender flinch and an insurer close the file. The house had spent a century making itself beautiful to the people it wanted inside. Jude intended to spend today making it ugly to everyone else.

Inside, the work grew stranger.

The hall was exactly as he'd left it—the great mirror above the fireplace dark and depthless, the parquet scuffed, the air holding none of the dust that should have accumulated in a sealed house. He measured the hall. He measured the mirror, because the form required glazing to be itemized, and he wrote: *One (1) wall mirror, cast glass, approx. 1890s. Non-reflective. Recommend removal.* Then he crossed out *recommend removal*, because recommending implied someone might act on it, might come here with tools and gloves and a van, and he could not in conscience send anyone into this room. He wrote instead: *Do not remove. Do not cover. Do not look into it longer than necessary.* He knew how it would read. He knew it would be flagged as unprofessional, possibly unhinged. He wrote it anyway, and beneath it, after a moment: *I am the surveyor of record. My name is on this page. Everything in this report is true.*

The truth was the only weapon he had left, and he meant to use all of it.

Room by room, floor by floor, he worked through the day. The drawing room: ceiling plaster cracked in a star pattern around the light fitting, sash windows painted shut, and one mirror, dark, which he measured and described and did not linger before. The library: dry rot in the floorboards beneath the bay window, bookshelves bowing under the weight of a collection he appraised at nil because the form asked for value and the books' value was not the point. The study, where the ledgers lived—he photographed every page, every struck-through name, before he let himself touch anything else. Ellery Vance, 1921. Thomas Ashe, 1953. Ruth Calloway, 1979. Half a name begun in 1987 and abandoned mid-letter, the ink trailing off as if the hand writing it had been called away. He scanned them all and emailed the files to himself from the drive, twice, and saved copies to a memory stick he put in his breast pocket, and another he taped under the dashboard of his car. If anyone ever came asking—if anyone ever tried to say none of this happened—there would be a record. Names deserved records. Even names that had been eaten.

In the east parlour, the dust sheet still lay over the shape on the divan. He had called the local police at seven that morning from the end of the lane, where his phone found a signal, and reported finding the owner deceased, natural causes, please attend. Two constables had come out while he worked upstairs; he heard their radios, their careful boots, their low voices doing the arithmetic of an old woman dead in a cold house. One of them found him in the nursery—of course there was a nursery; of course the house had prepared one—and asked if he was all right.

"Fine," Jude said. "Long job."

The constable looked at him a moment longer than the answer required. "Place give you the creeps? Old houses do, sometimes."

Jude considered the question with more seriousness than the young man could know. Down the corridor, a mirror reflected a hallway containing nothing, reflecting nothing, forever.

"It gave me a great deal," he said. "Mostly I'm glad to be done with it."

* * *

By late afternoon he reached the valuation section, and there he stopped, pen hovering, and made himself think it through properly, because this was the part that mattered. This was the part that would outlive him.

A standard appraisal reduced a property to comparables: recent sales of similar houses, adjusted for condition, location, land. Hollowmere had eleven acres, good road frontage, a sound basic structure beneath the decay. By the numbers, by the cold arithmetic of his profession, it was worth something. Perhaps a great deal, to the right buyer—the kind of buyer who saw dereliction and thought *project*, who walked through rot and smelled money, who loved a house the way Marguerite's predecessor had loved hers: as a vessel to be filled.

That was precisely the buyer the house was waiting for. Grief didn't always look like tears. Sometimes it looked like ambition. Sometimes it looked like a couple standing in a dusty hall saying *we can fix this, we can make it ours,* and the house listening with all its dark glass, and the visitor folding its hands and waiting to be invited.

Jude wrote:

*Valuation: NIL.*

*This property is uninhabitable and uninsurable. Structural defects are extensive and progressive. The undersigned surveyor further notes conditions observed during inspection which cannot be remedied by any works at any cost, and which render the property unfit for occupation or transfer. The undersigned recommends against sale, mortgage, insurance, or entry by any party for any purpose. This recommendation is absolute.*

He knew what would happen to the report. It would go back to the firm that had commissioned it—Hallow & Finch, whose letterhead Marguerite had used for decades, whose partners had perhaps never questioned why the Ashe appraisal renewed itself every generation like a lease. It would be challenged. He would be asked to justify a nil valuation on a property with eleven acres of Somerset land. He would lose the fee. He might lose the career; firms did not keep surveyors who wrote *do not enter* in the defects column.

He had already decided about the fee. It sat in his account on deposit pending completion—eight thousand pounds, the largest single payment of his working life, the money he'd needed so badly he hadn't asked why the terms were strange. He transferred it back that evening from his phone, sitting on the front step of Hollowmere in the last of the light, and typed a one-line note to Hallow & Finch: *Fee declined. Report speaks for itself. — J.V.*

Some prices should end the market. That was the sentence he kept turning over as he packed his instruments away, coil by coil, case by case. A house that fed on grief should not have a price, because a price was an invitation, and an invitation was all it had ever needed. Let it stand empty. Let the land go to scrub and the roof go to moss. Let the only people who ever approached it be vandals and weather, neither of whom signed anything.

Before he left, he did one last thing. He walked the ground floor and opened every curtain, every shutter, letting the evening in. Then he stood in the hall, in front of the great dark mirror, and spoke aloud—because he was done with silence, done with the old survival, and because if anything of the visitor remained, folded into the contract it couldn't parse, it deserved to hear the terms of its defeat stated plainly.

"The house is valued at nothing," he said. His voice sounded strange and large in the stillness. "No one is coming. No one will ever be invited. Whatever's left of you—you can stop rehearsing now. There's nothing here to copy."

The mirror held its darkness. But as he turned to go, he thought—only thought, and would never swear to it—that the darkness eased, very slightly, the way a fist eases. The way a held breath lets go.

He locked the front door and posted the key through the letterbox. Let the house keep its own key. It had earned that much, and it would need nothing else from anyone, ever again.

* * *

The report filed on Friday. On Monday, Hallow & Finch called.

"You've cost us the Ashe commission," said Mr. Prosser, a partner with a voice like wet gravel. "And quite possibly your position. A nil valuation, Vane? With a paragraph telling insurers to stay away in language I can't repeat to my wife? What exactly did you find out there?"

Jude sat in his kitchen with the framed page on the wall behind him—the backwards signature, *enav ejduj*, curling across the contract in ink that had dried black as tar—and chose his words with care. Not lies. He was done with lies too, even the small protective ones. But the whole truth was a door, and he had learned what came through doors.

"Structural failure throughout," he said. "Damp, rot, subsidence. And a history you should read before you argue with me. I've attached the ledgers. Ask yourself why the same house has been appraised every twenty-five years since 1893, and why every surveyor's name is struck through afterward, and why nobody at your firm ever wondered."

Silence on the line. Long enough that he heard the office behind Prosser: phones, keyboards, a kettle. The ordinary world, humming.

"There'll be an inquiry," Prosser said at last, heavily. "You understand that."

"I understand."

"And you won't revise?"

"No."

Another silence, and then—quietly, in a voice that had stepped off the partner's register and become just a tired man's voice—"My grandfather did a survey once, out that way. Ashcombe direction. Family never talked about it." A pause. "Keep your report accurate, Vane. I'll see it stands."

The call ended. Jude set the phone down and looked at his hands. Steady now. Had been steady since the sunrise, as if the shaking had been the house's all along, borrowed fear returned to its owner.

Over the following weeks the world processed Hollowmere the way the world processes everything: slowly, bureaucratically, and then not at all. The insurers declined. A developer who'd made enquiries withdrew after reading the report—Jude checked, and found the man had bought elsewhere within the month, some modern place with south-facing glass, and wished him well with a sincerity that surprised him. The council listed the grounds as a potential conservation site, which meant no construction, no subdivision, no reason for anyone to stand on that land and admire the view. The house sank into the public record as a problem property, a liability, a footnote. Unsellable. Uninsurable. Unwanted.

Which was, Jude thought, the kindest thing that had ever happened to it.

He visited once more, in November, just to look. From the end of the drive—he did not go up to the door; there was no need, and doors were doors—the house looked smaller again, and greyer, frost furring the dead flowerbeds, ivy beginning its patient work on the west wall. In the downstairs windows, the dark panes caught the winter light for the first time and threw it back dull and flat, like the eyes of a thing sleeping. Or a thing finally, truly, empty.

He stood there a while in the cold, hands in his pockets, and let himself say goodbye—to Marguerite, who had kept a hungry thing fed for forty years out of a love she couldn't remember; to the struck-through names in the study, Ellery and Thomas and Ruth and the half-finished one, wherever they moved now through rooms that reflected nothing; even to the visitor, which had only ever been loneliness wearing a shape it copied from the grieving. Then he got in the car and drove home, and did not look in the rear-view mirror until the house was fully out of sight behind the hedge.

When he did look, it showed only the road behind him, empty, exactly one second ago.

Exactly on time.

* * *

It was three weeks later, on an ordinary Tuesday evening in December, that Jude heard the noise.

He was washing dishes in his flat—the small top-floor place he'd rented for six years, the place with the radiator that knocked and the neighbour's television murmuring through the wall—and he heard, from the direction of the bedroom, a sound he knew with the whole of his body. A settling. Wood shifting against wood, slow and deliberate, the way a house makes when it is adjusting itself around the weight of someone new.

His hands went still in the water.

The old reflex rose instantly, smooth as water rising in a throat: *pipes. The radiator. Old buildings settle.* Thirty-eight years of explanations, queued and ready, each one worn soft with use. He could feel how easy it would be—say the word, dry the plate, turn on the radio, be small and safe and unseen until morning.

Instead he shut off the tap.

He dried his hands. He walked, unhurried, down the short hall to the bedroom, where the full-length mirror stood in the corner—the cheap one, bevelled glass, bought secondhand when he moved in. The lamp was on. The room was warm. Nothing in it had changed, except that everything in it was about to matter differently.

Jude stood in front of the glass and looked at himself—directly, fully, the way he'd practiced all night in a house that had taught him how—and he said, out loud, in the ordinary voice of an ordinary man at the end of an ordinary day:

"Hello. My name is Jude Vane. I live here."

And his reflection, perfectly on time, said it back.
