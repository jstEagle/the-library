## 2. One Second Late

The trouble with being the kind of person who photographs doorways is that you eventually photograph something that photographs you back.

I didn't know that yet, though. That Tuesday morning I knew only three things, in ascending order of importance: the coffee was burnt, the light through the bathroom window was doing that pale October thing where it makes everything look like evidence, and my reflection was late.

I want to be precise about "late," because precision is the only prayer I have. I raised my right hand to fix my hair. My reflection raised its hand a beat afterward — not the way a mirror lags when you're tired and your eyes are lying, not the smear of motion you get in cheap glass, but a clean, discrete delay. My hand went up. A pause, thin as a coin's edge. Then the hand in the mirror went up, following my movement like a student copying a teacher who'd already turned the page.

I lowered my hand. It lowered a beat later.

I stood there with my hair unfixed and my heart doing something arrhythmic and stupid, and I did what I always do when the world misfiles itself: I got out my phone and started documenting.

* * *

Here is what you should know about me, since you're stuck in my head: I count things. Exits, doors, stairs, the seconds between a lightning flash and its thunder. My mother used to say I was born with a tally in one hand and a map in the other, and she said it the way people say things that are half complaint and half epitaph. When I was nine I mapped our apartment building — all four floors, all thirty-one doors, including the one in the basement that opened onto nothing but cinder block and had been painted shut since before I was born. I gave copies to the neighbors. Most of them were charmed. Mr. Okonkwo in 2C framed his.

So when my reflection misbehaved, I didn't scream or run or do any of the things a sensible person does. I ran an experiment. Sensibility is just fear wearing a lab coat, and I have always been well dressed.

The metronome app on my phone is the free kind, the kind with ads for language learning and one review from a man named Gerald who gave it two stars because "it keeps perfect time, which is unsettling." Gerald, buddy, you have no idea.

I set it to sixty beats per minute. One click per second. I stood in front of the mirror, phone held at chest height, and I raised my hand on a click.

The reflection raised its hand on the next click.

I lowered on a click. It lowered on the next.

One full second. Exactly one. Not 0.9, not 1.1 — I ran it eleven times, filmed it, scrubbed the video frame by frame, and the delay was one second, flat, like a note held by someone with perfect pitch and terrible intentions.

I did the thing where you tell yourself the reasonable explanations. Rolling shutter. Frame-rate artifact. The mirror is old; old glass has waves in it; waves do things. I said all of this out loud to myself in the bathroom, which, I will note, meant I was saying it to the mirror, which meant I was saying it to *it*, and it stood there listening with my face and my burnt-coffee morning expression and did not interrupt me once.

Mirrors usually interrupt you. That's the whole transaction. You make a face, it makes the face. That's the contract. This thing had renegotiated the contract without a lawyer present.

I filmed it anyway. Fourteen minutes of footage. In the video, you can see me — the real me, the phone-side me — doing increasingly absurd things: waving, snapping my fingers, mouthing the alphabet. And the other me following along one second behind, patient as tide.

Here's the detail I keep returning to, the one that sits in my chest like a swallowed stone. At minute nine, I stopped moving. I just stood still, watching. The reflection stood still too — but for one second longer than I did. When I finally turned away, it turned away a second after, and in the last frames of the video, before the camera swings down to the sink, the reflection's eyes are already pointed back at the camera.

At me.

Not at where I had been. At where I would be standing when it caught up.

* * *

I did what any meticulous person does with an anomaly: I tested the boundaries. I checked every other reflective surface in the house like a doctor palpating for a tumor.

Kitchen window: normal. I moved; I moved in it. No delay. The copper pot hanging over the stove: normal, if unflattering. The little round mirror in the hallway with the beveled edge that my landlord swears is original to the house: normal.

Only the bathroom mirror lagged. Only that one, one second behind, growing no faster or slower, keeping time like Gerald's metronome.

That should have been comforting. One anomaly, isolated, quantifiable. That's a good anomaly. That's an anomaly you can write up. But comfort requires believing the anomaly is random, and nothing about this felt random. The bathroom mirror is the one I stand in front of every morning. It's the one that sees my face most. If something in this house were learning to be me, it would start there.

I put a towel over it. This felt like a victory for approximately four hours.

* * *

By evening I had a spreadsheet. Of course I had a spreadsheet. Columns: *Time, Surface, Lag (s), Notes*. The notes column started clinical — "hand raise, 1.0s," "blink test, 1.0s" — and deteriorated over the hours into things like "smiled at me when I hadn't smiled" and "DO NOT UNCOVER" and, at 11:47 p.m., simply, "why."

Because here's what happened at 11:47. I'd been measuring every twenty minutes, and the lag had held at 1.0 all day — steady, flat, a plateau. I lifted the towel expecting the same. I raised my hand on the click.

The reflection raised its hand one and a quarter seconds later.

I measured again. 1.25. Again. 1.3. Over the next ninety minutes it climbed — 1.3, 1.4, 1.5 — not smoothly, but in steps, like something climbing stairs. Like something that had been holding its breath all day and had finally decided to exhale.

The lag was growing. It had been growing all along, I realized — the flat 1.0 wasn't a plateau, it was a decision. Something had been sitting at one second the way a predator sits at the edge of a campfire's light, and now it had taken a step closer, and another, and the steps had a rhythm to them that I refused, that night, to name.

I named it anyway, later, in the dark, staring at the ceiling: the rhythm was patience. Not the patience of a machine. The patience of a thing that knows exactly how much time it has, and knows that it has more of it than I do.

* * *

I didn't sleep much. Around two in the morning I got up for water and caught myself, out of pure habit, glancing toward the bathroom. The door was shut. The towel was inside, over the glass, doing its useless brave work.

And I want to tell you I went back to bed like a rational adult. What I actually did was stand in the hallway for a long time, listening to my own house make its small nighttime sounds — the furnace ticking, the pipes settling, the old Brightwater bones of the place shifting the way old houses shift, which I had always found cozy and now found conspiratorial — and I thought about the cellar.

I'd mapped the cellar the first week I moved in. It's a small, honest cellar: stone foundation, boiler, shelves of the previous tenants' abandoned paint cans. Four walls, one door, no windows. I have the map in a binder with the rest of them, drawn in my tidy hand, labeled and dated.

Four walls. One door.

I counted them again in my head, lying in the dark, and for the first time since I was nine years old, the count came out wrong. Not the walls — the *dimensions*. The cellar, by my own survey, is eleven feet by nine. I have stood in it. I have measured it. But the stair down to it, I suddenly realized, has fourteen steps, and fourteen steps at seven inches a rise is a drop of just over eight feet, and the ceiling down there is barely seven, and eleven plus nine plus eight does not fit inside the footprint of this house the way it should. There was a discrepancy in my own map. A pocket of the house that was bigger inside than the house allowed. A space with no name on my survey, no door in my photographs, a volume of dark that my meticulous little binder had papered over with an assumption.

Something down there had room for a stairwell. Or a well. Or whatever a house like this keeps the way other houses keep a furnace.

The towel over the mirror suddenly seemed like a very small gesture. Like putting a Band-Aid on a dam.

I lay there until the birds started, doing the arithmetic over and over, getting the same wrong answer every time, and the last thing I thought before sleep finally took me was not *I should leave* or even *I should investigate.*

It was: *It waited until I was alone in the house to start falling behind.*

One second late. Growing. Patient. It had picked its day, its mirror, its person.

And tomorrow, I decided, watching the ceiling go gray, I was going to go down into that impossible cellar and count the steps myself — because if there was a stairwell behind that plaster that appeared on no survey, then whatever was in my mirror had come from somewhere, and I have never in my life been able to leave a door uncounted.
