## 7. Harvest Arithmetic

The ledger didn't want to be read. I want to be clear about that, because everything else in the Brightwater house had practically begged for attention — the false wall behind the plaster, the stairwell that appeared on no survey, the well chamber with its congregation of inward-angled mirrors. The house was a show-off. But the ledger, when I finally tracked it down to a flat file drawer in the county archive's basement, lay there like something playing dead.

It was bound in oxblood leather gone stiff as bark, and the clerk who fetched it handled it with two fingers, the way you'd handle a dead bird you'd found on your porch.

"Foundations and land grants, 1841 through 1860," she said. "You're the first person to request it since 1987."

"Who requested it then?"

She checked the card. "A Mrs. Aldous." She said it without inflection, but her eyes did a small thing — a flick toward the window, toward the direction of the Brightwater house, half a mile up the hill. "Local family. Old ones."

I carried the ledger to the reading table under the buzzing fluorescent tube and put on my white cotton gloves, because I am a meticulous person, because ritual is how I keep the world from sliding, and because some part of me — the part that had counted doors twice and gotten different numbers both times — thought maybe procedure could protect me. Gloves, camera, notebook. Measure twice. Photograph everything. That's the creed of every urban explorer worth their headlamp: document, don't disturb.

The house had already disturbed me plenty.

* * *

The early pages were boring in the way ledgers are boring, which is to say they were terrifying if you read them correctly. Entries for timber. Entries for mason's wages. Entries for lime and sand and ironwork. And then, threaded through the mundane commerce of building a house in 1843, entries that were not boring at all.

*Paid to H. Merrick, glazier, for glass — twelve panes, cut to no standard size. $40.*

Twelve panes. I counted mirrors in the well chamber once, before I'd learned better than to count anything in that house. Twelve.

*Paid to the same, for silvering, and for the keeping of the work secret. $25 additional.*

The keeping of the work secret. Not "for discretion," which is what you'd write if you'd done nothing wrong. "The keeping of" — as though secrecy were a physical substance, something that could be stored, maintained, fed.

And then, three pages later:

*Paid to E. Voss, for silence, one year. $60, renewable.*

Silence with a renewal clause. Silence as a subscription service. I sat back and laughed, one dry note that echoed off the archive shelves and embarrassed me. Because this was the thing I kept circling: the founders hadn't been madmen working in a fever. They'd been *administrators*. They'd itemized horror into a budget. There was something almost comforting in that recognition — evil, at least this vintage of it, ran on paperwork.

I photographed every page. My camera's shutter sounded very loud in the basement.

* * *

By the fourth hour I'd developed a system, which is my natural habitat. A spreadsheet in my notebook: dates in one column, payments in another, and a third column where I transcribed the recurring phrases. Glass. Silvering. Silence. The keeping of names.

That last one appeared only four times across nineteen years, always in October, always paid in cash with no vendor listed.

*October 1844. For the keeping of names, $100.*

*October 1851. For the keeping of names, $100.*

Four times in nineteen years. Four harvests. I thought about the faces I'd seen flicker in the waterless dark of the well — not dozens of them, I realized now. Dozens would have been merciful. What I'd seen was a sample. A representative selection. The way you don't empty a bank account all at once; you make withdrawals.

The arithmetic was starting to assemble itself in the back of my skull, cold and patient, and I hated it. I'm good at patterns. It's the whole basis of my competence. And the pattern here said: *this machine has an operating schedule.*

The last twenty pages of the ledger changed character entirely. The neat commercial hand gave way to a tighter script, cramped, urgent, the writing of someone hunched over the page by candlelight. No more dollars. Instead, instructions. Fragments. A recipe written by someone who kept losing their place.

*"…the moon full and risen past the elm line, the keeper standing at the glass…"*

*"…twelve reflections turned inward, each face given its second, each second given its—"*

Here the ink smeared, as if the page had been gripped by a wet hand.

*"…and the lag shall widen as the vessel fills, one breath, then two, until the keeper can no longer say which hand is his own, and at that sign the rite is ripe—"*

I stopped reading. I looked at my own hands, splayed on either side of the ledger, gloved in white cotton, and I made them both move — left fingers, right fingers, together, apart. Both hands obeyed. Both hands were mine.

But I thought about the bathroom mirror, and the metronome app, and the number I'd written in my phone three nights ago: **1.4 sec**. Growing. One breath, then two.

*"…and the keeper shall speak the closing words, and give to the well that which the well has earned, and the house shall stand finished, and the names shall be kept forever—"*

The final entry stopped mid-sentence. Mid-*word*, actually. The page ended with *"the closing words are these:"* followed by four blank lines and then nothing. No ink. Just the tooth of the paper, waiting for a sentence that never came.

Someone had interrupted this. Or something had. Either the founder died with the words still in his mouth, or he'd looked at what he was about to write and chosen, at the last possible moment, to leave the machine unfinished.

Either way: interrupted. Incomplete. A ritual with its keystone word missing, sitting under a house for a hundred and eighty years like a loaded gun with the safety half on.

And a gun, I knew from every locked building I'd ever slipped into, waits for a hand.

* * *

The archive closed at five. The clerk watched me return the ledger with the particular wariness people reserve for someone who came in cheerful and left quiet.

"Find what you needed?"

"I found what I found," I said, which wasn't an answer, and she nodded like it was.

Outside, the light was doing the thing October light does — long and amber and slightly dishonest, making everything look nostalgic for a version of itself that never existed. The Brightwater house sat on its hill above town, windows catching the sun, and I caught myself looking at those windows the way you look at a dog that has bitten someone. Waiting for movement. Knowing it's watching you back.

I walked home the long way, along the creek, because I wanted water that moved. Moving water doesn't hold a reflection long enough to lie about it.

That's when I did the math properly, sitting on the footbridge with my notebook open, and the math was worse than the ledger.

The full moon nearest the autumn equinox — the harvest moon — fell twelve days out. I checked twice, because checking twice is my whole personality, and because the alternative was trusting a single glance and I no longer trusted single glances of anything, including my own face.

Twelve days. And the ledger's fragments said the rite ripens when the lag widens — *one breath, then two.* My metronome app had logged 1.4 seconds three days ago. If the growth held steady — and I graphed it, because of course I graphed it, a little descending curve in blue ink that looked like a ski jump — the lag would reach two full seconds in eleven to thirteen days.

The mirror wasn't malfunctioning. It was *ripening*. The whole house was a fruit swelling toward one specific night, and the ledger told me what happened at ripeness: a keeper stands at the glass and speaks the closing words, and gives the well what it has earned.

*A living keeper.* That phrase recurred, or its cousins did — *the keeper standing,* *the keeper's breath,* *the keeper shall speak.* Every fragment assumed a person. Not a descendant, necessarily, not an heir — just whoever the house had. Whoever was standing closest when the moon crested.

Whoever had found the stairwell behind the plaster.

I sat on the footbridge and felt the countdown begin, not as a number but as a texture, a tightening, the way a drum skin tightens before it's played. Time itself had joined the other side. Every sunset from here to the harvest moon was a tick. I'd spent my adult life measuring things — doorways, exits, seconds — believing measurement was mastery, that a person who counted carefully could never be lost. Now the counting worked against me. The house had known I'd count. It had been built by people who understood that a meticulous person, shown a mystery with clean edges, will walk into it voluntarily, documenting every step.

I was not a trespasser in that house. I was an *ingredient* that had delivered itself.

* * *

Home by seven. Dusk coming earlier every day, the dark arriving like a creditor.

I went to the bathroom because I had to know, and knowing is the only prayer I've ever believed in. I opened the metronome app, set it to sixty beats per minute, propped the phone against the sink, and raised my right hand on the click.

The reflection raised its hand after.

One beat. Two—

On the second click, the mirrored hand was still rising. Still rising while my real hand had already begun to fall. I watched my double complete a motion I had finished a full lifetime ago — a full two seconds ago, which is nothing, which is an eternity, which is enough time to say a name or strike a match or decide something.

**2.0 sec.**

Right on schedule. Ahead of schedule, even. The ski-jump curve hadn't lied; time was complicit, and time was punctual.

I stood in the bathroom breathing through my mouth, because breathing through my nose meant fogging the mirror, and I did not want to give it a surface to write on. Two seconds. The ledger said at two seconds the keeper can no longer tell which hand is his own. I flexed my fingers. Mine obeyed. I was nearly sure.

Nearly sure was going to have to be enough for eleven days.

I backed out of the bathroom without turning around. You think that's superstition, and maybe it is, but I've also never once turned my back on an open elevator shaft, and I've been right about that every time.

In the kitchen I wrote the plan on paper instead of my phone, because my phone has a screen and screens reflect. Three columns, my handwriting small and square and steady, steadier than I felt.

*Column one: what finishes the ritual. The closing words — missing from the ledger. A keeper — me, apparently, by default of proximity. The harvest moon — twelve days.*

*Column two: what breaks it. Unknown. Possibly nothing. The ledger's author died — or stopped — before recording a failure mode, which tells me either there isn't one, or he didn't want it found.*

*Column three: who else knows. Mrs. Aldous, 1987, requested this exact ledger. Local family. Old ones.*

I stared at column three for a long time. Then I drew a box around it, because in thirty years of mapping inaccessible places I've learned one rule above all others: when you find evidence that someone got here before you and left no trace of what they concluded, that person is either dead, or hiding, or waiting.

The lamp flickered. Just once. Just enough.

And in the dark window over the sink — in the black glass that October had turned into a mirror for free — I saw myself sitting at the table, pen in hand, head bowed over the notebook.

Except my head wasn't bowed. I was looking straight at the window.

Two seconds later, my reflection lowered its gaze to the page, and caught up, and pretended it had always been there.

Twelve days. Eleven, really, by the time the moon rose. I picked up the pen and wrote one more line at the bottom of column three, pressing hard enough to emboss the next three pages:

*Find Mrs. Aldous. Tomorrow. Before it finds me.*
