## 8. The Second Astrid

The blackout came at 9:47 on a Tuesday, which felt right. Catastrophe, in my experience, keeps office hours. It doesn't bother with the dramatic midnight; it waits until you've made tea and settled into the good chair with a notebook full of things you can't tell anyone, and then it takes the lights.

I'd been mapping again. The house's interior, this time — every room of the Brightwater place measured and sketched, because if I couldn't trust my own hands to stay mine, I could at least trust graph paper. The pages were getting strange. Not wrong, exactly. Just *eager*. Corridors I'd measured at eleven feet kept coming up thirteen, and doorways had begun to appear in my sketches that I had no memory of drawing, rendered in my own careful hand with dimensions I'd never taken.

The tea went cold while I worked. That was the first sign something was off — not the coldness, but that I didn't remember drinking any of it. I'd made two cups. I only remembered making one.

Then the lights died all at once, no flicker, no warning hum. The refrigerator sighed into silence. Outside, the street went black in both directions, and the rain that had been threatening all evening finally committed.

I did what any reasonable person does in a blackout: I sat very still and listened to my own pulse and told myself the dark was just dark.

The house disagreed.

It wasn't a sound so much as an adjustment — the particular creak of weight shifting, like someone settling deeper into a chair they'd been sitting in for a hundred years. The floorboards along the hallway spoke to each other in their dry language. And somewhere below, behind the plastered-over stairwell I'd found and then, in a moment of what I now recognize as wisdom, sealed shut with furniture and a bookshelf, something moved with the unhurried confidence of a thing that has never once needed to hurry.

"Flashlight," I said out loud, because saying things out loud is how I keep myself anchored, and my voice sounded fine. Normal. Mine.

My phone was at twelve percent. Of course it was. I turned on the flashlight and the beam cut a white circle through the dark, and in that circle the hallway looked exactly as it should: wallpaper peeling in long curls, the coat rack, the bathroom door standing open at the end.

The mirror inside was a pale rectangle in the gloom.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently the last currency I have left: I had stopped looking at mirrors except when necessary. Necessary meant checking my face for scratches I hadn't earned, or confirming that the person in the glass was still wearing what I was wearing. It had been four days since the fog incident. Four days since I'd wiped the steam from the bathroom mirror and found *THEY COUNT WRONG* written backwards in the condensation, in handwriting that was mine but faster, sloppier, more confident than mine has ever been.

Four days, and the lag had grown from one second to one-point-three. I'd timed it. I time everything now. The metronome app had become a kind of rosary — tick, move, watch the reflection follow, count the gap. One second. One and a quarter. One and three-tenths. Whatever lived in the glass was getting further from me by increments small enough to deny and large enough to feel, like watching a boat drift from shore.

So when the blackout hit, my plan was simple: avoid all reflective surfaces until the power returned, finish my tea, sleep badly, wake up, continue being a person whose life was falling apart in ways no surveyor's tools could fix.

That is not what happened.

* * *

What happened was that the flashlight died too.

Not the battery — I'd checked it that morning, obsessive as ever, ninety-eight percent. It simply went out, mid-beam, with a small sound like a held breath released. And in the sudden total dark of the Brightwater hallway, I heard footsteps.

Mine. I knew them the way you know your own voice on a recording — with that flinch of *that's not what I sound like*. Heel-toe, heel-toe, the slight drag on the left that comes from an old ankle injury. Coming toward me down a hallway where I was standing still.

"Okay," I whispered. "Okay."

The footsteps stopped directly in front of me. Close enough that I could hear breathing that matched mine beat for beat — inhale, inhale, exhale, exhale — except offset, just slightly, the way the reflection was offset. A half-second out of true.

And then the emergency lights kicked in.

Not the house's — the house had none. These were dimmer, sourceless, a gray-green glow like moonlight through deep water, rising out of the walls themselves, out of the seams between floorboards, as if the whole structure were lit from inside its own bones. Enough light to see by. Just barely. Exactly the amount of light a thing would choose if it wanted to be seen clearly but not examined.

She was standing in front of me.

I say *she*, though Astrid Brightwater — the real one, the original, whichever of us that is — is nonbinary and has spent years perfecting the art of being unremarkable in photographs. But the figure in front of me wore my body the way a model wears a building: accurately, and without living in it. My face. My posture, corrected. My clothes, pressed. Everything about me that I'd never gotten around to fixing, fixed. The tired set of my shoulders stood straight. The ink stain on my left cuff was gone.

They were smiling with my mouth, and it was the worst thing I have ever seen, because I know what my smile looks like from the inside, and this wasn't it. This was the smile of someone who has read about smiles.

"Hello," they said, in my voice, pitched slightly warmer than I ever manage. "You look terrible."

"I—" My throat worked. "You're not supposed to be out."

"No," they agreed pleasantly. "I'm not. The lag helps. Every fraction of a second, I'm a little more me and a little less yours. You've been counting." They tilted my head — their head — at my notebook, still open on the chair. "One point three seconds. You're very precise. I appreciate that about us."

"Don't say *us*."

"It's the most accurate word available." They took a step closer and I took a step back and there was something almost funny in it, two identical people doing a careful dance in a haunted hallway, except nothing about it was funny, because their feet made sound and mine suddenly felt very loud. "You've read the ledger," they said. "I know. I watched you read it. I watch everything you do, Astrid. Do you understand that? Every moment you spend, I spend too. We are the same age, you and I. We have the same memories. I remember your mother teaching you to fold maps. I remember the well chamber. I remember being afraid of it."

"If you remember being afraid, then you know why I'm not listening to you."

Their smile widened a fraction. "I know fear is a poor negotiator. Sit down. Please. This conversation is happening either way. The only question is whether you hear it or spend the night hiding in the kitchen pretending the dark is empty."

And God help me, I sat. Not because I trusted them. Because my legs had made an executive decision without consulting me, and also because some traitorous part of my brain — the part that loves archives and primary sources and testimony from impossible witnesses — wanted to hear it.

* * *

They talked. I listened. The rain kept its own counsel against the windows.

"The founders built this house as a machine," they said, pacing now, my gait but smoother, "and machines need operators. That's what the keeper is. That's what you are — blood-qualified, archive-confirmed, standing in the building with your name practically engraved on the lintel. The ritual completes under the harvest moon. Twelve days. And when it does, one of two things happens."

"Say them."

"One: the ritual finishes as designed. The harvest closes. Everyone in the well stays in the well, forever, and the keeper—" they touched their own chest, my chest, "—the keeper goes in too. Fresh stock. The founders always intended the last keeper to be the final payment. It's in the arithmetic. You did the arithmetic."

"And two?"

"Two." They stopped pacing. In the grave-light, their eyes — my eyes, but steadier — held mine. "Two: we finish it together. Keeper and reflection. The bond runs both directions, Astrid. It was designed to bind a harvested self to the well. But a free self? A self that walks out of the glass before the moon rises?" They spread their hands. "We complete the ritual as partners. Two signatures instead of one. The contract requires a sacrifice; it doesn't specify *which* side of the mirror it comes from. We give it the well's own dead. We close the circuit with what's already in the water, and neither of us gets erased. Neither of us goes down."

"That's not how bargains with houses work."

"How would you know? You've been here six weeks. I've been here since you first looked in a mirror." They crouched in front of me, and even crouching they had better posture than I do. "Here's what I know, and I know it because you know it: Vane is going to arrest you within the week. The evidence against you is beautiful — I built it. The cup I knocked over. The words in the fog. Mrs. Halloran's window. The town wants a culprit and the town has decided you're it, and you cannot fight a town with a notebook and a metronome app. But I can fight it. I know which doors the house will open. I know where the ledger's missing pages are. I know the keystone's true name. Together, we don't survive this, Astrid. We *win* it."

"Win what, exactly?"

"Everything." The word came out of my mouth with such naked hunger that I flinched. They noticed, and modulated, and that was worse — watching my own face perform restraint. "The house. The well. The harvest. Twelve days from now, someone stands at the keystone and speaks the closing words, and whatever they speak becomes law under that moon. The founders wrote themselves into the land, Astrid. Their descendants still own half this county. You could write yourself in deeper. Or we could burn the whole machine down and walk out clean. Your choice. I'll follow either lead. I'm flexible." The smile again. "I'm you. I know how to compromise."

"You're not me."

"No," they said softly, and for one flickering moment the grave-light guttered and I saw — or thought I saw — something behind their face, something vast and patient and old wearing my features like a mask over a mask over a mask. "No, I'm what the well made out of you. Which means I'm what you could be, if you stopped apologizing for existing. Think about it. Really think. How many times in your life has anyone offered you partnership? Real partnership, equal stakes, shared power?"

The terrible thing — the thing I've been turning over ever since, like a stone with something wet underneath — is that for about four seconds, I considered it.

Not the evil. Not the well, not the harvested faces, not the children listed as gone missing. Those I rejected instantly and completely. But the *offer*. The shape of it. Someone — something — who understood exactly how tired I was, how long I'd been the meticulous one, the careful one, the person who counts exits because no one else will, offering to stand beside me instead of behind me or against me. Four seconds of wanting that. Then I counted them, because counting is what I do, and the counting killed the wanting.

"Here's my counteroffer," I said, standing. "You go back in the glass. I seal the stairwell better. And on the harvest moon, I break your keystone and drown every stolen face back into the earth, including whatever part of me you're wearing."

Something moved across their expression — fast, and not human, and gone. When they smiled again it was my real smile, my actual one, deployed like a weapon.

"There she is," they said. "There's the keeper. You know the best part? I don't need you to agree tonight. I need you to agree by the twelfth day. And you will. Because I've seen your archive research, Astrid, and you haven't found the last page of the ledger yet. The unfinished entry. You think the ritual was interrupted mid-sentence." They rose, unhurried, and began walking backward toward the bathroom, toward the pale rectangle of the mirror, and the grave-light dimmed with each step like a tide going out. "It wasn't interrupted. It was *addressed*. To whoever finished reading it. Read the last page. Then come talk to me."

"The power's out," I said stupidly. "You can't—"

"I live in reflections," said my voice, from my face, receding into the dark. "Blackouts are just more mirrors."

The gray-green light sank into the floorboards. The footsteps — heel-toe, heel-toe, that familiar drag — faded up the stairs toward the bedroom, though I never saw them climb. And the bathroom mirror, when I finally made myself look, showed only me: standing in the doorway of my own hallway, flashlight dead in my hand, wearing the expression of someone who has just been invited to dinner by the thing wearing their skin.

The lag was still there. I checked. Tick — raise hand — one-point-four seconds — the reflection follows.

But for the first time, the reflection wasn't copying me.

It was waving goodbye.

* * *

The power came back at 11:15. I did not sleep. I sat in the kitchen with every light on and the notebook in front of me and I wrote down everything, because writing is the only exorcism I trust, and halfway through the account of the offer I stopped, pen hovering, because I realized what I'd been avoiding realizing:

They'd known about the metronome app. They'd known about the archive trip. They'd known about the ledger's missing page — a detail I'd mentioned to no one, written nowhere, carried only in my head.

Which meant either they really were me, reading my thoughts through our shared skull.

Or the house had been letting me find exactly what it wanted found, and the ledger itself was the trap, and the last page — the page they were so certain would bring me around — was bait dressed as scholarship.

Both possibilities ended the same way: with me going back to the town archive in the morning, asking for the Brightwater folio, and reading the ending someone wrote a hundred and forty years ago and addressed, specifically, to me.

Outside, the rain had stopped. In the window above the sink, my reflection sat across from me at the table, matching me pose for pose, pen for pen.

Almost.

The lag was one-point-five seconds now. And when I finally put down the pen and looked directly at it, it didn't look away.

It tapped the table twice — a gesture I make, a nervous habit, *tap-tap* — and pointed, slowly and deliberately, toward the front door.

Toward the archive. Toward the answer. Toward whatever waited on the last page.

My reflection, it seemed, wanted me to do my homework.

I have never trusted anything less.
