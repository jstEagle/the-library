## 15. Three Days Dark

The cloth came from a fabric store two towns over, because I wasn't going to give the house the satisfaction of watching me buy it locally.

Six bolts of black cotton, matte, cheap, the kind of material that doesn't reflect anything so much as absorb the idea of light. Vane carried them to the car while I paid in cash and made small talk with a teenager at the register who asked if we were doing a haunted house for Halloween.

"Sort of," I said. "We're trying to make one less haunted."

She laughed. I didn't.

By the time we got back to Brightwater, the sun was low and the windows were doing that thing again — not reflecting so much as *considering*. I'd been avoiding looking directly at glass for two days, which is harder than it sounds when you live in a house built by people who apparently believed mirrors were a load-bearing element of the human soul. There were forty-one of them in the main structure alone. Forty-one. The founders had put mirrors in places mirrors had no business being: inside closet doors, along stair risers, set into the ceiling above the kitchen table like a skylight to nowhere. One was embedded in the pantry wall behind the flour, angled so you'd catch yourself reaching for sugar and think, briefly, *who is that woman hoarding carbohydrates in my house.*

"We start upstairs," Vane said, dropping the bolts on the hall floor with a thud that felt almost profanely ordinary. "Work down. Save the well chamber for last."

"You've thought about this."

"I've thought about everything," she said. "It's my only coping mechanism."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about covering forty-one mirrors: they don't want to be covered.

I don't mean they resist. I mean the work goes wrong in small, patient ways. The tacks bend. The tape loses its grip on plaster that has held paint for a century. You drape a sheet over a vanity mirror, step back to check your work, and find one corner has slipped — just enough to show a sliver of silvered glass, just enough to catch the room, and you stand there with your heart going like a trapped bird until you press the corner flat and hold it there longer than any piece of fabric requires.

Vane worked in silence beside me, methodical, deputy-precise, folding each cloth over its frame like she was making a bed for something she didn't trust not to bite. She'd covered her own bathroom mirror at home before coming over, she admitted. And her car's rearview. And the dark screen of her television.

"The TV isn't a mirror," I said.

"It hesitates," she said, and I didn't argue, because mine did too now.

The lag had reached two seconds by then. Two full seconds between what I did and what the glass admitted I'd done. It doesn't sound like much. Two seconds is the time it takes to say a name. It's the gap between lightning and thunder close enough to kill you. I had started living my life in that gap — doing things twice, once for me and once for whatever was wearing me on the other side of the world's worst membrane.

The second Astrid used those seconds. That was the whole point of them.

So: black cloth, every surface, top to bottom. We worked until midnight, and when the last window in the east bedroom was draped — because yes, the windows too, since Tuesday, since I'd caught my reflection in the rain-streaked pane raising a hand I hadn't raised — the house went quiet in a way I can only describe as *offended*.

"That's all of them," Vane said. She counted on her fingers, then counted again from her notebook. "Forty-one mirrors, nine windows, three picture frames under glass, the fish tank—" she paused, "—which, by the way, why does an abandoned house have a functioning fish tank?"

"It doesn't have fish anymore."

"Astrid."

"There were no fish when I found it. There was water, though. Still water." I let that sit. "There's no still water in this house anymore. Not if I can help it."

* * *

The trouble began around two in the morning, which is the hour houses choose for their arguments.

I woke to a sound like slow breathing. Not snoring — Vane had taken the guest room, and she slept like someone who'd learned to sleep during shift work, instantly and defensively. This was different. Wet. Rhythmic. Coming from everywhere and nowhere, the way a sound comes from a house's bones rather than its rooms.

I followed it downstairs with a flashlight I didn't need, because every lamp in the hall was on, though I distinctly remembered turning them off.

The dining room mirror — the big one, the one the founders had hung opposite the window so that anyone sitting at dinner would be reflected against the night, doubled and offered up — was sweating.

Not the glass. The cloth. The black cotton I'd stretched over it was beaded with moisture, droplets running down the fabric in lines that looked, if you squinted, like tally marks. And as I watched, the whole drape swelled — outward, gently, like a lung filling — and released. Swelled. Released.

Breathing. The cloth was breathing.

I stood in the doorway for a long time, flashlight shaking slightly in my grip, and I want to be honest here: my first coherent thought was not fear. It was *I used a quarter-inch hem allowance, this fabric was not rated for this.* There's a particular kind of exhaustion where the absurd and the terrible arrive holding hands, and you greet them both with the same flat stare.

Then the cloth exhaled, and in the exhale I heard — faintly, wetly, from underneath — humming. A tune I almost knew. Something my mother used to sing while she cooked.

I went and got Vane.

* * *

"We re-tack everything," she said, standing in the dining room with her arms crossed, staring at the heaving drape like it was a suspect refusing to cooperate. "Double layers. Weight the bottoms."

"With what?"

"Fishing weights. Buckets. I don't know. Books." She glanced at me. "You have books?"

"I have four hundred dollars of out-of-print architectural surveys, but sure, let's suffocate a haunted mirror with *Brick and Mortar in the Upper Valley, 1840–1890.*"

"If it works."

The thing was, it half worked. We layered the cloths, weighted them, sealed the edges with painter's tape until each mirror looked like a shrouded body in a very tidy morgue. The breathing slowed. Didn't stop. Slowed. By dawn the house sounded less like lungs and more like something sleeping under a blanket, dreaming badly.

And then the lag moved.

I noticed it first in the kitchen window over the sink, which I'd covered, but the morning light came through the fabric and threw a gray glow across the counter, and in that glow — in nothing, in mere light — I saw my hand move before my hand moved. Just light and shadow. Just the ghost of a gesture arriving early, or late, depending on which side of it you lived on.

"Vane," I said carefully. "Don't look at the window light."

"What?"

"Just — don't look at yourself in anything soft-edged. Anything that holds an image. Light through cloth counts now."

She looked at me the way cops look at informants who are technically right but exhausting. Then she picked up her phone to check the time, and stopped, and went very still.

The screen was dark. Off. And in the dead black of it, faint as breath on glass, her face was waiting. Two seconds behind her. Watching her watch it.

She turned the phone face-down on the table with the deliberate calm of a woman disarming herself. "Okay," she said. "Okay. New rule. Screens are mirrors now."

"Screens were always mirrors. That's literally what they are."

"I know what they are, Brightwater." But her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and I understood that the phone hadn't frightened her because of the lag. It had frightened her because the face in it had smiled. And Deputy Vane, I would have bet money, did not smile like that. Not at herself. Not with that much patience.

* * *

We fortified. That's the only word for it. Day two of the three became a siege, and sieges turn houses into fortresses and allies into family whether you consent to the arrangement or not.

Vane taped cardboard over her phone camera and left the phone in her car. I drained the vase on the hall table, wiped the kettle dry after every use, and put a towel down whenever I washed my hands so the sink couldn't hold even a coin-sized pool of standing water. We ate off paper plates because the glaze on the ceramics had started doing something reflective-adjacent that I refused to investigate. We talked in rooms without glass. We learned the house's new geography of safe corners the way soldiers learn sightlines.

And somewhere in the middle of it — somewhere between weighting the pantry mirror with architecture monographs and discovering that the hallway had grown four feet overnight, which we both pretended not to notice because noticing felt like giving it ideas — Vane made coffee at three in the morning and handed me a mug, and neither of us said anything clever, and it was the best moment I'd had in weeks.

"This is insane," she said, settling onto the floor beside me, backs against the cupboard, because chairs faced walls and walls had windows and windows had opinions now. "You know that. Objectively. If you described this to me six months ago I'd have run a wellness check on you."

"If you'd described it to me six months ago I'd have taken notes. That's the problem with me. I document first and panic later, and sometimes later never comes."

"When does it come?"

I turned the mug in my hands. "When I saw my mother's face in the well," I said. "That was pretty much the moment."

She was quiet. Outside, or what passed for outside in a house that rearranged itself nightly, the wind moved through trees I could no longer confidently place relative to the property line.

"My great-aunt's name is down there," Vane said finally. "Edith Vane. 1954. 'Gone missing,' the file says. My grandmother never spoke of her. Just — a silence in the family shaped like a person." She laughed, short and bitter. "I became a cop because of that silence, you know. Somebody should have looked. Nobody looked. So I look. It's the whole job. It turns out the whole job was also the trap."

"We'll get her out," I said. "All of them. That's the plan."

"Your plan involves breaking a mirror with a hammer while a better-dressed version of you watches."

"Yes."

"And possibly erasing yourself."

"...The plan has contingencies."

"Astrid." She said my name the way you set something fragile down. "If it comes to choosing — if it actually comes to that — you don't get to do the noble self-sacrifice thing alone. That's not how this works. I didn't spend two days taping bedsheets over forty-one haunted mirrors to watch you martyr yourself into a dry hole in the ground."

I looked at her. Tired, stubborn, wrapped in a cardigan she'd clearly grabbed in the dark, a smear of painter's tape stuck to her jaw. The most solid thing in a house designed to dissolve solidity.

"Noted," I said. "Deputy."

"Off duty," she said, and clinked her paper cup against my mug, and for one moment the siege felt almost — I won't say cozy. Defiant. Defiantly domestic. Two people drinking bad coffee on a kitchen floor inside a machine built to eat names, daring it to try.

* * *

The third night, the cloth fought back.

We heard it from the guest room — a tearing sound, slow and fibrous, like rope unraveling under strain. Vane was up first, gun drawn before her feet hit the floor, which I'd have teased her about if my own hands weren't already full of flashlight and terror. We followed the sound to the east bedroom, where the window drape — double-layered, weighted, taped — had split down the middle in a clean vertical line.

Through the split: moonlight, and my reflection in the glass, standing exactly where I stood, except its arms were crossed and mine weren't, and it was smiling the way you smile at a child who thinks they've hidden something well.

Behind it, in the reflection's version of the room, every mirror stood uncovered. Every cloth lay folded neatly on the floor. In the glass-world, our siege had never happened. In the glass-world, the house was bare-faced and waiting.

The lag in that window was no longer measurable in seconds. The reflection moved when it wanted. It raised one hand — slowly, mockingly — and pressed it to the inside of the glass, and I swear the pane warmed under my own palm where I hadn't touched it.

"Three days," it said, in my voice, pitched sweet. "Cute. Did you think cloth was a wall? Cloth is a curtain, Astrid. And curtains are for theaters."

Vane fired the flashlight beam straight into the pane and the image flinched — actually flinched — and the split in the cloth sealed itself with a sound like a scab forming. We stood in the dark breathing hard, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding.

Harvest moon. Twelve days, when I'd read the ledger. We'd spent three hiding under blankets.

Nine left. And the reflection had just told me, in my own voice, that it knew exactly how much time it needed — and that it had been letting us play at curtains while it finished its real preparations somewhere we hadn't thought to cover.

Because there was one reflective surface in that house we'd never draped. One we couldn't reach, couldn't weight, couldn't tape.

The well chamber below, ringed with mirrors angled inward, where the harvested faces waited — and where, I realized with a cold that started in my teeth, the founders' ledger had listed one final payment we'd never traced.

*'To M. Aldous, for the keeping of the heir's name.'*

I looked at Vane. She was already reaching for her notebook.

"Who's Aldous?" she asked.

"I don't know yet," I said. "But I think we have nine days to find out."
