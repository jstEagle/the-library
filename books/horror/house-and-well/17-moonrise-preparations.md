## 17. Moonrise Preparations

**Astrid**

The house undressed itself while we slept.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the last tool I have left in the box, and I intend to hold onto it the way a drowning person holds onto a door. At eleven o'clock last night, every mirror in the Brightwater house was covered. I know this because I covered them. I know it because Vane and I had done it together, sheet by sheet, staple by staple, three days of black cloth over glass, over chrome, over the polished lid of the piano nobody plays, over the inside of every window facing east. I have the staple gun. I have the blisters. I have the receipts.

At dawn, the sheets were folded.

Not torn. Not dragged off. *Folded* — laid over the banister in neat squares, corners aligned, the way my mother used to fold tea towels, the way I fold maps. The house had gone through my habits and found the ones I liked and used them against me, which is, at this point, less a surprise than a formality. It's the house's whole personality. It learns what you are and then it invoices you.

"They're all uncovered," Vane said from the landing. She had her service weapon in her hand, which is a thing I'd told her was unnecessary, and a thing I had privately been very glad she carried anyway. "Every one. Even the little hand mirror in the box in the cellar. Even the one in the locket."

"You opened the locket?"

"It was open. It was open and it was facing the stairwell, Astrid. Like a periscope."

I came down and stood beside her and looked at the long hall mirror at the end of the corridor, the one with the foxed silver and the water stains shaped like continents. It showed us the corridor. It showed us ourselves. And it showed us, in the glass, the faintest suggestion of light coming up through the floorboards — a gold, patient, subterranean glow, like a lamp being turned up under the skin of the house.

There is no light under the floorboards. I have mapped what's under the floorboards. I mapped it with a headlamp, a graph paper notebook, and the specific arrogance of a person who believes that anything can be known if you just measure it enough. What's under the floorboards is a stairwell that appears on no survey, a dry stone well, twelve mirrors angled inward like a jury, and a dark that has been eating faces for a hundred and forty years.

The light was coming from the well.

"It's filling up," I said.

"With what? There's no water."

"With moonlight," I said, and heard how it sounded, and kept talking anyway, because at this stage of a horror story you stop apologizing for the vocabulary. "It's not the harvest yet. It's the rehearsal. Mrs. Aldous said the well fills three nights early — the founders called it *priming the glass*. The ledger has an entry for it. 'Paid, oil and wicks, for the priming.' I thought it meant candles."

"Did it mean candles?"

"Nothing in this house has ever meant what it said. The house is a machine for lying about what it is."

Vane looked at her own reflection in the foxed mirror. Her reflection looked back — on time, for now, the lag in this room still under half a second, though I'd timed it that morning with the metronome app and watched it tick from 0.4 to 0.47 while I stood there, the way you watch ice creep across a windshield. "Then we lie back," she said. "Better than we do."

* * *

**Vane**

I've been a deputy for eleven years. In eleven years I have learned that ninety percent of crime is logistics. Somebody wanted something, somebody moved something, somebody wrote something down. The remaining ten percent is people screaming, and you get through it by remembering that the screaming also has logistics. There is always a floor plan. There is always a timeline. I am good at my job because I refuse to be impressed by anything, and I am standing in a haunted house guarding a stairwell with a sidearm I am ninety percent sure is useless, and the reason I'm doing it is that the evidence in the Astrid Brightwater case was too neat, and I have never once in my career met a crime scene that was neat on purpose unless somebody built it that way.

Somebody built this one. Several somebodies, over a hundred and forty years, with payments for glass and silence and the keeping of names.

So: logistics.

The stairwell is behind the plaster wall in the cellar, behind the door that isn't on any survey, and it goes down forty-one steps to a round chamber with a dry well in the middle and twelve mirrors angled at it like the petals of a stone flower. The thirteenth mirror — the keystone — is set into the well's rim itself, and it's older than the others, older than the glass trade that built this town, and it has a crack in it shaped like a river delta that was not there three weeks ago and is there now, and every hour it is a little longer.

Astrid is up in the founder's study with the ledger and Mrs. Aldous's notes, doing whatever Astrid does, which is turning horror into arithmetic. I'm at the top of the forty-one steps with a shotgun, a thermos, a flashlight, and a folding chair, because if this is going to be a siege, it's going to be a siege with ergonomics.

The well is glowing.

I want to describe it correctly, because I have a feeling nobody's going to believe me and I'd like at least one accurate witness statement on file, even if the file is just this. The glow comes up out of the dry dark the way light comes up out of water when you look down into a lake at noon — except it's night, and there's no water, and it's coming *up*, which light does not do, and it's the color of the harvest moon, which isn't even risen yet. It's been brightening all day, slow as a kettle coming to boil. By dusk you could read by it. By full dark you could do surgery.

And in the light, if you lean over the rim — which I have done exactly once, and will not do again — there are faces.

Not reflections. Reflections I could handle. Reflections are my whole problem lately; my own reflection hesitated at my bedroom window on Tuesday, a full beat behind my body, and I have decided to file that under *later*, in the drawer where I keep everything I'll open when this is over, if there is an after. These faces aren't reflections. They're more like — names, wearing faces. They look up out of the dry dark with the patience of things that have been kept. One of them had my great-aunt's chin.

Margaret Vane, kept 1954. I found the ledger entry myself. *Received, one name, Vane, M., payment rendered in full.* Payment rendered in full. My family took money. That's the part that keeps me up. Not the well, not the mirrors, not the lagging glass. My people were paid, and they signed, and they went home to a warm stove, and a woman with my chin went down forty-one steps and did not come back up, and the town paper said she'd gone to her sister in Ohio, and her sister in Ohio said she'd gone to us, and everybody let the contradiction sit there like furniture.

I understand now why the town let it sit. I saw the courthouse hearing. I saw half the town watch their own reflections lag and go quiet — not screaming, *quiet*, the quiet of people doing arithmetic they don't want to finish. Complicity doesn't always look like a signature. Sometimes it looks like not asking.

So I sit at the top of the stairs, and I guard the forty-one steps, and I do the logistics of the end of the world, because it's the only prayer I know.

* * *

**Astrid**

The ledger's last page is unfinished. I've told you that. What I haven't told you — what I only understood tonight, with the well lighting the study through the floor seams like a stage light under a floorboard — is *where* it's unfinished.

The closing words of the ritual run down the right-hand column in the founder's careful hand, and they stop. Not at the end of the page. Not at the end of a line. Mid-sentence, mid-word, the ink trailing off as though the pen were lifted, or as though the hand writing it stopped being the hand that started. Mrs. Aldous says the founder's heir is supposed to finish the sentence. She says the words don't work unless they're spoken by blood, under the risen moon, with the keeper — the living keeper, the one whose reflection has been fattening in the glass for weeks — standing at the keystone.

She also said, in her kitchen, over tea she made in a pot she'd covered with a dish towel, that my blood qualifies. A branch nobody recorded. A Brightwater who married out and changed the name and moved two counties over and had a daughter who grew up to be meticulous, to photograph doorways, to count exits, to map cellars on a whim. Built to be found by someone like them. I thought that meant the house wanted an explorer.

It wanted an heir.

Tonight I read the unfinished sentence aloud to an empty room, up to the break, just to hear the shape of it. My voice did something strange on the last syllable — it kept going a fraction of a second after I'd stopped, like an echo with opinions. The lag, in the study, is at two seconds now. I raised my hand and watched my reflection raise its hand two full seconds later, and in those two seconds I had time to think an entire thought — *I could leave, I could walk out the front door, I could be in Ohio by morning* — before my glass self finished the gesture and turned its head, slightly, toward the door, as if it had heard me think it.

It doesn't want me to leave. It wants me at the keystone. It has wanted that since the first second it stole.

Downstairs, the well-light is strong enough now to throw my shadow up the cellar stairs ahead of me, which is a sentence I never expected to write and which is, physically, impossible, and which happened anyway. The house is ready. The mirrors are bare. The moon is a coin behind cloud cover, one night from full, and the whole building is holding its breath the way a singer holds it before the downbeat.

I pack my bag the way I pack for any survey: headlamp, spare batteries, chalk, the graph paper notebook, the ledger, water I won't drink because you don't take anything from this house into your body. And then, because I am who I am, I photograph the doorways. All of them. Twice. The count comes out thirteen.

It came out thirteen last night too. I'd been telling myself it was my error.

It was not my error.

* * *

**Vane**

She came down the stairs at moonset-minus-three hours with a bag on her shoulder and her jaw set in the way I've learned means she's made a decision and is now going to be insufferable about it.

"Rules," she said.

"I'm the one with the gun."

"Rules anyway." She counted them off on her fingers, and I noticed her hands were steady, and I noticed I noticed, the way you notice a soldier's hands before a briefing. "One: you don't touch the keystone until the moon is fully up, because the ledger says the glass is soft before then and soft glass does things I don't want to learn about. Two: if your reflection shows up before you do — if I see you at the bottom of these stairs while you're standing next to me — I shoot it. I know it might not work. Shoot it anyway. Three: if you're not back up by the time the moon clears the ridge line, I'm coming down after you, and I don't care what the ledger says about keepers and heirs, because I have a shotgun and a grudge and my family's name is in that well."

"That's not how the ritual works," Astrid said. "If you come down—"

"I've read the ritual. I've read it four times. I'm telling you what *I* will do, which is a different document." I stood up off the folding chair and faced them, and the well-light came up the stairwell between us and turned us both the color of the harvest moon, and for a second I thought: *we look like we're already down there. We look like we're already kept.* "Four: you say the words your way. Not theirs. Whatever you've decided, I can see you've decided it, and I'm not going to ask, because if you tell me and I disagree, we'll waste the last three hours arguing, and I'd rather spend them like this."

"Like what?"

"Like people on the same side of a door."

Astrid looked at me for a long moment. In the foxed mirror at the stairwell's turning, our reflections looked at each other too — and mine, I noted, was half a beat late, and Astrid's was not late at all, which was somehow worse. "Vane," they said. "If this works, the charges dissolve. The planted evidence unravels. You should know that's part of why I'm doing it. Not the only part."

"I know. It's the part I like."

"And if it doesn't work—"

"Then I'll write the report," I said. "And it'll be accurate. That's the only immortality I'm after."

They almost smiled. Then they turned and went down the forty-one steps, and I stood at the top with the shotgun and watched the well-light climb their back like water rising, and I did the last piece of logistics left to me, which is the oldest one: I marked the time, and I waited.

* * *

**Astrid**

The chamber is full of light with nothing to make it. The twelve mirrors hold it and turn it and give it back, and the dry well breathes it upward, and the faces in the dark below the light are more distinct tonight than they have ever been — generations of them, a congregation, the kept and the kept-for. I can see the children. I was not prepared for the children. The old papers called them *gone missing*, and the ledger called them *received*, and the well calls them by no name at all, because their names are what was taken, and what is taken is what they are standing in.

They are all looking up. They are all looking at me. And as the moon touches the ridge line above — I can feel it through forty-one steps of stone, a pressure like a hand laid flat on the house — one of the faces comes forward out of the dark, and it has my mother's eyes, and it says my name.

Not *Astrid*. My full name. The name on the branch nobody recorded.

And at the keystone, waiting, hammer in hand, wearing my face and my posture and my meticulous, door-counting patience, my reflection turns and smiles — my smile, the one I use in photographs — and holds out the hammer handle-first, the way you offer a tool to a colleague, and says, in my voice, exactly one second before I would have:

"It's time. Say yes."

The moon clears the ridge.

The keystone mirror begins, very softly, to sing.
