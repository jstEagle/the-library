## 18. Descent

The stairs had moved again.

I'd mapped them twice that afternoon — forty-one steps, a landing, a dogleg left, thirty more. I'd photographed the chalk marks Vane and I had left at every seventh step, little white ticks like a prisoner's calendar. Now, standing at the top with the black cloth bundle under my arm and the moon already a fat orange promise through the cellar's broken window, the stairs were wrong. Forty-one steps had become fifty-three. The dogleg went right. My chalk marks were there, all of them, but arranged in an order that suggested they'd been counting down to something rather than up from anything.

"House is dressing for the occasion," Vane said behind me. She had her flashlight, her service weapon she'd finally admitted was useless — "You can't shoot arithmetic" — and a length of iron rebar she seemed to trust more than either.

"You could stay up here."

"I could also be struck by lightning in a town that hasn't seen a storm in weeks." She clicked her flashlight on and off, testing. "The last Vane who let this family handle its own business is still in the well, Astrid. I'm not adding to the collection."

I didn't argue. There wasn't time to argue well, and I've always believed if you're going to do a thing badly, do it quickly.

The moon crested as we reached the landing. I felt it happen the way you feel a change in pressure before weather — a softness in the ears, a settling, as though the whole house exhaled after holding its breath since 1897. The lag hit us on the stairs. I saw Vane's reflection in the brass fitting of my flashlight arrive at the turn a full three seconds after she did, pausing at the corner like a courteous guest, then following.

Three seconds. It had been two that morning. The house was accelerating toward its appointment.

* * *

The well chamber door — which had been oak, then pine, then something I chose not to identify — stood open. Of course it did. Houses don't knock when they're expecting you.

We descended into light.

That was the first obscenity of it: the chamber was lit, and there was no source. No lamps, no windows, no electrical anything — the founders had died before rural electrification, and whatever was down here predated their wiring by centuries. But the room glowed anyway, a pale gold wash rising out of the dry well itself, as though the darkness below had decided, tonight, to wear its harvest as a garment.

The mirrors ringed the well exactly as I'd mapped them — twelve great angled glasses, each taller than me, each tilted inward over the stone lip like mourners leaning over a grave. Except now they weren't reflecting the chamber. They weren't reflecting anything in the room at all.

They were reflecting people.

I stopped on the bottom stair and made myself look, because looking was the one skill I had that the house couldn't take from me. Faces floated in the waterless dark — dozens, then scores, then more than I could count, layered in the empty air above the well shaft like reflections in water that had never been poured. Old faces, young faces. A man in a collar that belonged to another century. A woman with a cameo brooch. Children.

Children.

One of them turned — not his head, nothing so mechanical; he simply *was* facing me now, the way things in dreams are suddenly facing you — and said my name.

"Astrid."

Then another voice. Then ten. Then all of them, a tide of it, gentle as rain on a roof: *Astrid, Astrid, Astrid.* Not menacing. That was the horror of it. They sounded glad. They sounded like a family reunion where everyone remembered you and you remembered no one.

And then, cutting through the chorus, clear and close and wearing a voice I had spent twenty years trying not to hear in my own mouth:

"Astrid Brightwater. You came home after all."

My mother's face rose to the surface of the dark like a bubble finding air.

She looked the way she had in the last photograph — tired, kind, thirty-nine forever. She had been listed in the county records as having left town in March of my eleventh year. I had spent my adolescence constructing elaborate theories about why she'd gone, and my adulthood constructing quieter ones about why she'd never written. Neither theory had included *the house kept her.*

"You're not her," I said. My voice held. I noted that with professional detachment, the way you note a load-bearing wall cracking but not yet falling.

"No," she agreed, and smiled my mother's smile, and it was worse than if she'd lied. "But I remember being her. Every second of her. They pour them all in together down here, sweetheart. Your face, your mother's face — we're neighbors now." Her eyes — my eyes — went past me to Vane. "Hello, Deputy. Your great-aunt says you have her stubbornness. She means it as a compliment. Mostly."

Vane made a sound I'd never heard from her, small and involuntary, and I understood that the house had just done to her what it had spent weeks doing to me: shown her the exact shape of her grief, polished it, and held it up.

"Don't answer them," I said quietly. "Anything they show you, don't answer. Answers are how they get your name."

"And how did you learn that?" asked my mother's face, gently. "From the ledger? Sweetheart, who do you think taught the founders?"

* * *

The keystone mirror stood at the far side of the well, set into the stone itself — not hung, not framed, but grown, if glass can be said to grow. It was smaller than the twelve, barely larger than a window, and older. Its silvering was foxed and mottled like the eye of an ancient dog. And standing before it, hammer in hand, patient as furniture, was me.

Not a reflection. Reflections stand where you stand. This one stood where it pleased, on the far side of the well, and it had dressed itself in the flannel shirt I'd worn the day I found the stairwell, the one I'd thrown away. It had been keeping souvenirs.

"Astrid," it said, in my voice, pitched exactly to carry. "Right on time. You always were punctual. It's the mapping, isn't it? The counting. You need the world to add up." It tapped the keystone glass with the hammer, a small bright *tink*. "Well. Here's everything adding up."

Behind me, the chorus of names continued, softer now, almost tender. *Astrid. Astrid. Come down. Come in. We saved you a place.*

"I know what you are," I said. "You're the part of me the house skimmed off. Every late second, you got a little more, and I got a little less, and now you've got enough to stand up and make offers."

"Clever. See, that's the thing — I don't want to destroy you. Destroying you would be destroying half my material." It hefted the hammer. "Here's the arithmetic you've been dreading. The ritual needs a keeper to finish it. One name spoken at moonrise, given willingly, to bind the harvest for another century. The founders meant it to be one of their own. The Aldous woman told you the rest — blood will serve. Yours serves beautifully."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the ritual finishes anyway, at dawn, unbound. Everything in the well comes up. Everyone." It gestured with the hammer at the floating faces, and they rippled, hundreds of them, pressing against the underside of the air like fish under ice. "Every name, loose, hungry, and homeless. Including hers." It nodded at my mother's face. "Including yours. Unbound, nobody gets erased, Astrid. Everybody just gets... distributed."

The trap's true shape opened in front of me, and I want it recorded that I saw it clearly, even then.

Because it wasn't a choice between me and the town. It never had been. The offer — *say yes, keep your body, rule what remains* — was the bait, but the hook underneath was simpler and crueller: the ritual required consent. Willing words. And the only way to save the harvested was to speak the closing words myself, which meant walking into the bargain with my eyes open, which meant the house would win either way — fed by a sacrifice or fed by a flood.

Unless the words themselves could be changed.

"That's why you brought the hammer," I said slowly. "You're offering to break the keystone. You'd end it right now — if I say yes first."

"If you say yes first." It smiled with my mouth, and for the first time all night the smile was warm, and that warmth was the most frightening thing in the room. "Consent opens the lock. Then I swing, the glass goes, the harvest drowns back into the earth, and you walk out of here whole. Both of us walk out. Partners. You keep the body; I'll keep the second. We'll split the difference."

Behind me, my mother's face drifted closer to the surface of the dark. "Astrid," she said, in the voice I'd spent two decades forgetting. "Don't listen to it. Or listen. I honestly don't—" Her face flickered, and beneath it, briefly, I saw something else looking out, something vast and patient and very old, wearing her kindness like the reflection wore my flannel.

The moonlight through the stairwell behind us sharpened. Somewhere above, the house uncovered its mirrors, one by one, cloth sliding off glass like skin off bone.

Vane's hand found my shoulder. "Whatever you're going to do," she murmured, "do it knowing I saw the ledger too. Page nine. The closing words aren't fixed. Only the *name* is."

The reflection heard. Of course it heard; it was made of my hearing. Its smile didn't waver, but the hammer shifted in its grip — a fraction, a tell, the first imperfect thing it had ever done.

"Yes first," it repeated softly. "Just say yes, Astrid. Say yes, and I'll break it. Say yes—"

And the well began to rise.
