## 3. A Door Sealed From the Inside

The call came at six in the morning, and Petra answered it standing in her kitchen in her underwear, coffee not yet made, the kind of hour where her voice came out gravel.

"Almeida." She never said Petra on the phone. Names were for people who wanted to be found.

"Ms. Almeida, this is Ray Dade, I'm running the excavation out at Harrow's Bend. You did some survey work on the storm system out there? Unofficial?"

"I did some mapping, yeah." She watched the kettle. "Who gave you my number?"

"Your maps gave me your number. There's an email address on the scans. You sell those to anybody, or just leave them lying around the internet like bait?"

The kettle clicked off. Petra poured water over the grounds and thought about the question honestly, which was a mistake at six a.m., because the honest answer was: she'd left them there the way a fisherman leaves a line. Not for clients. Clients paid the bills, but clients were a means. What she actually wanted was for someone with equipment and a budget to go where she couldn't alone, down into the branch that appeared on no survey since 1911, and tell her what was down there so she wouldn't have to go find out by herself.

"Bait," she said. "You found the branch?"

"We found something." A pause, and she heard him decide how much to give her. "The county wants a structural report before we pour foundations. Your maps say there's a void under the east parcel. The ground-penetrating radar says you're right. I need a consultant who already knows the layout. You available?"

She was available. She'd been available for eleven days, ever since she'd stood in the drainage outfall with her headlamp on and her heart going like a trapped bird, looking at the mouth of a tunnel that the 1911 municipal survey drew to a stop and every survey after pretended had never existed.

"When do you want me down there?"

"Today. Bring your own lights. And Ms. Almeida—" Dade had the flat, amused tone of a man who'd already decided she was worth the money and the trouble. "Bring a hard hat. We're not a hiking club."

* * *

The site was worse than she remembered, or maybe she remembered it better than it was. Memory did that with places you loved—it sanded off the mud, the smell, the way the light died twenty meters past the last working lamp. Harrow's Bend had been drowning, officially, since the reservoir went in in the fifties, and unofficially since long before that; the town had surrendered street by street, house by house, until what was left was a grid of foundations poking through the scrub like teeth, and the county had finally sold the whole east parcel to a development firm with more money than patience.

Dade was exactly what his voice promised: big, sunburned, forty-something, a man who'd spent his career being the strongest person on any job site and had never once wondered whether that was the same as being the right one. He shook her hand like he was testing the structural integrity of it.

"Almeida. You look like your maps. All lines and no fat."

"You look like your invoices," Petra said. "Expensive and hard to read."

He laughed, and she marked him down as someone who could be handled, which was not the same as someone who could be trusted. Behind him the shaft mouth gaped, freshly cut, timbered, a steel ladder bolted down one wall. Two of his crew were already at the bottom, their headlamps swimming in the dark like wasps in a bottle.

"Show me what you found," Dade said.

"You found it. Your radar found it. I just told you where to look."

"Then show me where you told us to look."

She went down the ladder ahead of him because if she went second she'd be thinking about what was above her the whole way, and she'd rather think about what was below. That was the deal she'd made with herself years ago, back when she'd started going into places the city had forgotten: fear is just information arriving early. You take it, you file it, you keep moving. Skepticism wasn't the absence of fear. It was fear with a filing system.

The main drain ran north under the old town grid, a Victorian brick barrel vault, dry for decades, its floor furred with a fine grey silt that held footprints like snow. She knew this stretch the way she knew her own hallway. Forty meters in, where the 1911 survey showed the line doglegging west toward the reservoir intake, the dogleg wasn't there. Instead the wall was solid brick, newer than the vault, laid neat as a grave.

"Here," she said, and put her hand on it.

The crew had already been at it. A ragged hole had been opened in the blocking wall, maybe a meter across, the broken bricks stacked to one side with the tidy care of men who'd been told not to make a mess. Through the hole, air moved. That was the first thing. Not stale air—moving air, cool and faintly sweet, with a smell underneath it she couldn't place and didn't want to. Not decay. Something older than decay. Something like a house that had been closed up for the summer.

"Radar says the void runs two hundred meters that way," Dade said, nodding through the hole. "Big. Bigger than a cistern. Bigger than anything on the 1911 drawings, and I've got the 1911 drawings." He tapped his tablet against his thigh. "Your survey says nothing's here. The county's survey says nothing's here. So either everybody who ever drew this system was blind, or somebody went to a lot of trouble to make sure nobody looked."

"Or the tunnel's newer than the surveys," Petra said. "Post-1911 construction. Unpermitted."

"Who builds two hundred meters of tunnel under a drowned town and doesn't permit it?"

"Somebody who didn't want it permitted." She said it lightly, to keep it light. "Look, before we go any further—I want to see the ground conditions at the face. If this void is sitting under the pour line for your foundations, the client needs to know whether it's stable before you put forty tons of concrete on top of it."

"That's the job, yeah." Dade was watching her with the particular patience of a man letting someone talk themselves into a corner. "You always this nervous on a site?"

"I'm never nervous on a site. Nervous is for people who don't know what they're looking at."

"And what are we looking at?"

She didn't have an answer she liked, so she gave him the professional one and crawled through the hole.

* * *

The tunnel beyond was wrong, and it took her thirty seconds of professional detachment to admit it even to herself.

Wrong in the brick. The main drain was water-made in its bones—Victorian brick laid over a natural channel, mortar washed to scallops by a century of flow. This was dry-laid. Hand-laid. The bricks were older, darker, some of them salvaged and stamped with foundry marks from three different decades, and they'd been laid by someone working in the dark, by feel, because the courses wandered like a sentence written with the eyes shut. No water had ever run here. The floor was swept.

That was the detail that got her. Not the wandering courses, not the salvaged brick, not the way the vault was too tall for drainage work—tall enough for a man to stand upright with a hat on, tall enough for furniture, if furniture were the kind of thing you brought down here. The floor was swept. There was no silt. There were no footprints. Two hundred meters of tunnel, sealed since before anyone now living was born, and the floor had been maintained.

"Almeida." Dade's voice came flat down the tunnel, stripped of its amusement. "You seeing this?"

"Seeing it."

"Swept."

"Seeing it."

The crew had stopped at a junction where the tunnel forked, and their headlamps were all pointed the same way, which was how she knew what they'd found before she saw it. She made herself walk the last twenty meters at a normal pace, one boot in front of the other, her lamp beam steady because she made it steady.

The door was iron.

It filled the tunnel wall to wall, floor to crown, set in a frame of bolted steel that had been built around the brick rather than with it, as if the door had come first and the tunnel had been dug up to it from both sides and then stopped, politely, the way you stop at a threshold. It was black with age but not rusted. That was the second wrong thing. Iron in a damp tunnel for a hundred years should have been a flaking ruin, and this was smooth, oiled, the hinges glistening like something tended.

And the bolts. She counted them without meaning to—six of them, throw bars as thick as her wrist, and every one of them was shot from the inside. The bolt-ends stood out of the door's face toward her, toward the tunnel, toward them, like knuckles. The door wasn't locked to keep something out.

It was locked to keep something in. Or—she made herself consider it, because skepticism was a discipline and you didn't get to skip the hard reps—to keep everyone else out, by people who had decided the outside was the danger.

"It's warm," said one of the crew, a young guy with a laser level slung on his shoulder. He had his palm flat against the iron, and he said it the way you'd say the floor is wet—reporting a condition, not a feeling. "Feel it. It's like—body warm."

"Don't touch it," Petra said, and heard her own voice come out too fast.

Dade looked at her. "It's a door, Almeida. It's ninety degrees, not ninety-eight."

"Then why is it warm at all? There's no heating down here. There's no source. Metal in a tunnel this deep should be sitting at ground temperature, and ground temperature at this depth is fifty-two degrees, year-round. I've measured it." She heard herself doing the thing she did, the armor thing, the numbers thing, and made herself stop. "I'm saying the readings don't close. Nothing about this closes. The brick is wrong, the floor is swept, the door is maintained from a side that has no business being maintained, and I am telling you, as the consultant you hired because I know this system better than your radar does: seal the shaft. Fill the hole we made in that blocking wall, cap the outfall, and write in your report that the void is unstable and unsuitable for construction. The client doesn't need to know what's down here. The client needs to know not to build on it."

Dade didn't answer right away. He walked the door's face slowly, his lamp tracking the bolt-ends, and Petra watched him do the arithmetic she'd already done and arrive somewhere else with it. She could see the arrival happen. It happened in his shoulders first, then his jaw.

"You know what this is," he said. "Don't you. This is why the parcel was cheap. This is why the county couldn't wait to unload it. There's something under Harrow's Bend that the town has been sitting on for a hundred years, and we just found it, and you want me to brick it back up and call it unstable ground." He turned. "Almeida. This is the find of the decade. Maybe the century. An unmapped structure under a vanished town, sealed from the inside, preserved—preserved, look at it, it's *maintained*—this is a press conference. This is funding. This is my name on the discovery."

"It's a hazard."

"It's a *door*, and doors open."

"Not this one." She heard the flat finality in her own voice and couldn't soften it, didn't want to. "Look at the bolts, Dade. Six of them, thrown from the inside. Somebody on the other side of that iron looked at the world and decided it should stay on its side of the door. That's not a mystery for a press conference. That's a request. The only professional response is to honor it."

"The only professional response is to find out what the client bought." He put his hand flat on the iron, right over the bolt-ends, and Petra had to look away from it, had to look at the swept floor instead. "It's warm," he said again, softer, and she hated that he said it like that, like a man putting his hand on a horse's flank.

"Dade. Don't."

"Relax." But he didn't take his hand off. He stood there with his palm against the iron for a long moment, long enough that the young guy with the laser level shifted his weight, long enough that the tunnel got quiet in the specific way tunnels get quiet when everyone in them is listening without admitting it. "Huh," Dade said. "You know what it feels like?"

"Ground temperature," Petra said. "Fifty-two degrees. Take your hand off the door."

"It feels like—" He pressed, just slightly, the way you'd check a wall for a stud. "It feels like it's pushing back. Very slightly. Like—"

"Like a heartbeat," Petra said.

She hadn't meant to say it. It came out of the place where she kept the thing she'd felt eleven days ago, alone, at the blocking wall, when she'd put her hand on the brick to steady herself and felt, beneath the mortar, beneath the stone, a rhythm that was slow and deep and not hers, a second pulse arriving a half-beat behind her own, patient, unhurried, like something sleeping on the other side of a wall it had built itself. She'd told herself it was her own pulse, reflected. Water hammer in old pipes. Blood in her own pressed fingertips. She'd filed it. She'd kept moving. That was the system, and the system worked, and she was not going to stand in front of four witnesses and dismantle it now.

But Dade was looking at her with his hand still on the iron, and the young guy with the laser level was looking at her, and the tunnel was quiet, and the door was warm.

"Water hammer," she said. "Old mains under pressure. You get a pulse in the structure. It's a known phenomenon in deep drainage work. I'll put it in the report."

"Will you." Dade took his hand off the door at last, and wiped it on his thigh, and she watched him decide not to say whatever he'd been about to say. "Okay. Here's what happens. I don't seal anything. I don't open anything yet, either—we do this by the book. I call the client, I call the county, we get an engineer out here, we get a structural opinion, and then we get a warrant if we need one, and then—" he put his hand on the door frame, not the door, a small distinction he clearly thought mattered "—then we find out who's been keeping house under Harrow's Bend for a hundred years."

"Dade—"

"Consultant's noted. Your objection's on the record. I like having you on record, Almeida, you're good for the liability file." He grinned, and it was a good grin, an honest grin, the grin of a man who had never once been on the wrong side of a door. "But we found it. It's real. And I don't put my name on a hole in the ground."

* * *

She stayed after the crew went up.

She told Dade she wanted to take readings for the report—temperature, humidity, a laser scan of the door face—and he waved her to it, because a thorough consultant was a cheap insurance policy, and climbed the ladder talking about press conferences. The lamps went up with him, one by one, and the dark came back the way it always did, not all at once but in layers, like water finding its level.

Petra stood alone in front of the door with her hand on her own headlamp switch, and did not turn it off, and did not leave.

"Okay," she said to the door, quietly, the way you'd talk to a horse. "Here's what I know. You're iron. You're a hundred years old, minimum. You're warm, which is impossible, and you're oiled, which is worse, and somebody on your side of you sweeps the floor and throws six bolts every night like clockwork. That's not a monster. Monsters don't oil hinges. That's people. That's people who are afraid of something on my side of the door, and you know what? Fair. I've seen what's on my side of the door."

The tunnel didn't answer. The tunnel never answered. That was the whole reason she loved tunnels, or had loved them, back when loving them was simple: a tunnel was the one place in the world that never pretended to be anything other than what it was. A tunnel didn't lie. A tunnel was a fact you could walk through.

But the door was warm, and the floor was swept, and when she put her palm flat against the iron—she did it, she couldn't not do it, she was a professional and professionals take readings—the pulse was there. Slow. Deep. A half-beat behind her own heart, so that for a moment the two rhythms walked together and then fell apart, hers quick and shallow, the other one patient, vast, unhurried, like something that had all the time that had ever been minted and intended to spend all of it.

She snatched her hand back. Her palm print stayed on the oiled iron for a second, faint, then faded as the metal equalized, the way a print fades on skin.

"Water hammer," she told the tunnel, and her voice came out steady, which was the only victory available, so she took it.

She climbed the ladder into the afternoon light, and stood blinking at the scrub and the foundations and the distant glint of the reservoir, and made herself do the thing she always did, the filing, the system: impossible thing, observed; impossible thing, unexplained; impossible thing, pending further data. It worked. It always worked.

It worked all the way through the drive home, and the shower, and the first beer, and it was still working at two in the morning when she lay in the dark with her laptop open on her chest, reading the 1911 survey for the fourth time, the one that drew the tunnel to a stop and drew nothing after it, and she found herself thinking about the bolts.

Six of them. Thrown from the inside. Which meant somebody, on some night a hundred years ago, had stood on the far side of that iron and shot them home one at a time, and then—this was the part her mind kept circling, the part the filing system couldn't hold—had kept coming back. For a hundred years. To oil the hinges. To sweep the floor. To keep the bolts true.

You didn't do that for a prison. You didn't do that for a tomb.

You did that for a home.

Petra closed the laptop. In the dark of her apartment, she could still feel the pulse in her palm, slow and patient, a half-beat behind her heart, and she lay there and told herself the only thing left in the filing system: that in the morning she would call Dade, and argue again, and win or lose she would write it all down, because writing things down was how you kept them from being true.

She was almost asleep when she understood, with the cold clarity of two a.m., that she was going back down. Not for the report. Not for Dade. Not for the client.

Because the ledger of her life had one column for things she knew and one column for things she'd felt, and the second column had an entry in it now, written in a hand she didn't recognize, and it was not going to close itself.

She'd go alone. At night. Through the maintenance gap behind the blocking wall that Dade's crew didn't know she'd already found—because she'd found it eleven days ago, before they ever called, and she'd told no one, and the telling-no-one had felt like caution at the time and felt like something else now.

She fell asleep with her palm still tingling, and dreamed, for the first time in years, of a door.
