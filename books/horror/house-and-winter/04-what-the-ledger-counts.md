## 4. What the Ledger Counts

Petra told herself she wasn't going back.

She said it out loud, in her truck, in the gravel lot above the shaft, with the engine ticking and the last of the crew's taillights dissolving down the county road. "You are not going back." The words came out flat and professional, the voice she used for clients who wanted to argue about invoice terms. It didn't work on herself any better than it worked on them.

The problem was the door.

She'd spent eleven years doing this—crawling through other people's forgotten infrastructure, mapping what cities preferred to forget—and she had a rule about doors. Doors meant intention. Water cut tunnels; people hung doors. And a door bolted from the inside, in a branch of tunnel that hadn't appeared on a single survey since 1911, was not a curiosity. It was a statement. Somebody had gone in and made very sure nobody followed.

Dade wanted it opened. Of course Dade wanted it opened. Dade had the particular hunger of a man who'd never found anything in his life except paychecks, and now the ground under Harrow's Bend was offering him a discovery, and he could already see the press release. Petra had argued for sealing the shaft—liability, unstable brick, unverified air quality, all the boring words that sometimes kept people alive—and Dade had smiled at her like she was being precious and said they'd bring in a structural engineer Monday.

Monday. Which gave her Sunday night.

That was the real reason, if she was honest, and honesty was a discipline she practiced the way other people practiced religion: irregularly, but with genuine feeling when it took hold. She wasn't going back to protect the site or document the door before the crew butchered it. She was going back because when she'd put her palm flat against that iron, she had felt something. A pulse. Slow, deep, patient, nothing like machinery and nothing like plumbing, and it had synced—not matched, *synced*—with her own heartbeat for three full seconds before she snatched her hand away and made a joke about old pipes.

Petra did not believe in things. That was the whole architecture of her. Her mother had believed in everything—psychics, prophets, the specific vengeance of saints—and Petra had built herself out of the refusal, brick by careful brick, a person who trusted only what she could measure, photograph, and invoice. So the pulse bothered her less as a haunting than as an error. Something in her instrumentation was wrong, and she needed to know what, because if her instruments were wrong down there, then her maps were wrong, and her maps were the only thing in the world that had ever been entirely hers.

She geared up in the dark. Helmet, headlamp, spare batteries, the good camera, the gas monitor clipped to her chest strap. She left a note on her dashboard saying where she was, because even skeptics die of bad protocol, and dropped into the shaft on the winch line the crew had rigged, the dark closing over her head like water.

* * *

The tunnel was different at night.

That was the first thing she noticed, and she cataloged it the way she cataloged everything, clinically, because cataloging was how she kept the fear in its cage. During the day, with the crew's generators humming and voices bouncing off the brick, the branch had felt like a worksite. Alone, at two in the morning, with only her lamp and the sound of her own breathing, it felt like a throat.

The brick was wrong. She'd noted it before and she noted it again now, running her glove along a course of mortar as she walked: this was bedrock country, limestone and shale, and yet here was brick, laid in courses too regular for flood debris, mortared by hands. Hands, plural, over a long time. Someone had built this corridor, or extended it, or maintained it, century after century, down here in the dark, and no survey had ever recorded it. She thought about the phrase *unmapped* and felt the small thrill she always felt—the thrill of being the only person who knew something the maps didn't—and then felt the thrill curdle, because there was a difference between finding a place the maps missed and being expected by one.

The door looked worse by lamplight. Or better. She couldn't decide. It was iron, banded, set into a brick frame that had been pointed recently—she could see the fresh mortar, pale against the old—and every hinge glistened faintly with oil. Not rusted shut after a hundred years. Maintained. The bolts were thrown from the inside; she'd confirmed that with a borescope yesterday, feeding the little camera through a weep hole and seeing the slide bars seated home, thick as her wrist.

But yesterday she'd also seen something else in the borescope feed, something she hadn't mentioned to Dade because mentioning it would have required believing it: behind the bolt mechanism, a gap. A maintenance gap, maybe eight inches wide, where the doorframe's inner lining didn't quite meet the rock—a service passage, the kind of thing builders left so the door itself could be serviced without opening it. Whoever built this door had assumed someone would need to get around it.

Someone who lived on the other side.

"Nobody lives on the other side," Petra said aloud, and her voice fell into the tunnel and died without an echo, which was its own kind of wrong. Tunnels echoed. She'd mapped enough of them to know. This one swallowed sound the way dry sand swallows water.

She knelt at the gap. The air coming through was warm. That was the second impossible thing, and she let herself hold it up to the light and turn it over: warm air, moving gently, carrying a smell she knew from her grandmother's kitchen and from museum reconstructions and from nowhere else—woodsmoke and bread and lamp oil. The smell of somewhere inhabited. The gas monitor read oxygen normal, CO2 negligible, no methane, no hydrogen sulfide. The air down here was better than the air in her truck.

"You're going to fit through an eight-inch gap," she said, "because you're stubborn and thin, and you're going to find a room full of nothing, and then you're going to write it up and bill for it."

She turned sideways, exhaled, and slid through.

* * *

The gap opened onto a passage that should not have existed, and the passage opened onto a house.

There was no better word for it. Not a chamber, not a vault—a house. A hallway with wainscoting, actual wainscoting, painted a deep green gone soft with age, and floorboards underfoot instead of rubble, worn pale along a path where feet had walked for generations. Wallpaper in the next room, faded roses. A coat hook by an interior door holding a child's wool coat, decades out of fashion, brushed clean.

Her headlamp seemed almost rude. She clicked it down to its lowest setting, then off entirely, because ahead of her, down the hall, light was already living.

Lamplight. Warm and yellow and steady, spilling from a doorway, and beneath the smell of bread was the sound of a house at night: a clock ticking somewhere, the settle of timber, a spoon against a pot. Sounds she had heard ten thousand times above ground and had never once expected to hear forty feet below the water table.

Petra stood in the dark hall of a house that predated the drowning of Harrow's Bend, preserved like a fly in amber, occupied, and felt her skepticism—the whole load-bearing structure of her—begin, very quietly, to crack.

She should have left. She knew that even then. There is a moment in every bad decision where the exit is still open and lit, and hers was an eight-inch gap behind her, and she looked at it, and then she looked at the lamplight, and she chose the lamplight, because she was, whatever else she was, a surveyor of the unknown, and the unknown had never once invited her in before.

She moved down the hall, weight on the outside edges of her feet, the way she'd learned for old floors. Past a parlor: furniture under dust sheets, but the sheets recently lifted, folded back with care, a rocking chair still swaying faintly as though someone had just risen from it. Past a wall of framed portraits—dozens of them, oil and sepia and faded color, faces arranged in a grid like a family tree pressed flat. She didn't stop. She would remember later that she didn't stop, and wonder why she'd been afraid to.

The lamplight came from a kitchen. Of course it did. The impossible, she was beginning to understand, was not dramatic. The impossible was domestic. It baked bread. It kept a clock wound.

A woman stood at the stove with her back to the doorway, and Petra had time to register details with the helpless precision of her profession: gray hair pinned in a coil, a dress of undyed linen, forearms bare and strong, a pot of something steaming. The woman said, without turning, without surprise, in a voice like a door opening in a quiet house:

"You're early."

Petra's mouth opened. Nothing came out. Every sentence she owned deserted her at once.

The woman turned. She was perhaps sixty, perhaps ninety—it was impossible to say; her face had the ageless quality of someone who had grown old indoors, away from weather. Her eyes went over Petra the way Petra's eyes went over maps: thoroughly, looking for errors. The helmet. The suit. The harness. The face.

Then the woman crossed the kitchen, took down a ledger from a shelf—cloth-bound, fat, its corners worn round—and opened it on the table, and turned pages with the unhurried certainty of someone consulting a document she had known her whole life. Her finger traveled down a column of names written in ink gone brown. It stopped.

She looked up.

"Winterbourne," she said. "P." A pause, in which the clock ticked twice. "You've been a long time coming."

"I think you have me confused—" Petra started, and her voice sounded absurdly loud, absurdly modern, in that room.

"No," the woman said. It was not unkind. It was not kind at all. It was simply certain, the way a level is certain, the way a plumb line is certain. She turned the ledger around on the table so it faced Petra, and held the lamp closer, and there, in a column of forty-one names, each closed with a date except one, was a line of brown ink that read:

*WINTERBOURNE.*

No first initial beyond the P. No date. An open entry, waiting, the way a place is set at a table.

"My name is Ilsa," the woman said, and closed the ledger, and rested both hands on its cover as if it were something sleeping. "And you're standing in my kitchen, so you'll sit, and you'll eat, because whatever you came thinking, you came a long way down, and no one travels that far on an empty stomach."

The chair scraped when Petra pulled it out. The bread was warm. The butter was real. The clock ticked, and ticked, and somewhere far above, faint as a memory, she thought she heard the sound of earth settling—or shifting—or turning over in its sleep.

Ilsa watched her eat, and did not answer a single question, and smiled once, briefly, like a hostess timing something.

"The moon's soon," she said. "We should talk about the count."
