## 6. Fifty Years Between Harvests

The moon came up over the ridge like a coin someone had bitten, and Ilsa Vane knew it was time because the lamps guttered all at once, every one of them, in the same breath.

She was eleven years old. She had been told about this night her whole life the way other children were told about birthdays and funerals, and she had believed it the way children believe things—completely and without weight. Now the lamps went out together and lit themselves again with a color wrong in them, a yellow gone toward amber gone toward something older, and belief arrived in her body before it arrived in her mind. Her stomach dropped. Her hands went cold around the bread knife she'd been holding.

"Mama," she said, and then didn't say anything else, because Mama was already moving through the house with her arms full of candles, calling names.

"Count off," Mama called. "Everyone to the hall. Count off."

And the family counted. That was the ritual. Forty voices from forty rooms, some sleepy, some already weeping, each one a name spoken into the lamplight while Papa stood at the ledger desk with the book open and made his small marks, one by one, pen scratching like a mouse in the walls.

"Vane, Marta."

"Here."

"Vane, Aldous."

"Here."

"Tomas," Mama called, and waited.

Old Tomas lived in the room at the end of the east corridor, the one with no windows because there were no windows anywhere, only painted ones, only skies that never changed. He was ninety-one years old and had been old since Ilsa could remember, so old that he remembered the harvest before last, 1911, when he was a young man and the door had been sealed behind them for the first time. He shuffled into the hall now, leaning on the wall, and said, "Here, Marta. I'm here." And everyone looked at him too long, and everyone looked away too fast.

Ilsa understood, in the way you understand a thing you have been forbidden to understand, what the looking meant.

Forty names on the ledger. The count had to come to the ledger whole. That was the rule beneath all the rules: *the count must not come up short.* Her grandmother had drilled it into her mother, who drilled it into all of them at supper, between the bread and the salt. Not why. Never why. Only *what*: the count must not come up short, because the house keeps score, and the house does not forgive an open entry.

* * *

The days before the moon were the strangest days Ilsa knew, stranger even than the sealing itself, because the house prepared alongside them. Doors they had walked past for years would be shut, and opening them revealed rooms rearranged—the long table moved to face the east wall, the chairs turned, the portraits hung lower, closer to eye level, as if the painted ancestors wanted a better view of what was coming. The stair down to the cistern lengthened by four steps. Nobody remarked on it. You learned early not to remark on the house's moods, the same way you learned not to remark on a dog growling at an empty corner.

In those days the family did everything gently. They spoke softly. They ate slowly. Old Tomas was given the best of everything—the heel of the honey loaf, the chair nearest the hearth, first turn at the water barrel—and Ilsa had always thought it was because he was oldest. Now, watching them serve him, watching him accept, she understood it was because he was *chosen*. The best of everything for the one who would not need it long.

Nobody said chosen. Nobody said taken. The word the family used, in their soft voices, in their gentle days, was *paid*.

"The count comes due," Papa said at supper, "and the house is patient, and we are grateful, and we pay what is owed."

"What's owed?" Ilsa asked, because she was eleven and eleven asks.

Marta put her hand flat on the table—not a slap, just pressure, just weight—and said, "Enough," and the meal continued in silence, and Ilsa ate her honey loaf crust and hated herself for how good it tasted.

* * *

The night itself began with the lamps and ended with the door.

Between those two events there were hours that Ilsa would spend the rest of her life trying not to remember, and trying to remember, because forgetting felt like betrayal and remembering felt like drowning, and she never found a way to hold both.

They dressed Tomas in his good coat. He let them, standing in the hall with his arms out like a scarecrow, patient as furniture. His eyes were clear. That was the worst part, she decided later—his eyes were completely clear, and he looked at each of them in turn, and when he reached her, eleven-year-old Ilsa pressed against the wall by the portrait of the woman who might have been her great-grandmother, he smiled.

"Don't cry, little one," he said. "I've been owed since 1911. It's a mercy, being paid out. Debts are heavy when you carry them long enough."

"I don't understand," she whispered.

"No," he agreed kindly. "Pray you never do. Pray you only keep the book."

Then the house began to breathe.

That was what the grown-ups called it, afterward, when they spoke of it at all—*the breathing.* The corridors swelled and eased, swelled and eased, slow as sleep, and the temperature rose, and the smell came, warm bread and warm earth and something underneath both, sweetish, like fruit gone over. The portraits' painted eyes followed movement—Ilsa had always half-believed that, the way you half-believe things in the dark—but tonight it wasn't half. Tonight Great-Grandmother Vera watched Tomas walk the corridor with her whole painted face, and her mouth, which had been painted closed in 1890 or whenever, was very slightly open.

The family walked him to the inner door. Not the iron door—the iron door faced outward, toward the world that had forgotten them, and it stayed bolted. The inner door stood at the heart of the house, where no one went except on paying nights: a door of dark wood with no handle, only a recess shaped exactly like two hands cupped together.

Papa set his hands in the recess. The door swung inward on air that glowed faintly, like light through closed eyelids.

Ilsa was not supposed to see. She was supposed to be in the west rooms with the younger children, singing the counting song loud enough to drown whatever sounds came from the heart of the house. But she had run ahead, or lagged behind—she never could remember which—and she stood at the corner of the corridor and saw her grandfather's generation guide old Tomas across the threshold, saw the glow take him the way water takes a stone, gently, without any violence at all. Saw him turn once, inside, and mouth something to the family. To the ledger. She couldn't read his lips. For sixty years she would try.

The door closed. The glow went out. The breathing stopped mid-breath, like a sleeper holding still to listen.

And then the house was satisfied. That was the only word for it. The lamps steadied. The warmth drained away. Somewhere deep below, a sound like a page turning.

Marta found Ilsa at the corridor's corner and didn't scold her. She knelt down, took her daughter's face in her flour-dusted hands, and said, "Now you know. And now you'll help me keep the count, won't you? All your life. Whatever happens. The count must not come up short."

"Why?" Ilsa asked. "What happens if—"

"If it comes up short," Marta said, "the house goes looking. And Ilsa—" her mother's voice did something Ilsa had never heard it do, cracked wide open, terrified, honest— "the house has been underground a hundred years, and it is *so hungry*, and it doesn't know its own strength anymore. If the count comes up short, it won't send for one of us. It will go through us. And then it will go through the door. Do you understand? It will go looking for whoever's names are written in it. All of them. Every line."

Ilsa thought of the ledger desk, the fat book with its leather gone soft as skin, the columns of names in ink browned with age. She had seen her own name there once, sneaking, VANE, I., and beside it a blank space waiting for a date, and she had run to her room and been sick.

"Yes, Mama," she said.

"Say the whole thing."

"The count must not come up short."

"Again."

"The count must not come up short."

Marta held her a moment longer than necessary, then stood, wiped her hands on her apron, and became again the woman who ran the household, and the family sealed the iron door that night with bolts and prayers and fresh oil on the hinges, because oiled hinges were the one vanity the house allowed—it liked to be opened smoothly, when it was time—and they banked the hearth fire that had burned since 1911 and they went to bed, and above them, far above, the moon crossed the ridge and kept going as if nothing had happened anywhere at all.

* * *

Afterward there were thirty-nine names in the hall, and the family counted them every single morning, and Ilsa grew tall doing it.

She took the pen when she was fourteen, because her mother's hands had begun to shake—not from age; Marta was fifty-two and had been shaking since the paying night, since the glow took her father-in-law, since she had knelt on cold brick and taught her daughter the sentence that would run her whole life. The shaking got worse each year. By the time Ilsa took the ledger, her mother couldn't hold the pen steady enough to close an entry, and the closing mattered, the ink mattered, everything about the book mattered, because the book was not a record of the house.

The book was the house. Or the house was the book. Ilsa never fully sorted which direction that truth ran, and eventually stopped trying, the way you stop trying to sort which end of the rope is holding which.

She learned the entries. She learned that the ledger predated 1911, that the oldest pages were written in a hand so fine it looked printed, and that the earliest names weren't Vanes at all. She learned that new lines appeared sometimes without anyone writing them—she would open the book and find a name added in ink that matched no pen in the house, a cousin's child born above ground decades ago, a stranger's surname she didn't know, and beside it a blank space, waiting. Waiting for what, she didn't ask. The blank spaces frightened her more than the paying did. A paid entry was grief. An open entry was appetite.

And she learned the arithmetic of it, the terrible simple arithmetic her mother had died still whispering: every fifty years, one name. One name per harvest, drawn from the ledger, from the family, from the lines the book claimed. The house was patient because it was certain. The house was certain because the book said so. And the book said so because—

Because someone had to keep writing.

Ilsa wrote. Through the lean winters when the flour ran gray, through the fever year that took three children under twelve and nearly broke the count itself—she entered them, dated them, closed them with a steady hand while her own heart hammered, because dead was dead but *unwritten* was unthinkable, an unwritten name was a hole in the hull. Through the years when the young ones asked her, the way she had asked, *what's owed,* and she gave them her mother's answer: Enough. Through the years when the asking stopped, which was worse.

Thirty-nine names. Then thirty-eight. Then fewer. The families married within, because there was no without, and the generations folded inward like hands, and the house watched, and the hearth burned, and Ilsa kept the count.

She was seventy-three years old now, and her hands had begun to shake, and she had passed the pen to her granddaughter, and every morning the family counted off in the lamplight, and every morning Ilsa checked the open entries the way other people check the weather.

There were forty open entries in the ledger now. Open lines. Names the book claimed but the house hadn't collected—descendants scattered above ground by the vanishing of 1911, lines that had slipped out from under the roof before the door was ever sealed. WINTERBOURNE was among them. It had been among them for a hundred years, a name in faded ink with a blank space after it, and Ilsa had looked at that space every day of her working life the way you look at a debt you cannot collect and cannot forgive.

Lately, though, the blank spaces had begun to trouble her differently. Because the house had started breathing again. Off-schedule. Early. Little shivers in the walls, doors drifting a hand's width from where they'd been left, the lamps guttering on ordinary evenings when no moon worth naming was anywhere near the ridge.

The house was getting restless. Fifty years was a rhythm, but hunger was a hunger.

And then, three nights ago, a stranger had come through the maintenance gap behind the iron door—a woman with chalk dust on her sleeves and a surveyor's lamp and Winterbourne cheekbones, walking into the lamplight as if she'd been expected, and Ilsa had opened the ledger with her heart going like a girl's, and read the entry, and looked up at the face above the open line.

Not overdue, she thought, watching the woman deny her own name, deny the bread, deny everything. *Arrived.*

Ilsa poured her tea anyway, and smiled the family smile, and began—quietly, in the place behind her ribs where the eleven-year-old still knelt at the corridor's corner—to make her plans.
