## 12. The Boy Who Waits at Doors

The door had been shut his whole life, and then one morning it wasn't.

Emeric was nine years old and he had never seen the sky. He knew the word the way he knew the word *ocean* — from the ledger, from Great-Aunt Ilsa's voice, from the portraits that hung in the Long Gallery where the painted people all looked slightly to the left, as if something had once walked past them and they were still waiting for it to come back. *Sky* was a word that belonged to the painted people. They had stood under it. Their faces had a particular kind of light on them that the lamps in the under-house could not make, a light that came from everywhere at once and cast no shadows, and when Emeric asked Ilsa what it was called she said *daylight*, and when he asked what daylight was like she thought for a long time and said, *like being looked at by something that loves you.*

He thought about that a lot. He decided he would know it when he felt it.

Then the door came open, and the light that came through the gap was not daylight — it was the wrong color, yellow-white and buzzing, and it made the dust in the air glitter like the sugar Ilsa rationed at Midwinter — but it was *light*, and it came from the other side of the world, the world where the sky was, and Emeric decided that was close enough to start.

* * *

The family called it the Breach, and they said it the way they said the word *ledger*, flat and careful, the way you say a word that is listening. The Breach had come with the digging men. Emeric had heard them for weeks before they arrived — a tapping in the walls, far off, like a woodpecker the size of a house — and he had told Ilsa, and Ilsa had put her hand flat against the brick and listened with her whole palm, and then she had called the family to the Long Gallery and read them the count.

The count was forty names. It was always forty names. Forty-one had gone into the house in 1911, and the house had taken one, and forty remained, and every fifty years the count was called again and the house took its due and the number went back to forty and the door was sealed and everyone lived. That was the arithmetic of Emeric's whole world. Forty names, one owed, fifty years. He could recite it before he could recite the alphabet, because the alphabet was just letters and the count was *them*.

But the digging men had come early. Fifty years was not up. Emeric had done the sums on his slate a dozen times, and Ilsa had done them too, and the sums said they had two more years, and the sums were wrong, and that frightened everyone except Emeric, who did not fully understand yet that the world outside the house was a place where sums could be wrong. In the under-house, the sums held. The lamps burned the same. The bread rose the same. Great-Uncle Anselm's cough came every February like a debt. Emeric had never been anywhere the rules broke, and so he could not yet imagine a place where they did.

The Breach was at the end of the Entry Hall, past the coat pegs that had held no coats in living memory, past the umbrella stand full of dried-out umbrellas that no rain had ever touched. The iron door stood open now — not swung wide, but ajar, a hand's width of the buzzing yellow light along its edge — because the digging men had cut the hinges and then, for reasons no one in the family understood, they had gone away and left it. Ilsa wanted it shut. Ilsa wanted it *bricked*. But the house had opinions now, the house was waking, and the house wanted the door open, and when the house wanted a thing, doors stayed where the house put them.

So Emeric sat by the door. Every day. He sat cross-legged on the cold flagstones with his back against the warm frame — the frame was always warm, like a sleeping animal — and he looked at the yellow light and he talked to the digging men.

They could not see him. He had learned this the first day, when he'd pressed his eye to the gap and seen a man in a yellow hat standing not three feet away, and had said *hello* in a voice that cracked with excitement, and the man had not turned his head. The man had been talking to another man about *load-bearing* this and *the client* that, words that meant nothing, words like weather from a country Emeric would never visit. But they talked *near* the door, sometimes, and if Emeric sat very close and spoke at the gap, sometimes they paused. Sometimes they said, *Did you hear that?* Sometimes they said, *It's the acoustics, this whole shaft is a throat.*

Emeric liked that. He was the voice in the throat. He was the house's small ghost, and the digging men were afraid of him, and being feared was, he had decided, the closest thing to being seen that the world offered.

* * *

His favorite was the one they called Dade.

Emeric knew this because the others said it — *Dade wants it opened, Dade's got the contract, Dade says the client wants access* — and because Dade was the one who came closest. Dade was big, with a voice like a rockslide, and he wore a light on his head that swung around and caught the gap in the door and made it blaze like a coin. Dade was not afraid. That was the thing Emeric liked best about him. The other men flinched at the voices from the throat; Dade leaned *toward* them. Once, Emeric had hummed — the Harvest Hymn, the one they sang at the count, the one with the low note that Ilsa said was older than the family — and Dade had gone quiet, and then Dade had hummed it *back*, wrong, but trying, and Emeric had pressed both hands to the warm iron and laughed until Ilsa found him there and pulled him away by the collar.

"You do not sing to them," she had said, kneeling, holding his shoulders, her face very close and very serious. "You do not sing to the ones outside. Do you understand me, Emeric?"

"Why?"

"Because the house listens when we sing. And the house is already listening too hard."

That was the other thing about the Breach. The house had begun to change. Everyone said so, in the careful flat voice they used for words that were listening. Doors that had been in the east wall were in the north wall. The stairs to the cellars had grown three steps overnight, and Uncle Piotr had counted them twice with a candle and made everyone come and look, and nobody had said *that's impossible* because nothing in the house was impossible, the house was the one place where everything that happened had always been about to happen. Ilsa said it was the count coming due. Ilsa said the house was stretching, like a man waking, like a dog turning in its sleep. Emeric thought it was more like the house was *leaning*. Leaning toward the open door. Leaning toward the yellow light and the voices in the throat, hungry the way Emeric was hungry, hungry for the same thing.

For the sky. Or for whatever the sky stood for. For the world that had forgotten them.

* * *

"You're not a ghost, are you."

Emeric went very still.

It was evening, by the house's reckoning — the lamps dimmed on a schedule Ilsa kept, an inheritance of days no one down here had ever seen — and the yellow light beyond the door had gone, and in its place was a different light, a flashlight, held low, and behind the flashlight was a face. A woman's face. Young, with dirt on it, and wide eyes, and hair pushed back behind her ears like she'd been crawling.

She was looking *at* him. Not through the gap, not past him. At him.

"You can see me," Emeric said. His voice came out small. He hadn't meant it as a question but it was one.

"Of course I can see you." The woman crouched, slow, the way you crouch near a dog you don't know. She was on the wrong side of the door. She was on the *sky* side of the door, and she was talking to him, and her voice was strange — not wrong, just unpracticed, an accent from nowhere he knew, the accent of a place with no echo. "Are you — how old are you?"

"Nine."

"Nine." She said it like the number was a stone she was turning over. "What's your name?"

He hesitated. Ilsa had rules about names. Names were currency; the ledger was full of them; you did not spend yours with strangers. But the stranger had seen him, and no one had ever seen him, and there is a hunger older than caution.

"Emeric," he said. "Emeric Voss."

Something crossed her face when he said the surname. He didn't have a word for it. It looked like the portraits looked, that look of recognizing something walking past.

"Petra," she said. "My name is Petra."

"That's a rock," said Emeric, who had learned his words from a ledger and a shelf of books saved from 1911.

"It's a name that used to be a rock." She was looking past him now, into the Entry Hall, at the lamps and the coat pegs and the long dim corridor beyond, and her face was doing the thing faces did in the house when they were counting. "How many of you are in there?"

"Forty."

"Forty," she repeated, and her voice went strange on it, flattened, careful. "And you live down here. All of you. You were born down here."

"I was born in the Blue Room," Emeric said, because it was true and because he wanted her to keep talking to him. "Everyone is born in the Blue Room. It's the warmest one. Are you from the sky?"

She looked at him for a long time. In the flashlight's glow her eyes were very bright and very tired.

"Yeah," she said finally. "I'm from the sky."

"What's it like?"

The question came out of him the way questions came out of him, all at once, unguarded, and he saw it land on her like a weight. She looked down at her hands. She was quiet for so long that Emeric thought she hadn't heard, and then she said, "It's big. It's so big that when you look at it you feel like you might fall up into it. And it changes. It's never the same twice. In the morning it's gray and then it's pink and then it's blue, this blue that doesn't — " She stopped. She pressed the back of her hand to her mouth for a second. "There's weather. Rain that comes from *above* you, from nowhere, just falls out of the sky. And wind. Wind is air that moves, all by itself, nobody pumping it, nobody — " She stopped again. "You've really never seen it. Any of it."

"Great-Aunt Ilsa says daylight is like being looked at by something that loves you."

Petra made a sound that was not quite a laugh. "Does she." She looked at him a long moment. "Listen. Emeric. I need you to do something for me. Can you do something for me?"

He nodded so hard his neck hurt.

"Stay away from this door. When I'm not here, stay away from it. And if the big man comes — Dade, the one with the head lamp — if he comes down here, you hide. You take everyone and you hide. Can you do that?"

"Why? Dade isn't bad. Dade hummed the hymn."

Something moved behind Petra's eyes, fast, like a fish under ice. "He hummed it *wrong*," she said, "and he hummed it back for the wrong reasons. Emeric, I mean it. The house is waking up. Do you feel it? The stairs, the doors?"

"The house is leaning," Emeric said.

Petra went still. "Is that what your family calls it?"

"That's what *I* call it. The house leans toward the door. Like me."

Petra looked at the door then, at the warm iron frame, and Emeric watched her touch it with two fingers, lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal you're not sure of, and he watched her take the fingers away very fast.

"I have to go," she said. "They'll notice I'm gone. Emeric — the count. Your family does a count. Every fifty years. What happens at the count?"

Emeric knew the answer the way he knew his own hands. "The house calls the roll," he said. "And the roll has to be full. And then the house takes what it's owed, and we seal the door, and we live."

"Takes what it's owed," Petra said. "Takes *what*."

"One of us," said Emeric, simply, because it was simply true, because he had known it since before he could read, the way you know the shape of your own room in the dark. "That's why there's forty. There were forty-one. The house took Great-Great-Uncle Tomas in the last count, before I was born, and Ilsa was a girl then, and she sealed the door herself. She has the strongest arm in the family. She's sealed it twice."

Petra had gone the color of the paper in the ledger. "And this time? This count? Who does the house take this time?"

Emeric shrugged. He had never wondered. It was like wondering who would be born next; the ledger knew, and the family didn't ask, and asking was a kind of bad luck, and besides — he was nine, and nine-year-olds were not taken, everyone knew that, the house took the old ones or the ones whose names had been open the longest. He was safe. He was the youngest. He was the future, and you did not spend the future; you kept it, the way Ilsa kept the sugar.

"I don't know," he said. "The ledger knows. Ilsa reads it. Are you coming back?"

Petra stood. She looked at him for a long time, and her face did the portrait thing again, the recognizing thing, and then she did something no one had ever done to Emeric in his whole life: she reached through the gap in the door and touched his hair. Just briefly. Just once. Her hand was cold and smelled like chalk and river water.

"Every day," she said. "I'll come back every day. Stay away from Dade. And Emeric — " she was already moving backward, into the dark of the shaft, her flashlight swinging — "if Ilsa ever opens the ledger in front of you, and your name is in it, and the entry isn't closed — you run. You don't ask questions. You run to me. Do you understand?"

"No," said Emeric honestly.

"Then just remember: run to me." And the flashlight swung away, and the yellow light beyond the door came back on, far away now, and boots crunched on gravel, and then there was nothing but the hum of the digging men's machines, dying out, and the warm door, and the dark hall behind him where forty people were waiting for him to come tell them what he'd seen.

* * *

He told them everything, of course, all except the touching of his hair, which he kept in the secret place behind his ribs where he kept the word *sky*. Ilsa listened with her arms folded and her face like a closed book, and when he was done she said, "You spoke to her. You told her your name."

"She saw me," Emeric said. "She's the first one who ever — "

"Names are currency." Ilsa's voice was not angry. It was worse than angry; it was *careful*. "You spent yours."

"She gave me hers," Emeric said. "Petra. It's a fair trade."

Ilsa looked at him for a long time, and something in her face wavered, some old thing, some girl-shaped thing from 1961, and then she knelt and took his hands and said, "Emeric. My boy. Listen to me. If that woman comes back — and she will come back — you be kind to her. Kinder than kind. Because I think she is going to save us, and I think it is going to cost her more than she knows, and I think — " and here Ilsa stopped, and looked over Emeric's head at the open door, at the yellow light, at the leaning house, and her voice dropped to almost nothing — "I think the ledger already knows her better than I do."

That night Emeric could not sleep. He lay in the Blue Room, which was warm, which was the warmest room, and he listened to the house lean. It was a sound like a great floor settling, except it didn't settle; it *shifted*, and held, and shifted again, and somewhere far off in the dark a door that had been in the east wall for a hundred years opened in the north wall, and closed, and opened again, practicing.

Emeric got up. He knew he wasn't supposed to. He walked the long hall in his bare feet, past the sleeping rooms, past the Long Gallery where the painted people looked slightly to the left, all the way to the Entry Hall, to the door.

The yellow light was off. The digging men had gone home to their sky. But the gap in the door was there, a hand's width of pure black, and the frame was warm under his hands, warmer than it had ever been, warm as a forehead, warm as a fever, and when Emeric pressed his ear to the iron he could hear it.

Not the machines. Not the gravel. Not anything from the sky side at all.

A sound from *inside* the door. From the iron itself. Slow and deep and patient, twice a minute, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

The house had a heartbeat. The house had always had a heartbeat. And it was counting.

Emeric stood there a long time, his small hands flat on the warm iron, listening to the count, until far off in the dark of the shaft he heard boots on gravel, and a flashlight beam came swinging down toward the door, and a voice he knew — Petra's voice, back already, back the very next night, just as she'd promised — said quietly, to no one, to the dark, to him:

"Emeric? Are you there? I need to show you something. It's about the ledger. It's about *your* name."
