## 11. Solstice Approaches

The lights went up on Main Street the Monday after Thanksgiving, and Cyrus Vasquez stood at his classroom window watching two men on a bucket truck wind strings of white bulbs through the bare branches of the elms, and felt something in his chest tighten like a fist closing around a small, cold stone.

He had lived in towns that decorated for Christmas. He had lived in one that strung lights for Halloween and another that hung paper lanterns for a harvest festival nobody could explain the origins of. But Hollowmere did not decorate for Christmas. The banners going up along the storefronts — he could see them from here, red and gold, sewn rather than printed — read *Solstice Festival*, with no mention of any holiday beneath it, no star, no snowman, nothing commercial or borrowed. Just the word itself, in a heavy serif font that looked older than the town's oldest building, as though it had been lifted directly from the ledger pages he had photographed three nights ago and hidden in a place Marisol had sworn no one would think to look.

*The bell must be rung at every solstice,* the founders had written, in a hand so careful it looked like devotion. *And the covenant renewed.*

Cyrus turned back to his desk, to his notebook, and wrote: *They're not preparing for a festival. They're preparing for a feeding.*

His handwriting had gotten worse over the past week. He noticed it now, the way you notice a limp — the letters leaning, cramped, as if his hand were trying to write faster than his mind wanted to admit what it was writing. He forced himself to slow down. Documentation was discipline. Documentation was the only thing standing between him and the soft, warm fog that seemed to settle over everyone else in this building, this town, this whole valley of smiling people who could not remember their own missing hours.

He flipped back through the notebook to the page he'd titled, in block capitals, THE COUNT.

Four names. He had found them in the new entries of the founders' ledger — entries that had not existed when he first opened the book, entries written in a hand that belonged to no living person, ink that dried black and then darkened further, like blood aging under a bandage. Four students, spanning four grades. He had checked each one against his class rosters, against the yearbook photos in the library, against the faces in the hallways.

Tomas Reyes, seventh grade. Quiet boy, chewed his pencils, drew elaborate mazes in the margins of his math homework.

Priya Chandrasekaran, ninth grade. First chair violin. Cyrus had heard her practicing through the wall of the music room, something mournful and beautiful he couldn't name.

Elliot Marsh, eleventh grade. Big kid, gentle, worked afternoons at his father's hardware store.

And Wren Halloway. His own student. Second period. The girl who had come to him with her hands shaking and asked him what was wrong with her, and whom he had lied to so badly that she'd known, immediately, absolutely, the way children always know.

Four names. And the ledger's final page — the page where the fifth entry should be — was blank.

Not empty. Blank. There was a difference, Cyrus had learned. Empty meant nothing had been written yet. Blank meant the space was being held. The paper there had a faint sheen to it, like a stage waiting for an actor, like a bed turned down for a guest whose arrival was expected but not yet scheduled.

He had counted the historical pattern twice, then a third time, cross-referencing the old attendance ledgers all the way back to 1931. Every generation. Always five. Never four, never six. Five names entered, five children taken for a day and returned diminished, and afterward — always afterward — the test scores climbed, the harvests came in, the businesses on Main Street thrived, the town glowed with that terrible, suffocating happiness that Cyrus had mistaken, during his first week, for simple civic pride.

This year required five.

There were four.

The arithmetic sat in his stomach like swallowed ice.

* * *

By Wednesday the whole town smelled of pine and candle wax. Cyrus walked home from school the long way, past the green, where a crew of volunteers was raising a scaffold around the base of the bell tower — the tower that had been sealed since 1974, boarded and chained and officially condemned, the tower Marisol had shown him in October with her breath steaming and her voice low, saying, *Nobody goes up there. Nobody's gone up there in fifty years. That's the point.*

The boards were gone. The chains were gone. The door — heavy oak, iron-banded, which Marisol said had swollen shut decades ago — stood open, propped wide with a sawhorse, and inside Cyrus could see fresh lantern light and the silhouettes of men moving, sweeping, working.

He stopped at the edge of the green and watched, and a woman beside him — bundled in a red coat, holding a thermos, one of the mothers from the pickup line whose name he still didn't know — followed his gaze and smiled.

"First time they've opened it since we were kids," she said warmly. "My father used to tell me about the ringing. You could hear it all the way out to the Kettle Road farms. Whole town would stop whatever they were doing." She sipped from her thermos. "It's good, isn't it? Having it back."

"Good," Cyrus repeated.

"The committee voted in October. Unanimous." She laughed, a bright, easy sound. "Funny — nobody could quite say why we waited so long. It just seemed like time."

*Nobody could quite say why.* Of course they couldn't. The decision had been made for them, somewhere below the level of memory, in the place where the Keeper kept its accounts. Cyrus made himself smile at the woman, made himself say something agreeable about tradition, and walked on with his heart hammering and his notebook burning in his coat pocket like a coal.

That night he added a line to the count page: *The tower is open. They are cleaning it themselves. The town is decorating its own altar.*

* * *

Thursday morning, Wren was absent.

Cyrus took attendance with his pen hovering over her name for three full seconds before marking it, and the pause cost him something he couldn't name. Across the room, the empty desk — second row by the window, the one where sunlight pooled at ten o'clock — looked like an accusation. He thought of the first time he'd seen that desk empty, back in September, before he understood anything, when absence had been just absence and not a symptom.

But she came back the next day. That was the thing. She came back the next day, pleasant and polite and hollow-eyed, and no one remembered she'd been gone.

Except this time, when she slid into her seat, she caught his eye across the room. And she didn't smile.

She mouthed something. Two words. He read them clearly, and his blood went cold.

*Five days.*

She held up her hand, fingers spread, and folded them down one at a time until only a fist remained, and then she looked away and opened her textbook like a girl who had done nothing remarkable at all.

Cyrus taught the rest of the period on autopilot. He was fairly sure he assigned a chapter. He was fairly sure he wrote on the board. What he actually remembered afterward was the count: five days until the solstice. Five days, four names, and a blank page waiting in a book written by dead men in a hand that wasn't theirs.

At lunch he locked himself in the archive room — the little windowless cell off the library where the district stored forty years of records nobody wanted — and spread the photographs of the ledger across the table again. He had taken them with Marisol's camera, printed them at the drugstore in Corning, twenty miles away, because he no longer trusted any machine inside Hollowmere's borders. The prints were grainy but legible. The four names stared up at him. Below them, the blankness of the fifth line seemed almost to breathe.

He had a theory about the blank line. He hated the theory. He had been carrying it around for a week like a stone in his shoe, refusing to look at it directly, and now, alone in the archive with the heating pipes ticking and the smell of old paper thick in his throat, he finally let himself look.

The Keeper had noticed him. That much was certain — Aldous's dinner, the gentle suggestions, the purged emails, the blanked pages of his first notebook. The school was editing him. Something in Hollowmere had marked him as a problem to be solved, a thread to be trimmed. And the ledger chose its five from among those the covenant touched most closely. That was the pattern he hadn't wanted to see: not random children, never random. The chosen ones were always connected — to the teachers, to the founding families, to the people whose belief kept the bargain alive. Tomas Reyes was the nephew of the vice principal. Priya's mother ran the festival committee. Elliot Marsh's family had signed the original 1931 petition; Cyrus had found the signature, *E. Marsh*, in the scanned pages, the ink faded to brown.

And Wren — Wren, who could hear the questions in her sleep, who had learned the rules, who had come to him with her plan half-formed and her eyes too old for her face.

Wren was connected to him. She was his student. She trusted him. And the Keeper, patient and flat-voiced in his recordings, answering questions no one had asked aloud, had spent weeks learning exactly who Cyrus Vasquez was and what he loved.

The fifth name would be hers. Unless it was his.

He gathered the photographs with hands that were not quite steady, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he had perhaps five days to find a way to break a ninety-year-old covenant or watch a fourteen-year-old girl walk into a bell tower with her smiling parents and come out the other side missing pieces she would never know she'd lost.

* * *

Friday evening he went to Marisol's.

She lived in a narrow house on Delver Street, behind the church, in a neighborhood where every porch light burned yellow and every yard had a broom leaned against the railing — swept clean, all of them, the whole street swept clean, and Cyrus wondered how many other small compulsions in this town were really wards, really rituals, really the sediment of a bargain settling into daily habit.

Marisol answered the door before he knocked. She'd been crying — he could see it in the rawness around her eyes — but her voice was level.

"You saw the tower," she said.

"I saw the tower."

"They finished the roof yesterday. New shingles. My uncle's roofing company did it, and he told my aunt it was the proudest job he'd ever worked." She stepped aside to let him in. "He cried a little, Cy. Said it felt like coming home. He doesn't remember ever seeing the tower before in his life."

Inside, the house was warm and cluttered and smelled of coffee and lemon oil. On the kitchen table, under a lamp, lay the surviving copy of everything — the photographs, the transcriptions, the Latin passages Marisol had translated in her careful schoolteacher's cursive, the recordings dubbed onto cassette because she didn't trust digital files, didn't trust anything that could be edited without leaving a mark. Cyrus had a duplicate set hidden in a storage unit in Corning. Between the two of them, they had built a small fortress of proof, brick by paranoid brick.

Marisol poured coffee neither of them drank and sat across from him, folding her hands.

"Say it," she said. "You've been carrying it all week. Say it out loud."

"The ledger needs five names. It has four." He laid the photograph of the final page on the table between them. "The fifth line is blank, Marisol. Not empty. Blank. It's waiting."

"And?"

"And I don't think it's waiting for a child." His voice came out lower than he intended. "I've been going through the historical entries. In 1953, the fifth name was a teacher. Ruth Calloway. She resigned mid-year and moved away, and the ledger has her name anyway, and the attendance record shows she missed one day in December and came back and taught for eleven more years and never once appears in a faculty photograph smiling. In 1938, the fifth name was the pastor's son. In 1991—" He stopped. Swallowed. "In 1991, the fifth name was a night custodian. Francisco Delgado. He left town in January. No forwarding address. The ledger has his name."

Marisol had gone very still. The lamp hummed. Somewhere outside, a car passed, tires hissing on cold asphalt, and the ordinary sound of it made the silence worse.

"Frankie," she said finally. "I knew Frankie. He taught me to drive stick in the parking lot, summer of '91. Nice man. Sad eyes." Her thumb moved over the edge of the table, back and forth, a small repetitive motion like a rosary. "I always wondered why he left so sudden. Everybody said he got a job offer. Nobody remembered what job."

"He was the watcher," Cyrus said. "Like me. Somebody who saw the pattern and started asking questions, and the covenant reached for him instead of a child, because the Keeper prefers—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked like a whip, and then softened immediately, apologetic, and he saw how hard she was gripping herself. "Don't say it prefers. Don't make it sound like it has taste. It's a thing that eats, Cy. That's all it is. A thing that eats, and a town that sets the table."

They sat with that. The clock on her stove ticked. Down the street, faintly, someone was singing — a few bars of the hymn, the off-key one from his recordings, hummed by a voice too far away to identify, drifting through the frozen air like smoke.

"Wren thinks there's another way," Cyrus said at last. "The rules she learned. It must answer truthfully in the bell's presence. It cannot take what is freely surrendered." He turned his coffee cup in a slow circle. "I keep turning those two rules against each other. If it can't take what's freely given — then what happens if someone gives it something big enough? Bigger than five children's missing days. Bigger than a handful of memories."

Marisol looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her arrive at the same place he'd been arriving at all week, the place he hadn't said aloud even to himself, and her face did something complicated — fear and grief and a fierce, stubborn love all fighting for the same ground.

"No," she said.

"It's an option."

"It's a stupid option. You give that thing your memories, you won't know why you gave them. You'll be standing in that tower tomorrow morning wondering who you are and why your pockets are full of paper." She leaned forward. "I lost my brother to this thing. Do you understand? I am the only person alive who remembers Daniel existed, and I remember because I fought for it, every single day, for fifty years. I am not losing anyone else. Not a child, and not some meticulous fool from out of town who writes everything down because he's scared of forgetting." Her voice broke on the last words, and she pressed her knuckles to her mouth for a moment. "We find another way. We have five days. We have Wren. We have the truth. That's more than anybody's ever had."

Cyrus wanted to believe her. He looked at the photographs spread across her table — ninety years of theft, neatly documented, the town's prosperity itemized in other people's missing hours — and he wanted very badly to believe there was a version of the next five days that ended with everyone whole.

"Okay," he said. "Another way. But we work fast, and we work tonight, because—"

Because the ledger was filling in. Because the tower was clean and lit and waiting. Because the whole town was winding white lights through bare branches and singing a hymn in the dark, and the blank fifth line was patient, and patience, in his experience, was the scariest quality a predator could have.

Marisol was reaching for the cassette player when the knock came at her door.

Three knocks. Even, unhurried, polite.

They both froze. Through the thin curtains of the front window, Cyrus could see a shape on the porch — a small shape, hooded against the cold, hands jammed in the pockets of a coat he recognized, because he'd seen it hanging on the hook outside his second-period classroom every morning for three months.

Wren Halloway stood on Marisol's porch at nine o'clock at night, five days before the solstice, and she was not supposed to be here, and she was not supposed to know this address, and when Marisol opened the door the girl looked past her straight at Cyrus with eyes that had seen too much, and said:

"It changed. Tonight. While I was walking here, I heard it — the way I hear it in my sleep, but awake, out loud, right next to my ear." Her breath came out in white plumes. "It stopped asking questions, Mr. Vasquez. It only asked one. And then it said the list was complete."

Behind her, far across the sleeping town, a single sound rose from the freshly cleaned tower — not the bell, not yet, but something like a bell clearing its throat, a low metallic hum that Cyrus felt in his teeth, in his sternum, in the pages of the notebook against his ribs.

Somewhere in a locked drawer in a locked office in Hollowmere School, in handwriting that belonged to no living person, the fifth name had just finished drying.
