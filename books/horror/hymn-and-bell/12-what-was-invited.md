## 12. What Was Invited

Marisol had not opened the ledger in forty years, and her hands remembered the weight of it anyway.

She carried it to the boiler room beneath the east wing, where the pipes sang their low winter note and no one came after ten o'clock. She had keys to every door in Hollowmere School, had held them for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had learned that the safest room was always the one nobody wanted. The boiler room smelled of rust and machine oil and the ghost of coal, and it was warm the way a den is warm, and she set the ledger on the workbench under the single caged bulb and stood over it for a long moment before touching it again.

Cyrus sat on an overturned milk crate across from her, notebook open on his knee, pen uncapped. He had brought two flashlights and a thermos of coffee he never drank. He kept looking at the ledger the way a man looks at a dog that has bitten someone — respectfully, from a distance.

"You could read Latin," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"My brother taught me." The words came out of her before she could stop them, the way they always did when Rafael's name got anywhere near the air. "Rafael. He wanted to be a priest until he decided he wanted to be free instead. He said the Latin was the same either way — you just had to know who was talking." She pulled on the white cotton gloves she'd taken from the archive box, because whatever else this book was, it was old paper and old paper deserved better than what was coming. "He'd have loved this. He'd have read it straight through and laughed at all the wrong parts."

"Marisol."

"I'm stalling." She said it plainly, because Cyrus Vasquez appreciated plainness; it was one of the few things about him she'd decided she liked. "I've been stalling since Tuesday. You want to know what they invited. I want to keep not knowing, the way I've kept not knowing for fifty years. But my brother's already gone into that book somewhere, isn't he. His missing day. So there's nothing left in it that can hurt me worse than it already has."

She turned the cover.

The leather was cracked like drought earth, and underneath it the first pages were soft as cloth. The handwriting inside was beautiful — that was the thing that struck her every time, the thing she'd forgotten between looks. Whoever these founders were, starving or not, desperate or not, they had written their damnation in a hand fit for a wedding invitation. Spencerian loops, flourished capitals, ink gone brown as dried blood but still confident, still *sure*.

"*In the year of our Lord nineteen hundred and thirty-one,*" she read aloud, translating as she went, her voice dropping into the cadence Rafael used to use when he showed off, "*the harvest failed.*"

*The harvest failed. The frost came early and stayed late, and what the frost spared the rot took, and by November the granaries of Hollowmere stood empty as churches on a Wednesday. We have buried four of our number. The children go gray at the cheek. Our wives boil bark.*

She stopped. Cyrus's pen had stopped too.

"They were eating tree bark," he said quietly.

"They were starving. Whole town. My grandmother talked about it once, just once, before she stopped talking about anything from those years. Said her mother made soup out of pine needles and called it tea." Marisol turned the page with a gloved fingertip. "Keep listening. This is where it stops being a history lesson."

*We have prayed as we were taught to pray, and heaven keeps its own counsel. So we have resolved to pray otherwise.*

There was a diagram on the next page. Marisol had seen it before, years ago, in the hour she'd stolen with this book before fear closed her hands — but seeing it now, under this bulb, with Cyrus breathing carefully beside her, it looked different. A circle of names. Thirty-one names, arranged in a ring around a central figure that wasn't drawn so much as *left blank* — a space where a drawing should have been, outlined in careful strokes, the interior left empty. As if whoever drew it understood that some things could only be represented by absence.

"That's them," Cyrus said. "The founders. All thirty-one."

"And that," Marisol touched the blank center without touching it, "is what they invited. Look at the words under it."

*We do not summon a devil. Let no reader mistake us. Devils are imposed upon a place; they come in chains and hate their masters. What we seek is a guest. A keeper. That which the old country called Custos Integritatis — the Keeper of Wholeness — which feeds not on flesh nor soul nor any coin of hell, but on memory alone, and gives back continuity, cohesion, belonging. It does not devour a town. It knits a town. It takes the loose threads of us — our griefs, our grudges, our terrible individual days — and it makes of them a single cloth.*

"It sounds almost kind," Cyrus said, and his voice had gone strange.

"That's how it gets you." Marisol heard the hardness in her own voice and didn't soften it. "That's how it got them. Read the next part. Read what they promised it."

*Its price we know from the old accounts: memory, freely given, in measured portions. Not the whole of a man — the Keeper is no thief — but days. Hours. Selected threads, cut close, so the garment hangs the same and weighs less. And in return: plenty. Continuance. The bell rung at each solstice to seal the covenant, and the town kept whole against famine, against fire, against the slow cold death of forgetting itself.*

"Freely given," Cyrus repeated. "It can't take. It can only receive."

"So it makes you *want* to give." Marisol turned pages faster now, the Latin unspooling under her tongue, and she let herself fall into Rafael's voice entirely, because it was easier to survive this if she pretended he was reading it beside her, sixteen years old and furious at God, translating damnation for his little sister while their mother boiled pine needles downstairs. *We the undersigned do covenant ourselves and our heirs to this arrangement, in love, in desperation, and in the firm belief that a town which forgets its sorrows is a town that can finally be happy.*

"There it is," she said. "Read it again. In love. They wrote *in love*. They signed thirty-one names under that sentence and they meant it, Cyrus. That's the part nobody understands about this town. Nobody sold anybody. Nobody made a deal with the devil in a crossroads at midnight. Thirty-one starving people sat in a room with their children dying gray-cheeked around them and they *loved each other*, and they did this *because* they loved each other, and they wrote it down in their best handwriting so their grandchildren would understand it was a gift."

The boiler ticked. Somewhere above them, a door closed — the ordinary sound of an ordinary school at night, except that neither of them believed in ordinary anymore.

Cyrus was writing. She watched him write, fast, pressing hard, and something in his face had changed. Not fear exactly. Recognition. "The solstice bell," he said. "It seals it. Every toll renews the terms."

"The bell's been sealed since 1974." Marisol heard her own voice go flat and careful, the way it went whenever she approached the year from any direction. "You know that. You've seen the tower."

"I've seen the tower. I haven't seen 1974." He looked up. "What happened in 1974, Marisol?"

And there it was. The question she had walked around her entire life the way you walk around a hole in the floor in the dark — knowing its shape, knowing its depth, never once letting your foot find the edge.

She turned the pages instead. Past the beautiful founding hand into other hands, other decades, entries in English now, bookkeeping entries, terse as a bank statement. *Solstice observed. Five portions received. Town well.* *Solstice observed. Four portions received. The Henderson girl resisted; portion taken complete. Town very well.* The ledgers of a parasite written by its hosts, and every entry signed, and every signature steady.

Then 1974.

The handwriting changed. It was still beautiful — it was Aldous's hand, she realized, younger, rounder, but unmistakably the same — and it shook.

*The covenant is amended. The Keeper has grown accustomed to abundance and will no longer accept five portions per generation. It requires more, and more often, and it has begun choosing for itself. We rang the bell eleven times at midsummer to bind it tighter and it took Elias Reyes's day in payment of the binding itself. We have sealed the tower. We do not know if sealing the tower binds it or merely blinds us. The town remains whole. The town remains happy. God forgive us, we remain unwilling to stop.*

"Elias Reyes," Cyrus said softly.

"My brother." Marisol closed the ledger. Her gloved hands stayed flat on the cover, holding it shut the way you hold a lid on something. "Rafael Reyes. They wrote it down wrong, or the book wrote itself wrong, or maybe by 1974 nobody even remembered his real name anymore — that's the joke of it, isn't it, that's the whole rotten joke. The Keeper eats memory and the book forgot my brother's name." She was surprised to find her eyes wet. Fifty years, and the grief still lived in her body like a splinter working toward the surface. "He lost a day. Just one day, that's what everyone said, that's what the school said, that's what my parents said because the school told them to say it. One day, and then he came back and he wasn't — "

She stopped. Breathed.

"He wasn't *finished*," she said. "That's the best way I can put it. He came back and everything that made him Rafael was thinner. Like a coat worn through at the elbows. He still smiled. He smiled all the time, everybody here smiles all the time, have you noticed that, have you noticed how this town smiles — but he couldn't tell you why. And then the next year another day. And another. And by the time he was twenty he was a pleasant young man who worked at the hardware store and laughed at jokes a half-second late, and my mother cried in the kitchen where she thought I couldn't hear, and my father rang the church bell on Sundays like he was trying to call something home." She lifted her hands off the ledger. "And everyone else in this town looked at Rafael and saw a fine boy doing fine. Because the Keeper doesn't just take from the ones it takes from. It takes the *noticing*. It reaches into every head in Hollowmere and smooths the question flat before it can form. *Why does Wren seem smaller? Why can't I remember October?* Gone. Smoothed. Whole."

Cyrus hadn't written anything in a long time. He was staring at the ledger with an expression she recognized from her own mirror — the look of a person doing arithmetic they don't want finished.

"Ninety years," he said. "Five portions a generation, then more. How many children, Marisol? Since 1931. How many Rafas?"

"I tried counting once. When I was your age." She began stripping off the gloves, finger by finger. "I got past sixty and I stopped, because I realized I was counting the recorded ones, and the book is written by the people the Keeper has already smoothed. Who knows what's missing. Who knows how many days never got written down because nobody was left who could notice them." She folded the gloves and set them on the bench like a small dead thing. "Here's what I need you to understand, Mr. Vasquez, teacher, documenter, keeper of notebooks. Those founders weren't monsters. I need you to sit with that, because it matters for what comes next. They were hungry and terrified and they loved their children, and they found a thing in the dark that promised to save those children, and it *did*. Ninety years, no famine, no fire, no lean year. Test scores up. Crops up. Fortunes up. Every bargain kept, every term honored, to the letter. It is not a liar. It is not cruel. It is a *guest*, and it behaves like one, and it has been sitting at this town's table for ninety years being fed its meals in polite portions by people who smile while they serve it."

"Until it started choosing for itself."

"Until it got hungry the way guests get comfortable." She looked at him, and she made herself say the rest, because he needed it and because saying it out loud was the only weapon she had left. "Wren is on this year's list. You know that. Four names, and the solstice weeks away, and the tower unsealed and scrubbed clean — somebody's preparing the table, Cyrus. Somebody's polishing the silver. And the fifth name isn't written yet, but this book writes itself now, and I have read enough of it to know that it saves the worst line for last."

The caged bulb flickered. Neither of them moved.

"What do we do?" Cyrus asked. His voice was steady. His pen, she noticed, had torn a small hole in the page from pressing.

Marisol looked at the ledger — at the beautiful, loving, damning hand of people long dead who had only wanted their town to survive — and she thought of Rafael laughing half a second late, and Wren's face going pleasant and empty, and the hymn the whole town hummed without knowing why.

"We learn its rules," she said. "Everything that bargains has rules. Everything that must be fed politely can be starved politely, or trapped, or *renegotiated*. We read this book until we know it better than the Keeper knows itself. And then, Mr. Vasquez—" she pushed the ledger across the bench toward him, and the sound of leather on steel was very loud in the singing dark— "we decide whether we're the kind of town that feeds its children to stay happy. Or the kind that burns."

Above them, faint through the concrete floor, someone began to whistle.

Off-key. Patient. The tune from the recordings.

Marisol went cold to the bone, because she knew every pipe and every footstep in this building, and the whistling was coming from the sealed stairwell — the one that hadn't been opened since 1974.

The one that was, as of tonight, standing open.
