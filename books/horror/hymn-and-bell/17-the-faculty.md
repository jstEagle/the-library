## 17. The Faculty

Cyrus did not sleep. He sat at his kitchen table with the fifth ledger entry open before him — *Vasquez, Cyrus* — written in that hand that belonged to no one, in ink that had dried brown and old though he had watched the page only hours after it filled itself. He read his own name until the letters stopped meaning anything, and then he read them again until they meant too much.

At 6:40 in the morning, he packed the surviving notebook into his coat, against his heart, and drove to Hollowmere School in the blue-black hour before dawn. The snowplows hadn't reached the side streets yet. The town lay white and silent under its festival lights, wreaths on every door, candles in every window, a whole community dressed for a celebration he now understood as an altar dressing.

He arrived before anyone. He let himself into the faculty lounge with the key Aldous had given him his first week, back when everything here had seemed merely pleasant, merely small, merely the kind of place where people remembered your coffee order.

He made coffee. He waited.

They came one at a time, as they always did, shaking snow from their coats, stamping their boots, calling out their good mornings in voices warm as bread. Mrs. Okafor from the fourth grade. Mr. Pell, who taught chemistry and coached track. Miss Tran, young, barely two years out of her program, who still put stickers on returned homework. They stopped when they saw him sitting at the head of the long table, the attendance ledgers spread out before him — decades of them, borrowed from the archive, fanned open like the wings of something pinned.

"Cyrus?" Mrs. Okafor said, and her smile flickered — just once, just at the edges — before it reassembled itself. "You're here early."

"So are all of you," Cyrus said. "Earlier than usual. Every day this week, actually. Since the tower was unsealed." He turned a ledger toward them. "Since the solstice got close. Did you notice? I notice things. It's what I do. It's apparently what I *am*, now, according to a book that writes itself."

Pell set down his travel mug very carefully, the way a man does when his hands want to shake and he won't permit it. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"October fourteenth," Cyrus said. "Wren Halloway. Absent one day. Returned with a day missing that no one else remembers either. And then I found this." He opened another ledger, 1988, and laid his finger on a name. "Daniel Okafor. Your nephew, Mrs. Okafor. Or your son — the records are ambiguous, which is interesting in itself. Missing October ninth, 1988. Returned diminished. And here's 1961, and 1973, and 1952, going all the way back. Every generation. A handful of children, taken for a day, returned hollowed, and afterward — always afterward — the test scores rise, the harvest comes in, the mill stays open, the town thrives." He looked around the table, at each of them in turn. "You know. Don't you. You all know."

The lounge was very quiet. Outside, snow ticked against the window. Somewhere down the hall, the boiler knocked its familiar rhythm, and Cyrus thought: even the building sounds like a heartbeat pretending to be machinery.

Miss Tran started to cry. She didn't sob; she simply wept, silently, the way people cry when they have been crying about something for years and have simply never been permitted to do it in front of witnesses. "I was eleven," she said. "I came back and my mother told me I'd been at school the whole time. I believed her. Why wouldn't I believe her?" She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "I've taught here six years and I've never once wondered why I believed her."

"Sit down, Linh," Mrs. Okafor said gently, and pulled out the chair beside her.

The door opened. Principal Aldous stood in the frame with his coat still on, snow melting on his shoulders, and Cyrus understood that he had heard — that he had perhaps been standing there listening, letting his teachers be seen, because Aldous had decided the moment for pretense was over.

"Thank you all," Aldous said quietly. "I'll take it from here."

They filed out past him. Miss Tran paused, wet-eyed, and touched Cyrus's shoulder as she passed — a small human gesture, an apology or a plea, he couldn't tell which. Then the door closed, and the two men were alone with the ledgers and the coffee going cold between them.

Aldous took off his coat. He hung it on the rack by the door with the same unhurried care he brought to everything, and then he sat down across from Cyrus and folded his hands.

"You went through the archive," he said.

"I did."

"The ledger. The Latin. Marisol Reyes helped you, I imagine." Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Something older and wearier. "She's the only one who ever could. The rest of us, we can't hold the whole shape of it. We remember in pieces. That's how it was designed, I think. Or how we designed ourselves, to survive it."

"How long have you known?"

Aldous smiled, and it was the worst thing yet, because it was a kind smile, a genuinely kind smile, the smile of a man who had thought long and hard about what he was about to say and loved the person he was saying it to. "I was taken in March of 1979. I was nine years old. I lost a Tuesday. When I came back, my mother held me and cried, and I didn't understand why, and by supper I'd forgotten she'd cried at all. I became a teacher because I wanted to give children what I couldn't quite reach anymore. And then I became principal because someone has to keep the calendar. Someone has to know when the solstice is coming, and make sure the tower is ready, and sit with the families, and explain — gently, so gently — that their child will be fine, better than fine, that the whole town will be better, and that the missing day is nothing, is a dream, is a gap in a film reel you won't even notice once the picture starts moving again."

"And you believe that."

"I *know* that," Aldous said, and leaned forward. "That's what you don't understand, Cyrus. You've been here three months. You see a horror story. I've lived inside it for forty-five years, and what I see is a town that should have died in 1931. Look at the county records sometime. Every town within fifty miles of us shrank through the Depression. Ours grew. Every mill town on the river went under in '58. Ours didn't. We have no opioid crisis. No exodus of the young. Our graduates come *back*, Cyrus — do you understand how strange that is? How unnatural? People come home to Hollowmere the way salmon come home to spawn, and they can't tell you why, and neither can I, but I can tell you what it costs, and the cost is one day, from a handful of children, once a generation." His voice softened. "One day. Against ninety years of wholeness."

"It's not one day," Cyrus said. "It's everything the day contained. Wren came back and couldn't remember her own birthday party. Her grandmother drove two hours to surprise her and the child looked at her like a stranger. That's not a gap in a film reel. That's a hole with edges that keep spreading."

"She'll heal."

"She'll *hollow*. You did. Look at yourself, Aldous. Look at your teachers. You're all so *pleasant*. You're the kindest faculty I've ever worked with and none of you can tell me what you dreamed last night, because you don't dream anymore, do you? I've checked. I asked. Eleven teachers and not one of them dreams." He stood up, palms flat on the table. "It doesn't take a day and leave the rest. It takes a day and leaves the rest *thinner*. And it's coming back for five more in four days' time, and the fifth name in that book is mine."

Aldous didn't flinch. That was the terrible part. He had known. Of course he had known; the ledger wrote itself in the tower and the tower had been unsealed for a week, and Aldous kept the calendar.

"Yes," Aldous said simply. "I saw. I'm sorry. Truly. I hoped it would pass you over — it usually passes over adults. But it noticed you. The one who watches gets watched back; that's the oldest rule of them all." He rose too, and came around the table, and Cyrus braced himself for threat, for coercion, for the smooth institutional menace he had imagined in the dark hours.

What he got instead was a hand on his arm, gentle as a father's.

"Leave," Aldous said. "I mean it. Pack your car tonight and drive until the snow stops and don't send for your things. If you're gone before the solstice, maybe the claim lapses. Maybe it forgets you. Marisol will tell you the same thing — she's been telling you, hasn't she? Flee, she says. And she's right. You could live. You could have a life somewhere the bells don't ring."

"And the children?"

Aldous closed his eyes. For a moment — just a moment — the pleasantness fell away entirely, and Cyrus saw underneath it: a nine-year-old boy standing in a kitchen watching his mother cry for reasons no one would ever explain to him. When Aldous spoke again, his voice was almost a whisper.

"There were five names the year I was taken, too. Four children and one man — a substitute, a drifter, nobody's friend. He ran. He got as far as the county line." Aldous opened his eyes. "The bell rang anyway. The covenant found its fifth. Do you understand? It doesn't need your consent. It needs a *name*, and the book provides. If you run, it takes some other adult — some parent, some stranger passing through, some poor soul whose car breaks down on Route 9 that night. The book balances. It always balances. The only mercy in ninety years is that it prefers the willing."

"Then I'll break it. Marisol and I — we know what it is now. What it truly is. There are questions it has to answer—"

"*Don't.*" The word cracked out of him, sharp and sudden, and Aldous gripped Cyrus's arm hard enough to bruise, and for one naked instant Cyrus saw not the principal but the congregation — the whole town, ninety years deep, every smiling face at the solstice festival, every candle in every window — looking at him through this one tired man's eyes. "Don't ask it. Whatever Wren told you, whatever rules you think you've found — if you ring that bell and ask it what it is, you won't get justice, you'll get *grief*. Ninety years of it. Every stolen day coming home at once, to every family, all at once, with nowhere to put it. You'll make these children mourn lives they can't even remember losing. You'll make the mothers remember crying. Is that mercy? Is that your documentation? A town drowned in a grief it has no shore for?" His grip gentled again, and his voice with it, and that was the cruelest weapon of all, because it was sincere. "Cyrus. I am begging you. Not as your principal. As a man who was nine years old once, and happy, and never knew what it cost. Let them keep the happiness. Please. Some knowledge is a wound. You of all people — a man who writes everything down — must know that some pages are better left blank."

For a long moment Cyrus said nothing. The boiler knocked. The snow ticked. Somewhere far off, faint as memory, he thought he heard a bell that wasn't ringing yet.

And the horrible thing — the thing he would carry, in whatever form of himself survived — was that he understood. Standing there under Aldous's hand, he felt the full weight of the argument, its warmth, its terrible reasonableness. Compassion, offered like a sedative. Love, asking him to look away.

"That's exactly why I have to do it," Cyrus said quietly. "You're not wrong, Aldous. Grief is coming either way. The only question is whether it comes honestly, or whether it keeps coming back, generation after generation, wearing a festival's clothes." He stepped back, out of the man's reach, and gathered the ledgers into his arms. "You were nine years old, and no one told you why your mother cried. I'm going to make sure someone tells them."

Aldous lowered his hand slowly. When he spoke again, the pleasantness had returned like frost regrowing over a window, and beneath it Cyrus could hear something that might have been sorrow, or might have been warning.

"The tower will be guarded tomorrow night," Aldous said. "By everyone who loves this town. Which is everyone." He picked up his cold coffee, and paused at the door without turning around. "Marisol kept her brother's memory alive for fifty years, Cyrus, and what did it buy her? Fifty years of being the only one grieving. Think about what you're really offering those children. Think about whether you're saving them, or just making sure they suffer like she has."

The door clicked shut behind him.

Cyrus stood alone in the lounge with the dead ledgers and the cooling air, and outside the window the snow kept falling on a town dressed for a wedding, and in his coat, against his heart, the hidden notebook felt suddenly very light and very thin — one man's handwriting, one man's witness, against ninety years of wholeness and four days left to spend it.

Down the hall, faintly, the first bell of the morning began to ring.

* * *

He found Marisol at lunchtime, not in the building but outside it, standing at the edge of the parking lot looking up at the bell tower with her hands jammed in her armpits against the cold. Snow had gathered on her shoulders and she hadn't brushed it off.

"He talked to you," she said, without turning.

"He begged me to run."

"Mm." She was quiet a while. "He begged me too, in '74. Right before they sealed the tower. He was nineteen then. He held the door shut while his father welded it, and he cried the whole time, and the next morning he'd forgotten his own tears." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "So. Did he convince you?"

"No."

"Good." She pulled something from her coat pocket: a crowbar, wrapped in a rag. "Because I've been thinking about what Wren told us. It answers truthfully in the bell's presence. It cannot take what's freely surrendered." She nodded up at the tower, at the clean-swept stone, the oiled hinges, the rope hanging ready in its new-braided coil. "They've made it beautiful for it, Cyrus. They've made it *welcoming*. Tomorrow night there'll be candles and singing and four children walking in on their parents' arms, and the day after, the book gets its fifth name." She pressed the crowbar into his hands. It was colder than the air. "So we go tonight instead. Before they're ready. Before the town is watching."

"Tonight," Cyrus repeated. The word felt like stepping off a ledge.

"Tonight," Marisol said. "While it's still ours to choose."
