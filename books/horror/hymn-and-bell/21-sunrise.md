## 21. Sunrise

Marisol had not slept. She had not slept in fifty years, if she was honest about it — not since the winter of 1974, when the bell stopped tolling and her brother's name stopped appearing anywhere but inside her own head. But this night had been different. This night she had stood in the bell tower with chalk dust on her sleeves and a dead man's notebooks under her arm, and she had watched Cyrus Vasquez put his whole life into a stranger's hands.

Now the sky over Hollowmere was going gray at the edges, the way it did before dawn in late December, and Marisol sat on the tower's frozen steps with her back against the cold stone and waited to see what would be left of the world.

The Keeper had gone out of the town like a tide going out. She couldn't explain how she knew that; she only knew that the pressure behind her eyes — the one she'd lived with so long she'd mistaken it for her own skull — was gone. The silence it left behind was enormous. It was the silence of a house after a party, of a hospital room after the machines stop. She could hear the wind moving through the pines on the ridge. She could hear her own breath. She could hear, faintly, from the direction of Wren Halloway's house on Corvus Lane, a child screaming.

That was the sound of remembering.

Marisol closed her eyes. *Let it come,* she thought, to whatever was listening now that the other thing wasn't. *Let all of it come.*

* * *

The first light came up copper-colored, catching the frost on the tower windows until the whole upper chamber glowed like the inside of an ember. Cyrus Vasquez stood at the center of it, exactly where he'd been standing when the sun found him, holding his notebook against his chest with both hands the way a man holds a life preserver.

He hadn't moved in an hour. Marisol had watched him not move. Once, near the darkest part of the night, he had opened the notebook and looked down at the pages for a long time, and then he had said, quietly, in a voice she'd never heard from him before — flat, uninflected, a stranger's voice:

"Is this mine?"

"It's yours," she'd told him.

"I don't know what any of it says." He turned a page. "It looks like my handwriting."

"It is your handwriting."

He'd nodded slowly, the way people nod when they've decided to accept something they cannot survive rejecting, and closed the book, and held it again against his chest, and gone back to standing still while the night drained out of the sky.

Now the copper light reached his face and Marisol saw what grief looked like on a man who didn't know what he was grieving. He wasn't crying. He was doing something worse: he was searching. His eyes went around the tower — the chalk circle, scrubbed half away by their own hands; the ledger lying open where it had fallen; the bell itself, cracked clean through its crown, hanging silent as a skull — and every time his gaze landed on something, Marisol could see him reaching for a name for it and finding nothing there. Not confusion. Confusion resolves. This was absence. A house with the furniture carried out.

"The children," he said suddenly, turning to her. His voice cracked on the second word, and he frowned, surprised by his own feeling, a man discovering a bruise he couldn't account for. "Are the children all right?"

"You remember them?"

"No." He said it without flinching, which frightened her more than weeping would have. "But I can't stop worrying about them. Isn't that strange? I have nothing in my head and there's a hole in me shaped like five children."

"Four," Marisol said. "The fifth was you." She got up off the steps, knees cracking, and crossed the tower to him. Up close she could see the exhaustion carved into his face, the fine tremor in his hands. "Cyrus. Listen to me. Last night you gave everything you had documented — every page, every recording, every memory you'd written down — to buy those kids back. You did it free and clear, nobody coerced you, and the thing had to honor it because that was the oldest rule it had. You may not remember being brave. But I watched you be brave, and I'm going to keep telling you about it until some of it sticks. That's a promise."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, politely, the way you speak to someone whose name you've lost: "Do I know you?"

The question went through her like a nail through the palm. Fifty years she had been the one who remembered alone. She had thought she understood loneliness. She had not understood it at all until she heard it asked in that gentle, courteous voice by the man who had given away his mind to save children he could no longer name.

"Yes," she said. "You do. You just can't find where you put me." She took his elbow. "Come on. There are people downstairs who need us. And there's coffee in the teachers' lounge, and I'm told you were very particular about coffee."

His face flickered — something surfacing, almost breaking the water, sinking again. "Am I?" he said. "Was I?"

"You'll find out," Marisol said, and led him toward the stairs.

* * *

Hollowmere woke hard.

It happened street by street, house by house, as the sun came over the ridge and struck the frosted windows, and in each house it happened differently, because the stolen years came home the way soldiers come home — out of order, ragged, demanding to be fed.

On Alder Street, Ruth Okonkwo dropped a casserole dish in the sink and stood ankle-deep in shards, sobbing, because she had just remembered her son Daniel's missing week from 1989 — the week the school had told her he'd had the flu, the week he'd actually spent somewhere else, and he had come back from it smiling and called her Mom and she had never once, in thirty years, thought to ask why he wouldn't say where he'd been. Now she knew. Now she knew, and the knowing was a physical thing, a swallowed stone.

At the diner on Main, Earl Pruitt set down his coffee and began quietly telling his wife about 1961, about the girl named Alice Fenwick who used to wait for him by the flagpole, and how after that December she'd been pleasant to him forever after but had never once known his name, and how he had married someone else and never wondered why Alice's smile made him feel like a man looking into a well. His wife listened. Then she started talking too, faster and faster, about her sister, about the year her sister came back from school different, and halfway through her own story she stopped, stricken, because she realized she was describing the same December Earl was describing, and the two Decembers lay down together in the middle of the table between them like a body.

In the school parking lot, parents who had walked their chosen children up the tower stairs the night before with candles and hymns and love in their faces stood in the snow in their coats, unable to look at one another. Nobody had forced them. That was the worst of it, and everyone arrived at it within the hour, independently, the way people arrive at a shared wound: nobody had forced them. They had sung the hymn. They had held their children's hands. They had believed — the way the town had believed for ninety years, the way the covenant required them to believe — that wholeness was worth a day here, a week there, a childhood sanded smooth at the edges. Some of them knelt right there in the parking lot. Some of them drove out of town and kept driving. One father, Hollis Brandt, walked to the base of the bell tower and stood beneath it in the falling snow for six hours without moving, and when Marisol passed him on her way in with Cyrus, he said only, "My girl woke up crying and she remembered everything, and the first thing she asked me was whether I knew. Whether I knew." His voice broke. "What do I tell her?"

"The truth," Marisol said. "It's the only currency we've got left."

* * *

By nine o'clock the school was full of weeping.

It was, Marisol thought, standing in the corridor outside the fourth-grade rooms, the most terrible and beautiful sound the building had ever made. For ninety years Hollowmere School had hummed with that false cheerfulness, that polished, lacquered joy that Cyrus had noticed his very first week and written about in a notebook he could no longer read. Now the corridors rang with honest grief — teachers sitting on the floors of their classrooms with their heads in their hands, remembering their own missing days; students clinging to each other in knots of two and three, trading fragments back and forth, building the lost hours out loud like survivors rebuilding a town from photographs.

Principal Aldous sat in his office with the door wide open and did not ask anyone to lower their voices. He was an old man now, and Marisol had known him as a boy, and she saw that he had aged ten years since midnight. When she passed his doorway he looked up at her with red eyes and said, "I was taken in 1958. I never knew. I *never knew*, Marisol, I thought I was happy—" and then he put his face in his hands, and she stood in the doorway a moment, not comforting him, because some griefs need witnesses more than they need comfort, and then she moved on.

Wren Halloway found her by the trophy case.

The girl looked wrecked and luminous, the way people look after fever breaks. Her eyes were swollen, her nose was chapped, and she was gripping a thermos of cocoa in both hands like a weapon or a talisman. She fell into step beside Marisol without a word, and they walked together past the case — past the yellowed team photos going back to 1931, past the faces of ninety years of children, some of whom were smiling in those pictures with days missing from behind their smiles — and out toward the gym doors, and finally Wren said:

"He doesn't know me."

Marisol stopped. "You've seen him."

"He was in the parking lot earlier. Mr. Brandt was yelling at him — at everybody — and Mr. Vasquez just stood there taking it, real polite, like a substitute teacher. And I went up to him and I said, 'Mr. Vasquez, it's me, it's Wren,' and he looked at me—" Her voice caught. She mastered it the way she'd learned to master things in the dark of the last month, jaw tight, breath counted. "He looked at me the way you look at a word you almost remember. And then he said, 'Should I know you?' And I said yes. And he said, 'Then I'm sorry.' Just like that. *Then I'm sorry.* Like it was a formality."

"It isn't," Marisol said. "It's the truest thing he's got."

"He gave it all away for us." Wren stared at the gym doors, at the light coming through their wired glass, gold and merciless. "I heard it happen. In my head, I mean — I still hear it a little, even though it's gone. When he surrendered, the whole tower lit up like — like a receipt being printed. Everything he ever wrote down, itemized, and handed over. And it took it, and it kept its rule, and it let us go, and then it was just... weather leaving." She wiped her nose with her sleeve. "Ms. Reyes, I want to give it back."

"You can't."

"I know I can't. That's not what giving back means." Wren turned to face her, and Marisol saw in the girl's face the same thing she had seen in her brother Tomás's face fifty years ago, the last time she'd seen him whole: the stubbornness of the small against the enormous. "What I mean is, somebody has to read his notebook to him. All of it. Every page. Because it's his, and he should have it, even if he has to have it secondhand. Even if it's like hearing stories about somebody else. Somebody has to tell him who he was." She lifted her chin. "And I already started. Last night, before anyone would let me go home. I read him the part where he wrote about his first day, and how he noticed the October gap in the attendance ledger, and he listened so hard. He couldn't remember any of it but he listened like it mattered, and at the end he said, 'He sounds careful. Was he?' And I said, 'He was the most careful person I ever met.' And he smiled. Ms. Reyes, he *smiled*, like it was good news about a stranger."

Marisol put her hand on the girl's shoulder. Outside, the snow had stopped and the light was climbing the flagpole, the ridge, the water tower, laying itself across the town like a hand laid across a forehead, checking for fever.

"That's the job," Marisol said. "Both of us. We're going to read him back to himself, page by page, and it's going to take as long as it takes, and some days he'll hate it. And when we get to the end of the notebook, we start over. That's the covenant now. Ours."

* * *

She went back to the tower at noon, alone, because there was one thing she had to do before she could do anything else.

The stairwell smelled of chalk and cold stone and, faintly, of the hymn — that off-key melody soaked so deep into the masonry that she suspected it would outlast the building. Sunlight fell through the broken crown of the bell in long dusty blades. The crack ran through the bronze like a river on a map, and the bell hung motionless, mute, finished. Ninety years of tolling, ended by one man's hands on the rope and four words spoken into the ringing air: *What was truly taken?*

Marisol climbed to the platform and stood beneath the bell, and let herself remember.

Not the fragments — she had always had the fragments, the way you have splinters. She let herself have the whole of it. Tomás Reyes, fourteen years old, December 1974, a boy who whittled animals out of pine knots and gave them to girls he was too shy to talk to. A boy who had once carried her a mile home on his back when she broke her ankle sledding, singing the hymn wrong on purpose to make her laugh. A boy who went to school on the third of December and came back on the fourth, pleasant and polite, calling her *ma'am* instead of *Malita*, and who never whittled again, and who grew up into a man who worked at the feed store and was kind to everyone and known by no one, least of all himself, and who died at forty-one of a heart no doctor could explain, worn out, hollowed, a coat with nobody in it.

She had remembered all of that for fifty years, alone, in a town that had forgotten how to grieve him. But what she had never been able to hold — what the Keeper's departure had finally unlocked, the last locked drawer — was the missing day itself. The third of December. She had it now, whole, arriving in her mind like water finding a dry channel: Tomás in the tower, Tomás in the circle, Tomás looking up at the bell with his chin trembling and saying, in a small clear voice she would carry until her own heart quit, *"Malita, if I forget, will you keep it for me?"*

"Yes," Marisol said aloud, to the sunlight and the dust and the dead bell. "I kept it. I kept all of it. I'm sorry it took me so long to bring it home."

She cried then, standing under the bell, and it was the first time in fifty years that crying had felt like release instead of erosion. Grief and gratitude in the same breath, tangled together the way roots tangle under a tree — she could not have separated them and did not try. When it was done she knelt and pressed her palm flat against the cold floor of the tower, where the chalk circle had been, and said the rest of it quietly: "Rest now, Tomás. You're all there. Every piece. I checked."

* * *

She found Cyrus at dusk in his classroom, Room 12, sitting at his own desk with the notebook open in front of him and Wren beside him, reading aloud.

"—and then he writes, 'October gap confirmed. Nine students over eleven years, always in autumn or midwinter, always returning pleasant. Beginning audio documentation tonight. If anyone finds this notebook, I was right, and I am probably in danger.'" Wren glanced up. "That's the end of the page."

Cyrus was leaning forward with his elbows on the desk, his chin on his laced fingers, listening with the absolute attention of a man at a stranger's deathbed. When Wren stopped, he was quiet a moment, and then he said, "Read it again. The part about being right."

Wren read it again.

"Hm," Cyrus said. He touched the page with one fingertip, lightly, the way you touch a photograph of someone you've been told you loved. "He sounds frightened here. But he kept going anyway." He looked up at Marisol, standing in the doorway with snow melting on her shoulders, and something moved behind his eyes — not memory, not yet, maybe never memory, but the shape where memory goes, a coat hook waiting for a coat. "Did I know you? In the parts you haven't read yet?"

"Oh, yes," Marisol said. She came into the room, pulled the desk from the next row up alongside his, and sat down in it, knees complaining, like a student. "Sit tight. We're getting to the good part. Page sixty-one is where you meet me, and let me tell you, the man who wrote it makes me sound far more mysterious than I am."

Outside the window, the last light left the ridge and the first stars came out over Hollowmere — ordinary stars, indifferent stars, stars that had watched the whole ninety years and taken no side. Somewhere down the hall a teacher was crying, and somewhere else a child was laughing, and both sounds were true, and neither one was hiding anything anymore.

"Page sixty-one," said Wren, and began to read him himself.
