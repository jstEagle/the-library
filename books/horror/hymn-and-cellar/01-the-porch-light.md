## 1. The Porch Light

The town of Hollow Creek has exactly one claim to fame, and it's a lie on a historical marker: "Founded 1887 by settlers of uncommon perseverance." I've photographed forty-one condemned houses in three states, and every one of them had a plaque like that somewhere nearby, some civic attempt to make rot sound like grit. Perseverance is what you call it when nobody leaves and nobody will say why.

I came back for the houses, that's the story I told myself, and I'm good at telling myself stories. Twelve years of practice. I document condemned and abandoned structures for a preservation archive that pays me in exposure and gas money, and Hollow Creek, population dwindling, water table rising, mine shafts collapsing under half the east side — Hollow Creek was a target-rich environment. That's what I called it in my pitch email. Target-rich. Like the town was something to be harvested instead of survived.

The bus let me off at the corner of Mill and Second at 4:47 in the afternoon, which gave me ninety minutes of good light, and I spent all of them photographing the Hollis place, whose porch roof had folded inward like a hand closing. I got a beautiful shot of the staircase where the banister had rotted through, each step sagging toward the center as if the house were trying to swallow its own throat. I got the wallpaper, striped, peeling in long curls that looked deliberate, decorative, like the walls were molting into something else.

What I did not do, in those ninety minutes, was look down Mill Street toward my grandmother's house. I want that on the record. Ninety minutes and I found forty reasons to keep my camera pointed anywhere else.

Because here's the thing about coming home after twelve years: the town remembers your shape. You walk down streets that knew you at nine and they fit wrong now, like shoes from a childhood photo. Every corner has an opinion about you. The creek behind the Kessler property still runs orange with iron, and when I was a kid I told everyone it was rust from a buried car, and everyone believed me because I was the kind of kid who always had an explanation. I was nine years old and already curating reality. Already deciding what was real and filing everything else under *later*.

Under *never*, mostly.

My grandmother's house sits at the end of Corliss Street where the pavement gives up and becomes gravel becomes dirt becomes the treeline, and above the treeline there's the ridge, and under the ridge there's — well. There's what's under the ridge. We'll get there. I spent twelve years getting there slowly, one rationalization at a time, and I'd like to think I arrived with some dignity.

The porch light was on.

It was 5:40 in the evening in late October, so technically dusk, but the light wasn't on because of darkness. Edele Kavanagh did not turn on lights for darkness; she turned them on for company, and she hadn't had company worth lighting for since before I left. That bulb burned most nights, Delia told me on the phone once, the one time a year we spoke longer than four minutes. Burns all night. Mom thinks it's the dementia. Grandma says she's expecting someone.

I stood at the foot of the gravel drive with my camera bag on my shoulder and told myself the light meant nothing. Lights mean nothing. They're filament and glass and a switch, and switches get flipped by old women with bad memories, and that's all. I have a whole taxonomy of things that mean nothing. It's very well organized. It has subcategories.

Then I saw who was sitting on the porch.

*

Let me describe Wren, because describing Wren is the only way I know how to hold onto the edges of what happened next.

Wren sat on the top step of my grandmother's porch with their knees drawn up and their chin on their knees, the way they always sat, the way no child actually sits because it looks uncomfortable and children are honest about comfort. Small. Maybe eight years old, maybe ten — Wren never aged past the range where adults stop guessing. Hair the color of wet bark, cut unevenly, like it had been trimmed by someone working from memory. Clothes that didn't match any decade: corduroy jacket, canvas sneakers gone soft and gray, a scarf knotted twice around the neck even though October in Hollow Creek is merely brisk. And the eyes. Everyone who invents an imaginary friend gives them something uncanny without knowing why, and I gave Wren eyes the color of the water in the flooded quarry — that green-gray that isn't quite either, that looks different depending on how much of yourself you're willing to see reflected in it.

Wren looked exactly like that. At twenty-nine years old, standing in the gravel with twelve years and six hundred miles between me and the last time I'd seen them, I can report with a photographer's precision: unchanged. Not preserved, not eerie-in-a-way-I-could-explain-later. Unchanged, the way a photograph is unchanged. The way a thing is unchanged when it was never really part of time to begin with.

Here is what my brain did, in order:

One. It offered me *hallucination*, stress-induced, sleep-deprived, consistent with a family history of early dementia that I have privately dreaded since I turned twenty-five. Two, it offered *a real child*, some neighbor's kid, coincidence of appearance, children look alike, that's practically science. Three, it offered *grief hallucination* — except nobody relevant had died, unless you counted the version of me that left this town, and honestly, maybe I did count him. Four, it offered the explanation I'd been building since I was sixteen, the load-bearing one, the one that held up the whole architecture: *you made Wren up, Orsen. You were a lonely kid in a house full of silences, and you invented a friend, and friends you invent stay invented. That's the deal. That's physics.*

And then Wren lifted their head, and smiled with the specific crooked asymmetry I would have sworn on any holy book I could not name, and said:

"You're late."

Not *hello*. Not *Orsen*. Not *it's been a long time*. Just — *you're late*, in a voice like a screen door in summer, like chalk on slate, like every afternoon between the ages of nine and fourteen compressed into two syllables. And my entire taxonomy of things-that-mean-nothing went over like a card table in a flood.

"I—" My voice came out wrong, pitched too high, a teenager's voice. I cleared my throat. Tried again. "Twelve years. That's not late, that's — that's a schedule change."

Wren considered this with their head tilted, and I watched myself do it, watched myself negotiate with the impossible rather than flee from it, because apparently that's just what I'm like. Twelve years of urban exploration, of walking into buildings condemned by every authority that could condemn them, and I finally understood in that moment that I'd been practicing. All those collapsed floors and sealed shafts and houses that groaned when the wind hit them right — I'd been rehearsing for the day the world admitted what it actually was.

"Twelve years," Wren agreed. "Four thousand three hundred and eighty days. I counted."

Of course they counted. Wren counted everything. When I was nine I thought it was a game. When I was eleven I thought it was a tic. When I was sixteen I stopped thinking about it at all, which took work, which took the concerted application of a teenage boy's full talent for not-thinking, and I want to be clear that the not-thinking was the hardest project of my life and also the most successful, right up until a small figure on a porch steps said *I counted* and the whole dam went at once.

"You can't have," I said. "Counted. Time doesn't — people don't—"

"People don't," Wren agreed pleasantly. "But I kept the porch light on."

"You kept—" I looked at the bulb. Yellow, ordinary, sixty watts, moths already beginning their evening orbit around it. "Grandma keeps it on."

"Grandma lets me keep it on." Wren unfolded from the step, and moved the way Wren always moved, which is to say without any of the noise a body makes, no creak of joints, no scuff of shoe, arriving at the bottom of the stairs the way a thought arrives. Close enough now that I could see the scarf was the same scarf. The same actual scarf, frayed at the same spot, the spot where thirteen-year-old me had used it to tie off a rope in the cellar and frayed it, and then lied about it, and then felt sick about lying, and then stopped feeling anything about it at all because that was the price of leaving.

"There's a moth," Wren said, nodding at the light. "It's been circling since June. It doesn't land and it doesn't leave. I named it Delegate."

"That's — why?"

"Because it's considering." Wren smiled again, that crooked smile, and for one vertiginous second I was nine years old on this exact patch of gravel, holding a flashlight with a dying battery, being told by my best friend that the sounds under the ridge were just the tunnels breathing and there was nothing to be scared of, and believing it, needing to believe it, because the alternative was that the world had teeth.

I am thirty-eight inches of composure taller than I was at nine. It didn't help.

"How are you here?" I asked. And then, because I'm a professional, because I document things, because the camera bag on my shoulder suddenly weighed as much as a headstone: "Actually — wait. Don't answer that. Let me—"

I got the camera out. Hands shaking, which the viewfinder made honest and enormous. I raised it. Framed Wren against the porch rail, against the lit window, against the last gray light coming down off the ridge. Wren looked directly into the lens with those quarry-water eyes and did not blink, and I pressed the shutter, and the camera made its small satisfied click, and I lowered it and turned the screen around to check.

The porch was empty. Rail, window, moth, light. No Wren. A smear of something at the edge of the frame that might have been lens flare or might have been a smudge on the sensor or might have been, if I'm honest, which I am trying to be lately, the photographic evidence of a presence the camera declined to record.

I looked up. Wren was still there, head tilted, watching me check the screen with an expression of gentle pity, the way you'd watch a dog chase a laser pointer.

"It doesn't like being counted," Wren said. "Cameras count."

"What does that mean? What counts?"

"Everything counts, Orsen." They said my name carefully, tasting it, like checking whether fruit had ripened in their absence. "That's sort of the whole problem."

Somewhere inside the house, faintly, a floorboard settled. Or something walked across it. From where I stood, in the failing light, with my useless camera and my collapsed taxonomy and my heart going like a trapped bird, those sounded exactly the same, and I understood with total clarity that I had come home to photograph condemned houses, and the first condemned thing I'd found was the story I'd built my whole adult life on.

"Is Grandma okay?" I asked, because underneath everything, under the vertigo and the dread and the shameful joy of seeing my best friend alive on a porch, that was the question I'd driven six hundred miles not-asking.

"She's waiting," Wren said. "She's very good at it. Better than me, even, and I've had practice." They glanced up at the ridge, where the last light was going, where the treeline stood black and dense as a closed mouth. Then back at me. "You should come inside. She'll want to see you before it gets dark."

"Why before dark?"

Wren's smile stayed exactly where it was. Everything else about their face moved slightly, subtly, the way water moves under ice.

"Because after dark," they said, "the counting gets easier to hear. And you've got twelve years of it to catch up on."

The porch light buzzed. The moth — Delegate, I'm never going to unlearn that name — circled once more and held its position, hovering, considering, neither landing nor leaving.

I picked up my camera bag and climbed the steps toward the door, toward the light, toward my grandmother's house that I had sworn at sixteen I would never enter again. Behind me, at the bottom of the stairs, Wren followed without making a sound, the way they always had.

The door was unlocked. Of course it was unlocked.

She'd been expecting someone.
