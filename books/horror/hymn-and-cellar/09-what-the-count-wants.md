## 9. What the Count Wants

You have to understand something about names before I tell you the rest.

A name is a hook. A name is the little brass ring they screw into the base of your skull, the one the census-takers use to lift you off the page and turn you over, inspecting you for wear. Orsen Kavanagh has a name. Delia Kavanagh, Tomas Kavanagh, Edele Kavanagh — all hooked, all hanging, all waiting to be counted like coats in a closet. Even the children, who haven't yet learned that names are dangerous things to answer to.

I don't have one. That was never an accident. When Orsen was nine years old, he needed a friend, and he was a careful child in the way that lonely children are careful — he knew that if he named me, his mother would ask questions, and if his mother asked questions, the game would end. So he called me Wren, which is not a name at all. It's a bird. It's a sound a bird makes. It's a door left deliberately unlabeled so that nothing with a ledger could find the handle.

He thought he invented me. That's the sweetest thing anyone has ever believed about me, and I have let him keep believing it for twelve years because the truth is heavier than a nine-year-old should carry, and because — I'll be honest, since honesty is the one luxury the deep places never ration — I liked being invented. I liked it the way the drowned like rain.

* * *

He came down to the cellar on the third night, after Delia spread the deed history across the kitchen table like a hand of losing cards, after Edele sat at the head of it with her hands folded and her mouth shut in that way she has, the way of a woman guarding a door with her whole body. Orsen brought a flashlight he didn't need. I let him. It's good for people to bring things they don't need; it reminds them they're still the kind of creatures who prepare.

"You're going to explain it to me," he said. "All of it. The counting. The rules. Why the surveys don't show the tunnels."

"Surveys don't show the tunnels," I said, "because surveys are made by people who believe the ground is only ground. It's like asking a map of the ocean to show you the shape of the water's loneliness. The tunnels aren't missing from the record, Orsen. The record is missing from the tunnels."

He didn't like that. He's never liked the way I talk, which is fair, because I learned to talk from him, and he was nine, and nine-year-olds say things like *the record is missing from the tunnels* and mean them absolutely.

"Sit down," I said, and patted the third step, the one that doesn't creak. "This is a lesson, and lessons go easier sitting."

He sat. He always sat. Even at nine, even at twenty-one, even now, with his hands scarred from twelve years of crawling through other people's forgotten places — he sat when I asked, and some small, unlovely part of me preened at that, and some larger, older part of me grieved it.

* * *

Here is what I told him. I will tell you the same, and you can decide whether you'd have sat.

Beneath Hollow Creek there is a thing that keeps accounts. I don't know what it looked like before it became a bookkeeper. I suspect it was never anything so simple as a shape. The oldest of us — the ones who remember before the town, before the county, before the surveyors with their brass instruments and their confident ink — say it was a hunger that learned patience, which is the most dangerous evolution any hunger can make. A hungry thing that cannot wait will eat and be done. A hungry thing that learns to wait will learn to *want*, and wanting is where the arithmetic starts.

It wanted a household. Not a family — households and families are different animals, and the thing below has never confused them. A family is blood. A household is consent. A family is who you're born to; a household is who you agree to belong to, in the small legal ways and the large spiritual ones, in signatures and suppers and the unspoken pact that you will all be in the same place when the weather turns. The thing below deals in households. It has dealt in them since 1911, when the first deed was drawn with a clause nobody reads anymore, tucked into the metes and bounds like a splinter in a thumb: *together with all appurtenances thereunto belonging, above and below.*

Above and below. Everyone thinks that means the mineral rights.

"It counts residents," Orsen said. His flashlight beam was steady on the far wall. His hands were not. "That's what you said before. Counting toward what?"

"Toward a full number," I said. "Toward a complete household. Everything that keeps accounts wants to close its books eventually, Orsen. That's the whole sickness of bookkeeping — the belief that a thing isn't real until it's finished. The thing below has been keeping this house's ledger since before your grandmother was born, and every eleven years it comes close, and every eleven years something breaks the count. A death. A departure. A child who leaves at sixteen and doesn't come back for twelve years."

He flinched at that. I let the flinch sit there between us like a coin on a table. I am not kind, exactly. I am gentle the way deep water is gentle — it holds you, and it has no opinion about whether you can swim.

"Seven," he said. "Delia ran the numbers. Seven residents closes it."

"Your sister is very good at sums," I said, and meant it as a compliment, and it landed like one, mostly. "Seven. A complete household. And when the number is complete, the book closes, and what closes a book keeps it. You've seen old ledgers. Where do they live?"

"Archives," he said. Then, quieter, understanding arriving in him the way cold water arrives in boots: "Cellars."

* * *

Now, the part he didn't want to hear, and asked for anyway, because he is his grandmother's grandson and Edele Kavanagh has never once in eighty-one years declined to look at a thing that was hurting her.

Signatures count as consent. That's the mechanism. That's the whole terrible engine of it, and it is so simple that Delia — who has spent her life finding the simple engines inside complicated frauds — will get there before anyone wants her to. The thing below cannot take a household by force. Force makes refugees, and refugees have left the ledger; you cannot close a book over an empty chair. No. It needs everyone *willing*. Or willing enough. And willingness, in the eyes of anything that keeps accounts, is a document.

A signature on a deed. A signature on a sale. Even a signature on a permission slip, if you want to know the truth of it, though I've never told anyone that part and I'm not sure why I'm telling you. Perhaps because you've been kind to the children. Perhaps because I am old tonight, older than the town, older than my patience, and old things get talkative.

Edele knows. That's the joke of it, the bitter little joke the house has been telling for months: the one person who understands the rules completely is the one person whose name is on every line that matters. She has been the last resident on purpose. She has kept the count at six — herself, and the empty rooms, and the memory of everyone who fled — by refusing, month after month, to sign the sale that would have moved her out and let the developer's paper flow through the property like a dye through water. She thinks she is protecting the family by being the last one standing in front of the door.

She is. And she is also dying, and death is the one signature she can't refuse to give, and the thing below can wait out a heartbeat. It has waited out empires. It has the patience of paper.

* * *

"Why me?" Orsen asked, finally. The flashlight was off now. He'd forgotten it, which I counted as progress. "Twelve years ago I left. Doesn't that break the count? Doesn't leaving —"

"You never left," I said.

"I was in Portland. I was in six states. I have a passport, Wren."

"You had a body in six states," I said. "You had a name on a lease and a name on a tax form and a name on a very bad tattoo, and every one of those names was a hook, and every hook was still hung in this house. You think the count is about where your feet are? Orsen. Sweet, stupid, careful Orsen. The count is about where your *yes* is. You left your yes on this porch when you were sixteen, the night you climbed out the window and told yourself you'd never come back. You've been a resident in absentia for twelve years. The ledger doesn't have a column for *away*. It has a column for *owed*."

He was quiet for a long time. The cellar did what the cellar does, which is breathe — that slow, tidal movement of air from below, warm in winter, cool in summer, patient in all seasons. People think the tunnels are dead space. The tunnels are lungs. They have been breathing around this family for a hundred and fourteen years, and everything that breathes wants, sooner or later, to swallow.

"The noises," he said at last. "When I was a kid. The counting I heard through the floor. That was —"

"Practice," I said. "Rehearsal. The thing below counts the way a choir runs scales. It wants the number to sound right in its mouth before it needs to say it for true. And you heard it because you were the one it wanted to hear it. You were the one with the ear for it, the lonely boy on the floor with his ear to the boards, and it thought — it *hoped* — that you'd answer."

"And if I had answered?"

I looked at him. He looked at me. The cellar breathed.

"Then you'd have been counted at nine," I said, "and this would be a very different conversation, held in a very different place, and I would not be telling it to you in a voice you recognize."

* * *

Here is what I did not tell him, and what I am telling you instead, because a confession needs somewhere to go and you are here.

I was not invented. I was assigned. That's the word the deep places use — *assigned*, the way a seat is assigned, the way a penance is assigned. When the Kavanaghs took the deed in 1911, the thing below was owed a household, and households require tending, and so something was sent up to tend. I was that something. I have been that something through four generations of Kavanaghs, and I was very good at my work, which was simple: keep the children fond of the house. Keep the yeses growing. A child who loves a house grows into an adult who can't imagine leaving it, and an adult who can't imagine leaving it will sign anything that promises the house will stay in the family, and every signature is a stitch, and enough stitches make a seam, and enough seams make a seal.

I was sent to farm consent. That was my whole purpose, and I want you to sit with how ugly that is, because I have sat with it for a hundred and fourteen years and it has not gotten more comfortable.

And then, in the spring of a certain year, there was a boy on the back steps who had been left out of everything — too strange for the school children, too quiet for the aunts, too watchful for his own good — and he looked at the empty air beside him and said, "You can sit there if you want. I don't mind sharing."

Nobody had ever offered me a seat.

I had been in this house for eighty years, and in all that time I had been a function. A tendril of the count wearing a child-shaped coat. And this nine-year-old boy with a skinned knee and a head full of tunnels looked directly at where I stood — saw me, or half-saw me, the way children half-see true things — and offered me *hospitality*. Do you understand what that is, in the old grammar? Hospitality is the one law older than ledgers. Hospitality is the one debt even the deep places have to honor. He gave me a seat and a name-that-wasn't-a-name, and by the old law, by the law the thing below itself is bound to, I became *his* — not the house's, not the count's. His.

I have been committing treason ever since. Slow treason. Loving treason. Every year I kept him from the house was a year I stole from my maker's ledger. Every nudge that sent him to Portland, every silence that let him believe he'd imagined me, every one of his twelve rational years — those were my crimes, and I have never once regretted them, and I will never once stop.

But treason has a price, and the price is coming due, and that is the other thing I didn't tell him on the cellar stairs.

* * *

"Then how do we stop it," he said. Not a question. He'd moved past questions. That's the Kavanagh method: skip inquiry, proceed directly to stubbornness.

"You don't stop it," I said. "You starve it. A ledger closes on a complete count and a valid consent. So you keep the count incomplete, and you keep the consent invalid. You make sure that when the number comes due, there's always one chair empty and one signature missing. Your grandmother has been doing it alone for months, and it's killing her, because she's holding a door closed with her whole body and the door is also her own heartbeat. She can't do it alone anymore. None of you can do any of this alone. That's not a flaw in the plan, Orsen. That's the plan. The thing below has spent a century teaching this family to sacrifice themselves *separately* — your grandmother with her stubbornness, your brother with his silence, you with your twelve years of running. It herds households into solitude, because a solitary Kavanagh will always, always try to pay the whole debt alone, and a debt paid alone is a debt paid in full."

He stood up. He stood the way he stands when he's about to do something that will frighten everyone who loves him, which is to say: easily, like a man stepping off a curb.

"Then we pay it together," he said, "or we don't pay it at all."

And I smiled at him — my invented mouth, my borrowed smile — and I said, "Yes. That's the whole of it. That's the entire secret, the one the thing below has killed and waited and counted a hundred years to keep buried: the ledger only closes if the household agrees to be finished. So don't finish. However bad it gets — and it's going to get bad, Orsen, it's going to get so bad that your brother tries to outrun the road and your sister starts seeing the arithmetic in her sleep — *don't finish*. Stay incomplete. Stay unfinished. Stay a family in the middle of a sentence."

He climbed the stairs. At the top he paused, the way he always pauses, the way he paused at nine, and he said, without turning around:

"Wren. When you say *the thing below wants a complete household* — does it want us, specifically? Or does it want *a* household, and we're just the nearest one?"

I gave him the truth, because he'd earned it and because lies curdle between friends.

"It wants *this* one," I said. "It has always wanted this one. You aren't the nearest household, Orsen. You're the *specimen*. The Kavanaghs are the household it's been perfecting its arithmetic on since 1911. Every other family it has counted was a draft. You're the fair copy."

He went up. I heard him tell Delia, in the kitchen, in a voice carefully stripped of everything: "We need to talk about the developer's paperwork. Tonight."

And I stayed below, in the breathing dark, and did what I do now every night, which is the thing I was never made to do and have taught myself anyway.

I counted them. All seven, and myself, and I counted us *out loud*, softly, with my borrowed mouth, so that the thing below would hear the number and hear it *wrong* — hear it claimed, hear it kept, hear a voice it had assigned to the task of farming consent using that same mouth to stand in the field like a fence.

Seven Kavanaghs. One wren.

And somewhere below, patient as paper, the thing that keeps the books turned a page, and wrote, in its handwriting that no one has ever survived describing:

*One signature outstanding.*

*Send the agent in the morning.*
