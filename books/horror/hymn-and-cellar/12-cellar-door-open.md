## 12. Cellar Door, Open

I have been a widow for thirty-one years and a stubborn woman for considerably longer, so when the cellar door opened at eleven minutes past midnight on a Tuesday, my first thought was not terror. It was irritation. I had just gotten comfortable in the kitchen chair with my tea going lukewarm and my hymnal open to page 412, and I did not appreciate being interrupted by whatever lived under my house.

You will have to forgive me. When you are eighty-four years old, you learn to triage your fears. The doctor's calls frighten me more than the dark does. The dark, at least, has never asked me about my cholesterol.

The cellar door had been sealed since 1993. My husband Gerald nailed it shut himself — two-by-fours across the frame, screws long enough to make you wince — and he did it without explanation, which was unlike him, because Gerald explained everything. He explained the proper way to sharpen a lawnmower blade. He explained why the Cubs would never win a pennant in his lifetime. He explained, once, at three in the morning, sitting up in bed with his hands shaking, that the house had been built over something that kept accounts, and that we were never, ever to let it finish its arithmetic.

Then he died eleven years later, almost to the day, and I stopped explaining things too.

So: the door. The two-by-fours were still nailed across the frame — I could see them from where I sat, whole and unbroken — but the door itself stood open behind them, swung inward like a mouth behind bars, and the darkness below it was breathing. Not air moving through a vent. Breathing. Slow, patient, the kind of breath a very large thing takes when it is being polite.

I set down my tea. I picked up my hymnal. And I did what any sensible woman of my generation would do.

I started singing.

* * *

Let me tell you what I know about faith, since apparently I am the one in this family who has any left. Faith is not the feeling that God is in the room. Faith is singing anyway when you are fairly sure He isn't, on the off chance He's listening from the next room over, and on the further off chance that whatever else might be listening gets confused about which of us it should pay attention to.

That second part is not theology. That is tactics. My mother taught me it during the war, though she called it something else. She said there were things in this world that could not abide a hymn sung all the way through, because a hymn is a promise with a beginning, middle, and end, and they cannot stand endings. They want everything open. Everything unfinished. A ledger with no last line.

I sang "It Is Well with My Soul," which is a presumptuous thing to sing under the circumstances, and then "Be Thou My Vision," and then I went back to the beginning and started over, because the breathing below the floorboards had not stopped, and neither was I going to.

Around verse three of the second pass, Orsen came down the stairs in his socks.

He is my grandson, the one who left, the one who photographs dead buildings now as if he can apologize to houses by cataloguing their corpses. He looked at me, then at the open cellar door, then at me again, and his face did something complicated that ended in resignation.

"Grandma," he said. "There's an open door."

"I noticed."

"It's supposed to be sealed."

"I sealed it. Well. Gerald did. In 1993." I turned the page. "He used deck screws. He bought the long ones. He said if he was going to do a job, he'd do it properly."

"Grandma, the boards are still nailed across the frame. The door is open *behind* them."

"I have eyes, Orsen."

"Right. Yes. Okay." He rubbed his face with both hands, a habit he's had since he was six, and I felt the old ache, the one that lives between guilt and love and doesn't let you sleep. "Wren says—"

"That child is not a child," I said, "and I've known it since before you were born, and I'll thank you not to explain my own house to me."

He blinked. To be fair, I'd never said it out loud before. There is a difference between knowing a thing and saying it, the same way there's a difference between a door being closed and a door being *sealed*. Some things you keep shut with nails. Some things you keep shut with silence. I had spent forty years choosing silence, and look how well that worked out; the silence had rotted through like untreated wood, and here we all were, gathered around an open mouth in my kitchen floor.

"Wren says it's counting us," Orsen said quietly. "The people in the house. It needs a full number, and then it takes them."

"Yes."

"You knew?"

"I knew it needed something," I said. "Gerald told me the shape of it, not the size. He said it counts its residents. He said when the count comes out even, the household closes." I looked down the stairs into the dark, which was breathing its slow polite breath, and I sang another line before answering, because I wanted it to hear me say the rest. "So I have made very sure the count stays wrong."

* * *

Here is what I did, all those years, and why my children think I am difficult, and why Delia's spreadsheets of deed histories made her go pale in my kitchen last week.

A census requires residents. So I refused, in every way I knew how, to be a complete resident. I kept the property in my name alone. I refused to add anyone to the deed. When Tomas wanted to move back after his divorce, I told him no, and I let him think I was being cruel, or proud, or senile, whichever hurt him least. When Orsen left at sixteen — ran, really, with a duffel bag and twelve years of noises he'd explained away — I let him go, and I did not chase him, and I have regretted it every single morning since, because chasing him would have meant asking him to come back, and coming back meant being counted.

I stayed alone in this house on purpose. One resident. An incomplete number. A ledger that couldn't close because the last line was missing, and I was the last line, and I would not sign myself into wholeness.

And then, God help me, my children started coming home.

Delia first, uninvited, with her forensic accountant's eyes and her folder of anniversaries spaced exactly eleven years apart. Then Orsen, with his camera and his boyhood friend who isn't imaginary and never was. Then Tomas, with the grandchildren, because I let him believe I was dying — which, to be fair, I am, just slower than the paperwork suggests — and now there are eight souls under this roof some nights, and the thing beneath my kitchen has begun, I think, to lick its lips.

Eight. I don't know what number it wants. But I know the sound it makes when the number gets closer, and lately the floorboards hum at night like a tuning fork someone won't stop striking.

* * *

Orsen wanted to call the others down. I told him no.

"They're asleep," I said. "Delia hasn't slept properly in four days. Tomas's boy wet the bed again and nobody's said anything about it but everybody knows. Let them sleep. This is mine."

"This is *mine*," he said, and there it was, the family disease, the oldest wound we carry: every Kavanagh believes suffering is a solo performance. Gerald tried to seal the cellar alone. I tried to hold the count alone. Orsen ran away alone at sixteen and came back alone at twenty-eight, and now he stood in my kitchen at one in the morning wanting to fight the dark by himself.

"We're a very stupid family," I told him fondly. "Sit down. Hand me the water glass. Singing dries the throat."

He sat. He didn't sing — his voice went somewhere around adolescence and never came back — but he held the flashlight pointed down the stairwell, and he stayed, and that was its own kind of hymn, I suppose. Presence is a note too.

Below us, the breathing changed. It had been slow and even, the breath of a patient animal. Now it came in shorter pulls, and underneath it — faint, far down, deeper than any cellar Gerald ever dug — I heard something new.

Counting.

Not words. More like the *feeling* of words, the way you can feel a neighbor's television through a wall, muffled syllables with a rhythm you almost recognize. One. Two. Three. It climbed slowly, deliberately, a miser running coins through his fingers, and when it reached seven it paused.

The pause lasted a long time.

Then it began again at one, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water poured down the back of my dress, that it wasn't counting what it had. It was counting what it lacked. Seven was the shape of the hole. Seven was the fullness it was waiting to pour itself into.

Seven residents, and we were eight, and I thought: *one too many. One of us is spare.* And then I thought, with the terrible arithmetic of a grandmother: *or one of us is the margin of error, and margins get erased.*

"Keep the light steady," I said, and sang louder.

* * *

At half past three, the humming in the floorboards stopped, and the breathing stopped, and the silence below was worse than either, because silence means attention. Something down there had stopped passing time and started spending it on us.

The temperature dropped. Not draft-cold — *weighted* cold, the cold of standing water in a place the sun has never audited. My breath fogged. The hymnal's pages stiffened in my fingers. Orsen's flashlight beam bent slightly downward at the edge, as if the light itself was tired.

And then, from the bottom of the stairs, in a voice like a man speaking through a foot of packed earth, it said:

"*Edele.*"

My name. My name in my own mother's cadence, the way she said it when I'd tracked mud across her clean floor. It knew that. It had listened to this house for a hundred years and it knew how my mother said my name, and it used it, and I hated it, and I nearly answered.

Nearly.

"Orsen," I whispered. "Don't answer it. Whatever it says. However it sounds. We don't answer."

"What if it sounds like—"

"It will sound like everyone. That's the trick of it. It's a census, love. A census asks questions until you tell it who you are." I gripped the hymnal hard enough to crease the cover. "So we don't tell it who we are. We tell it whose we are. There's a difference."

It said my name twice more, and then it said Gerald's name, and then — and this is the one I will not write down fully, the one I sang over, verse after verse, my voice cracking on the high notes — it said a small name, a name from 1911, a name that belonged to a child who died in this house before any of us were born, and I realized the thing below was not only counting us.

It was remembering them. All of them. Every resident it had ever tallied, kept down there in the dark like pressed flowers, and it was showing me its collection because it wanted me to understand that I would be in good company.

I sang until my throat bled a little. I sang "Amazing Grace" six times. I sang the doxology, which is short and therefore efficient, and then I sang it in the key my mother used, which is lower and meaner.

* * *

Dawn came the way it always does in Hollow Creek — gray, apologetic, seeping through the curtains rather than arriving. At 6:14 by the stove clock, the cold lifted. The breathing resumed, slower now, farther down, receding like a tide that had only come up to look at us.

The cellar door stayed open. Of course it did. Sealing it again would require going down the stairs, and none of us were fools, whatever our other faults.

Orsen fell asleep in the kitchen chair around five, sitting upright with the flashlight still on, guarding me in the useless devoted way of the young. I kept singing softly after the danger passed, partly from habit and partly because my legs had stiffened and I didn't trust them to carry me to bed.

Delia found us at seven. She took in the scene — her brother asleep, her grandmother hoarse, the impossible open door behind its impossible intact boards — and she did not scream or pray or ask what happened. She got a blanket for Orsen, poured me fresh tea, and sat down across from me with her laptop like a woman preparing for battle.

"Grandma," she said. "How many people does it need?"

I looked at her — sharp-eyed, sleepless, spreadsheet-hearted Delia, who had found the pattern in the deeds when the rest of us only felt it — and I gave her the only honest answer I had.

"Seven," I said. "It counted to seven and stopped. And there are eight of us in this house."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she opened her laptop, and her fingers started moving, fast, the way they do when order is the only weapon left, and I thought: *there. There is my girl. The count wants a full number?*

*Then let's see how it likes doing sums with Delia.*
