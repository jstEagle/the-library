## 13. Running the Numbers

I have reconciled ledgers for dead men, for men who wished they were dead, and once — memorably — for a man who was legally three separate people in two states. I thought I had seen every way a number could lie.

I had not seen a number that tells the truth about people who aren't supposed to exist.

It started, as most of my disasters do, with a spreadsheet. Orsen had gone down into the tunnels again — of course he had, my brother treats "don't go into the murder basement" as a suggestion from a coworker he doesn't respect — and Wren sat on the porch steps like a gargoyle with better posture, watching me through the window while I worked at the kitchen table. Edele was sleeping. Tomas's kids were drawing on the good furniture. And I had the deed history spread out in front of me, eleven years at a time, like a metronome made of obituaries.

1911. 1922. 1933. 1944. 1955. 1966. 1977. 1988. 1999. 2010.

Every eleventh year, someone in this house stopped being alive or stopped being findable, and the county records closed the file with the bureaucratic equivalent of a shrug. I'd built the timeline at two in the morning because I couldn't sleep, because the house hummed at night in a register I refused to call singing, and because arithmetic is what I do instead of panicking. It has served me well. Arithmetic doesn't care if you believe in it. That's the whole appeal.

So I did what I always do when a pattern presents itself: I asked it what it wanted.

* * *

The answer came to me around four in the afternoon on a Tuesday, in the middle of counting heads at dinner, which should have been a joke and wasn't.

Edele. Orsen. Me. Tomas. His kids, Penny and August. And Wren.

Seven at the table. Eight in the house, if you counted Wren, and by then I had learned to count Wren, mostly because Wren counted me back with an expression of infinite patient interest, like I was a coin being appraised.

I pushed my plate away and got the legal pad.

"Delia," Tomas said, in the tone he'd used since we were children, the one that means *you're doing the thing where you turn grief into homework.* "Sit down and eat."

"I am sitting. I'm eating. I'm also working." I wrote HOUSEHOLD at the top of the page and underlined it twice. "Tomas, how many people does it take to make a family? Legally. For tax purposes."

He blinked at me. "That depends on—"

"It's a census question," said Wren, from the end of the table, and the temperature of the room dropped four degrees, or maybe I just felt it drop, which amounts to the same thing in this house. "Census questions are the only ones that matter here."

Everyone went quiet except August, who was stabbing a meatloaf with the commitment of a much older man.

"Wren," I said carefully, "when you say the house counts its residents—"

"I didn't say that." Wren smiled. Wren's smiles are nine years old and something else entirely underneath. "Orsen said that. When he was little. He said, 'Wren, I think something down there is taking attendance.' Those were his words. I remember all his words."

Of course it did. Of course it remembered. Everything in this town remembered; that was the problem. Memory was the local currency and we were all overdrawn.

I turned to a fresh page. If the pattern ran on elevens, and each eleventh year closed an account — one resident, taken, ledger balanced — then the mechanism wanted a specific number. Not a maximum. A total. You don't take attendance unless you're waiting for everyone to be present.

"How many," I said aloud, to no one, to the house, to whatever kept the books below us, "do you need?"

The lights flickered. Once. Politely. Like a teller tapping the glass.

* * *

I want to be clear about what I did next, because my family has spent two weeks calling it brave and I need the record to show it was neither brave nor complicated. It was accounting.

I spent the evening building the household. Not the family — the household. There's a difference, and the difference is load-bearing. A family is a feeling. A household is a roster. Rosters can be audited.

Resident one: Edele Kavanagh, owner of record, ninety-one years old, dying on a schedule her doctors described as "soon" and she described as "finally, I have things to do."

Two: Orsen. Returned after twelve years. Present.

Three: me. Arrived uninvited, stayed unexplained. Present.

Four: Tomas. Arrived for the death vigil, stayed because his truck now drove him in circles back to the porch no matter which direction he pointed it. Present, against his will, which apparently still counts. The house was not particular about enthusiasm.

Five: Penny, age nine. Six: August, age six.

And seven—

I stopped writing. Because seven was the number the lights had flickered at, and seven was the number at the table, and seven was the number of names I had, and the eighth chair — the old wooden one at the end that nobody had sat in since I was a child — was occupied anyway, had been occupied since the day I arrived, by a person-shaped thing that every adult in this family had spent thirty years agreeing not to see.

Wren.

Eight residents. One signature pending. And a ledger below the floorboards that wanted exactly seven.

I did the subtraction three times, because that's what you do when the answer is unbearable. You check your work. You hope you made an error. I have never once in my career hoped to have made an error, and there was a horrible freedom in finally getting to.

One too many.

Unless someone left.

* * *

Orsen found me on the porch at midnight. He does that — appears beside me without sound, smelling faintly of chalk dust and cellar air, wearing the expression of a man who has been having conversations with his childhood hallucination and finds them going well. Twelve years of therapy money wasted, our mother used to say. Our mother, incidentally, whose name was not on any list I could find, because she had the sense to die somewhere else, off the property, off the pattern, off the books. Smart woman. Terrible cook. Good instincts.

"You've figured it out," he said.

"Obviously."

"And you're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you've done the math and the math is bad news and you're deciding how much to tell me based on how much sleep you think I need." He sat down next to me on the swing. The chains creaked. Somewhere under the floorboards, under the foundation, under the dark wet throat of the tunnel system that exists on no survey ever filed in this county, something shifted its weight. Patiently. The way you shift your weight when you're comfortable, when you're settled in for a long wait, when you already know how the evening ends.

"The count needs seven," I said. "We're eight."

He didn't ask how I knew. That's the thing about my brother — twelve years away and he came home already fluent in the local dialect of accepting impossible premises. He just nodded slowly, the way you nod at a diagnosis.

"One too many," he repeated. Then, quieter: "Unless someone leaves."

"That's the arithmetic."

"Arithmetic isn't destiny, Delia."

"No," I agreed. "But it's a schedule. And we're on it." I tapped the legal pad. "Look at the pattern. Every eleven years, the account closes. One name comes off. 2010 was the last one — a Mr. Aldous Vann, lived here as a boarder, walked out for cigarettes, never filed a forwarding address, never surfaced anywhere. I checked. I checked *everywhere*, Orsen. No death certificate, no credit activity, nothing. He didn't leave. He was *removed*. Balanced out." I looked at him. "2021. This year. The account wants to close again, and this time it doesn't want a boarder. It wants a full household. Seven names, one signature, done. Edele signs the property over, the sale completes, consent gets documented, and the ledger closes with everyone present and accounted for."

"Accounted for," Orsen said. "You're using their language."

"I'm using *language*. It happens to be theirs too. That's what frightens me. It's not alien, whatever's down there. It's not chaos. It's bookkeeping. It wants everything reconciled. And I know how reconciliation works, Orsen. I've done it my whole life. You don't get to close a book until every entry matches."

The porch light buzzed above us. Down the street, Hollow Creek lay flat and dark, every other house asleep, every other house presumably just a house, though after two weeks here I no longer trusted that assumption and suspected I never would again.

"So we leave," Orsen said. "All of us. Tonight. We put Edele in the car and we drive until the road stops looping."

"Tomas tried that. Three a.m., kids in the truck, ended up back at the porch. You can't outrun a census. The road belongs to the same jurisdiction we do." I hugged my knees. "No. We can't run and we can't hide. But we might be able to *misfile*."

He looked at me.

"A ledger needs entries," I said. "Consent, signatures, presence. Fine. I'm a forensic accountant. Breaking a paper trail is literally my profession. We starve it. We make the sale fail. We break the deed chain. We get Edele unsigned and keep her unsigned, and then—" I stopped, because the next part was the part I'd been circling all night, the part the math kept returning to no matter how many times I checked the work.

"And then?" Orsen prompted.

"And then there are still eight of us," I said, "and it still wants seven, and signing or not signing, dying or not dying, the count keeps running. Someone is extra, Orsen. The math says so. The house says so. The thing under the floorboards has been saying so all week, patiently, the way you'd wait for a bus."

The silence between us filled up with everything we weren't saying. I watched him do his own version of my arithmetic — I could see it moving behind his eyes, my brother, the boy who took attendance at nine years old and never stopped — and I watched him arrive at the same terrible sum I had.

Because there were only two ways to go from eight to seven. Someone leaves the house.

Or someone leaves the world.

"Don't," I said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to volunteer. You get the same look Dad used to get before he announced he'd handle the creditors himself." I grabbed his wrist. My brother, who photographs condemned buildings for a living, who walks into collapsing structures for fun, who came home to die in the place he'd escaped — I held onto his wrist like he was a client's asset about to be liquidated. "Nobody volunteers. Nobody sacrifices alone. That's the family disease, Orsen. Dad had it, Mom had it, Edele has it so bad she's been holding a whole house hostage with her own heartbeat for months. Everyone trying to be the hero separately, in the dark, without telling anyone. Well, not this time. Not on my watch. If someone goes into that count, we all decide together, and then we find a third option, because there is *always* a third option. Fraud, if necessary. I'm not above fraud."

Something that was almost a laugh came out of him. "Delia Kavanagh, proposing fraud."

"Forensic accounting is just fraud with a conscience and a subpoena."

Behind us, the screen door opened without a sound, and Wren stepped out onto the porch, barefoot, in the same clothes it had worn for thirty years, and looked at the two of us with an expression I couldn't file under anything I knew.

"I heard my name," Wren said.

"We weren't talking about you," Orsen said, too fast.

"You were talking about the count." Wren came and stood at the edge of the steps, looking out at the dark street the way an old employee looks at a factory. "About who's extra." A pause. The porch light buzzed. "I've been thinking about that too."

My stomach did something cold and professional. "Wren—"

"I was here before any of you," Wren said pleasantly, to the street, to the dark, to the humming ground. "Did you know that? Before the Kavanaghs. Before the town. I know what the ledger looks like from the inside. And I know something about it that none of you do yet — something I remembered recently, down where the air moves." Wren turned, and the smile was nine years old, and the eyes behind it were not. "There may be a way to make eight into seven that doesn't cost anyone anything. But I'll need to show Orsen. Tomorrow. Downstairs."

"Absolutely not," I said.

"It's sweet that you think that matters," Wren said, and went back inside, and the screen door closed without a sound, and the porch light burned steady, and under the floorboards, patient as interest accruing, something waited for morning.

Orsen and I looked at each other.

"Well," I said, reaching for the legal pad, "now I need a new column."
