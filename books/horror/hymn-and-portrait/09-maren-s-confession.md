## 9. Maren's Confession

Maren Osgood lived at the end of Tallow Row, in a house that looked like it had been built by someone who expected to be besieged. Low windows, thick walls, a door you could have parked a cart against. The garden was all rosemary and old stone, and the gate didn't so much open as concede.

I'd knocked on half the doors in Hollowmere by then, and every one of them had given me the same performance: polite bafflement, a small shrug, *lullaby? Never heard of one, love.* It would have been almost impressive if it weren't so obviously rehearsed. Villages lie the way choirs sing — together, and with feeling.

Maren opened her door before I finished knocking.

"You're the folklorist," she said. Not a question. "The one who recorded Edith."

"Edith?"

"The woman who sang for you." She stepped back from the doorway, which I understood, dimly, was an invitation of historic proportions. "Come in before someone sees you standing there. They'll count that too."

*They'll count that too.* I want to tell you I laughed. I want to tell you I made some wry little remark about village paranoia and went home to my stopwatch and my increasingly indefensible mirror problem. What I actually did was step inside, because by day nine in Hollowmere, *they'll count that too* no longer sounded like madness. It sounded like grammar.

* * *

Her kitchen smelled of beeswax and something older underneath, like the house itself was pickled in its own past. There were photographs on the walls — dozens of them, generations of stiff-collared Osgoods staring out with the particular solemnity of people who hadn't yet learned cameras could be fun.

Except.

I noticed it before I let myself notice it, the way you notice a hole in a conversation. The oldest photographs — the ones gone sepia and soft at the edges — were empty. Not blank; that would have been eerie in a clean way, an easy way. No: they showed rooms. Parlours, doorsteps, a christening party arranged around a font. Fully composed, carefully lit, everyone present.

Everyone except the Osgoods. Where a family should have stood, there was only background. Wallpaper. A chair. A gap in the row of faces shaped exactly like a person, like the photographer had framed around an absence on purpose.

"Maren," I said, "your family photos are missing your family."

"They're not missing us." She set two cups down without asking what I drank. "We're not in them. There's a difference."

"There is?"

"There always is, with us." She sat across from me, folded her hands, and looked at me with the flat patience of a woman who had been waiting decades for this conversation and resented every year of the wait. "You've seen the ledger."

It wasn't a question either. This village communicated entirely in statements dressed as questions, or maybe the other way round.

"The parish archive," I said. "The counts. Names tallied after every singing. Some struck through. The last entry dated the week the previous tenants of my cottage disappeared."

"And you think we don't know. You've been going door to door thinking we're ignorant, or cowardly, or both." She sipped her tea. "We're not ignorant, Dravenmoor. Ignorance would be a mercy. We know exactly what happens here. We've known since 1741."

"Known what, exactly?" And here my voice did the thing voices do when rationalism makes its last stand — it got very reasonable, very gentle, the tone you'd use with a confused aunt. "That a lullaby feeds some... entity? That mirrors lag? Maren, I'm a scholar. I deal in sources. Give me a source."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she got up, went to the dresser, and took out a book.

Not a ledger this time. Smaller. Leather gone black with age, pages soft as cloth. She laid it on the table between us like a card player laying down a hand she'd been holding all game.

"This is the source," she said. "Written by my great-great-great-grandmother, Temperance Osgood, who was there when it started. Read it. Then decide whether I'm a confused aunt."

* * *

Temperance Osgood wrote a cramped, furious hand, the script of a woman writing fast because paper cost money and truth cost more. I read it while Maren watched, and I will give it to you as straight as I can, because Lena Dravenmoor the scholar owes you that much, even now.

In 1741, Hollowmere was dying. Bad harvests, worse winters, a wool trade collapsing under them like a chair losing legs. The founding families — Osgood, Pell, Harrow, Vane, and Cottle — met in the church on the longest night of the year and did what desperate people do: they bargained with whatever was listening.

And something was listening. Something that lived — *inhabited* is Temperance's word, and she underlined it twice — in reflective surfaces. Glass, still water, polished steel. It had no name because, Temperance wrote, *it traded its name away long before it came to us, as it trades everything.* It wanted reflections. Not souls — souls, apparently, were beneath its interest, too noisy, too entangled. Reflections. The image of a person, the part of them that answers when answered.

In exchange: prosperity. Full barns, full purses, full pews.

Five families signed. Temperance described the signing — not ink, but each head of household pressing their palm to a mirror until their reflection pressed back and *stayed*. After that night, the mirrors in those five houses held nothing. You'd lean close and see the room behind you, the candle, the window — everything but yourself.

And Hollowmere prospered. Oh, how it prospered. Temperance's account turns almost sick with it — granaries overflowing, lambs born in pairs, debts forgiven by creditors who couldn't remember why they'd been owed anything at all.

"But," Maren said, when I looked up, "there's a maintenance clause."

"A what?"

"You've read contracts, haven't you? Nothing is ever just sold. There's always upkeep." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "The Thing doesn't take the reflection once and call it done. It takes it once and then it *watches*. And watching, Dravenmoor, is hungry work. So the founders agreed to feed it. A song. Sung aloud, in the village, at intervals. The lullaby."

"The payment schedule," I said. My mouth had gone dry enough that the words came out papery.

"That's what Temperance called it, near the end, when she'd stopped pretending it was anything holier. Each singing is a payment. And each payment has to be addressed to somebody." She turned the small black book toward me, to a page near the back where Temperance's handwriting deteriorated into something jagged. "*The song does not choose blind,*" I read. "*The song names the next household. Whoever hears it sung and carries it out of the room — theirs is the face on the next tally.*"

The tea in my cup had gone cold. I remember noticing that with absurd clarity, the skin of it, the way the surface held the kitchen window perfectly still — not lagging, not yet, just still — and thinking: *even the tea is more honest than these people.*

"Edith knew," I said. "When she sang for me. She knew she was naming someone."

"She knew she was paying. That's the ritual's cruelty — it makes every singer complicit. You sing to save your own house another season, and you sing someone else's name into the tally. Edith's husband was a Pell. Their turn was coming. Yours came instead." Maren refolded her hands. "She went pale because she liked you. Or because after two hundred years, even the guilty get tired."

"Why tell me now? Why does anyone tell me anything?"

"Because you're already counted, girl. The lag started in your house, didn't it?" I must have flinched, because she nodded slowly, unsurprised. "Mirrors first. Then the portraits. Then the roads. It always goes in that order. Once the lag starts, the telling isn't a betrayal anymore. It's just... paperwork."

*Paperwork.* I nearly laughed, and the laugh would have come out wrong, so I swallowed it, and it's been sitting in my chest ever since, undissolved.

"What happened to the five families' reflections?" I asked. "If they sold them in 1741, what's been counting since?"

Maren stood. Walked to the wall of photographs. Touched the frame of the oldest one — the christening party, the gap in the row of faces.

"We don't know what it does with them," she said quietly. "But I'll tell you what we know about *us*. My family sold our reflection in 1741 and prospered for a hundred years. And then, one by one, over one generation, the Osgoods stopped appearing in photographs. Not refused — stopped. The plate would develop and there'd be wallpaper where grandmother stood. Painters came to do portraits and found they couldn't hold our faces; the paint wouldn't take. By 1900 there wasn't an image of an Osgood left anywhere in England. We'd sold the reflection, and the reflection, it turns out, is what remembers you. Without it, the world forgets you slowly, from the edges inward. First pictures. Then names in parish books fading like bad ink. My grandfather's gravestone is blank. The mason swears he cut letters into it."

She turned back to me, and her eyes were wet, which somehow made her look more formidable, not less.

"I am the last of a family of ghosts, Dravenmoor. I have spent seventy years being forgotten by a village my blood bought with its face. And every time that lullaby is sung, I stand in my kitchen and I listen, and I wonder if this is the verse that finally comes for what's left of me." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, angrily. "So yes. I told you. Because you'll do what the others never dared. You'll try to break it. And I'd rather help you and be wrong than stay silent and be right."

* * *

I walked back along Tallow Row as the light went amber and long, and I thought about my brother asleep in the cottage with his daughter down the hall, and about Ivy saying the mirrors *feel slow*, and about the stopwatch in my pocket ticking against my hip like a second, smaller heart.

Prosperity bought with reflections. A lullaby as an invoice. A village of descendants, two hundred years deep in a debt they'd inherited like eye colour, each generation signing again by staying silent.

I hated them. I want to be honest about that — I hated every closed door on Tallow Row, every choir-perfect denial. But walking home in that amber light, hating them, I caught my own reflection in a dark cottage window, and it kept pace with me perfectly, faithfully, one whole second behind, and I understood the thing Maren hadn't said out loud:

Complicity doesn't require consent. It only requires inheritance.

And I had brought my family into the house on Lagging Lane, and hung my coat beside a hallway mirror, and slept two nights under a roof that was already, patiently, learning our faces.

Behind me, in the glass, my reflection stopped walking a moment before I did.

Just to practise, probably.

Just to practise.
