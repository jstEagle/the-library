## 21. Portrait

The morning the roads opened, Theo Almeida woke at dawn without an alarm, which was strange, because he hadn't slept properly in eleven days and had fully expected to sleep until the heat death of the sun. But there it was: light through the curtain gap, birdsong, the ordinary machinery of a world that had apparently decided, overnight, to stop trying to kill them.

He lay in bed and listened for the lag.

For eleven days the house had been full of it — that stretched, syrupy delay in every reflective surface, the sense that the world was a recording played back a half-beat late by something patient and enormous. He'd gotten so used to bracing for it that its absence felt like a sound in itself. A held breath released.

He got up. Checked the bathroom mirror — bare glass, his own face, instantaneous, unremarkable, his. He waved. The wave came back with him. He felt ridiculous doing it and did it anyway, because for eleven days he'd earned the right to feel ridiculous.

Ivy was already awake, sitting at the kitchen table in her pajamas, drawing. She looked up when he came in and smiled, and it was her smile, the real one, the one that arrived on time.

"Morning," she said.

"Morning, monster. Sleep okay?"

"I dreamed about Aunt Lena." She said it the way children say things — flat, factual, already returning to her crayons. "But I can't remember what she looks like. Isn't that weird?"

Theo's hand paused on the kettle. "Weird," he agreed, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect.

Because that was the thing. That was the whole terrible, merciful, unforgivable thing. He'd woken up and reached for his sister the way you reach for your phone in the dark — automatic, certain of its location — and found nothing. Not a hole. Holes you could feel. This was worse: a smoothness. A wall with fresh paint on it, so well done you'd never know a door had been there. He knew — in the way you know the rules of a dream even while inside it — that he had a sister, that she had come to Hollowmere with them, that she had done something. But the *her* of it, the face, the laugh, the way she'd said *Theo* when she was annoyed at him, which was always — gone. Filed away. Paid out.

He filled the kettle with hands that did not shake, mostly.

"We're leaving today," he told Ivy. "Right after — "

"After we wait," Ivy said, not looking up. "At the edge. She said."

Theo went very still. "Who said?"

Ivy frowned, a small crease of genuine puzzlement, a child encountering a word she knew but couldn't source. "I don't know," she said slowly. "I just — we have to wait at the edge of the village. Before the sign. Someone's coming out, and we have to be there, and we can't say her name because — " The crease deepened. "There isn't a name. Isn't that funny? I keep trying and there's just... a shape where it should be."

The kettle began its low rising note. Theo turned it off. He didn't want tea. He wanted to be standing at the edge of Hollowmere when whatever was coming out of it came out, because some animal part of him — the part that had slept with the mirrors covered, the part that had watched his own reflection pick up a hairbrush and smile at nothing — understood that this was not optional. That the waiting was part of the price. That somewhere, a ledger was still open, and the last line of it was being written this morning, and he was required to be present for the reading.

"Get dressed," he said. "We're going."

* * *

The village was out in force, which was its way of saying nothing at all.

They stood in their doorways and gardens as Theo walked Ivy down the high street, and they watched, and they did not speak, and Theo understood that this was the closest Hollowmere came to a eulogy. Maren stood at her gate, ancient and upright as a fencepost, and when Ivy passed she pressed something into the child's hand — a boiled sweet, wrapped in wax paper, the kind of gesture that outlives every explanation you could give it.

"Go on, then," Maren said to Theo. Her voice was gravel over grief. "You've paid. Go and be free of us."

"You could come," Theo said. It wasn't generosity. It was spite, a little, and he didn't pretend otherwise. "Roads are open. Nothing's stopping you."

Maren smiled, and it was the saddest thing he'd seen in a village full of sad things. "Boy, I signed that book in 1843. My name's in it twice. There's no road for me that doesn't go through what I did." She turned back to her garden, to her empty house, to her portraits of ancestors whose faces had been blank for a century. "Go on."

They walked. The village thinned. The last house gave way to hedgerow, the hedgerow to the long shallow rise where the road sign stood — WELCOME TO HOLLOWMERE, PLEASE DRIVE CAREFULLY, its paint fresh and its sentiment, Theo thought, darkly funny. They waited there, he and Ivy, in the cold morning, and Ivy ate her boiled sweet and swung her legs on the milestone, and Theo watched the road coming down from the village and tried to feel the shape where his sister should be.

It was like pressing on a bruise that wasn't there. He knew the bruise's history. He knew he'd been told it would hurt. But the skin was unbroken, and the ache was only the ache of knowing.

* * *

She came out of Hollowmere at nine minutes past eight, and Theo's heart broke in a way he would never be able to describe to anyone, because the breaking happened in the shapeless place where she used to live.

She walked up the road at an ordinary pace, a woman in a long coat, carrying a canvas satchel and a songbook bound in cracked green leather. Her hair was the same. Her walk was the same — he knew that without knowing why he knew it, the way you know a tune you can't name. She moved like someone who had walked a very long way inside a very small place and was still remembering how distance worked.

She was looking at the songbook as she walked. Turning the pages. Frowning at them.

Theo stepped forward, and Ivy slid off the milestone and took his hand, and they stood there, the three of them, in the middle of the road, strangers to one another in every way that the world kept records of.

"Excuse me," the woman said, and her voice did something to him — landed somewhere with no name, rang a bell in a room he'd been locked out of. "Sorry — do you live here? I can't make sense of this. It's written in..." She turned a page toward him, and he saw the old script, the dialect dead two hundred years, the verses he'd heard hummed under a recording in a dark kitchen at three in the morning. "I can't read any of it. I don't even know why I'm carrying it. I woke up on the road and it was in my bag, and there's a key in my pocket, and I can't — " She laughed, a short, embarrassed sound, and it went through Theo like a blade through water. "I'm sorry. You don't want a stranger's problems at eight in the morning. I'm looking for my car. I think I have a car. There's a key."

"Silver one?" Theo said. "Toyota. There's a dent in the back bumper from a wall in Bath."

The woman stared at him. "How did you — "

"Lucky guess," Theo said. "It's parked at the cottage. The one on Lagging Lane. I can walk you."

"You're very kind," she said, and meant it, and had no idea what she was saying or to whom, and Theo had to look at the road sign for a moment because his eyes had decided to do something embarrassing.

"Are you lost?" Ivy asked. She had stepped forward. She was looking up at the woman with the frank, unblinking attention children reserve for the genuinely important.

"A bit," the woman said, and crouched down to Ivy's level, and smiled, and Theo watched the smile arrive — on time, whole, human, hers — and something in him tore cleanly down the middle. "I think I was looking for something," she said to Ivy, "and I found it, and now I can't remember what it was. Does that ever happen to you?"

"All the time," Ivy said solemnly.

"I expect it gets better," the woman said.

"No," Ivy said. "I expect it doesn't."

The woman laughed again, and Theo thought: *she used to laugh like that at dinner. She used to — * and the thought dissolved like sugar in rain, and he was left holding only the knowledge of having held it.

"I'm Theo," he said, because someone had to. "This is Ivy. We're — we were staying in the village. Leaving this morning."

"Pleased to meet you," the woman said, and offered her hand, and Theo shook it. Her grip was warm. Her hand was steady. There was a callus on the middle finger, pen-callus, the callus of someone who wrote for a living, and he looked at it and understood nothing and understood everything.

"I'm a writer," she said, following his glance, misreading it kindly. "Or I think I am. There's a notebook in my bag full of — notes. About a village. Folk songs. It's all in my handwriting and I don't remember doing any of it." She shook her head, rueful. "The doctor in the next town over is going to get an earful, I expect. Amnesia, apparently. The real thing. Like something out of a novel."

"Like something out of a novel," Theo agreed, and his voice only cracked a little.

* * *

They walked her to the cottage. It seemed the correct thing to do, and also the only thing, because the price had a shape and the shape required it: she had to be walked out by the people she'd bought, and she had to not know them, and they had to carry it.

She looked at the cottage with a small frown. "I know this house," she said. "Isn't that strange? I feel like I've been here."

"People say that about rented places," Theo said. "Déjà vu."

"It's more than that." She stood in the lane, the songbook under her arm, and looked up at the attic window, and for a moment — one long moment — her face did something. Not a memory. Memories were gone, sold, spent. But a shape moved behind her eyes, the way a fish moves under ice: visible, unreachable. "There were portraits up there," she said slowly. "In the attic. Faces with the eyes — "

She stopped. Blinked. Frowned at herself.

"Sorry," she said. "I don't know where that came from. There's nothing up there. I'm sure there's nothing up there."

"There's nothing up there," Theo said. He had checked, that morning, at five a.m., with a hammer. There was nothing up there now. There was a great deal of sawdust.

Inside, she collected her car keys from the hook by the door — she went straight to the hook, no hesitation, her hand rising to it as if the hand remembered — and stood in the front hall, and looked at the wall where the hallway mirror had hung for eleven days like a held breath. The wall was bare. A clean rectangle of less-faded wallpaper marked where it had been.

"There was a mirror there," she said.

"We took it down," Theo said. "It was cracked."

She nodded, accepting this, and then she didn't. She looked at the rectangle on the wall for a long time. Theo watched her look at it and saw her arrive at the edge of something and turn back from it — deliberately, the way you turn back from a door you've decided not to open — and he understood that some part of her had made the same trade he had: not memory, but the *search* for memory. A peace bought at the same counter.

"Strange house," she said at last, lightly. "I don't envy whoever rents it next."

"No," Theo said. "Neither do I."

Ivy tugged the woman's coat. "Will you come back?"

"Ivy," Theo said.

"I don't see why not," the woman said, crouching again, and Theo's chest did something structural and painful. "I have to return the car, don't I? It's not mine. There must be an owner somewhere." She smiled at Ivy. "You take care of your uncle. He looks like he worries."

"He does," Ivy said. "He sleeps with the lights on."

"Ivy."

"Well, you do."

The woman laughed, and gathered her songbook, and Theo walked her out to the Toyota, and she drove it — competently, automatically, her hands finding the wheel and the mirrors and the gearstick with the dumb fluency of the body, which forgets nothing even when everything else is sold — down the lane, past the sign, onto the open road.

She stopped at the bend. He watched her pause there, watch her look back at the village in the rearview mirror, and he saw her raise one hand — a small wave, to no one, to the town, to the shape of a morning — and then the road took the bend and she was gone.

Theo stood in the lane with his niece's hand in his, and the village of Hollowmere sat quiet behind them, and the morning was so ordinary it was almost an insult.

"Uncle Theo," Ivy said. "Who was she?"

He looked down at her. Her face was open, curious, clean. She didn't remember either. None of them remembered, and that was the payment, and it had cleared, and the count was closed, and the roads were open, and it was the best thing that had ever happened to any of them and it had cost exactly everything.

"I don't know," Theo said. Which was true. Which was the truest thing he'd ever said. "Someone who helped us, I think."

"Oh," Ivy said. She considered this. Then: "I liked her."

"Yeah," Theo said. "Me too."

They packed the car. They left the key to the cottage on the kitchen counter, under the one boiled sweet Maren had pressed on Ivy that Ivy hadn't eaten — saved, Theo realized, saved deliberately, though he couldn't have said how he knew — and they drove out of Hollowmere with the windows down, and the sign slid past, and the road did the beautiful, unremarkable, miraculous thing that roads do: it went somewhere else.

Ivy fell asleep twenty minutes out. Theo drove and drove and did not turn on the radio, and at the top of the first real hill he pulled over, and sat, and let himself shake for a while, quietly, with the engine ticking and his niece breathing in the seat beside him.

He didn't remember his sister. He never would. But he remembered — the way you remember a debt, the way the body remembers a fall — that he had loved her, that she had loved them, that she had walked into a mirror at dawn so that he and a six-year-old could drive out of a valley. The love was gone. The fact of the love remained, a fossil, an imprint in stone where the living thing had been.

He wiped his face. He started the car.

And in the glovebox — he found it that evening, at the motorway services, while looking for napkins — he found a photograph he had never seen. A woman he didn't recognize, arm around a younger Theo, both of them squinting into summer sun. On the back, in handwriting that made his hand tremble without his permission, without his understanding: *So you don't forget that you forgot. — L.*

He stared at it for a long time.

Then he put it back in the glovebox, face down, and drove on, because some doors you don't open twice.

He didn't notice Ivy watching from the back seat. He didn't notice her humming — soft, tuneless, patient — a small looping melody she'd never been taught, from a song she'd never heard, in a language two hundred years dead.

He didn't notice that she was smiling half a second before anything happened to smile about.

But somewhere behind them, in a thrift-store window in a town they'd never see, a woman who was not Lena Dravenmoor anymore paused in front of a mirror, and her reflection smiled first — and she hummed along.
