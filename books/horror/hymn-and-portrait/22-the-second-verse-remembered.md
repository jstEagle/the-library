## 22. The Second Verse, Remembered

The thing about forgetting someone is that nobody warns you it feels like a word stuck on the tip of your tongue. You know the shape of it. You know where it should sit in your mouth. But every time you reach for it, you come away with your hands empty and a strange ache behind your teeth.

My name is Ivy Almeida. I am nine years old, I live with my dad Theo in a flat above a bakery that smells like cinnamon at four in the morning, and until three weeks ago I thought there were two of us.

There's a photograph on our mantelpiece now. Dad says it came with us from Hollowmere, though he can't remember taking it. It shows a cottage doorway, and me, age maybe six, sitting on the step with a woman behind me. Her hand is on my shoulder. Her face is slightly blurred, the way faces get when the camera catches them mid-laugh, except she isn't laughing. She's looking just past the camera, like something behind the photographer had said her name.

Dad calls her "the landlady." He says it easily, without thinking, the way you say a word you've said ten thousand times. *The landlady's coat is still in the hall closet. The landlady must have liked this spot by the window.* And every time he says it, something in my chest goes tight and hot, like swallowing a marble.

Because I know — not remember, *know*, the way you know how to breathe — that she wasn't the landlady.

I don't know who she was. That's the trick of it. The knowing has no name attached. It's like a coat hook with nothing hanging on it, still holding the shape of shoulders.

* * *

School started again last week. Miss Okafor asked us to bring in a song from home, something our families sing, for the harvest assembly. Kids brought football chants and one boy brought the entire extended remix of a song about a shark, which Miss Okafor regretted immediately.

When it was my turn, I opened my mouth to say I didn't have one.

And out came a melody.

It wasn't loud. It was low, more hum than song, the kind of sound you make when you're rocking something small to sleep. The classroom went quiet in that way classrooms do when they realize they're hearing something they weren't supposed to hear. Even Shark Boy stopped fidgeting.

Miss Okafor leaned forward. "That's lovely, Ivy. What's it called?"

"I don't know," I said, which was true, and also the most frightening sentence I've ever said, because my mouth kept going while my brain stood at the back shouting questions. Words came with the melody. Old words. Words that felt heavy in my mouth, like stones warmed by someone else's fire. I sang four lines before I caught myself and shut my jaw so hard my teeth clicked.

"That's an odd dialect," Miss Okafor said. "Where did you learn it?"

Nowhere. That was the answer. Nowhere, no one, never. Except somewhere, someone, always, and the somewhere had been scooped out of me like the middle of an apple, leaving the skin standing upright and pretending to be whole.

"A lady taught me," I said, because it was easier than the truth.

"What lady?"

I looked at the mantelpiece photograph in my mind — the blurred face, the hand on my shoulder — and said, "I forget."

Miss Okafor smiled and moved on to the next kid, and I sat there with the melody coiled in my chest like a sleeping snake, warm and terrible, and I thought: *somebody paid for me to be here.* I don't know how I knew that either. But I did, the way I knew the second verse, the way I knew the mirror-girl's smile was wrong before anyone told me mirrors could be wrong at all.

Some things you don't learn. Some things are left in you, like furniture in a house when the family moves out in the night.

* * *

At home, I tested it. That's what you do in Hollowmere-adjacent life: you test. You check your reflection twice. You count the seconds between you and the glass. These habits came back with us too, though Dad thinks they're just tics, the way he thinks the landlady is the landlady, the way he thinks the bad dreams he won't describe are just dreams.

I stood in front of the bathroom mirror — a cheap one, silvering peeling at the corners like old wallpaper — and I hummed the verse.

The girl in the mirror hummed it too.

On time. Exactly on time. Not early, not late. I watched her mouth for a full minute, waiting for the half-second slip, the smile that arrives before its reason does. Nothing. She was just a girl with sleep-tangled hair and a school jumper with gravy on the collar, doing exactly what I did, when I did it.

"Good," I whispered.

"Good," she whispered, and for a moment — half a heartbeat, no more — I saw something flicker across her face that wasn't mine. Not a smile. Something older and sadder. Recognition, maybe. Or grief wearing recognition's coat.

Then she was just me again, and I went and ate toast, because I'm nine and even hauntings have to wait until after tea.

* * *

But here's the part I haven't told Dad, the part I keep folded up small inside the melody where nobody checks:

The verse has a third line. Everyone thinks it ends where I stopped singing in class. It doesn't. There's a third line that only comes if you sing the whole thing alone, in a room with a mirror, after dark. I know this the way I know the melody itself — not learned, *left*. And the third line isn't a lullaby at all. Lullabies are for sending people to sleep.

The third line is for waking them up.

I haven't sung it yet.

I'm saving it.

* * *

Three hundred miles north, in a city where nobody had ever heard of Hollowmere, a woman woke up in a rented room with a songbook she couldn't read and a car key she couldn't explain.

Her name — the name on the single piece of ID in her pocket, the name she'd accepted the way you accept weather — was Lena Dravenmoor. She'd found it written inside the cover of the songbook, along with a phone number that disconnected, an address that turned out to be a burned lot, and a pressed flower she suspected, without knowing why, had once been tucked there by small hands.

She was a folklorist. This fact arrived in her head fully formed, like furniture delivered and assembled: she collected songs, especially songs sung only once, especially songs people were afraid of. She owned recording equipment she had no memory of buying. Her handwriting in the margins of the songbook was confident, scholarly, annotated in three languages — and she understood none of the annotations, as though a stranger had borrowed her hand and given it back full of someone else's learning.

The songbook contained one song. The notation was archaic. The lyrics were in a dialect dead for two centuries, and though she could parse the grammar — some stubborn scholarly reflex doing the work without her permission — the meaning slid off her like water off glass. Every time she reached for what the words meant, she came away with the shape of a word stuck on the tip of her tongue and an ache behind her teeth.

She'd learned to stop reaching. Mostly.

* * *

The thrift store was called Second Chances, which she found funnier than the shop deserved. She hadn't meant to go in. She'd been walking off another night of dreams she wouldn't remember by morning — dreams that left her pillow damp and her chest hollowed out, dreams with the specific texture of farewell — and the window display snagged her the way certain songs snagged her: a chipped teapot, a taxidermy pike, and a mirror.

An ordinary mirror. Oval, dark-framed, the silvering spotted with age. The kind of thing that hangs in ten thousand hallways and reflects ten thousand unremarkable lives.

She went in. She told herself it was for the teapot.

The mirror cost four pounds. The man at the till wrapped it in newspaper and said, "Funny one, that. Came in with an estate lot from down south. Village called—" he snapped his fingers, "—Hollow-something. Whole house clearance. Sad story, apparently."

"Was it?" she said.

"Dunno. Nobody'd say." He shrugged. "Buyer backed out twice. Said the thing gave them the creeps. You want the receipt?"

"No," she said. "I think it's coming home with me regardless."

She carried it back to her room through streets going amber with evening, and she noticed — distantly, academically, the way you notice a stranger's cough on a train — that she kept glancing at her reflection in shop windows as she walked. Checking. Counting, almost, though she had no idea what she'd be counting toward.

* * *

She hung the mirror on the wall opposite her bed, because that was the only wall with a nail already in it, and made tea, and sat on the bed with the songbook open on her knees, and looked at herself.

A woman in her thirties looked back. Tired around the eyes. A small scar on her chin she had no history for. Hair that needed cutting. Someone's sister, possibly. Someone's — 

The thought arrived and dissolved, the way all those thoughts did. *Someone's* blank. The tip-of-the-tongue ache flared and faded.

"You look like you've lost something," she told her reflection, wry, because talking to yourself was cheaper than therapy and the room was too quiet otherwise.

The reflection agreed silently, mouth moving a half-beat behind hers, as reflections do.

Except.

She frowned. Ran it back. Replayed it the way she'd replay field recordings, hunting anomalies, because that reflex at least was hers, or close enough to hers to steer by.

Her mouth had moved. Then the reflection's mouth had moved.

No. Other way round. The reflection had finished the sentence *first.*

Lena Dravenmoor — the name, the borrowed name, sat oddly on her just then, like a coat belonging to a taller woman — set down her tea very carefully, the way you set down a cup when the floor might be listening. She raised her right hand.

The reflection raised its right hand. On time. Perfectly on time.

She lowered it. On time.

She smiled — a small, testing, professional smile, the smile of a scholar confirming a citation.

And the reflection smiled *back* — a half-second *early*, arriving before her own muscles got the memo, a smile with no cause yet on either side of the glass, blooming in the mirror like a flower opening before its stem has grown.

Anyone else would have screamed, or run, or at minimum put the mirror face-down in the bathtub. She sat very still instead, heart tapping out a quick irregular rhythm, and felt something worse than fear.

She felt *recognition.* The way you feel when a melody you've never heard turns out to be one you somehow know all the words to.

"All right," she said quietly, to the woman in the glass. "Let's try that again. Slowly."

The reflection waited, patient, attentive, with the particular patience of something that has counted many things and is very good at waiting for the next one.

* * *

That night she dreamed — properly dreamed, remembered-dreamed, for the first time since she'd woken with a stranger's name — of a cottage doorway at dusk. Of a small girl on the step with jam on her chin. Of a tall young man complaining about the price of petrol while his eyes said something kinder. Of a hallway with a mirror at the end of it, and a stopwatch, and her own voice saying *one second, exactly one second, I measured it,* in a tone of triumphant disbelief.

Of singing. Three verses. The third one costing more than a voice has any right to cost, each note a door closing inside her, rooms going dark one by one, and her singing anyway, singing *through* it, because the alternative was letting the dark take the rooms with the people still in them.

She woke at dawn with wet cheeks and the melody complete in her head, all three verses, and no idea why the third verse tasted like salt.

She got up. She washed her face. She avoided the mirror while she did it, which she noted, dryly, was becoming a habit she had no basis for.

Then she sat on the edge of the bed with the songbook, and she hummed.

Just the second verse. Just to hear it in daylight, defanged, ordinary. A lullaby is only a lullaby, she told herself. A song is only a song. Whatever it cost whoever sang it, that ledger is closed; the accounts are settled; four pounds and a chipped frame, that's all this is.

She hummed the second verse, the old dead words rising in her throat like water finding an old channel.

And from the mirror — from the oval of spotted silver across the room — a voice hummed along.

Not behind her. Not ahead of her. *With* her. Harmonizing, low and sweet, a second voice under hers the way a root runs under a tree, and when she stopped, mid-phrase, heart slamming, the voice continued alone for three more notes before it, too, fell silent.

Lena looked at the mirror for a long time.

The woman in the mirror looked back. Tired around the eyes. Scar on the chin. And smiling — not the wrong smile, not the early one, not the hungry patient one from the counting houses of wherever counting houses are. A small, tired, *familiar* smile. The smile of someone greeting you at a door you both thought you'd never see again.

"Well," said Lena Dravenmoor, whose name was a coat that fit better every day, to the woman in the glass who wore the same coat. "You know the words too, then."

The reflection nodded. Once. Slowly. And began, softly, patiently, sweetly — the way you teach a child, the way you teach someone you love, the way you teach someone you intend to keep —

to sing the third verse.

* * *

In a flat above a bakery, nine-year-old Ivy Almeida woke at dawn with the melody coiled warm in her chest, and lay still, listening to her father snore, and made a decision she'd been making in her sleep for weeks:

Tonight. After dark. Alone, in the bathroom, with the cheap mirror with the peeling silver.

Tonight she would sing the third line, the waking line, the line that was left inside her like furniture in an abandoned house.

Because the coat hook by their front door still held the shape of shoulders. Because the marble in her chest rolled over every time Dad said *the landlady.* Because somebody had paid everything to leave her here safe, and safe was not the same as *finished*, and Ivy Almeida had inherited exactly one thing from the woman with the blurred face:

she had never once, in her whole life, left a song unsung.
