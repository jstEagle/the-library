## 3. The Papers

Lena had always trusted paper more than people. Paper did not look at you with the wrong name in its mouth. Paper stayed where you left it, held its ink, kept its small promises of permanence. So on his second morning in Ashmoor, while the village outside went about its business of insisting his sister was alive, he went looking for paper.

The parish office stood between the chapel and the green, a low stone building with a slate roof furred with lichen. Its door was unlocked. Of course it was. Ashmoor seemed to have no locks at all, no bolts, no latches—every door he had tried since arriving had swung open at a touch, as if the village had been waiting for him to open things.

Inside smelled of dust and candle wax and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, like water that had never seen light. A desk. A filing cabinet with one drawer hanging crooked. And along the back wall, shelves of ledgers bound in grey cloth, their spines stamped with years going back further than any reasonable village should have records: 1921, 1888, 1861, 1849.

He pulled the oldest down first. Habit. When you wanted to understand a machine, you started at the beginning of it.

The ledger was titled, in faded copperplate across the first page, *Register of Signatures — Ashmoor-cum-Underchapel*. Beneath that, in smaller script: *Being an account of those who have consented to keep.*

Lena sat down on the office's only chair—a wooden thing with a rush seat worn into the shape of someone else—and began to read.

The entries were simple. A name. A date. A line for a signature, most of them signed in a careful nineteenth-century hand, some later ones scrawled or shaky or pressed so hard into the page they'd torn through. And after each signature, in whatever hand had made the entry, a single word, small and neat, like a clerk's marginal note:

*Remembered.*

Or:

*Kept.*

He turned pages. The pattern held without exception. 1849: Thomas Averill, remembered. Sarah Averill, remembered. Josiah Pell, kept. 1851: Mercy Pell, kept. 1854: a run of names from a family called Corrow, all remembered but one—Elias Corrow, kept. On and on, decade by decade, the two words alternating down the columns like weather, like a tide chart, like something being counted.

He understood, distantly, that his heart was beating faster. This was the feeling he knew best in the world—the investigator's lift, the sense of a lock giving its first quarter-turn. There was a system here. Systems could be learned. Systems could be beaten.

Then he reached 1976, and found his own surname.

*Pemberton, Lena. Pemberton, Mara.* The date was the twelfth of March, the year they were born. Two entries, side by side, in the same blue-black ink. But where every other entry carried a signature, these two carried none. Where every other entry carried its notation, these two were followed by a dash—a short, deliberate horizontal stroke, as if the clerk had lifted his pen, considered, and set it down again without deciding.

Unsigned. Unmarked. Unfinished.

His sister's name sat on the page in front of him, and it was not written by her. It was written *for* her, forty-eight years before she died, before she had ever drawn breath in this village she'd supposedly never left. Someone had entered her into this book when she was hours old. Someone had expected her to come back and sign.

And someone had expected him to come back and not.

Lena closed the ledger carefully, the way you close a door on a room where something is sleeping. His hands were not quite steady. The thrill was still there, but it had begun to curdle, the way milk curdles—not ruined exactly, but changed into something you couldn't drink. Because now the logic of the book was assembling itself in his head, piece by piece, and each piece was worse than the last.

Every resident signed. Every resident was then either *remembered* or *kept*. And the villagers he had met—the funeral director who called him Mara, the woman at the shop who'd asked after "your sister, is she well?"—they were remembered. They remembered nothing true. They remembered what the village needed them to remember.

Which meant *kept* meant something else entirely. Something the book didn't say. Something that happened to the ones who were kept, the Josiah Pells and Elias Corrows, generation after generation, names that appeared once in the register and then, he suspected, nowhere else at all.

He thought of Edith Corrow, standing in her doorway at dusk, telling him never to answer a bell after dark. He thought of how she had looked at him—not at Mara-in-him, but at *him*, the way no one else in Ashmoor could manage. As if she were also a dash. An unfinished entry.

* * *

The chapel crypt was behind the chapel, which was itself at the end of a lane that seemed slightly longer on the walk out than on the walk back. Lena noted this and filed it away with everything else: the lane's elasticity, the bell that rang though it had been melted in 1911, the guestbook at the funeral home bearing his dead sister's living signature. Ashmoor was a place that kept two sets of books, and he was beginning to suspect the visible one was the forgery.

The crypt door was iron, painted over so many times it had gone soft-edged, and it opened onto stairs going down into a cold that felt less like temperature than like opinion. The air below was damp and still. His torch beam found stone walls weeping moisture, arched ceilings lost in shadow, and niches cut into the rock—dozens of them, generations of them—most standing empty.

Not empty because the bodies had been moved. Empty because the bodies had never been filled. The niches were cut to standard dimensions, spaced with a workmanlike regularity, and dust lay in them undisturbed, thick as felt. Whatever the founders of Ashmoor had built this crypt to hold, it had never been coffins.

At the far end, the floor sloped down toward a low archway, and beyond the archway came a sound he felt before he heard it: water. Not moving water. Water holding still, breathing very quietly, the way a large animal sleeps.

He should have turned back. He knew that even then; some part of him that had survived childhood in this village, that had learned to count doors and lights and exits, was tugging his sleeve and saying *not yet, not alone, not without Edith.* But the other part of him—the part that had spent forty-eight years being overlooked, unclaimed, invisible, the part that had watched his brilliant, luminous twin collect all the attention like rain while he collected only the puddles—that part wanted the truth the way a drowning man wants air, without dignity, without proportion.

He stepped through the archway.

The flooded chamber was small, maybe ten feet square, its ceiling low enough that he had to stoop. Black water covered the floor to ankle depth, perfectly flat, perfectly reflective, and in it his torchlight doubled itself so that he seemed to stand inside a lantern. Along one wall ran a stone shelf, dry above the waterline, and on the shelf lay objects arranged with terrible neatness: a brass bowl gone green with age. A bundle of tallow candles fused into one mass. A length of rope coiled in perfect circles. And a book.

It was larger than the ledgers upstairs, bound in boards covered with what might have been vellum or might have been something he preferred not to name, and it was dry. Everything else in the crypt wore its dampness openly, but the book sat on its shelf untouched by water or rot, as if the wetness itself had agreed to keep away from it.

Lena crouched at the shelf's edge, not touching, and read the title stamped into the cover in letters worn nearly smooth:

*Census Hymnal — Being the Roll of Souls, and the Manner of Their Keeping.*

A hymnal. A census. The words shouldn't have belonged together, but here they did, and somewhere in the walls above him—or beneath him, or in the water at his feet—he felt something shift its weight, slow and vast, the way a house settles, the way a sleeper turns.

He did not touch the hymnal. He wrote down the title in his notebook instead, copying each letter with a care that felt almost devotional, and it was only afterward, climbing the crypt stairs with his heart slamming and the cold peeling off him like a second coat, that he understood why he hadn't touched it.

Because the register upstairs had told him what the ritual required: signatures, consent, keeping. And the hymnal's title had told him what the ritual *was*: a roll call. A naming. Every soul accounted for, sung aloud, kept.

And his sister's name was in the register, unsigned, waiting.

Mara had come back to Ashmoor—to be buried here, of all places, when there had been a hundred sensible crematoria between London and the coast—and Lena had assumed it was sentiment, some pull of childhood he'd never shared because he'd never been allowed to share anything. But the ledger suggested otherwise. The ledger suggested Mara had come home to finish something. To sign, perhaps. Or to refuse.

And the dash beside her name—the clerk's hesitation, frozen for half a century—suggested that whoever kept this book had never been sure which she would choose.

Neither, now, was Lena. He stood in the churchyard in the failing afternoon light, notebook in his pocket, the melted bell tower rising against a sky the colour of a bruise going yellow at the edges, and he realized that the question the ledger had been asking since 1976 was about to be answered whether he liked it or not.

Behind him, in the empty crypt, in the black and sleeping water, something began—very faintly, very patiently—to hum.

It hummed a tune he did not know.

And then, without changing the melody at all, it hummed it in his sister's voice.
