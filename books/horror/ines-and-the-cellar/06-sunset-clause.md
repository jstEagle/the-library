## 6. Sunset Clause

Marcus Vane had read the founding documents of Underhill & Vane four times in his life: once at his admission to the partnership, once at his father's funeral, once on the night his wife left him, and once last Tuesday, alone in the office after hours, with the door locked and a glass of something expensive sweating on the blotter.

The first three readings had been professional. The fourth was a surrender.

He stood now in the east parlor of Marrow House, watching the fog press itself against the windows like a face too polite to knock, and rehearsed what he would say when the nurse asked her questions again. Because she would ask them. She had that look — the hospice look, the one that said *I have watched five hundred people die and I will not be managed by a man in a good suit.*

"Marcus." His sister Prudence swept in, trailing perfume and grievance in equal measure. "Tell me you've got the papers ready. Mother's been asleep since three and I refuse to sit through another supper of soup and silence."

"The papers are ready."

"And she'll sign?"

"She'll sign," Marcus said, "or someone will sign for her. It's all in the instrument, Pru. There's a provision."

Prudence's eyes did the thing they always did — flicked toward the ceiling, toward the money, toward the house itself, which she regarded less as a home than as a very large, very slow dividend. "There's always a provision with you. That's why we keep you."

That's why they keep you. He smiled the smile he'd been practicing since 1998 and thought: *you have no idea what you keep me for.*

* * *

He had found it, that Tuesday night, in Schedule C of the firm's articles of association — the page every lawyer signs without reading because it sits behind forty pages of indemnity boilerplate, and because no one, in one hundred and fifty years, had ever had reason to look.

*In consideration of certain services rendered and continuing,* it began, in handwriting so old the ink had gone the color of dried blood, which Marcus supposed it might be.

He had read it twice more with a magnifying lamp. Then he'd poured the drink.

The terms were simple, as the oldest contracts always are. The firm — his firm, his name on the door, half of it anyway — existed to administer the Vane arrangement. Every thirty years, an invitation renewed. In exchange: prosperity, longevity, the peculiar good luck that had followed the Vanes and their lawyers through wars and crashes and the death of entire industries. And in exchange for *that*: a debt, held against the family collectively, redeemable at the discretion of the party below.

Marcus had done the arithmetic the way he did all arithmetic — coldly, at midnight, with the calculator app because even doom deserved accuracy. The debt came due this year. The renewal papers he'd drafted — the inheritance instruments sitting upstairs in his briefcase, notarized, immaculate, waiting — were not a will. They were a lease extension.

And here was the clause that had made him set down the glass with a hand that only shook a little:

*Should the renewal be executed by a single signatory acting with full knowledge and free consent, the obligations herein shall vest solely in that signatory, and all other parties named or unnamed shall be released therefrom.*

Solely in that signatory.

All others released.

He had sat in the dark of his office for a long time, thinking about his brother-in-law's gambling debts, about Pru's second mortgage, about Sylvie — poor soft Sylvie, who still sent Mother birthday cards with actual sentences in them. Thinking about the thing in the cellar, if there was a thing in the cellar, which there was, because he had read the 1974 correspondence too, and his grandfather's letters did not read like the letters of a man who was joking.

Then he had thought about himself, alone, unencumbered, fifty-one years old and tired of carrying everyone.

*Free consent.* Well. He could manage consent. As for full knowledge — he had that too, more than anyone alive except Edith, and Edith was dying, and dying women sign nothing.

* * *

The nurse found him in the corridor outside Edith's room at dusk, and she asked her questions, exactly as predicted.

"Mr. Vane. Your mother told me the papers are a renewal. That signing them renews some kind of invitation. I'd like to know what she meant."

There it was. Hospice eyes. He gave her the corridor smile, the one calibrated for juries and grieving widows. "Miss Amberdale. My mother is ninety-one, on enough morphine to fell a horse, and has spent six decades trying to disinherit her children out of spite. You've heard a confused old woman's nightmare. I'd expect better discernment from a medical professional."

"I'd expect a son to sit with his dying mother instead of guarding the hallway."

Ouch. Fair. He let the smile drop a millimeter. "My mother and I have an understanding."

"Do you." Hazel Amberdale folded her arms. She was smaller than him, younger, tired around the eyes in a way that made her seem older than all of them put together. "Because from where I stand, your understanding looks like three vultures circling a bed, and you're the one holding the paperwork."

"You know," Marcus said pleasantly, "in my experience, people who use the word 'vulture' are usually hiding carrion of their own. What is it you want, nurse? A cut? A reference? A moment of drama before you go back to whatever small life brought you here?"

Something crossed her face — not hurt. Recognition. The look of a woman filing away a fact for later, the way she probably filed away pupil dilation and skin temperature. "I want your mother to die in peace," she said. "That's all. It's a short list. You're not on it."

She turned to go back into the room, and Marcus — calmly, regretfully, with the part of him that still remembered being a decent man wincing somewhere far away — stepped past her and opened the linen room door instead.

"Actually," he said, "there's a provision."

* * *

The linen room smelled of lavender and camphor and forty years of Edith Vane's bedlinens, and it was, Marcus reflected as he turned the key in the old lock, the least terrible thing he had ever done in service of a contract. Not the worst. Just the most recent.

Her voice came through the door almost immediately. Not screaming — he'd expected screaming. Worse than screaming. Reasonable.

"Mr. Vane. Open the door."

"No."

"You know what the papers are. You've read the same documents I have — I can tell, because you didn't laugh at me just now. A man who thinks his mother is senile doesn't lock her nurse in a closet. He apologizes and calls her a taxi." A pause. The sound of her palm flat against the wood, testing it. "You've read Schedule C."

Marcus closed his eyes. "You should never have seen those papers."

"Edith showed me. She wants to die unsigned. She wants it over — the whole arrangement, ended, the debt defaulted, the family freed by losing everything. And you'd rather—"

"I'd rather my family survive," Marcus said quietly, "than be free. Freedom's worthless to the dead, Miss Amberdale, and that's what the alternative makes us. All of us. My mother included — especially my mother, who signed the original invitation and has been dodging the interest for fifty years." He leaned his forehead against the door. Through it, faintly, he could hear her breathing, steady, clinical, counting something under her breath — he caught the numbers, descending, and felt an absurd urge to apologize.

"There's a clause," he said. "Single signatory, full knowledge, free consent. Sole vesting. Everyone else released. Pru, Dominic, Sylvie — clean. Their debts die with the arrangement. The house, the money, the obligation — mine alone. One soul on the ledger instead of five. That's not greed, nurse. That's triage. You of all people should understand triage."

Silence. Then, softly, horribly gently: "You've decided you're the one who gets to be saved. And you're calling it medicine."

"I'm calling it the only deal on the table."

"It's not the only deal. There's one where nobody signs and everybody walks away poorer and alive. Your mother found it. She's been sitting on it for months, waiting for one of her children to be brave enough to take it." A pause, and then, quieter, almost kind: "You looked, didn't you? In the mirrors. You saw yourself a second late, and you told yourself it was fatigue."

Marcus's hand tightened on the key until the teeth bit his palm. "Goodnight, Miss Amberdale."

"You'll see them tonight," her voice followed him down the corridor. "The versions of yourself that didn't do this. They'll wave you over. Don't go. Whatever they promise you — don't—"

The door of Edith's room stood open ahead of him, lamplight spilling gold across the floorboards, and beyond it the long hall of Marrow House stretched away into the gathering dark, its mirrors catching the light and holding it — just slightly too long. Just a breath too long. Long enough, if a man were honest, to notice.

Marcus straightened his tie, lifted his briefcase, and went in to get his mother's signature.

Behind him, from the linen room, the nurse had stopped counting backwards.

She had started counting up.
