## 9. Until Sunrise

The linen room smelled of lavender and old lies.

Hazel had spent forty minutes picking the lock with a bobby pin and a tongue depressor, which she felt should qualify her for some kind of nursing award — *Most Resourceful Under Supernatural Duress* — and when the door finally gave, the hallway beyond was wrong in a way she could feel through her slippers. The floorboards hummed. Not metaphorically. They hummed, low and patient, like a throat clearing somewhere beneath the house.

The mirrors were all broken now except one at the corridor's end, and that one was full of dark water that did not spill.

She didn't look at it. She'd learned that much. Looking was an invitation, and Marrow House had enough invitations already — it was practically a wedding venue for the damned.

Sylvie was waiting at Edith's door, pale as candle wax, clutching the ledger to her chest like a baby. Behind her, from the bedroom, came the sound of Marcus's voice, smooth and rehearsed, reading clauses aloud to a woman who could no longer lift a pen.

"You got out," Sylvie whispered.

"I got out." Hazel took the ledger. It was heavier than a book had any right to be, as though it had been absorbing weight for fifty years — every signature, every soul-debt, pressed down into its binding. "Where are the papers?"

"Marcus has them. He's making her sign by proxy. Richard already—" Sylvie's voice cracked. "Richard signed on her behalf. I heard the pen."

Hazel closed her eyes. Somewhere below them, something had begun to climb.

They both heard it then: not footsteps exactly, but the *idea* of footsteps, a slow ascending pressure, each one arriving a half-second after you expected it. One second late. Everything in this house was one second late, and now the lateness itself was coming up the stairs.

"The cellar," Hazel said. "We burn it down there or not at all. Edith was clear. The revocation only holds where the invitation lives."

"She also said," Sylvie managed, "that if we died doing it, she'd be very cross with us in whatever comes next."

"That sounds like her."

"It does, actually."

From the bedroom, Marcus's voice rose, delighted: "*Executed and witnessed.*"

And every clock Hazel couldn't see began ticking faster.

* * *

The cellar door stood open at the end of the kitchen passage, breathing cold air upward like a mouth. Someone — the house, presumably, wanting its guests comfortable — had removed the old nails entirely. The hinges didn't even creak. That was somehow worse than creaking. A door that creaked was still arguing with you. This door had simply decided to agree.

"Okay," Hazel said, ledger under one arm, Edith's brass candle-lighter in her pocket, and a box of kitchen matches in her fist because she did not trust this house with electricity anymore. "We go down fast, we burn fast, we come up faster. We do not stop. We do not answer anything that calls us. And Sylvie—"

"Yes?"

"If you see me standing at the bottom of the stairs before I get there, that is not me. Do not be polite about it. Run past."

"Noted. Same rule applies to me?"

"Especially to you. You have a very trustworthy face. It'll use that."

Sylvie almost smiled. In the dark, going down toward the thing their family had fed for fifty years, she almost smiled, and Hazel thought: *this one takes after Edith.*

The stairs went down farther than they had that afternoon. Hazel counted eleven steps on the way down earlier; now there were seventeen, then twenty-three, the walls sweating cold, the smell thickening — dust and tallow and something organic underneath, the smell of a room remembering being occupied, of a guest who had never checked out. The dark below was not empty. It was *attentive*.

Halfway down, a voice came up to meet them.

It used Edith's voice first. *"Hazel, dear, don't bother, come sit with me instead, the papers don't matter, your hands are cold."*

"Don't answer," Hazel breathed.

Then it used Sylvie's mother's voice, long dead. Then Richard's, wheedling. Then — and this was the clever, cruel part — it used Hazel's own voice, warm and reasonable, the voice she used with frightened families: *"You know how these cases end. Everyone dies anyway. Why exhaust yourself? Come down. Rest. You've earned rest."*

"Gentle humor," Hazel muttered through her teeth, "is how I cope, and you're ruining my coping mechanism."

*"Give me a name,"* said the dark, dropping all pretense, and the voice became a chorus of everyone who had ever whispered in this house, *"and I will let you carry your little book back up into the light. One name. A small price. Names are renewable."*

"We're not trading," Hazel said. "We're evicting."

The stairs ended. The cellar opened around them — and there it was, rising.

It had been climbing for hours and it was nearly done. It stood where the candle ring was, tall and folded like wet laundry hung to dry in a shape no body had ever made, and its face — it had made itself a face, assembled from fifty years of watching the Vane family through the lag in the glass — was almost kind. That was the horror of it. It had practiced kindness in the mirror until it got the timing right.

Behind it, the ring of candle stubs waited, blackened, patient, older than the ledger.

"Sylvie," Hazel said quietly. "The candles. Light everything. Don't stop lighting until I tell you."

"And you?"

"I'm going to read aloud. Apparently that's what I'm for." She set the ledger down in the center of the ring, weighted the inheritance papers on top of it — Sylvie had lifted them off Marcus's briefcase while he toasted himself, and Hazel could have kissed her — and struck a match.

The thing moved. Not fast. It never needed to be fast. It simply *arrived*, the way its reflection always arrived one second late, suddenly nearer, folding down toward her, its borrowed face filling her whole field of vision.

*"A name,"* it said, and now it wasn't asking. *"The fire needs feeding. All contracts close with a signature. Give me yours and the debt passes clean. Give me hers"—the face tilted toward the ceiling, toward the dying woman above—*"and she dies tonight, in pain, unclaimed, and I take the house anyway. There is no version of this where you leave without paying."*

Hazel held the match steady. Her hand shook. She let it shake. Rationalists were allowed to shake; they just weren't allowed to stop.

"Edith taught me the clause," she said. "'By my own breath and no other's, I withdraw what was offered, I unname what was welcomed, and I close the door from the inside.'"

She touched the flame to the corner of the ledger.

Fifty years of signatures caught at once, flame running through the pages like water finding its level, and the thing *screamed* — a sound with no lag at all, immediate, present, real, the first honest thing it had ever produced in this house.

*"A NAME!"* it roared, lunging, and the fire leapt up between them, and Hazel looked it in its practiced, stolen face and gave it what it demanded.

"Take mine," she said. "My name is Edith Vane."

The cellar went silent.

*"That is not your name."*

"It is tonight." Her voice didn't waver, and she was privately astonished by that. "You asked for a name in trade. You didn't ask for the truth. Those are different contracts, and you of all things should know the difference. My name — the name I give you, freely, in this ring, by this fire — is Edith Vane. Take it. Close the account. Let the dead woman keep her death."

*"You would spend her?"*

"No." And here Hazel leaned closer, into the heat, into the stink of burning ink, and said the truest sentence of her life: "I'm spending *you*. She taught me this clause herself. She wrote her name into the revocation. You're not taking her from me — I'm handing you *her goodbye*. Signed. Witnessed. Executed." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile, and she meant it to be. "Read the fine print. You people love fine print."

The ledger burned. The papers burned. The candle ring flared white, every stub igniting at once, and the thing in the cellar began to come apart the way fog comes apart — not destroyed, but *uninvited*, unwelcomed, forgotten by the house that had hosted it, its edges thinning even as it reached for her with hands made of everyone's reflections.

*"I will remember you,"* it hissed, smaller now, thinner, a voice at the bottom of a well.

"No," Hazel said. "You won't. That's rather the point."

The last page curled to ash. The dark folded inward like a closing eye.

And upstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, a clock that had been holding its breath since 1974 finally exhaled.

* * *

They came up the stairs at a dead run, because neither of them trusted the count of steps to behave, and burst into the kitchen at — Hazel checked the window — 4:51 in the morning. The sky over the fog was going grey at the edges, the color of a bruise healing.

Sylvie sat down hard on the floor and started laughing, the shaky, unhinged laughter of someone who has just committed arson against their own inheritance. "You told it your name was Edith."

"It seemed rude to give it mine. It hadn't earned mine."

"You lied to a demon."

"I declined to volunteer information to a contractor. There's a difference. Ask Marcus." She held out her hand and pulled Sylvie up. "Come on. We have a patient."

They took the stairs two at a time.

In the bedroom, dawn was pressing grey fingers against the curtains. Edith lay small and still against her pillows, and for one terrible heartbeat Hazel's nurse's heart seized — but no. The chest rose. Fell. Rose again. Slow, but hers. Unhurried. Unowned.

Edith's eyes opened. They found Hazel first, then Sylvie, then the smell of smoke on their clothes, and the old woman's mouth curved.

"You burned it," she whispered.

"In the ring," Hazel said. "With the clause. And I may have committed mild identity fraud in the process."

Edith laughed — a real laugh, thin and crackling, costing her something and worth every penny. "Atta girl." Her eyes drifted to the window, to the light coming up over the fog. "It's almost morning."

"Yes," Hazel said softly. She sat on the edge of the bed and took the papery hand in both of hers. "You're free to go whenever you like. No paperwork required. I checked."

Outside, the sun crested the hills, and the last mirror in the hallway — the one full of dark water — shivered, cleared, and reflected the corridor exactly as it was, in perfect, punctual, ordinary time.

Edith watched the light move across the ceiling. Her breathing slowed, easy now, unhurried, and her lips began to move.

Hazel leaned close.

She was counting.

Forwards.
