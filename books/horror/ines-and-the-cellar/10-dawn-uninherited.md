## 10. Dawn, Uninherited

The fire in the cellar had gone out on its own, the way a candle does when everyone has finally left the room.

Hazel climbed the stairs with soot on her knuckles and Sylvie's hand gripping her sleeve, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing left down there to speak about. The ledger was ash. The papers were ash. The candle ring was a ring of wax and nothing else, and the smell — that terrible smell of a room remembering being occupied — had faded into something as ordinary as cold stone and old potatoes. The cellar smelled like a cellar. It was almost disappointing.

At the top of the stairs, the door stood open. Not ajar. Open, wide and easy, the way doors stand when nobody has anything to hide behind them. The nails Sylvie remembered from childhood were still in the frame, rusted little heads of them, but they held nothing now. They were just nails.

"Is it supposed to look like that?" Sylvie whispered. "So... boring?"

"I don't know what it's supposed to look like," Hazel said. "But I've worked in a lot of houses, and this is what most cellars look like. Stairs, damp, a shelf of jam nobody will ever eat."

Sylvie made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been the tail end of six hours of terror finding its way out sideways. Hazel squeezed her hand and let it be both.

* * *

Edith Vane died at 6:14 in the morning, which is a good hour for it, if you must do such a thing at all.

Hazel had seen hundreds of deaths by then — the gasping ones, the fighting ones, the ones where the family crowded the doorway like spectators at a race they'd bet against. She had learned not to flatter herself that she could tell a peaceful death from a merely quiet one until it was over. But this one she knew. This one she felt in the room itself, the way you feel sun come through a window before you turn your head.

Edith's breathing had slowed through the small hours, long even swells of it, and somewhere around five she opened her eyes — really opened them, clear and brown and unclouded for the first time since Hazel had arrived — and looked at the ceiling as though reading something pleasant there.

"Nurse Amberdale," she said. Her voice was thin but steady. "You didn't tell it your name."

"No, ma'am."

"Clever girl." A pause. The old woman's fingers moved against the blanket, and Hazel realized, with a jolt, what they were doing. Counting. But forwards. *One. Two. Three.* Like a child counting stars. Like someone counting up toward something instead of down away from it.

"What are you counting, Mrs. Vane?"

"The minutes," Edith said, "until my husband comes to collect me himself. He always was late. Forty years of marriage and he never once arrived anywhere first." Her mouth curved. "I expect he'll be along directly. He'll want an explanation for the state of the finances."

"He'll get one," Hazel said. "It's all in the ashes."

That earned her a real laugh, small and crackling like a struck match, and then Edith closed her eyes and counted onward — *fourteen, fifteen, sixteen* — unhurried, unafraid, a woman walking toward a door she'd left open on purpose. At some point after six the counting stopped, and the breathing stopped a breath later, and the room did not go cold or strange or heavy. It simply went still, the way a house goes still when its last guest has been seen safely off the premises.

Hazel sat with her for a while. She straightened the blanket. She closed the window she'd opened at midnight because Edith had asked for air. She did the small, ordinary offices of her profession, and they were enough, and for the first time in nine days they were only enough — no more than that, nothing hiding underneath.

* * *

The mirrors were the strangest part, in the end. Stranger than the climbing, stranger than the dark water filling the last unbroken glass, stranger even than the thing's voice asking for a name — because the mirrors went back to being nothing at all.

Hazel tested every one of them that morning, methodically, the way she checked a patient's pupils. The hallway mirror, the one she'd taped a bedsheet over on her second night: she peeled the tape away and her reflection blinked when she blinked. Simultaneously. She raised her right hand; it raised its right hand. She stuck out her tongue at it, feeling slightly ridiculous, and it stuck out its tongue back with no discernible delay whatsoever.

"Well," she said aloud. "Good."

Her reflection said "Well. Good." at exactly the same moment, mouth moving in perfect sync, and it looked so thoroughly, boringly like a mirror that Hazel laughed — a wet, exhausted laugh, but a real one.

She found the others in the morning room, sitting in the wreckage of their inheritance like survivors of a shipwreck who had all hated each other's lifeboats. Marcus stood by the window with his phone pressed to his ear, saying phrases like *void ab initio* and *no, I'm telling you, the instrument was never validly executed* and *because the testatrix burned it, Gerald, that's why*. His copy of the papers was in his hand, creased and singed at one corner, legally worthless and visibly weighing more than any document should.

The eldest, Richard, sat with his head in his hands. He kept looking at his own fingers as if they might confess something. He had signed, Hazel remembered. He had lifted the pen at midnight with his mother's dying breath in the room, and the mirror had shattered, and whatever he'd thought he was signing for had climbed halfway up the cellar stairs before two women and a box of matches sent it back down forever. He would be a long time forgiving his hands. Hazel found she could not muster much sympathy, but she could manage patience, which was nearly the same thing and cost less.

And Sylvie sat beside her mother's empty chair, holding one of the broken shards of the vanity mirror, turning it over, checking it.

"It doesn't lag," she said when Hazel came in. "I've been watching it for an hour. It's just glass."

"Just glass," Hazel agreed.

"Was it ever anything else? I mean—" Sylvie turned the shard in the light. "Was the whole house ever just a house? Or did we imagine the rest?"

"You didn't imagine the locked linen room," Hazel said. "Or the nails in the cellar door. Or the way your brother's cologne couldn't cover the smell of him sweating through his suit when he read the fine print." She paused. "You didn't imagine your mother begging you not to sign."

Sylvie set the shard down gently, face-down, the way you'd close a book you weren't going to finish. "No," she said. "I suppose I didn't."

Marcus hung up his phone. "The estate is void," he announced, to no one and everyone. "The firm's founding partnership — the original articles, 1974 — they're entangled with it. If the invitation lapsed, the consideration underlying everything..." He stopped, because none of them were listening, and because he had perhaps just heard, for the first time, how it sounded. "There's nothing," he said quietly. "There's the house, and the house is worth nothing now, and there's each other."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Richard laughed — a short, cracked, awful laugh — and said, "Each other. God help us," and Sylvie said, "Someone has to," and somehow that was the beginning of it, though Hazel wouldn't stay to see what it became. Families, she knew, were like fractures: they healed crooked or they didn't heal, but either way the body went on using them.

* * *

She packed slowly, because there was no reason not to. Fog again outside, but thinner now, the polite daytime kind that burns off by ten. She washed the soot off her knuckles. She folded the bedsheet she'd taped over the hallway mirror and put it in her bag — habit, mostly, though some part of her thought a nurse could never have too many bedsheets.

On her way out she passed the cellar door, standing open on its plain wooden hinges, showing nothing but stairs going down into honest darkness. She stopped. She looked. There was a shelf of jam, as promised. There was a lightbulb on a pull-chain, dead, needing a new bulb like any lightbulb in any cellar in England. There was a smell of stone and dust and forty years of nothing in particular.

"Hazel Amberdale," she said to it, clearly, at conversational volume, just to see.

The stairs did nothing. The jam did nothing. Somewhere above her a pipe ticked as the heating came on, the most mundane sound in the world, and Hazel Underhill — no, Amberdale, she corrected herself, tired down to the marrow of her name — smiled at a cellar like an idiot and went to say goodbye.

Edith lay composed in her bed, hands folded, face smoothed into something between sleep and satisfaction. Hazel pulled the sheet up over her shoulders, the last small office, and stood a moment.

"Forwards," she said. "You counted forwards. In thirty years of nursing I never saw anyone do that."

The room, being only a room, offered no opinion.

* * *

Outside, the fog was lifting off the drive in long rags, and the three Vane children stood by their cars in a loose, unwilling triangle, arguing about who would arrange the funeral in the tone of people who had recently discovered that arguing was all they had left. Sylvie caught Hazel's eye over Richard's shoulder and mouthed *thank you*, and Hazel nodded, and that was the whole ceremony.

She walked out past the gate, past the fog-thinned yews, her bag over her shoulder, and she took stock of herself the way she did after every case. Grief: present, manageable, hers. Justice: achieved, technically illegal, entirely outside her jurisdiction, and she found she could live with that. Belief:

She turned it over on the long walk to the bus stop, testing it like a joint, checking its range of motion. She had seen a mirror lag. She had heard something climb stairs at a patient pace. She had watched a ring of candles burn a ledger down to nothing while she recited words an old woman taught her, and she had given away a name that wasn't hers and walked back out untouched.

"I don't believe it," she told the fog conversationally. "Any of it. Mirrors are glass, cellars are cellars, and dying people count backwards because that's what dying brains do."

The fog, being fog, agreed with her.

And that was the thing she carried out of Marrow House, lighter than any inheritance: she still didn't believe. She never would. But standing at the bus stop in the grey-gold light, she understood at last that belief had never been the price of admission. Attention was. Care was. You didn't have to believe in the dark to keep the lamp lit — you only had to keep lighting it anyway, patient as a hospice nurse, for whoever was counting in the room next door.

The bus came. She got on. Behind her, Marrow House stood empty and ordinary in the clearing mist, its windows reflecting nothing but morning —

— except that as the bus rounded the bend, Hazel could have sworn — could have *sworn* — that one upstairs window reflected the road a half-second before the road arrived.

She decided, firmly and forever, that it hadn't.

The bus drove on. Somewhere behind it, faint as a struck match, someone was counting: *one, two, three.*
