## 4. One Second Behind

The trouble began, as most troubles do, with something small enough to dismiss.

Wendell Halloran had lived in the caretaker's cottage behind Hollowmere School for eleven days, and in that time he had learned the town's rhythms the way one learns a piece of music: the groan of the foghorn off the reservoir at half past five, the creak of Mrs. Aldous's boots on the gravel path an hour before dawn, the way the light through the kitchen window turned the color of weak tea by mid-morning and stayed that color, in October, until it gave up altogether. He had learned to count the doors at dusk. Nineteen of them, always, in the cottage and its outbuildings and the cellar hatch he had not yet opened. He counted them because Mrs. Aldous had shown him how, her knuckles rapping each frame twice, her voice flat as a hymnal: *Count the doors, Mr. Halloran. Lock nothing else.*

He had laughed, the first night. He had not laughed since.

On the twelfth morning, standing at the bathroom sink with his razor halfway to his jaw, he raised his left hand to steady the mirror — an old habit, a small pressure against the glass to keep it from fogging — and saw, in the silvered rectangle, his own hand rise a half-second after his real one had already risen.

He stood very still.

The mirror caught up. His reflected hand arrived at the glass and pressed flat, and Wendell watched it do this the way one watches a dog perform a trick it should not know: with a smile that had no warmth in it anywhere.

*Condensation,* he told himself. *Old glass. Warped silvering. Houses like this settle; mirrors lie in old houses the way floors slope.* He finished shaving without looking up again, which required a certain inventive courage, and he dried his face on a towel that smelled of cedar and something faintly sweet beneath it, like fruit going over, and he went downstairs and made coffee and did not think about it, or rather he thought about nothing else, which amounts to the same thing.

* * *

The school that day was ordinary in the way Hollowmere was always ordinary, which is to say it was ordinary the way a pond is still — perfectly, deliberately, with things standing under the surface holding their breath. Nineteen students. Nineteen faces turned toward him when he wrote on the board. He had stopped writing near the windows; he had noticed in the first week how their eyes tracked not the chalk but the glass behind him, and how, when his hand crossed the frame of a window, nineteen small spines went rigid as piano wire.

He did not write near the windows. He was learning.

At lunch he sat in the empty classroom with his thermos and the register, running his pencil down the column of names. Twenty-six names. Nineteen desks. He had asked Mrs. Aldous about it on his second day and received an answer that was technically an answer: *Some years the class is smaller.* He had asked which years. She had looked at him with her pale, patient eyes, eyes like the backs of spoons, and said, *The ones that owe more,* and had gone back to oiling the hinges of the gymnasium doors, which never needed oiling, which she oiled every single day.

Wendell set the pencil down. Across the room, the tall windows threw their long gray parallelograms of light onto the floorboards, and in each one the schoolyard was reflected, upside down, the swing set hanging from the sky like a gallows in a country where the sky had learned to keep prisoners.

He thought: *If the mirror in my bathroom is lagging, what about these?*

He did not test them. He wants it noted, in whatever ledger such things are finally written in — and he would learn, later, that there was such a ledger, and that his name was already in it — he wants it noted that he did not test them. He was not a brave man that morning. He was a man who understood, in the wordless way the body understands a dropped glass before the ears do, that some knowledge, once taken, cannot be set back on the shelf.

* * *

By four o'clock, when the last of the students had gone — and he noticed, that day, how they left in strict order, never two abreast, never one lingering, a little procession of nineteen moving through the corridor like beads down a string — the fog had come in off the reservoir the way it did every evening, thick and gray and patient, folding the schoolhouse into itself until the windows showed nothing but white.

He drove home the short way, past Ines Varga's feed store. The lights were on. She was out front, hauling a sack of cracked corn into the doorway, and she looked up as his headlights swept past, and for a moment — only a moment — she raised one hand, as if to flag him down, as if to say something. Then her eyes went past him, to something behind the car, and her hand dropped, and she stepped backward into the store and shut the door.

Wendell looked in his rearview mirror.

The road behind him was empty. The road behind him in the mirror was empty a moment *later* — he could not have said how he knew, but he knew, the way you know a note is flat, the way you know a room has someone in it before you see them. The white fog in the mirror arrived after the white fog in the windshield. The mirror was running behind. The whole silvered world in that small rectangle was trailing reality like a coat caught on a nail.

He did not look again. He drove the rest of the way home with his eyes fixed on the road ahead, which is, when you think about it, the only direction a man can afford to look once he suspects the rest have been sold.

* * *

The cottage was cold. He lit the stove, put water on for tea, and stood in the kitchen doing the thing he had been avoiding all day: he raised his right hand, slowly, in front of the window.

The window was old, wavy glass, more mirror than window after dark, the kitchen reflected in it ghostly over the black yard. His real hand rose. The hand in the glass rose.

One beat behind. Two. The glass caught up, the way the bathroom mirror had, the way the rearview had, and he stood there with his arm in the air feeling like a conductor whose orchestra had gone deaf — everything arriving, everything correct, everything *late.*

*All right,* he said aloud, to the kitchen, to himself, to whatever was listening from the silver side of the world. *All right. It's the house. Old glass. Old silver. It's the house.*

But even as he said it, some cold clerk in the back of his mind was stamping the paperwork: *Denied.*

Because it was not the house. Because at half past seven, washing his hands before supper, he caught the lag in the brass face of the kettle — a dim, warped, thumb-sized mirror, and even there, even in that little distorted world of stretched metal, his reflection's hands came up a beat after his own. Because at eight, lighting the lamp in the front room, the flame's reflection in the glass chimney of the lamp hesitated — *fire*, now, fire itself arriving late, and fire does not care about silvering, fire does not care about old glass, fire is light, and light does not dawdle unless something is holding it back to take its measure first.

By nine o'clock, Wendell Halloran had conducted a full and terrible inventory, alone, moving through his own house like a man checking for burglars, and had found that every reflective surface in the cottage — the bathroom mirror, the windows, the kettle, the lamp chimney, the dark face of the tea in his cup, the polished brass of the door hinge, the sheen on the oilcloth table, even, when he held a match close enough, the wet of his own eyes — every one of them was running one second behind the world.

One second. Not more. Not less. As though something had taken a reading, established a margin, and was holding to it. As though the lag were not a malfunction but a *term.*

He sat down at the kitchen table with the lamp turned low and made himself think it through, because thinking was the only tool he had and it was, he was beginning to understand, a tool with a very short handle.

Mirrors did not lag. Reflections were not events; they were consequences, light arriving, physics keeping its appointments. A reflection that arrived late was not a reflection. It was a *reply.* Something was receiving the image of him and returning it, and the round trip now cost one second, and the something on the other end was — what? Slow? Careful? *Interested?*

He thought of Ines Varga, standing in the doorway of her store, hand half-raised, eyes going past him to the road behind. *When the mirrors start lagging, stop looking.* She had told him that on his third day in town, at the counter, among the sacks and the smell of grain and creosote, and she had said it the way people say things they have said many times to people who did not listen, with a flatness that was not indifference but armor. He had laughed. He remembered laughing — a polite, teacherly laugh, the laugh of a man humoring a local eccentric — and he remembered the way her face had closed after it, like a drawer pushed shut.

*Stop looking.* But how did you stop looking in a world that looked back? Every window was a mirror after dark. Every spoon, every coin, every still puddle in the yard. He could close his eyes; he could not close the world's.

And beneath the fear, in the cellar of himself where he kept the thoughts he did not take out in company, a colder question had begun to stir: *Why me? Why now?* He had been in Hollowmere eleven days. The lag had begun this morning. Something had started counting him the way he counted the doors — and had found, apparently, that he was one more than yesterday.

* * *

At ten minutes to eleven, he did the only thing left that felt like an action. He put on his coat, took the lamp, and went outside to count the doors.

The fog was down heavy, the kind of fog that does not drift but *stands*, that has weight, that presses on the shoulders like a hand meaning no harm yet, only checking. The lamp made a yellow room around him eight feet across, and beyond it the world simply ended. His boots crunched the gravel. His breath hung and did not disperse. Somewhere off to the left, the reservoir horn sounded, and the fog took the sound and kept it, giving back only a muffled suggestion, a rumor of a horn.

He started at the cottage front door, as Mrs. Aldous had taught him, knuckles rapping the frame twice — he did it without deciding to, his hand simply performing the ritual, and he noted with distant unease how naturally the gesture had taken root, how the town got into the body before it got into the mind.

One. The front door.

Two. The back door, off the kitchen.

Three. The cellar hatch, which he had never opened, which sat in the ground behind the woodshed like a closed eye.

Four. The woodshed door.

Five and six. The double doors of the old carriage bay, swollen shut since before the war, whoever's war that had been.

Seven. The door to the pump house.

Eight. The little door at the base of the chimney — he had never understood it, a door eighteen inches high, and Mrs. Aldous had rapped it twice on his first evening without comment, and he had not asked, and now he was glad he had not asked, because he suspected the answers in Hollowmere were worse than the silences.

Nine through fourteen. The six doors of the garden sheds, in their row, like teeth.

Fifteen. The school's back entrance, across the yard, which Mrs. Aldous had included in his count without explanation — *the cottage and its dependencies,* she had said, and the school's back door had been counted, and he had not argued, because by then he had already learned not to ask.

Sixteen through eighteen. The three doors of the school's side porch, stacked one above another in a way no architect would defend, a door at ground level, a door at the top of four steps that led nowhere, a door set into the wall above the second one, at head height, opening onto nothing, a door that had never been opened in living memory and which the ivy had long since claimed.

Nineteen. The school's front door, great and dark, its windows reflecting his lamp back at him in two pale squares.

He stood before it, breathing fog, and made himself look at those two pale squares, because he was tired of flinching, because a man can only avert his eyes so long before the averting becomes the living.

In the left-hand pane, his reflection stood where he stood, lamp in hand, fog crowding close.

In the right-hand pane, his reflection stood where he stood, lamp in hand, fog crowding close.

And then the right-hand reflection raised its lamp — and Wendell's own arm was still at his side.

It was not the lag. He knew the lag now; he knew its texture, its one-second courtesy, its bookkeeping precision. This was not a delay. This was a *departure.* The reflection moved when he did not, and it moved with intention, and it raised the lamp higher, higher, until the light in the glass was full upon its own face — and the face was his face, and the face was *smiling*, and Wendell Halloran was not smiling.

He dropped the lamp. It went out in the gravel with a small glass sound, and the dark came in like water, and he stood in it with his heart going like a fist on a door, and he did not look at the school's front windows again. He walked back to the cottage in the black fog counting his own footsteps because he had to count something, and the number he reached at his own threshold was a number he did not let himself examine.

Inside, he relit the lamp with hands that shook and then, gradually, did not. There is a steadiness that comes after terror has spent its first coin, a cold administrative calm, and in that calm Wendell did the last thing he would do that night.

He counted the doors again. All of them. In order. Rapping each frame twice, because his hand insisted, because the ritual had teeth in him now.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen. Seventeen. Eighteen. Nineteen.

Twenty.

He stood before the garden sheds, his knuckles still raised, his lamp high, and looked at the door at the end of the row.

Yesterday there had been six sheds. He had counted them every dusk for eleven nights, and every night there had been six, and six doors, and the number had been nineteen, and nineteen had been a kind of covenant, a small dry contract between him and the dark: *you keep your count, I'll keep mine.*

There were seven sheds now.

The seventh stood at the end of the row where the fog was thickest, and it was made of the same gray boards as the others, weathered the same, roofed the same, and it had a door with a latch, and the door was shut. It looked as though it had stood there for forty years. It looked as though it had been waiting for forty years, which is not the same thing, and Wendell understood, standing in the fog with his lamp trembling, that it was not the shed that was new.

It was his count that had been corrected.

Behind him, in the dark window of the kitchen, one second late, his reflection turned from the sheds and began, unhurried, to walk back toward the cottage door — and Wendell, who had not moved, watched it go, and heard, very faintly, from the silver side of the glass, a sound he would spend the rest of his life trying not to remember: a low, patient humming, in three-four time, like a song practiced by someone who already knew every verse.

Except one.

He slept that night with the lamp burning and his coat on and the brass poker across his knees, and in the morning he would go to his classroom, and nineteen faces would look up at him, and one of them — small, sharp-eyed Petra, with her chewed crayons and her careful silence — would look at him a moment too long, the way you look at a clock you suspect is running down. And she would know. He would see her know. The children always knew; that was the thing he had not yet learned about Hollowmere, that the children were not its innocents but its archivists, its reluctant experts, keeping the rules no adult would speak aloud.

But that was morning. For now there was only the fog, and the lamp, and the seventh shed standing at the end of its row with its door shut, and the one second of silence between the world and its reflection — the one second in which, Wendell now understood, everything that would happen to him was already being written down.
