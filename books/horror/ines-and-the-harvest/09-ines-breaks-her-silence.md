## 9. Ines Breaks Her Silence

The feed store smelled of oats and creosote and the particular cold that lived in Hollowmere's bones no matter what month it was. Ines Varga had learned to love that smell the way other people loved perfume — not because it was pleasant, but because it was hers, earned, the only inheritance her family's name still carried that didn't frighten her.

She was stacking seed bags when the bell above the door rang, and she knew before she turned around who it would be. She'd felt him coming for three days now, the way you feel weather in an old scar. The new teacher. The one whose reflection was already starting to lag.

"Mr. Halloran." She kept her back to him a moment longer than politeness required, pressing her palm flat against a bag of winter rye as if the grain could tell her something useful. "Store closes at six."

"It's four-thirty."

"Then you have time to buy something or say whatever it is you came to say."

She heard him move down the aisle, his boots soft on the sawdust-strewn floorboards. He stopped near the window — of course he did; they always gravitated to light without knowing why, like moths who hadn't yet learned what the glass was for — and when she finally turned, she caught him looking at himself in the pane. Not admiring. Checking. The way you check a wound.

"You've noticed," she said.

He started slightly. "Noticed what?"

"Don't do that." She came around the counter, wiping her hands on her apron. "Don't do the thing where you pretend you haven't seen it. I did that for two weeks before my October, and it nearly cost me everything. You don't have two weeks. You might not have two days."

* * *

His name was Tomas and he was eleven years older than her and he whistled while he worked, always off-key, always the same three bars of some song their mother had sung before the singing of songs became a thing Ines could not bear to hear.

Nine years ago she had been fourteen, and Mrs. Aldous had come to the house with a ledger under her arm and a look on her face like a woman delivering a bill she was ashamed of but would collect regardless. Ines remembered the kitchen table, the oilcloth with its pattern of faded lemons, the way her mother's hands went still over the bread dough. Her father had poured three glasses of the good whiskey and drunk all of them himself.

*Chosen,* they called it. As though it were an honor. As though the Gleaner were a college and the harvest a scholarship.

Her mother had sewn her a new dress for the assembly. That was the part Ines still could not make sense of, even now, even after everything: the dress. Blue, with white collar trim, because the taken were meant to look their best when they stepped through. Because the town wanted the Gleaner pleased. Because a pleased Gleaner meant rain in April and no blight on the apple orchards and full cribs through the lean months, and a town fed on the singing away of its children learns to call the singing a blessing.

Tomas had not made her a dress. Tomas had spent those last six days in his room with the door locked and the lamp burning past midnight, writing.

She hadn't known what he was writing then. She'd thought he was grieving in the way boys grieved — badly, silently, behind closed doors. Their parents let him be. Everyone let everyone be, in Hollowmere; grief here was a private country with strict borders, and crossing into someone else's uninvited was worse than rudeness. It was temptation. It was asking questions the town had agreed, generation upon generation, never to ask aloud.

On the night of the harvest assembly, the whole town sang. Three verses, every voice from seven years old to seventy, filling the gymnasium the way floodwater fills a cellar — everywhere at once, inescapable, warm-seeming until you understood you were drowning in it. Ines stood at the front in her blue dress. Mrs. Aldous held the ledger open. And the whole room sang the third verse, and on the fourth line of that verse — the line that was always mumbled, always swallowed, a mouthful of sound with no words anyone would own to — the entire town hummed instead. Two hundred throats making the shape of a secret.

Ines had stepped toward the gymnasium mirror. It had already been waiting for her; she could see herself inside it, standing a half-step ahead, dressed in the same blue, smiling a smile she wasn't making. Behind her reflection there had been a corridor of silver fog, and something moving in it, patient as erosion.

And then Tomas had begun to sing.

* * *

"I've told you the rules," Ines said now, in the feed store, nine years later, her hands steady on the counter because she had practiced this steadiness for nearly a decade. "Never sing along in assembly. Never answer your reflection. Never be last out of a room. You'll have heard them from the children by now."

"They won't explain them."

"No. They can't. Explaining is how it gets its hooks deeper — the Gleaner lives in the terms, Mr. Halloran, in the fine print, in the understanding of things. Every rule the children know is a scar someone else paid for. They give you the rules the way you'd hand a stranger a map of a minefield. You don't ask them where each mine came from."

Wendell Halloran sat down heavily on the stool by the grain scale. He looked, she thought, exactly like what he was: a man from outside who had come to Hollowmere believing a teaching post was a teaching post, believing fog was just fog, believing that towns were made of people rather than ledgers. His face had the particular hollowness of someone who had counted doors at dusk and gotten a different number than yesterday.

"The fourth line of the third verse," he said quietly. "At practice today. Nobody would sing it. Principal Voss mumbled through it so fast I thought maybe he'd forgotten the words. But the students—" Wendell's throat moved. "The students looked at me. All of them. Like they were checking whether I'd noticed."

"They were checking whether you'd noticed." Ines pulled the ledger of her own store across the counter — feed accounts, nothing more, numbers that meant only money — and touched it without opening it, a habit, a talisman against the other kind of bookkeeping. "Because whether you notice determines what happens next. A teacher who doesn't notice goes through the harvest like the children do. Quietly. On schedule. A teacher who notices—"

"Becomes a problem."

"Becomes *interesting.*" She let the word sit between them. "And the Gleaner has never once, in two hundred years, let anything interesting go."

Outside, the fog had thickened against the windows until the streetlights beyond were only smudges, soft orange wounds in gray wool. Ines watched Wendell watch the glass, and saw him flinch — small, quickly mastered — at his own dim shape hovering there. Lagging. Already lagging. She wondered how far behind him it was now. Half a second? A full one? By the end it would be seconds, then minutes, then the reflection would simply stop pretending and start living its own life on the other side of the silver, learning his face the way a thief learns a lock.

"Tomas," Wendell said. "Your brother. Ines Varga — you're related to Tomas Varga?"

The name hit her the way it always did, low in the chest, below the ribs, where she kept everything she couldn't afford to carry higher up where it might show on her face.

"You know the name."

"There's a memorial plaque in the school stairwell. 1987. No cause listed. Just his name and the dates." Wendell hesitated. "I asked Mrs. Aldous about him. She said he was 'a boy who asked too many questions.'"

Ines laughed. It came out wrong — too sharp, too short, a bark with nothing alive in it — and she pressed her knuckles against her mouth until it stopped.

"A boy who asked too many questions," she repeated. "Yes. That's what they'd say. That's what they'd carve in brass, because brass is small and the truth isn't." She untied her apron with fingers that wanted very much to shake, and folded it, and set it down, and made herself meet Wendell Halloran's eyes. "You want to know what happened nine years ago. Fine. But you're going to stand there and hear all of it, and you're not going to interrupt me, and when I'm done you're going to understand that whatever plan is forming behind your eyes right now — and something is forming, I can see it, teachers are all the same, you think you can fix things — you need to hear how badly the last man who tried it failed."

* * *

Tomas had sung the fourth line of the third verse.

That was the whole of it, and it was everything. While the town hummed its coward's note, while Ines's hand reached for the mirror glass, her brother stood up in the back row of the assembly — sixteen years old, gangly, his shirttail out, his voice cracking the way it always cracked — and he sang the forbidden line loud and clear and complete.

He had learned it from his notebook. From weeks of digging through the school basement archives, through the old hymnals with pages torn out, through the personal papers of a music teacher from 1931 who had gone quiet one October and left behind a transcription hidden in a piano bench. The harvest song had a complete form. Four verses, not three. And the fourth line of the third verse — the mumbled one, the swallowed one — was not a corruption or a forgetting. It was a lock. Sung correctly, in full, by someone outside the glass, it broke the Gleaner's claim on the chosen. It pried the door open.

It worked.

Ines remembered the moment with a clarity that had never dulled and never softened: the corridor of fog recoiling like a hand snatched back from a stove. Her reflection stumbling, catching up to her, becoming merely a reflection again. The mirror going ordinary — just glass, just a gymnasium, just her own frightened face. The song dying in two hundred throats all at once, the silence afterward so total she could hear the furnace ticking in the walls.

And then the Gleaner, bound by its terms, patient as erosion, hungry as winter, doing what any creditor does when one account closes.

It opened another.

Tomas was still standing. Still singing. The last note of the true line still hanging in the air of the gymnasium, and Ines screaming his name, and the mirror beside the trophy case — not her mirror, a different one, there had always been more than one, there had always been mirrors enough — rippling outward like the surface of a struck pond. And her brother turning, at the last, not afraid, which was the worst of it, which was the thing she carried hardest: he had turned and looked at her and he had been *glad*. Glad the way you're glad when the fever breaks in someone you love, even if it breaks in you instead.

Then the glass took him, and the surface stilled, and the town of Hollowmere stood in the wreckage of its own silence and began, within the hour, to sing the closing benediction as though nothing had happened, because the harvest had been paid after all. One name substituted for another. The ledger balanced. The accounts current.

Her parents had not spoken to her for a year. Not from anger — from the unbearable arithmetic of it. Every meal she ate was a meal Tomas's death had purchased. Every morning she woke in her own bed was a morning bought with his. They looked at her and saw the price of their daughter, and some debts are too large to look at directly, so they didn't.

Mrs. Aldous had come to the house a week after. Had set salt and a small brass bell on the kitchen table — the same gifts, Ines would learn much later, that she gave to every marked soul — and had said, in her dry paper voice: *The town regrets the irregularity. The accounts will be reconciled in due course.* As though her brother were a misprinted invoice. As though grief were a clerical matter awaiting the next fiscal quarter.

Ines had left Hollowmere at seventeen. She had gotten as far as the county line, twice, and both times the road had bent beneath her tires like a river bending around a stone, and she had found herself driving back past the school with no memory of taking the turn. The third time she hadn't tried. She had opened the feed store instead, because a person must live somewhere, and because staying was its own kind of sentence and she had already served enough of those.

But she had gone to her brother's room. Once a year, every year, on the anniversary. She dusted it. She kept the lamp oiled. And she never, ever opened the desk drawer where the notebook lay, because she had read enough of it at sixteen to know what was inside, and knowing it existed was a weight she could carry, and reading it again was a weight that had once come close to crushing her.

* * *

"So the verse works," Wendell said. His voice was rough. His hands were flat on his knees, gripping them, and Ines recognized the posture — a man holding himself in place by force. "Sung right, from outside, it frees whoever's been taken."

"It freed me. Yes."

"And it took Tomas instead."

"The Gleaner doesn't forgive a debt, Mr. Halloran. It transfers it. That's the cruelty built into the terms — the same terms that keep the town fed, that keep the apples sweet, that keep the fog off the wheat. Everything in Hollowmere runs on balance. Nothing runs on mercy." She came out from behind the counter at last, because the distance between them had become a lie, and some things needed saying close. "For nine years I have told myself that Tomas knew. That he chose it. That a boy who spent six days transcribing a forbidden verse from a dead woman's papers knew exactly what singing it would cost, and sang it anyway, because I was worth more to him than his own life. And maybe that's true. Maybe. But I was fourteen, and I got to live, and he got to spend nine years — ten Octobers now — on the other side of the glass, and I have never once known if he's still in there. If they're all still in there. If being taken means dying slowly or just... waiting."

"Have you ever tried to find out?"

"How?" The word tore out of her sharper than she intended. "Hold a mirror up and knock? Ask the thing that ate my brother for visitation rights?" She turned away, pressed her forehead briefly against the cold shelf of the seed cabinet, feeling the frost of it bite her skin. When she spoke again her voice was lower. "There's a hand mirror in my brother's room. His. He used it for — I don't know what. Experiments. Notes in the margins about 'contact.' I've never been able to bring myself to use it. Some doors you don't knock on unless you're ready for what answers."

Wendell was quiet for a long moment. The fog pressed against the windows. Somewhere down the street, a dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

"The notebook," he said finally. "The complete verse. Does it still exist?"

Ines closed her eyes.

She had known this question was coming from the moment he walked in — had felt it gathering in him like thunderheads, had seen it in the way he checked his reflection in the window with the desperate diligence of a man taking his own pulse. He was marked. He was entered in the ledger. Whatever count the town's books held against this year, his name was in it now, written in handwriting almost his own, and the harvest was weeks away at most.

Every instinct she had honed across nine years of survival said: *tell him nothing, give him nothing, let him run blind like everyone else runs, because hope is the hook the Gleaner sets deepest.*

But Tomas had not died of hope. Tomas had died of love, and love was the only currency in Hollowmere that had ever bought anything worth having.

"It exists," she said. "Water-damaged. The final lines are legible only if you hold a lamp at an angle, low, so the light rakes across the page. Tomas figured that out. He wrote that down too — he wrote everything down, he was that kind of boy, he trusted paper more than people." She opened her eyes and looked at Wendell Halloran, at the lagging ghost of him faintly visible in the dark window behind his shoulder, moving a half-beat late, mouthing something she could not hear and did not want to. "My brother's room has been locked since the night they took him. I have the key. I have never used it."

"And now?"

Ines reached beneath the counter, into the drawer where she kept the things that were not for sale — the salt, the spare key, the photograph of a boy mid-laugh with his shirttail out — and lifted out a small iron key gone brown with disuse. It was warm from the wood. Or perhaps, she thought, it had never once cooled in nine years. Perhaps some keys remember the doors they're meant to open.

"Now," she said, "you're going to help me close the store early. And then we're going to walk to my house, and I'm going to unlock my brother's door for the first time since the glass took him. And you're going to hold the lamp at the angle he described, and we're going to read what he died to leave us." She set the key in his palm and closed his fingers over it, because she could not carry it across that threshold herself; she knew it, and she suspected he did too. "And Mr. Halloran — when the lights start dimming while we read, when they dim in rhythm with the words — you will not stop. No matter what you feel watching you from the dark of the room. No matter whose breathing you hear that shouldn't be there. We read to the end, or we don't read at all."

She was already reaching for the lamp oil when she saw his face change — saw the color leave it, saw his hand drift up, unconsciously, toward the window and the lagging thing that wore his shape there.

"Ines," he whispered. "It's never done that before."

Behind the glass, his reflection had stopped mimicking him entirely. It stood perfectly still, hands at its sides, and it was smiling.

And it was mouthing, over and over, silent and patient and glad:

*Hurry.*
