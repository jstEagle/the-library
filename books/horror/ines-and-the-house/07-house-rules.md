## 7. House Rules

The tea was the color of strong regret and tasted faintly of cloves, and Corinne Vale watched me drink it the way a customs officer watches you open your suitcase.

"Good," she said, when I'd swallowed. Not a question. A verdict.

"It's lovely," I said, which was sixty percent true and one hundred percent diplomatic. My mother raised me to lie about tea. It's the only inheritance I've ever spent wisely.

We were back in the kitchen — the kitchen being, as far as I could tell, the only room in Wintermoor House that had agreed to stay where I put it. The morning light came through the window over the sink and did nothing whatsoever to warm the room. Outside, the fog had settled in against the glass like a cat that had decided this was its window now.

Corinne set down her own cup, untouched, and folded her hands.

"Before you go up to check your traps," she said, "there are some things you should know."

Here we go, said the part of my brain that had done this job for eleven years. Here comes the bit where the client tells you the house is haunted and then gets offended when you don't immediately agree.

What she actually said was: "There are rules."

I got out my logbook. This is a habit my first supervisor beat into me — *if it isn't written down, Pemberton, it didn't happen* — and I have never once regretted it, except possibly now, because writing down what Corinne Vale said next made it real in a way that merely hearing it had not.

"Rule one," she said. "If anything speaks to you after midnight, you do not answer. Not a word. Not a cough. If you must make noise, hum."

"Hum," I repeated.

"Something cheerful. It doesn't like cheerful."

I wrote that down. *Doesn't like cheerful.* Underneath it, out of pure professional reflex, I wrote *neither do I before coffee,* and then felt guilty about it, though I couldn't have told you why.

"Rule two." Her voice didn't change at all. That was the thing about Corinne Vale — she delivered everything in the same level tone, whether it was an instruction about tea or something that would have gotten a lesser woman sectioned. "The sixth door stays closed. You'll know it when you see it. Don't touch the handle. Don't knock on it. Don't stand near it longer than you need to."

"How will I know it?"

"You'll know it," she said again, and something moved behind her eyes that I chose, generously, to interpret as tiredness rather than fear.

"And rule three?"

"Ines eats first."

I looked up from my book. "Ines eats first."

"At every meal. Whatever's served, whatever's cooked, Ines's portion goes down first. Even if she isn't there. Especially if she isn't there." She said it the way you'd say *the bins go out on Tuesday* — a fact so worn smooth by repetition that all the sharp edges of it had long since rubbed away. "You'll be working here during meal times. You'll wait."

I am not a proud man about food. I once ate a service-station pasty of genuinely criminal provenance because it was there. But there was something in the way she said *you'll wait* that made my stomach do a small, cold somersault.

"Mrs. Vale," I said, carefully, "in my experience, when a household has rules like these, there's usually a reason. And usually the reason has teeth."

For the first time that morning, Corinne smiled. It was a small smile, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes, and it made me like her less, somehow, than no smile at all would have.

"Yes," she said. "It does."

* * *

Tobias was in the hallway when I left the kitchen, sitting on the bottom stair with his knees drawn up, doing an extremely committed impression of furniture. He was maybe fifteen, all elbows and shadow, with the particular stillness of a boy who learned young that quiet boys get left alone.

"Tobias," Corinne called from behind me, in a voice that was pleasant the way a locked door is pleasant. "Go and help Mr. Pemberton."

"I'm fine on my own," I said, because I was, and also because I wanted to see what the boy would do.

He didn't move. Corinne appeared at my shoulder — she moved quietly for a woman her size; everyone in this house moved quietly, which struck me, even then, as odd for a place with scratching in the walls — and touched him once, lightly, on the head. Like you'd steady a lamp.

"Help Mr. Pemberton," she said again, and Tobias stood up as if someone had cut his strings upward instead of letting them drop.

So the two of us went upstairs together, the pest controller and the silent son, and for three floors he said nothing at all, which was fine by me. I talk enough for any job. I narrate my work partly for the clients' benefit and partly because houses listen better than people do, and if you say what you're doing out loud, you notice faster when something disagrees.

"There's bait stations along this wall," I told him, crouching by the skirting board on the second-floor landing. "Little black boxes. Poison inside, but it's locked up safe — childproof, dog-proof, Tobias-proof. If you find one knocked over, don't touch it, just come get me. And these—" I patted the small grey boxes I'd mounted at intervals along the corridor, each with its little red light breathing slow and patient, "—are recorders. They listen while you sleep."

Tobias looked at them. Then, very quietly, he looked at me.

"Why?"

"Because whatever's in your walls makes noise at night, and I want to hear it. Noise tells you what an animal is. Where it goes. What it wants." I clicked the last recorder into place and sat back on my heels. "Everything that lives leaves a pattern. Rats run walls to floorboards to pipes. Mice are messier. Squirrels are anarchists. You learn to read it."

"What if it's not an animal?"

I turned around. He hadn't come closer. He was standing with his back against the opposite wall, arms wrapped around himself, and up close I could see he had his mother's exact coloring and none of her composure. His hands were shaking.

"Well," I said, "then the recordings will tell me that too."

"They won't let you hear it," he whispered.

"*They?*"

"The rules." His eyes flicked toward the ceiling, then the stairs, then — briefly, terribly — toward a door at the end of the corridor. White paint, brass handle, perfectly ordinary. Except that every other door on this landing stood open onto morning-lit bedrooms, and this one was shut, and the air around it had the quality of held breath. "Mum checks the recordings. She checks everything. You can't—"

"Tobias!" Corinne's voice floated up the stairwell, warm as a hot water bottle and about as deep. "Mr. Pemberton needs his tea refreshed!"

The change in the boy was instant and complete. One moment he was a person telling me something; the next he was a boy again, blank-faced, hands still, and he said, in a flat little voice, "I have to go," and went.

I stayed crouched by the skirting board for a good minute after his footsteps faded downstairs.

Then I took out my logbook and wrote: *Son tried to tell me something. Mother interrupted. Door at end of second-floor corridor — count later.*

And underneath that, because my hand seemed to want to: *Count later. Count twice.*

* * *

I did count. Of course I counted. Counting doors is not a quirk, whatever my ex used to say about it; it's the whole job. A house is a sum, and every door is a number in it, and when the numbers stop adding up, something is either wrong with the house or wrong with you, and I have learned to bet on the house.

Ground floor: front door, back door, cellar door, sitting room, dining room, cupboard under the stairs. Six interior doors plus two exterior. Fine. Normal.

First floor: four bedroom doors, one bathroom, one linen cupboard. Six. Fine.

Second floor: three bedroom doors, bathroom, airing cupboard. Five.

Plus the white door at the end of the corridor.

Six.

Except when I walked the corridor a second time, slowly, counting under my breath like a madwoman, I got five doors and the white one, and then on the third pass I counted six doors *including* the white one, and one of the bedrooms had gone missing somewhere between passes, and I stood in that corridor with my heart going like a woodpecker and thought: *right, that's enough of that.*

Houses settle. Houses shift. Old houses especially — foundations move, plaster moves, and a tired mind on its third cup of clove tea will absolutely miscount. There is always a boring explanation. I have built an entire career on finding the boring explanation, and I clung to it now the way a drowning man clings to a door that may or may not be one of the six.

I wrote it all down anyway. Times, counts, both versions. If it isn't written down, it didn't happen — and if it happened twice, differently, well. Then it really didn't happen, and I'd deal with that later, preferably from a motel with a deadbolt.

* * *

Lunch was soup. Corinne set three bowls on the scrubbed pine table — hers, mine, and a third, smaller bowl at the head of the table, which she filled first, from the pot, before anyone else's.

"Ines isn't joining us?" I asked.

"She takes meals in her room," Corinne said.

"But her bowl comes down here first."

"Her portion is served first," Corinne corrected, with the mild precision of a woman who had been making this distinction for years and would be making it until she died. "She eats first. Always."

I looked at the small bowl, steaming gently at the empty place, and I looked at Corinne, and I made a decision that eleven years of professionalism told me was correct and eleven years of being a person told me was insane.

"May I?" I said, and carried the bowl to the foot of the stairs.

Her door was the padlocked one — third floor, end of the hall, heavy padlock hanging off a hasp screwed into wood that looked, I noticed now, oddly new. Screws bright. Hasp unworn. Nine years old, that lock, if the story was nine years old, and it looked like it had been hung last month.

I set the bowl down on the mat outside the door.

"Ines?" I said. "Your mum sent lunch."

Nothing. No sound from inside at all — no movement, no breathing, nothing. A room that empty should echo. This one absorbed.

I stood there a moment longer than I needed to, and I will be honest with you, whoever ends up reading this log: I wanted to knock. My knuckles actually lifted. Something about that silence pulled at me like a rip current, and I think — I *think* — if I'd knocked, I'd have heard something answer.

But rule one was rattling around in my head, and it wasn't even midnight, and besides, Corinne was watching from the bottom of the stairs with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

"Thank you," said Corinne, when I came back down.

"For carrying a bowl?"

"For following the rules." She ladled my soup — finally — and pushed the bread board toward me, and for a moment, over the steam, her face did something complicated. "People who follow the rules do well in this house."

"And people who don't?"

The ladle stopped. Just for a second. A pause you could have balanced a coin on.

"We've never had one," she said.

* * *

I spent the afternoon in the walls, or as close to the walls as a man of my build can reasonably get. Pulled the kick panel behind the boiler in the utility room. Found the grease trail along the pipe run in the pantry — classic rodent highway, textbook, the most reassuring forty minutes of my entire visit. Set two more stations. Logged everything. Normal work. Honest work. I held onto it like a handrail.

The gnawed wood I found under the stairs at half four was not normal work.

At first glance: mouse damage, maybe squirrel, chewed through the corner of an old doorframe panel. At second glance, crouched with my torch: wrong. The tooth marks were too fine, too regular, like stitching. And the material itself — I pressed my thumb into it and it gave, softly, the way skin gives, and it was warm, warmer than the surrounding frame, and when I drew my thumb back there was the faintest flush of pink fading out of the dent I'd made, like blood coming back to squeezed flesh.

Fibrous. Warm. Faintly pulsing, if I held my breath and my torch steady and believed my own eyes, which I did not particularly want to.

I photographed it. I sampled it into a bag. I wrote *sample 4 — anomalous — send to lab* in my logbook, and my handwriting, I noticed, had gone slightly uphill.

That was when Tobias spoke to me again.

He was standing at the mouth of the understairs cupboard, holding the door open, and I hadn't heard him approach, which at this point in the day felt less like rudeness and more like a housekeeping decision made on my behalf.

"Did it hurt you?" he whispered.

"It's wood, son. Wood can't hurt me."

"It's not wood." He said it simply, without drama, the way you'd tell someone they'd dropped their glove. "None of it is. Not anymore."

I looked at the sample bag in my hand. Warm through the plastic. I could feel it against my palm like a small animal's heartbeat slowed way, way down.

"Tobias," I said, keeping my voice easy, keeping the handrail grip on *boring explanation, boring explanation*, "how long has your family lived here?"

"All my life."

"And how long has the house been like this?"

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough that I turned around properly to look at him. He was staring at the sample bag, or maybe through it, and his face had gone the color of the fog outside.

"Nine years," he said.

"Something happened nine years ago?"

His mouth opened. Closed. Somewhere above us — third floor, I'd have sworn it, directly overhead — something shifted its weight, slow and deliberate, the way a person shifts in a chair when a conversation has gotten interesting.

Tobias flinched. His eyes went up, then to the stairs, then to me, and when he finally spoke it was barely a sound at all, a thread of a whisper pitched under the hum of the boiler, meant for me and no one else, and it landed in the middle of my chest and stayed there:

"My sister died nine years ago."

The boiler ticked. The sample bag pulsed, once, faintly, against my palm.

"So who," whispered Tobias, "do you think has been eating first?"
