## 14. Negotiation

The kitchen had stopped pretending to be a kitchen.

Hazel had noticed this about an hour after the eleventh door appeared — that the room's furniture was less furniture than suggestion, the way a stage set is a kitchen if you agree not to look too hard at the stove. The stove was warm. The stove had always been warm. Hazel had decided, as a matter of professional policy, not to think about what was warming it.

The recorder sat in the middle of the table. Hazel sat across from it, hands wrapped around a mug of tea that Corinne had made before retreating upstairs, tea that Hazel had — against all training, against every instinct that had kept them alive in this trade for eleven years — drunk. It was good tea. That was somehow the worst part. The house wanted Hazel calm. The house wanted Hazel *seated*.

"Right," Hazel said aloud, to the empty room, in the tone they used for difficult clients and wasps' nests. "Let's get this on the record properly, shall we."

They pressed record. The little red light blinked. Somewhere above them, a floorboard shifted its weight like a large animal resettling.

"My name is Hazel Winterbourne. I'm a pest control technician, license number 4471-C, though I suspect the licensing board would have opinions about what I'm doing tonight. It is—" they checked their phone, which showed a time and no signal and, when tilted, a faint reflection of a hallway that was not behind them, "—some hour of the night. I'm sitting in a kitchen that has too many doors, with a recording device, and I'm going to ask some questions. If anyone's listening — and I know you're listening, you've been listening since I parked the van, you've probably listened to me sing in the shower through the motel wall, which, for the record, I'm not embarrassed about, everyone sounds better with echo — then I'd like to have a conversation. Not an interview. A conversation. Those are different. An interview is me asking and you answering and neither of us learning anything."

Hazel paused. The house made a sound like a house settling, if houses could settle *nervously*.

"First question. And I want you to think about it, because I've been thinking about it since I heard the tapes." Hazel leaned forward, forearms on the table, close to the recorder the way you'd lean close to a shy animal at a shelter. "What do you want?"

Silence. The fridge hummed. The fridge had been humming a note that Hazel was almost certain was not a note fridges could hum — something between B and grief — and now it hummed louder, as though clearing its throat.

Then the recorder's speaker crackled, though Hazel hadn't touched playback, and the voice came out of it.

It was Ines's voice. It was also, unmistakably, not only Ines's voice. It was Ines's voice the way a chord is a note — a child's register at the center, and around it, layered underneath like strata in cut earth, other voices. Older ones. A woman's. A man's. Something that might have been very old indeed, something with the texture of a page that had been turned by many hands.

"A verdict," the voices said, together and slightly out of phase, so the word arrived three times, like a knock repeated at three different doors.

Hazel's stomach performed a slow, professional roll. They kept their voice level. "A verdict on what?"

"On her."

"Corinne."

"Yes."

Hazel sat back. Outside the window — the sealed window, the one with the small deliberate fingerprints on the inside of the glass — the dark pressed in with the patience of something that had been pressing for a very long time.

"Let me make sure I understand," Hazel said. "She confesses to you every night. Nine years. I've heard the tapes. She tells you things she's never told another living soul. She's given you — what, three thousand nights of the truth? And that's not enough?"

The layered voice was quiet for a moment. When it came again, the child-voice at its center was more distinct, as though the other voices had stepped back to let her speak.

"It is enough," Ines said. "That is the problem."

"Explain that to me."

"She pours it out and it goes into the house and the house keeps it, and in the morning she is exactly as full as she was before. It is like—" a pause; the child was searching for the word, and Hazel had the strange, awful sense of a twelve-year-old's vocabulary being asked to carry a concept that predated vocabulary. "It is like bailing a boat with a hole in it. She confesses and it runs out of her and pools in the floorboards and by nightfall she has filled back up with the same lie and she tips it out again and calls it penance."

"Because she's never *finished*," Hazel said slowly. "She's never said the whole thing. The actual — the sentence."

"The sentence," the voices agreed, and this time the man's voice underneath said it too, and the woman's, and the old one, and for a moment Hazel heard what the house heard: not one verdict wanted but a chorus of verdicts wanted, a whole architecture of unfinished judgments, the Vales only the most recent tenants of a debt that had been circulating for longer than the house had stood.

"Nine years," Hazel said. "Every night. And no one has ever asked you—" they stopped, and restarted, because the question had assembled itself in their mouth and they wanted to say it exactly right. "Has anyone, in all this time, ever asked you what happens *after*? After the verdict. After she says it. What happens to you?"

The silence that followed was different in kind from the other silences. Hazel had spent the last several days learning this house's silences the way you learn a dog's moods — the silence of watching, the silence of hunger, the silence of something deciding. This one was none of those. This one was the silence of a question being asked for the first time in nine years, landing in a room where no one had ever thought to ask it.

"No," Ines said. Just the child's voice now, small and precise. "No one has asked."

"Not Corinne."

"Corinne asks me to wait. Corinne asks me to be patient. Corinne asks me, every night, *what do you want from me*, which is not the same question. It is a question about her."

"And Tobias?"

"Tobias asks me to leave him alone." A pause, and something almost — almost — like humor moved through the layered voices, dry as dust in a wall cavity. "He is the most sensible one in this house."

Hazel huffed out a laugh before they could stop themselves, and the house seemed to accept it, the way a tense meeting accepts the first joke. They took a sip of the tea. It was still warm. Everything in this house was still warm; that was going in the report, if there was ever a report, if there was ever a world with paperwork in it again.

"Okay," Hazel said. "Then let's do this properly. I'm asking. What happens after? She says the sentence — and I think we both know what the sentence is, don't we, I think you've been waiting nine years to hear it in her voice and not mine — and then what? What are the terms? Because I'll be honest with you, Ines, and I'd rather be honest than safe in this particular room: I've read enough about bargains to know the terms matter more than the promise. And I've read enough about grief to know that sometimes the thing doing the haunting doesn't actually want the punishment. It wants it to be *over*. Those look identical from the outside and they are not the same thing at all."

The recorder's red light blinked. The fridge hummed its grief-note. Somewhere upstairs, a door that was not a door opened onto a room that was not a room, and closed again, politely, like someone checking whether the adults were still awake.

"I do not know," Ines said at last.

"You don't know, or you're not allowed to say?"

"I do not know. No one has ever gotten as far as after." The child's voice frayed slightly, and underneath it the older voices stirred like sediment. "I was made from a rite and a room and a girl who was not let out. I know what I am owed. I do not know what I am *for*. Those are different questions and I have only ever been asked the first one."

Hazel sat with that. Outside, the dark leaned on the glass. Inside, Hazel leaned on the table, and for a moment the two pressures balanced, and the kitchen felt almost like a kitchen, almost like a place where two people might sit across from each other and work something out.

"Then here's my proposal," Hazel said. "Advocacy. That's the word for it — I looked it up once for a court case I had to testify in, the Kessler place, the one with the — never mind, irrelevant. The point is: an advocate doesn't decide for you. An advocate makes sure the terms are fair before anyone signs. So. You want a verdict. Fine. I think — and I say this as a professional who has crawled under a lot of floors and found a lot of things people hoped stayed hidden — I think you're owed it. What she did, what she's been not-saying for nine years while feeding you the edges of it every night like she's paying off a debt in installments she chose the size of — you're owed the whole sentence, spoken, out loud, by her, unprompted. I'm not arguing that."

"Then what are you arguing?"

"I'm arguing about the *after*. Because I found the tally marks, Ines. In the closet, in the real room. Hundreds of them. She was counting days in the dark, that girl, the actual girl, before—" Hazel's voice did something they weren't proud of, and they let it, because this seemed like a room where you were allowed to let your voice do things. "Before whatever happened to her happened. And I don't think a girl who counts days in the dark wants a punishment. I think she wants to be *done*. I think she wants to put the pencil down. And I think you — whatever you are, whatever the rite made you out of her and the room and nine years of Corinne's half-confessions — I think you've been wearing her shape so long you've started to want what she would have wanted, and you can't tell the difference anymore, and nobody's ever asked, so you've never had to."

The layered voices were silent for a long time. When they came back, the strata had shifted — the child's voice on top now, the others far down, like voices heard through a floor.

"You are strange," Ines said. "The others who came — the surveyor, the priest Ada sent for, the man with the dogs — they all wanted to know how to make me stop. You are the first who wants to know how to make me *finish*."

"There's a difference?"

"There is all the difference. Stopping is what happens to a machine. Finishing is what happens to a story." A pause. "I would like to finish."

"Then we negotiate terms." Hazel pulled their logbook across the table — the meticulous one, the one with the door counts, five outside six inside, then six inside eleven inside, columns of numbers that had stopped describing a building and started describing a mood. They clicked a pen. "Term one. Corinne speaks the sentence. Fully. In her own words, not words fed to her, not words she's rehearsed into ritual. Unprompted. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said the voices, all of them, and the fridge hummed a fraction flatter, and Hazel chose to believe that was a signature.

"Term two. When it's said — when it's truly said, the whole of it, no installments — you release this house. The doors go back to being doors, the walls go back to being walls, the family walks out into the morning. All of them. Even her."

A longer pause. The dark at the window seemed to gather, the way birds gather on a wire before deciding.

"Even her," Ines repeated. Not agreement. Testing the weight of it.

"Even her. That's what a verdict *is*, Ines. It's not you getting what you feel. It's what's *owed* — measured by someone who wasn't burned. And what's owed to her, for nine years of feeding you and nine years of being eaten, is a chance to walk out of this house and into a courtroom with actual humans in it and say it all again where it counts. You get the truth. The world gets her. That's the trade."

"And what do you get, Hazel Winterbourne, license 4471-C?" The child's voice had an edge of something wry in it now, and Hazel was struck — not for the first time — by how *twelve* she could sound when she wanted to, how the horror of her was always in danger of being interrupted by the girl in it.

"I get to leave before sunrise with everyone's pulse still going," Hazel said. "And I get the answer to my question. What happens to *you*, after. Not because I'm negotiating for it. Because somebody should know. Somebody should carry it out of this house so it happened to a witness and not just to you."

The layered voices went very quiet. The fridge stopped humming altogether, and in the new silence Hazel could hear the house itself listening — the walls, the multiplying doors, the warm fibrous not-wood in the floor joists, all of it leaning in.

"If she says it," Ines said, "and it is whole, and it is hers — then I think I stop being a verdict waiting to happen. I think I become what's left when a debt is paid." A pause, and the smallest voice Hazel had heard from her yet: "I think I get to be finished. And I do not know what finished feels like. I would like to find out."

Hazel wrote it down. *Terms agreed, contingent on full confession, unprompted. Entity released upon completion. Witness to remain for the after.* Their hand shook slightly on the last word, and they let it.

"One problem," Hazel said, capping the pen. "I've got the terms. You've agreed. But I'm missing something — the fine print. This whole arrangement started with a rite. Somebody wrote it down. Rites like this, in my experience, always come with fine print, and the fine print is always worse than the brochure."

The voices stirred again, and this time it was the oldest one, deep down, that answered — a voice like a cellar, like paper gone soft with damp:

"Merrick," it said. "Ada Merrick kept the book. Ada Merrick kept the terms. What the book says the book says, and no rite has ever been run to its end, so no one living knows what the end says."

"Where's the book?"

"Where the house keeps what it was built on," said the voices, together, and every door in the kitchen — all eleven, all patient, all warm — turned its handle a quarter-inch at the same moment, pointing, the way compasses point, *down*.

Hazel looked at the cellar door for a long moment. Then at the recorder, still blinking its patient red eye. Then they picked up their torch, and their logbook, and the last cold inch of tea.

"Right," they said, to the house, to the girl in the recorder, to whatever was waiting at the bottom of the stairs with a book in its hands. "Fine print first. Then the verdict."

The cellar door opened before they reached it — courteously, the way a good advocate holds the gate.
