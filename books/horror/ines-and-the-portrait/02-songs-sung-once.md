## 2. Songs Sung Once

The rain came to Vellmarsh the way gossip did — sideways, and settling in where it wasn't wanted.

Ines had woken at dawn in her grandmother's bed, under a quilt that smelled of cedar and something older beneath it, something like cold stone. She had lain still for a long moment, listening to the house tick and settle around her, cataloguing the sounds the way she catalogued everything: gutter-water ticking against the sill, a beam somewhere above expanding with warmth it would never quite hold, the particular silence of a room that had been waiting for someone to breathe in it again.

Naming things kept them safe. That was the first lesson her grandmother had taught her, though Marisol Dunmore had never said it so plainly. She'd said it in the way she touched every object in the kitchen before using it — *the blue bowl, the dented kettle, the knife with the loose handle* — murmuring their names like small prayers. Ines had built a life on that habit. Names were handles. A thing properly named could be carried, could be set down, could be understood.

A thing unnamed sat in the dark of you and grew.

She dressed, made tea in the dented kettle — she'd found its name written on a strip of masking tape inside the cupboard door, in Marisol's cramped hand, as if even the kettle might forget itself — and opened her field notebook to a fresh page.

*Vellmarsh. Day two.*

*Objective: record the once-sung songs. The funeral laments, the harvest verses nobody will sing twice, the christening rounds that stop at the third line. Document performer, context, melody (where permitted), lyrics (where remembered), and — critically — why they are not repeated.*

That last column was the one that had drawn her here, three hundred miles from the university archive where she spent her days among tapes labeled in other people's handwriting. Every folk tradition had its songs sung once. Usually the reason was mundane: grief too raw, luck too fragile, a superstition worn smooth by time until no one remembered what it had been protecting. But Vellmarsh was unusual. Here, *nothing* was sung twice. Not the hymns in the church — she'd checked with the vicar's wife on the phone last spring, fishing politely — not the carols at Christmas, not even the rounds children skipped rope to. One pass, and then silence, and then something else entirely.

Her grandmother had grown up inside that silence. Had left it at nineteen and never once spoken of it, except once, near the end, when the morphine had loosened whatever knots she'd kept her whole life tied.

*"Some songs are doors, love. You want to be very sure which side of the door you're standing on before you sing."*

Ines had written it down at the time, in the hospital notebook she kept for last words, and had filed it under *poetry*. Now, drinking tea in the hollow of Vellmarsh with rain writing its own grey script down the windows, she wondered if she should have filed it elsewhere.

* * *

Tam Wicker lived at the top of the village, in a low stone cottage whose garden had surrendered gracefully to nettles. She'd been given his name by the woman at the post office — a small, quick woman named Mrs. Petch who answered questions the way a drawbridge answered travelers: all at once, and mostly up. *"Tam Wicker knows the old songs. If he'll sing them for you, which he won't."*

He opened the door before she knocked, which unsettled her more than she let show. He was very old and very thin, built like something the wind had been arguing with for eighty years, and his eyes were pale grey and unblinking as river stones.

"You'll be Marisol's girl," he said.

"Granddaughter. Ines."

"I know who you are." He stood aside. "Come in out of the wet, then. You've got her look about you. The looking look."

Inside, the cottage was dim and dense with objects — brass, bone, bundles of dried herbs hanging from beams like sleeping bats — but it was clean, fiercely clean, everything polished or swept or folded. Ines recognized the tidiness for what it was. It was the same tidiness Marisol had kept. The tidiness of people who needed the world to stay in its places.

He put a kettle on without asking if she wanted tea, and she took out her recorder while his back was turned, setting it on the table between a tin of buttons and a horse-brass shaped like a sunburst.

"That going to listen to me?" Tam asked, sitting down across from her. His voice was dry as seed husks, but there was iron under it.

"If you'll let it."

"And what happens to what it hears?"

"It gets kept," Ines said. "Archived. Labeled. Listened to by people studying songs, mostly, and mostly not listening very hard." She smiled. "That's the honest answer. Songs get preserved better than they get heard."

Tam looked at the machine for a long moment. Rain ticked against the window behind him. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse moved through its small economy of living.

"Marisol used to say that," he said at last. "About keeping. She said a song in a box couldn't do any harm." His mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "I told her then what I'll tell you now. A song isn't in the box. The box is in the song. Everything that ever sang it is in there, and everything it ever sang *to*."

Ines kept her voice level, her pen moving. "You think songs carry things."

"I don't think anything, girl. I remember things. Thinking's for people who've got the luxury of forgetting." He poured tea into two heavy mugs, pushed one toward her, and wrapped both his hands around his own as if the day were colder than it was. "You want the lament. The Wicker lament. Your grandmother recorded half the village before she left, I expect she told you that much."

"She didn't tell me much of anything about Vellmarsh."

"No," Tam said softly. "No, she wouldn't have. That was her kindness, though I doubt anyone ever thanked her for it."

* * *

It took him most of the first pot of tea to agree, and most of the second to begin.

What moved him, Ines thought later, was not her arguments but her attention. She did not push. She laid out her notebook, showed him the empty columns, explained the project the way she'd explain it to a funding board — plainly, without romance — and then she simply waited, and listened to him talk about his father, and his father's father, and the way the village had buried its dead for six generations with a song that began low in the chest and climbed like smoke.

"They sang it once," Tam said. "At the graveside. Once only, and only over the body, and only by those who'd known the dead alive. You sing it to send them. Singing it twice is calling them back, see. Or—" he paused, and his eyes went briefly to the dark window, "—answering when something else calls back first."

"What else would call back?"

Tam drank his tea. Outside, the rain thickened, and the light in the cottage went the color of pewter.

"The Wicker lament has four verses," he said, instead of answering. "First verse names the road the dead walk. Second verse names the hands that hold the lantern. Third verse asks the ground to lie easy. Fourth verse—" He stopped. His thumb moved along the rim of his mug, around and around, a slow orbit. "Fourth verse says goodbye. Properly. With the name in it."

"And you'll sing all four?"

"I'll sing what I can sing." He set down the mug. "Start your machine, girl. And you'll not ask me to go again."

She started the recorder. The little red light glowed steady, and she felt the familiar calm of it, the sense that the world was being gathered safely into evidence.

Tam closed his eyes. When he began to sing, his voice transformed — the dry husk gone, replaced by something rounder and darker, a sound like water moving under ice. The melody was modal, old, built on intervals that modern ears heard as almost-wrong, each note leaning somewhere it shouldn't and arriving anyway. The words were dialect, half of them, and Ines transcribed phonetically as fast as she could:

*The road runs dark and the road runs deep,*
*and the dead walk it alone—*

His voice dropped for the second verse, naming the lantern-hands, and Ines felt the hairs rise along her arms, not from fear exactly but from proximity — the sense of standing close to something vast and moving in the dark. This was why she did this work. This was the whole of it. A song like this was a room that had been locked for a hundred years, and she was holding the key, and the air inside was still warm.

Third verse. *Ask the ground to lie easy.* Tam's eyes were streaming now, though his face stayed composed, tears running into the seams of his cheeks like rain finding channels in stone.

Then the fourth verse began, and stopped.

Not trailed off. Stopped. Mid-word, mid-breath, like a door slammed. Tam's mouth remained open on the shape of a syllable that never arrived, and his eyes came open too, and Ines saw — clearly, unmistakably — terror.

Not grief. She knew grief; she'd bathed in it for a year since Marisol died. This was the animal thing underneath grief. This was a man hearing a sound in the night and knowing the house was supposed to be empty.

"Mr. Wicker—"

"Don't." His voice cracked back to its husk. He pressed the heel of his hand against his sternum as if holding something down. "Don't say it. Don't hum it. Don't you dare write it down and mouth it to yourself tonight to see how it fits your voice."

"I wouldn't—"

"You would. It's what you're for." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Your grandmother was for it too. Keeping. Writing. Putting names on things so they hold still." He looked at the recorder, its red eye still burning, and something in his face curdled. "Turn it off."

Ines reached out and clicked the machine silent. The absence of the red light made the room feel suddenly larger and less inhabited.

Tam sat breathing for a while, long deliberate breaths, the way men breathe after lifting something too heavy. The rain filled the silence for him. When he spoke again, his voice was quieter, and older, and it did not sound like a man sharing folklore anymore. It sounded like a man giving testimony.

"When my dad died, I sang that verse over him. Name and all. 'Goodnight, Aldous Wicker, sleep you sound, the lantern's lit, the road is found.' Forty years ago." His thumb resumed its orbit on the mug-rim. "And the ground lay easy, and we walked home, and that was that. Except." He stopped the thumb. "Except that night, and every night after for a season, I'd wake at the same hour — the hour we'd sung it, four in the morning, the dead's hour — and I'd hear the verse. From outside. Under the window. Sung back."

Ines's pen had gone still against the page. "Sung back how?"

"Same tune. Same words. My voice." He met her eyes, and his were flat and grey and terribly patient. "Note for note, girl. Like something had caught it in its teeth and was learning the shape of it. Learning me." He leaned forward. "So you take your machine and your notebook and you go home to your city, and you write your book about the pretty songs, the ones that don't mind being sung. Because some songs are doors. And doors open from both sides — but some of them, somebody's already standing on the far side of, pushing."

* * *

She walked back down through the village in the failing afternoon, rain needling the hood of her coat, and the silence of Vellmarsh pressed against her ears like deep water.

She tried to make it make sense. That was her discipline, her comfort, her craft: the world yielded to method. An old man's grief, replaying itself as hypnagogic hallucination — well documented, perfectly ordinary. A village-wide taboo about repetition — classic apotropaic practice, the same instinct that made people knock wood or avoid whistling backstage. Tam Wicker was ninety-one years old, mourning his father across forty years, and she had asked him to open the wound and sing into it. Of course he'd stopped. Of course he'd seen something in her recorder's red eye that frightened him. Machines held voices now; of course the old feared them.

All of it tidy. All of it named. All of it safe.

But walking past the churchyard, where the wet stones leaned together like conspirators, Ines caught herself doing something she hadn't done since childhood: counting the houses. Counting the windows. Making sure of the village the way you make sure of your change, without deciding to, the way the body counts exits in an unfamiliar room.

And she noticed — noticed the way you notice a wrong note in a song you know — that the villagers she passed, two women under an umbrella, a boy hauling a bicycle, an old man sheltering in a bus stop none of the buses served anymore — that not one of them was humming. Not one was singing to himself, or to a child, or to the rain. In a hundred villages she'd worked, music leaked out of doorways like heat. Here the doorways gave off nothing. The whole valley was holding its breath, and had been, she understood suddenly, for sixty years — not because the singing had been forgotten, but because it had been *put away*, carefully, the way you put away the matches after a fire.

They weren't superstitious. They were disciplined. There was a difference, and the difference was the worst thing she'd learned all day.

Back at the house, the light was going. She stood in the hall, dripping, and looked down the corridor toward the parlor, where her boxes of tapes waited — Marisol's recordings, decades of them, the whole village's voice gathered and shelved. Her inheritance. Her project. The reason she'd come.

Somewhere in that parlor, on those shelves, was a tape of Tam Wicker's father singing the fourth verse. And a tape of Marisol herself, young, singing God knew what to God knew whom.

Ines hung up her coat, and did not go into the parlor yet. She stood in the darkening hall and listened to the house tick, and told herself the unease crawling up her spine was only the weather, only tiredness, only an old man's ghosts doing what ghosts of grief do.

Then, from the parlor — faint, unmistakable, impossible — came the sound of the tape deck's motor turning.

She had not left it running.

She had not left it *loaded*.
