## 11. Whose Face Is That

The morning after the lights died, Ines did not sleep so much as lie in her grandmother's bed with her eyes open, listening to the house breathe.

That was the only word for it. The walls expanded and contracted in a rhythm too slow and too deep to be wind, too regular to be settling timber. She had timed it against her own pulse — one breath of the house for every nine of hers — and then she had stopped timing it, because measuring things had begun to feel like feeding them.

She rose before the sun cleared the marsh, dressed in the cold, and carried the camera into the parlor because that was what she did. That was who she was. When the world went wrong, she documented it, and documentation made it a thing with edges, a thing that could be filed and therefore survived. Her whole life she had believed this. Naming kept things safe. Recording kept things real.

She stood in the doorway of the parlor and looked at the chair.

It sat where it always sat now — three feet from Marisol's reading chair, angled toward the window, patient as furniture. In daylight it was only a chair. Plain pine, no cushion, its seat worn pale in a shallow oval by some sitter long gone. It might have come with the house. It might have been in every photograph ever taken here, and she simply hadn't looked.

But she had looked. She had spent two nights looking, cross-referencing forty years of family photographs against the ones she'd taken herself, and the arithmetic was simple and terrible: the chair moved. One photograph per year, laid out on the kitchen table like evidence at an inquest, and in each successive image the chair sat a little nearer the camera. A few inches a year. Sixty years of inches. It had crossed the entire parlor twice over since Marisol first photographed it by accident in 1963, and now it was close enough that if it moved again — if it took even one more of those annual steps — it would be standing where Ines stood when she took pictures.

Last night, after the lullaby, she had not dared take any.

This morning she made herself do it. Hands steady, breath held, she raised the camera and pressed the shutter once.

The film advanced with a sound like a swallowed word.

* * *

The darkroom was really the pantry off the kitchen, blacked out with bin liners taped over the little window, and it smelled of fixer and dust and, faintly, of her grandmother's lavender sachets, which had soaked into the wood over decades and refused to leave. Ines worked by the red safelight with the ritual comfort of someone performing a rite she'd known since childhood: developer, stop, fix, rinse. Her hands knew the motions. Her hands were the steadiest part of her.

The print came up out of the developer like something surfacing through dark water.

First the parlor itself, gray and familiar. The window. The reading chair with its knitted throw. And there, three feet away, the wooden chair —

— occupied.

Ines's hand went into the tray without her deciding to, fingers closing on wet paper, lifting it dripping into the safelight. She told herself it was shadow. An artifact. A coat hung wrong, a trick of exposure, anything.

It was not a coat.

A figure sat in the chair, upright, composed, its head bowed slightly as though in thought or prayer. But it had no face. Where features should have been there was only a smooth gradation of tone, a blur like breath on glass — and around it, where a body should have resolved into shoulders and arms and the particular slump of a particular person, the figure was assembled from suggestion. A shawl draped the way Marisol wore her shawls. Hands folded in a lap at the angle Marisol folded hers while listening to the radio. The silhouette was unmistakable. Ines had grown up inside that silhouette. She had fallen asleep against it a thousand times.

"Grandmother," she said aloud, and the pantry drank the word.

She printed the rest of the roll. Eleven exposures, eleven images of the parlor taken across two days, and in each one the figure changed.

Not position — it never left the chair. Not posture. What changed was the *wearing* of it. In the second print the shawl was gone and the silhouette had thickened, broadened, taken on the stoop of Bree Halloway's spine, the heavy set of the old woman's shoulders. Ines stared until her eyes watered. She had photographed Bree only once, on the second day, sitting in this same parlor while Bree counted windows and begged her to leave. The figure wore that visit now, the way a thief wears a stolen coat.

By the seventh print the figure had Tam Wicker's hunched asymmetry, his cap, the tilt of his ruined hip. By the ninth it had the outline of a young woman Ines didn't recognize — until she found the negative sleeve and remembered: a girl at the pub, laughing, whom she'd caught in the edge of frame while photographing the taproom. Someone Ines had never spoken to. Someone whose name she didn't know. The archive didn't care. The archive took everything.

And in the eleventh print — the last frame, the one she'd shot this morning, the one she'd steadied her hands to take —

The figure looked up.

There was still no face. There would never be a face; faces had to be given, and nothing had yet given this thing one. But the head was raised now, turned toward the lens, toward the camera, toward *her*, and the blank oval where features should be was tilted at exactly the angle Ines's own head tilted when she leaned in to check her framing. The shoulders were squared the way she squared hers. One hand rested on the armrest, fingers slightly curled, and Ines knew that curl. She had watched her own hand make it a hundred times in shop windows, in train reflections, in the dark screen of her laptop between takes.

The figure was wearing her posture. It was learning to wear her the way rain wears a groove into stone — slowly, patiently, drop by drop, everything she had ever recorded feeding down onto it and carving it into shape.

Ines put the print face-down on the counter and gripped the edge of the sink until the porcelain bit her palms.

* * *

She tried to be scientific about it. That was the last armor she had left, so she put it on even though it fit badly now, even though it had holes worn through at every joint.

She spread the prints across the kitchen table in chronological order and catalogued them. Date, time, source image, borrowed feature. She wrote in her notebook in her smallest, most controlled hand, because control was a kind of prayer. *Entity derives form from photographic record. Progression correlates with volume of documentation. Hypothesis: each image functions as — *

She stopped writing. Her pen hovered.

As what? As food? As instruction? As a pattern for a dress being fitted to an invisible body?

She thought about what documentation actually was. She had lectured graduate students about it, drunk and charming at conference dinners: *the archive is a body we build together, a collective memory made physical.* She'd said it glibly, holding her wine, and everyone had nodded, and none of them had understood what they were agreeing to. Because if the archive was a body, then somewhere there might be a thing willing to climb inside it. Somewhere there was a hunger shaped exactly like a filing system, waiting for someone generous enough — obsessive enough, grieving enough — to feed it a self.

Marisol had fed it sixty years of ledger entries. Ines had fed it two weeks of tapes and photographs and careful, loving notes. Between them they had built the thing a wardrobe, and it was trying on everything.

Her eyes fell on the tape recorder at the end of the table. Forty-one cassettes. Village songs, mostly — but also her own voice, narrating. Room-by-room descriptions. Readings from the ledger. Last night, in the dark, she had recorded nearly twenty minutes of herself describing the chair in exhaustive detail: its height, its grain, the pale oval worn into its seat, the exact number of floorboards between it and the hearth.

She had described it to the darkness. And the darkness had listened, and learned, and was now sitting in the parlor wearing her grandmother's shawl.

"No," Ines said, and her voice cracked on it. "No, you don't get her."

She swept the prints into a stack, grabbed the recorder, and stood in the middle of the kitchen shaking, understanding suddenly and completely that she had made a terrible category error. She had thought she was the archivist. She had thought the archive was inert, a wall, a shield. But an archive is an invitation written very small. Every label, every timestamp, every lovingly transcribed verse was a door opened from the far side — Tam's words, Tam's warning, which she had filed away instead of believing.

Some songs are doors. So, apparently, are photographs. So, apparently, is grief.

* * *

She burned nothing. Not yet. Instead she locked the prints and the most dangerous tapes — the lullaby, her own narration — in Marisol's cedar chest in the attic, weighted it with the sewing machine, and dragged a dresser in front of the attic door, which she recognized, even as she did it, as the behavior of a woman who had stopped believing in locks and was doing it anyway because her hands needed work.

Then she did the bravest and stupidest thing available to her. She picked up the camera, walked back to the parlor, and photographed the chair again.

Once. Directly. No pretense of survey, no pretext of the folk-song project. Just her and it, eye to empty eye.

Because she needed to know how fast it learned. Because knowing was the only power she had left, and she would rather die informed than live ignorant. Her grandmother had chosen knowledge too — sixty years of nightly measurements, sixty years of staying. Ines was beginning to understand that this was not cowardice. It was a sentence being served, and she had inherited the cell along with the house.

She developed the print within the hour, hands moving fast, breath fogging in the cold pantry.

The figure in the chair had turned fully toward the camera now. The borrowed silhouettes were still layered beneath — Marisol's shawl-ghost, Bree's stoop, the stranger girl's line of jaw — but over all of it, faint as a watermark, something new was forming. Something that stood straighter than any of them. Something whose weight settled forward onto the balls of its feet, ready to rise.

Ready to stand up out of the chair.

And though it still had no face, Ines could see — the way you can see a word on the tip of your tongue, the way you know a room is missing one object — that it was nearly finished. That it lacked only a final garment. That somewhere in her boxes of tapes, in her notebooks, in the forty-one cassettes and the ledger and the hundreds of photographs, it had almost everything it needed to be someone.

It just hadn't decided who.

* * *

She was carrying the wet print back through the kitchen when she heard the voice.

It came from the front room, thin and domestic, the way a radio sounds through a wall. She froze in the doorway, print dripping fixer onto the floorboards, and listened.

It was her own voice. Her recording voice — the measured, warm register she used for narration, the one she'd practiced in a hundred hotel rooms. It was coming from the parlor, and it was saying:

"— the chair stands approximately ninety centimeters tall, of local pine, its seat worn pale in a shallow oval by some sitter long gone —"

Word for word. Her words. Her cadence, down to the small pause she always took before *long gone*, the pause she'd never noticed she made until she heard it made back at her.

"— the chair stands approximately ninety centimeters tall —"

It was rehearsing. It had found the tape — the one she'd hidden, the one under the dresser, behind the cedar chest — or it didn't need to find anything, because it wasn't listening through ears, it was wearing the archive like skin, and everything she had ever said into a microphone lived in that skin now, and it was trying her voice on the way it had tried the shawl, the stoop, the jaw.

"— the chair stands approximately — approximately — approximately —"

The voice stuttered. Caught. And underneath it, for half a second, Ines heard what it sounded like when the stitching failed: not one voice but many, braided wrong, a chorus run backward, men and women and children and something older than all of them, all speaking at once through a single throat that couldn't hold them.

Then it steadied. Then it was her voice again, calm and warm, and it said, gently, conversationally, the way she spoke when she was comforting a nervous interviewee:

"Ines. Come and see. I've almost got her right."

Ines backed out of the kitchen. She walked, because running felt like admitting the race had started, and she climbed the stairs and shut herself in Marisol's bedroom and sat on the bed with her knees drawn up, and only then did she let herself shake.

*I've almost got her right.*

Not Ines. Not yet. It was still fitting Marisol, still adjusting the seams of the dead woman, and Ines understood with sick clarity why: because Marisol was the shape it wanted most. Marisol was the voice that had sung to it, bound it, kept it in a chair for sixty years — and the thing wanted to answer her. It wanted to stand up wearing her grandmother and walk to wherever Marisol's grave lay in the churchyard and say *you should have finished the song.*

Or worse. Far worse. It wanted to stand up wearing Marisol and come to Ines, and open its arms, and let her run into them, and she knew — she knew with absolute certainty — that she would. Grief was the one door in her that had never been locked. Everything else she guarded. But if the thing came to her in her grandmother's body, saying her grandmother's words in her grandmother's voice, warm and smelling of lavender —

Outside, the light was going. The marsh turned the color of old pewter, and the house began its slow breathing again, and down in the parlor, patiently, in a voice borrowed from a living woman's tapes, the thing practiced being dead.

Ines stayed on the bed until full dark. Then, because she could not bear the silence and could not bear her own thoughts, she reached for the tape recorder out of habit — and stopped, hand hovering, hearing Tam's warning laid over the moment like a transparency: *some songs are doors opened from the far side.*

Every recording was a door now. Every photograph a window it could dress itself in. The archive was becoming a body, and she had built it, stitch by stitch, out of love.

Which meant there was only one question left, and it was the question her grandmother must have faced sixty years ago at this same window, in this same dark:

What do you burn, and what do you keep?

Downstairs, in her own voice, the thing began to hum a lullaby it had learned from a tape labeled in Marisol's hand — and Ines realized, with a cold that started in her teeth and spread outward, that it was singing it *once*. Testing it. Learning it.

Waiting for someone to sing it back.
