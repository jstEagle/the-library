## 12. Marisol's Last Tape

The tape had been waiting for her.

Ines found it at the bottom of the ledger box, after she'd read the last unfinished line a dozen times and then stopped reading it altogether because the words had begun to rearrange themselves in her memory — *It asked me to sing it back* becoming *sing me back* becoming *asked* becoming nothing but breath. The cassette was wrapped in a dish towel embroidered with hollyhocks, the kind Marisol used to drape over rising bread, and it was labeled in her grandmother's small, upright hand: *FOR INES. PLAY LAST.*

Not *play when I'm gone.* Not *play if you must.* Just *last*, as though there were an order to things, as though Marisol had known exactly which of her granddaughter's discoveries would come before this one — the photographs, the ledger, the window that wasn't there by day, the lullaby sung twice. As though she had arranged the house like a sentence and this tape was its final clause.

Ines sat on the kitchen floor with her back against the cold of the stove and held the cassette for a long time without putting it in the machine. Outside, the light was going the color of weak tea. She had checked the parlor twice already today; the chair stood where it always stood now, close enough to the doorway that she could see the grain of its seat from the hall, and she had made herself look at it only in the mirror over the mantel, per Bree's instruction, so that what she saw was reversed and therefore somehow less real, the way your own face is less real in reflection.

Her recorder sat beside her. Her notebook lay open to a page of questions she no longer expected anyone to answer. She pressed record anyway — habit, armor, whatever it was now that documentation had turned traitor — and said, "Tape forty-one. Found the last one. Playing it now." Her voice sounded thin in the big quiet of the kitchen, and beneath it, faintly, something breathed that was not her.

She put the cassette in.

* * *

Static first, long and soft, like wind moving through wheat. Then a click, and then Marisol's voice, closer than death should allow, so present that Ines's whole body flinched toward the machine as if toward a hand.

*"Testing. Testing. Is this thing on. There — the red eye. Hello, mija. If you're hearing this, then you found the ledger, which means you found the chair, which means you've been sleeping badly and eating worse, because you are my granddaughter down to the bone and I know how you grieve: you make lists."*

Ines laughed. It came out of her like a hiccup, wet and involuntary, and then it became crying, brief and hard, and she let it, because there was no one in the house to hear except the thing that heard everything.

*"Don't apologize,"* Marisol said, as if she could hear it across the years. *"Cry fast and get to the end. We haven't much tape and I haven't much night."*

A pause. On the recording, somewhere behind Marisol's voice, a clock ticked — the parlor clock, Ines realized, the one with the brass pendulum that had stopped at 3:17 the week of the funeral. On the tape it was still running. Time moved differently inside the machine, or had moved differently then, or both.

*"You'll want to know why,"* Marisol said. *"Why I sang it twice. Bree will have told you not to. Tam will have told you some songs are doors. They're right, both of them, and they hate me a little for what I did, and they love me too, and I don't blame them for either. But they don't know the whole of it, because I never told the whole of it, because the whole of it is ugly in a way that doesn't wash out."*

Another pause. When the voice came again it was lower, and slower, and Ines understood that her grandmother was choosing every word the way you choose footholds crossing ice.

*"Vellmarsh has always lost people strangely. You'll have heard it called the hollow, and thought it was just geography. It isn't. A hollow is a shape made by something leaning in. Grief here was never clean. Old Fenner buried his wife and dug her up twice to be sure. The Aldous child was found three fields from where she wandered off, dry as paper, months later, smiling. People said wolves. People say wolves the way they say weather — to have something to stand under."*

*"I came here a young wife who sang. That's all I was, at first. Your grandfather liked that I sang. The village liked it. And I noticed — because I notice things, the way you notice things, God help us both — that when I sang at the wakes, the grieving went easier. Too easy. The widow would smile halfway through the second verse and never cry again, and by spring she'd be thin as a candle and pleasant as milk, and everyone would say how well she was bearing it."*

*"They weren't bearing it. It was being taken. Something in Vellmarsh was eating the dead — and not just the dead. It ate the mourning first, and mourning is part of a person. Take it away and what's left doesn't hold together. It just... agrees. With everything. Forever."*

Ines's hands had gone cold around the recorder. She thought of the villagers' faces at the pub, the way they had looked at her — not unkindly, not cruelly, but with the terrible patience of people watching someone walk toward a cliff edge they'd all been pulled back from once. She thought of Tam stopping mid-verse, pale as tallow, refusing to repeat a single line. *Some songs are doors opened from the far side.* He hadn't meant superstition. He'd meant appetite.

*"I didn't understand it all at once,"* Marisol continued. *"Understanding came the way rot comes — slowly, then all at once. What I understood first was that the thing liked my singing. It came close when I sang. Closer than it ever came to the priests or the salt or the iron horseshoe over the Aldouses' door. All their protections, sixty years of them, and it walked past every one to stand near a woman with a tune."*

*"So I did what foolish women do. I got curious."*

*A sound on the tape — Marisol shifting, the creak of the kitchen chair, the same chair Ines was sitting against, and the intimacy of that nearly undid her. Sixty years between them and the same wood under the same bones.*

*"There was an old song. The once-sung. Every village has one it won't sing twice — you know this, you've spent your life collecting the ones nobody repeats. Mostly they're nothing. Mostly the prohibition is just fear wearing tradition's coat. But this one — I found the verses in the church vestry, written on the flyleaf of a burial register from 1811, in a hand that shook. Nine verses. The ninth was torn out. And the song was about a guest. A guest who comes when called and cannot leave until released, who has no face of its own, who wears what it is given. Do you see, mija? It was never a demon in the way the church means. It was a hunger with manners. It waited to be invited, and it waited to be shaped, and everything it took from us it took because we kept handing it ourselves."*

*"I sang it once, alone, in the parlor, on a night in November much like tonight. Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. Once-sung is only a knock. Twice is opening the door."*

*"And I want you to listen very carefully now, because this is the part Bree doesn't know, that Tam doesn't know, that no one living knows but you."*

The tape hissed. In the hiss, Ines could almost hear the house listening.

*"I sang it twice on purpose."*

*"Because a thing that eats grief cannot be killed — there's no killing a hunger. But a hunger can be fed on terms. It wanted in. It wanted faces, voices, shapes — it wanted to be somebody, and it could only become somebody out of what we gave it. So I gave it a chair."*

*"I set the chair in the middle of the parlor, and I sang the lullaby the second time with my hand on the chair's back, and I sang the binding into it — not banishment, mija, binding. You can't send it anywhere, because it isn't from anywhere. It's a hole wearing patience. But a hole can be plugged, and a song sung over an object makes the object a door, and a door can be shut on something and held shut."*

*"It sat down. God forgive me, it sat down like a tired traveler, and it stayed. For sixty years it has sat in that chair and eaten nothing but what I fed it: my evenings, my attention, my fear, measured out nightly like medicine. I logged its position because position was power — the moment I stopped watching, it would drift, and drifting is how it starts walking. Every entry in that ledger was a hand on the door."*

*"And the village lived. Do you understand? The widows wept their grief out and kept their selves. The dead stayed buried and the missing stayed found. Sixty years, and the only price was mine — mine and the chair's, sitting opposite each other in the dark like two old women playing cards neither can win."*

Marisol's voice caught then — not with tears, Ines thought, but with something harder, the sound of a person setting down a weight they've carried so long their spine has grown around it.

*"I'm telling you this so you don't hate me. Or rather — hate me if you need to, but hate me accurately. I didn't fail to protect you from this. I handed it to you, deliberately, the way my mother handed me the bread recipe. Because the binding holds only while someone sings the song alone — do you hear the flaw in that? Alone. One throat. One life. When I die, the song dies with me unless it lives in someone else, and if it dies, the door opens, and everything I starved for sixty years walks out hungry."*

*"I could have let it go. I thought about it, near the end. Let Vellmarsh pay its own debts. But I watched Mrs. Aldous at her son's funeral last spring, weeping like rain, ugly and human and whole, and I thought: I bought that. I bought every honest tear in this valley with sixty years of my nights. And I could not spend my last strength unwinding it."*

*"So I left it to you. Not the burden — I'd never. The choice. That's the only inheritance worth anything, mija. Everything else — the house, the tapes, the name — those are just materials. What you build from them is yours. Keep the door shut and sing alone and it will eat your life the way it ate mine. Burn it all and walk away and it comes out starving and takes the village apart verse by verse. Or find the third thing. I never found the third thing. I ran out of nights looking."*

On the tape, a long silence. Ines realized she was gripping the recorder so hard the casing creaked. The tea-colored light outside had gone to ash; the kitchen window showed her own reflection and, behind it, the dark hallway, and she did not turn around.

*"One more thing,"* Marisol said, softer now, and Ines heard the tiredness in it, the enormous ordinary tiredness of the dying. *"It will try to wear me. It has my voice from these tapes, my shape from the photographs, sixty years of my habits soaked into that house like smoke into plaster. When it speaks as me — and it will — remember this: I would never ask you to sit. Whatever love it borrows from my voice, it cannot borrow this. I spent my life keeping people out of that chair. If something in it asks you to sit, that is the one proof you'll ever need that it isn't me."*

*"Sing loud, little one. Sing with company, if you can find a way. And when you finally burn my tapes — and you should, most of them, they're full of me and it's been feeding on me through them — keep one song. Any song. A glad one. Play it in the kitchen with the windows open."*

*"That's how you'll know I'm gone. Not the funeral. The singing."*

A click. The red eye of the old machine going dark, preserved forever in the new machine's memory. Then thirty seconds of room tone — the tick of the parlor clock, the settling of the house — and beneath it, so faint that Ines played it back three times to be sure, a fourth sound.

Scraping. Slow, patient wood across floorboards. Moving closer to the microphone.

Then the tape ended.

* * *

Ines sat on the kitchen floor until the dark was total, the dead recorder warm in her lap like a sleeping animal. Her grandmother had not been the haunted one. She had been the keeper. Sixty years of ledger entries flashed behind Ines's eyes — not the log of a victim documenting her haunting, but a jailer counting her prisoner's steps, night after night, decade after decade, alone.

*Alone.* The word rang in her like a struck bell. *The binding holds only while someone sings the song alone.* Marisol had named the flaw herself and died without solving it, and now the flaw was Ines's, and the door was hers to hold, and her throat was one throat.

But —

She thought of Bree Halloway's face in the lamplight, the way the old woman had said *we were the children she sang to* and stopped, as if the sentence had more rooms in it she couldn't bear to enter. Four children. Only Bree remained unclaimed. But unclaimed was not untouched — and if the binding needed a single voice, then perhaps what broke it needed many. Tam knew verses he refused to speak. Somewhere in Vellmarsh or beyond it, two others had grown old carrying whatever fragment of that song Marisol had planted in them like seeds.

Ines stood. Her knees cracked; the kitchen smelled of dust and cold iron and, faintly, of hollyhocks from the dish towel, though nothing had bloomed here in years. She wound the tape back to its beginning, held it a moment — *keep one song, a glad one* — and set it carefully in her coat pocket, next to Tam's stolen page.

Then she picked up her recorder, opened the front door, and stepped out into the dark toward Bree Halloway's cottage, where a light still burned — the only one on the lane — as if the old woman had been waiting up, all along, for exactly this night.

Behind her, in the parlor, the chair scraped once against the floorboards.

Not closer.

Impatient.
