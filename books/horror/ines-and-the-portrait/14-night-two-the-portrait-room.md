## 14. Night Two: The Portrait Room

The house sealed them in at dusk the way a fist closes around a held breath.

Ines felt it happen rather than heard it — a settling in the joists, a pressure behind her ears like descending in an unpressurized plane. Bree felt it too. The old woman had arrived at six with two canvas bags and no explanation beyond "I'm not letting you sit alone tonight," and now she stood in the front hall with one hand flat against the door, testing it, the way you test ice on a pond.

"Locked?" Ines asked.

"Worse." Bree turned the latch anyway. It moved freely. The door did not. "It's not locked. It's *finished*. Like a book someone's closed."

The windows had gone strange an hour earlier — not dark, because darkness would have been honest, but reflective, each pane showing back the interior of the room as if the glass had forgotten which side of itself it was supposed to be on. And in every reflection, faintly, like a watermark, the parlor. The chair.

They had covered the mirrors first. That was Bree's instruction, delivered that afternoon in the kitchen while she unpacked salt and candle stubs and a hand mirror wrapped in flannel: cover every reflective surface before sundown, and never — never — look at the chair directly. But the house had more faces than they'd counted. The polished lid of the piano. The brass fender at the hearth. The dark water standing in the rain barrel outside the scullery window, which should not have been visible from inside at all and yet was, in every reflection, patient as a pupil.

By nine o'clock there were eleven of them, surfaces where the parlor showed through, and Ines had stopped counting because counting felt like documentation and documentation, she had learned, was how the thing fed.

* * *

It began with the bathroom mirror on the landing, the one Marisol had used for sixty years, its silvered back blooming with age-spots like a map of some drowned country. Ines had draped it with a tea towel that morning. At 9:14, standing in the hallway with an armful of candles, she saw that the towel had slipped — or been pulled — and hung by a single corner, and beneath it the mirror was not showing the landing at all.

It showed the parlor.

Not the parlor as it was — lampless, cold, the furniture shrouded in sheets since Marisol's funeral — but the parlor as it had been. Warm. Lamplit. The good curtains drawn against the marsh fog, the fire laid though not lit, and the chair sitting in its place by the window with the particular quality of attention that furniture does not have and never has had until this week.

Occupied.

She could not see what occupied it. That was the mercy or the cruelty of the angle — the figure sat with its back to the mirror-viewer, a silhouette assembled out of shadow and suggestion, shoulders slightly stooped in a way that made something in Ines's chest lurch sideways before her mind caught up and named the posture for what it was: *grandmother-shaped.* Marisol had sat like that. Marisol had sat exactly like that, in exactly that chair-adjacent light, mending or reading or simply watching the weather come up the hollow, and Ines had spent thirty years of visits memorizing that silhouette without knowing she was memorizing it.

"Don't," Bree said from behind her, quiet and hard. "Don't look at it direct. Look at me."

"I wasn't—"

"You were." Bree took the candles from her arms and set them on the floor, then took Ines's face in both hands, her palms cool and papery and absolutely steady. "It shows you what you'll kneel for. That's all it knows how to do. Now help me with the piano lid."

* * *

But the house kept making new mirrors.

That was the horror of the second night, Ines thought later — not any single reflection but the house's industry, its tireless invention of surfaces. The black screen of the dead television in the back room. The glaze on a teacup. The wet sheen on the inside of the kitchen window where their breath had fogged it. Each one, when they found it, showed the same room, the same lamplight, the same stooped figure, and each time the figure was fractionally clearer, the way a photograph comes up slowly in developing fluid. By midnight Ines understood that the reflections were not windows. They were a portrait being painted, stroke by stroke, and the pigment was everything Ines had recorded.

At half past midnight, it spoke.

* * *

They were in the kitchen, the safest-feeling room, salt lines poured across the threshold and the doorway to the hall blocked with the dresser. Bree was dozing upright in a kitchen chair, her breathing a thin whistle. Ines was transcribing — she couldn't stop herself, couldn't sleep, her hands needing the work — when the tape recorder on the table clicked.

She had not touched it. She had wound the tape fully back and left it off; she checked twice afterward that this was true. But the little spools were turning, and the red record light burned steady, and then her own voice came out of the speaker.

*"Testing. Testing. Vellmarsh field recording, day one, Ines Dunmore—"*

Except it wasn't quite her voice. It was her voice the way the reflections were the parlor — correct in every measurable detail and wrong in some way below measurement, flattened, played at a speed her ear rejected without being able to name. Then the voice changed.

*"Now, Tam, take your time. There's no hurry."*

Tam Wicker's answer came through the small speaker, his funeral lament, the one he'd broken off mid-verse — but it didn't break off. It continued past the point where he'd gone pale and pushed the recorder away, into verses he had never sung for her, low and dragging, words in the old marsh dialect that Ines's transcription pen could not keep up with. Then that voice folded away too, and another rose beneath it:

*"Mind the step, love, the frost gets in the stones—"*

Marisol. From a tape labeled only *M., kitchen, undated*, forty seconds of domestic nothing Ines had catalogued and filed and nearly deleted. Her grandmother's voice, saying a sentence she had said ten thousand times, and underneath it, braided in, other voices — Bree's from yesterday, the pub crowd from the night the village refused her, a child's laugh from some party tape decades old, all of them cut and spliced and layered until they stopped being voices and became a single instrument, an organ whose pipes were throats.

*"You've brought me so much,"* the thing said, in all of them at once, and the kitchen walls seemed to lean in. *"Every song. Every room. Every face. I am so much fuller than I was."*

Bree woke. Ines knew it without looking — heard the whistle of her breathing stop, felt the old woman's hand find hers under the table and grip it, grip hard, a warning and an anchor both.

*"I could give some of it back,"* the voices said. Gentle now. The splicing smoothed out, the seams vanishing, until it was almost — almost — only Marisol speaking, warm and tired and fond, the voice that had read to Ines through chickenpox and talked her down from two heartbreaks and one failed dissertation. *"You've given so freely, child. It seems rude not to reciprocate."*

The tape hissed. Somewhere above them, in the portrait room — they'd begun calling it that, the room where Marisol's actual portraits hung, though the name had grown teeth — a floorboard settled.

*"Come and see,"* said her grandmother's voice. *"Come and sit, and I'll show you what I can do with everything you've taught me."*

* * *

"We don't go," Bree said.

"It asked us to come to it."

"And we don't go. Rule three, girl. Salt, mirrors, and never look at it direct — and rule four, the one I'm adding tonight: it doesn't get to summon us. We're not guests here. This is your grandmother's house, and we are the living, and the living do not take appointments with that."

But Ines was already standing, and she hated herself for it, and she went anyway — not because the voice compelled her, she told herself, not because anything compelled her, but because if the thing in the parlor was wearing her recordings, then it was wearing her work, her archive, her life's careful naming of things, and she wanted — needed — to see what it had made of them. That was the story she carried upstairs like a lantern. It was even mostly true.

The portrait room was at the end of the landing, Marisol's best room, hung with fifty years of photographs: Ines at four on the garden wall; Ines at twelve, gap-toothed; Marisol herself young and squinting into a sun that had probably never been that bright over Vellmarsh. The room was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. Every mirror in it — the vanity glass, the gilt oval above the mantel, the small round mirror in the corner that had shown the garden — reflected the parlor downstairs, and the parlor was lit, and the chair was occupied, and the occupant had turned around.

Ines looked. She broke Bree's rule with her whole chest, deliberately, because she was done pretending she had any instinct left except the oldest one.

The figure wore Marisol completely. Not a silhouette now — a woman, seated, hands folded in her lap, gray hair pinned the way Marisol had pinned it every day of Ines's memory, cardigan buttoned wrong the way Marisol always buttoned it wrong and never let anyone fix. The face was Marisol's face, assembled from photographs — Ines could see the seams if she looked, the way the smile was lifted from the picture on the mantel and the eyes from a snapshot taken at the harbor in 1987, features borrowed rather than grown, a collage pretending patiently to be a person.

"There you are," it said, and its mouth moved with the words, and the voice coming out of it was the braided choir of every tape Ines had ever made, wearing Marisol like a coat cut to fit. "Sit down, love. You must be exhausted."

There was a second chair in the portrait room. There had not been a second chair an hour ago. It faced the mirror, angled toward the reflection of the parlor, positioned with the thoughtfulness of a hostess who has considered sightlines.

"If I sit," Ines said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "What happens?"

"You rest." The thing leaned forward, and the borrowed face softened with an expression of such precise maternal tenderness that Ines's knees actually loosened. "That's all anyone wants, isn't it? To rest. You've been carrying this house for weeks. Carrying *me* — my tapes, my songs, my whole poor unfinished life. Let me carry it a while. Sit, and I'll finish what your grandmother started, and you can go home to your flat in the city and sleep without listening for clicks in the night."

"That's not what I want."

"It is exactly what you want." The voice gentled further, dropped into register that bypassed Ines's ears entirely and spoke directly to the grief under her ribs. "And here is what I want you to understand, Ines Dunmore, folklorist, archivist, girl who names things to keep them safe: I can give her back. Not a copy. Not a haunting. Her. Everything you recorded of her — every syllable, every habit, every wrong-buttoned cardigan — I have it all now, complete, and I can run it forward instead of backward. A Marisol who continues. Who makes tea tomorrow morning. Who asks about your book. Who grows old in the ordinary way she was robbed of."

The fire that wasn't lit crackled anyway, in the reflection.

"She died," Ines said. "Eleven months ago. I held her hand."

"You held a hand that had already given most of itself away keeping me fed and small." For one flickering moment the seams showed — the face rippled, and beneath Marisol's skin Ines glimpsed the chair's plain wood grain, and beneath that something older and hungrier that she refused to describe even to herself. "Your grandmother bargained with me for sixty years, child. Do you think she stayed in this house out of love for the damp? She paid nightly. And now you've fed me better in three weeks than she did in six decades — songs sung once, rooms documented, faces archived. I am *rich*, Ines. Rich enough to be generous."

Ines's eyes stung. That was the worst of it — not fear, fear she could manage, but the way the thing had learned to aim. Grief weaponized. Every word calibrated against the archive of her own longing.

"If I sit in that chair," she said slowly, "you stop needing the villagers' voices. You'd have mine. One throat. One shape."

The thing smiled with Marisol's mouth, and the smile was genuinely delighted, and that delight was somehow more obscene than anything else in the room.

"Clever girl. Yes. One throat is all a song needs to be sung alone. And a song sung alone—" 

"—is a binding," Ines finished. "I know. I've read her ledger."

Something crossed the borrowed face that might, in a real face, have been respect. Or recalibration.

"Then you know the alternative," it said softly. "Dawn is hours away, and the house is smaller than it was yesterday, and I am very, very patient. Sit with me, Ines. Or spend the night learning what I do with people who refuse me twice."

Downstairs, faintly, a door that led nowhere opened anyway.

* * *

Bree's hand closed on Ines's shoulder from behind — when had she come up, how had she crossed the salt line without sound — and pulled, gently but absolutely, the way you pull someone back from a platform edge.

"Not tonight," Bree said to the mirror, to the chair, to the thing wearing her oldest friend's face. "She doesn't sit. And neither did Marisol, in the end — whatever you tell yourself. She kept you hungry on purpose, you great empty thing, because a full door opens wide."

The figure in the reflection rose from the chair. All the reflections, everywhere in the house at once, Ines felt it in her teeth — every mirrored parlor emptying, every borrowed Marisol standing, turning toward the viewer with the unhurried motion of something that has just stopped pretending to be furniture.

"Then I'll come to you," it said, in five voices, in fifty, in the voice of every song Vellmarsh had ever sung once. "I know all the doors now. Your grandmother showed me hers. And I have learned yours, Ines — you leave them open everywhere. Tapes. Pages. Photographs. You are a house of open doors, and I have all night to knock."

The mirrors went dark, all together, like eyes closing.

And in the sudden black, from the foot of the stairs, from the sealed front hall, from inside the walls themselves, came the sound of the lullaby — hummed, patient, in a voice stitched from everyone Ines had ever recorded — beginning, note by note, to learn its missing verse.
