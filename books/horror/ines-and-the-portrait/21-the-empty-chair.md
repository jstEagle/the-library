## 21. The Empty Chair

The photograph had been drying in the bottom drawer of Marisol's writing desk for three weeks before Ines could bring herself to touch it.

Not drying — she corrected herself, as she corrected herself every time the old vocabulary of haunting rose unbidden. Photographs did not dry after twenty-four hours, let alone three weeks. What she meant was that she had needed three weeks to be certain it would stay what it was: a picture of a parlor in clean morning light, dust motes caught mid-gold above the rug, and a plain wooden chair standing empty against the far wall.

Just a chair. Just light. Just an ordinary room that happened to have held more grief than most churches.

She carried it to the mantel on the last day of October, when the light through the front windows came low and honeyed and the hollow smelled of leaf-rot and woodsmoke from Bree Halloway's chimney two fields over. The mantel was brick, cold under her fingertips, and bare except for the clock that had stopped at some hour she'd never bothered to learn and now refused to start again no matter how many times she wound it. She had thought about fixing the clock. She had decided against it. Some stopped things deserved to stay stopped.

The frame she'd bought in Ashcombe — plain black ashwood, no ornamentation, because ornamentation felt like lying. She set the photograph at the center of the mantel, adjusted it twice until it sat perfectly level, and then stood back with her arms crossed over her chest like a woman appraising a painting she wasn't sure she liked.

It was not a beautiful picture. That was the point of it. It was a true one, and truth, she was learning, had its own severe beauty — the beauty of a thing that has stopped pretending.

In the image the chair waited, vacant, patient as furniture, and if you looked long enough — Ines allowed herself exactly ten seconds of looking, a discipline she kept the way Tam Wicker kept his Sundays — you could almost believe it had always been only a chair. Almost. There remained, at the very edge of perception, something in the way the grain of the wood ran — a faint suggestion of rings within rings, like the growth lines of something that had lived a very long time and been cut down and made to sit still.

*Forever vacant,* she had written in her notebook the morning after the singing, her hand still shaking, Bree asleep in the armchair across the room with a blanket over her knees and salt in both pockets. *That is the whole of the ritual now. Not banishment. Vacancy.*

She touched the frame once, lightly, the way you'd touch a headstone or a sleeping animal, and went to make tea.

* * *

The book began, as most of her work began, with a list.

She had filled eleven notebooks since coming to Vellmarsh — field recordings transcribed, verse structures mapped, variant lyrics cross-referenced between singers three villages apart — but this book was different, and she knew it from the first line she wrote, which was not a line about songs at all:

*This is a book about what we keep, and what keeping costs.*

She wrote at the kitchen table because the study still smelled, faintly and forever, of Marisol's pipe tobacco, and she could work in the kitchen without crying, which was a skill she had developed the way other people developed calluses. The mornings were for transcription; the afternoons for the manuscript itself; the evenings, increasingly, for company, because Bree came down the lane most nights now with a covered dish and an opinion, and Tam Wicker came Thursdays, and on Sundays — this was new, this was strange and good — the others came too.

Maren Ostler, who had left Vellmarsh at nineteen and built a life in Norwich so deliberately distant that she'd changed the spelling of her surname, and who had arrived at Marisol's door in September gray-faced and silent, and who now sang alto in a voice like river stones. Dev Anand, the youngest of the five, who had been a baby when Marisol sang over his cradle and who remembered nothing except that he had always, all his life, been afraid of empty chairs in photographs, and who wept the first time the five of them sang together and laughed at himself for weeping. And old Sef Calder, oldest of them all, half-deaf, who sang by watching mouths and feeling the floorboards vibrate, and who had told Ines, the first Sunday, patting her hand with fingers like tree roots: *Your grandmother saved us before we knew there was anything to be saved from. You finish what she started, girl. We'll hold the door.*

They were not family. That was the word Ines kept circling in the manuscript, worrying it like a loose tooth. They were not family, and they were not colleagues, and they were not quite friends, because those categories were too small for what they were. They were people who had sung together in a house that tried to eat them, and there was no existing noun for that, which was perhaps why she was writing a book — to build a shelf big enough to hold the thing.

*Dedicated to the five who sang,* she wrote on the flyleaf of her working copy, and beneath it, smaller: *and to M.E.D., who sang first, and alone, for sixty years.*

Marisol Edith Dunmore. She had found the initials embroidered inside the ledger's back cover, hidden stitching, the kind of secret a woman keeps even from her own paper. Ines had pressed her thumb against the letters for a long time that first evening, feeling nothing but thread, and had understood — finally, fully, in her hands and not just her head — that her grandmother had chosen this. Had chosen the burden, the nightly counting, the chair at the edge of every photograph like a debt written in the margin of a life. Had chosen it so that five children could grow up, and leave, and forget, and live.

Identity is not what is inherited, she wrote. Identity is what is chosen. Her grandmother had chosen to be the woman who counted windows. Ines was choosing to be the woman who wrote it down.

* * *

There were rules now, and she kept them the way monks keep hours.

Never sing the lullaby alone. Never record it at all — she had burned those tapes in the yard in September, watched the plastic curl and the magnetic ribbon spool out like black entrails, and the smoke had gone straight up in windless air, and Bree had said afterward, quietly, "Good," and that was all anyone said about it.

Never look at the chair directly after dark. This rule had softened with time — the chair in the photograph was only a chair, and the actual chair, which still sat in the parlor because removing it had felt like both desecration and temptation, was only wood — but rules kept in calm weather are what hold in storms. So she looked at it obliquely, always, the way you look at the sun.

And once a week, on Saturdays, she took a single photograph of the parlor. One exposure, one print, checked against the mantel picture. The chair stayed where it stood. The grain of the wood stayed grain. Six Saturdays now, and six identical prints, and she had begun — carefully, the way you test ice — to believe.

The villagers' other songs she recorded freely, joyfully, with their blessing. Tam's harvest rounds. Maren's mother's spinning song, which Maren had thought forgotten until her own mouth remembered it. Sef's boat-launching chant, sung in a voice like a hull taking water, magnificent. These she catalogued with the old methodical comfort, naming each one, dating it, noting the singer's breath and hesitations, because naming things kept them safe — that belief had survived everything else, and she clung to it now not as armor but as creed. The archive was no longer a body the thing could wear. It was a hearth the living could gather at.

Some songs, she noted in the manuscript, are doors opened from the far side. Others are doors opened from this one, outward, into the dark, with lanterns. The difference is not in the song. It is in the number of throats, and the reason for singing.

* * *

On the fourth of November she walked up to the churchyard with a trowel and a small pot of white paint.

Marisol's stone read only her name and her years, because Marisol had left instructions of unusual specificity and the stonemason had followed them to the letter. No epitaph. Marisol had written, in the letter Ines found folded inside the ledger: *No words on the stone. Words invite reading, and reading invites questions, and I have answered enough questions. Let the grass do the talking.*

But Ines had brought paint anyway, and knelt in the wet grass, and painted — small, neat, at the base of the stone where only someone kneeling would ever see it — a single empty chair. Just the outline. Two legs, a seat, a back. Five strokes of the brush.

Then she sat back on her heels and talked to the stone for a while, the way she'd never managed to talk to the house.

"I'm staying through winter," she said. "Bree asked. I said yes before she finished asking, which I think surprised us both." A crow argued with another crow somewhere down the hill. "I'm writing it all down. All of it. The chair, the tapes, the hallway, the night the mirrors lied. I'm putting your name in it — your real name, the whole thing, embroidered letters and all. You hid yourself so well for sixty years, Gran. Somebody should say it out loud."

She wiped the brush on the grass and stood, knees cracking, forty-one years old and feeling every one of them in the good way, the way that means a body has done hard things and survived them.

"You should know the house counts right now," she added, at the gate. "Eleven windows. I counted them myself, twice, in daylight and after dark. Eleven." She paused. "I think you'd have liked that."

* * *

The manuscript grew the way gardens grow — unevenly, with weeds, requiring patience. By the second week of November she had ninety pages and a title she neither loved nor hated: *Once-Sung: A Year in Vellmarsh.* Working title. Everything was a working title; everything was a working life.

Bree read the first three chapters aloud in the kitchen one evening, slowly, because her eyes tired easily, while Ines cooked and pretended not to listen and listened to every word. When Bree reached the passage about the ledger — about the decades of nightly entries, the positions logged to the inch, the final unfinished line — she stopped reading and lowered the pages.

"You're kinder to her than she was to herself," Bree said.

"She earned kindness retroactively."

"Mm." Bree folded the pages along their existing creases, precisely, a woman who had spent a lifetime folding things. "My mother used to say mercy is just memory with the sharp edges sanded. Write it kind, then. But write it true. Both, or neither." She tapped the page. "This line here — *the village closed like a hand* — that's true. Don't sand that one. We deserve to be seen closing. That's how nobody gets fooled next time."

"Next time," Ines repeated. "You think there'll be a next time."

Bree looked at her for a long moment, and outside the window the last light went off the hills, and the kitchen got very quiet in the particular way kitchens get quiet when two women are being honest.

"I think," Bree said, "that doors don't stop being doors because you've shut one. I think the world is mostly doorway, if you've got the eyes for it. And I think you've got the eyes, girl, God help you, same as your gran." She picked the pages back up. "So you write the book. And you teach the song to more than five throats. And you keep the picture on the mantel where you can see it from the table."

"Why the mantel?"

"Because a warning you can't see is just a story," Bree said, "and a promise you can't see is just a wish. Put it where both can catch you daily."

* * *

December came down the valley like a slow gray tide. Frost furred the hedgerows. The pub put its fairy lights up and Tam Wicker, of all people, climbed the ladder to hang them, refusing help, swearing cheerfully at the guttering. Ines recorded the carol service from the back pew, nineteen voices braided on a winter night, and played it back in the kitchen afterward with the volume low, and it sounded like what it was: people, alive, singing at the dark on purpose.

The photograph on the mantel gathered its film of household dust and she cleaned it every Sunday, ten seconds of looking, no more. The chair stayed vacant. On the winter solstice — the longest night, the night she had dreaded since September — she invited all five of them to the house, and they ate until they ached, and at midnight they stood in the parlor and sang the complete song, all the verses including Tam's stolen page, five voices and hers making six, and the name went around the circle like a cup passed hand to hand, no single throat to claim it, and the fire burned steady, and the chair stood empty, and nothing knocked, and nothing breathed under anyone's voice, and at half past midnight Sef Calder fell asleep in his chair with his mouth open and had to be woken with tea.

Afterward, walking Bree to her gate under a sky scoured bright with stars, Ines said, "I keep waiting to feel finished."

"You won't," Bree said comfortably. "Finished is for furniture." She fumbled her key out of her glove. "You'll feel *kept*. Different animal entirely. Now go home, girl, it's freezing, and mind —"

She stopped. Ines stopped.

Down the lane, past the frozen ford, a pair of headlights swung slow through the dark, unfamiliar, unhurried, and died as the car turned somewhere out of sight toward the village. Just a stranger lost on the moor road, probably. Happened all the time in winter. It meant nothing.

Probably.

"Mind what?" Ines said.

Bree tucked her hand through Ines's arm, warm through two coats, and steered her homeward. "Mind you keep singing," she said. "Under your breath, on your way, wherever you go. Doors open from both sides, love. Best to be heard coming."

The lights of Marisol's house showed gold through the trees ahead, all eleven windows burning, and Ines counted them as she walked, and began, softly, to sing.
