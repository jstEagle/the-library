## 7. What Was Invited In

I didn't sleep. I sat in the attic with the journals spread around me like a ring of salt, and when the sky went grey at the edges I carried them down the stairs one stack at a time, past Wren's door, which was closed, and humming, and did not open it.

The village square at seven in the morning was almost empty. A man was hosing down the steps of the church. A woman was taking in milk bottles. Ordinary work, ordinary light. But every one of them saw me coming and found something else to look at, and that was its own kind of confession.

Maren's cottage was the last house before the fields, blue paint gone chalky, a garden gone to seed in a way that looked deliberate — nothing planted, everything permitted. She opened the door before I knocked.

"I wondered how long you'd sit up there," she said. "Come in, then."

Inside smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs and something older underneath, a sweetness like fruit going over. She poured tea without asking and set out two cups, and I understood that she had been setting out two cups for thirty years, waiting for a Delacroix to come down off that hill with questions.

I put the journals on her table between us.

"Tell me what Tam was," I said.

Her hands went still around the teapot. Not startled. Still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"You've read them," she said.

"All night. Every generation. Every child." My voice came out flat, which surprised me; I'd expected it to shake. "Every friend who never aged. Every entry that ends the same way — *given to the road.* So tell me what my cousin was given to, Maren. Tell me what my family kept."

She sat down across from me. She looked, for the first time since I'd known her, old — not weathered, not capable-old, but old the way a house is old, load-bearing and tired.

"You want the beginning," she said. "Everyone wants the beginning, as if the beginning explains it. The beginning is just a winter."

* * *

She told it plainly, the way people tell things they've had to carry a long way and have finally set down.

Eighteen forty-something. Before the church had its roof, before any of the family names were carved anywhere. The famine came up the valley like weather. Cattle first, then crops, then the well water turning sweet and wrong, and the children dying in an order nobody could find a pattern to, which was worse than a pattern. The village tried everything villages try. They buried coins under the doorstep. They left bread on windowsills. They sang the founding song — the one I'd recorded, the one Maren broke off in the seventh verse of — because the song was older than the famine and songs are what you spend when you have nothing else.

"And the song worked," Maren said. "That's the part no one wants written down. It worked too well. Something heard it."

Something came down from the high pasture where nothing grazed, and it stood at the edge of the firelight looking like whatever each person needed it to look like, and it said, in a voice like a child's, that it could end the hunger. It asked for only one thing. Not blood. Not worship.

"To be remembered," Maren said. "It said it had been forgotten by everyone who ever knew it, and forgetting was the only hunger it couldn't feed itself. It wanted a family. It wanted to be thought of daily, sung to, named. It wanted to belong to someone the way a name belongs to a face."

"And they believed it."

"They were starving, Gideon. Believing is cheap when you're starving." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on the saucer, and turned it back. "It kept its word. By spring the cattle were fat and the wells ran clean. And it kept being so *lovely*. It played with the children. It remembered their birthdays. It sat on porches in the evening and listened to the singing. For eleven years, Hollowmere was the luckiest village in the county, and we paid for it exactly what it asked — we remembered it. There was even a verse in the song for it. The seventh. You sing a thing's true name in the seventh verse, that's the old custom, keeps it bound and keeps it fed."

My mouth had gone dry around the tea. "Then what changed?"

"A generation grew up that hadn't seen the famine," Maren said. "And children grow embarrassed of childhood things. A boy decided Wren was beneath him and told his friends so, loudly, and stopped singing the verse, and his friends' parents laughed about it at market — kindly, you understand, but laughter is a knife when you're made of being remembered." Her eyes came up to mine. "And that spring, the boy's little sister walked out toward the high pasture and didn't come back, and the famine-smell came back to the wells, and the village understood the terms all at once, far too late, and far more precisely than before."

"The terms changed."

"The terms sharpened." She said it without flinching, and I hated her a little for the flinch she was refusing me. "It doesn't take. Understand that. It has never once taken anyone. What it does is worse. When the remembering thins — when the family that promised lets the verse go unsung too long, lets the name go unspoken — the forgetting spreads. First the village forgets the child exists. Then the child forgets themselves, walking, patient as weather, toward the place where they'll be held. Given to the road, the journals call it, because that's what it looks like from outside: a child on a road, going somewhere, smiling. And once they're held—" she gestured vaguely upward, toward the hill, toward the house, "—they're kept. Remembered perfectly. Forever. It doesn't eat them, Gideon. It *keeps* them. That's the horror your grandmother finally wrote down in the last journal, the one you haven't finished reading. Being loved by it is the punishment."

The room tilted slightly. I put my hand flat on the table.

"Tam," I said.

"Tam," she agreed softly. "Your grandmother let the verse lapse. One summer. She was grieving your grandfather and she was tired and she thought — everyone thinks — that it's just a song, just a story, just a game the children play. Tam was nine." Maren's voice didn't waver, but her hands had begun to shake around the cup, fine tremors, tea shivering in rings. "Afterward your grandmother picked the promise back up and carried it like a mule carries a pack. She sang the verse every day of her life after that. She wrote those journals so the next keeper would know what they were carrying. She meant them for your mother. Your mother left before—"

"Before she could be told." I heard my own voice from very far away. "So it fell to me. Through the letters. Through the house."

"It fell to whoever remembered," Maren said. "That's what a Delacroix *is*, child. We're not a family. We're a hinge. The promise lives in our line because our line is the one that invited it, and the invitation can only be renewed or ended by the blood that spoke it. Every Delacroix child gets a Wren. Every Delacroix child is raised inside the friendship so the bond holds. And every Delacroix child is the next payment if the keeping fails." She leaned forward. "You think you came here by accident? You think the house waited empty for thirty years out of sentiment? It was waiting for *you*. The last one. The last Delacroix. And Wren has been sitting on that porch since the week after your grandmother died, patient as Sunday, because the promise is nearly spent and you are what's owed."

* * *

I walked back up through the village and the whole village watched me do it, doors opening, faces in windows, and no one spoke, and I understood that they weren't shunning me. They were afraid to talk to me. Talking builds memory, and memory, this morning, was a debt collection.

At the foot of my own lane, the young postman caught up to me — the one who'd waved me in two days ago, the one who'd told me the shortcut. He was pale and he wouldn't come closer than ten feet.

"He's been asking after you," the man said. "All morning. Standing at your gate, calling you by that little name. We can hear it from the square." He swallowed. "Miss — Gideon. Thirty years ago there was another one. Tam. I was a boy. I remember him walking up this same lane, and I remember thinking how happy he looked, and I remember my mother dragging me indoors and burning the bread off the windowsills and telling me never to say his name again, and I never did, none of us ever did, and that's why—" His voice cracked. "That's why it's always a Delacroix who pays. Because we *can't* remember them. Only your family can. Only your family ever could."

He turned and walked fast back toward the village, and I stood alone on the lane with the journals heavy in my bag and the wind coming down off the high pasture, carrying, faintly, from the porch of my grandmother's house, a sound I had known all my life and never once questioned:

a small voice, singing the seventh verse, complete, word-perfect, waiting for me to come home and sing along.

Wren had never lied to me. That was the worst of it. Everything it had told me on that porch, in that house, in the dark of my own buried summers — all of it true, all of it loving, all of it a cage built out of nothing but perfect attention. It had offered me belonging, and belonging was exactly what it fed on, and I had been raised — chosen, groomed, inherited — to be hungry for it.

Tam wasn't lost. Tam was the price. And I was next.

Unless the promise could be ended instead of renewed — and Maren had said the words herself, before I understood them: *the invitation can only be renewed or ended by the blood that spoke it.*

By a Delacroix.

By me.

I looked up the lane. Wren stood at the gate now, small and unchanged and lit from within by something that was not sunlight, and it smiled at me with thirty years of patience, and beckoned.

"Gideon," it called, in the voice no living person should know. "Come and hear the rest of the song. I've saved the best verse for you."
