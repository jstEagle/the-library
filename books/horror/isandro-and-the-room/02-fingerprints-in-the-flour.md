## 2. Fingerprints in the Flour

The kitchen was the warmest room in Hollowmere.

Isandro noticed it before he noticed anything else — before the flour, before the prints, before the woman on the stairs. It was a small, stupid detail, the kind of thing his training told him to log and move past: the thermostat on the wall read 71 degrees while every window in the house wept frost on the inside of the glass. He'd been in a hundred houses like this one, houses that had lost their heating years ago because nobody lived in them properly anymore, and those houses were always cold in a way that got into your bones and stayed there. This house was warm the way a body is warm. Warm from the inside out.

He stood in the doorway with his kit bag on his shoulder and let that fact sit wrong in his chest for a moment before he made himself move.

"Mrs. Joubert?" he called, though she'd already shown him in. "I'll start down here, if that's all right."

No answer. The hallway behind him was dim, the stairs rising into shadow, and he had the distinct feeling of being watched — not hostilely, but thoroughly, the way you'd watch a man work a combination lock to see if he actually knew the numbers.

He set his bag on a kitchen chair and did what he always did first: nothing. Stood still. Let the room tell him about itself. Twenty years of night shifts had taught him that the first thirty seconds of standing in a strange kitchen were worth more than an hour of poking around. Rats announce themselves if you're patient. Mice too. Even squirrels in the soffits, even wasps in the wall cavity — everything that lives in a house has a schedule, a route, a sound it can't help making.

This kitchen was silent in a way that wasn't patient at all. It was silent the way a room is silent when someone has just left it.

He crossed to the window over the sink and looked out at the yard. Frost everywhere, thick as spilled sugar, the grass silvered stiff. Behind him, the radiator under the counter ticked gently, doing its honest work. He ran a finger along the windowsill and came away with a smear of dust so fine it was almost liquid. Old dust. Undisturbed dust.

Except.

He frowned and leaned closer. On the sill, in the gray film, there was a line of marks running left to right, ending at the frame of the closed window. Not scratches. Not the drag-marks of a branch outside or a cat's claws. They were regular. Spaced. Deliberate.

Fingertips.

Five of them, pressed into the dust, each one longer than his whole hand.

Isandro Vey had seen a lot of things pressed into dust. He'd built half his career on reading dust the way other men read weather. Dog prints, kid prints, the smudges of exterminators who'd come before him and been sloppy about it. He knew what a human fingertip looked like in a fine medium — a whorled oval, maybe two centimeters across, the pressure point slightly darker where the pad bore down.

These were ovals eight inches long. And they weren't whorled. Under the flashlight's raking angle he could see ridges — actual friction ridges, the kind you'd find on skin — but the pattern was wrong, spiraling outward instead of looping, like a fingerprint drawn by something that had been told about fingerprints secondhand.

They ended at the window frame. Facing it. As if whatever had made them had been standing at the glass, looking out.

Or looking in.

His mouth had gone dry. He was aware, distantly, of his own breathing, and of the radiator ticking its small domestic tick, and of the fact that the room was seventy-one degrees and the window was sealed shut under a quarter inch of ice that had formed from the *inside*.

"Okay," he said aloud, because saying things aloud was how he kept his footing. "Okay."

* * *

The flour was worse.

It was on the counter beside the bread bin — a bag of plain white flour, split along one seam, maybe a cup and a half of it spilled across the laminate in a soft drift. He might have walked right past it. People spill things; kitchens collect evidence of living. But his eye caught on the surface of the spill the way it catches on a face in a crowd, and he stopped, and he set down his flashlight, and he did not touch it for a long moment.

There were prints in the flour.

A line of them, coming from the direction of the back door, crossing the counter, and stopping — stopping cleanly, mid-drift, as though the walker had simply ceased to exist halfway through a step. Five-fingered handprints alternating with footprints, a walking gait recorded in white powder, except that no part of any print matched any animal Isandro had ever tracked through twenty years of attics and crawlspaces.

The hands were human in shape. That was the worst part. He could see the thumb, offset correctly. He could see the four fingers fanning in proportion, the palm's heel, the arch of the lifeline pressed deep where the weight had rolled forward. Everything was where it should be.

Everything was three times too large.

He crouched until his eyes were level with the countertop and looked at the prints the way he'd look at a track in mud, and his professional mind — the calm, cataloguing machine he'd spent two decades building — started feeding him answers, and every answer was garbage.

Bear? No. Bears don't have thumbs. Bear prints don't have five fingers arranged like a man's glove.

Prank? He turned the idea over and found it hollow. To fake these you'd need a cast of a hand that doesn't exist, and you'd need to press it with enough force to leave ridge detail in loose flour without smearing the surrounding powder, and then you'd need to stop mid-stride and vanish, because the exit prints — he checked twice, circling the counter, dropping to his knees to check the floor, the cabinet faces, the tops of the doorframes — the exit prints did not exist anywhere in the room.

Whatever had walked through this flour had arrived and then stopped being here.

And the fingerprints in the dust upstairs on the sill — the ones facing the door, Ada had said on the phone, always facing the front door — those had been made by the same hand. He was sure of it. Same impossible length, same wrong spiral. Something in this house had a habit of pressing its palms against surfaces and leaving its signature, and the signature said: *I am shaped like you. I am not you.*

Isandro realized he was holding his breath and let it out slowly, the way he did when he needed his hands steady.

Here is what he felt, and he would remember it later with uncomfortable precision: not fear, not yet. Humiliation. A hot, specific shame, like being asked a question in a language he'd claimed fluency in and understanding nothing. Because he was good at this. He was the man other pest controllers called when their own tricks failed. He could read a droppings pile like a diary. He knew the difference between the gnaw of a rat and the gnaw of a squirrel the way a sommelier knows cork from screw cap. His whole identity — the thing that had kept him upright through the debts and the nights and the long gray stretch of his forties — was that the world made sense to him in a way it didn't make sense to other people. Houses talked to him. He spoke house.

This kitchen was speaking a language with no words he owned.

He took out his phone and photographed the flour. Then, because the photos would never be enough, never convey the scale, he laid his own hand beside one of the prints, fingers aligned, and photographed that too. In the image his hand looked like a child's resting next to its father's. He stared at that for a moment too long and then put the phone away, because looking at it made something cold walk down his spine that had nothing to do with the frost on the windows.

The radiator ticked. Somewhere above him, a floorboard settled with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"You're seeing this," he murmured to the empty room. "Tell me you're seeing this."

The room declined to comment.

* * *

He worked anyway. That was the discipline, and discipline was all he had some nights: you do the job in front of you, and you do it completely, and you don't let the job do you. So he checked under the sink — clean, no droppings, no gnaw marks on the trap, though the trap was dry as bone, which meant the pipes hadn't run in years. He checked the pantry, the gap behind the fridge, the kick-space under the cabinets. He checked the back door and found it locked from the inside, bolted top and bottom, the bolt bright with use. He checked the flue of the old wood stove and found it swept and capped.

Nothing. No entry point for anything larger than a spider. Which meant either the prints had come from nowhere, or —

Or they'd come from inside the house.

He didn't finish the thought. He stood in the middle of that warm, silent kitchen with his flashlight off and listened to the house breathe its radiator-tick silence, and he thought about the word *inside*, and he decided, professionally, deliberately, to go find the homeowner and ask her some questions.

That was when he saw her.

She was on the stairs. Third step from the bottom, sitting with her forearms on her knees, watching him through the kitchen doorway with an expression he couldn't immediately name because he'd almost never seen it before on a client's face. She was perhaps fifty, gray-streaked hair pinned up carelessly, wearing a cardigan the color of wet bark. She held a mug of something in both hands. She was not startled. She was not embarrassed, the way people are when caught watching. She was watching him the way you watch a doctor read an X-ray — with a terrible patience, waiting for the verdict she already knows.

"How long have you been sitting there?" Isandro asked.

"Awhile." Her voice was low and even, unhurried. "You didn't scream. Most of them scream, or laugh, or start explaining. You put your hand next to it." She nodded toward the counter. "For scale. Nobody does that."

"It's procedure," he said, and heard how thin it sounded.

"Mm." She sipped from her mug. Steam curled up past her face. "Did your procedure reach a conclusion?"

Isandro looked at the flour. He looked at the woman. He was aware of the fork in the road, the one that appears in every job eventually: the moment where you decide whether you're going to be the professional who gives the client the answer they've paid for, or the honest one who tells them what he actually found. Usually the two are the same. Usually the world cooperates.

"The prints are human in morphology," he said, and hated himself for the word *morphology*. "Five digits, opposable thumb, correct plantar gait pattern. They are approximately three hundred percent outside normal human range. There is no ingress. There is no egress. There is—" He stopped. Started again. "There's no animal in Hollowmere, ma'am, that makes a track like that. There's no animal anywhere."

"I know," Ada Joubert said.

"That's not a pest problem."

"No."

"That's not anything I'm licensed for."

"I know that too." She rose from the stair, unhurried, joints cracking softly, and came to the kitchen doorway. Up close her eyes were pale gray and very steady, and there were shadows under them of the kind that don't come from one bad night but from seven years of them. She looked at the flour prints the way you'd look at photographs of someone you loved who had done something unforgivable. "I've had eleven men out here over the years. Exterminators, surveyors, a structural engineer, two policemen, one priest." A pause. "The priest was the worst. He charged me double and prayed in his car for forty minutes before he'd come in."

"What did the others say?"

"Rats. Settling foundations. Condensation. One very confident young man told me I was doing it myself, sleepwalking, and left me a pamphlet." She smiled without any warmth reaching it. "You're the first one who measured."

"Somebody has to," Isandro said. "If nobody measures, then it's just a story. Stories don't get solved."

"And you solve things."

"When I can."

Something moved behind her eyes — not warmth exactly, but recognition, the look of one solitary animal sighting another across a field. "Mr. Vey. Can I show you something? It's why I called you specifically. Why I waited for you, after the others."

"You waited for me?"

"I asked around. The men at the depot say you're the one who doesn't quit a job and doesn't lie about one." She said it flatly, factually, and it landed in Isandro's chest with more force than any compliment had in years, because it was true, and because being known for it — being *counted on* for it — was the only currency he had left.

"There's a room upstairs," Ada said. "Full of cassette tapes. Seven years of recordings of me sleeping. Every single night." She turned and started up the stairs, and paused on the third step, not looking back. "I made them to prove the noises had explanations. Rats. Pipes. Wood settling. That was the whole point — proof, evidence, something I could take to the council, to anyone, and say *see, it's only the house.*"

The radiator ticked. The frost on the kitchen window had grown, Isandro noticed, since he'd entered the room — a new fern of ice feathering inward from the corner, delicate and deliberate, like handwriting.

"Last month," Ada Joubert said quietly, "the tapes started answering back."

She climbed into the dark of the upper hall, and Isandro Vey, who had a rule about following clients into darkened upper halls at three in the morning, picked up his flashlight and followed her anyway.
