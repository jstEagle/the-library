## 25. The Argument at the Edge

**Isandro**

The ice road was still there when they came back for the truck.

I'd hoped, in some small and stupid part of myself, that the frost would have given up overnight — that the ditches would have thawed, that the river would run black and loud again, that Hollowmere would look like a town instead of a held breath. But the cold had built its road with a mason's patience. The river lay flat and white from bank to bank, running straight as a surveyor's line toward the drowned heart of the town, and every few yards a fern of frost bloomed up out of the ice like a hand raised in greeting.

Ada stood at the edge of it and didn't move.

"It's showing off," she said.

"It's working," I said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?" She turned to me, and her breath came out in a long white ribbon that the wind took sideways. "Because from where I'm standing, it looks like something that wants us to walk on it. Something that spent all night building us a road and is now waiting by the door with its coat on."

"That's not what this is about."

"No." Her voice went quiet, which was worse than shouting. "This is about the ledger page you burned. This is about the phone call you lied to me about. This is about the fact that you've been rehearsing a speech since the motel, Isandro, and you think I can't hear you practicing it behind your teeth."

The wind came down the valley and pushed at both of us, and neither of us moved. Behind her, the ice glittered. It actually glittered — I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only thing I have left to offer anyone. The morning sun caught the frost ferns and threw them back silver, and the whole road to Hollowmere looked like something out of a wedding. Like a thing built for a procession.

"I'm not going to let you do it," I said.

"You don't get to *not let* me—"

"You're right. I don't." I stepped closer. "Nobody gets to stop anybody here. That's the whole horror of it, Ada. Your father didn't ask you. Sallow didn't ask anybody. The priest signed a piece of paper in 1987 and fourteen families knelt in the water and none of them asked the children who'd have to pay. So no, I don't get to stop you. All I get to do is stand between you and the ice and say what I'm going to say, and then say it again tomorrow, and the day after, until one of us runs out of nights."

"And how many nights do you think we have?"

"One," I said. "Maybe two."

* * *

**Ada**

He said it like a man reading his own sentence, and I hated him for it, and I loved him for it, and the two feelings were so tangled in my chest by then that I couldn't have told you which one was making my hands shake.

"You don't even know if it works," I said. "You heard what Father Ellery said. A volunteer's window stays open past sunrise. You'd be signing into something with no end date. No discharge clause. Nothing. You'd be trading a debt I might survive for a debt nobody ever comes home from."

"And you'd be paying a debt your father took out before you could walk."

"He took it out *for me.*"

"He took it out *instead of you,*" Isandro said, "which is exactly what I'm doing, which means either we're both wrong or we're both right, and either way one of us standing on this bank while the other walks out onto that ice is a thing I will not watch. I've watched it before. Chapter of my life you haven't read yet, and I'll tell it to you someday over something stronger than coffee, but the short version is: I stood on a bank once and let someone walk out and told myself afterward that there was nothing I could have done. And there wasn't. There genuinely wasn't. But there is now. Do you understand? For the first time in my life there is something I can actually *do,* and it's standing right in front of me being brave, and I am asking you — I am begging you — to be less brave."

The river cracked somewhere downstream. A long sound, like a door settling in its frame.

"My father begged too," I said.

His face changed. I saw it land, saw it go in under the ribs, and I kept going because if I stopped I would take it back, and I couldn't take it back, because everything he'd said was true and that was the unbearable part.

"He begged the water," I said. "He begged the floods. He begged whatever was listening in the dark under Hollowmere, and it answered him, and the price was me — deferred, filed, stamped *payable upon the death of the last Joubert.* He thought he was saving me. He was just choosing the year. That's all any of this is, Isandro. Choosing the year. And you want to choose yours early, and call it love, and I am telling you that from where I stand it looks exactly like what my father did. Another man deciding my life is currency."

"That's not—"

"Isn't it?" My voice cracked and I let it. "You hid the ledger. You lied about the phone call. You've been managing me like a job site, like one more infestation you're going to clear out before dawn, and I know why, I know your heart is in the right place, I know you'd die before you let anything touch me — but that's the problem, don't you see? *Dying isn't helping.* Dying is just another way of leaving. I have been left by everyone who ever loved me, and they all had excellent reasons."

* * *

**Isandro**

The ice road waited through all of it. That was the worst thing. We stood ten feet from it screaming our hearts at each other and it just lay there, patient as a creditor, ferns opening along its length one by one like it was counting us. Like it had all the time in the world, because it did.

"You're right," I said finally.

She blinked. "What?"

"You're right. About all of it. The ledger, the call, the managing. I've been treating you like a client." I made myself keep going. "So here's the truth with nothing managed out of it: I called the thing last night. From the motel. After you fell asleep. I picked up the phone and I said *yes.*

The word dropped between us like a stone into a well, and I listened for the splash and never heard one.

"You *what.*"

"I said yes. Freely. In my own words. I did the whole rite, or enough of it — I named myself, I named the debt, I offered. And you know what happened?" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Nothing. It said *not yet.* Same words it said on the tape seven years ago. Not yet. Because it doesn't want a volunteer standing next to the bloodline. It wants the choice clean. It wants you to walk out there knowing I offered and it refused, so that when you sign, you sign alone, with no one to blame but yourself and your father. It's engineering consent, Ada. It's been engineering it since 1987. And the only way to beat an engineer is to refuse the blueprint."

"So what's the blueprint-breaking plan?" she said, and her voice was shaking now, furious and frightened and alive. "Because from here it looks like your plan is to out-stubborn me on a riverbank until we both freeze, and the thing wins either way."

"The plan is the third option. Neither signs. We go back in together and we hold the house until sunrise and the window closes and it goes home hungry. That's the plan. It's a terrible plan. It's the only plan where we both wake up."

"And if sunrise doesn't close it?"

"Then we burn the house down around it and stand in the ashes holding hands, and it can write *remember* in the frost over our bodies."

She stared at me. And then — God help me — she laughed. One short, cracked, furious laugh that steamed in the cold.

"You're insane."

"Certifiably. Night-shift pest control in a drowned town. They don't give that job to the well-adjusted."

* * *

**Ada**

We were still arguing when we started walking. That's the thing I'll remember longest — that neither of us conceded, that the fight never ended, it just changed vehicles. We argued walking up the bank. We argued getting into the truck. We argued with the heater roaring and the windows fogging and the ice road sliding past us in the mirror, patient, patient, patient, and somewhere around the second mile I realized my hand had found his across the gearstick and neither of us had mentioned it and neither of us let go.

"You know it's going to ask again," I said quietly, somewhere past the old mill. "Tonight. It'll ask me whether I forgive him. It already knows the answer. It's been listening to my sleep for seven years — it knows I forgave him before I knew there was anything to forgive."

"Then we don't answer."

"You can't not answer a question forever."

"You can if you stay awake taking turns," he said. "Shifts. Like the night shift. Four hours on, four hours off. Nobody sleeps alone in that house ever again."

I looked at him. Frost-freckled, exhausted, stubborn as a nail, holding my hand like it was the last warm thing in the county.

"If we live through tonight," I said, "you're telling me about your brother."

"If we live through tonight," he said, "I'll tell you everything. Starting with why I said yes on that phone."

Ahead, 14 Marrow Lane rose out of the white with every window lit — though we'd left no lights on — glowing a soft, deliberate gold, like a house holding a lantern for travelers it had been expecting all along.

And on the front door, tall as the door itself, pressed deep into the frost:

a single handprint.

Facing inward.
