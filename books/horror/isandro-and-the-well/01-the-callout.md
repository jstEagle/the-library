## 1. The Callout

The job came in on a Tuesday, which Odile Lockridge would later think of as the last ordinary day of her life, though she didn't know it then and wouldn't have believed it if someone had told her. Tuesdays were for follow-ups. Wednesdays were wasps. The call had come through the dispatch line at 7:40 in the morning, a woman's voice, dry as August grass, asking for the mouse man.

"We don't do mice specifically," Odile had said, because it was true and because she liked being precise about things. "We do integrated pest management. Rodents, insects, exclusion work. What seems to be the problem?"

"Sounds," the voice said. "In the walls. And something's been at the well."

"The well."

"There's a well."

Odile wrote down the address—Rural Route 4, the old Lockridge place—and felt the name go through her like a swallowed stone. She'd known, of course. Everyone in the county knew whose farm that was. She'd just never expected the call to route to her, out of every outfit within fifty miles, and she'd certainly never expected the caller to say *Lockridge* out loud, flat and unembellished, the way you'd say *Johnson* or *the Henderson place*, as if the word didn't carry thirty years of silence behind it.

"Who's paying for this?" she asked, because her mother had raised her to ask about money before feelings, and because the question was easier than the other one sitting in her throat.

"Account's been open since March," the dispatcher said. "Prepaid. Six months standing."

Six months. Her grandmother had died in April. Odile had seen the obituary, clipped it from the paper with kitchen scissors, and put it in the drawer where she kept batteries and take-out menus, which was as close to grieving as she intended to get. The funeral had been on a Saturday. She'd worked a flea job in Corning instead, told herself the drive was too long, the schedule too tight, the whole business too little too late after three decades of nothing. Her mother hadn't gone either. Between the two of them they'd produced exactly zero Lockridges at the Lockridge funeral, and Odile had decided that symmetry was its own kind of loyalty.

But somebody was still paying for exterminations at a dead woman's house. Somebody with an account opened while the old lady was still alive, six months prepaid, and no heir to walk the property. Odile looked at the invoice number, the blank in the client-name field, and the note the dispatcher had typed: *customer requests M. Lockridge if available.*

*M. Lockridge.* Not Odile. Mara? No. There was no M. Lockridge but her grandmother, Marion, dead four months in the ground, and yet here was her name on a work order like a hand reaching up through the soil.

Odile put the work order in her truck and drove out Route 9 with the windows down and the radio off, telling herself the whole way that this was a simple job. Animals get into houses. Houses sit empty and the mice move in like squatters, the wasps build in the eaves, the raccoons take the attic. There was always an explanation. She'd built a career on explanations—biology, bad wiring, gaps under doors no wider than a pencil. Give her any haunting in the county and she'd find you the draft that caused it, the pipe that knocked, the bird in the chimney flue. People wanted ghosts; Odile sold them insulation foam and a bill of health.

The farmhouse sat back from the road at the end of a gravel lane, and even from the truck she could see it had been waiting for her. That was the phrase that came, unbidden, and she disliked it immediately. Houses don't wait. Houses rot on their own schedule, indifferent to who's watching. But there was something in the way the place presented itself—the porch boards silvered, the paint gone gray as driftwood, the yard gone to goldenrod and milkweed—that suggested not abandonment but patience. A held breath.

She got out. The air smelled of cut hay and pond water and, underneath it, something sweeter and wronger, like fruit going over on the counter. September light lay across everything at a long, honeyed angle. Somewhere a crow said one thing and quit.

The front door was unlocked. Of course it was. She let herself in, calling out the way she always did—"Pest control, anyone home?"—to the empty rooms, and the empty rooms gave back her own voice slightly wrong, slightly delayed, the way sound behaves in houses that have been quiet too long.

Inside, the place was clean. That was the first thing that snagged her professional attention. Empty houses fall apart fast—dust furring the sills, cobwebs roping the corners, mouse runs greased dark along the baseboards. This house had none of it. The floors were swept. The counters were bare and wiped. Someone—or something—had been keeping house for a woman who was dead, and Odile stood in the kitchen with her flashlight off for a long moment, listening to the particular quality of the silence, which was not the silence of emptiness but the silence of a room where someone has just left.

"All right," she said quietly, to nobody, to herself. "Let's see what you are."

* * *

She started in the cellar, because that's where the evidence would be, and cellars were honest. The stairs went down into cool dark that smelled of earth and jarred fruit—she could make out shelves along the far wall, ranks of quart jars glowing amber and garnet in her flashlight beam, tomatoes and plums and something dark she didn't identify, all sealed, all patient. The floor was packed dirt. Old houses, old dirt, old problems.

And there: droppings. A scatter of them along the wall beneath the stairs, black and neat, and Odile crouched with her gloved hand out and felt her professional certainty begin, very quietly, to come apart.

They weren't mouse. Mouse droppings are rice-grain small, pointed, scattered like punctuation. Rat are larger, blunter, capsule-shaped. These were neither. They were long—too long, nearly two inches—and tapered at both ends like something between a dropping and a claw clipping, and they were hollow. She turned one over with a penlight and saw the thin translucence of the shell, the way it caught light like a fingernail. No rodent in North America left droppings like this. No mammal did. She thought, absurdly, of cicada shells, of the husks things leave when they've climbed out of themselves and gone elsewhere, and she put the sample bag away with hands that were steadier than she felt.

"Biology," she said out loud, and the cellar took the word and kept it.

There were tracks in the dust near the fruit shelves—not prints, exactly, but drags, as if something had been carried or had pulled itself along by its arms. The dust showed five furrows apiece, evenly spaced, ending in points. Whatever made them had come out of the corner where the foundation met the earth, where the stones were fitted together without mortar, and among those stones, low to the ground, was a gap. A gap the width of a well bucket. A gap that breathed, if you knelt close enough—a faint movement of air, cool and mineral, smelling of deep water and iron.

Odile did not kneel close enough.

* * *

The well stood forty feet from the back porch under a low wooden cover, the kind with a peaked roof and a crank, the kind you see on calendars and never in real life anymore. It was real here. The wood was silvered like the porch, and the crank handle hung still, and the whole structure had the same patient quality as the house—as if it, too, had been waiting, and was better at it.

The account notes said *something's been at the well,* and Odile circled it twice, looking for what. The cover was intact. The stones were sound. The rope was new—actually new, bright hemp against all that weathered gray, coiled on the crank as if recently used. She frowned at that. Who'd re-rigged a rope on a well at an empty house?

Then she lifted the cover hatch, and the smell came up and introduced itself.

Cold water and stone and iron, yes—but under that, sweet again, the over-ripe sweetness from the cellar, concentrated, like the air itself had been fermenting down there in the dark. Her flashlight beam went down the shaft and found water maybe twenty feet below, black and perfectly still, holding the circle of light like a pupil holds a star.

And on the *inside* of the cover—on the underside of the hatch, the side that faced down into the dark—were the claw marks.

Four gouges, deep, splintering the old wood, spaced wide as a man's spread hand. They ran upward. That was the detail her mind kept returning to as she stood there in the September light with her skin crawling in a way it hadn't since she was a child afraid of basement steps: the marks ran *upward*, from below, as if something had climbed the inside of the shaft, reached the underside of the closed cover, and tried to dig its way out. Or in. The gouges were fresh—the wood pale at the bottom of each groove, raw as a wound.

Wells don't have claws, she told herself. Wells don't have anything. Something fell in—an animal, a coon, a deer, God knows—and tried to get out, and died, and the smell is decomposition, and the droppings are some local species you haven't catalogued, and the rope is a neighbor's doing, and the clean floors are—

The clean floors had no explanation. She kept circling back to them. Nobody had cleaned this house. Nobody had inherited it. The estate lawyer's office had confirmed it twice: no will on file beyond a handwritten page leaving everything to "whoever comes home." Whoever comes home. Odile had read that phrase in the fax and put the fax face-down under her coffee mug, and had not picked the mug up again until the coffee was cold.

She photographed the claw marks. She photographed the droppings. She measured the gap in the foundation stones and wrote *possible entry point, recommend sealing* in her logbook, and her handwriting came out very straight and very small, the way it did when she was holding something down.

On the way back to the truck she passed through the kitchen one more time, and stopped.

There was a chair at the table.

One wooden chair, drawn up neatly, facing the door she'd come in through. The rest of the table was bare. The floor around the chair was clean—cleaner than clean, scoured-looking, as though the chair had stood there so long the finish of the floor had worn in a ring around its legs, a pale halo decades deep.

Odile stood in the doorway with her bag over her shoulder and looked at the chair, and the chair seemed to look back—not menacingly, nothing so dramatic. Expectantly. The way a set place at a table looks when everyone else knows dinner's coming and is waiting for you to understand your part.

Her grandmother had owned a hundred chairs in her life, probably. Farmhouses accumulated chairs. There was nothing strange about a chair.

Except that Odile had grown up in this house until she was six, and she could remember the kitchen—remember the woodstove, the calendar with the feed-store logo, the window over the sink where her grandmother used to stand shelling peas—and she could not remember a chair at that table. Her grandmother had eaten standing up, at the counter, always, and Odile had asked her once why, and Marion Lockridge had said, in the flat tone adults use when they're not going to explain:

"Because I'm not sitting down to anything that isn't finished."

Odile got in the truck. She sat with both hands on the wheel and the engine running and the September light going long and gold across the fields, and she told herself, in the voice she used on frightened clients, that there was an explanation for all of it. There was always an explanation. Biology, bad wiring, gaps under doors.

Then she opened her logbook to write up the service report, and found that at some point—she could not say when—she had written a single line at the top of the page, in her own handwriting, in ink she did not remember uncapping:

*It knows I came home.*

She drove back to town with the windows up. Behind her, in the rearview mirror, the farmhouse held its breath, and somewhere under the silvered cover, forty feet from the porch, the well waited with its new rope coiled and ready, patient as a debt.
