## 6. The Bargain Before Birth

The letters were in the cedar chest at the foot of her grandmother's bed, beneath a quilt that smelled of camphor and thirty years of patience.

Odile had gone up to the bedroom looking for tax records. That was the story she told herself, anyway—estate business, boring and necessary, a thing she could hold with both hands. The house had other ideas. The hallway pulled her toward the master bedroom the way water finds a low place, and the cedar chest sat there with its brass latch turned open, as if someone had been expecting her.

Inside: letters bundled with butcher's twine. A christening gown yellowed to the color of old teeth. And a shoebox of photographs she hadn't seen before, all from before 1987, all mercifully chairless.

She took the letters downstairs because the bedroom felt watched, and because the kitchen table was where a person did paperwork. She told herself that too. She was getting very good at telling herself things.

The first envelope was postmarked March 1987, addressed in her mother's handwriting—the handwriting Odile knew from birthday cards that stopped coming when Odile turned nine, from grocery lists, from the single note left on the kitchen counter the morning her parents drove away for good. *Back by Sunday.* They had not come back by Sunday. They had not come back at all.

She put on gloves. Not for preservation. For distance.

*March 14, 1987.*

*Mama —*

*I don't know how to say this so I'll just say it. I'm pregnant. Eight weeks. We're leaving. Dale's got a job lined up in Ohio and we're not coming back to this farm, not ever, and you can't make us.*

*Before you start—I know what you'll say. I know what you told me about your mother and hers. I've heard it my whole life and I never believed it, and I still don't, mostly. But I saw the chair, Mama. In the pictures. I counted them like you taught me when I was small and I thought it was a game. It's not a game, is it.*

*So here's what I'm asking. Take what's owed and leave us out of it. You always said the debt follows the blood. Fine. Then let it follow YOUR blood and stop where you stand. Don't let it reach past you to me or to what's coming.*

*We'll send money. Whatever you need. Just keep its hands off my baby.*

*— Ruthie*

Odile read it twice. Her mother's voice rose off the page—young, furious, terrified—and something in Odile's chest went tight and hot. *Don't let it reach past you to me.* As if Odile were a package to be redirected. As if the whole question of her existence had been a matter of shipping instructions.

She reached for the next letter because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

* * *

*March 30, 1987.*

*Ruthie —*

*You were always the one who ran. Your brother stayed and paid. Your grandmother stayed and paid. I have stayed and paid since 1954, and I will tell you what none of them told you until it was too late: running doesn't end the debt. It only changes who carries it.*

*It has already smelled the child. Do you understand me? It knew before you did. There is a chair in my kitchen right now that wasn't there yesterday, and it is small. Small enough for a little one. It has never made itself small before. It has never been patient like this.*

*I can defer. I cannot erase. Every year on the harvest moon I can go down to the well and give it enough to buy another twelve months, and every year it will take a little more and ask a little more, until one year I am too old or too dead to pay, and then it comes for what it was promised.*

*Unless.*

*There is a way. Old Hettie's mother knew it, and Hettie knows it now, though she'll hate me for telling you. A debt named before birth can be transferred—but only to one who consents, and only once, and only while the debtor is still in the womb. Unborn souls can agree to things the rest of us would never sign. That's not mercy. That's how they get you.*

*I won't do it. I want you to know that plainly, so you can't ever say I chose the farm over you. I will pay the interest until my hands give out. But if you take that child away and raise her far from here and never bring her back—if you keep her name off every paper, if you never speak this place into her ears—maybe the debt goes hungry. Maybe it starves slow. Hunger isn't freedom, Ruthie. But it's the closest thing to it I can offer.*

*Come see me before you go. Bring the baby's heartbeat home one time. Let me put my hand on you both.*

*— Mama*

Odile set the letter down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be sleeping.

*Her.* Her grandmother had written *her* before anyone knew. Before the ultrasound, before the name went on the birth certificate. Odile looked at the window—at the wet smear of fingerprints that had appeared there two nights ago, five-fingered, too long, pressed against the glass from the inside—and felt the shape of a sentence assembling itself in the dark of her: *it has known me my entire life.*

* * *

There were more letters. A decade of them. Odile made coffee she didn't drink and worked through the bundle as the afternoon light went copper and then gray, and the ledger excerpts her grandmother had copied out—deliberately, it seemed, in a second hand, the way you'd copy a confession—told the other half of the story.

*Harvest moon, 1987. Paid in full: one lamb, black, firstborn of the spring flock. Owed: deferred. Interest accepted. The debt sleeps twelve months.*

*Harvest moon, 1991. Paid: the south pasture, fallowed. It wanted blood. I gave it grass and it sulked but it took it. Owed: deferred. Note—it is learning to wait. Waiting is worse than asking.*

*Harvest moon, 1996. Paid: my own blood, three pints' worth taken across the night. I was sick until Christmas. Owed: deferred. It said her name tonight. It has never said a name before. It said "the girl under the waning moon." I burned salt for an hour after and did not sleep.*

Odile's birthday was September 26th. She'd checked the phase of the moon once, years ago, for no reason she could name—a horoscope app, idle curiosity. Waning crescent. Born under a waning moon, three days past the harvest fullness, when the debt's appetite was at its most exhausted and indulgent. A child born in the slack tide of the thing's hunger. A child it had agreed to wait for, because waiting was the one cruelty it had learned to love.

*Harvest moon, 2003. Paid: the piano. It took the sound out of it first and then the wood went soft as bread. Owed: deferred. Ruthie sent a card for my birthday. First contact in six years. She asked if the girl is happy. I wrote back: she is. I didn't tell her the chair came to my dreams wearing a party hat. Some things are mine to carry alone.*

*Harvest moon, 2009. Paid: forty years of my sight in the left eye, taken in a single night. The optometrist says macular degeneration. The optometrist is wrong. Owed: deferred. I am running out of self to spend. I have begun putting things in order. If you are reading this, girl, I am sorry for the way you found out. I tried to buy you a whole life. I could only afford pieces.*

Odile stopped reading. She sat at the kitchen table with her gloved hands flat on the wood and listened to the house breathe—that faint settling groan in the walls that she had spent two weeks telling herself was plumbing, was wind, was bad insulation, was anything.

Rage was there. She could feel it banked in her ribs like coals: rage at Ruthie, who had signed nothing and promised nothing and run anyway; rage at Dale, who had driven the car; rage at the whole architecture of adults who had decided what she was worth before she drew breath. *We'll send money,* her mother had written, as if the problem were invoicing.

But underneath the rage, inconveniently, was the other thing.

Thirty years. Thirty harvest moons. Her grandmother had fed the well lambs and pasture and piano-wood and pints of her own body, year after year, alone, with neighbors who helped and couldn't help enough, buying Odile one ordinary year at a time. School years. Birthday cakes. A childhood in Ohio with no well in it, no chair, no ledger. Every unremarkable day of Odile's life had been purchased at interest by a woman she hadn't spoken to in eleven years, and the payments had stopped only because the payer had died.

And the last payment had bounced.

Odile looked at the cellar door. It stood closed at the end of the hall, and behind it, below it, the well waited under its cover with the claw marks scored into the inside of the lid—scratches made by something that had been locked in and wanted out, or something that had been knocking politely for a very long time and was done being polite.

"You knew," she said aloud, to the empty kitchen, to the woman who wasn't in it. "You knew my birthday before I did."

The house creaked. It might have been agreement. It might have been hunger.

* * *

The last letter in the bundle was undated, written in a hand gone thin and shaky, the ink pooled where the pen had rested too long between words.

*Girl —*

*If you're reading this, then the interest stopped, which means I stopped, which means it's coming to collect the principal. I'm sorry. I bought you thirty-four years. I'd have bought you eighty if the well took installments that long.*

*Here is what you need to know, and I need you to believe it because I have no one left to tell it true: the debt is real, but debts are contracts, and contracts can be read. Read everything. The ledger, the letters, the marks on the doorframe. It lies the way rivers lie—by being patient. But it keeps honest books. Whatever it promised, it wrote down. Whatever binds it binds you too, and that means the binding runs both directions.*

*Hettie knows the old terms. The Okafors know the salt. Use them. I know you don't like help. I know you get that from me.*

*One more thing. Your mother didn't abandon you to it. She abandoned you to keep you from it, which is a different sin, and maybe a smaller one, and maybe not. I've had thirty years to decide and I still don't know. You'll have to decide for yourself. That's the inheritance I actually left you: the deciding.*

*Don't pay it. Don't run from it. Make it wish it had never opened an account.*

*— Grandma V*

Odile folded the letter along its original creases and held it against her sternum for a moment, an involuntary gesture, the kind of thing people do with letters from the dead.

Outside, the light was failing. On the kitchen table, where she had not put it, where nothing had put it, the extra wooden chair stood facing the cellar door—patient as a creditor, close enough now that she could count the rings in its seat.

Tomorrow the moon would begin to swell.
