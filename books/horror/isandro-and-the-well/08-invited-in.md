## 8. Invited In

The house began speaking at eleven minutes past midnight.

Odile knew the time because she had written it in the ledger's margin, in her grandmother's habit of notation, because if she was going to die in this kitchen she wanted the record to be precise. The moon stood just off full through the window over the sink, swollen and orange as a bruise going septic, and its light came through the glass and lay across the floorboards like something spilled.

Then the walls exhaled.

Not wind—she checked, instinctively, professionally, the way she'd checked everything for thirty-one years. No draft under the door. No gap in the eaves. The sound came from inside the plaster itself, a long slow release of air, and when it finished, the house said her name.

"Odile."

It did not come from any one direction. It came from the grain of the wood, from the nails in the studs, from the old wiring she'd have sworn was dead. It sounded like her grandmother's voice played backward through honey—warm, slowed, wrong in a way that made her back teeth ache.

"I know what you are," Odile said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "You're not a haunting. Hettie told me. You're a negotiator."

The chair at the head of the table creaked, settling its weight, though nothing sat in it.

"Then let us negotiate," the house said.

* * *

She had spent the evening preparing for this, or telling herself she had. Salt lines poured thick along every threshold, iron nails driven into the four corners of the kitchen, the ledger open on the table to the page with her birthday in it, weighted down with a jar of her grandmother's preserved plums because it seemed right that something of Edith Lockridge should hold the paper flat. The Okafors and Hettie were in the barn across the yard, per the plan they'd used for forty years: stay out of the house, keep the lantern lit, come running only if the screaming started.

"Give it what it wants and survive the night," Emeka Okafor had said, pressing a horseshoe into her hand like a communion wafer. "That's all anyone's ever done. That's all we know how to do."

But Odile had never been able to leave a problem unsolved. It was why she was good at her job. It was why her parents had left, maybe—because they'd solved their problem by running, and she'd inherited the part of them that couldn't.

So when the house spoke, she didn't run for the salt. She pulled out the second chair—the ordinary one, the one that belonged—and sat down across from the empty one like a woman opening a bid meeting.

"Talk," she said.

* * *

The house talked.

It started small, the way infestations start. A tick in the wall clock that resolved into rhythm. A settling of the floorboards that resolved into footsteps pacing the length of the hall above her, unhurried, patient, the walk of someone who has waited a very long time and expects to wait longer still. The temperature dropped not like cold but like altitude, like the room had been lifted somewhere thin and high where breath comes hard.

"You are the last of them," the house said. "Edith is ash. Your mother is a stranger in a city with your face on her mantel and no memory of why she flinches at wells. The line ends with you. Do you understand what that means?"

"That you're out of options."

"That *you* are." Something moved behind the cellar door. Not opening it—pressing against it, the way a dog presses its nose to a closed door, wanting only to be let in. "A debt requires two parties. I have held this contract for ninety years because there was always another Lockridge willing to sign. When you are gone, the paper burns itself. And I—" the house paused, and the pause had texture, had hunger in it like a smell, "—I go back to waiting. I am very good at waiting. But I would rather not."

The wet fingerprints appeared on the window beside her, one by one, pressing up from outside against the glass, though the sill was three feet off the ground and the night beyond it held nothing but moonlight and the black shape of the well cover lying where it had dragged itself aside. Small prints. Child-sized. She made herself look at them and made herself think about dermal ridges, pressure distribution, anything with Latin in it.

"What do you want?" she asked.

"The same thing I offered before you were born," the house said gently. "The same thing your parents took."

* * *

And then it showed her.

It didn't speak the offer so much as pour it into the room, into the walls, into the water standing in the sink, until every surface reflected it back at her: a version of her life with the farm cut out of it like a bad tooth.

She saw herself driving away from the county line tomorrow morning with the windows down. She saw the ledger burning in a coffee can on Hettie's porch, pages curling to gray lace. She saw herself back in her apartment above the dry cleaner, back to her route sheet and her bait stations and her customers who called her ma'am and meant it kindly. She saw the memory of this week softening in her mind like a photograph left in the sun—the extra chair fading, the fingerprints drying into nothing, her grandmother's face going smooth and anonymous as any dead stranger's. She saw herself at forty, at fifty, whole and unhaunted and light.

*No debt,* the house murmured through the radiator pipes, through the tap water, through the hum in her own skull. *No harvest. No well. Someone else's granddaughter, some other decade, some other family too tired or too greedy to fight. Not yours. Never yours again.*

"And the cost?" Odile asked. Her hands were flat on the table. She noticed she'd braced them there without deciding to, as if the room might tilt.

"Nothing," the house said, and it said it the way a trap says free cheese. "Only that you forget. Only that you walk out and let the door close behind you and never once look back. Is that not what everyone you have ever loved has done?"

* * *

That was the knife of it, and the house knew exactly where to put the blade.

Because it showed her that too. It opened her up the way she'd open a wall looking for the source of a smell, and it showed her the loneliness it fed on—not as accusation but as inventory, itemized, thorough, professional. Her father's voice on the phone, twice a year, holidays, the conversation lasting four minutes and containing no questions. Her mother's letters that stopped arriving when Odile turned nineteen, and how Odile had told herself she hadn't noticed for eight months. The apartment she kept immaculate because no one else ever touched it. The birthday last March, spent in a motel outside Bakersfield between jobs, eating gas-station cake alone and calling it freedom.

*I have eaten loneliness for a hundred years,* the house said, and its voice went almost tender, almost grandmotherly, and that was the worst thing yet. *I know its flavor better than anyone living. Yours is not the worst I have tasted. But it is deep. It goes all the way down. You built your whole self on top of it—your science, your sarcasm, your little van with your little ladders—and underneath, you are a child who was traded before she could draw breath, and you have known it your entire life without knowing you knew it.*

"Stop," Odile said.

*Come to me,* the house whispered, and the chair at the head of the table slid six inches toward her, wood shrieking softly on wood, and stopped. Waiting. Courteous. *I will take the loneliness out of you like a splinter. You will not even miss it. You will be the first Lockridge in nine decades to leave this farm happy.*

Her eyes were burning. She wiped them with the heel of her hand, furious at the wetness, furious at how precisely it had found her, how cleanly it had mapped the rot under her floorboards. Everything she'd explained away with biology and bad wiring—all of it, her whole armored life—laid open on the table like a carcass she'd been dissecting without ever once looking at what she was cutting.

And beneath the fury, quiet and terrible, temptation. Real temptation. The deal was good. God help her, the deal was *good*. Walk away, forget, live. Let the debt fall on some unborn stranger whose parents would make the same trade hers had, because people always did, because fear always won, because—

Because that was the trick. That was the whole architecture of the thing. It didn't eat families. It ate the moment when a frightened person chose themselves over whoever came next. Ninety years of Lockridges, and every single one of them had stood exactly where she stood, hearing exactly this voice, and every one had found the arithmetic persuasive.

Her grandmother had spent thirty years paying interest on a debt that wasn't hers, buying time for a granddaughter she'd met perhaps six times, out of guilt or love or both, and had died alone in this kitchen with jars of plums and a ledger full of other people's sins.

Odile looked at the empty chair.

"No," she said.

*Think—*

"I said no." She stood up. Her legs shook and she let them; shaking wasn't weakness, shaking was current, and she needed every volt she had. "Here's my counteroffer. You don't get my loneliness. You don't get my forgetting. And you don't get some kid in 2061 whose parents panic. This contract dies tonight, with me sitting right here, and I'm not leaving this kitchen until the sun does its job or you do yours."

The house went silent.

Then, all around her, slowly, the wet fingerprints began to climb the walls—dozens of them, hundreds, rising from the floorboards toward the ceiling like something ascending a staircase only it could see—and the chair at the head of the table turned, deliberately, to face her.

"You wish to renegotiate," the house said, and the warmth drained out of its voice, and what remained was older than the farm, older than the county, older than the word *harvest*. "Very well, Odile Lockridge. Show me your terms."

She picked up the ledger. She picked up the paring knife from the drying rack, the one with the wooden handle worn smooth by her grandmother's thumb.

Outside, far off across the fields, a rooster that shouldn't have been crowing yet made a confused, premature sound at the orange moon, and Odile drew the blade across her left palm and said:

"Clause one."
