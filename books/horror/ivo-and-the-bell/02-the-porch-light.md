## 2. The Porch Light

The drive home took eleven minutes and Mara spent all of them arguing with himself.

It was a prank. It had to be a prank. Somebody in the building had a sick sense of humor, or Doris Halloway had a daughter with her mother's phone records and a grudge against the station, or—best case—he'd dreamed the whole call. He did the show live from midnight to three, five nights a week, twenty-two years on the air, and he knew better than anyone how the mind behaved at that hour. It went soft at the edges. It filled gaps. He'd once interviewed a caller for six minutes before realizing the man was reciting lines from a movie.

But Doris hadn't been reciting anything. Doris had asked about his sinuses, because she always asked about his sinuses, because she'd heard him sniffle through an entire February in 2019 and had never let it go. She'd mentioned the frost coming early this year. She'd said, *You sound tired, sweetheart. You should eat something green.* And then she'd said goodnight in that particular way of hers, the way she signed off every call: *I'll be listening.*

He pulled into his spot behind the house and sat with the engine ticking down. The porch light was on.

That was wrong. He killed that light every morning when he left for the station. He'd done it that morning; he could almost feel the switch under his thumb, the little plastic click, the way he did it without thinking, the way he did everything without thinking, which was more or less the organizing principle of his life.

The light was on, and under it, sitting on the top step with his hands folded in his lap like a child waiting to be picked up from school, was a small figure in a patched coat.

Mara's body made the decision before his mind caught up. He was out of the car, keys in his fist, one key extended between two fingers like a knife—the way they taught you in some article he'd half-read—and then he stopped, ten feet from the steps, because the figure turned its head and smiled at him, and thirty years fell away like a coat slipping off a shoulder.

"Ivo," he said. His voice came out wrong. Young.

"Hello, Mara." The same voice. That was the thing that broke something loose in his chest—the same voice, unchanged, unaged, still carrying that faint lilt of patience, as if Ivo had all the time in the world and had never once resented spending any of it. The same patched coat, brown wool gone soft at the elbows, the elbow patches themselves patched. The same cowlick that no amount of invisible combing ever fixed. The same face, round-cheeked, seven years old forever, looking up at him now from the top step with an expression of mild, bottomless contentment.

"You're not real," Mara said.

"No," Ivo agreed pleasantly. "I'm not."

"That's—" Mara's laugh came out cracked. "Okay. Good. Great. So you're a hallucination. Stress. The Doris thing today has me—" He stopped. "How do you know about Doris?"

Ivo tilted his head, and the porch light caught his eyes, and there was something in them that had not been there when Mara was seven. Or maybe it had been there all along and he simply hadn't known what he was looking at. Something old looking out through a child's face, patient as sediment.

"You stopped answering," Ivo said.

Mara's mouth went dry. "I was twelve."

"The count doesn't stop."

The words landed with a weight completely out of proportion to their size. Six syllables. They settled over the yard like frost forming, silent and total. Somewhere down the block a dog started barking and then thought better of it.

"What count?" Mara heard himself ask, and hated the question even as he asked it, because part of him—the seven-year-old part, the part that had invented this creature out of loneliness and then discovered, one terrible wonderful night, that invention worked both ways—already knew. Had always known. Had filed it away under things too large to think about and built a whole adult life on top of the lid.

Ivo just looked at him. Smiled. Folded his small hands more neatly in his lap.

"You can't sit on my porch," Mara said. "It's three in the morning. My neighbors—"

"It's cold," Ivo said, agreeably. "Frost tonight. First one."

And it was true; Mara could see his breath now, could see the grass silvering at the edges of the light, and the sight of it made his skin crawl in a way he couldn't name yet, wouldn't name until later, lying awake with the covers pulled to his chin.

"Go away," he said, and it came out smaller than he meant. "Please. Whatever you are. Go away."

Ivo's smile didn't waver, but something in it changed—a softening, a sadness, the way a parent looks at a child insisting the dark is empty. "You used to save me the window seat," he said. "On the bus. You told everyone I was your cousin."

"I was a kid."

"Yes," Ivo said. "You were. You were the best kind of kid." He unfolded his hands and stood, and he was so small, barely four feet of patched coat and cowlick, and somehow the yard felt fuller with him standing than it had with him sitting. "I'll wait here. I'm good at waiting. You know that."

"I'm calling the police."

"They'll see the porch light," Ivo said, "and an empty step." He turned toward the street, then paused, and over his shoulder he added, gently, the way you'd remind someone of an umbrella on a rainy day: "They'll want to know why you left it on, Mara. After all these years. Some habits are promises."

Then he walked down the walk and out past the fence line and the frost, and he didn't so much disappear as become difficult to look at, the way a star is when clouds come. The porch light buzzed. The dog down the block tried barking again, once, and gave up.

* * *

Mara locked the door. Then the deadbolt. Then the chain he'd never used, whose purpose he'd forgotten until his fingers found it by touch. He stood in his own hallway breathing like a man who'd run a distance he hadn't.

The house was the same house. Same sagging couch, same dishes in the rack, same stack of unopened mail he kept meaning to sort. Everything exactly where he'd left it that afternoon, and yet the whole place had rearranged itself around the fact of what was outside, the way furniture looks different after a funeral.

He checked the window twice. The porch light burned on. The step was empty.

*Empty step,* he thought. *An empty step and a light left on for thirty years.*

He made tea he didn't drink. He sat on the couch with his phone in his hand and scrolled to Nell's contact and typed *you up?* and deleted it. Typed *weird night at work* and deleted that too. What would he even say? *Hey sis, remember when I had an imaginary friend? He's on my porch. He hasn't aged. He knows about the frost.* She'd hear it in his voice anyway, even over text, the crack running through the middle of him. She'd drive up from Cedar Falls with the kids and find him sitting in the dark talking about a boy in a patched coat, and she'd start making calls, the gentle kind, the kind with waiting rooms attached.

So he told no one. He put the phone face-down on the cushion like closing a casket.

Here was the thing he couldn't explain to anyone, the thing he could barely admit to himself while the tea went cold in his hands: underneath the terror, underneath the animal certainty that the thing on his porch was wrong the way a stopped clock is wrong, ran a current of something else. Something warm and shameful. Because when Ivo had said *hello, Mara*, some locked room in him had lit up like a window at dusk. Because for one stupid second, standing in his own frosted yard, forty-one years old and alone in the way he'd engineered his whole life to be alone, Mara had felt *found*.

That was the horror of it. Not the dead caller, not the impossible agelessness. The horror was the homesickness. Thirty years since he'd sat with that small presence in the crook of the stairwell while his parents fought below, thirty years since a voice only he could hear had said *it's alright, I've got the watch tonight*—and some cellar of him had missed it every single day, the way you miss a limb, the way you miss a language you were never allowed to keep speaking.

*I didn't abandon you,* he wanted to say. *I grew up. That's what you do. That's what's supposed to happen.*

But he'd said it out loud, that last time. Age twelve, in the schoolyard, to a circle of boys who needed proof he wasn't a baby. *Ivo isn't real. I made him up. He's nothing.* And he'd felt something go out of the world like a breath snuffed between wet fingers, and he'd told himself for thirty years that what he felt was relief.

The clock on the microwave said 4:17. Through the front window, the porch light burned steady over its empty step.

Mara slept in his clothes on top of the covers, with the TV on low, volume a number instead of silence. At some point he surfaced from a shallow sleep and the static between infomercials had shaped itself, briefly, unmistakably, into a hummed tune. Four notes up, two notes down. A tune from a stairwell, from a bus seat saved for a cousin nobody else could see.

He lay in the dark with his heart going hard and told himself: prank, stress, dream, prank, stress, dream.

Outside, the first frost of the year wrote itself across the glass of every window in the house, fine and white and even, except on the kitchen window, where the frost came in lines. Short strokes. Grouped in fives.

Like someone keeping count.
