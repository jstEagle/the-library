## 9. Rope and Bell

The house had always been wrong and Mara had never let himself know it.

He saw it now, standing in the hall with frost on his eyelashes and Nell's taillights finally—finally—swallowed by the road south. The wallpaper repeated a pattern that wasn't quite a pattern, vines that grew a little further up the wall each time he looked away and back. The staircase climbed to a second floor that was one step longer than it should have been. And under his feet, beneath the floorboards where no cellar had ever been drawn on any deed or blueprint, something was tolling.

Not ringing. Tolling. Slow as a heart in a body that was dying politely.

"You knew," Mara said.

Ivo stood at the end of the hall where the coat rack should have been, patched coat, patient smile, thirty years unaged. "You knew too," Ivo said. "Children always know. That's why they invent us. Not because they're lonely." He tilted his head. "Because they can see the doors."

The floor opened like a throat.

Mara went down without deciding to. Stairs that hadn't existed carried him in a spiral, past walls of packed earth threaded with roots, roots threaded with hair, and the tolling grew until it wasn't sound anymore but pressure, a hand closing around his ribs from the inside. At the bottom was a room shaped like the inside of a bell.

And there was the counter-thing.

It sat—or coiled, or knelt; his eyes kept refusing to finish the sentence—in the center of the earthen floor, vast and gray as weather. It had no face, only a place where faces went, a smooth depression like a thumbprint in dough, and across its whole slow body were marks. Tally marks. Thousands of them, cut into it like scars, and each one was a name. He could feel that without being told. Each stroke was someone who had been counted at first frost and found still breathing, still owing, still *available*.

Doris Halloway's name was not among them. Neither was the man whose death Mara had read aloud last winter. The counted-and-unclaimed got no marks. They just drifted, calling radio stations, humming a tune from 1987, waiting for someone to say their names back to them.

"Before the final pattern," Mara said. His voice came out small in all that earth. "That's what your callers kept saying. This is it. The final pattern."

"The count reaches whole households," Ivo said softly, behind him. "Your mother's street. Your sister's road south. It loops. You saw it loop. Nothing leaves once the frost writes their window." A pause, gentle as snowfall. "Unless the count is paid."

Mara turned. Ivo was holding out the rope.

It was nothing like a bell rope. It was braided from things he recognized—the yellow cord of his childhood walkie-talkies, the drawstring of the coat he'd outgrown, a shoelace, a length of Christmas ribbon, thirty years of small tied-off things—and it ran upward into darkness, to a bell he couldn't see but could feel, hanging somewhere above the counter-thing like a verdict.

"I rang it for four hundred years," Ivo said. "Before you. Before the others. Children invented me in eleven countries, did you know that? The lonely ones. The ones who could see doors. They made me so the thing below would look at me instead of them." His smile didn't waver, but something behind it did. "That was the arrangement. A ringer stands between. A ringer is seen, so the household doesn't have to be."

"And if the ringer quits," Mara said. "If the ringer gets pretended away by a twelve-year-old who wanted to be normal—"

"Then the count goes looking for what it was promised." Ivo's voice stayed level. Patient. Always patient; that had been the worst part, even now, even here. "I never blamed you, Mara. I want you to understand that before anything else happens. I waited thirty years and I never blamed you. Because there is one way out of this, one rule older than me, and I have been waiting to see if you would find it."

"Tell me."

"I may be replaced," Ivo said, "only by someone who loves me enough to take the rope."

* * *

The room got very quiet. Even the counter-thing stilled, its tally-scarred bulk going smooth and attentive, the faceless hollow of its head tilting toward them both.

"That's not love," Mara said. "That's a hostage trade dressed up in nicer words."

"It's the oldest kind of love there is," Ivo said. "The kind that stands in front. Your mother stood in front of you at the bus stop when the dogs got loose. Your sister stood in front of you in the flood year. Love isn't a feeling, Mara. Feelings are what people have instead of standing anywhere." He extended the rope another inch. "I am asking you to stand somewhere."

"And then what? I ring the bell and the count takes me and Nell's kids grow up telling stories about their uncle the dead man?"

"No." For the first time in thirty years, Ivo's patience cracked—not into anger, but into something rawer, almost pleading. "You ring the bell and speak your true name into the count. Not *Mara Vane*, the name on the deed of a house that was never yours, the name on the license, the name on the air. Your true name. The one you were called before anyone called you anything. The count cannot hold what it cannot name, and it cannot name what gives itself freely. That is the final pattern. Every ringer before you gave themselves under compulsion, and so every ringer was held. Give yourself freely—" his voice caught, an ancient hinge rusting open, "—and the claim transfers clean. I go free. You stay. Seen. Counted. Belonging. All the things you've been starving for since you were seven years old and decided wanting them was shameful."

Mara stared at him. "You've known my true name this whole time."

"I've known everything about you this whole time." Ivo's smile trembled at the edges. "That's what I'm *for*. I was made out of your seeing. You think I don't know what you called yourself when you played alone in the yard? You think I wasn't listening?"

The counter-thing shifted. Somewhere above, unseen, the bell hummed in sympathy—a low, hungry note, like a dog hearing its name.

Down the spiral stairs, faint and impossible, Mara heard a radio. His own show. His own voice, signing off: *that's the hour, friends, drive safe, leave the porch light on.* The broadcast from the studio that burned in 1994. The loop, still looping, waiting to see what he did.

His family was on a highway that bent back on itself. His mother was in a care facility with frost writing tallies on her window. Doris Halloway was somewhere in the static, humming, waiting for someone to say her name kindly.

Seven years of rational explanations. Thirty years of pretending. And underneath all of it, one small boy in a backyard saying to the empty air, *you're real, you're my friend, nobody else can see you,* and meaning it as the proudest secret of his life.

He stepped forward and took the rope.

It was warm. Of course it was warm. It had been held for centuries by someone who never once put it down.

"Ivo," Mara said. "Look at me."

Ivo looked. And Mara understood, finally, what the rule really was—not a mechanism, not a bargain clause, but this: that the thing counting them all had built mercy into its own arithmetic, one loophole wide enough for a choice. Ivo had spent thirty years not haunting him, not punishing him, not even hurrying him. Just sitting on porches. Just waiting to be chosen freely, by the one person whose choosing would cost something.

"I'm sorry I pretended you away," Mara said. "I didn't imagine you. I betrayed you. Those are different sins and I served time for the wrong one." He wrapped the rope twice around his fist. "And I'm not doing this because the count needs paying. I'm doing it because you waited thirty years in the cold for me and never once knocked. Somebody should have left the light on for *you*."

Ivo made a sound.

It took Mara a moment to identify it, because nothing in the folklore, nothing in the callers, nothing in the tally-marked dark had prepared him for it. Ivo was weeping. Four hundred years of ringing, eleven countries of invented children, centuries of standing between—and the tears came now, silent and enormous, cutting clean lines down a face that had never once changed, and where they fell, the earthen floor bloomed. Small white flowers. Frost-flowers. First-of-the-season flowers.

"Oh," Ivo said, and it was the smallest word Mara had ever heard. "Oh, you *do*."

"Always did," Mara said. "Bad at showing it. Family trait."

He pulled the rope.

* * *

The bell did not make a sound so much as it made a decision.

The tolling stopped—the counter-thing's slow dying-heart rhythm cut off mid-beat—and then the bell above spoke once, a note so deep and total that Mara felt it in his teeth, his bones, the fillings in his teeth, the water in the well three counties over. The tally marks on the counter-thing's body began to lift, peeling off it like scabs, rising into the air as strokes of light, and each stroke became a name spoken in a voice like frost forming, and the names were released—one, two, hundreds, thousands—dissolving upward through the earth, through the floorboards, through the roof of the house that was never his, going home.

Then it was his turn.

The count turned to him. The faceless hollow focused, and Mara felt himself being read, catalogued, the pen hovering over the line. *Mara Vane,* it began to write—

"No," Mara said, and his voice filled the bell-shaped room like the bell's own note. "That's the borrowed one. Here's mine."

He spoke the name from the backyard. The name from age seven, shouted at the sky over a game only two players knew the rules to, the name no adult had ever used, the name Ivo had answered to and answered *for*: his true name, given freely, spoken into the count like a coin dropped into a well.

The pen stopped.

The claim transferred. He felt it happen—not pain, not cold, but a settling, the way a house settles at night, the way a coat fits after years of wearing. He was seen. Completely, terribly, permanently seen, by the thing that counts, and it did not devour him. It simply marked him present. Accounted for. *Belonging.*

The counter-thing folded itself closed like a book at the end of winter, and was gone, and the room was just a room, and the rope in Mara's hands went slack and ordinary and smelled like dust and ribbon.

Ivo stood in front of him, tear-tracks still shining, and for the first time in four hundred years his hands were empty.

"Well," Ivo said, wobbling somewhere between laughter and more weeping. "What do you do in the mornings?"

"I sleep till noon and regret it," Mara said. "Come on. I'll show you the studio. You'll hate the music."

They climbed the stairs together, and behind them, faint and fading, the static in the walls shaped itself—for just a moment—into a familiar hum. Not a warning. A lullaby. The tune from 1987, sung by everyone who had ever been counted and not claimed, on their way home at last.

At the top of the stairs, the front door stood open to the frost, and on the porch, in the cold, the porch light was already on.
