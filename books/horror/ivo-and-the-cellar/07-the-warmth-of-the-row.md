## 7. The Warmth of the Row

The trestle tables went up on the green the way they had gone up every October for as long as anyone could remember—though no one could remember exactly how long, which was its own kind of tradition in Coldharbour Row.

Ellen Marsh arrived first, as she always did, with a crate of apples from the vicarage garden and the particular smile she wore for feast days. She set the crate down beside Mrs. Aldery's chair—the old woman was already installed at the head of the long table like a figurehead, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate—and stood a moment watching the village assemble itself around her.

They came out of their houses all at once, the way they always did. Not gradually, not straggling. All at once, as if a door somewhere had opened and let them through. The Pikes with their pies. The Wickers with Tom's cider, pressed that morning, cloudy and sweet. The Hollis children running circles until their mother caught one by the collar and pointed him at the table. Dev Okonkwo carrying folding chairs two at a time, sleeves rolled, calling out greetings to people whose names he'd only learned last spring and now knew as if he'd been born to them.

And Marguerite Halloran, coming down the lane from the old house with a covered dish held carefully in both hands, walking like someone approaching an altar.

*There*, Ellen thought, and felt the warmth move through her chest, the warmth that had lived there for thirty years now, banked and steady as a stove. *There they are.*

She had been received on a night much like this one. Harvest, 1994. She remembered the taste of the cider, the weight of the toast cup in her hands, the way the whole village had turned toward her at once—not menacing, never menacing, but *attentive*, the way a field is attentive to rain. She had been lonely then, newly posted to a parish that frightened her predecessors into early retirement, and when the warmth came she had wept into her sleeve with gratitude.

She still did, some nights. That was the part no one warned you about: it kept working. You didn't build up a tolerance. Every inclusion landed fresh.

"Vicar." Mrs. Aldery's voice, dry as seed husks. "You're staring."

"I'm admiring," Ellen said. "It's a fine evening for it."

"It's a fine evening for what it's for." The old woman's eyes, pale as skimmed milk, found Marguerite across the green. "Twelve years she's sat at the end of these tables. Look where they've put her tonight."

Ellen looked. The center seat. The place of honor, between Mrs. Aldery herself and the toast cup. Someone—no one would say who—had laid a garland of rowan berries down the table's length, ending at Marguerite's place like an arrow.

"She'll be received soon," Ellen said softly. "Won't she."

Mrs. Aldery said nothing. But her hand, resting on the table, curled slowly closed around nothing at all, and Ellen saw it, and pretended she hadn't, because pretending was also a tradition in Coldharbour Row, and a very old one.

* * *

The feast began the way feasts begin everywhere, which is to say it began nowhere in particular. Soup. Bread. Tom Wicker's cider going around in jugs. The Hollis boy dropped a plate and everyone laughed and the laughing went on a half-second longer than laughing usually does, a warm sustained note, and nobody noticed but Dev, who noticed everything lately and liked it less each day.

Marguerite sat in the center of the table and could not eat.

It wasn't the food. The food was good—Annette Pike's soup was genuinely excellent, and Marguerite said so, and meant it, and hated how easily the words came now. It was the attention. It came off the villagers like heat off a kiln wall. When Marguerite lifted a spoon, forty faces tracked it. When Marguerite laughed—a real laugh, startled out of them by something Tom Wicker said about the bishop—forty faces brightened as though lit from within, and the brightness did not fade afterward. It stayed. They stayed lit.

"You've gone quiet," said Annette Pike, leaning in on Marguerite's left. Her breath smelled of cider and nutmeg. "Eat, love. You're too thin. You've been too thin twelve years and I've said so twelve years and now I'm sitting next to you so I can finally do something about it."

She ladled more soup into Marguerite's bowl without asking. Her hand lingered near Marguerite's wrist a moment longer than serving required. The touch was warm. Everything here was warm. Marguerite had spent twelve years keeping the village at arm's length and the arm had grown tired, and now the warmth poured against them like water against a dam with a hairline crack, finding every seam.

"To Marguerite!" called Tom Wicker, standing, jug raised. "Twelve years among us and finally where they belong!"

The toast went up around the whole table, forty voices, one voice, and Marguerite heard it happen again—that fusion, the way the separate sounds braided into a single tone with a strange harmonic underneath it, low and round, a note that belonged to no throat present.

A bell-shaped note.

Marguerite drank because refusing would have been worse. The cider tasted of apples and, beneath the apples, faintly of bronze.

* * *

Dev had checked the ledger twice. Then a third time, sober, by daylight, with his reading glasses on and a ruler under each line, because the second time he'd been sure he'd made an error and the error was impossible and therefore must be his.

It wasn't his.

He'd transcribed the names onto a sheet of archive paper in his neat historian's hand: entry dates, death dates, the interval between. He'd done the arithmetic twice per name, once forward and once backward, the way you check subtraction. Ambrose Pike, entered 1747, died 1759. Twelve years. Sarah Hollis, entered 1761, died 1773. Twelve years. Josiah Wicker, 1802–1814. Twelve. On and on, down the columns, generation after generation, the interval holding constant as gravity. Not approximately twelve. Not "around" twelve. Twelve years to the month, in most cases. To the *day*, in several.

Everyone marked *received* died exactly twelve years after their entry.

Except Mrs. Aldery, entered 1911, marked *interrupted*, still alive at ninety-something and looking, Dev thought now, watching her across the table, neither alive nor dead but *kept*, like bread in a tin, like a letter in a drawer.

And except the newest entries. Except the column that had begun, twelve years ago, with a name inked in a hand that matched no other in the ledger:

*Marguerite Halloran — received (tendering).*

Dev had drunk more cider than usual. He wasn't sure whether that was courage or cowardice. He watched Marguerite at the center of the table being loved so hard it looked like weather, and he turned his cup, and he waited for the toast to die down, and then he leaned across the garland of rowan berries until his mouth was close to Marguerite's ear.

"I checked it twice," he whispered. "Three times. Marguerite. Listen to me."

Marguerite turned. Their eyes were the eyes of someone who has been drowning politely for weeks and has just seen another face under the water.

"The ledger," Dev said, very quietly, under the noise of the feast, under Tom Wicker striking up a song on a fiddle that no one remembered buying. "Every person marked *received*. I ran the dates. Entry to death. Every single one."

"How long?" Marguerite asked. No preamble. No *what are you talking about*. They knew. Dev could see that they knew; they'd known since the footprints in the dust, maybe since before, since the noises in the cottage they'd catalogued like survey reports for twelve years rather than think about.

"Twelve years," Dev said. "Exactly. To the day, sometimes. Ambrose Pike, 1747 to 1759. Sarah Hollis, 1761 to 1773. It doesn't vary. It's never varied in three hundred years. Marguerite—" His voice cracked and he took a breath and steadied it, because half the table was watching them now, smiling, fond, patient. "Your entry is dated twelve years ago this month."

The fiddle played. The children danced. Somewhere down the table Annette Pike refilled a bowl, and Mrs. Aldery sat at the head like a stone at the center of a ring of water, and the light came off forty faces and did not fade.

Marguerite looked at their own hands on the table. Turned them over slowly, palms up, as though checking for a date stamped there.

"That's why the noise started," they said. Almost to themselves. "When I arrived. That's why it stopped being deniable this autumn." A pause. "It's not a welcome. It's a countdown."

"I don't know what it is," Dev said, which was a lie, because he knew exactly what it was, he simply couldn't say the word *harvest* out loud at a feast without hearing how it fit. "But nobody's ever gotten out of it. Three hundred years, Marguerite. Nobody."

Across the table, Mrs. Aldery raised her cup to them—an old woman's slow salute—and drank, and over the rim her milk-pale eyes held something that in any other village, at any other table, Dev would have unhesitatingly named as pity.

In this village he named it warning.

* * *

The feast wound down the way it had begun: all at once. One moment the tables were full and singing; the next, the plates were cleared and the villagers were drifting homeward in loose warm clusters, calling goodnights that overlapped into a single chord. The Hollis children were carried sleeping on shoulders. Tom Wicker tucked his fiddle under his arm and clapped Dev on the back hard enough to stagger him. "Good feast," Tom said. "Best in years. She's happy tonight." And he nodded up at the church tower, black against the stars, where no bell hung and no bell had hung since 1911, and walked home whistling.

Ellen Marsh stacked cups with the efficiency of thirty years' practice. She paused beside Marguerite, who hadn't moved from the center seat.

"You'll come to supper Sunday," she said. Not quite a question. "After the service. There's things it's time you and I talked about."

"What things?"

Ellen smiled her feast-day smile. "Belonging things," she said, and gathered her crate of apples, and went.

The green emptied. Lanterns guttered. Dev helped fold the trestles, and neither of them spoke, and the silence between them had changed shape—it was no longer the polite distance of surveyor and historian but the close quarters of two people in a small boat, listening to the water come in.

At the edge of the green, Mrs. Aldery rose from her chair of honor, unhurried, joints cracking like green wood. She crossed to where Marguerite stood and stopped close enough that Marguerite could smell the shawl—wool and camphor and, beneath it, that bronze note again, faint as a memory of a memory.

"Enjoyed your feast, did you?" she said.

"They were very kind."

"Mm." The old woman looked up at the dark tower. "Kindness is the cheapest thing we grow here. It comes up every year, like the apples." She turned those pale eyes on Marguerite, and for a moment—just a moment—her voice dropped its crust of age and sounded young underneath, a child's voice, 1911, standing in a cellar watching her grandmother lift a rope. "Ask me sometime what happens at the end of the twelve years. Ask me what I saw interrupted." She patted Marguerite's hand once, gently, the way you pat a doorframe in passing. "Or don't ask. It changes nothing either way. The bell keeps its own calendar."

Then she walked home alone down the middle of the empty road, upright as a candle, and every lantern on the Row flickered once as she passed, all together, like a held breath.

Dev came to stand beside Marguerite in the cold. Above them the church tower stood silent against more stars than the sky should have held.

"Twelve years this month," Dev said quietly.

Above the tower, clouds were moving in from the west, fast, in straight lines, arranged—though Dev would spend the walk home telling himself he hadn't seen it—in rings.
