## 4. Grandmother's Ledger

The estate took three weekends to sort and would have taken one if Ulyana hadn't kept stopping.

That was the thing about grief nobody warned you about: it wasn't a wave, it was friction. Every object in Odile Delacroix's house had a coefficient of resistance, and Ulyana had to drag each one across their attention before they could put it in a box. The good china—donate. The coats—there was a shelter on Delancey that took winter wear. The photograph albums—keep, obviously, though Ulyana hadn't opened them yet and wasn't sure when they'd be able to.

They worked in the kitchen because the mirrors in the hallway were still wrong.

It had been eleven days since Harrow House. Eleven days since the brass key on its faded ribbon, since the tunnels that weren't on any survey, since the moment in the dark when their flashlight beam seemed to arrive a half-second after they moved—a stutter, an echo, nothing, surely nothing. They'd told themselves it was adrenaline. They'd told themselves a lot of things.

The mirror lag was harder to explain away. At home, every reflective surface ran one second behind. Ulyana had tested it with a stopwatch held up to the bathroom glass: raise your hand, watch the reflection raise its hand, count the gap. One second. Every time. Phone cameras showed nothing unusual—the video of the mirror looked perfectly normal, which meant either the camera couldn't see it or the mirror knew when it was being recorded, and both of those options were insane, so Ulyana had stopped filming and started covering mirrors with bath towels like a superstitious grandmother.

Like Odile.

The thought arrived uninvited, the way thoughts about the dead do. Because here was the thing Ulyana had found, two days into sorting the estate, folded inside a box of linens: a newspaper clipping from 1981 about a woman in Vermont who claimed her mirrors were showing her rooms from her childhood. And in the margin, in Odile's small precise hand: *She covered hers too.*

Ulyana had sat down on the stairs with that clipping for twenty minutes.

Odile Delacroix had been the least superstitious person Ulyana ever knew. She'd been a bookkeeper for forty years—double-entry, balanced to the penny, allergic to anything she couldn't verify. She'd taught Ulyana card tricks as a child and then immediately explained how each one worked, because *wonder is fine, sweetheart, but knowing is better.* She'd rolled her eyes at horoscopes, at prayer, at the town's little autumn festival with its unnamed memorial table. When Ulyana told her, at fourteen, that they wanted to explore abandoned buildings, Odile had said only: *Take photos. Photos don't lie.*

Photos don't lie. That had become the whole creed. If Ulyana couldn't photograph it, file it, map it, it didn't go in the ledger of what was real.

And now their own apartment's mirrors were running late, and their dead grandmother had left them a note about a woman who covered hers.

* * *

The ledger turned up on the third Saturday, at the bottom of a cedar chest in the back bedroom, under a quilt that smelled like forty years of mothballs and lavender.

It was a household ledger—the old-fashioned kind, cloth-bound, columns ruled in faint blue. *Delacroix* embossed on the spine in gold leaf gone dull. Ulyana recognized it instantly; Odile had kept ones like it all her life, logging groceries and utility bills and the cost of roof repairs in her tiny upright figures. There were probably a dozen of them in the attic of the house on Corben Street where Ulyana had grown up.

This one was different in exactly two ways.

The first way was the cover. Ulyana opened it expecting grocery columns and instead found, just inside the front board, written directly on the endpaper in pencil, in letters that wobbled and grew:

**HARVEST**

A child's hand. Ulyana knew children's handwriting when they saw it—the oversized capitals, the uneven pressure, the way the R leaned like it was running somewhere. It wasn't Odile's. It wasn't anyone adult's. Someone had taken this careful, grown-up book of accounts and written one word across the inside cover in the handwriting of someone maybe eight or nine years old.

HARVEST. Capital letters. Pressed hard enough to dent the page beneath.

Ulyana sat very still on the bedroom floor. Outside, October wind moved through the maples, and the light in the room went gold and then dim and then gold again, and none of that required explanation.

The second way the ledger was different was the pages.

Household ledgers are chronological. You can feel the year moving through them—winter bills heavier than summer ones, the little spike around holidays. This one began in January 1974, ordinary as anything: flour, lamp oil, postage stamps, a payment to a Mr. Voss for "fence work" that seemed expensive for fence work. February. March. Odile's hand steady as a metronome.

Then, between the entry for April 9th and the entry for April 12th, the book changed.

Someone had cut pages out. Not torn—cut, with a razor or a very thin blade, flush against the spine, so cleanly that at first glance the ledger simply skipped, as if those days had never happened. Ulyana counted. Eleven leaves removed. Twenty-two sides of paper, gone. Whatever Odile had recorded between April 9th and April 12th, 1974, she—or someone—had decided the world should not read it.

The entries resumed on April 12th, and something in them had shifted. Not the handwriting—that stayed perfect. The content.

*April 12 — Paid the butcher. Do not go back to the house. Burned my good coat.*

*April 15 — E. came by. Told him we are done and he agreed too quickly. I do not trust quick agreement. I have not slept.*

*April 19 — Wrote to no one. There is no one to write to. We all signed the same silence.*

*May 2 — Bought new curtains for the front room. The room does not need curtains. I bought them anyway. A house should look lived in from the street.*

*May 30 — The well water tastes sweet again. I cannot decide if that is a mercy.*

Ulyana read these standing up, then sitting down, then standing again, because some part of their body kept trying to leave. *We all signed the same silence.* Signed what? Who was E.? What house? What had happened in the eleven missing days that required burning a coat?

And underneath all of it, quieter, the question that actually mattered, the one Ulyana kept pushing down like a ball in water:

*Why did my grandmother keep this?*

You razor out the pages if you want the record clean. But you keep the book. You keep the book and you hide it in a cedar chest under a quilt, and you live another fifty years, and you die at eighty-nine in a hospital bed with your affairs in impeccable order, and among those affairs you leave behind a mutilated ledger with a child's word dented into its cover.

That's not destruction. That's evidence storage.

* * *

Ulyana did what they always did with things that frightened them: they made tea, they spread everything out on the kitchen table under good light, and they photographed it.

Every page. Front cover, back cover, the endpaper with HARVEST, each surviving entry, close-ups of the cut edges of the stubs where the pages had been removed. The camera did its honest work. In the photos, the ledger looked exactly like what it was—an old book, slightly damaged, full of mundane entries and a few strange ones. No lag. No rooms that shouldn't exist. Photographing the ledger felt like holding onto a railing.

It was while reviewing the photos that evening that Ulyana noticed the indentation.

Page 47, the entry for June 3rd, 1974. Standard fare—eggs, sugar, a repair to the porch step. But photographed at an angle, raked by lamplight, the page showed ghost-grooves above the ink. Writing pressed through from a sheet that was no longer there—one of the razored pages, or a page that had once lain atop this one. Ulyana got a soft graphite stick and a sheet of tracing paper and, hands not entirely steady, rubbed the impression the way they'd learned doing rubbings of gravestones as a kid.

Letters surfaced. Fragments only. The pressure had been light and the paper fibrous.

*...the tithe is not money...*

*...six families, six names, one child...*

*...he trusted me. God forgive me, he trusted me and I held his hand the whole way down...*

Ulyana put the tracing paper down and walked to the sink and stood there a long time, water running over their wrists, cold as far as the tap could make it.

*A child.* Six families. One child. Held his hand the whole way down.

Down where? Down where, Odile?

Their grandmother—who had checked their bike helmet straps twice, who had sat up all night when Ulyana had the flu at nine, who had smelled of lavender and pencil shavings and had never once raised her voice—had written, in her own immaculate hand, about taking a child somewhere down.

The skeptic in Ulyana did its job. It offered alternatives. Metaphor, maybe—religious language, some church thing, the town was old and Catholic enough. Or a game, a ritual kids played, and Odile writing about it later with borrowed gravity. Or fiction; Odile had read a lot of novels; people wrote strange things in private books. The skeptic laid these out neatly, like tools on a drop cloth.

But the skeptic had also spent eleven days explaining a one-second mirror lag, and its credibility was shot.

* * *

There was one more thing in the cedar chest, and Ulyana almost missed it, because it wasn't hidden at all. That was the trick of it, Ulyana realized later—hide a thing under a quilt and everyone digs; leave a thing in plain sight and it becomes furniture.

A school composition notebook. The cheap marbled kind, black-and-white, spiral broken on one side. On the cover, in pencil, in the same wobbly capitals as the endpaper: a name.

Not Ivo. Ulyana didn't know any name yet. The cover said **MY BOOK** and below it, smaller, *for keeping things that are mine.*

Inside was a boy's schoolwork. Fractions, half finished, the numbers marching downhill across the page. A spelling list: *believe, harvest, orchard, promise, cellar, remember.* He'd gotten *remember* wrong twice—*rember*, *remermber*—and then right, and next to the right version someone had drawn a small star in red ink. A teacher's star. An encouraging one.

And drawings. Crayon drawings on the backs of worksheets. A house with too many windows, all of them dark except one. A row of stick figures holding hands—six tall ones and one short one—and the short one drawn again, separately, larger, with careful attention paid to his shoes. A tunnel. Of course a tunnel; kids draw tunnels; kids draw caves and worms and mines. But this tunnel was drawn from the inside looking out, a small bright rectangle at the far end, and the crayon strokes around it were pressed so hard the paper had buckled.

On the last used page, in that same laborious hand, a single line, no drawing, dated in the corner—October 1974—in a child's approximation of a date format:

*i am not sposed to forget but i forget a little more every day and thats how they win*

Ulyana closed the notebook with both hands, gently, the way you close something sleeping.

They sat in their grandmother's kitchen until the light failed completely, and they did not turn on the lamp, because the window over the sink was growing dark and glassy and they did not particularly want to see what it reflected, or how promptly.

Here was the shape of it, whether they consented to the shape or not: their grandmother had helped do something to a child in 1974. Something involving six families and a word—harvest—written in that child's own hand. Something the child himself had understood, in his last written sentence, with terrible clarity: that forgetting was the weapon, and it was being used on him first.

And now, fifty years later, Ulyana had walked into the house where it happened, taken a key labeled with that exact year, and carried it home. And their mirrors had begun to run late.

Coincidence was still on the table. Coincidence was always on the table; that was the whole discipline. But coincidence had stopped feeling like an explanation and started feeling like a courtesy extended to people who needed one.

Ulyana picked up their phone and searched, for the first time, the county archives for children born in 1965. Any child. Every child. Because if a boy had been erased—if records had been razored as cleanly as ledger pages—then the erasure itself would be the record. A hole has a shape.

The search spun. Results loaded.

And the first hit was a digitized county register, 1965, births, page 114—and there, between two other names, a gap in the numbering. Entry 2147, entry 2148, then a blank line where 2149 should be, the ruled space empty but the faintest shadow of erased ink still legible if you zoomed far enough, four letters, maybe five—

The image pixelated. The archive site timed out.

When Ulyana refreshed it, page 114 had been reindexed, and the blank line was gone entirely, the entries renumbered, seamless, tidy.

Balanced to the penny. Just like Odile would have wanted.

Ulyana stared at the screen in the dark kitchen, heart going hard, and understood—with the calm, total certainty that ends one life and starts another—that tomorrow they were going to find out who the boy was.
