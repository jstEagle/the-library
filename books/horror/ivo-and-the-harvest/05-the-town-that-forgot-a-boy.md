## 5. The Town That Forgot a Boy

The county archive opened at nine on a Tuesday, and Ulyana was standing at the door at 8:47 with a thermos of coffee and the ledger zipped into their backpack like contraband.

They had a system. They always had a system. Urban exploration had taught them that every building kept its paperwork somewhere—the permits, the fire inspections, the angry letters from neighbors—and if you wanted to understand a place, you read its paper. Buildings lied with their bones. Paper lied less.

The Harrow County Records Annex occupied a former bank on Calloway Street, all marble floors and brass teller windows repurposed as research desks. A woman named Dot ran the local history room, seventy-something, reading glasses on a beaded chain, and she looked up when Ulyana came in with the expression of someone who had seen every species of researcher and could classify this one instantly.

"You're the Delacroix girl," she said. "Odile's grandchild."

"Ulyana. And not really a girl."

"Everyone's somebody's girl in this town." Dot waved them toward the back. "Genealogy's the third drawer. Births, deaths, marriages. Census records are on microfilm. What are you hunting?"

Ulyana hesitated. They'd rehearsed this in the car—a cover story about family history, which wasn't even a lie—but the word *Ivo* sat in their throat like a stone.

"A boy," they said finally. "Name of Ivo. Probably born sometime in the sixties. I don't have a last name."

Dot's pen stopped moving.

"Ivo," she repeated. Not confused. Something else. Something closer to the way you'd repeat a word in a language you'd been made to forget. "That's an unusual name for around here."

"So you've heard it?"

"No." She said it too fast, and then busied herself with the drawer key. "Third drawer. Sign the log."

* * *

The drawer gave Ulyana four hours of increasingly frantic searching.

Birth registers, 1955 through 1975: no Ivo. They checked spellings—Eyvoh, Ivor, Evo, Yvo. Nothing. Death indexes for the same span: nothing. Missing persons files, which the archive kept in a separate folder because the sheriff's department had never digitized them: a handful of runaways, two drownings, one elderly man who'd wandered off in 1981 and been found in a drainage culvert three counties over. No children missing in 1974. No children missing ever, apparently, in the entire recorded history of Harrow County, which was statistically impossible and both of them knew it.

Ulyana sat back and stared at the index cards.

Every county loses children. Drownings, accidents, kids who wander into quarries and woods and old wells. It was grim arithmetic, but it was arithmetic. A county this size, across a hundred years, should have shown dozens. The folder held eleven entries since 1920, and eight of those were adults.

It was like looking at a ledger where someone had gone through with an eraser. Not removing everything—just smoothing out the parts that would raise questions. Leaving enough bodies to seem normal, subtracting the ones that would add up wrong.

Their phone buzzed. A message from their friend Priya, responding to a photo Ulyana had sent last night of the ledger's inside cover:

**PRIYA:** HARVEST written by a kid? that's creepy lol. whose handwriting

**ULYANA:** that's the thing. i think i know whose. or whose it matches

Because here was what Ulyana hadn't told anyone yet, what they'd turned over all night like a stone looking for what lived under it: after finding the ledger, they'd gone back through the boxes from Odile's estate—the boxes they'd sorted in a grief-haze weeks ago and sealed again—and found, at the bottom of a box marked KITCHEN MISC, a manila envelope that did not say kitchen anything.

Inside were school papers. Old, brittle, done in pencil on paper that had gone soft as cloth. A spelling test: *harvest, orchard, cellar, promise.* Scored 9/10, the misspelled word *cellar* corrected in a different hand—Odile's hand, Ulyana knew it instantly, the same firm downstrokes as the ledger. A drawing of a house with too many windows, each window filled with a small scribbled face. And at the top of every page, in careful block letters a child had practiced until they were proud of it:

**IVO**

No last name. Just IVO, the way you'd write your name if your name was the only thing anyone had ever let you keep.

And the handwriting matched the ledger. The H in HARVEST on the inside cover had the same lopsided crossbar. The same R with its little kick at the end, like a leg stepping forward. Ulyana had held the spelling test against the ledger page under a lamp and felt something in their chest go cold and quiet.

A dead boy's homework, kept hidden in his killer's—or his keeper's, or his neighbor's, or whatever Odile had been—kitchen box for fifty years.

* * *

By noon Ulyana had expanded the search to everything the annex had.

Newspapers on microfilm. The *Harrow Courier* was digitized back to 1962, fully searchable, and Ulyana typed **IVO** into the archive and got four results, all false positives—Ivo's Restaurant (closed 1988), a horse named Ivory in a 1971 racing column, a typo in a classified ad ("ivoy sofa, good condition"), and a 1974 piece about the fall harvest festival.

They tried combinations. *Missing child.* *Boy lost.* *Search party.* The Courier's coverage of 1974 was strange in a way Ulyana couldn't immediately name, and then could: there was almost no crime reporting that year. A town of eleven thousand, and the police blotter for twelve straight months was fender-benders, shoplifting, a dog problem on Mill Street. No burglaries worth mentioning. No assaults. One drowning—that was there, August 1974, a fisherman named Aldous Crane, ruled accidental—and then nothing.

Even the obituaries were thin. People died; the paper just didn't always say so. In October 1974 the death notices ran short all month, as if the typesetter had been rationing ink.

Ulyana pulled the reel for the week of October 21, 1974, because the ribbon on the key said 1974 and because the ledger's razored pages clustered around late October, and found the festival coverage. Harvest Queen, a photo of a float shaped like a cornucopia, a pie contest. On page six, a single paragraph:

*Councilman E. Hale announced at Tuesday's meeting that the county's centennial fund has exceeded all projections, crediting "the continued goodwill between our founding families and the community we all share."*

Marcus Hale. Or rather, Marcus Hale's grandfather, or great-uncle—E. Hale. Ulyana photographed the paragraph and moved on, but the sentence stuck in them like a splinter. *Goodwill between our founding families and the community.* As if they were separate things. As if the families were doing the community a favor by belonging to it.

* * *

At two o'clock, Dot appeared at their elbow with two cups of coffee from the machine in the hall, which meant she'd decided Ulyana was worth bad coffee.

"Find your boy?"

"No." Ulyana closed the birth register with more force than intended. "He doesn't exist. Officially. No birth record, no death record, no missing person report, no newspaper mention. Nothing."

Dot set the coffees down. She didn't drink hers. She looked at the registers stacked on the desk with an expression Ulyana couldn't read—something older than skepticism.

"How thorough were you?"

"Thorough."

"And you're sure of the name. Ivo."

"There's handwriting," Ulyana said. "Dozens of pages of it. Schoolwork. My grandmother kept it hidden."

Something moved behind Dot's face then, quick as a fish under ice. She picked up her coffee, changed her mind, put it down again.

"When I started here," she said slowly, "in '89, there was a woman who worked the front desk before me. Vera. Retired to her sister's place in Ohio. Before she left, she told me something I thought was just old-woman talk. She said the archive had a policy—not written down, mind, a policy the way a house has a smell—about certain names. That some requests got quietly steered away. That some drawers stuck." She met Ulyana's eyes. "I laughed. Then my first year, I went to pull the 1974 tax assessment rolls and the binding was so swollen with damp the pages wouldn't separate. Only that year. Every other year opened fine. I had the whole reel re-bound and when it came back, it opened fine too. I told myself it was damp."

"Dot—"

"I'm telling you what I'm telling you," she said, a little sharply, as if annoyed at herself. "I'm not telling you anything else. I've got a pension and a granddaughter." She stood, smoothed her cardigan. "But if I were a younger person with fewer ties, I might wonder why a county would bother forgetting a child. Counties forget scandals. Counties forget debts. Forgetting a *person*—" she shook her head. "That takes effort. That takes everyone."

She walked back to her desk, and the conversation was over, and Ulyana sat in the marble quiet of the old bank feeling the floor tilt beneath a certainty they'd spent thirty years constructing.

* * *

They drove home the long way, past the turnoff for Harrow House, and didn't take it, and hated themselves a little for how hard they gripped the wheel at the intersection anyway.

The apartment was waiting with its lagging mirrors. Ulyana had developed a protocol: towels over the bathroom mirror, the hallway mirror turned to face the wall, the bedroom closet doors shut. The apartment looked like a place where someone was hiding from themselves, which, fine. Accurate.

They spread the evidence on the kitchen table instead of looking at any reflective surface.

The ledger. The razored stubs of pages. The manila envelope. The spelling test. The drawing of the house with too many windows—they counted them now, properly, for the first time: fourteen windows, thirteen with faces, one dark. They photographed everything again, though the photographs had never once shown them anything the mirrors wouldn't. Photos were honest. Photos were the one thing Ulyana still trusted.

Then they did the thing they'd been avoiding. They laid the spelling test beside the ledger's remaining pages and matched words.

*Promise.* The same P, the loop slightly open at the bottom, in the ledger and on the test.
*Cellar.* Corrected in Odile's hand in the margin of the test; written cleanly, in the same child's developing script, on a surviving ledger page dated March 1974: *cellar — 3 bags lime.*
*Lime.* Ulyana looked at that word for a long time. Bags of lime went into cellars for reasons that had nothing to do with gardening.

The boy had helped keep the ledger. That was the shape of it, horrible and plain: a child doing chores, running errands, writing down deliveries for the adults who kept him—whoever kept him, wherever he'd lived, in a house with fourteen windows. And then, in October 1974, every page mentioning him razored out, and the boy himself—

Ulyana pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes.

Here was the war, and they could feel both fronts clearly. Front one: *my grandmother.* Odile Delacroix, who'd taught them to gut a fish and swear in French, who'd sent five dollars in every birthday card well past the point of sense, who'd once driven four hours in a snowstorm because Ulyana had a fever and sounded scared on the phone. That woman had kept a murdered—or worse—child's homework in a kitchen box for half a century. Had kept it *hidden*. Hidden meant ashamed. Hidden meant knowing. You don't hide a keepsake; you hide evidence.

Front two: *but she kept it.* Fifty years. Through moves and cleanings and the slow winnowing of an old woman's possessions, those papers survived. She could have burned them a thousand times. Some part of Odile Delacroix had carried that boy's spelling test through her entire remaining life like a stone in her shoe, unable to drop it, unable to look at it, unwilling to destroy the only proof he'd existed.

Both fronts were true. That was the worst part. Grief and horror weren't taking turns; they were holding hands.

* * *

Near midnight, Ulyana gave up on sleep and did what skeptics do when the world stops cooperating: they got more rigorous.

They built a timeline on butcher paper taped to the wall, because the walls in this apartment had no mirrors and therefore could be trusted.

1974, January–March: ledger entries in the boy's hand. Deliveries, lime, lamp oil. The word HARVEST appearing with increasing frequency, sometimes capitalized, sometimes underlined twice.
August 1974: Aldous Crane drowns. The only death the paper reported all year.
October 1974: harvest festival. Centennial fund exceeds projections. Ledger pages razored. Ribbon tied around a brass key. A child's handwriting writes HARVEST inside a book cover and is never heard from again.
After: fifty years of silence, swollen bindings, sticking drawers, a policy like a smell.

And underneath it all, the arithmetic that would not balance: a county does not forget a child by accident. Records don't all fail at once. Microfilm doesn't selectively blur. Eleven missing persons in a century, when the national average for a county this size would be ten times that. Someone had gone through the whole town's memory the way Ulyana went through abandoned buildings—methodically, room by room, taking only what mattered and leaving the structure standing.

Except Ulyana took souvenirs. And someone had left one too.

The spelling test. The ledger. The key. Odile had kept the proof, and proof, once it exists, wants out.

Ulyana stood in front of the butcher paper until 3 a.m., and somewhere in that hour the skeptic's creed—the photograph-it-or-it-didn't-happen creed that had carried them safely through thirty years of dark houses—quietly changed shape. Not broken. Sharpened. Because the photographs were real, the records were real, the absences were real, and absence, Ulyana now understood, was a kind of evidence too. You could photograph a hole where a boy used to be. They had four hours of microfilm that amounted to exactly that.

The phone buzzed on the table. An email this time, from the county historical society, auto-replying to a records request Ulyana had fired off before leaving the annex:

*Your inquiry regarding "Ivo" has been received. Please note that the Society's holdings concerning the year 1974 are incomplete due to a documented storage loss.*

Documented storage loss. Ulyana almost laughed. Even the cover story had been filed.

They were still staring at it when a second notification slid in—an alert they'd set days ago on the digital archive, keyword-based, mostly useless until now:

**MATCH: "Harrow House" — Harrow Courier, Nov. 14, 1974, p.2 — "HARROW HOUSE CLOSED TO PUBLIC FOLLOWING STRUCTURAL SURVEY"**

One line. The house, open for tours every autumn for decades, abruptly shuttered two weeks after the festival, forever. Structural survey. Sure.

And below the article text, in the scanned page's margin, in ink that predated the scan, someone—some reader, some librarian, some hand—had written a single annotation, faint but unmistakable under the archive's enhancement filter:

*ask the Ambrose woman. she'll know what we paid.*

Ambrose. Ruth Ambrose. Ninety-one years old, if the society's donor rolls were current, and living eleven miles east on a road with no streetlights.

Ulyana looked at the dark towel hanging over the bathroom doorway, at the hallway mirror turned like a punished child to face the wall, and felt the last of their disbelief go the way of the razored pages—cut out cleanly, leaving a smooth stub where a whole person used to be.

They packed the bag. Key, ledger, spelling test, printed pages. Eleven miles east.
