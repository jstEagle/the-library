## 4. The Porch

The morning after the tally marks, Xanthe did what any reasonable man would do when his own handwriting betrayed him: he put the notebook in the glove box of his rental car and locked the car.

Then he unlocked it, retrieved the notebook, and put it in his jacket pocket instead, because leaving it in the car felt like leaving a child at a rest stop. Then he took it out of his pocket and set it on the kitchen table, face down, like a card he was ashamed of.

"You're being ridiculous," he told himself, in the cheerful, reasonable voice he used on himself in moments of crisis. "You're a grown man with a mortgage and a dentist. Dentists do not hallucinate. That's practically the whole point of dentists."

The farmhouse did not answer. The farmhouse never answered. That was the thing about the Eriksen house — it had spent forty years making noises at him and had never once, in all that time, said a single word.

He spent the morning doing chores, because chores were the Eriksen family's answer to everything. Grief? Chop wood. Dread? Fix a fence. Existential horror? There was always a gutter that needed clearing. His mother had gone into town "for funeral things," which was a phrase that had been doing a lot of work in this house lately, and Xanthe was alone with the house and the house was alone with him, and they were both pretending very hard that this arrangement was fine.

He fixed the third fence post from the gate, the same one that had been loose since he was nine. He cleared the gutter over the porch, and found, wedged in the downspout, a tennis ball he had lost in 1996, gray and soft as a moth. He held it for a long moment. He did not throw it. He put it in his pocket next to nothing, because the notebook was on the kitchen table, and he went around the side of the house to look at the porch.

That was when he saw Ivo.

* * *

Ivo was sitting on the porch steps.

Not standing near the porch. Not glimpsed at the edge of the yard, the way childhood things are supposed to appear, half-seen and deniable. Sitting. On the third step, in the exact spot where Xanthe had sat every summer evening of his childhood, with his knees drawn up and his long arms wrapped around them, in the posture of someone who has been waiting and has decided, philosophically, to be patient about it.

He was exactly the same.

That was the first thing, and it took Xanthe's breath out of his body like a punch. Ivo was exactly, precisely, insultingly the same. Seven years since Xanthe had last seen him — no, longer, because you stop seeing them gradually, the way you stop hearing a clock, and the last time had been the last time without Xanthe knowing it — and Ivo had not aged a day. Not a day. He was still a lanky, jointed impossibility, all elbows and shins, like a scarecrow that had been left out in a good wind and had decided to make the most of it. His head was a soft burlap oval. His eyes were two black buttons, four-holed, sewn on with red thread. His mouth was a line of stitches, a wide, patient, hand-sewn smile that had comforted Xanthe through every nightmare of his first ten years and was now, at twenty-six, doing the opposite.

His overalls were the same overalls. There was the same patch on the left knee — a square of gingham, cut from one of Wren's old dresses, because Wren had insisted that Ivo should carry a piece of her everywhere, and nine-year-old Wren's insistence was a force of nature that adults had learned to route around.

"Ivo," Xanthe said.

His voice came out strange. Small. Twenty-six years old and nine years old at the same time, like a chord played in two octaves.

Ivo tilted his head. The buttons caught the morning light and gave nothing back, because buttons don't. That was the whole point of buttons. You could look into them and see only yourself, small and bent and upside down, and whatever you thought you saw looking back was your own business.

"Hello, Xanthe," Ivo said.

His voice was exactly as Xanthe remembered it, which was to say it was a voice made of felt and afternoon light and the specific quality of silence in a house where the grown-ups were napping. It was a voice that had no origin anywhere in the physical world, and hearing it again after so long was like hearing a song from a dream you'd forgotten you had.

"You're not real," Xanthe said. It came out automatically, the old liturgy, the words they'd said a hundred times, Wren and him, sitting on these very steps: *You're not real, you're ours, you're not real, you're ours.* It had been a spell. It had been a kindness. It had been, he understood now, standing here with his heart going like a woodpecker in a screen door, a rule they had made up to keep the grown-ups from asking questions.

"I never claimed to be," Ivo said. His stitched smile did not move — it couldn't; it was thread — and yet somehow the smile *changed*, the way a room changes when a lamp comes on. "You've gotten tall."

"I'm twenty-six."

"I know. I counted." Ivo patted the step beside him. The gesture was so familiar, so exactly the gesture — *sit down, tell me about your day, tell me everything* — that Xanthe's legs nearly obeyed on their own, some old muscle memory of devotion firing in the dark of him.

He did not sit. He stayed where he was, three steps up and six feet away, a man keeping a careful distance from the best friend of his childhood.

"You're not real," he said again, and hated how it sounded now. Not a spell. A plea.

"No," Ivo agreed pleasantly. "And yet."

* * *

Xanthe sat down on the top step, as far from Ivo as the porch allowed, which was not far, because the porch was small and Ivo's presence had always been the kind that filled a space the way water fills a boot. He put his elbows on his knees. He looked out at the yard, at the dying orchard, at the barn with its sagging roofline, anywhere that wasn't two black buttons.

"I'm either dreaming," he said, "or having some kind of episode. Grief does this. I read about it. Grief hallucinations are extremely common and completely normal and I am handling this normally."

"Mm," said Ivo.

"My sister died."

"I know."

"The funeral is Saturday. Is. The funeral is Saturday." He'd lost track. That was another thing the house did to time; it made taffy of it. "There's a casket, and there's a body in the casket, except there isn't, because the body in the casket is *wrong*, and everyone keeps telling me it isn't, and I've started writing things down to check whether I'm losing my mind, and this morning my notebook had tally marks in it that I don't remember making. Two hundred and six of them. I counted twice."

"I know," Ivo said again, and there was something in his voice now, under the felt and the light, something old and careful, the way you'd speak near a sleeping dog.

Xanthe finally turned and looked at him directly. It was harder than it should have been, like looking into the sun through a window screen. "Then tell me what's happening. You clearly know something. You always knew things. You knew where Wren's lost earring was before she lost it. You knew it was going to rain at the county fair in '98. You knew—" He stopped. His throat had gone tight. "You knew when to sit with me. Every time. Every single bad night, you were already on the porch, or at the foot of the bed, or under the apple tree, before I even knew I needed sitting with. How did you know, Ivo?"

The buttons regarded him. Somewhere behind the house, a crow said something rude and flapped away.

"I was made for it," Ivo said simply. "That's what I was for."

"Made."

"Mm."

"Made *how*? Made by who? Wren and I made you up. You were — you were a game. A game we were so good at we forgot we were playing it." He heard his own voice climb and didn't care. "That's what Mom said. That's what the school counselor said. *Twins do that, it's healthy, it's a shared fantasy, they'll grow out of it.* And we did. We grew out of you. I grew out of you, and you *stayed the same*, and that's not how games work, Ivo. Games end. You don't get to just sit on a porch for twenty years waiting—"

"Seventy-one," Ivo said.

Xanthe stopped.

"Seventy-one years," Ivo said, "give or take. I sat on this porch before your mother was born. I sat on it before your *grandmother* was born." He unfolded himself, joint by patient joint, and stood, and he was taller than Xanthe remembered, taller than he had any right to be, his shadow lying long and wrong across the boards. "You grew out of me the way you grow out of a coat. But the coat was never yours, Xanthe. It was handed down."

The wind moved through the orchard, and every dead tree creaked at once, and for one absurd moment Xanthe thought: *they're all turning to look.*

"I don't understand," he said, and it was the truest thing he'd said in seven years.

"No," Ivo agreed, and sat back down, folding up again, becoming small and familiar and safe, and Xanthe hated how much he wanted that, how badly the child in him lunged toward it. "But you brought it back with you."

The sentence went into Xanthe like a splinter — small, clean, and instantly, unignorably *there*.

"Brought what back."

Ivo did not answer. He turned his button eyes toward the house — toward the east window on the second floor, the one with the curtains drawn, the one Xanthe's mother had told him was "just storage" — and the stitched smile held its patient curve, and the crow behind the barn said nothing at all now, and the whole farm had gone quiet in the way a field goes quiet when the hawk's shadow crosses it.

"Ivo," Xanthe said. "Brought *what* back."

"You know," Ivo said gently. "You've known since the funeral home. You looked at that casket and you knew, and then you did what your family does, which is you made a list of sensible reasons, and you nailed the knowing shut under them." He patted the step again. "You were always the one who nailed things shut. Wren was the one who opened them. That's why it took her."

Xanthe stood up so fast his vision sparked. "Don't." His voice cracked on it, and he let it crack, because there was no one here to perform composure for except a doll who had watched him wet the bed at six. "Don't talk about her. Don't you *dare*—"

"I sat with her too," Ivo said, and the felt had gone out of his voice, and what was underneath was older than the house. "Every night you two didn't know about. I sat with her for seven years, Xanthe. I sat with her in the dark in the east room, and I listened, and I could not move, because I was made to watch and not to touch, made to *comfort* and not to *help*, and I am telling you—" the red-threaded mouth did not move but the whole of him leaned forward, straining against his own seams, "—I am telling you that whatever is wearing your sister up there is *not your sister*, and it has been waiting a very long time for you to come home so soon."

The words landed one by one, like stones dropped down a well.

"Soon," Xanthe whispered. "Mom said that. She said, *why did you come home so soon.*"

"I know," said Ivo. "I heard her."

The porch boards were warm in the sun. The orchard creaked. Somewhere upstairs, behind the drawn curtain of the east window, something shifted its weight — one soft, patient footstep across a floor that had not been walked on in years — and stopped, as if listening to hear whether it had been heard.

Xanthe looked at the window. The window looked back, curtained and dark.

"Ivo," he said, and his mouth had gone dry as August. "What's behind that door?"

His old friend, his impossible, inherited, hand-me-down friend, folded his long hands in his lap and gave him the answer he would carry up those stairs before the week was out:

"Come inside," Ivo said, "and I'll show you what this family keeps for dinner."
