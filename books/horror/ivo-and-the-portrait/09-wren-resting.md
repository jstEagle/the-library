## 9. Wren, Resting

The key had been in the kitchen drawer for as long as I could remember, which was the first strange thing about it. A locked door whose key lives in plain sight is not a lock at all. It's a suggestion. It's a mother saying *don't* without having to say it out loud.

I found it on my third night home, sitting exactly where it had always sat — a small brass thing on a loop of red yarn, buried under takeout menus from restaurants that had closed before I was born. I'd seen it a thousand times as a boy and never once thought to ask what it opened. Children are excellent at not asking questions. It's practically our whole job description.

I stood in the hallway holding it for a long time, listening to the house do its evening breathing. The walls had gone quiet since dinner, which I'd decided to interpret as a good sign, the way you decide a dog that has stopped growling at the closet is probably fine now.

The east bedroom door was at the end of the hall, past the bathroom with the warped floorboard, past the framed cross-stitch of the Serenity Prayer that Mom had hung sometime in the nineties and never moved even when she repainted everything else. The door itself was ordinary. White paint, six panels, a brass knob gone dull with hand-oil. Wren's door. Except Wren's room had been the one over the kitchen, and everyone knew that, and the fact that this door existed at all — locked, at the end of the hall, in a house where I could draw every room blindfolded — meant the house had more rooms than it admitted to.

Or fewer. With this house I honestly couldn't rule either out.

"Wren?" I said, because apparently I was going to be polite about it.

The key turned like the lock had been oiled yesterday.

* * *

The room inside was dark in a way rooms aren't supposed to be dark. Not unlit — the curtains were thin and the moon was up and there should have been gray light everywhere. Instead the darkness sat in the corners like furniture, dense and specific, and the air smelled faintly of turpentine and cut grass and the orange peel smell of Wren's shampoo, all at once, all wrong for October.

She was sitting on the bed with her back against the headboard, knees drawn up, and she looked so much like herself that my body reacted before my mind could file an objection. My chest did the thing it does. Seven years of not-crying tried to happen all at once and I had to stand very still in the doorway and breathe through my nose like a man at his own wedding.

"Hey," she said. "Took you long enough."

Her voice was right. That was the cruelest part. Voices are hard — I'd learned that much from bad impressions at school assemblies — pitch and rhythm and the little catch she got when she was about to laugh at her own joke before she finished telling it. This voice had the catch. This voice had everything.

"You're supposed to be resting," I said. "That's what Mom said. She's been setting you a plate."

"Mom says a lot of things." Wren smiled, and the smile was right too, crooked on the left side first. "Sit down, Xan. You look terrible."

"I've been told." I didn't sit. "You look—"

"Like me?"

"Like you," I admitted.

"Good." She patted the mattress beside her. "Because I've got a lot to catch you up on, and you're going to want to be comfortable."

I stayed by the door. She shrugged like it didn't matter, like we had all the time in the world, and began to talk.

* * *

Here is what she recited, in order, without pausing once to think:

The summer we were seven, when we buried a shoebox of "treasures" under the third apple tree from the fence — four bottle caps, a bird skull, half a harmonica — and dug it up two summers later to find the harmonica had rusted into something that looked like coral, and Wren cried, and I told her it was treasure now, real treasure, because treasure is just junk that waited long enough.

The winter we were nine, when the power went out for three days and we slept in front of the fireplace in sleeping bags and invented a game called Lighthouse Keeper where one of us was the keeper and one of us was the storm, and she always made me be the storm because, she said, I was better at being loud and sad at the same time.

The day we were eleven, behind the barn, when Ivo went missing for a week — button eyes and stitched grin, our lanky impossible friend — and Wren cried again, and I said he'd come back, and he did come back, and neither of us ever asked each other how we both knew he would.

The fight we were fifteen. The exact words. Hers: *"You don't get to leave and take the whole sky with you."* Mine: *"Watch me."* The sound the screen door made. The fact that she threw my baseball glove into the well field and I spent forty minutes looking for it in the dark and never found it, and never told her I hadn't, and let her believe it turned up.

Every detail correct. Every detail in her cadence, with her pauses, with the little exhale she did through her nose instead of laughing.

And then the ones only Wren could know.

The night before I left, when she came to my room at two in the morning and sat on the end of my bed and didn't say anything for twenty minutes, and finally said, "If it's between staying for me and going for you, go for you," and then left before I could answer, and we never spoke of it, and I built seven years on top of that sentence like it was a foundation stone.

She knew it. She gave it back to me word for word, in the dark, in her voice, and watched me with her eyes while my whole chest came apart.

"There," she said softly. "Now you know I'm me. Only Wren knows that. Right?"

"Right," I said, and hated how grateful I sounded.

* * *

I want to be honest here, because honesty is supposedly the theme of my life now, whether I auditioned for it or not.

Part of me believed her. Not the reasonable part — the reasonable part was standing by the door cataloguing wrongnesses like a customs officer: the darkness that didn't behave, the smell of turpentine, the fact that she never blinked quite on schedule, the fact that the room's shadows fell toward her instead of away. But there's a part of every person that is not a customs officer. There's a part that is just a twin, a brother, a nine-year-old in a sleeping bag who lost his sister twice — once to the grave and once to geography — and that part heard her say *go for you* in her own voice and thought, with a relief so total it felt like drowning in reverse: *oh thank God, she's alive, it was all some mistake, some mix-up at the funeral home, and I can stop.*

Longing doesn't argue. That's the thing nobody tells you. Longing doesn't make its case or answer objections. It just opens its coat and shows you what you've been starving for, and your hunger does the rest.

"So," she said, drawing her knees up tighter, chin on them, exactly the way she used to watch television. "Here's the offer. Stay."

"Stay," I repeated.

"Stay here. Stay home. The farm needs you, Xan. Mom needs you. I need you." She unfolded slightly, reached out, and I watched her hand — her hand, chipped nail polish, the scar on the knuckle from the harmonica shovel — hover in the air between us. "Seven years, Xan. You were gone seven years and every single day this place got quieter and sadder and I got lonelier, and now you're here, and I'm asking. Stay forever. That's all. Just stay."

"Forever's a long word."

"It's a short life," she said. "That's the trick of it. Forever sounds huge until you're the one offering it, and then it turns out it's just — Tuesdays. Soup on Sundays. Fixing the fence in spring. It's just a life, Xan. Our life. The one we were supposed to have if you hadn't run."

"I didn't run."

"You ran," she said gently, without any accusation at all, which was somehow worse. "It's okay. I forgave you before you finished packing. But you can stop running now. That's what I'm offering. Not punishment. Not even an apology. Just — home. Everything the way it was. Me, the way I was. You never have to lose anything again."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere far off, impossibly, I heard the screen door — the same sound from the fight, the same spring-creak-and-slap — though no door in the house had opened.

"And if I say no?" I asked.

Her face did something I will spend the rest of my life trying not to remember. It kept smiling. The smile stayed exactly where it was, warm and crooked, but underneath it something *moved*, the way you'll see a shape move under a tarp in the wind, and for half a second I understood that the smile was a picture of a smile, hung carefully on whatever was looking out through my sister's face.

Then it settled, and she was Wren again, and I could breathe.

"Why would you say no?" she asked, genuinely puzzled, and that was the most frightening tone yet — not menace, but incomprehension, the bafflement of a thing that had studied love from the outside and couldn't find the seam where the wanting stopped and the trap began.

* * *

I made myself ask the question. It took three tries; the first two came out as nothing, just breath shaped like words.

"The casket," I said. "At the funeral home. Wren — the real Wren — whoever she was — there wasn't anyone in it. There was a face. It looked like mine."

For a moment she said nothing, and the darkness in the corners leaned in.

"That was a mistake," she said. "A cruel one. Someone wanted to hurt you."

"Who?"

"People who don't understand families like ours." She slid off the bed and stood, and she was wearing the flannel shirt she'd worn the last morning I saw her, and I realized I had never actually seen what she was buried in, because I had run out of the funeral home before they closed the lid, and oh, that was convenient, wasn't it, that I'd never looked properly, that grief had given me such excellent excuses. "Families that keep things. Families that stay. They think belonging is a cage, Xan. They think love is a debt. But it isn't. It's a door, and doors only work if everyone agrees to keep them open."

"Where is she?" My voice cracked on it. "Wren. The actual — where is my sister?"

"She's resting," Wren said, and smiled, and pointed one finger at the bed. "Right here. I'm taking very good care of her. Of us. Of everything you love."

And God help me, part of me — the hungry part, the nine-year-old part, the part that had spent seven years eating alone in cities where nobody knew what a harmonica sounded like after it rusted — that part believed her, and that part was already unpacking.

I backed toward the door. She didn't follow. That was almost worse; she just watched me go with her head tilted, patient as a portrait, and as my hand found the knob she said, sweetly, in Wren's voice, with Wren's catch-before-laughter:

"Dinner's at six tomorrow. Mom's making your favorite. And Xan—" the smile widened one tooth too far "—the tally marks in your notebook? You should count them again. All of them. Count how many days you've actually been home."

I closed the door. I stood in the hallway shaking, doing arithmetic I didn't want to do, because I had arrived on a Tuesday and tonight felt like the fourth night, and the number in my gut was much larger than four.

Downstairs, faintly, I heard my mother setting the table. Three plates.

No — I counted the soft ceramic clicks through the floorboards.

Four.
