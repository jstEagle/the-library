## 12. Mother's Confession

I found her in the kitchen at six in the morning, which was the first strange thing, because my mother had not been up before seven since Dad died, and even then it had taken a fire alarm.

She was standing at the counter with her back to me, doing something with her hands that I couldn't quite parse — arranging something, or counting something — and when I said her name she didn't jump. She just kept going for a moment, the way you do when you've been caught and are deciding whether to pretend otherwise.

"Mom," I said again. "The portrait's gone."

"I know," she said.

"You *know*?"

"I know a lot of things, Xanthe." She turned around, and she had flour on her hands, which was the second strange thing, because my mother hadn't baked anything since 2011, when the doctor told her to stop pretending she enjoyed it. "Sit down. I'll make eggs."

"You don't have to make eggs."

"I'm making eggs." She cracked two into a bowl with the crisp efficiency of someone who has done this ten thousand times in some other life and is only now remembering how. "You'll want to eat. This is going to be a long conversation, and I find people believe things better on a full stomach. That's why churches serve dinner before the hard sermons."

"That is not why churches serve dinner."

"It's one reason." She whisked. The whisk made a sound like a small animal being patient. "Where did you put the painting? Not where. Never mind. It doesn't matter where you put it. It matters what you're planning to do with it, and I can see from your face that the answer is something stupid and brave, which is your father all over, God rest him, he once tried to fight a combine harvester over a matter of principle—"

"Mom."

"—and lost, Xanthe, he lost to a *combine*—"

"*Mom.*"

She stopped whisking. The kitchen went very quiet, the particular quiet of a house that has been listening this whole time and is pleased to finally be addressed.

"The casket was empty," I said. "You knew."

My mother set down the whisk. She looked at it for a moment as if it had asked her a question. Then she pulled out the chair across from me and sat, folding her hands on the table like a woman preparing to negotiate a mortgage, a divorce, or an exorcism, and possibly all three.

"Yes," she said.

* * *

Here is what I had expected, in the feverish hours since finding the attic empty and the plaster dust still settling on my shoulders: denial. Anger. Maybe tears, maybe a phone call to Dr. Prasad about my medication, which she'd threatened twice already this week. What I had not expected was the simple, level, terrible calm of a woman who has been waiting decades for this conversation and has rehearsed it in the shower.

"Say more than yes," I said.

"All right." She took a breath. "Your sister is not dead. Your sister is not in the ground. Your sister is upstairs, in the east room, where she has been since October, and the thing we buried on Tuesday is nothing at all, and I stood at that graveside and I let Reverend Halloran say the Lord's Prayer over an empty box because the alternative was letting everyone in this county know what our family keeps, and I would rather lie to God than to the neighbors, because God forgives and Mrs. Delgado does not."

The eggs sat forgotten in their bowl. Somewhere above us — there is no floor above us; there is attic, and then sky — something shifted its weight.

"When did you find out?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "That she wasn't—"

"October ninth. She went out to check the well cap, because the frost cracks it every year, and she didn't come back, and I went out, and she was standing by the barn looking at me with her own face, and I knew. Mothers know. There's a half-second delay, honey, a half-second where the thing wearing your child hasn't figured out yet how the smile works, and in that half-second I saw what was underneath, and then it smiled, and it was Wren again, perfect, better than Wren, Wren without the chapped lips and the bad mood, and I understood that I could scream or I could nod." Her hands stayed folded. Her knuckles were white as the flour. "I nodded."

"You nodded."

"I nodded, and I made breakfast, and I set three plates, and I have set three plates every morning since, and I want you to understand—" and here her voice finally cracked, one clean break down the middle, like ice on a pond in March — "I want you to understand that I did not do it because I am stupid, or evil, or because I love the farm more than I loved her. I did it because it told me, that first morning, very politely, over scrambled eggs, that if I made trouble it would keep her anyway but I wouldn't get the visits."

"The visits."

"She comes downstairs sometimes. In the evening. Sits where she used to sit. Tells me about her day." My mother's eyes were dry and enormous. "It's not her. I know it's not her. But it knows everything she knew, and it does the laugh, the little snort-laugh she did when she thought something was funnier than she wanted to admit, and I am fifty-eight years old and alone on a dying farm, and I took the deal. I took the deal and I have hated myself every single day since, and I would take it again tomorrow, and that, Xanthe, is the thing I cannot forgive myself for. Not the lying. The *taking*."

* * *

I should have said something then. I have replayed that moment a hundred times since, looking for the version of myself who reached across the table, who said *it's okay, Mom, we'll fix it*, who did anything other than sit there with his mouth open like a mailbox.

What I actually said was: "The farm. You said it keeps the farm alive."

And my mother laughed — a short, ugly, grateful laugh, the kind you give someone who has just proven they're paying attention.

"There it is. There's the Eriksen nose for the soft spot." She got up, finally, and poured the eggs into the pan, and the hiss filled the silence while she gathered herself. "You noticed the orchards on your way in. Of course you did. Half of them died the year you left, did you know that? The year you left, Xanthe. Not gradually. All at once, like they'd been holding on until you were safely gone."

"I ran away from home, Mom. Trees don't care."

"These trees cared." She stirred. "Aldous planted them in '75. Every tree on this property came up the spring after he brought the boy home. And every year since, the harvest comes in wrong-but-good — too big, too sweet, fruit that keeps until Christmas without rotting — and every year we sell it, and every year the money arrives exactly when the bank letter does, and I stopped asking questions about that around the same time I stopped asking questions about the plates. The thing upstairs doesn't just wear Wren. It wears the whole farm. It's been wearing this family since before I was born, keeping us fed and solvent and *together*, and in exchange—"

"In exchange we feed it."

"We *house* it." She turned, spatula in hand, and for a moment she looked so much like Grandma Eriksen that my spine went cold. "There's a difference. We don't give it anything. We just... don't ask it to leave. That's the whole contract, honey. Seventy pages of legal boilerplate reduced to one sentence: *don't ask it to leave.* Aldous asked, once. Look how that ended."

"Aldous is under the apple tree by the fence."

"Aldous is under the apple tree by the fence," she agreed, "and the apples over that particular tree come in sweeter than anywhere else, and I have never eaten one, and neither should you."

She plated the eggs — three plates, out of habit, and I watched her realize it, and watched her decide not to apologize for it, and something in my chest hurt in a new place.

"Eat," she said, pushing a plate at me. "And then I'm going to tell you the part I've never told anyone, and you're going to wish I hadn't, and then you're going to go do whatever fool thing you're planning anyway, because you have your father's jaw and his judgment, and I learned long ago that both are load-bearing and neither can be repaired."

* * *

We ate. The eggs were good. That felt like a betrayal somehow — that the eggs were good, that morning light was coming through the window the way it always had, gold and ordinary, laying itself across a table where a woman was about to explain why she'd let her daughter be stolen and called it hospitality.

"The part you've never told anyone," I prompted.

She set down her fork. "When I was nineteen — your age, when you left, isn't that funny, the house seems to collect us at nineteen — I went into the east room. On purpose. Everyone always says the east room is locked, and it is, but the key hangs in the pantry behind the flour tin and it always has, and every child in this family finds it by sixteen and pretends they haven't, because finding it is a test and opening the door is a different test entirely."

"And you opened it."

"I opened it." She smiled faintly. "There was a girl in there. Sitting on the bed. About my age, wearing a dress from the forties, and she looked up at me and said, *Oh good, you're the new one,* and I said, the new what, and she said, *the new one who gets asked,* and I said, asked what, and she said—" my mother's voice dropped, and she looked at the third plate, the untouched one, the one steaming gently for nobody — "*asked whether you'd like to stay forever. Everybody gets asked eventually. Some of us say yes.*"

"What happened?"

"I said no. I said it fast, before I could think about it, and the girl's face did something I have spent forty years failing to describe — it didn't get angry, it got *relieved*, like I'd taken a weight off her — and she stood up and walked into the wall, and the wall took her the way water takes a stone, and I locked the door and put the key back and didn't sleep for a week." She looked up. "But here's the part, Xanthe. Here's the part I've never said out loud. For one second — one — before I answered, I *wanted* to say yes. Forever. Warm room, no bills, somebody who knows your name and wants you. One second of wanting, and I have always suspected the house counted that second. I have always suspected that's why it chose Wren instead of me, when my time came. Because I hesitated, and Wren—" her voice broke again, and this time she let it — "Wren would have said no instantly, loudly, rudely, with that little snort-laugh, and it takes the ones who hesitate, honey. It has always taken the ones who hesitate. That's what the asking *is*. It's not consent it wants. It's the pause before the no."

The kitchen clock ticked. Above us, the weight shifted again, closer to the ceiling over the table, patient as flour sifting through a sieve.

"So don't hesitate," I said.

"Don't hesitate," she agreed. Then she reached across the table and gripped my wrist, hard, her nails in my skin, the first truly warm thing she'd touched me with since I arrived. "And Xanthe — when you go up there, or out there, wherever you're going with that painting — there's one more thing. The button-eyed one. Ivo."

My blood went still. "You can see him?"

"I could always see him. So could Aldous. So could Great-Grandma Ruth, who talked to him right up until the end." My mother's grip tightened. "He was ours first, you understand? Before he was yours. He belonged to this family before any of us were born, and whatever he's told you, whatever he's promised you — remember that a thing made to watch children learns to love them, and a thing that loves will lie for you, and a thing that lies for you will lie *to* you, and you will not be able to tell the difference until it's too late to matter."

"That's not fair to him."

"No," she said softly, letting go of my wrist. "It isn't. Nothing about this family has ever been fair to him. But fairness is a luxury for families that aren't paying rent to something in the walls."

She stood, collected the plates — all three, stacking the untouched one on top with hands that only shook a little — and paused at the sink with her back to me.

"One last thing," she said. "The ledger. If it shows you a ledger, and it will, and there's a line with your name on it — don't read past your name. Whatever's written underneath, whatever date, whatever's been added in fresh ink — don't read it. Some knowledge is a signature. Reading is agreeing. That's how it got Aldous, in the end. He read."

"Read what?"

She turned on the tap, and the water covered whatever she said next, and when she looked back at me her face was smooth as poured wax and warm as bread, and she said, "More eggs?" as if none of it had happened.

Above the ceiling, directly over my head, something began — very softly, very sweetly, in a voice I had known since before I could talk — to hum the lullaby Wren used to hum when I couldn't sleep.

And beneath the humming, almost too quiet to catch, another voice was counting.

Not tally marks this time.

Days.
