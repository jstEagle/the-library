## 17. Carrying the Portrait

The ledger tore in half in Ivo's hands, and Ivo began to tear with it.

I will never be able to describe it properly — not to a therapist, not to a priest, not to some poor bartender who made the mistake of asking how my week was going. The best I can manage is this: imagine a rag doll made of dusk, and someone pulling a thread. Ivo came apart from the edges first. His stitched smile unraveled into loose gray filament. One button eye dropped off his face and rolled across the floorboards of the endless hallway, and where it touched the wood, the wood remembered being an orchard for one confused second — blossoms, birdsong, sunlight — before folding back into corridor.

"Run," Ivo said, and his voice sounded like a music box winding down. "Xanthe. *Run.* You have until the moon clears the ridge."

I ran.

I had the portrait under my arm like a football I'd stolen from God. It was heavier than a painting should be. Heavier than a painting could be. It shifted against my ribs with each stride, and I would swear on any Bible you put in front of me that I felt it breathing — slow, patient breaths, like something asleep that did not intend to stay asleep.

Behind me, the house woke up.

Not the thing wearing Wren's face. Not yet. First came everything else. Everything the Eriksens had spent fifty years pretending was normal, all of it peeling off the walls at once, all of it coming after me with the wounded indignation of a lie that has finally been asked to tell the truth.

The grandfather clock in the hall — which had been stopped at 3:14 since before I was born — began to tick. Then to chime. Then to *scream*, a sound like a hundred dinner bells rung underwater, and its pendulum swung out of the case entirely and chased me down the hallway like a scythe with opinions.

I ducked. The pendulum embedded itself in the wallpaper, and the wallpaper — Mother's pride, the little yellow roses she'd hung when I was six — peeled away in sheets and flapped after me like a flock of furious yellow birds.

"Sorry about your roses!" I shouted, because apparently even mid-apocalypse I am my mother's son, apologizing to furniture.

The hallway did not end. That was the problem. Our farmhouse was a modest single-story rectangle; I had measured it as a child with Wren's tape measure during one of our interminable rainy summers, and I could have drawn its floor plan drunk and blindfolded. But now every door opened onto the same hallway. I burst through what should have been the bathroom and was still running forward through corridor. I slammed through the kitchen door and the kitchen was a hallway wearing a kitchen's clothes — counters receding into distance, cabinets stretching away like train cars, and somewhere far ahead of me, a kettle beginning to whistle in a voice that was almost, but not quite, Wren's.

*Don't listen to the kettle,* I told myself. *That is rule one now. Rule two: keep running.*

Behind me, the normal things gained. I could hear them: the wet slap of dish towels wringing themselves out into shapes; the creak of the good rocking chair rocking toward me at speed; the low, mournful mooing of the ceramic cow cookie jar, which had never once contained cookies and had always, I now understood, contained something else entirely.

And underneath all of it, threaded through the walls like a pulse, the scratching had stopped — and the silence was so much worse. The house had stopped pretending there were explanations. The explanations were chasing me.

* * *

I want to say something here about what it's like to run through your own childhood while it tries to eat you, and the honest answer is: it's familiar. That's the horrible part. I'd been running through this house my whole life — running past the room where Aldous's letters were hidden, running past the east bedroom, running past the well behind the barn whenever Mother said we don't go back there, Xanthe, and meaning it in a way no nine-year-old should ever hear. The house had always been a hallway with doors that didn't open. Tonight it had simply stopped lying about it.

The portrait squirmed under my arm. I adjusted my grip and my hand came away smeared with paint — fresh paint, wet paint, oil colors that hadn't been wet since Nixon was president. The drowned boy in the frame had turned his head. Just slightly. Just enough that he was looking at me now instead of out at the orchard, and his painted mouth had curved into something that was either gratitude or hunger, and I genuinely could not tell you which frightened me more.

"Not now," I told him. "We'll talk when we get to the well. Maybe. If you're nice."

He breathed. I kept running.

The hallway bent. Physically bent, like a straw pinched between enormous fingers, and around the bend came Mother.

She was standing at the far door — the front door, the real one, the one with the glass pane I'd pressed my nose against every morning of my childhood waiting for the school bus. Moonlight poured through the glass behind her, silver and full and wrong, three days early. She stood silhouetted in it, small and stooped, and she was weeping, and both her hands were flat against the door.

Holding it shut.

"Mama," I said, slowing, because what kind of monster doesn't slow down for his mother? "Mama, move. Please."

"You can't take it out," she said. Her voice was wrecked, sanded down to the grain. "Xanthe, sweetheart, if it goes back in the well, everything goes. The farm. The orchard. Fifty years of — of keeping it fed, of keeping it *happy*, do you know what we did, do you know what your grandmother did, what *I* did—"

"I read the letters, Mama."

"I know." She sobbed, and her hands stayed flat on the door. "I know you read the letters. I heard you up there. I've heard everything you've done since you drove through my gate, and I let you, because some part of me — some small, tired part of me — wanted you to end it. And then you got close to actually doing it, and now the rest of me wants to nail that door shut with my own body."

"That's not loyalty, Mama. That's just fear wearing loyalty's coat."

She laughed then — a cracked, astonished little laugh, the laugh of a woman hearing her own sermon quoted back at her by the child she'd lied to for thirty years. "Where did you learn to talk like that?"

"From a doll with button eyes. It's been an educational week."

Behind me, the rocking chair rounded the corner. Behind the chair came the yellow wallpaper, flying in formation. Behind the wallpaper came the smell — paint and turpentine and cut grass and funeral lilies, all the smells of every year this family had chosen comfort over truth, rolling down the hallway like weather.

Mother looked past me at all of it. Her face did the thing it always did — went smooth as poured wax, the family expression, the one I'd seen at funerals and holidays and every difficult conversation we'd ever aborted halfway through. For one terrible moment I thought the wax had won. I thought she was going to hold that door until the moon crested and the ledger rewrote itself with my name dated tomorrow.

Then her hands moved.

One stayed on the door. The other reached out — trembling, old, paint-stained from decades of touching up the porch rail every spring because the farm had to look alive, had to look *kept* — and she grabbed my sleeve.

"Hold on to me," she whispered. "Whatever comes through that door after you, hold on to me and it can't follow. Family line. Old rule. Older than the bargain." Her eyes were wet and fierce and human, finally, finally human. "I held the door shut for fifty years, baby. Let me hold it open right."

"Mama—"

"I set a plate for a dead girl every night for seven years because I couldn't bear the empty chair." Her grip tightened. "Do you understand? I chose the lie every single day, and it cost me everything, and it wasn't worth it, and I am *done*. Go. GO."

She pulled the door open onto moonlight and dying orchard grass, and I threw myself through it with the portrait clutched to my chest, and behind me the whole house screamed — every door, every clock, every cookie jar — as the things that had pretended to be normal found themselves pressed up against a threshold they could not cross while a mother held her son.

But the thing wearing Wren's face was already outside.

It stood at the edge of the yard, between me and the barn, dressed in my sister's burial dress, and the harvest moon rose over its shoulder like a spotlight finding the accused. Its head tilted. My sister's head. My own face, aged wrong, smiled at me with Wren's smile.

"There you are," it said, in a voice like honey poured over broken glass. "You brought the painting. Good boy. Now give it here, and we can all stop pretending, and you can come inside, and I'll make pancakes on Sunday mornings just like she used to, and I'll remember every single thing, Xanthe. Every birthday. Every secret. Everything Wren ever—"

"No," I said.

And I ran for the barn.

* * *

The last stretch was maybe two hundred yards and it was the longest run of my life, longer than the hallway, longer than the seven years I'd stayed away. The orchard trees clawed at me as I passed — dead branches that had been dead for decades suddenly grasping, suddenly *hungry*, the farm itself trying to keep its meal. The portrait thrashed under my arm. The moon climbed. Somewhere behind me Mother was screaming my name, or the house was screaming it using her voice, and I chose — deliberately, desperately — to believe it was her, cheering me on.

At the barn door I risked one look back.

Ivo stood in the doorway of the house, holding it open with what was left of himself. He was mostly thread now, mostly suggestion, a lanky outline of dusk with one button eye remaining, and he raised one unraveling hand — not waving goodbye. Pointing.

Toward the well.

Toward the capstone carved with our name, cracked open from the inside, breathing cold air that smelled of turpentine and patience.

The thing in Wren's shape was ten feet behind me and closing, and it was speaking with her true voice now — the real one, the one from before, the one that had named constellations wrong on purpose to make me correct her, the one that had dared me to eat June apples off the ground — and it said:

"Do it. Please."

And I turned to the well, portrait in my arms, moonlight on the stone, and understood that whatever happened next, I would only get to do it once.

The capstone waited. So did the moon.
