## 18. At the Well

The hallway did not end.

Xanthe had run down it for what felt like the length of his whole life — past the kitchen door that opened onto the kitchen that was no longer there, past his own bedroom door opening onto his own bedroom opening onto his own bedroom, a corridor of repeated childhoods like cards shuffled by a cheating dealer. The portrait banged against his chest where he'd strapped it to his back with his belt, and every time it knocked against his spine he could feel it *breathing*, a slow swell of canvas, patient as tide.

Behind him came the sounds of everything the family had ever pretended was normal.

The screen door slamming in a windless summer. His mother's humming, pitched wrong, the melody of a lullaby played backward. Footsteps on stairs in a house whose stairs had folded away hours ago. And underneath it all, threaded through it like a worm through an apple, Wren's voice — not the thing's version of her voice, but hers, truly hers, saying things she had actually said:

*"Xanthe, if you eat the last of the strawberry jam I will haunt you."*

*"Race you to the fence line. Loser has to tell Mom we broke the birdbath."*

*"You're my favorite person. Don't tell Ivo."*

He ran harder. His lungs burned with air that smelled of paint and turpentine and cut grass from summers before either of them was born.

The far end of the hallway — the only end, the one real door among ten thousand false ones — glowed faintly gray. Moonlight. Behind it, silhouetted, stood a small figure holding the door open with both hands, or possibly shut; from this distance, weeping, it was impossible to tell which.

"Mom," Xanthe gasped, because it was her, of course it was her, diminished now and human and crying in a way he hadn't seen since Dad left, since before, since the first crack in everything.

"You have to understand," she said, and her hands were still on the door, trembling, pushing and pulling at once, a woman trying to be two mothers in one body. "It keeps the farm alive. It kept *us* alive. Your grandmother fed it, and her mother before her, and I thought — I thought if we were kind to it, if we set its plate and answered when it called—"

"Mom." He reached the door. He took her wrists, gently, the way you take the wrists of someone standing too close to a ledge. "Let go."

"I named you both after birds," she whispered. "Did you know that? Wren and Xanthe. Little birds. I thought names were cages strong enough to keep anything out."

"They weren't," he said. "But thank you for building them anyway."

And then — whether she let go or whether he pulled free, he would never afterward be able to say — he was through, and the night hit him like cold water, real night, orchard night, the sky above the farmhouse torn open with clouds and the harvest moon hanging low and swollen and *nearly* full, fuller than it had any right to be three days early, so close to complete that the missing sliver looked less like shadow than like a held breath.

The barn loomed black against the silvered grass. Beyond it, the well.

He ran.

* * *

The capstone had been cracked open from the inside for longer than Xanthe had been alive — Ivo had told him that much, in his button-eyed, matter-of-fact way, the way you tell a child the stove is hot. Up close, in the moonlight, the crack looked like a mouth that had been politely closed for fifty years and had finally, wearily, given up pretending. Cold air rose out of it in a slow column, carrying turpentine and linseed oil and something older underneath, something like pond water and something like church.

Xanthe shrugged the belt off his shoulder and dragged the portrait around in front of him.

In the moonlight the drowned boy was more alive than ever. Aldous Eriksen had painted him in 1974 with a love so naked it should have embarrassed the paint itself — every freckle tender, every wet curl of hair rendered with the devotion of a man who had lost something and refused to accept the loss. The boy's eyes followed Xanthe, the way painted eyes do, except these did it *kindly*. That was the worst part. Even now, even knowing what had come through the door behind the boy's face, the painting looked at him with nothing but welcome.

On the back of the frame, in Aldous's careful hand: *TOMAS VINTER, aged 9, taken by the water, returned by love.* And beneath it, fresher, in ink that still shone wet after seven years: *WREN.*

And beneath that, fresher still, dated tomorrow: *XANTHE.*

He didn't look at that one. He made himself not look at that one. There is a kind of not-looking that is the hardest work a person can do.

"Put it down."

The voice came from behind the barn, and it was Wren's voice, and it was not Wren.

She stepped around the corner the way their mother stepped out of church — composed, smoothed, dressed in the navy dress they'd buried the empty casket in. Her face was Wren's face done by someone working from a photograph and a great deal of practice. Only the smile was off, the way a copied key turns almost-right and then doesn't.

"You're tired," it said, in her voice, with her cadence, with the little upward lilt she got at the ends of sentences when she was being gentle with him. "You've been running all night, sweetheart. You've been running for seven years. Put the painting down and come inside and I'll make you eggs. I remember how you like them. Scrambled, but *barely*, so they're still a little wet, and you pretend you don't want toast but you always eat mine."

"That's true," Xanthe said. His voice shook. "That's all true."

"Everything I know is true." It came closer, across the dead grass, and the grass did not bend under it. "That's what nobody understands. I'm not a lie, Xanthe. I'm the opposite of a lie. I am every true thing about her, kept safe, kept *warm*, while everyone else forgot. Do you know what your mother served for dinner the night we broke the birdbath? I do. Do you know what song Dad hummed in the truck? I do. I'm the twin who stayed. You're the twin who ran. And I forgave you the second you turned down the driveway, because that's what twins do."

The moon crested the tree line. The missing sliver of it was thinner now. Xanthe could swear he could see it closing, like an eye deciding.

"The ledger's in the house," the thing said softly. "Aldous's ledger. One signature. That's all consent ever was, all it's ever been — just a name, written kindly, next to another name. You wouldn't be losing her. You'd be *keeping* her. Forever. Isn't forever what you came home for?"

And here is the truth of Xanthe Eriksen, nineteen years old when he fled and twenty-six years old now and tired down to the marrow: some part of him wanted it. Some part of him had wanted it since the funeral home, since he lifted the lid and saw his own face aged wrong in the casket — wanted to stop running, stop counting, stop being the boy who left. The thing knew that. It had always known that. It had been built, in 1974, out of exactly that wanting.

"Yes," Xanthe said aloud, because Ivo had taught him that much — that the way to disarm a trap built from your own longing is to say the longing out loud, in daylight, where it loses its teeth. "I want my sister back. I want my family back. I want the house the way it was before. And wanting it isn't enough, and that's the whole point, isn't it? That's what Aldous learned. You can't paint someone back. You can only open a door and hope."

The thing's face went smooth as poured wax.

Then — like wax melting, like a mask slipping in heat — the smoothness broke, and underneath it, for one instant, one grace-note of an instant, was Wren. Not the copy. Not the comfort. Wren as she had been at the end, afraid, furious, *herself*, looking out through a face that was being worn like a coat from the inside.

Her hand came up — her real hand, trembling, human — and pressed against her own chest, over the heart, the way she used to when she was small and had a nightmare and needed proof of her own heartbeat.

"Do it," said Wren Eriksen, in her true voice, the voice that had raced him to the fence line and haunted him over strawberry jam and told him he was her favorite person. "*Please.*"

The moon's last sliver thinned to a thread.

Xanthe lifted the portrait.

The thing wearing his sister screamed with every voice the house had ever used — the scratching in the walls, the footsteps overhead, the screen door, the humming, fifty years of polite noises all screaming at once — and the capstone's crack yawned wide, and the well exhaled up at him, smelling of paint and pond water and 1974.

"TOMAS VINTER," Xanthe said, reading the name on the back of the frame one last time, giving it back its dignity first. "Returned by love. I'm sorry it went wrong. I'm sorry they kept you."

And he lowered the portrait into the well.

It went down slowly, the way a leaf goes down onto still water, though there was no water — only dark, only depth, only the long throat of the family's oldest silence. The drowned boy's painted eyes stayed on Xanthe all the way down, kindly, forgiving, until the darkness took even the white of the canvas and the well swallowed and was quiet.

The thread of the moon snapped shut.

Full.

The scream stopped mid-note. The thing wearing Wren stood in the silvered grass with its head tipped back, and the navy dress collapsed empty, folding down like clothes slipping off a hanger, and out of the collapsing shape rose something small and bright and bird-quick — a wren, an actual wren, impossibly out of season, out of hour, out of world — that circled Xanthe's head once, twice, three times, close enough that he felt the wind of its wings on his cheek, close enough that he laughed and sobbed in the same breath.

Then it flew toward the farmhouse, low and fast and joyful, straight through the wall of the east bedroom, and did not come out again, and did not need to.

The cold stopped rising from the well.

In the sudden stillness, Xanthe became aware of a weight against his side — slight, stitched, familiar. Ivo stood beside him at the capstone, lanky and button-eyed, his sewn smile catching the full moonlight, and he was coming apart at the seams. Not violently. Gently. A thread loosening at his shoulder, drifting up like dandelion silk. Another at his knee. His outline softening at the edges the way chalk softens under a thumb.

"You did it right," Ivo said, in his half-childlike voice, sounding pleased the way a child sounds pleased, entirely and without reservation. "You said the true thing. You always were better at that than Wren. Don't tell her I said so."

"Ivo—" Xanthe grabbed for him, and his hands passed through, and didn't, and did, like holding smoke that loved him back.

"It's all right. This is what I was *for*, silly. I was made to keep watch, and now the watching's done." A whole seam along his arm opened, and what came out wasn't stuffing. It was light. It was summer. It was 1996, and two nine-year-olds in a hayloft inventing a friend because the house was lonely and the house was hungry and somebody had to stand between. "You remembered me truly, at the end. That's better than staying. Nobody stays. But being remembered truly — that lasts."

"I don't want to forget you," Xanthe said, crying freely now, twenty-six years old and nine years old at once. "I won't. Whatever happens. I'll write it down. I'll write all of it down, the true version, the whole—"

"I know," said Ivo, smiling his stitched smile, unraveling into gold. "You already started. Page one. *Noises have explanations.*"

And he laughed — a sound like a screen door in summer, like jam spoons licked clean, like a birdbath breaking — and came apart entirely, into the moonlight, into the orchard, into whatever warm place invented friends go when their children finally don't need protecting.

Xanthe stood alone at the well.

The farmhouse behind him was silent — truly silent, for the first time in fifty years, the silence of a house exhaling. And in that silence, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the kitchen, he heard a sound that made his blood sing and his knees give:

His mother, putting a kettle on. Two cups clinking. Then, after a pause, a third.

* * *

He walked back through the orchard as the false moonlight softened toward honest dawn. The doors of the farmhouse, when he tried them, opened onto their own rooms again, each one minding its own business, each one forgetting the others. The endless hallway had folded itself away like a letter no longer needed.

At the porch steps he stopped, because sitting on the top step, in the graying light, was his mother — diminished, human, wrapped in a quilt, her face tracked with tears that had finally finished falling.

"He unmade himself," Xanthe said. "For us. For me."

"I know." She patted the step beside her. "He was the best of what we made. Sit down, little bird. The kettle's on."

He sat. Overhead, in the east bedroom window, sunlight gathered like something waiting patiently to be let in.

Tomorrow there would be graves to dig — two of them, one empty, one fifty years late. Tomorrow there would be letters to read aloud, and a confession to finish, and a town to tell the truth to, properly, at last.

Tonight there was tea, and a quiet house, and a wren-shaped absence in the east room that felt, for the first time in seven years, like peace instead of a wound.

The kettle began to sing.

Xanthe reached into his pocket for his notebook, to write it all down while it was still true — and found the pages falling open on their own to the tally marks. Hundreds of them, decades of them, counting down to tonight.

They were fading as he watched, line by line, like breath off glass — until only one remained, the very last mark, fresh and small and unmistakably not written by him.

Someone had drawn it in the shape of a tiny bird.
