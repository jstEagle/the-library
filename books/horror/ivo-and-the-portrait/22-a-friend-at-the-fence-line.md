## 22. A Friend at the Fence Line

The porch took three coats.

Xanthe had bought the paint in town — a soft gray-green called Heron's Rest, which he'd chosen mostly because the man at the hardware store had said, "That's what I'd put on that old place," and Xanthe had decided, somewhere in the last several months, to start trusting people who gave unprompted opinions about paint. It was a small faith. It was a beginning.

He rolled the second coat on in long, even strokes while the evening came down over the valley like a hand smoothing a bedsheet. The orchard stood behind him, heavy now with actual apples — not the waxen, unripe things the trees had produced for decades out of pure obligation, but real fruit, lopsided and freckled and sweet. He'd eaten one that morning standing under the oldest tree, juice running down his wrist, and had laughed out loud at nothing, at everything, at the sheer ordinary miracle of an apple tasting like an apple.

His mother had come out to watch him paint, then thought better of it and gone back inside. That was how it went these days. She was learning to be in the house without performing it. Some evenings she sat in the kitchen doing the crossword in pen — pen! — and got whole corners wrong and left them wrong, and Xanthe considered this the single most hopeful sign he'd ever witnessed. A woman who could abide a wrong answer was a woman who had stopped being afraid of imperfection, and a woman who had stopped being afraid of imperfection was, at long last, only his mother.

"Third coat tomorrow," she'd said through the screen door, apropos of nothing, and he'd said, "Third coat tomorrow," and that had been the entire conversation, and it had been enough.

* * *

He kept the notebook.

It lived on the kitchen table now, next to the salt, and he wrote in it every night — not tallies, never tallies, but small true things. *The east bedroom gets sun until four.* *Mrs. Halloran brought rhubarb and stayed forty minutes, which is twenty more than she used to.* *Mom laughed at her own joke before the punchline. Progress.*

Some nights he wrote down memories instead, deliberately, on purpose, because Ivo had taught him — in the last strange unraveling minutes of him — that remembering truly was its own kind of keeping. So: *Wren bit me at the county fair because I said her caramel apple looked like a brain. She was right to. It did.* And: *Wren hid my shoes on the roof once and we both got in trouble and neither of us ever told Mom it was her idea. She made me swear on Ivo's name. I didn't know then what I was swearing on.*

He wrote those entries slowly. They cost something. But the cost felt honest, like the ache in his shoulders from painting, like the blisters that meant real work on a real house.

The well behind the barn stayed capped. He'd had a mason out in June — a broad, cheerful woman named Petra who whistled while she worked — to seal the crack properly, mortaring a new stone into the old capstone where the family name had split down the middle. She'd asked if he wanted the name recarved.

"No," he'd said. "Leave it broken."

She'd raised an eyebrow, and he'd smiled, and she'd shrugged the shrug of a woman who had been paid to ask no further questions, and he had liked her immensely.

The broken name felt right. Families were broken names. The trick — the whole trick, the lesson of 1974 and every year after — was whether you papered over the break or let it show and built around it honestly. The Eriksens had papered for fifty years. Xanthe intended to leave the crack visible and live beside it, the way you live beside a scar: aware of it, grateful to be past it, unwilling to pretend it wasn't there.

* * *

In August, he drove to the county records office and requested the correction.

Wren Eriksen, born 1991, died — the date was still contested in his heart, but the paperwork required one, so he gave the day the thing wearing her face had first knocked on the inside of the east bedroom door, because it was as good as any and truer than most. Cause of death, he wrote, after a long moment: *taken.* The clerk, a young man with kind eyes and a stapler he clearly loved, frowned at the form.

"We can't put 'taken,' sir."

"I know," Xanthe said. "Put 'undetermined.' But I want you to know that between us, off the record, it was taken. And I want there to be a record somewhere, even just this conversation, that somebody knew."

The clerk looked at him for a moment. Then he opened a drawer, pulled out a plain index card, wrote *Wren Eriksen — taken, per her brother* on it in careful handwriting, dated it, and dropped it in a box of miscellaneous cards nobody would ever officially file and nobody would ever throw away.

"There," he said. "Now it exists somewhere."

Xanthe shook his hand and meant it. Small faiths. Beginnings.

* * *

September arrived the way September should — gradually, honestly, without bending any calendar to suit itself. The harvest moon rose full on the actual night the almanac promised, round and orange and utterly indifferent to the Eriksen farm, and Xanthe sat on the freshly painted porch steps with two cups of coffee and watched it climb over the orchard and felt nothing in the walls at all, because the walls were just walls now, lathe and plaster and mouse, and the mice were audibly, mundanely mice.

He still set out three cups some mornings. Habit, or grief, or love — the categories overlapped more than people admitted. He'd pour coffee into all three, sit down, remember, laugh at himself, and drink all three, which his mother said was going to stunt his growth, and he'd say he was thirty-one and fully grown, thank you, and she'd say that remained to be seen, and this exchange happened nearly every morning and neither of them ever tired of it. It was their liturgy now. Their new ritual, built to replace the old ones — not denial dressed up as routine, but affection dressed up as teasing, which was sturdier material altogether.

On the anniversary week, he did something he'd been putting off. He went up to the attic — the real attic now, the one with straight beams and honest dust and a window that opened onto actual sky — and he unpacked the box he'd carried down from the funeral home in the spring. Wren's things. Or rather, the things that had been sent home with the empty casket, which were, he'd verified one terrible afternoon, genuinely hers: her scarf, her chipped enamel pin of a wren, her half-finished crossword book with her pencil still tucked in the spine at a puzzle she'd abandoned mid-word.

He sat on the attic floor and finished the crossword. It took him an hour. She'd gotten eleven across — *seven letters, "what the dead do"* — and written GHOSTS in light pencil, then erased it, then written nothing. Xanthe filled in RESTING, crossed it out immediately, and finally settled on SLEEPING, which felt right, which felt like permission granted rather than lie maintained. Then he closed the book, set the wren pin on the windowsill where the sun could find it, and went downstairs feeling lighter than he had any right to feel, carrying grief the way you carry a well-packed box: carefully, with bent knees, knowing exactly what it weighed.

* * *

It happened on an ordinary Tuesday in late September, at dusk, when the light goes long and gold and forgiving.

He was on the porch with the third coat drying behind him — he'd gotten impatient and done it that very afternoon, against all advice, and it looked fine, it looked better than fine — when he saw the figure at the fence line.

Small. Child-sized. Standing just past the gate where the property ended and the road began, in the exact spot where, seven years ago, a lanky figure with button eyes had once waited for two kids to come outside on summer mornings.

But this was not Ivo. Xanthe knew that the way you know your own name. This figure was new-made — he could see it in the seams, which were fresh and slightly crooked, stitched by an inexperienced hand or perhaps by no hand at all, by the farm itself, by whatever patient leftover kindness lived in the soil here now that the bargain was ash. It was maybe four feet tall. It wore a shape like a child's drawing of a person: round head, simple body, two buttons for eyes that caught the last of the sunset and held it, glowing faintly amber, like lanterns seen through fog.

Its mouth was a stitched smile, but a shy one — smaller than Ivo's had been, less certain, the smile of someone who has practiced waving in private and is now attempting it in public for the first time.

It waved.

Xanthe's heart did several things at once, most of them contradictory. There was fear, of course — old fear, cellar-deep, the fear of things at the property line, things that wait, things that want in. Seven months ago he would have run for the truck. Six months ago he would have run for the well.

But he had learned things since. He had learned that the horror of this family had never been the strange things at the edges; it had been the lies at the center. He had learned that a door held shut out of fear breeds worse than a door held shut out of honesty. And he had learned — this was Ivo's last gift, given while coming apart gently in his arms, thanking him for remembering him truly — that friendship is not the same as possession, and love is not the same as keeping, and the difference between them is everything.

So he did not run. He set down his coffee, stood, and walked to the gate.

Up close, the little figure was even more homemade-looking. One of its button eyes was slightly larger than the other. Its stitching was red thread, uneven, enthusiastic. It smelled faintly of cut grass and turpentine — no, not turpentine anymore. Of paint. Fresh paint. Heron's Rest gray-green. It smelled like the porch.

"Well," Xanthe said. "Hello."

The figure waved again, harder, its whole arm involved, and Xanthe laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him, warm in the chest.

"You're new," he said. It nodded, the nod slightly too big for its head, and he crouched down to be at its level, forearms on his knees. "I'm Xanthe. This is my family's farm. Well. My family's farm is buried in the back field, honestly, and I'm just the current tenant of the truth. But it's mine to look after."

The figure tilted its head. Its button eyes regarded him with an expression he recognized, and recognizing it cracked something open in him that was not painful for once — something like the first blossom on a tree that had been dead for decades.

It reminded him of Ivo. Not in appearance — nothing would ever look like Ivo again, and he was glad of that, glad the world hadn't simply issued a replacement, because Ivo deserved to be irreplaceable. But in the *quality* of its attention. In the way it looked at him like he was the most interesting thing in the valley. Like being seen by him mattered.

"I had a friend like you once," Xanthe said quietly. "He was taller. Terrible at hide-and-seek — he always hid in the same wall. Wonderful at listening, though. He listened to us for years. Two kids who needed inventing, so we invented him, and he turned out more real than half the family." He paused. "He's gone now. We said goodbye properly. You can't replace somebody like that."

The figure's shoulders dipped. Just slightly. Shy, and sorry, and — he was almost sure — understanding.

"But," Xanthe went on, "that's not your fault, and it doesn't mean there isn't room in the world for somebody new. It just means you have to be your own somebody. Which, frankly, is better. Being somebody's echo is a rotten job. I've watched it done."

The figure straightened. Considered this. Then pointed one stubby stitched finger at itself, questioningly.

"Yeah," Xanthe said. "You're your own somebody. Congratulations. It's terrifying and great."

They regarded each other across the gate for a while. The dusk deepened. Somewhere in the orchard, an owl began its shift, punctual as a clerk with a beloved stapler. The first star came out over the barn, and the well behind it stayed silent and sealed and done.

Here was the thing Xanthe understood, standing there, and understood all the way down, in the place where the tally marks used to be: some friendships are taken. The old way — the Eriksen way — was taking. Bargains and ledgers and consent harvested like crops, friendships that were doors propped open for things to walk through. His family had spent fifty years mistaking company for covenant, had fed the dark at their table because they couldn't bear to eat alone.

But some friendships are given back. Returned, like the portrait. Lowered into the dark with love and honesty, and — somehow, impossibly, in the slow alchemy of a healed place — given back changed. Smaller. Kinder. New-made, out of the goodwill that accumulates in ground where nothing false has been buried lately. Not a door. Not a debt. Just a kid-shaped kindness standing at the edge of the light, hoping to be waved at.

You don't invite it in. That was the whole lesson, the final exam, and Xanthe passed it without bitterness, which he counted as his life's finest achievement. Inviting it in would make it Ivo's ghost, Wren's shadow, the farm's hunger wearing a new stitch. The door stays closed. The door *should* stay closed. Closed doors and open hearts — that was the arrangement now, and it was a good one, and it would hold.

But you can wave.

"Goodnight," Xanthe said. He lifted his hand and waved — unhurried, warm, the wave of a neighbor, of a man at a fence at dusk with paint on his sleeves. "It was nice to meet you. Come stand there anytime you like. I'll be around. I'm staying long enough to tell the truth properly, and that turns out to take years."

The figure waved back with its whole body, both arms, head wobbling, button eyes blazing happy amber in the blue dusk, and Xanthe laughed again, and turned, and walked back up the path to his porch.

At the door he glanced back once. The little figure was still there, silhouetted against the last light, patient as a fence post, content as a finished crossword. It gave one final small wave — a private one, the practiced kind — and Xanthe raised two fingers in reply, and went inside, and closed the door, and locked it, because locks are just honesty about boundaries and boundaries are just love with good posture.

Inside, his mother had left a plate covered with a towel — meatloaf, by the smell, her one reliable dish — and a note in pen: *Gone to Mrs. Halloran's for cards. Back by ten. Feed yourself. — M.* Underneath, smaller: *P.S. Third coat looks crooked on the north corner.*

"It does not," Xanthe said to the empty kitchen, grinning, and ate his meatloaf, and washed three cups — habit — and set out two for the morning, because habits could be edited, that was allowed, that was healthy.

He climbed the stairs past the east bedroom, door open to the moonlight, the wren pin catching silver on its windowsill, and he said goodnight to the room the way you say goodnight to a grave you've tended well. No answer came, and none was needed. Sleeping. At rest. Truly.

He got into bed. The house settled around him — beams ticking, mice commuting, the refrigerator humming its one note — all of it ordinary, all of it accounted for, every noise in the walls having a boring, beautiful explanation.

Through the window, he could see the fence line, and the small amber glow of two mismatched button eyes, keeping their friendly watch at the border of things, wanted nowhere, owed nothing, given back.

Xanthe smiled, pulled the quilt to his chin, and slept soundly — soundly, and deeply, and without a single tally — until morning came, right on schedule, exactly when it was supposed to.
