## 2. A Bell That Wasn't

The linen closet door was still open when Bram made their rounds at eleven.

They had closed it at nine. They were certain of it—certain the way one is certain of small rituals performed ten thousand times, the way a hand knows its own signature. Bram closed that door every night of every shift, had done so for seven years, and in all that time the door had never once opened on its own. It was a heavy oak thing, older than the house's plumbing, older than the hospice license framed in Matron's office, and it stuck in its frame with a sound like a held breath released. You had to *want* it closed.

It stood open now by perhaps four inches. The darkness inside was not the darkness of a closet. It was warmer. It moved.

Bram stood in the corridor with their torch beam on the gap and told themselves the ordinary story: the hinge had settled; the wood had swollen and shrunk with the season; Mrs. Aldous's wandering had extended further than usual before she slipped into her final sleep. There was always an ordinary story. Seven years of night nursing taught you that the dark was only ever unlit air, that sounds were pipes, that smells were drains, and that the dead did not come back—they simply stopped, like clocks no one wound. Bram had built a whole life on the load-bearing truth of ordinary stories.

But they did not close the door. They noted, distantly, with the part of the mind that watches the rest of the mind work, that they did not close it. They wrote *linen closet — check latch* on the handover sheet instead, which was what a person did when they wanted an ordinary story to hold their fear for them.

At half past eleven, Bram sat in the nurses' station with cold tea and the parish history Mrs. Ellery had lent them years ago, back when Bram first came to Marlowe House and asked, idly, why the village church had no bell. *Melted down in 1911*, Mrs. Ellery had said, pleased to be asked. *War metal, though there wasn't a war then. Recast into factory bells, they say. Some said the foundress paid for it herself, after.* After what, Mrs. Ellery had never said. The book confirmed it in dry Victorian prose: *the bell removed and broken up, Anno Domini 1911, the metal sold*. There had been no bell in St. Aldhelm's tower for a hundred and thirteen years. The tower held pigeons and a nest of jackdaws and nothing else.

Bram knew this. Bram had known it for seven years, the way you know the shape of a room you've never entered.

So when the bell rang at midnight, Bram's body knew before their mind did. Their spine went rigid. Their tea slopped cold over their wrist. Because the sound came rolling down the hill from the direction of the church, deep and bronze and unmistakably a bell—and it rang the way bells ring in dreams, each stroke arriving slightly before you expected it, as if it were ringing backward through time to reach you.

One. Two. Three.

Bram counted without meaning to. Counting was the house's language; everyone here counted. The dying counted backwards from ten, and the living counted forwards through the night, and somewhere between the two directions the hours passed.

Four. Five.

Bram went to the window. The village lay below Marlowe House like spilled salt, forty roofs and one church tower, and not a single light had come on. No dogs barked. No windows glowed. If a bell that had not existed for a century were ringing over sleeping rooftops, someone would have stirred. Someone would have cursed it, or filmed it, or called the police about vandals in the tower. The village heard nothing.

Six. Seven.

But upstairs, the residents were stirring. Bram felt it rather than heard it—a change in the texture of the silence, the particular rustle of many bodies turning in sleep. They left the window and walked the corridor with their heart going hard against their ribs, and the bell kept tolling overhead, though the ceiling between Bram and the sky was plaster and lath and slate, and no bell could sound so close unless—

Eight.

Unless it was ringing inside the walls.

Bram looked in on Mrs. Aldous first. She lay exactly as she had at eleven, hands folded, face smooth—but her lips were moving. Bram bent close and caught the shape of the words, silent as breath on glass: *four... three...* She had stopped at four last night. She was finishing her count now, or being finished, and as Bram watched, her mouth curved into a smile so gentle and so complete that Bram's eyes stung. In seven years of deaths, Bram had seen peace, relief, terror, confusion. They had never seen a smile like that. It was the smile of someone who has just remembered they are expected somewhere.

Nine. Ten.

Down the corridor, Mr. Petch turned in his bed and murmured something rhythmic, half-hummed. Old Sister Verity, who had not spoken a full sentence since spring, laughed—one short, clear note, like a spoon set down on china. And in the room at the end, the small room with the paper boats on the sill, Ivo slept with his blanket thrown off and his head tipped toward the wall as if listening, his lips parted around a word he hadn't yet learned.

The bell rang once more—not a number this time, but a long, low, resonant stroke that seemed to come from under the floor, from the foundations, from the hill itself—and then stopped. The silence afterward was enormous. It pressed on Bram's eardrums like water.

In the morning, Mrs. Ellery would tell them the night had been quiet as the grave. Bram already knew she would. The bell belonged to the house alone.

* * *

Bram did not go straight to the parlor. They made themselves finish the rounds first—temperatures, turns, the small liturgies of care—and this too was telling: they needed the routine like a handrail, because some part of them understood that whatever waited in the parlor would take the ordinary story out of their hands entirely.

The parlor of Marlowe House was a room designed for waiting. Faded chintz chairs, a clock that had run down years ago and been allowed to keep its own counsel, a table in the center polished by decades of elbows. Bram had crossed it a thousand times without looking at the table.

There was a ledger on it.

It was a thick book bound in green leather gone soft and dark at the corners, the kind of ledger a great house might once have kept for accounts or guests. It had not been there at eleven. Bram was certain of this the way they had been certain of the closet door, and the certainty was beginning to feel less like knowledge and more like a joke the house was telling at their expense: *you were certain, weren't you?*

The book lay open. The page facing up was headed in faded copperplate: *Marlowe House — Register of Those Who Stay.*

Nine names were written below it.

Bram read them twice, because the first reading refused to mean anything. Mrs. Aldous. Mr. Petch. Sister Verity. All nine residents—the dying, the dreaming, the ones whose families sent cards at Christmas and visited at funerals. Nine signatures in nine different hands, some shaky, some formal, one in a beautiful looping script that could only belong to quiet Miss Fenwick who signed her morphine charts with a flourish. And yet.

And yet beneath the ink, fainter than the ink, as if each name had been written twice—once by the signer and once by something practicing—ran a second version of every name. And the second hand was Bram's own.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. It was Bram's handwriting: the way they hooked their lowercase g's, the flat-topped t's, the slight forward lean of a hand always trying to write faster than it should. Bram had written thousands of notes in seven years, and their hand was as distinctive as a voice. Every name in that ledger wore their handwriting like a borrowed coat.

Bram touched the page. The paper was warm.

That was the moment denial curdled. Denial had been doing its work all night—*settled hinge, dream, migraine aura, someone's idea of a prank*—and it had been competent work, but warmth cannot be explained by a prankster, and a century-dead bell cannot be explained by anything, and a smile like Mrs. Aldous's cannot be explained by morphine. Bram stood in the parlor with their finger on warm paper and felt the ordinary stories collapse one after another like chairs kicked away.

They tried, anyway. They tore the page out. It was an absurd gesture, a child's gesture—tearing a page from a ghost's guestbook—and the paper came away with a sound like a sigh, and Bram crumpled it in their fist and stood there breathing, and then looked down at the book.

The page was whole. Nine names. The torn piece in Bram's hand was blank on both sides, ordinary paper, as if the ledger had simply declined to be wounded and handed them a decoy instead.

Bram dropped it. On the parlor carpet it lay curled like a dead leaf.

The house was collecting something. The word arrived in Bram's mind unbidden and unwelcome, dressed in the language of the handover sheet, the language of care plans and final wishes: *collecting*. The residents were signing themselves over, one by one, smiling in their sleep, counting down to a bell that existed nowhere but here. And the register said *Those Who Stay*, and the handwriting underneath was Bram's, which meant the house had already decided—or had long ago decided—that Bram's name belonged among them. That Bram, of all people, who had spent seven years learning how to be unseen, was already half-written into its pages.

Somewhere above, in a tower that held only pigeons, something shifted its weight.

Bram looked toward the corridor, toward the small room at the end where Ivo slept with his head tipped toward the wall, listening. Toward the linen closet door, which stood open still, four inches of warm darkness breathing into the hall.

The clock that hadn't run in years ticked once.

Then, softly, from everywhere and nowhere—from the radiators, the skirting boards, the space behind the wallpaper—a hum began. Nine voices' worth of hum, rising and falling together, patient as tide. It was almost a hymn. It was the verse before the last verse of something, and it was waiting.

Bram went to find out why a nine-year-old boy was smiling in his sleep.
