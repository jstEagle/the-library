## 7. Seven Years of Explanations

Bram had always been good at not being looked at.

It was a skill, like any other. It had to be learned, and Bram had learned it young, the way other children learned to whistle or ride a bicycle—by practice, by failure, by the slow accumulation of small successes. By seven, Bram could sit at a dinner table and be furniture. By ten, could walk the length of a school corridor without a single eye turning. By fifteen, could stand in a room full of relatives and be so thoroughly unremarked that when the family later described the gathering, Bram's name would not come up at all—not out of malice, not even out of forgetfulness, but out of a completeness of erasure so perfect it was almost an achievement.

The Hartwells had not wanted a third child. This was never said aloud. It was said in a hundred other ways: the photograph on the mantel that included only the eldest two, retaken twice when Bram somehow appeared at the edge of the frame. The school forms filled out in a hurry, the *mother's occupation* line left blank, the *number of children* line corrected with a pen scratch. The Christmas when Bram was eleven and their stocking simply wasn't hung, and no one noticed, and Bram stood in the doorway of the morning-lit living room holding their one small gift—bought with their own saved coins, wrapped in their own hands—and understood, with the terrible clarity children reserve for the facts no adult will confirm, that they had been subtracted.

So Bram subtracted themself first. It seemed only fair. It seemed, after a while, only sensible. If you erase yourself, no one else can do it to you. If you make yourself small enough, quiet enough, useful enough in an unremarkable way, you become a kind of ghost on your own terms—and a ghost on its own terms, Bram had reasoned, was at least a ghost with dignity.

That was why the night shift suited them. That was why, seven years ago, when the position at Marlowe House opened—a hospice at the edge of a village that appeared on no postcards, tending the dying through the hours no one else wanted—Bram had applied before they'd finished reading the advertisement. Nights were when the world agreed not to look. Nights were when being unseen was not a wound but a uniform.

Bram had thought, all these years, that they had chosen Marlowe House.

Standing in the linen closet corridor at three in the morning, with the doorframe breathing warm air against their face and Ivo's small hand curled trustingly around two of their fingers, Bram understood, for the first time, that they had been chosen.

* * *

The house had been mirroring them all along.

Bram saw it now, in the way one sees one's own handwriting in a stranger's letter—suddenly, horribly, unmistakably. The walls that breathed only when unwatched. The bell that rang for no one. The residents growing lighter, more translucent, humming themselves into a softness the world could no longer hold onto. The ledger with nine signatures in a hand like Bram's own.

*Like my own.* Bram had thought it an eerie coincidence, in the parlor, on that first midnight. It was not a coincidence. It was a confession. The house wrote the way Bram had learned to live: present on the page, absent from the record, a name that signed itself and signed itself and was never once *seen*.

Even the Room behind the linen closet—Bram had avoided thinking about why it unsettled them so much more than it frightened them. But the truth was simple and sat in their chest like a swallowed stone. The Room wanted what no one else would keep. And Bram was what no one else had kept. Bram was a thing the world had set down and walked away from, and the house had spent seven years holding them gently in its walls, the way a lonely child holds a stray cat it has not yet been allowed to name.

*It is not cruel,* the dying had said, the house speaking through Mrs. Aldous's papery mouth, through Mr. Vane's last breath. *It is only unbearably lonely. It believes a finished song means a finished family.*

Bram knew that belief. Had *been* that belief. Had spent thirty-four years mistaking being needed for being loved, mistaking usefulness for belonging, mistaking the night shift's quiet for peace rather than what it actually was: a room no one else had wanted, occupied by a person no one else had wanted, and both of them calling the arrangement kindness.

"You're doing the face," Ivo said.

Bram looked down. The boy was sitting cross-legged on the corridor floor, his back against the wall, his stuffed hare—Captain, one ear loved to a grey fuzz—balanced on his knee. He was nine years old and he had heard the bell his whole life, and he was looking up at Bram with the flat, patient directness of a child who has already learned that adults lie mostly by not saying things.

"What face?"

"The one where you go away but you're still standing there." Ivo patted the floor beside him. "Miss Prewitt does it. Before she signed. She'd be holding your hand and she'd be gone somewhere, and her hand would get lighter and lighter." He paused. "You're doing it with your whole self."

* * *

Later—after Ivo had been coaxed back to bed, after Captain had been tucked under his chin, after the boy had drifted off with the bell's absence ringing louder than any presence—Bram sat in the nurses' station and did not do the charts. The lamp made a small yellow room inside the dark, and Bram sat inside it and let the memories come, because the house had pried the lid off them and there was no closing it now.

The day Bram left home. Nineteen years old, one suitcase, standing in the hallway while the family ate dinner in the next room. No one had told Bram to go. No one had told Bram to stay. Bram had stood there a long time—Bram remembered the wallpaper, a pattern of faded roses, and how they had counted the roses the way they counted everything, because counting was a way of not feeling—and then had walked out, and the front door had closed behind them with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence, and no one had come to the window. No one had called their name. The silence had been so complete that Bram had, absurdly, been proud of it. *I did it so well they didn't even notice I left.* It had taken years to hear that sentence for what it was.

The first year at Marlowe House. Sister Agnes, then matron, showing Bram the linen closet on the first night. *We keep it locked,* she'd said. *Old buildings settle. Doors swell. Don't worry about it.* And Bram, who had built a life out of not worrying about things, had not worried about it. Had taken the key on the ring without asking why the key was newer than the lock. Had walked past that door four hundred nights a year and never once wondered what breathed behind it, because wondering meant looking, and looking meant being seen looking, and being seen was the one thing Bram had trained themself out of like a dog trained out of barking.

The residents. Seven years of them. Bram had been good at this work—truly good, not the counterfeit kind—and had told themself the goodness was the point. Ease the passing. Hold the hand. Count them down from ten when the counting came, and never flinch, and never weep where anyone could see, and never, ever ask the house for anything, because asking meant wanting, and wanting meant mattering, and mattering meant that someday the subtraction could happen again.

And the whole time—Bram pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes—the whole time, the house had been learning them. Learning the shape of their silence. Learning the exact dimensions of the hole where a family should have been. And it had done what lonely things always do with a shape like that.

It had tried to fill it.

* * *

The bell did not ring that night. That was worse. The silence had a held-breath quality now, the whole house leaning forward, waiting for midnight the way the residents once waited for their medications. Bram could feel the walls attending. Could feel the ledger in the parlor like a tooth that needed pulling.

Bram went down the corridor to Ivo's room, because the charts were impossible and the lamp was too small and there was nowhere else to put the enormous thing rising in their chest.

Ivo was awake. Of course he was awake. He was sitting up with Captain in his lap, and in the light from the corridor his eyes were very wide and very steady.

"I heard you thinking," he said. "Through the wall. It's loud."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Tell me." He scooted over, making a small Ivo-shaped absence in the blankets, and Bram—Bram, who had not shared a bed or a confidence or a single unguarded hour with another living soul in nineteen years—sat down on the edge of it.

"I've been invisible a long time," Bram said. It came out rough. "On purpose. It was safer. If no one sees you, no one can take you away, because—because they have to see you first, to take you. And this house—" Bram stopped. Started again. "This house has been doing what I do. Making itself unnoticeable. Holding things no one else would keep. And I think it learned it from watching me. Or I learned it from watching it. I honestly don't know which anymore."

Ivo considered this with the gravity of a judge.

"That's a very stupid plan," he said at last.

Bram laughed—a cracked, startled sound, the first in longer than they could calculate.

"You made yourself disappear so nobody could hurt you," Ivo said, "and now the house wants to make you disappear for real, and you were going to let it, weren't you. You were going to do the face. The whole-self face." His chin trembled, but his voice held. "Well, I see you. I see you *all the time*. You count wrong on purpose so the residents win. You put extra sugar in Miss Prewitt's cocoa even after she signed. You hum when you think nobody's listening and you hum *badly*. I see you, Bram Brightwater, and I am not—" his voice cracked and he pushed through it— "I am *not* letting you go into that Room. I don't have anybody either. That's the whole point. That's the *only* point. If we both disappear, then nobody was ever here at all, and that can't be true, because I *remember* you."

Bram could not speak. The stone in their chest had turned over and become something else, something warm and unbearable, and it took a long moment before they could put their hand on the boy's head, on the soft cropped hair, and say, "Then we'd better think of something better than stupid plans."

"Obviously," said Ivo, with nine-year-old scorn, and burrowed down under the blankets, and after a while his breathing went long and even, his hand still holding two of Bram's fingers, holding on the way small children hold on in crowds—as if letting go meant being lost forever.

Bram sat in the dark and did not do the face.

And that was when Bram heard it—faint, from the direction of the parlor, a sound like a pen nib whispering across paper, though no one was awake, though no one was left to sign.

Everyone had signed.

Everyone except Ivo.

And the ledger, Bram realized with a cold that started in the spine and spread outward like frost across a window, had begun turning its own pages—back to the first, back to the beginning, reading itself aloud in a hand like Bram's own.

It had not finished collecting. It had only finished the residents.

It was starting on the staff.
