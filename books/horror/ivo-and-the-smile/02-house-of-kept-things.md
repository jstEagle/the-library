## 2. House of Kept Things

The Vance house sat at the end of a lane that the rain had turned into a brown ribbon, and it was larger than Petra had expected — a long, low farmhouse with a slate roof gone soft at the edges and chimneys like two raised eyebrows. Smoke came from only one of them. The other stood cold, as if the house were saving its breath.

Edith Vance let them in without ceremony, which in Hollowmere, Petra was learning, was itself a kind of welcome.

"You'll have the blue room," Edith said, taking Petra's coat before she could protest. "It was my mother's. She'd have liked someone sleeping in it who asks questions. God knows nobody in this family does."

"Mother," said Marek, in the tone of a man who had lost this argument many times and had come to enjoy losing it.

"I'm making tea," announced a voice from the kitchen, and a woman appeared — Lottie, Marek's wife — wiping her hands on an apron patterned with cheerful hens. She was small and quick-eyed, with flour on one cheekbone, and she looked Petra over the way a woman looks over weather she intends to work around. "You'll be wanting something hot after that walk. Everyone arrives wet here. It's practically our civic greeting."

"Tea would be wonderful."

"Tea and toast and half a jar of last year's damson jam, because Marek will eat the rest if I don't give it away." Lottie smiled, and it was such an ordinary, generous smile that Petra felt some knot in her shoulders — the one she'd been carrying since the village went silent around Edith's song — loosen by a single turn.

Then Lottie's eyes flicked, just once, to the hallway behind Petra, and the smile stayed exactly where it was while everything else about her face went still. It lasted less than a second. Then she was all warmth again, steering Petra toward the kitchen with a hand between her shoulder blades.

Petra filed it away. That was what she did; it was the whole of her profession, really. Folklorists were magpies of other people's hesitations.

* * *

The kitchen was the warm heart of the house, and clearly the room where the family actually lived, whatever the front parlors pretended. A dresser crowded with mismatched crockery. A table scarred by decades of homework and bread-kneading. A clock that ticked with the self-satisfied authority of a clock that has never once been wrong, or has never once been checked.

Two children sat at the table doing sums.

"This is Ivo and this is Sela," Lottie said. "Twins. Don't let them tell you they're not identical, because they're not, but they enjoy confusing people."

"We don't look alike at all," said the girl solemnly. She had a gap where a tooth had recently departed and the air of someone conducting important negotiations.

"You look extremely alike," said the boy, not looking up from his arithmetic. He had his mother's quick eyes and his grandmother's mouth, set in a line of permanent mild grievance. "That's how we confuse people. Sela says we don't and then everyone stares at us trying to work out if we do."

"It works every time," Sela said, with satisfaction.

Ivo glanced up at Petra then — one glance, quick, assessing — and went back to his sums. But Petra had spent twenty years being looked at by strangers' children in strangers' kitchens, and she knew the difference between a child glancing at a guest and a child checking whether a guest was safe. There had been something careful in it. Something practiced.

"Your tape recorder," Ivo said, without looking up again. "It's got a red light."

"When it records, yes."

"Does it record everything? Even quiet things?"

"Especially quiet things. Quiet things are usually the most interesting."

He considered this. "Grandmother says some quiet things are better left unrecorded."

"Ivo," said Lottie, lightly, in the voice of a woman closing a door on a draft. "Sums."

Petra drank her tea and did not push. The tea was strong enough to stand a spoon in, and the toast came with butter and the promised damson jam, dark and sweet and slightly sharp, and for ten minutes the kitchen was simply a kitchen: Sela narrating her sums like a sports commentator, Marek coming in shaking rain off his shoulders, Edith sitting by the stove with her mending and saying nothing at all in a way that somehow anchored the whole room.

It was, Petra thought, the kind of household she had visited all over the country and never quite managed to belong to. There was a rhythm to it, worn smooth like a stone in a pocket. She found herself already composing the opening of a paper she would never write: *Domestic continuity as cultural preservation in declining rural communities.* And underneath the scholarly phrasing, something simpler and more embarrassing: *I wish I lived here.*

* * *

They gave her the blue room after supper, and it was lovely — a wide bed under a quilt stitched in faded hexagons, a washstand with a china jug, a window looking out over the garden to the dark shoulder of the hill. On the hill, black against blacker sky, she could just make out the squat shape of the well house, its cap like a closed eye.

She unpacked slowly, the way she always did, letting a room tell her about itself. And the room told her several things, none of which it seemed aware it was telling.

The first was the salt.

There was a thin, neat line of it along the threshold of the door, packed tight against the frame, the way you'd caulk a window against winter. It wasn't spilled salt. Spilled salt scatters. This had been poured deliberately, from a steady hand, and — she crouched to check — renewed recently. The grains were dry and separate, no dust among them. Someone did this often. Someone did this everywhere, probably.

She checked. Downstairs, every doorframe had its line: kitchen, parlor, pantry, the back door, even the little door under the stairs that opened onto nothing but coats and boots. The lines were discreet, tucked close to the wood, easy to step over and easier not to see. They weren't hidden, exactly. They were *unremarked*, the way a family stops seeing the crack in the ceiling or the water stain shaped like a rabbit. Part of the architecture of ordinary life.

The second thing the room told her was about the mirrors.

There was one above the washstand — a good Victorian piece, silvering going cloudy at the corners. It was draped with a linen cloth, folded neatly over the top of the frame so the glass hung covered like a face under a veil. In the corner of the room stood a cheval mirror, tall and elegant, wearing its own cloth like a shawled woman waiting out bad weather.

Petra lifted the cloth from the washstand mirror.

The glass showed her a tired woman in her late thirties with rain-frizzed hair and ink stains on her fingers, standing in a stranger's bedroom. Ordinary. Unremarkable. She lowered the cloth again, feeling oddly as though she'd opened someone else's mail.

At dinner she'd noticed the same treatment throughout the house — the hall mirror shrouded, the gilt-framed glass in the parlor draped like furniture in a dead relative's room. She'd assumed mourning, or modesty, or some local superstition about vanity. Plenty of cultures covered mirrors: after a death, during a storm, at night, so the soul wouldn't linger, so the dead wouldn't multiply. It was one of folklore's most portable beliefs. She'd catalogued it a dozen times.

But those households covered mirrors *sometimes*. Here they were covered the way smoke alarms are mounted — permanently, routinely, without discussion. And there was no recent death in the family that Petra had heard mentioned. Unless, she thought, watching Edith's hands move steadily through her mending, the mourning was for something else entirely.

* * *

"So," said Marek, finding her on the landing with her notebook open, an hour later. He had a candle in one hand and an expression of polite resignation. "You've seen the salt."

It wasn't a question, and Petra decided there was no percentage in pretending otherwise. "And the mirrors."

"Mm." He leaned against the banister. Up close he had his mother's steadiness and none of her ease; he looked like a man carrying a sack of grain up a hill he'd been climbing his whole life. "Lottie will be mortified you noticed. She likes guests to feel the house is normal."

"The house feels very normal," Petra said. "That's rather the remarkable part. Most people who live with this much precaution talk about it constantly, or never at all. Your family has managed both."

That earned her the ghost of a smile. "We're overachievers." He nodded toward the blue room. "Bed's aired. If the quilt smells of lavender, that's moth-repellent, not sentiment. Mother buys lavender by the sack."

"And the salt?"

"Moths," said Marek.

"In doorframes."

"Very determined moths."

They regarded each other. Petra had interviewed smugglers, snake-handlers, and one memorable woman in Cornwall who claimed to be married to the sea, and she knew when a source was stonewalling out of hostility versus stonewalling out of love. Marek's silence had the texture of the second kind. He wasn't protecting a secret from her. He was protecting her from the secret, the way you'd keep a child from the room where the dog had died.

"I'm not here to expose anything," she said quietly. "Whatever it is. I'm here because your mother sang me a song today that may exist nowhere else on earth, and because songs like that die when the last person who loves them dies, and I would very much like this one not to die. That's all. Whatever else is going on in this house—" she gestured, taking in the salt lines, the veiled mirrors, the whole quiet grammar of defense— "is yours. I didn't come for it. I won't write about it."

Marek looked at her for a long moment. The candle flame stood perfectly still between them, as if listening.

"That's a generous offer," he said at last. "And I believe you mean it. But it's not mine to accept, and it's not mine to explain. You'll want to ask Mother. Everyone does, eventually." He straightened. "Fair warning: she'll feed you first. Everything in this house gets fed before it gets answered. Guests, questions, ghosts. Mother's theory is that nothing makes trouble on a full stomach."

"Is that a folk belief?"

"That's an Edith belief. Older than any of the others." He raised the candle in mock salute and started down the stairs, then paused. "Dr. Reyes. The twins. If they say anything odd — anything at all — you'll tell Lottie, won't you? Not write it down. Just... tell her."

Something in the way he said it made the hair on Petra's arms lift, gently, like a draft from a window she couldn't see.

"Of course," she said.

* * *

She wrote up her notes by lamplight, the tape recorder beside her like a small black familiar. Outside, the rain had stopped and the wind had dropped, and the silence of Hollowmere pressed against the windows — not empty silence but *attentive* silence, the hush of a room full of people waiting for someone else to speak.

Her notes ran long. The lullaby's structure. Edith's phrasing, the little catch on the fourth verse. The village silence. The salt. The mirrors. She drew a small map of the ground floor and marked each doorway with a dotted line, and when she finished she looked at the map and thought it looked less like a floor plan than a diagram of a body's defenses — skin after skin, barrier after barrier, all of it arranged around the warm central kitchen where the family gathered, as if the whole house were an organism keeping its heart protected.

*Kept things,* she wrote, and underlined it twice. *This is a house of kept things.*

She was blowing out the lamp when she heard the children.

Not words — the walls were thick — just the murmur of voices through the floor, Ivo and Sela in their room next door, supposed to be asleep. One voice low and insistent, the other bright with giggling, then a sudden hush, then the low voice again, urgent now, and then nothing at all. Children's bedtime noises. Perfectly ordinary. She'd heard a thousand versions of it through a thousand thin floors.

Except that the giggle had stopped mid-note, cut off clean, the way sound stops when someone claps a hand over a mouth. And the silence afterward had a quality she recognized, because she'd stood in the middle of it that afternoon on the village green: the silence of people listening for something.

Petra lay in Edith's mother's bed under the hexagon quilt and listened too. The house ticked and settled. Somewhere below, a floorboard eased. The well on the hill kept its own counsel behind its capped stone eye.

She was nearly asleep when it occurred to her, with the peculiar clarity of the almost-dreaming, that in every household she'd ever visited with veiled mirrors, the covering was taken down in the morning. That the veil was for night, for grief, for danger passed.

Nobody in this house had uncovered anything since she arrived.

And nobody, she realized as sleep took her, had asked her to.
