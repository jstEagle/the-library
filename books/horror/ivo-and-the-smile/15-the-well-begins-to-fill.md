## 15. The Well Begins to Fill

Petra woke to the sound of the sea.

That was her first thought, half-asleep and unreasonable: *the sea has followed me inland.* Hollowmere sat a hundred miles from any coast worth the name, and the nearest body of water was a trout pond that Edith Vance described, with some contempt, as "a puddle that got ideas." But the sound was unmistakable — the long, patient breathing of deep water against stone, rising up through the floorboards of the guest room like something exhaling in its sleep.

She lay still in the dark and listened.

There was no plumbing below this room. Marek had told her so cheerfully on her first morning, when she'd asked about the pipes — "Nothing under you but old joists and older dust" — and had seemed pleased by the question, as if it were rare for anyone to show interest in the architecture of the house. So the sound could not be pipes. It could not be a drain, or a cistern, or any of the ordinary machinery a house uses to move water from one honest place to another.

It was beneath her, and it was moving, and it sounded patient.

Petra Reyes had spent nineteen years training herself to meet the inexplicable with a notebook rather than a scream. She reached for the notebook now, out of habit more than courage, and found her hand was shaking too badly to write anything legible. She settled for listening, cataloguing in her head: low-frequency, rhythmic, intermittent — no, not intermittent. Swelling. Like a tide deciding, very slowly, to come in.

At some point near dawn, the sound stopped. Not faded. Stopped, the way a person stops talking when they realize someone is listening.

She did not sleep again.

* * *

"You look terrible," Lottie said, sliding a plate of eggs in front of her at breakfast. "In a scholarly way. Very tenured."

"Thank you," Petra said. "I've worked hard on it."

Lottie Vance was the only member of the household who seemed to have decided that humor was a load-bearing wall, and Petra loved her for it. She was small and quick, with flour permanently on one forearm and an air of a woman who had looked directly at the family's secret sometime around her wedding day and concluded that if she didn't laugh about it she'd have to leave, and she wasn't leaving.

The twins were at the table too, Ivo and Sela, eating their eggs with the grim efficiency of children who had been told breakfast was not optional. Ivo kept glancing at the window over the sink. Sela kept her back to the hallway mirror — draped, like all the others, in a bedsheet — but Petra noticed she'd arranged her chair so that she never quite faced it.

"Frost last night," Marek said, coming in from the yard with an armful of kindling. He said it lightly, the way he said everything, but Petra had learned to hear the weight under his lightness. "Early for it."

"It's nearly December," Edith said from her chair by the stove. "Frost is what December does."

"Not on the inside of the glass," Marek said.

The kitchen went quiet in the particular way it went quiet in this house — not startled silence but practiced silence, a silence with grooves worn into it, like a path walked so often it needed no deciding.

"I saw it too," Petra said.

Everyone looked at her.

"On my window. When I got up in the night." She turned her notebook to the page she'd filled before dawn, because writing things down was how she made them real, and making them real was how she made them survivable. "I sketched it."

She'd drawn it three times, trying to get it right, and none of the attempts satisfied her. Frost had formed on the inside of her bedroom window in the shape of a woman — not a silhouette, not a suggestion, but a figure with the terrible specificity of a portrait: long hair spread wide as if floating, arms slightly away from the body, head tilted as though listening through the glass. As though pressed against it. The frost had been thickest where the mouth would be.

Sela put down her fork.

"That's her," the little girl said. "That's the wet lady. That's exactly her."

"Sela," Lottie began.

"She listens at windows," Sela said, with the flat certainty of a child reporting weather. "She told me she likes to know what people are saying about her. She says most people say wrong things."

"And what do you say?" Petra asked gently.

Sela considered this with great seriousness. "I say thank you for the verses."

Edith's teacup stopped halfway to her mouth. For a moment the old woman looked, not frightened, but stricken — the expression of someone watching a door they'd nailed shut begin, very politely, to open itself.

"We don't thank her," Edith said quietly. "We don't speak to her at all. We sing the count, we keep the ledger, and we do not converse. That is how it has been done, and how it will go on being done until—" She stopped herself, which was unlike her; Edith Vance finished sentences the way other people finished arguments.

"Until what, Gran?" Ivo asked.

"Until it isn't needed anymore," Edith said, and drank her tea, and the conversation closed over the subject like water.

But Petra had seen the frost-woman's face, and she had heard the sea under the floorboards, and she thought: *until the count finishes. Until the well fills. And then nothing anyone does or doesn't say will matter.*

* * *

After breakfast she did what she always did with dread: she took it outside and made it walk.

Marek accompanied her up the hill toward the capped well, ostensibly to show her the boundary stones, actually, she suspected, to keep her from going alone. The morning was hard and bright, the kind of cold that makes the world look newly minted, and the village below them lay gray and small, its chimneys smoking like a fleet of tiny reluctant ships.

"The sound," she said, when they were out of earshot of the house. "Under the floorboards. Last night. Water."

Marek didn't pretend not to understand. That was one thing about the Vances — they had given up pretending decades ago; they'd just substituted silence, which was cheaper.

"There's no water under the house," he said. "There hasn't been since they capped the well and cut the spring in my grandfather's time. But the house remembers where it sits. On dry years you'll hear it sometimes, a trickle, nothing. My mother calls it the house clearing its throat." He walked a few steps. "It's never been loud enough to wake anyone. Never been a tide."

"But it was last night."

"It was last night." He stopped at the boundary stone, a waist-high slab furred with lichen, and put his hand on it the way you'd touch the shoulder of an old horse. "Petra. Can I ask you something, and can you answer me honestly, because I think we're past the point where kindness helps?"

"Always."

"When you recorded Gran singing. When you played it back. Did you play it more than once?"

The question landed in her stomach like a swallowed stone. She thought of the nights hunched over the tape deck, headphones on, the second voice counting beneath Edith's song — ahead of it, always ahead — replaying the passage again and again, telling herself that repetition was rigor, that scholarship meant listening until the listening gave up its secrets.

"More than once," she said. "Many times."

Marek nodded slowly. He didn't blame her — that was somehow worse than if he had. He looked at her the way you look at someone who has stepped on a mine and hasn't gone off yet.

"The song is a count," he said. "You know that much. Every verse is a number. Sung once, properly, once a generation, it keeps the tally current. Keeps her counted. A thing that is counted stays where counting puts it." He rubbed his face. "Played back — heard over and over — it doesn't keep the count. It advances it. Each hearing is a verse sung. You've been singing to her every night, Petra. In your room. With your little machine."

The wind came down off the hill and moved through the dead bracken, and somewhere far below them, muffled by earth and distance, Petra would have sworn she heard the well swallow.

"How far?" she managed.

"Gran checks the ledger every morning. Two days ago the tally matched the year — the count was where two hundred years of careful singing had put it. This morning—" He shook his head. "This morning the count stood where it should stand in twelve days. At solstice. At the end."

Twelve days. Petra did the arithmetic automatically, the way she did everything: today was the ninth of December. Solstice fell on the twenty-first. Twelve days until the count completed. Twelve days until the well refilled with whatever had been drowned in it, and came up through the reflections, and climbed into the rooms where the children slept.

"Then we stop," she said. "We burn the tapes, we seal the house, we—"

"And then the count stands unfinished," Marek said, "and an unfinished count is worse than a finished one. Ask Brigid's grandmother's street what an unfinished count sounds like." His jaw tightened. "There's no stopping it now. There's only finishing it somewhere else, or finishing it here, or—" He stopped.

"Or?"

He looked down the hill at his house, at the smoke rising from its chimney, at the small warm life inside it that he guarded the way other men guard treasure.

"Or somebody holds it," he said. "Somebody takes the count and keeps it, the way the well keeps water. But nobody's ever volunteered, and I'm not going to ask anyone to." He started walking again, faster. "Come on. Cold's getting in."

* * *

That evening Petra laid everything out on the guest room floor like a general planning a retreat: her tapes, her field notes, four notebooks, the annotated transcription of the lullaby with its second voice marked in red ink down the margin like a wound. Nineteen years of work. Three fellowships. One book that reviewers had called "the definitive treatment." All of it, she now understood, was ammunition — and she had spent two weeks handing it to the enemy, verse by verse, playback by playback.

She sat cross-legged among her papers and forced herself to think like a scholar instead of a woman who wanted very badly to cry.

Fact: the count had jumped twelve days ahead. Fact: the acceleration correlated with her playbacks. Fact: the entity — the wet lady, the drowned singer, the woman in the frost — had begun appearing to the children directly, which she had not done, according to the ledger, in living memory. Fact: the water under the house was rising, or pretending to rise, or rehearsing rising.

Conclusion: Petra Reyes was not observing this event. She had never been observing this event. From the moment Edith Vance sang into her microphone, she had been participating in it, and there was a word for a scholar who participates without acknowledging it, and the word was *liar.*

"All right," she said aloud to the empty room, because talking to yourself was either madness or methodology and she preferred to believe the latter. "Options."

Option one: flee. Take the tapes, take the family, drive until the mirrors lagged no longer. But the count lived in the tapes now, and possibly in the notebooks, and certainly — she touched her own temple — beginning to live somewhere behind her eyes, where she sometimes caught herself counting under her breath and couldn't remember starting. Running carried it. Running was delivery.

Option two: destroy everything. Burn the tapes, burn the notes, burn the transcription. Buy time — Marek had said the mirrors had shuddered once before, decades ago, when a fire took the east wing and half the old recordings with it. Time was not victory. But time was twelve days' worth of children getting to be children, and Petra had begun to suspect that in this story, time was the only currency that mattered.

Option three: finish it. Sing the count to its end, here, deliberately, and meet whatever came up the well with salt and prayer and whatever the Vances had learned in two hundred years of keeping watch. She wrote this option down, looked at it for a long moment, and crossed it out, because she had seen the shape of the woman in the frost and knew exactly how well armed prayer was.

Below the floorboards, faint but unmistakable, the water breathed in.

And out.

And in.

Petra gathered her papers into a stack, squared their edges with the compulsive neatness of her profession, and set her hand flat on top of them. Nineteen years. Her whole adult self, built page by page. She thought of Ivo testing his reflection with a candle and telling no one. Of Sela thanking the thing at the window because no one had taught her there was anything else to say to it. Of Lottie scrubbing a mirror until her hands bled. Of Edith, ancient and upright, keeping a ledger for people who were mostly dead.

They had taken her in. They had fed her eggs and made jokes about tenure and let her sleep under their roof while she fed their nightmare with her scholarship. Whatever happened at solstice, it would not happen because Petra Reyes chose her archive over these people.

She would burn it. She already knew that, the way you know a thing you haven't admitted yet. But not tonight. Tonight she would sit with her life's work a few hours longer, and say goodbye to it properly, the way you sit up with the dying.

She opened the notebook to a fresh page and wrote, in her best field hand:

*December 9th. Twelve days. The well is filling. I am going to get them out of here if it costs me everything I have ever written.*

She underlined it twice, then — because even now, especially now, the archivist in her demanded precision — added a footnote:

*Note: subject (self) exhibits elevated pulse and an inexplicable urge to count backward.*

She stopped.

She had not written that footnote as a joke.

Somewhere beneath the floorboards, the water paused — listening — and then, softly, patiently, it began to rise.
