## 19. Solstice

**Petra**

The water had been climbing the stairwell for eleven nights, one step at a time, patient as arithmetic.

Petra sat at the kitchen table at eleven forty on solstice night with her notebook open and a candle burning low, doing sums she already knew the answers to. Twelve days of preparation. Salt in every threshold, mirrors turned to the walls or smashed outright — though smashing, they'd learned, only made more shards, and shards were still glass, and glass still remembered. Lottie had covered the windows with flour paste and brown paper like it was the war. Edith had walked the house each evening whispering the song *backward*, syllable by inverted syllable, the way Petra had transcribed it for them — a lullaby run in reverse, a count uncounted.

It bought time. It did not buy victory.

"You're doing that thing," Marek said from the doorway, "where you write and mutter and look like a woman defusing a bomb."

"I am defusing a bomb," Petra said. "A very old one. With terrible documentation."

He almost smiled. That was the miracle of this family, she thought — that after everything, after the handprints inside the glass and the wet lady at the landing and the ledger full of names that stopped mid-line, they could still make jokes over cocoa at midnight. Love practiced as defiance. She'd have written a paper on it, once. She'd have gotten the citation style wrong and Brigid would have teased her about it forever.

Below the floorboards, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of water moving where no pipes ran.

Petra looked at her notebook. At the last page, which had been blank when she burned the tapes, and which now held lines of handwriting in a slanted, dripping script that was not hers: numbers, climbing. The count had moved into her. She was the ledger now. She understood that the way you understand a stone is falling — not as theory but as weight.

And if she was the ledger, then maybe —

"Edith," she called softly. "I need to show you something. And I need you not to hit me with your cane."

* * *

**Edith**

Edith Vance had kept the ledger for thirty-one years, and in thirty-one years she had never once hoped. Hope was a luxury the wardens couldn't afford; her mother had said so, and her grandmother before her, women who died mid-count with their pens still warm. You did not hope. You tallied. You sang once, you salted the doors, you taught your children the shape of silence.

But tonight, looking at the girl's notebook — at the numbers crawling up the page in that drowned woman's hand — Edith felt something crack open in her chest that she'd boarded shut at nine years old.

"It's counting through her," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Old bodies learn to carry shocks the way mules carry loads. "The way it counted through the ledger. Through me."

"And if the ledger can move," Petra said, "then the count isn't fixed to this house. Or this well. Or—" She stopped. She was too kind to finish it, so Edith finished it for her.

"Or it could be carried away by someone willing." Edith set down her tea. Her hands, she noticed distantly, had stopped shaking for the first time since 1961. "You're proposing to take my family's curse home in your suitcase, Miss Reyes."

"I'm proposing to end it here, tonight, if I can. But I need to know what happens to a warden who stops being a warden."

Edith looked at this woman — this outsider who had arrived with a tape recorder and good intentions and had nearly ended them all, and who had then burned six years of her life's work in the yard without hesitation, feeding her scholarship to the flames page by page while Lottie held her coat against the cold. Who had then stayed. Nobody stayed. Wardens were born into it; nobody ever *chose* it.

"What happens," Edith said, "is what happened to my great-aunt Wren. She tried to take the count out of herself. Into the well, she thought — give it back, close the account." She paused. "The well didn't want it back. It wanted it *forward*. There's a difference, child, between putting a thing down and handing it on."

"Then I won't put it down," Petra said quietly. "I'll hold it. All of it. Every verse, every name, every year. If someone has to be the archive—"

"Don't." The word came out of Edith harder than she meant. "Don't you dare finish that sentence in my kitchen."

From upstairs, faintly, Sela began to cry.

* * *

**Lottie**

Lottie reached the twins' room first, salt packet in her pocket and heart in her throat, and found both children sitting up in bed, fully dressed, boots on.

"We heard it filling," Ivo said. He was ten years old and his face was gray as dishwater, but his voice was level. "The well. It's done waiting."

"The frost," Sela whispered, pointing at the window.

On the brown paper over the glass, frost had bloomed through in the shape of a woman — arms open, hair spread like weed in current. Not menacing. That was the worst part, Lottie thought, the part she would never be able to explain to anyone outside this house. The shape wasn't menacing. It was *waiting*. It looked like a mother at a window watching a road.

"Mum," Sela said, "she's not singing the scary verses anymore. She's singing the sad ones. The ones Gran never taught anyone."

Lottie's blood went cold and warm at once. "What sad ones?"

Sela's small brow furrowed, working to remember. "'Hush now, hush, my borrowed lamb / I sang for yours, you drowned my own—'" She stopped. "Mum, why would she sing that? Whose lamb?"

Downstairs, a door opened. Edith's voice, very old and very quiet, floated up the stairs:

"That would be mine to answer. And I think — God forgive me — I think tonight's the night I answer it."

* * *

**Ivo**

Ivo had decided, somewhere around the ninth step of water, that he was done being the boy who noticed things and told no one. Noticing things and telling no one had nearly gotten his sister taken. So now he noticed things and told everyone, constantly, whether they wanted it or not. His father said he'd make a fine town crier someday. Ivo intended to make a fine something that got people *out* of towns.

So when the landing mirror — the one they hadn't been able to pry off the wall, the one that had eaten their great-aunt's room whole — began to glow like moonlit water, Ivo grabbed Sela's hand and stood in front of her bedroom door and shouted down the stairs:

"SHE'S COMING UP! THE LANDING ONE! IT'S MIDNIGHT AND SHE'S COMING UP!"

He heard chairs scrape. He heard his mother's feet on the stairs two at a time. And he heard, beneath all of it, rising through the floorboards like breath through a reed, the sound of a well reaching its brim.

The mirror's surface went liquid. A hand came through first — pale, dripping, gentle, fingers spread like someone testing rain. Then an arm in soaked sleeve. The wet lady came up through the glass the way a swimmer comes up through a lake at dusk, unhurried, streaming, her hair dark with water that fell and fell and never pooled on the floorboards. She was beautiful, Ivo realized, in the way of things in old photographs. Younger than Mum. Tired past tired.

She looked at him standing guard before his sister's door, and something moved across her face that might have been surprise.

"You're the counter's child," she said. Her voice was water over stones. "You sing backward."

"Yes," Ivo said, terrified and furious and certain, all three at once. "And you're not getting past me."

"I don't want your sister." Sadness moved through her voice like a fish under ice. "I never wanted the children. I sang *for* the children. They were the ones who—" She stopped. Looked down the stairs, where Edith stood holding the ledger, and where Petra stood behind her with the notebook pressed to her chest like a shield, or a wound.

"Oh," the wet lady said softly. "*There* you are. Two hundred years, and there you are."

* * *

**Brigid**

Brigid had promised herself she'd wait in the truck with the engine running, and she had kept that promise for almost four minutes before marching back up to the house because she was not going to sit in a cold cab while the people she loved most in the world faced down a drowned folk healer without even a kettle on.

So she stood in the hall of Vance House at twelve minutes past midnight, snow melting off her shoulders, and watched the impossible assemble itself in front of her: the wet lady standing dripping on the landing; Edith below her with the ledger open at the first page; Petra with her notebook; the whole family gathered like witnesses at a trial two centuries overdue.

"Read it," the wet lady said. "Read the first entry. Aloud. She wrote it down; let her hear it read."

Edith's hands trembled over the yellowed page. Brigid saw her draw one long breath, the kind you take before diving.

"'Winter solstice, 1798,'" Edith read. "'We gave her to the well on the village's word. She went in singing. She has not stopped.'" Her voice caught. "'Entry to be continued by my daughter, and hers, until the debt is paid.'"

"Say my name," the woman said. "It's on the next line. No one has said it aloud since."

Edith looked at the line. Brigid watched her lips form it silently first, testing, the way you test ice.

"Maren," Edith said. "Maren Ostrowe. Healer. Mother of one."

The whole house shuddered. Not violently — deeply, the way a bell shudders after the strike. Water sounds rose through every wall at once, and every surface in the hall — the windows, the polished dresser, the brass kettle, the dark TV screen, the eyes of the portrait above the mantel — filled with the same reflection: a young woman's face, weeping without sound, *looking back* instead of merely showing.

"She was blamed for a fever," Brigid said, before she could stop herself. "In the parish records. They drowned a healer for a fever and then made her own song into a leash."

"They made it into a *count*," Maren corrected gently. "A count is a keeping. As long as they sang it, I was remembered. Do you understand? I was *counted*. It was the only mercy left to me, and it was also a cage, and I could not tell, anymore, which I wanted less." Her gaze settled on Petra. "And then you came with your machine, and your hunger to keep things, and you sang me louder than I have ever been sung. And the count ran ahead. And the well began to fill. Because a song that loud cannot stay under."

"I'm sorry," Petra said. "God, I'm sorry. I thought I was saving you from being forgotten."

"You were," Maren said. "That was the cruelty of it. Both things were true at once."

* * *

**Petra**

Here is what Petra Reyes understood, standing in a flooded-with-reflection hallway at a quarter past midnight, while a family she loved stood around her like a wall she had no right to hide behind:

The count could not be erased. She had proven that with fire — burn the tapes, and the song simply moves into flesh. It could not be abandoned; abandon it, and the well refills and takes its price in children, starting, always, with whoever is nearest. It could not be handed off like a hot coal forever, generation to generation, because a debt passed down is a debt never paid.

But a debt *taken up willingly* — freely, knowingly, by someone with no blood obligation and no fear and nothing left to lose except work that was already ash —

A willing ledger was not a cage. It was a witness.

"You need to be counted," Petra said slowly, working it through aloud, the way she worked through everything, annoying three generations of Vances. "Not kept. Counted. Fully. Every verse, forward and backward, every name, every year, all of it held by someone who chooses to hold it — because the cage only works when the keeper is unwilling, and the drowning only works when the singer is forgotten, and the only state that's neither is—"

"A living archive," Maren whispered.

"Somebody once told me documents want to be read," Petra said. Her voice cracked on it. "I intend to be very thoroughly read."

"No," Lottie said flatly. "No, absolutely not, pick someone else—"

"There is no one else, love," Edith said, and her voice broke clean in half. "Look at her. There never was anyone else. We just weren't brave enough to see it."

Marek stepped toward Petra and stopped, fists clenched, a man whose whole body wanted to argue and whose whole heart knew the argument was lost. "Twelve days ago I let a stranger across my threshold," he said hoarsely, "because my mother asked. Best and worst decision this family ever made."

"Both true," Petra agreed. "Can be both."

Ivo tugged her sleeve. He was crying, furiously, silently, the way boys are taught not to and shouldn't be. "Teach me the backward song first," he demanded. "Before you go. In case. In case I have to hold the line again."

Petra knelt and took his face in her hands. "You held it already, my darling. Longer than any adult in this house." She kissed his forehead. "But yes. First thing tomorrow. Homework."

Above them, the well crested. They felt it rather than heard it — a vast, satisfied settling, like a lung filling after years of shallow breaths — and every mirror in Hollowmere, every window, every darkened screen in every silent house, filled at once with water and light.

Maren extended her hand, dripping, patient, and waited — two hundred years of patience distilled into one open palm.

Petra took the notebook off her chest, hugged it once, hard, the way you hug a person, and passed it to Brigid.

"Get them out," she said. "All of them. Salted door, straight to the truck, don't look at anything that reflects. And Brigid—" Her smile was wet and bright and absolutely steady. "Take care of my footnotes."

Then she turned, took the drowned woman's hand, and walked toward the sound of the rising water — singing, clear and unafraid, the lullaby backward, uncounting the world one verse at a time.

Behind her, the front door opened onto snow, and the Vances began, terribly, to obey her.
