## 4. The Second Voice

The recordings arrived in a grey plastic crate, stacked like loaves of bread.

August found them on his desk the morning after his first full week at Hollow Fen Primary—eleven cassette tapes in labelled cases, each one bearing a child's name in Mrs. Vane's small, precise hand: *Hollis, M. — Night 4. Okonkwo, D. — Night 4. Wren, A. — Night 4.* Beside them sat a note on school letterhead, its tone brisk as a timetable:

*Mr. Underhill — As agreed, you will review the night recordings and flag any irregularities. The monitoring is a condition of our boarding provision. Irregularities are rare. Do not be alarmed by ordinary sleep-talk; children mutter. Return the crate to my office by Friday. — M.V.*

He had agreed to it during his interview, or he supposed he had. There had been a clause in his contract about *pastoral audio oversight*, and he had signed where the line was drawn, because that was what one did with lines. He was beginning to understand that at Hollow Fen, everything strange arrived dressed as procedure. The missing three children were *transferred*. The locked door on the lower corridor was *storage*. The nightly recording of sleeping children was *pastoral care*.

And so he sat in his small office above the assembly hall on Thursday evening, with a secondhand tape player borrowed from the music room and a mug of tea going cold beside him, and he began to listen to children sleep.

The first three tapes were nothing. Breathing. The tick of a radiator. Once, a boy named Hollis murmured something about a dog, and August smiled despite himself—a real, ordinary fragment, a dog somewhere in a child's past, trotting through a dream. He wrote *no irregularities* on his log sheet and felt briefly foolish for the ritual of it. This was what Vane had promised him: bureaucracy, tedium, the great flat machinery of institutional care grinding harmlessly through the night.

Wren's tape was fourth from the top.

He knew her by then, of course. Everyone did. She was the girl who had told him, with the flat matter-of-factness of a child reporting the weather, that *the well-teacher counts them too*—the words that had sent him back to the floor plans, hunting for a door that appeared on none of them and finding it anyway, warm under his palm like something breathing. She was nine years old, sharp-boned, watchful, with a habit of looking at adults the way an auditor looks at arithmetic: waiting for the sum to come out wrong. She had taken to greeting him each morning with a report of how many lights were left burning in the corridor overnight, delivered without comment, as though she were simply keeping him informed of facts he would want to know.

He slotted her tape into the player and pressed play.

For forty minutes, nothing. Her breathing was light and quick, the breathing of a child who slept near the surface of herself. A bed creaked once. Rain against a window, far off. August made notes about radiator noise and began to compose, in his head, the polite sentence he would use to ask Tilda whether the microphones could be repositioned.

Then, at 3:11 a.m., the second voice spoke.

It came in low, unhurried, from nowhere in particular—from everywhere in the room at once, as though the microphone itself had learned to speak. It was not cold. That was the first thing August understood, sitting very still in his office while the rain hissed softly behind the voice. It was not cold, and it was not cruel, and it was not strange in any way he could have reported to Mrs. Vane. It sounded like someone reading a bedtime story to a child who was nearly asleep. It sounded patient. It sounded, most terribly of all, familiar—the way a voice heard once, long ago, in another life, stays familiar without ever resolving into a face.

"Do you remember your mother's face?" it asked.

There was a pause long enough that August thought Wren was asleep still, that the question had fallen into her dreams and would lie there unclaimed until morning.

Then she answered.

"She's got a mark," Wren said. Her voice was thick with sleep but steady, honest, the voice of a child who has never yet learned that some questions should be met with silence. "Under her ear. Like a little comma."

"Like a little comma," the voice repeated, gently, tasting the words. "And do you remember her laugh?"

"It goes up too fast. Mum says—" A pause. A frown you could hear. "Mum *said*. It goes up too fast, like she's surprised."

"And your house. What colour was the front door?"

"Blue." Wren's voice had changed slightly. Thinner. "Blue. It was blue."

"Was," the voice agreed, softly, without malice, the way one agrees with a child about the weather. "And the kitchen table—what stood in the middle of it?"

"A bowl." Slower now. "A bowl with... there was fruit in it. Or—" The sound of a small brow furrowing against a pillow. "Or keys. I think it was keys. Was it keys?"

"What stood in the middle of it?" the voice asked again, with infinite gentleness, and August understood with a lurch that this was not correction. This was collection. The question was being asked twice because the first answer had been accepted, taken in, *kept*, and now the well—or whatever spoke for it—was reaching for the space the memory had occupied, checking that it was empty, smoothing the sheets.

"I don't..." Wren's breath caught. "I don't remember. There was a bowl. Why can't I—"

"You're sleepy," the voice said kindly. "That's all. Sleepy children forget. It's perfectly natural."

And here was the thing that made August stop the tape with his finger shaking slightly on the button: the voice was right. It was *perfectly natural*. Any teacher, any matron, any parent listening to a child fret over a forgotten detail at three in the morning would say exactly that, would smooth the hair and murmur *back to sleep now*, and no harm done, no harm done anywhere, because forgetting is what childhood does, forgetting is innocent, forgetting is—

August pressed play again.

The questions continued for eleven minutes. They came slowly, spaced out like drops from a tap, and each one was small, domestic, harmless-sounding: the name of a pet. The street where she'd lived before the fen. What her grandmother kept in the tin on the mantelpiece. And each time, Wren answered honestly—because she was nine, because she was brave, because no one had ever taught her that honesty could be a door left unlocked—and each time, when the question circled back, the answer had gone soft at the edges, uncertain, and then gone altogether.

By the end of the tape, the voice was asking different questions. Larger ones.

"Do you know why you are here, Wren?"

"Because of Dad's work," she said. Her voice was very small now. "Because of Dad's—because of..."

"Why are you here?"

Silence. Then, in a whisper that August would carry for the rest of his life, in a whisper that would wake him at 3:11 every morning thereafter no matter what town he slept in:

"Not yet. Not me."

"That's right," the voice said, warm as a hand on a forehead. "Not yet. Sleep now."

The tape ran on for another twenty minutes. Nothing else. Just breathing, lighter than before somehow, smoother, the breathing of a child sinking toward a deeper floor. August sat with his finger on the stop button long after the hiss of empty tape filled the room, and outside his window the fen lay black and flat beneath a moonless sky, and somewhere below the lower corridor, behind a door that appeared on no floor plan, he imagined—he could not stop imagining—something patient turning over what it had collected, filing it, counting it.

*

He did not sleep. He told himself it was the tea, and then the cold, and then the quality of the silence in the boarding wing, which was not an empty silence but a *full* one, the silence of many small breaths being monitored by many small machines. At half past five he gave up, dressed, and went down to the matron's station to wait for morning.

Tilda was already awake. She was always already awake; August suspected she slept the way cats do, in shallow installments, one ear open. She was a broad, grey-templed woman with forearms like a farm worker's and a face that gave away nothing except tiredness, and she took one look at him pouring himself coffee from her pot and said, "You listened to them, then."

"You know what's on the tapes."

"I change the tapes." She said it without looking up from the medication chart she was annotating. "I don't listen to them. Listening's your job. That's how they've set it up—everyone holds one piece, nobody holds the whole." She capped her pen. "What did you hear?"

August hesitated. He was aware, suddenly and acutely, of how it would sound spoken aloud: *a voice in the walls asking a child about her mother.* He was new here. He was the man who counted doors. But Tilda was watching him now with those flat, unhurried eyes, and he understood that she had asked the question the way a doctor asks *where does it hurt*—not because she doubted the pain, but because she needed to know its shape.

"There's a second voice," he said. "On Wren's tape. Low. Calm. It asks her questions and she answers, and—" His own voice snagged. "And the things she answers with stop being true. By the end of the tape she couldn't remember what was on her own kitchen table. It wasn't confusion. It was like watching someone take coins out of a purse, one at a time, and the purse getting lighter."

Tilda said nothing for a long moment. Down the corridor, a pipe knocked twice and went quiet, as though listening.

"Ask her," Tilda said finally. "This morning. Ask her her middle name."

"Why her middle name?"

"Because it's the kind of thing they never lose all at once." Something moved behind her eyes—not fear exactly; more like the weary recognition of an old pattern. "It comes back wrong first. Ask her."

*

Morning assembly smelled of chalk and porridge, and nineteen children sat in rows while Mrs. Vane read out notices in her dry, unhurried voice. August stood at the side of the hall and watched Wren across the room. She looked normal. That was the horror of it, he thought—that she looked entirely normal, pink-cheeked from the cold walk between dormitory and hall, whispering to the girl beside her, fidgeting with the hem of her sleeve. Whatever had been taken from her last night had been taken neatly, surgically, leaving no visible wound.

When the children spilled out into the corridor, he caught up with her by the coat pegs.

"Wren. Have you got a minute?"

She looked up at him with that auditor's gaze, weighing him. "Is this about the lights? Because I counted them this morning and it's the same as yesterday, fourteen on, two out, and the two out are the ones by the—"

"It's not about the lights." He crouched to her level, which felt important, though he couldn't have said why. "Just a question. Off the record. What's your middle name?"

Something flickered across her face. Not confusion—not at first. More like the expression of someone reaching into a pocket and finding it sewn shut.

"Aisling," she said. Then, immediately, less certainly: "No. Ashling. No—" She laughed, a short, startled sound with no humour in it. "Why did I say it differently? It's Aisling. With the—there's a way you say it. My mum always said it with the—" Her hands made a small, helpless shape in the air, holding something invisible that was no longer there. "With the *fada*, the accent over the—over the..."

She stopped. She looked at August, and for the first time since he'd known her, the sharpness in her face cracked, and underneath it was just a nine-year-old standing in a corridor holding a name that didn't fit her anymore.

"Mr. Underhill," she said quietly, "why did you ask me that?"

And August, who had spent his whole life making himself unremarkable, who had built invisibility like a wall around himself brick by brick—counting doors, keeping lights on, never staying anywhere long enough to be missed—found that he could not produce a single lie. Not because she frightened him, but because she deserved better than the ones he had.

"Because last night," he said, "someone asked you a lot of questions while you were asleep. And I think they took the answers with them."

Wren stared at him. Around them the corridor filled and emptied, children streaming past toward lessons, doors opening and closing, the ordinary machinery of a school morning running on rails. Somewhere below them, behind a warm locked door, something waited with the patience of water filling a well.

Then Wren did something he did not expect. She reached into the pocket of her cardigan and pulled out a folded scrap of paper, soft with handling, and held it out to him.

"I write them down," she said. "The true things. Before it can get them. You should hear what it asks *me* sometimes."

He unfolded the paper with careful fingers. In a child's uneven pencil, in a list that had clearly been added to night after night, were lines like:

*Mum's laugh goes up too fast.*
*Gran keeps buttons in the blue tin.*
*Aisling. A-I-S-L-I-N-G. Say it with the fada.*

And at the bottom, in fresher pencil, underlined twice:

*not yet. not me. NOT YET. NOT ME.*

August looked up from the paper, and Wren was watching him with her head tilted, studying his face the way she studied the corridor lights—as if counting, as if keeping track of something he couldn't see.

"You already knew," she said. It wasn't a question. "You've heard it too, haven't you. On the tapes." Her eyes narrowed, and the sharpness came back into her face like a blade returning to its sheath—but pointed now, aimed. "Mr. Underhill. When it asks me questions at night—it asks about *you* sometimes too."

The corridor seemed to tilt, very slightly, the way a floor tilts in a dream.

"What does it ask about me?"

Wren glanced down the corridor, both ways, the practiced caution of a child who has learned which walls might listen. When she spoke, her voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"It asks if I trust you."
