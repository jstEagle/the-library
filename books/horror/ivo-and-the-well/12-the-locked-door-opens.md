## 12. The Locked Door Opens

He had counted the doors of Hollow Fen Primary four hundred and eleven times since September, and he could have done it blindfolded, in the dark, in a fever. Nineteen along the upper corridor if you included the cupboard where Tilda kept the spare blankets. Fourteen below, including the boiler room and the fire exit that stuck. Two in the entrance hall, one of which was painted shut and hadn't opened since before the war, whichever war you chose.

The door at the end of the lower corridor was not among them.

It appeared on no floor plan. He had checked the plans twice—once in the county archive, once in the dusty folder Mrs. Vane kept in her office under a paperweight shaped like a sleeping cat—and both times the corridor simply ended in blank wall, rendered in confident 1950s ink, as though the architect had drawn it from memory of a building that had never quite existed. And yet every morning when August made his rounds, keys cold against his palm, breath fogging in the unheated lower hall, there it was: a door of dark wood gone grey at the edges, iron hinges furred with rust, and a lock plate polished bright by hands.

That was the detail he couldn't shake. Everything else about the door was decay—decay so thorough it seemed less like neglect than like surrender—but the lock plate gleamed. Something touched it often. Something wanted through, or something wanted in.

And it was warm. Not warm the way a radiator-adjacent door is warm. Warm the way a hand is warm. He had pressed his palm flat against it once, in October, and snatched it back with a small involuntary sound, because the warmth had a rhythm to it, faint but unmistakable, like pulse.

Tonight he had brought the crowbar from the maintenance shed.

He told himself it was for Wren. That was the arithmetic he'd been doing all week, the only sum that mattered: three children transferred, their houses hollowed out, their neighbors struck dumb with a forgetting so complete they couldn't even describe the shape of what they'd lost. His own name in a ledger dated forty years before his birth. The second voice in the recordings, patient as water wearing down stone. And Wren, sharp little Wren, who hid true memories inside counting rhymes like smugglers hiding coins in flour sacks, asking him in her flat, unflinching way: *Are you real, Mr. Underhill? Or are you already its?*

He hadn't been able to answer her. So tonight he would go and ask the thing that knew.

* * *

The school at midnight was a different animal than the school by day. By day it smelled of chalk and boiled cabbage and the particular sweet staleness of children's coats. At midnight it smelled of stone and old water, and the silence had texture, a kind of listening quality, as though the building were an ear pressed to the other side of a wall.

August moved through it with his ritual, because the ritual steadied him. Lights on—the emergency lamp over the stairwell, the dim bulb above the trophy case, the one in the matron's room left burning for Tilda, who never slept properly anyway. Doors counted. *Nineteen, fourteen, two.* He said the numbers aloud, barely a whisper, and felt the familiar small comfort of them, the way a rosary comforts even a man who no longer believes in what the beads stand for.

The lower corridor received him without comment. His torch beam slid along the wall—brick, brick, brick, the ghost-outline of a removed radiator, brick—and then the door, waiting exactly where it always waited, patient as everything else in this place.

Up close, in full light, he noticed things he'd refused to notice before. The grain of the wood didn't run the way wood grain runs; it swirled, concentric, like ripples around a dropped stone. The rust on the hinges wasn't orange but brown-black, the color of dried blood or old earth. And carved into the lintel, so shallow you'd miss it unless the light raked across it low and sideways, was a single word:

*UNDERHILL.*

His surname. His family's name. The name in the ledger, entered in a careful antique hand forty years before he was born, beside a column headed *given.*

"Right," he said aloud, to nobody, to himself, to whatever listened. His voice sounded very small in the corridor. "Right."

The crowbar's flat end went into the gap between door and frame. The wood was soft—softer than it should be, yielding like flesh over bone—and then, with a sound like a long-held breath released, the lock gave. Not broke. Gave. As though it had only ever been holding itself shut out of politeness, and had decided, tonight, to stop.

The door swung inward onto darkness, and warm air rose up out of it and touched his face.

It smelled of well water. Of course it did. It smelled of deep water and green stone and, beneath that, faintly, unbearably, of something sweet—hay, maybe, or cut grass, some summer smell from a childhood he could no longer fully picture. He stood in the doorway breathing it and felt grief arrive without any memory attached to it, grief like rain falling on ground where nothing is planted.

Steps led down. Rough-hewn, older than the school, older than anything the school pretended to be.

August took the lamp off its bracket, held it in front of him like a small brave sun, and began to descend.

* * *

The stairs went further than the building had depth. He understood this after perhaps sixty steps, when he calculated that he must now be beneath the foundations, beneath the fen's water table, beneath any sane place for a staircase to be, and the steps kept coming. The walls changed around him: brick gave way to fieldstone, fieldstone to something older, unhewn, damp-slicked, veined with white mineral trails like scars. Roots threaded through cracks overhead, pale and eyeless, reaching downward instead of up.

The air grew warmer. The pulse-warmth of the door was everywhere now, in the walls themselves, in the stone under his feet, a slow tidal beat he felt more than heard. Once he stopped and pressed his hand flat against the wall and let himself admit what he'd known since October: it was not like a heartbeat. It was a heartbeat. Or something that had learned to imitate one so well that the distinction had stopped mattering.

At the bottom of the stairs, the shaft opened.

It was wider than he expected—a chamber, really, circular, its ceiling lost in darkness above, its floor a disc of fitted stones laid centuries ago by hands that had cared about symmetry. In the center, where a well should stand, there was no wellhead, no bucket, no rope. There was only a round opening in the floor, rimmed with a low curb of stone worn smooth and glossy, and from it came the sound.

Not rushing water. A slow, deep movement, like breathing underwater. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep, contentedly.

And the walls—

August raised the lamp and turned, slowly, and the light found the names.

They covered every surface. Scratched into the stone at every height—knee-high, shoulder-high, high above where anyone could reach except by standing on another's shoulders—names upon names upon names, generations deep. Some were crude, gouged by children's nails or knives. Some were elegant, copperplate, done with care. Some had been scratched out and rewritten. Some had been scratched out and *replaced*, a newer name driven over the ghost of an older one like a nail through a shadow.

He walked the wall with the lamp. Dates ran alongside many of them, in styles that marched backward through time: 1961, 1934, 1902, 1887, dates in handwriting so archaic the letters themselves looked like different creatures. And interleaved with the names, smaller, in the same hands, little annotations, messages, last words:

*she asked for my blue eyes and I gave them gladly*

*mama I am sorry about the dog*

*I only wished to be good at something*

*tell Thomas I did not cry*

August's throat closed. He read on. Near the bottom of one wall, close to the floor, at the height of a kneeling child, a cluster of names in a shaky young hand, each followed by the same phrase, repeated like a refrain, like a prayer, like a counting rhyme:

*not yet. not me. not yet. not me.*

He crouched. The handwriting was his own.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. *His own*—the forward-slanting capitals, the way his *g* looped below the line, the little hook he put on the end of his *t*. He stared at it until the letters blurred, and then he did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do since the archive, since the ledger, since the entry that bore his name and a date decades before his birth.

He had written these. Or a version of him had. Or the version of him that the well had drafted, long ago, before the current draft, before the revision, before whatever he was now—had knelt here at the age of eight or nine and begged, in the only language the well respected, which was the language of names.

"Not yet," he said aloud, and his voice cracked in the middle of it. "Not me."

From the wellhead, the water-sound paused.

Then, gently, warmly, from everywhere and nowhere—from the walls, from the floor, from inside his own skull where a voice has no right to be—it answered.

*"Hello, Ivo."*

* * *

The lamp nearly slipped from his hand.

No living person knew that name. He was certain of it, certain the way you are certain of your own bones. His mother had called him Ivo—only his mother, only in the house, only in the years before—before what? He reached for the memory and found the familiar smooth absence, the polished blank where his mother's face should have been. He could remember that she had called him Ivo. He could not remember her saying it. He could not remember her voice at all. The well had taken the sound and left him the fact, the way you keep a receipt for something stolen.

"You know that name," he said. His voice sounded strange down here, flattened by stone, stripped of echo. "How do you know that name?"

*"You gave it to me."* The voice was low, unhurried, genderless in the way of things that predate the need for gender. It was also, horribly, almost familiar—not like a stranger's voice at all, but like a voice heard through a wall, through sleep, through the membrane between dreaming and waking. *"Long ago. You were small. You were frightened of the dark of your room, and your mother told you the dark was only a door, and doors open both ways. You liked that. You came down here and you told me your name so I would know whose door to knock on."*

"I've never been down here."

*"You have. Many times. You simply don't keep those visits anymore."* A pause, and in the pause the water turned over again, slow and satisfied. *"Memory is such a generous currency, Ivo. You spend it and forget you ever had it. Most of my keepers die rich in everything else."*

"I'm not your keeper." He heard how thin it sounded. He planted his feet anyway. "I'm a teacher. I came here to teach."

*"You came here to count."* Amusement moved through the voice like a fish under ice. *"Your family has always come here to count. Underhills built the school above me so the children would be near. Underhills kept the register. Underhills wrote the ledger. Every generation, one of you comes down these stairs and takes up the tally, and every generation, one of you tells yourself a story about why."* The voice softened, went tender, went maternal, and that was the worst thing yet, because it was borrowing a tenderness he had lost and could not check it against anything. *"You are tired, Ivo. You are half-spent already—I have been taking my portion nightly, politely, a spoonful at a time, since your first night under this roof. Your mother's face. Your reason for the classroom. Small things. Necessary things. You haven't even missed most of them yet."*

"Stop." It came out as a whisper. He made himself say it louder. "Stop taking."

*"I take nothing that isn't traded."* The voice was reasonable, patient, infinitely fair, the fairness of scales. *"That is the whole of the law, and your family wrote it, and it has kept this town fed and dry and prosperous for two hundred years while every fen around us drowned. Vane understands. Vane calls it stewardship. She is not wrong, Ivo. Someone must choose, or everyone is chosen. Would you rather it were random?"*

He thought of Wren. Her sharp face, her rhymes, the way she'd watched him across the classroom with those measuring eyes, deciding whether he was worth trusting and arriving, provisionally, at yes. He thought of the three transferred children whose neighbors could not describe them. He thought of the wall of names, and the child-height scratches, and *tell Thomas I did not cry.*

"What do you want from me?" he asked. "Tonight. Why open the door tonight?"

*"Because the audit comes,"* the voice said, *"and the audit requires a keeper's voice. Vane can manage the paperwork, but the count itself—the authorizing word—must come from an Underhill. That is the covenant. Your name opens the mouth."* The water sighed, rose slightly, lapped at the inner curve of the wellhead, and settled. *"So I have opened the door early, to give you time. To make you an offer first, keeper to keeper. Come down willingly. Take up the tally. Choose who is given and who is spared, and spare generously—you may spare the girl, if she matters to you. Spare whole classrooms, if you like. One voice per generation is all I strictly need. Yours would do beautifully. Yours already knows the way down."*

The chamber waited. The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere far above, muffled by fifty tons of earth and brick, a pipe knocked in the school's heating system, and the ordinary sound was so human, so shabby and mortal, that August's eyes stung.

"And if I refuse?" he said.

*"Then the audit proceeds without preference,"* the voice said, still gentle, still fair. *"And I will take whom I take, as I have always taken, and you will stand in your classroom afterward and not remember why the desks are emptier. You are already half mine, Ivo. Refusal costs you nothing you have left to lose."*

He looked at the wellhead. The darkness inside it was total, and warm, and breathing. He looked at the walls, at the thousands of names, at the small desperate refrain at knee height in his own handwriting. *Not yet. Not me.*

"No," August said.

The word fell into the shaft and did not echo. The water went very still.

*"Not yet,"* the voice agreed, and there was something new in it now, something he recognized from the recordings, from the tapes of sleeping children—the patience of a thing that has never once needed to hurry. *"But soon, keeper. And when it is you, you will say yes. You'll see. Everyone says yes eventually. Even the ones who scratch."*

August backed toward the stairs. He kept the lamp raised and his eyes on the wellhead the whole way, the way you keep your eyes on a dog you do not trust, and the voice did not speak again, but as he climbed—as the stairs took him up through stone and root and brick—he became aware of a sensation he would carry for the rest of his life, however much of it remained: the feeling of being *counted*. Slowly. Thoroughly. From the heels upward, like a measure being taken for a garment he had not ordered.

At the top, the unmapped door stood open behind him. Beyond it, the corridor stretched away toward the boarding wing, toward the nineteen small beds, toward the night's recording equipment humming in its cabinet, red lights blinking like a row of tiny patient hearts.

Tomorrow the audit began. Tomorrow Vane locked the doors and started the machines and the voice would move bed to bed like a toll collector.

August stood in the doorway, warm air rising past him out of the dark, and understood suddenly, completely, what he had to do—and how little time there was to learn how.

Behind him, far below, the well exhaled.

*"Goodnight, Ivo,"* it said, in his mother's voice.
