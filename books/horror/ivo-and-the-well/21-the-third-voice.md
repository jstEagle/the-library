## 21. The Third Voice

The coastal school was called St. Brendan's-in-the-Dunes, and it had the great mercy of being forgettable. August had chosen it for that reason alone. It sat at the end of a shingle road where the land gave up on itself, a low grey building with salt-scoured windows and a playground that smelled of tar and bladderwrack. The children there were ordinary in the way he had come to treasure: they lost their jumpers, lied badly about homework, cried over scraped knees, forgot things in the harmless way children forget—where they'd left a pencil, what day the swimming trip was. Their forgetting had no appetite in it.

He kept his rituals. Every morning he counted the doors—eleven, counting the boiler room, which was not really a door but he counted it anyway because counting it made eleven and eleven was a number he could hold in his hand like a stone. He kept the corridor lights burning through the night hours, all of them, though the caretaker complained about the electricity bill and August paid the difference out of his own salary without explanation. He slept with a lamp on. He wrote.

The writing had become the largest ritual of all. Each evening, after marking, he sat at the kitchen table of his rented cottage—the one with the view of nothing but dune grass and the grey lip of the sea—and he wrote down memories. Not a diary. A ledger, though he flinched from the word. He wrote them as precisely as he could: *My mother wore her hair pinned back with a tortoiseshell clasp shaped like a leaf.* He did not know if this was true. That was the point. He had written it down years ago, in Hollow Fen, in the weeks before the exodus, when he could still half-see her face, and now the sentence was all that remained—a fossil of a face, the bone without the warmth. But it was written, and what was written could be checked, and checked again, and so long as he checked it every night the well—or whatever wore its patience now—could not quietly take it from him and leave him agreeing with the theft.

There were four exercise books now. He numbered them. He kept them in a locked drawer and told himself the lock was against damp.

* * *

The sleep recordings were Tilda's idea originally, or rather they had been Hollow Fen's idea first and Tilda's second, and August had carried the habit away like a splinter too deep to dig out. At St. Brendan's there was no boarding wing, no night matron, no institutional machinery humming through the small hours. Most of the children went home at three o'clock to houses that existed and parents who could name them. But two of them boarded during term—brothers, the Ferrier boys, whose father worked an offshore rig—and the school kept a small sleepover room for them above the library, and the headmaster, a mild, tired man named Okafor who had hired August without asking a single question about why a teacher with his references would leave a good post mid-year, had asked whether he'd mind keeping an ear on the boys at night.

August had volunteered for more than an ear. He bought a recorder with his own money, a plain digital thing, and set it running each night on the shelf between the boys' beds. In the morning he reviewed the file. This was vigilance, he told himself. This was love wearing the mask of paranoia, or paranoia wearing the mask of love—he no longer troubled to separate them. Nine months he had listened. Nine months of snoring, of sleep-talk about football and a dog called Biscuit, of the small wet sounds of children dreaming. Nothing. Nothing, nothing, nothing.

Until the last week of November, when the file from Tuesday night ran forty minutes longer than usual, and August, skimming it at his kitchen table with the sea making its one grey sound outside, heard the second voice.

It came in at 3:11 a.m.

He had known it would. Some part of him had been waiting for 3:11 for nine months, checking the timestamp of every quiet night with a dread so constant it had worn smooth, like a pebble in the pocket you stop feeling until you take it out and look at it. And there it was. The Ferrier boy—Danny, the younger one, seven years old—murmured something in his sleep, the half-words children make, and then the voice came, low and unhurried, pitched beneath hearing almost, the voice of someone reading aloud in the next room. Patient. Familiar. Familiar in the worst way: familiar the way your own house is familiar in the dark, so that you can walk it blind, and then one night you reach for the banister and it isn't where it should be.

"Do you remember your mother's face?"

August's tea went cold in his hands. He did not move. Outside, the tide worked at the shingle.

Danny answered in his sleep, honest as children are honest, giving away some small true thing about a woman named Priya and a scar on her chin from a bicycle, and the voice said nothing back, only waited, the silence between questions stretching long and comfortable, a silence that knew it would be filled. Then:

"And your brother? Do you remember when he was born?"

And Danny, seven years old, asleep above a library by the sea, four hundred miles from Hollow Fen, gave that away too.

August played the file through twice, then a third time with headphones, the volume high enough to hurt. The voice was not the same voice he remembered from Wren's recordings—that one had been older, drier, with a grain to it like wind through a keyhole. This one was smoother. Younger. Almost gentle. But the questions were the questions. The grammar of it was the same: never a command, never a threat, always an invitation, always the tone of someone who has all the time in the world because time is what it eats.

He sat until dawn with the exercise books open in front of him, checking. *Tortoiseshell clasp, leaf-shaped.* Still there. Still his. He read every page aloud to himself in the grey light, and his own voice sounded strange in the cottage, thinner than it used to be, and he could not say how he knew that, only that he did.

* * *

He did not tell Okafor. There was no version of it that survived translation into headmaster language. Instead he did what he had learned to do in another life: he built a private census. He started recording the whole school—not just the boarders—by persuading Okafor that a listening device in each classroom overnight was a sensible safeguard against the break-ins that had lately troubled the village (there had been no break-ins; August reported the first one himself, a window found ajar, nothing taken). Within a fortnight he had six recorders running, and every morning he sat with his headphones and his cold tea and listened to empty classrooms breathing: radiator ticks, wind at the eaves, once the scrabble of a gull on a sill.

On the ninth morning, in the Year Four room, at 3:11 exactly, a child spoke.

Not a child of his. Not any child enrolled at St. Brendan's. The register held sixty-one names and he knew them all, had counted them, had counted them twice, and none of them was the girl whose voice came thin and sleepy through the recorder in the dark: *"not yet, not me."*

The old phrase. The phrase from the nineteen small beds, the phrase murmured in unison by children who did not know they were saying it. August played it back a dozen times. The girl's voice was faint, distant, as if recorded through a wall—which was impossible, because the recorder sat in a locked classroom and the nearest house was a quarter mile down the shingle road. Unless the wall it came through was not a wall at all.

Unless the recording was not coming from the room at all, but through it. The way water comes through stone.

He began sleeping at the school. He told himself it was to guard the recorders; he knew better. He slept in the staff room with the lights on, doors counted, and each morning collected the files like a man collecting evidence of his own haunting. The girl's voice appeared twice more that week, always at 3:11, always the same phrase, and each time it sounded a little closer, a little less filtered, as though whatever stood on the other side of the static was walking toward the microphone across a floor only it could feel.

Then, on Thursday night, the third voice came.

* * *

He heard it first as an error. That is how he would describe it afterward, in the fourth exercise book, in handwriting pressed hard enough to dent the pages beneath: *I thought I had mislabeled the files.* Because the second voice asked its question—"Do you remember why you became a teacher?"—and an answer came, calm and fluent and immediate, and the answer was given in a voice August knew better than any voice in the world, because it was the voice he heard every time he read his ledgers aloud, every time he counted his doors under his breath, every time he woke at 3:11 with the lamp burning and said his own name to the dark to make sure it still fit.

His own voice. Coming out of the recorder. Answering the well.

"No," his voice said, and it sounded rested, unhurried, easy. "That's not right. I became a teacher because of my mother. She taught Sunday school in a church with a green door, and I wanted to stand where she stood."

August sat very still. The sea said its one grey sound. His heart performed its work without him.

Because it was wrong. It was wrong the way a forged painting is wrong—every brushstroke confident, the whole composition false. His mother had never taught Sunday school. There was no green door in any memory he owned or had written down. And yet the voice gave it away so warmly, so completely, that for one vertiginous moment August doubted himself—doubted the ledgers, doubted the fossil sentences, doubted the man sitting in the dark with headphones—and understood, with a lurch, that this was the mechanism entire. This was how it had always worked. Not force. Doubt. You heard yourself give something away, and the hearing unmade you faster than the taking.

But the second voice was patient, and patient things are thorough. It asked again, gently correcting, the way a tutor corrects a promising pupil.

"Try again. What do you actually remember?"

And his voice—his voice—was silent for a moment, and when it spoke again there was something new in it, something August recognized with horror as honesty.

"A clasp," his voice said. "Tortoiseshell. Shaped like a leaf. But I don't—I can't see the face anymore. I wrote it down. I check it every night. Is that—is that remembering? If you have to check?"

"Oh," said the second voice, soft as a hand smoothing a sheet, "that's better than remembering. That's keeping. And keepers are what we've been looking for."

The recording ran on for eleven more minutes. August let it play to the end because he could not make himself stop it, the way you cannot look away from a wound. His voice gave away the green door it had invented, and then, unprompted, offered true things instead, small ones, testing them like ice: the smell of chalk and rain in the lower corridor at Hollow Fen; the exact weight of Wren's hand when she gripped his sleeve that last morning and asked him, matter-of-fact, whether he was real; the sound of Mrs. Vane's shoes on the stairs the night the audit began. Each offering was received in silence, and the silence had texture, had appetite, and August understood that he was listening to a transaction, a tithe, a keeper resuming the count one memory at a time—and that the voice on the recording was not being impersonated.

It was being worn. The way you wear a coat left behind by someone who isn't coming back for it.

Except he had come back. He was here. He was sitting in a staff room four hundred miles from the fen with the lights on and his name still in his own mouth, and somewhere—at 3:11, through walls that were not walls, across a distance the well had never respected—something was speaking with his voice and giving away his true things, and every word it surrendered made the voice more fluent, more himself, while the man in the staff room felt the words going out of him like heat from an open door.

At the end of the recording, the second voice said, "Good. Rest now. We'll continue tomorrow."

And his own voice—calm, fluent, almost fond—said: "I'll leave the light on."

* * *

August did not sleep that day. He walked the dunes until the cold got into his coat, and he made himself think it through the way he had once made himself count doors: methodically, one at a time, holding each thought up to the light.

The well had learned him. That was the simplest truth and he made himself say it plainly. Ten years of recordings at Hollow Fen—his voice on the night tapes, his interviews, his whispered checks of the register, the confession he had poured into the microphone on audit night, that flood of false selves meant to choke the tally—it had all gone down the shaft, all of it, true and false together, and the well did not distinguish. It had drunk ten years of August Underhill and learned the shape of him the way water learns the shape of the cup that holds it. Jamming the tally had not destroyed it. Jamming the tally had *fed* it. He had handed it contradiction upon contradiction, and contradictions are only identities it hasn't reconciled yet, and it had all the time in the world, and now it had reconciled him.

And it had followed the families. Of course it had. It had followed *him*. The girl saying *not yet, not me* through locked classroom walls—Wren? Was it Wren? He could not know; the voice was a child's and children blur—but someone had come out of Hollow Fen carrying the fen inside them, a seed, a spore, a sleeping question, and it had rooted here on the coast where the land gave up on itself, and it had reached for the nearest keeper's voice in the dark.

His.

He went back to the school in the late afternoon and took the recorder from the Year Four room and held it in both hands and very nearly threw it into the sea. He stood on the shingle with the wind pushing at his back and the grey water saying *take it, take it,* and he did not throw it. Because stopping the recordings would not stop the voice; it would only stop him hearing it. Blindness was not safety. Blindness was how towns like Hollow Fen happened—everyone not-listening, decade upon decade, until the count was done.

So he did the harder thing. He carried the recorder back inside, set it on the shelf, and prepared for the next night the way you prepare for a siege: lamp lit, doors counted, ledgers open, pen ready. And then, because there was one weapon left that he had not tried, one thing the well could not wear because it did not exist anywhere but in ink, he turned to a fresh page in the fourth exercise book and began to write—to Wren, or to whoever she was now, wherever she was, whether she remembered him or greeted him as a stranger; he addressed it to her anyway, because an address is a kind of anchor—

*If you are reading this, then I managed to write it down before it was taken. Here is what I know, and here is what I am afraid of, and here is the thing I have never told anyone:*

The pen stopped.

He stared at the blank space after the colon. He had intended to write about the ledger, the entry dated forty years before his birth, the Underhill name, the inheritance. But the thing he had never told anyone rose in him all at once, urgent and cold, and it was this: that last night, lying awake in the staff room at 3:11, he had heard footsteps in the corridor. Slow, unhurried, familiar. His own footsteps. And he had lain there with the lamp burning and counted the doors—eleven, counting the boiler room—and heard them pass his door without pausing, and heard, at the far end of the corridor, a door open that he had already counted shut.

Outside, the light failed over the dunes. Inside, the recorder's small red eye blinked awake for the night, patient as a kept promise, and August picked up his pen with a hand that was not quite steady and wrote, in the firmest hand he could manage, the opening line of the account that would become the book you are holding:

*My name is August Underhill, and I need to write everything down quickly, because tonight I heard my own voice leaving me—*
