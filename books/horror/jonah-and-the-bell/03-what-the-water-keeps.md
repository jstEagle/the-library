## 3. What the Water Keeps

The reservoir was lowest in October, and that was when Marrow's End let itself look at what it had done.

August drove out on a Thursday morning, after the show, after the four hours of sleep that had become his ration, with the windows down and the cold coming off the water in long grey breaths. The road along the eastern shore had no name anymore — the maps just called it Reservoir Access — but the old people still called it the Drowned Road, because before 1911 it had been the high street, and because the village had never found a way to say *flood* without flinching.

He parked where the gravel gave out and walked the last half mile on foot. The water lay flat as poured glass, holding the sky so perfectly that the hills seemed to float on nothing. It was beautiful in the way that things are beautiful when they are pretending. August had lived beside this water for thirty-one years and he had never once seen it move without reason. Wind came up the valley most afternoons, and still the surface kept its composure, like a face arranged for a photograph.

Halfway along the shore, the steeple broke the surface.

It always did, in the dry months — a grey stone finger rising perhaps nine feet above the waterline, crusted white with bird lime, crowned with the rotted stub of its wooden spire. From a distance it looked like a tooth. That was the word everyone used, though nobody remembered who'd said it first: *the tooth*. Children dared each other to swim out and touch it. Adults looked away from it at dinner tables near the window seats. And every year, when the autumn drawdown lowered the water another few feet, more of the church emerged — first the steeple, then the gable ends of the nave, then, in the driest years anyone could remember, the ridge of the roof itself, black with silt, running out into the deep like the spine of something enormous sleeping just under the skin of the world.

August stood at the margin where the drowned road went under and looked at it for a long time.

*You're being ridiculous,* he told himself. *You came out here because you couldn't sleep, not because a dead woman mentioned her own funeral on air.*

But that wasn't quite true either, and he knew it. He'd come because of the way Mrs. Halloway had laughed — bright and unbothered, a woman discussing a hat she'd bought — and because when he'd checked the parish notices afterward, wedged between the harvest sale and a notice about guttering grants, there she'd been. *Elsie Halloway, beloved wife of the late Gerald, passed peacefully Tuesday at Marrow's End Cottage.* Tuesday. Two days before she'd called him, cheerful as Sunday, to say the flowers had been lovely and the vicar had gone on a bit.

He hadn't told Ruth. He hadn't told anyone. There was no one to tell, really, who wouldn't either laugh or go quiet in the wrong way. In Marrow's End there were two kinds of silence, and he was learning to tell them apart: the silence of people who didn't know, and the silence of people who had decided long ago not to know any harder than they already did.

The tide of the reservoir — such as it was, governed by the water company's schedules rather than the moon — had pulled back farther than usual this year. The mudflats below the drowned road were exposed in a wide apron of grey-brown silt, fissured into hexagons by the sun, and out of that silt, here and there, rose the small dark shoulders of walls. Field boundaries. Garden paths. A line of gateposts marching into the water like mourners wading in after a coffin.

The old quarter. Lower Marrow's End. The part of the village that had been standing, occupied, lived-in, loved-in, on the night the sluices opened.

August stepped down onto the flats.

The mud took his boots to the ankle with a sound like a slow kiss, and each step forward required a small negotiation, a sucking release, a promise to the ground that he would keep moving. He didn't care. Something in him needed to be down here, in the place itself, rather than looking at it from the safe height of the shore. His grandmother — Edith, dead thirty years now, a woman he remembered mostly as hands and lavender and a voice on the radio — had grown up down here. Had walked these streets when they were streets. Had been eleven years old the year the water came.

Nobody talked about why the water came. That was the thing. The official story sat in the county archive in a folder as thin as a hymnal: *reservoir construction, compulsory purchase, controlled inundation, March 1911.* But compulsory purchase meant compensation, and compensation meant records, and the records — August had looked once, years ago, as a bored teenager — showed payments made to families who had already left, weeks before the flood, some of them paid twice, paid *extra*, paid in sums that made no sense unless someone wanted them gone quickly and quietly and without questions asked.

And the church — St. Brendan's, its dedication surviving only in the archive and in the name of the new church up in the town, St. Brendan-upon-the-Hill — the church had not been deconsecrated. He'd checked that too. Every other structure in the drowned quarter had been formally closed, its records transferred, its stones accounted for. The church alone had been left consecrated, left furnished, left with its bell in the tower, and flooded with everything inside.

You did not drown a living church by accident. You drowned a living church to keep something in it.

August reached the first of the exposed walls and put his hand on it. The stone was slick and cold and furred with a growth like dark velvet. Below his palm, almost lost in the silt, ran a course of dressed blocks — good work, careful work, the work of men who'd expected their wall to stand for centuries. He followed the wall's line until it turned, tracing the corner of a room, and stood inside what had been somebody's kitchen. An iron range lay tipped on its side nearby, its door hanging open, its firebox full of a century of grey water-smoothed mud. A child's boot stood upright beside it, leather gone to lace, laced to the knee, waiting for a foot that had been somewhere else for a hundred and thirteen years.

He should have felt nothing. It was archaeology; it was sad the way all drowned places are sad. Instead he felt the particular pressure of being *regarded*, and it raised the hair on his arms beneath his jacket.

He turned. The shore was empty. The road above was empty. The hills held their usual patient sheep, and the sky moved its usual clouds, and nothing anywhere was looking at him except, he realized, the tooth.

That was nonsense, of course. Stone doesn't look. But the steeple stood out there in the shallows with its lancet windows open to the air — the belfry louvres, he corrected himself, the openings through which the bell's voice had once carried across the valley — and light fell into those openings and nothing came back out of them, and the effect was of eyes that had been open so long they had stopped blinking. Watching without watching. The way a portrait watches. The way a well watches.

*When the bell rings, don't learn the words.*

The caller from last night had said it fast, low, a man's voice roughened as if by gravel or grief, and then the line had gone to static and the log showed no call received at all. August had told himself it was a prank. He was still telling himself that. But pranks had a shape, a punchline, a follow-up call giggling; this had only the words, sitting in him like a swallowed stone.

He waded on toward the steeple. The water deepened past his knees, then his thighs, shockingly cold, the cold of a depth that never saw sun. Silt swirled up around him and hung in the water like breath. Ten feet from the tower he stopped, because ahead of him the submerged masonry dropped away into green-black darkness, and he could see, faintly, far down, the pale suggestion of an arch — the tower's west door, maybe, or the entrance to the nave — and standing in that arch, or seeming to stand there, a shape.

Not a body. Not exactly. More like the memory of one, the way you see your own reflection in a dark window when the room behind you is lit. Tall. Still. Facing outward, facing him, at a depth where no diver could stand and no light should reach.

August's heart went hard against his ribs. He told himself: *silt formation. A pier. A pillar of the arcade showing through the water, distorted.* The drowned churches he'd read about — the ones in Italy, in Brazil — were full of such illusions. Statues became figures; columns became crowds. The eye wanted people down there. The eye had been trained by a hundred years of stories to expect them.

The shape did not move. That was somehow worse. Everything in water moves — weed sways, silt drifts, light bends. This held its position with the patience of furniture.

"Hello?" he said, and heard how foolish it sounded, and hated that his voice shook anyway.

The word went out over the water and came back to him off the hills, flattened, wrong, as if something between his mouth and the shore had eaten the warmth out of it. And then — he would spend years deciding whether this happened — the water around his legs went still. Not calm. *Still*, in the way a held breath is still. The little wind-chop that had been ticking against his thighs stopped all at once, everywhere, as if the whole reservoir had turned its attention inward, listening.

Somewhere below him, down in the dark where the nave ran out into the deep channel the engineers had never fully mapped, something made a sound. Not a roar, not a groan. A tone. Low, round, bronze-dark, felt in the bones of his legs before it reached his ears — a single sustained note, like the ghost of a bell struck once, gently, a hundred feet down.

It lasted three seconds. Then the water resumed its small ordinary motions, the chop returned, the light bent back into its usual lies, and the shape in the arch was only stone, only shadow, only the eye's old hunger.

August got out of the water faster than dignity allowed, mud sucking at him, cold wrung through him to the spine, and climbed the bank with his hands as much as his feet, and stood on the drowned road breathing hard with his heart slamming like a fist on a door.

Above him, on the shore path, an old man had stopped to watch. August hadn't heard him approach. He wore a waxed coat gone soft with age and a flat cap, and his face was one of those village faces that might belong to any of a dozen families — weathered, folded, watchful. August knew him vaguely: Tam Preece, or Preece's brother, one of the family that kept boats at the north slip.

"You'll want to come away from the edge before the drawdown finishes," the old man said. Not unkindly. "Water goes down another two meters by month's end. Whole nave'll be dry as a bone." He paused, and his eyes went past August to the tooth, and something moved in his face that was not quite fear and not quite grief. "They say we ought to feel proud of it. Heritage, they call it now. Divers come up from the city with their cameras." He spat, carefully, away from the water. "My grandfather helped open the sluices. Family don't say it loud, but we know."

"Why are you telling me this?"

The old man looked at him properly then, and August saw that his eyes were the pale washed-out blue of things kept too long in water. "Because you're a Voss," he said, "and I thought somebody should say it to your face instead of behind their hands. Your lot cast the bell. My lot drowned the church. Between us, Mr. Voss, we've kept this valley's oldest promise, and neither family's ever been thanked for it." He settled his cap. "Come the full drawdown, stay out of the nave. Whatever you hear down there calling — and you'll hear something, you Vosses always do — it isn't calling *to* you kindly."

He walked on along the shore path without hurrying, an old man taking his morning walk, and left August standing in the wind with the taste of bronze at the back of his throat.

* * *

He did not go home. He drove instead to the parish office on Chapel Street, a squat brick building that smelled of paper and mouse, and found it shut until Monday, and stood on the step feeling the absurdity of it — the great dread in his chest, and a CLOSED sign swinging gently on its nail.

So he went to the library, which kept the local history shelf, and pulled everything with *Marrow's End* or *1911* on the spine, and read for three hours in the window seat while the light went long and gold across the floor.

Most of it was the official story, retold with the smoothness of things nobody had ever examined. Reservoir construction. Progress. The sacrifice of the few for the many. Photographs of workmen at the dam, of dignitaries with silver spades, of the last service at St. Brendan's — and August stopped at that one, a grainy print of a congregation on the hillside above the water, all of them facing the camera, none of them smiling, and in the tower behind them, framed in the belfry opening, the dark curve of the bell itself, caught mid-swing or mid-hang, impossible to tell.

The caption read: *The final ringing, March 3rd, 1911.*

Except the bell had never been removed. He'd known that since childhood; everyone knew it; the divers' photographs proved it every few summers — the great bell still in its tower, green with age, forty feet down. So the caption was wrong, or the ceremony had rung something else, or —

Or the final ringing had happened *after* the photograph, after the water, down in the dark, by hands that had no business still existing.

There was one other book. A self-published memoir, spiral-bound, its plastic comb cracked, by a schoolmistress named Winifred Aldous who had taught in the old quarter and died in 1979. Most of it was recipes and reminiscence. But on page sixty-one, in a chapter titled *Things Best Left*, she wrote:

*The bell was recast that winter, you know. Not new — recast. Every family in the parish brought metal to the Voss foundry, as was the old custom, and the crucifixes and candlesticks and grandmothers' spoons all went into the crucible together, and what came out was our bell, and it had all of us in it, and we thought that was holy. We were young then and did not ask what else the custom was *for*. Old Man Voss wept at the pouring, and his son wept, and I remember thinking it strange that founders should cry at a casting, unless they knew what the metal would be asked to remember.*

*After the water came, some nights, if the wind was right, you could hear it. Underneath. Ringing where no ringer stood. The council said it was gas from the drowned ground, or echo off the dam face. The council said a great many things.*

*We did not sing in church again. Not the old hymns. Not ever.*

August closed the book and sat in the gold light with his hands flat on the cover, and outside the window, far off, the reservoir lay in the hollow of the hills, holding its glass-still surface, keeping whatever it keeps.

Three days until the next show. Eight days, if Tam Preece was right, until the drawdown finished and the nave stood open to the air for the first time in a century.

He thought of the shape in the arch. He thought of a voice on the radio saying *when the bell rings, don't learn the words,* and of Mrs. Halloway laughing about her funeral flowers, and of his grandmother's hands, and of a note struck a hundred feet down that he had felt in his bones before he heard it — felt, he now admitted to himself in the empty library, *recognized*, the way you recognize a family voice through a closed door.

Something down there knew his name.

And in eight days, the water was going to introduce them properly.
