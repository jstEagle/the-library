## 9. Grandmother's Frequency

The studio at 2 a.m. was a room made of small lights.

August sat in the glow of the mixing desk with his sleeves rolled up and his tie long since abandoned on the back of the chair, watching the VU needles doze in their green cradles. The show had ended an hour ago — *Nightfall Frequencies*, Sundays through Thursdays, 11 to 1, "the voice that keeps Marrow's End company" — but he never left straight away. Leaving meant the drive home along the reservoir road, past the steeple tip standing out of the water like a black tooth, and then the house with its counted doors and its burning lamps. Here, at least, there was the illusion of purpose: logging tapes, cueing records, letting the transmitter hum him calm.

He had not slept properly in nine days. Not since verse one.

He could still feel it in his teeth. That was the only way to describe it — the melody lived behind his molars like a seed lodged in the gum, and when he was very tired it began to hum itself, low and patient, and he had to press his tongue to the roof of his mouth and think of anything else. The whole village carried it now. He'd heard the postman humming it at the corner shop, three notes over and over, his eyes fixed on nothing. He'd heard Ruth humming it while she restocked the record shelf, and when she caught him looking she'd flushed and said, "Sorry — sorry, I didn't even—" and pressed her knuckles to her lips.

Everyone hummed it. Nobody had decided to. That was the part August couldn't stop turning over: nobody had *decided*.

He reached for the fader to run the overnight test tone — station policy, keep the carrier warm till six — and that was when the radio did something it had never done before.

The needle jumped.

Not the VU meters; the tuning needle. It slid off 104.7 all by itself, gliding down the band like a finger drawn along a piano key, past the dead frequencies, past the static, and settled at a point between stations where there should have been nothing but the hiss of empty air.

August froze with his hand still on the fader.

Static. Then — under the static, woven into it — a voice.

A woman, singing.

It wasn't broadcasting quality. It sounded close, close as a mouth at his ear, breathy and unhurried, and it was singing harmony to the hymn. Verse one — the verse the whole village carried in its teeth — but underneath it, around it, a second line he had never heard, rising and falling against the melody the way ivy climbs a wall. His skin went cold from the scalp down. Because he knew that voice.

Thirty years dead, and he knew it.

"Edith," he said aloud, stupidly, to an empty studio.

The singing stopped. In its place: silence with a shape in it, the way a room has a shape when someone is standing in the doorway.

"...You've got your grandfather's chin," the voice said at last. "And his habit of talking to radios."

August's hands were shaking. He looked at the board — every channel dark, no caller light, no line open, the transmitter reading normal — and then at the radio itself, the old valve set bolted above the desk that had come with the station, older than the station, older than him. The needle trembled at its impossible frequency like a compass finding north.

"This isn't funny," he said. Whoever *this* was. A pirate signal. A prank. A bleed from some other broadcast bleeding wrong. His mind offered explanations the way a drowning man offers prayers — automatically, without belief.

"No," the voice agreed gently. "It never is."

He knew her face from photographs: a narrow woman with dark hair pinned severely, standing beside his grandfather in front of the foundry gate, both of them squinting into a sun that had gone out of the world decades ago. Edith Voss. Dead of a winter fever when August was nine. He remembered almost nothing — the smell of lavender and coal smoke, a hand on his head, a hymn sung half under her breath while she shelled peas. He had spent his childhood assuming the hymn was just something grandmothers did.

Now the hymn lived in his teeth, and his grandmother lived between two radio stations, and August understood with a lurching, seasick certainty that these facts were the same fact.

"How are you—" he started.

"Don't waste the signal on that," she said. There was something practical in her tone, brisk and warm at once, a woman who had raised sons through hard winters. "Lines like this don't stay open long, and I've got things to say that can't wait for you to be brave enough to ask."

* * *

He pulled the chair closer. Absurdly, he checked the recorder — hit the button, watched the tape spools turn. If this was real, he wanted it on tape. If it wasn't real, the tape would tell him that too.

"I hear you," he said. "Go ahead."

A soft sound came down the line that might have been a laugh. "Always the broadcaster. Your grandfather was the same. Give him a crisis and he'd reach for a pencil." A pause, filled with faint static, like wind moving through a room he couldn't see. "August. Do you know what your family did? What we actually did, before the flood took the stories?"

"The foundry," he said. "Voss & Sons. Bells and church bronze. You cast the bell of St. Alder's in 1911, from scrap the village donated. I read the ledger."

"You read the ledger." Something in her voice turned, careful as a hand turning a page. "Then you know every family gave metal. The Harrows gave their candlesticks. The Marshes gave their plough fittings. My mother-in-law gave the crucifix off our own wall, Christ and all, because the priest said the bell should hold something holy if it was to hold what we needed it to hold."

"What did you need it to hold?"

The static breathed. When Edith spoke again, her voice had dropped, the way voices drop in sickrooms.

"Not what. Who. Or — no. That's not right either." He heard her take a breath, and the strange intimacy of it made his chest ache; thirty years dead and still breathing somewhere. "August, listen to me now, because I'll only say this once clean, and after tonight everything gets louder and harder and you won't trust your own ears. The bell wasn't cast to call people to church. It was cast to sing *down*. Into the ground. Under the old quarter, under where the water is now, there is something that sleeps, and it sleeps badly, and it wakes to song. Any song. It hears music the way you hear your name across a crowded room."

"That's—" He stopped. Every rational objection he owned lay in pieces around him already; Mrs. Halloway had phoned in cheerfully discussing her own funeral, and the village dogs had gone silent at 3:11 a.m., and his own name was waiting somewhere in the dark water with a date attached. Rationality had left the building weeks ago. "Go on."

"When they poured the bronze in 1911, my husband's father stood at the crucible, and my husband beside him, and their father's brothers, and the foundry men who shared our name and blood. Seven Vosses at the pour. And when the metal cooled and they struck it for the first time, the sound it made was the hymn — the hymn you're carrying in your teeth right now — and the seven of them walked up the tower and sang themselves into it."

August's mouth had gone dry. "Sang themselves—"

"Willingly," Edith said. "That's the word you're reaching for, and yes. Willingly. The bell needed voices inside it to sing the keeping-song forever, so the thing below would stay asleep, hearing the same four verses until the end of time. Four verses, August. Written and finished and closed, like a lid. The Vosses became the lid. That's why the flood came the year after — why the village drowned its own streets — because once the bell was in the tower and the singers were in the bell, the water was just another lock on the door. They buried the church alive and called it a reservoir accident, and everyone agreed to call it that, and the agreement held for a hundred years."

He thought of the parish ledger, the underlined name. *Inheritance revealed as obligation.* He had written those words in his notebook like a man writing about someone else.

"But the bell's ringing again," he said. "The bell's *underwater*. How is it ringing?"

"Because the lid is being lifted," Edith said simply. "Something pried it loose — I don't know what, and I don't want to know, and neither do you, not yet. The singers were holding it shut from inside, and now the song is coming apart, verse by verse, and the dead are calling you because the dead can see what's happening from where they stand. The song needs finishing, August. A fifth verse. And a fifth verse can't be written by the dead."

"Why not?"

"Because the dead already belong to the song. It takes a living mouth. A free mouth. A mouth with blood in it." The static rose briefly, and when her voice came through again it was closer, quieter, terrible with tenderness. "A Voss mouth. The bell remembers its makers, love. It's been remembering you since the day you were born. Why do you think you took a job talking into the dark to a town full of listeners? Why do you think the dead call *your* number?"

He had no answer. The hymn stirred behind his teeth, unbidden, three soft notes, and he clamped down on it.

"Edith." His voice cracked on her name; it felt enormous in his mouth, saying it aloud, the first time in thirty years anyone in his family had spoken it. "Are you in the bell? Are you one of the singers?"

A long pause. Long enough that he checked the needle, still trembling at its impossible station.

"I'm where the singers go when the singing stops mattering," she said at last. "I held my note for sixty years, and then the crack came, and we started to slip, and some of us slid out into whatever's next. I found the radio waves. They're thin here, but they run everywhere, like roots. Like water." Another breath. "I've been listening to your show, August. Every night. You're good at it. Keeping people company in the dark — it's the family trade, when you think about it. We were always in the business of voices that hold things back."

Outside the studio window, the reservoir road lay silvered under a fat moon, and far out, at the edge of the black water, the steeple tip stood motionless. But the water around it — he leaned forward, pressing his forehead nearly to the glass — the water around it seemed to be moving without wind. Turning. Slowly. Like something settling deeper into its pillow. Or like something beginning, after a hundred years, to turn over.

"Tell me how to stop it," he said.

The silence that followed had weight, the way deep water has weight.

"You can't stop it," Edith said. "That's the first true thing, so hold it carefully. The song is awake and it will be finished — by you or through you or despite you. What you can choose is *how*." Her voice frayed suddenly, a thread pulled too fine, and the static surged like a tide coming in. "I'm losing the frequency. Listen — the verses one through four, they'll come to you whether you want them or not, and you must learn them, do you hear me? Learn them perfectly. Because the fifth verse is a question, and you cannot answer a question you haven't heard, and you cannot fight what you refuse to know. Learn the song, August. All of it. And then come find the girl who wouldn't sing — Ida Marsh, ninety-nine years old, mute since the pouring. She's the only soul left who's heard the whole shape of it and kept her mouth shut. She knows where the edges are."

"The girl who—" But the static swallowed it.

"August." Her voice came through one last time, thin as light through a curtain, and it was singing — the harmony line, the one that climbed beneath the melody like ivy, the one she shouldn't have known and yet had known all her life, all her death. "When you hear me in the wire, don't be afraid. I'll be as close as I can get. Closer than the water. Closer than the bell."

The needle slid home to 104.7. The carrier hummed, ordinary, empty, faithful. The tape spools turned on, recording nothing but the hiss of a band where no one was broadcasting.

August sat in the small lights for a long time. Then he opened his notebook to a fresh page and wrote, in a hand steadier than he felt:

*Learn the song. Find Ida Marsh.*

Behind his teeth, softly, patiently, the hymn hummed the first three notes — and for the first time, he realized he could hear, underneath them, waiting like a held breath, the place where the fourth verse would end and the question begin.
