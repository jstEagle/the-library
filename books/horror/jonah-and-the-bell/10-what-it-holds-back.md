## 10. What It Holds Back

The voice came back on the third night after the second verse, and this time it did not sing.

August had been sitting in the studio with the transmitter off — a small rebellion he permitted himself between one and two in the morning, when the council's emergency mandate was least likely to be checked. The ON AIR sign glowed its dead red letter at him like an eye that had gone blind. He'd taken to keeping the microphone hot anyway, the fader up, because some part of him had learned in the last week that silence was not empty in Marrow's End. Silence was a held breath.

At 1:47 by the studio clock — which still ran, though it lost four minutes a day now, as if time itself were draining toward the reservoir — the dial of the old backup receiver drifted on its own. He watched it happen. The needle slid off 98.6, past static, past the ghost-whisper of a French station two hundred miles away, and settled into a band where nothing was licensed, nothing was numbered, nothing should have been.

And his grandmother said: *Hello, August.*

He did not jump. He'd stopped jumping three nights ago, when she first sang harmony to a hymn no living throat had taught her. Now he only leaned forward and pulled his chair closer to the console, the way he had as a boy leaning toward the kitchen wireless while Edith Voss told him stories about the old quarter before the water took it.

"Gran," he said. His voice sounded very loud and very mortal in the room.

*You've got verses one and two in you now,* she said. Not a question. *I can hear them sitting in you, the way you can hear stones in a boot.*

"I hum them," he admitted. "In my sleep. Ruth says half the village does."

*Half the village is being measured,* Edith said, and there was something in her tone he had never heard from her in life — not when he was six and she was dying slow in the back bedroom, not ever. Patience worn down to wire. *So you'd best understand what's being measured for. And I'll tell you once, properly, because your grandfather never told me until it was too late to matter, and I swore I wouldn't make his mistake.*

August reached for a cassette. His hand hovered over it — the instinct of thirty years in radio, record everything — and then he drew it back. Some things were not for archives. Some things were only for ears.

"I'm listening," he said.

*Good. Then don't interrupt, because I've waited thirty years to say it and the dead don't get many windows.*

*The flood wasn't an accident.*

The words landed in the studio like a dropped glass. August found he was holding his breath.

*Every schoolbook in this county says the sluice failed in the spring of 1911. Rain, they say. Bad engineering. A tragedy, they say, forty-one souls.* A pause, long enough that he checked the dial was still lit. *Your great-great-grandfather opened that sluice himself, August. With his own hands. And eleven men of the parish stood behind him with lanterns, and not one of them tried to stop him, and not one of them slept sound again for the rest of their lives.*

"Why?" The word came out cracked. "The old quarter — the Halloways lived there, the Marshes, the—"

*Ida Marsh's family. Yes. They knew. That's the worst of it, love — nobody drowned who didn't agree to drown. There were boats waiting at the high field. Every soul in the old quarter walked into their houses that last night knowing the water was coming up the lane by morning, and they locked their own doors from the inside.*

August thought of Mrs. Halloway on the air, cheerful as Sunday, describing the lilies on her coffin lid. Descended from Halloways. He thought of Ida Marsh, ninety-nine years old, mute since childhood, who had written on her chalkboard: *I KEPT MY MOUTH SHUT. IT TOOK MY VOICE INSTEAD.*

"What was down there," he said quietly, "that was worth forty-one lives?"

Edith's voice, when it came, had dropped low — the register she'd used for telling him about the thing under the stairs when he was small, the register that made the dark feel crowded.

*Nothing,* she said. *That's what you have to understand. It wasn't worth anything. It wasn't worth anything at all. It was worth less than nothing — it was a debt. And the bell rang it awake.*

* * *

*You know the bell was recast that same year,* Edith went on. *Everyone knows that much. Scrap bronze, the parish gave gladly — candlesticks, door knockers, a crucifix or two. What the ledger doesn't say is why the old bell came down in the first place. It cracked, they told each other. Bells crack. But bells don't crack from ringing, August. That bell cracked from the inside. Something pushed.*

*There are things under the world the way there are rats under a floor. Old. Patient. Mostly they stay down because down is what they know. But sound — sound travels through stone and clay and water better than anything man has made, and in the winter of 1910 that bell rang for a funeral, and it rang wrong, and whatever lies beneath the valley heard it and turned over in its sleep and began to climb.*

*Your family cast the new bell to sing it back down. That's what the Vosses have always done. We were founders — metal-men — going back further than the parish records go. There's a reason every generation of us knew the hymn. The song isn't music, August. It's a weight. Four verses laid over the thing below like four beams laid over a well-mouth, and the whole village humming them without knowing keeps the beams true.*

He thought of the sleepwalkers standing in the shallows, mouths moving. Thought of Ruth at her desk, eyes wet, humming. The whole village, unknowing, holding down a lid.

"And if the song stops?"

*Then the beams come off,* Edith said simply. *And it climbs.*

August stood. He couldn't help it; his body wanted to be upright, wanted to pace, wanted to do something with the cold that had gotten into his hands. He walked to the window. Below, the town lay quiet under its sodium lamps, and beyond the rooftops the reservoir was a black absence in the blacker land, and somewhere out there the steeple tip broke the surface like a tooth, and beneath the tooth —

"How close?" he asked the glass. "How close is it now?"

*Closer than it was,* said his grandmother. *It's been climbing since the bell woke fully. Verse one bought it a night's sleep. Verse two bought another. But listen to me, August, because here is the trap, here is the teeth of it, and I want you to hear it plain:*

*Stopping the song wakes it. Finishing it feeds it.*

He turned around. "Feeds it? You said the song holds it down. You said—"

*I said the four verses hold it down. I said nothing about the fifth.* Her voice frayed at the edges now, radio-ghost thinning, and she seemed to gather herself. *A song of four verses is a lid. A song of five is a door. Do you see? The first four press it down. But a finished hymn — a complete thing, sung whole by a mouth that means it — that isn't a weight anymore. That's an invitation. That's a name called correctly. The thing below doesn't want the song stopped and it doesn't want the song finished, and so it has spent a hundred years making sure both happen at once, in the order that serves it. Let the village stop singing — panic, flee, fall silent — and it rises. Let the song run to its end — and it rises fed, wearing the fifth verse like a key.*

"Then what's left?" His voice rose and he didn't care. "If stopping kills us and finishing kills us, what am I supposed to do, Gran? What's the move?"

Silence on the dead frequency. Long enough that he thought she'd gone. Then:

*There's a third thing,* Edith said. *There's always a third thing. It's just that the third thing always costs.*

*Your grandfather faced this same choice, August, in his own way, in his own year. He chose to do nothing. To keep the show running, keep the village humming, keep the lid on, and pass the problem down like a debt to his son, and his son passed it to me, and I sat on it thirty years because I was a coward and then I was dead and it was too late. And now the bell has put your name inside itself with a date beside it, and the date is coming, and doing nothing is no longer among your options. The bell has seen to that.*

"The fifth verse," August said slowly. "It needs someone to write it. To sing it first. That's what the date is."

*It needs a Voss mouth,* Edith said. *The bell remembers its makers. It wants the blood that poured it, and it wants that blood to finish the song willingly, sweetly, believing it's laying the final beam — and the moment the fifth verse leaves a Voss throat, the lid becomes a door and everything we ever held down walks up into the light wearing our voices.*

Outside, far across the water, something moved. Not a sound — a displacement, a sense of enormous mass shifting beneath the reservoir, the way you can feel a train pass through the soles of your feet from a house a street away. The lamp on the desk buzzed. The studio clock ticked, lost another fraction of a second.

August pressed his forehead against the cold glass.

"There's a third thing," he repeated. "Tell me."

*Not tonight.* And there — beneath the weariness — something almost tender, almost apologetic. *Tonight you need to sleep, and you need to eat, and you need to go and sit with that poor Marsh woman, because she's the only soul living who's heard the whole shape of the song, question and all, and she paid more than you can imagine for it. She knows about the third thing, August. She's known since 1911. She's been waiting fifty years for a Voss with the spine to ask.*

The dial began to drift. The needle sliding home toward 98.6, the dead frequency dissolving back into static, and Edith's voice stretched thin as taffy across the widening gap:

*Don't let them finish writing it, love. Whatever you do at that date on the bell — don't give it what it's asking for. Find the answer that isn't yes.*

Then static, clean and ordinary, filling the studio like water filling a lock.

August stood a long time with his hand flat on the receiver's warm wooden case, the way you'd rest a hand on the flank of a horse that had carried you hard. On the desk, the studio clock ticked off another losing minute. 2:03. 2:04. Somewhere beneath the town, beneath the fields, beneath forty-one locked doors sleeping under sixty feet of green water, something shifted its weight and settled in to wait, patient as bronze.

He looked at the frequency dial. Then at the phone. Then at the chalk-dusted number Ida Marsh's nephew had pressed into his palm outside the parish office, and which had sat in his wallet all week like a splinter he hadn't worked up the courage to push.

Tomorrow, he thought. No — not tomorrow. Tonight. Before the sun came up and made the daylight lie to him again about how much time there was.

He picked up the phone and dialed, and far across the dark water, in a narrow house with its lights burning, a ninety-nine-year-old woman heard the ring and reached, before it could sound twice, for the chalkboard she kept by every chair.
