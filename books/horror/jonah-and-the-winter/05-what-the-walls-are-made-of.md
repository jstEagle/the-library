## 5. What the Walls Are Made Of

I've opened a lot of walls in my career. You develop a relationship with the insides of buildings. Most of them are honest: fiberglass batts gone grey, mouse middens, a child's tennis ball that fell through a gap in 1987 and has been waiting there ever since like a message in a bottle nobody sent. Buildings keep their secrets in the walls the way people keep theirs in the backs of closets, and ninety percent of the time the secret is disappointing.

Winterhallow's walls were not disappointing.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me do this properly, in order, the way I'd write it up for the file — because writing it up is how I keep my footing. Facts, sequence, no adjectives where a measurement will do. That's the religion I was raised in: if you can't count it, don't believe it.

* * *

I came back on Thursday with a borescope, a moisture meter, and a plaster saw, because Tuesday had ended with me standing in front of a hallway mirror holding my wrist up to my face like an idiot, and the only cure for that kind of afternoon is work. Physical, dusty, unambiguous work. There is no lag in a wall cavity. A wall cavity contains what it contains.

Maren Voss watched me lay out my tools on a drop cloth in the upstairs corridor with the expression of a woman observing a very earnest child build a sandcastle below the high-tide line.

"You'll want the east wall," she said. "Between the nursery and the linen cupboard."

"Is that where you've heard the scratching?"

"I didn't say I'd heard scratching." She folded her hands on her cane. "You said scratching. Your client said scratching. I said the house remembers."

"Mrs. Voss, with respect — either there's something in your walls or there isn't."

"Oh, there's something in my walls." She smiled at me, and it was a lovely smile, warm and crinkled and entirely wrong, the way a beautifully wrapped box is wrong when you can hear something shifting inside it. "I've never disputed that. I simply wouldn't call them *my* walls anymore. Go on. I'll be downstairs if you need tea. Or afterwards, when you need tea. You strike me as someone who needs tea afterwards."

She went down the stairs one deliberate step at a time, and I stood in the corridor listening to the house. It was doing its immaculate silence again — that held-breath quality, the sense of a room paying attention. No pipes ticking. No wind finding the eaves. Old houses are supposed to creak; creaking is just a building talking in its sleep. Winterhallow wasn't asleep. Winterhallow was listening.

The east wall ran about four metres between two doorways, papered in a faded rose pattern that someone, sometime in the last century, had decided was timeless. I knocked along it with a knuckle, reading the hollowness the way you'd read braille. Solid, solid, solid — then a change at chest height, roughly a metre wide, where the knock came back with a different voice. Not hollow exactly. Full. The way a drum is full.

I checked the moisture meter out of habit. Dry as a sermon. Whatever was in there hadn't come in with a leak.

Here's the thing I want on the record: I did the sane thing first. I drilled a pilot hole — three millimetres, dust extraction running, the whole professional ritual — and fed the borescope camera through into the cavity, expecting insulation, expecting lathe, expecting the ordinary archaeology of a house this age.

The screen showed me hair.

Not a clump of hair. Hair the way a wall shows insulation — packed, layered, installed. Long strands combed flat against the inside of the plaster, running horizontally in courses, like someone had built the wall around it deliberately, course by course, the way a bricklayer lays brick. Brown going grey going white as the courses rose toward the ceiling. Two hundred years of it, maybe. One head per generation, laid in like rebar.

I am not a woman who startles easily. I once found a starling colony behind a pub ceiling and ate my lunch underneath it. But I sat down on the drop cloth for a minute, because my knees had filed a complaint.

Then I got back up, because the alternative to looking is worse than anything you find by looking. That's another article of faith. It has taken some damage lately, but I'm keeping it.

* * *

The plaster saw made short work of it. I cut a neat inspection panel — I'm not a vandal; whatever else was true about this job, I intended to leave the wall better sealed than I found it — and pried the section free, and the smell came out first.

It wasn't decay. That's what I want to be clear about, because decay I could have handled. Decay has a grammar I know fluently. This smelled like the inside of a jewellery box, like cedar and lavender and something faintly mineral, the way old coins smell, or the air in a cave. A preserved smell. A *kept* smell.

The cavity was packed from stud to stud.

Hair, yes — that was the outer layer, the facing, like wallpaper made of heads. Behind it, fingernail parings. Thousands of them. They'd been sorted by hand, I could see that immediately, the crescents laid tip-to-tip in long chains that spiralled inward, each chain a slightly different size. Big ones, adult ones. Small ones, child-sized, pale as rice grains. Someone had spent hours — years — arranging the trimmings of everyone who'd ever lived in this house into patterns inside the wall, the way other families arrange photographs in albums.

And threaded through the parings, tucked into the gaps like letters into a drawer, were scraps of paper.

I lifted one out with tweezers, because by now my hands had opinions of their own and had voted against touching things directly. Heavy rag paper, foxed at the edges, ink gone brown. Handwriting — several different handwritings, actually, which I noticed before I registered the words. A spidery Victorian copperplate. A schoolroom scrawl. A rounder, softer script that looked like it belonged to someone who wrote slowly and meant every word.

The words were:

*Hush now, hush, the dark is deep,*
*the house will hold you while you sleep.*

A lullaby. A fragment of one, anyway — two lines, nothing more, the scrap ending mid-phrase as though torn on purpose.

I took out the next scrap. Different handwriting, same song, further along:

*What walks above shall walk below,*
*and what we keep, we do not know—*

Another. Another. I laid them out on the drop cloth in what felt like chronological order, oldest paper first, and the lullaby assembled itself across two centuries of handwriting like a relay race. Verse after verse. The same verses Maren had hummed fragments of at our first meeting, though I hadn't known then that they were fragments of anything. Verses about sleeping and keeping, about doors and names, about a resident who mustn't wake. Children's rhymes with load-bearing dread in them, the kind of thing you sing to a baby without thinking, except these had been written down and hidden in a wall with hair and nails, which is not what you do with a song you're not afraid of.

Every verse but the last.

I know how that sounds — dramatic, convenient, the horror-novel shape of a thing. But I counted. The scraps form a complete structure: each verse answers the one before, rhyme answering rhyme, the whole song visibly building toward a close the way a staircase builds toward a landing. And then the scraps stop. The last three I found were all attempts — I could see that too, see the hesitation in the ink. Three different hands, three different generations, each starting the final verse and stopping after a line or two, as if the pen had been taken out of them.

One scrap just said, in pencil, in a shaky modern hand: *cannot remember how it ends. She sang it but I was small.* Underlined twice.

The scratching started while I was reading. That low, patient rasp from deeper in the wall, the sound that had brought me here. I looked up at the open cavity, at the packed courses of hair going back into the dark beyond my inspection panel, and the sound was coming from behind them. From further in. From wherever the wall kept whatever part of itself it didn't show.

Then I reached in to move the last scrap of paper, the pencilled one, and the scratching stopped.

Not faded. Not paused. Stopped — completely, instantly, the way a conversation stops when the person you're gossiping about walks into the room. I took my hand off the paper. The scratching resumed, further away now, almost sulky. I touched the paper again. Silence.

I did this eleven times. I have the tally in my notebook, because the notebook is where I live. Eleven for eleven. The wall full of hair and nail parings and two hundred years of a half-finished lullaby did not want me handling the song, and it was willing to hold its breath to make that clear.

"Right," I said out loud, to the wall, to the house, to whoever was on shift. "Noted."

The house, immaculate and cold, said nothing. But the temperature in the corridor dropped by a degree, and I want to stress that I checked — I carry a thermometer, I told you, counting is my religion — and it dropped by exactly one degree, which is somehow worse than ten, because ten degrees is weather and one degree is *intent*.

* * *

That evening I sat at my kitchen table with the scraps photographed and catalogued, cross-referencing the handwritings against the family tree I'd pulled from the county archive, and I realised I had a problem: the pencilled note — *she sang it but I was small* — was recent. Recent-ish. Fifties or sixties, judging by the ballpoint over-pencil corrections. Which meant someone alive might remember the woman who couldn't finish the verse. Might remember being the small one.

Which meant Maren Voss had some explaining to do, and I intended to ask her for it in the morning.

But that night, going through the photocopies I'd requested from the archive — the archivist had thrown in a box of miscellaneous Voss material, deeds and letters and one exercise book, saying "it all came from the house, take it or leave it" — I found the diary.

* * *

*Maren Voss, aged fourteen. An exercise book with a floral cover, entries in fountain pen. The relevant pages, transcribed exactly:*

*12th March 1963.*

*Tomas has been strange since Christmas. He says the walls talk to him but he says it smiling so I don't know whether it's a joke and I daren't ask. Mama caught him picking at the plaster in the east passage and slapped him for it, which she has never done, and he cried and she cried and neither of them would say why.*

*I asked Granny about the song tonight. We were doing the mending and I sang the first verse without thinking, the way you do, and Granny went still as a dead bird. She said, "Where did you hear that." I said from her, obviously, from everyone, it's the house song, you sing it to babies here the way other people say grace. She put down her sewing.*

*"Never sing it past the fourth verse," she said. "Not to yourself, not to him, not to anyone. Especially not to Tomas."*

*I asked why and she said because the song is a rope and the last verse is the knot, and a rope without a knot holds nothing, but a rope with a knot holds everything, and she would not explain further. She burned her toast and she never burns toast.*

*14th March.*

*Tomas scratched a hole in the wallpaper today. Just a little one, behind the wardrobe, the size of a shilling. I saw it when I was hiding from piano practice. Inside the hole there was hair. Long hair, brown, and I thought it was a mouse nest but mice don't comb their nests flat and tidy.*

*I showed Tomas and he went white and said, "You weren't supposed to find that," in a voice that wasn't his voice, older, and then he blinked and laughed and said he meant I'd get in trouble with Mama. But he helped me paste the wallpaper back down and he did it carefully, smoothing it with his thumb, like he was tucking something in.*

*He hummed while he did it. He got to the end of the fourth verse and stopped, right on the edge of the fifth, and his mouth hung open for a second like a drawer pulled out too far, and then he shut it and started the song over from the beginning.*

*I have started writing the song down. All the verses I know. Granny says never sing past the fourth but she didn't say never WRITE them, and I want it on paper in case I forget, in case anyone forgets, in case the house eats the words out of us the way it seems to eat everything else. If I write them all down then the song belongs to us and not to the house.*

*I have written four verses. My pen stops at the fifth. I don't mean I lose courage. I mean it stops. The nib goes dry. I dip it and dip it and the ink runs off the page like water off glass. I have tried three pens and a pencil. The pencil line fades while I watch it.*

*Granny saw the exercise book in my room tonight. She didn't shout. She sat on my bed and held my hand and said, "You clever girl, and you poor girl, and now listen to me. The house lets us keep the song the way a farmer lets hens keep their feathers. Write down every verse you like. But the last verse isn't written anywhere because the last verse isn't ours. It never was. It's sung, not said. It passes mouth to ear and no further, and the house cannot stop that, and that is the only reason we are still the ones holding the rope."*

*I said, "Who does the house want to hold it instead?"*

*And Granny looked at me for a long time, and outside the window the garden was very quiet, no birds, and she said, "Anyone who learns it whole. That's why you must never learn it whole, Maren. Promise me. Whatever you hear, however much you love whoever sings it — keep your mouth closed. Let them finish alone. Swallow the last verse like a stone and let it sit in you and never let it out through your teeth."*

*I promised. I am promising it again here, on paper, where the house can read it: I will never sing the last verse. Not if I love the singer. ESPECIALLY if I love the singer.*

*20th March.*

*Tomas has been moved to the attic room. Mama says it's for his health, the fresh air up under the eaves. He waved to me from the top of the stairs and his smile was fine but his hands were gripping the rail hard enough to go white, and as I passed him he whispered, quick as a bird, "Don't sign anything, Mare. Whatever they bring you, whatever it says. Sign nothing."*

*I don't know what he means. Nobody has asked me to sign anything. I am fourteen.*

*But I have written it here so it stays: SIGN NOTHING.*

* * *

I closed the exercise book and sat in my kitchen with the radiator ticking — my radiator, my ticking, my honest boiler with its honest faults — and I thought about a fourteen-year-old girl making a promise on paper because paper was the only witness she trusted. I thought about her brother whispering *sign nothing* from a stairwell, sixty years before I ever heard the phrase, and how he'd been moved to the attic after, and how the prints on the attic door had matched a man dead since the sixties.

I thought about Maren, ninety-one, asking me at our first meeting whether I could carry a tune.

The house wanted the song finished. That much was arithmetic now: a rope without a knot, a lullaby with a missing close, a thing in the walls that stopped scratching whenever the scraps were touched — guarding them, or grieving them, or waiting. And the last verse existed in exactly one place, according to a dead grandmother's rules: mouth to ear, sung whole, never written, never learned by singing along.

Someone was going to have to sing it. And someone else was going to have to stand there with their mouth shut and let them.

In the morning I drove back to Winterhallow with my tool bag in the boot and the diary photocopies in a folder on the passenger seat, and I rehearsed questions the whole way — calm ones, procedural ones, pest-controller questions. When I turned into the drive, the house stood grey and patient against the sky, and every window on the east side was dark even though the sun was behind me and should have been filling them.

Maren opened the door before I knocked.

"You found the wall," she said. It wasn't a question either.

"I found the wall," I said. "Mrs. Voss, we need to talk about the last verse."

Something crossed her face — relief and terror sharing a coat — and behind her, deep in the hall, in the covered mirror I'd draped with a bedsheet two days ago, something shifted, and the sheet slid an inch, all by itself, like a breath drawn in.
