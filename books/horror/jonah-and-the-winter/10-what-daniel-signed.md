## 10. What Daniel Signed

Daniel Voss slept like a man who had never once been kept awake by a house.

I know this because I checked. Not out of paranoia—well, fine, out of paranoia, but paranoia is just due diligence applied to the wrong object—and because I'd taken to doing rounds at night the way I used to do them on sites with active wasp nests. You learn where the danger keeps its hours. Wasps sleep at dark. Rats work the small hours. Whatever lived in Winterhallow's walls worked whenever it felt like it, which was the part I liked least about it. A pest with a schedule can be trapped. A pest with moods is a landlord.

Daniel's door was shut. Under it, no light. From inside, the slow tidal sound of a man breathing who trusted the building he was breathing in. I stood there for a moment listening to it and felt something I couldn't immediately name, and then I could: it was pity, arriving early, the way weather arrives before a storm you've already read about.

Then I went downstairs to commit burglary.

Not real burglary. Document burglary. There should be a word for that—there probably is, in some country that has suffered enough history to coin one—but the practical version is this: Maren had gone up at nine with her cocoa and her pills, Daniel had gone up at ten with a glass of the good whisky he'd found in the study and an air of a man inspecting his own future real estate, and the lawyer's folder had been left on the hall table like a guest coat nobody claimed.

I had told myself I wasn't going to touch it. I had told myself that for two days. But the mirrors had started showing me things I hadn't done yet, and when your own face starts keeping secrets from you, you stop being picky about whose paperwork you read.

* * *

The folder was heavy cream card stock with a solicitor's crest embossed in gold: Halloway & Finch, Estate Administration, est. 1911. Even their letterhead looked like it had been waiting. Inside were three documents, each clipped separately, each with its own little tab of colored paper like a filing system designed by someone who expected the reader to be grieving or greedy and wanted to accommodate either.

The first was the standard summary of assets. Land, roughly ninety acres. The house. Contents. A portfolio of shares that had been quietly compounding since before the war—the first war—which explained how a family could scatter and prosper while one member per generation stayed behind to die in situ. You don't need everyone in the mine if the vein pays out to the whole family forever. You just need someone down there.

The second document was the deed of transfer itself. That was the one I'd come for, and it took me four minutes to find what I was looking for, because it was hiding in plain sight the way all the best horrors do—not in the margins, not in a codicil, but in clause eleven, sub-section (c), printed in ink so faded it might have been deliberately mixed that way, a brown-grey wash like old water:

*the Signatory hereby assumes all obligations appertaining to the Estate, resident and otherwise.*

Resident and otherwise.

I read it six times. It doesn't change on the sixth reading. That's the thing about bad clauses—they don't need to be clever, they just need to be signed. "Resident" was doing a lot of work in that sentence, and "otherwise" was doing the rest. Any lawyer worth their fees would strike it. Any client worth their salt would ask. But clauses like this don't survive two hundred years of conveyancing by being noticed; they survive by sitting in faded ink inside forty pages of boilerplate, waiting for someone who wants the house more than they want to understand it.

I thought about Daniel humming at dinner. Three verses, absently, between bites of lamb, the way you'd hum something you'd heard in a cot. He didn't even know he knew it. The house had been teaching him since before he had language, and now here he was, sleeping upstairs in the guest wing, dreaming about acreage.

Loyalty and greed, I thought, are two doors into the same room. Maren had walked through hers out of love. Daniel was walking through his out of appetite. The room didn't care which handle you used.

The third document was thinner. A single page, headed *Preliminary Acceptance and Advance of Funds*, and it was signed.

Signed in blue biro, dated three days ago—the day he arrived—with Daniel Voss's name in a hand that slanted forward like a man leaning into a doorway. Below it, a line of smaller text: *execution of this instrument shall be deemed acceptance in principle of all terms of the final instrument, including those not yet disclosed to the Signatory.*

Including those not yet disclosed.

I stood in the hall holding a dead woman's—no. Holding a dying woman's estate in my hands, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the archive. Every generation, one stays. The rest sign. And the signing isn't the end of the transaction—it's the beginning of it. The papers weren't how the family got the house. The papers were how the house got the family. Two hundred years of solicitors' crests and cream card stock and gold-embossed patience, and underneath it all it was the oldest contract there is: something in the walls, and something owed.

The scratching started above me. In the ceiling. Directly above me, as if it had been waiting for me to reach the third document, which—given everything else—I was no longer willing to rule out.

I put the folder back exactly as I'd found it, tab angles and all. Professional habit. Also: I did not want the house knowing I'd read its mail. Though I was starting to suspect the house knew everything I did approximately one second after I did it, and was falling further behind only because it was busy learning to be ahead.

* * *

Here is what I want to say about the mirror in the downstairs cloakroom, and I want to say it carefully, because I have spent my whole professional life distinguishing between what people see and what is there, and I am aware that I am now on the wrong side of that distinction and sinking.

The lag was three seconds. I'd timed it that morning against my watch, because apparently I needed the data even though the data was killing me. Raise hand; count one-Mississippi, two-Mississippi; reflection raises hand. Three seconds. Yesterday it had been two. The day before, one. The house wasn't broken. It was loading.

But tonight, standing in front of the cloakroom glass with the deed's faded clause still swimming behind my eyes, the lag did something new.

My reflection raised its hand first.

Not much first. A half-second, maybe less—a flicker, the way a film jumps a frame. I hadn't moved. I was holding the folder, both hands, and my reflection was holding nothing, and it had one hand raised, palm out, fingers slightly spread, in a gesture I recognized because I make it myself: the flat-palm check pest controllers use when testing airflow from a vent. Except there was no vent in the cloakroom. There was just me, and the glass, and the version of me that was rehearsing.

It stood slightly wrong. That's the only way I can put it. Weight on the wrong heel. Shoulders a degree too square, like someone wearing my posture who had only ever seen it described. My face, but arranged rather than inhabited—the difference between a person and a portrait of a person done by a very diligent student.

I did not move. We regarded each other across three seconds of lag that apparently ran in both directions now, and I understood, with the calm that arrives when panic has simply run out of floor, what the widening was for. The lag wasn't a malfunction. It was a rehearsal schedule. Every second the mirror fell behind was another second it spent practicing me—my gestures, my weight, my flat-palm check—so that one day soon it could stand level with the glass and step through wearing my timing like a well-fitted glove.

The thing with fingerprints didn't just wear faces. It wore faces until they fit.

I went and got the bedsheets. All of them, off every bed in the guest wing, plus the dust sheets from the furniture Maren kept shrouded anyway, and I hung them over every mirror on the ground floor with drawing pins from my kit bag, working fast, working badly, leaving the kind of job I'd have failed an apprentice for. Glass is patient. Sheets are not. But sheets were what I had.

The last mirror was the cloakroom one. As I lifted the sheet to it, my reflection lowered its hand—slowly, deliberately, the way you lower a hand when you want the other party to see you choosing not to wave.

I pinned the sheet over its face and stood in the dark with my heart going like a staple gun.

* * *

Maren was awake. Of course she was awake. Ninety-one-year-olds keep the hours of lighthouses.

She was sitting up in bed with her shawl around her shoulders and her reading lamp on, and when I came in without knocking she looked at me the way you look at weather you've been promised.

"You've seen it," she said. Not a question.

"I've seen a draft of me," I said. "Standing wrong. Practicing." I sat on the edge of her bed because my legs had opinions about standing. "Mrs. Voss. Clause eleven, sub-section c. 'All obligations, resident and otherwise.' Your solicitor wrote that in 1911 and it's still in there, faded to almost nothing."

"It fades," Maren said. "Every time a new draft is drawn up, it fades a little more. By the time anyone reads it, it's nearly invisible." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever been on the receiving end of. "Nearly. You read it."

"I read it. And I read the preliminary acceptance Daniel signed. 'Terms not yet disclosed.'" I rubbed my face. "He's sold himself already, hasn't he. Before he even knows what he's buying. The money unlocked the day he signed, and the obligation attached the same day, and the final deed is just—" I groped for it. "It's just the receipt."

"The final deed is the door closing behind you," Maren said. "The preliminary acceptance is the invitation. There's always an invitation. It wouldn't be fair otherwise." She said *fair* the way you'd say a word in a dead language—carefully, ironically, with the accent of long disuse. "That's the one rule it has never broken. It always lets you choose. Loyalty or greed, dear. It doesn't mind which. It has never once minded which."

"And if you refuse? If nobody signs?"

Her hands smoothed the blanket over her knees, once, twice. "Then it waits. It's very good at waiting. It waited out my aunt Edith for eleven years." She looked at me then, directly, and her eyes were clear as cold water. "You signed nothing, did you. When you took this job. Just the pest control contract."

"Just the pest control contract."

"Good girl." She said it briskly, like praise for remembering galoshes. Then, quieter: "Petra. What did it look like. Your draft."

"Me," I said. "Slightly wrong. Like a translation of me into a language I don't speak."

Maren nodded slowly, and her hand came out and covered mine on the blanket, and her fingers were paper and bird-bone and warm.

"When Tomas was nineteen," she said, "he told me the same thing. About his draft. Word for word—a translation. He laughed when he said it." Her voice did not break. It did something worse; it stayed perfectly level, the way voices go when breaking has been postponed too long to be possible anymore. "He'd been in the house three days."

Above us, in the ceiling, the scratching began again. Slow. Patient. Almost rhythmic.

Almost like humming.

I sat very still on the edge of a dying woman's bed while the house above us practiced its scales, and I thought about clause eleven, and about a boy of nineteen laughing at his own translation, and about the fact that somewhere under the sheets downstairs, behind pinned cotton, my reflection was standing in the dark with its hand raised, waiting for me to come back and finish learning my own face.

"Maren," I said. "Tell me about your brother."

And she said, "Which one?"
