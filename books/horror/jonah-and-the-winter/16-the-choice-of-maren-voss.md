## 16. The Choice of Maren Voss

### Petra

I want to say I saw it coming, but the truth is I was elbow-deep in a kettle when Maren said it.

Not a metaphorical kettle. An actual one. I'd decided that if the house was going to try to eat me, it could at least watch me make tea like a civilized person, and there was something about the ritual of it—the click of the switch, the blue glow of the element through the plastic—that kept me anchored to the version of reality where water boils because of physics and not because something under the floorboards wills it so.

Maren was at the kitchen table with her hands folded, which is how she sat when she was about to say something she'd rehearsed. Ninety-one years old and she still rehearsed. I found that oddly comforting. Rehearsal implied an audience you respected.

"Petra," she said. "I want to talk to you about leaving."

"Leaving." I set the kettle down. "You're leaving?"

"No, dear. You are."

The kettle began its low climb toward a whistle. In the window above the sink—uncovered, because it wasn't a mirror, though after this week I checked every reflective surface twice before trusting it—I watched the garden go grey with evening. Somewhere upstairs, Daniel's door closed. He'd taken to spending hours up there alone, and he came down each time looking slightly more polished, slightly less present, like a photograph of himself that someone kept re-exposing.

"I'm not finished the job," I said.

"The job was finished three days ago. You know that. There were never any rats." She smiled, and it was a real smile, tired at the edges. "There was never going to be any way for you to finish this job. That's rather the point of it."

"Maren—"

"Let me finish. I've rehearsed." She unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the table, palms down, the way people do when they're steadying themselves against something invisible. "Here is what happens if you walk out tonight and never come back. The house takes Daniel first—he's already halfway gone, poor boy, he signed his name without reading what he was signing, which makes him family in the way that matters here. Then it takes me. And then it sleeps. That's the pattern, Petra. Two hundred years of it. One generation feeds, and then the song holds it quiet while the rest of them go off to London and Melbourne and wherever else, and prosper, and send Christmas cards they never expect answered."

"You're talking about yourself like a line item."

"I'm talking about myself like a woman who has done the arithmetic." Her voice didn't waver. That was the worst part. I'd heard people talk about their own deaths before—a pest controller hears things, tenants explaining why they won't leave the flat with the wasp nest, old men describing the damp that's killing them—and there's always a waver. Maren Voss had none. She'd balanced these books long ago. "It sleeps for a generation. Maybe two. Children grow up who never hear the scratching. And then some great-grandchild inherits a house they've never seen, and comes to sign papers, and we begin again. But that's not your problem. That's the Voss family's problem. You're not a Voss."

"No," I agreed. "I'm just the exterminator."

"You're the only person in sixty years who came into this house to help and not to take." She said it simply, without drama, and it landed harder than any of the screaming walls or lagging mirrors had managed. "That makes you the most dangerous thing that's ever walked through that door. And it makes you the thing the house wants most."

The kettle whistled. Neither of us moved to it. It whistled itself out, which felt symbolic, though I was past pretending I knew of what.

"So leave," Maren said softly. "Tonight. Take your van, take your tools, take that lovely rationalist brain of yours and go home to whatever flat you keep it in. Don't look in the rear-view mirror until morning—actually, don't look in it at all, buy a new one, consider it my final invoice. And let us be what we are. Let the house have what it was always going to have. You can't save us, Petra. But you can refuse to be eaten alongside us, and honestly? After everything, that would be enough. That would be more than anyone's managed."

I looked at her across the kitchen table—at ninety-one years of her, at the sharpness she'd kept like a blade honed on grief—and I thought about all the versions of myself I'd been in the last two weeks. The woman who believed in rats and dry rot. The woman who timed her reflection against her watch. The woman who covered mirrors with bedsheets and hummed half a lullaby before catching herself, eleven seconds too late.

I had never believed in anything supernatural in my life. I still didn't, exactly. What I believed in now was worse: I believed in evidence. And the evidence said this house ate people, and it had started eating me, and the only exit offered was one that required me to walk past a dying woman and a hollowed-out young man and pretend the door closed behind me.

"I don't leave jobs unfinished," I said.

"Petra—"

"It's unprofessional," I said, and my voice cracked on the second syllable, which ruined the delivery completely, and Maren laughed—a short, wet, genuine laugh—and reached across the table and took my hand.

Her fingers were cold. Everything in this house was cold except the walls, which breathed warm.

"Oh, you stubborn creature," she said. "You'll stay, then."

"I'll stay."

"Even knowing what it costs?"

"Especially knowing." I turned my hand over and held hers properly. "You asked me once if I could carry a tune. I never answered you straight. Here's my answer: I can't sing. Not one note of that song, not whole, not partial, not hummed in the shower. But I can sit next to you while you do whatever has to be done. I'm very good at sitting. It's most of the job, really. Sitting with things people can't face alone."

Maren looked at me for a long moment. Then she squeezed my hand once, hard, and let go, and became brisk again, because that was how she survived: by converting unbearable things into tasks.

"Well," she said. "Then we'd best get the attic key out of hiding. And you should eat something. Dying households run on tea and biscuits, it's practically regulation."

* * *

### Maren

I had rehearsed the offer forty times, and she refused it in eleven seconds. I would have been proud of her if I weren't so frightened.

Frightened for her, understand. Not for me. My fear of dying left sometime around my seventieth birthday, the way a lodger leaves quietly while you're asleep, taking nothing, owing nothing. What remained was a different animal entirely: the fear of finishing wrong. Of singing the last verse into empty air because everyone who might have held my hands was dead or fled or worn.

And now here was this woman—this dry, funny, stubborn woman with her tool bags and her watch and her refusal to believe in anything she hadn't measured—sitting in my kitchen at nine o'clock at night, eating a digestive biscuit with the gravity of a woman defusing a bomb, telling me she'd stay.

"You understand," I said, "that staying means watching. Some of what happens next will not be kind to look at."

"I've seen the wallpaper," she said. "I have context."

I laughed again. Twice in one night. Tomas used to say I laughed like a gate hinge, and I'd forgotten that for fifty years and remembered it just now, sitting across from a stranger who wasn't a stranger anymore, and had to press my knuckles to my mouth.

"The key," I said, when I could speak. "You'll need to know where it is. If I go suddenly—if the house decides to hurry things along—you'll need to find it without me."

"Where is it?"

"Tomas's room. Under the third floorboard from the window, the loose one. He hid things there when we were children. Conkers, cigarettes he was too frightened to smoke, a tin soldier missing an arm." I paused. "He hid there too, sometimes. From Father. From the sounds in the walls. He always said the floorboards were the only honest part of the house."

Petra wrote it down. Actually wrote it down, in a little waterproof notebook, in handwriting like engineering. I could have kissed her.

"And the verse?" she asked carefully. "When the time comes. How do you want—"

"When the time comes," I said, "you will hold my hands and you will listen and you will keep your mouth shut as God is your witness. Do you understand me, Petra Yarrow? Whatever you hear, however much your throat wants to join—that's the trap. That's how it gets you. The song wants voices. It collects them. Every voice that sings it whole belongs to the house forever, and the house wears them, and—" I stopped. My hands had begun to shake, and I despised them for it. "I have spent ninety-one years not singing that song. I intend to die having sung it exactly once, alone, on purpose, for him. You will not rob me of that by harmonizing."

She put down the biscuit. She looked at me with those level grey eyes, and she said, "Understood. No singing. I'll bite my tongue if I have to."

"You may have to."

"Then I'll bite it."

* * *

### Petra

We went up together after that, Maren leaning on my arm on the stairs, to check the boards over Tomas's room—to check, she said, that the floorboard hadn't been disturbed. As if the house might have gotten there first. As if the house read our mail.

On the landing outside the guest wing, we passed the hall of mirrors, and I made the mistake of glancing sideways.

My reflection was there, of course. Standing where I stood. Wearing what I wore. But the lag had changed character. It wasn't behind me anymore—it was beside me, matching me stride for stride, close enough that if glass were air we'd have been walking shoulder to shoulder. And it was smiling. I wasn't smiling.

"Petra." Maren's grip tightened on my arm. "Don't give it your face. Look at the floor. Walk."

I looked at the floor. The boards were old pine, scarred, honest. I counted fourteen of them to the end of the corridor, and behind me, faintly, I heard footsteps continue past fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen. Stopping somewhere I wasn't.

"She's getting better at me," I said quietly.

"They all do, near the end." Maren's voice was steady, but her hand trembled against my sleeve. "My grandmother saw hers in 1931. Said it waved goodbye to her from the stairwell window as she drove away. Said it had her smile exactly right by then, and her eyes not at all." She stopped at Tomas's door and rested her palm against the wood. "The trick of it, dear, is that it can only wear what you give it. Your face, your voice, your habits. So we starve it. We give it nothing between now and the end. Nothing it can keep."

"And what does it get in the end anyway? When you sing?"

Maren was quiet a moment. Through the door, I could hear the room breathing—that slow, tidal swell the walls made now, no longer bothering to hide it.

"It gets him released," she said. "And it gets nothing else, because I will have given the song to no one. A song sung once, by a dying woman, into the ear of a woman sworn to silence—that's a song with nowhere to live. It dies with the singing. That's the theory, anyway." She smiled, thin as paper. "Grandmother never got to test it. She died mid-verse, and the house has been hungry ever since. Fifty years of an unfinished meal, Petra. You can hear it in the walls if you listen. It's not angry. It's starving."

* * *

### Maren

I sent her to bed at midnight and she went, because even stubborn women need sleep, and I sat in my chair by the window with the blanket over my knees and did what I had done every night since the mirrors started lagging: I talked to Tomas.

Not aloud. Never aloud. Aloud was dangerous; the house collected spoken words like a magpie collects silver. But in my head, in the old private shorthand of siblings, I told him it was nearly time. I told him the girl was staying. I told him she had refused the door I'd opened for her, which was such a Tomas thing to do that I almost laughed again, and then almost wept, and settled for both at once, quietly, the way you learn to after eighty years of practice.

Below me, in the guest wing, Daniel's light burned on. I could hear him humming. Verse two, then verse four, then verse two again, looping, looping, the way a man hums when the humming isn't his anymore. They'd worn him down to a whistle in a suit. I had failed him—there was no honest way around it. I had let charm and impatience walk into my house and sign his name in faded ink, and whatever was left of Daniel when the house finished dressing itself in him would not thank me.

But Petra had refused the door.

I looked out at the garden, at the birch tree Tomas climbed in 1948 and fell out of in 1952, and I made my peace with the arithmetic one final time. The house would take me. Fine. I had been its resident for ninety-one years whether I admitted it or not; better to be taken on purpose than by default. And if the song died with me—if the theory held—then the thing with fingerprints would finally be evicted from my brother, and my brother would have one minute, maybe less, of being nineteen and tired and grateful, and then the dark, and then done.

One minute. Ninety-one years of holding the door shut for one minute of open sky.

It was, I reflected, the worst bargain in the history of bargains, and I would sign it gladly, and I would sign nothing else.

Down the hall, a mirror shattered. Just one. Small, high up, the little oval in the linen cupboard—the one that had shown my mother's face last week, young, asking to be let in.

The house was clearing its throat.

I folded my blanket, set my lamp on the table where Petra would find it, and went to wake her, because we had perhaps a day left, and there was a floorboard to lift, and a key to find, and a verse I had carried in my skull for eighty years that was finally, finally ready to come out.

* * *

### Petra

She woke me by knocking on my doorframe the way nurses knock—two soft raps, no apology—and said, "Get dressed, dear. The house has started breaking its own mirrors, and I'd rather not be caught undressed when it finishes."

I was dressed in ninety seconds. In the hallway, Maren stood straight-backed in her cardigan despite the hour, and behind her, at the far end of the corridor, the sheet I'd hung over the tall mirror had slipped on one side, and in the gap I could see glass, and in the glass I could see the corridor, and in the corridor of the glass there were three figures standing where two of us stood.

One of them was wearing my coat.

"Attic key," Maren said calmly. "Tomas's room. Third floorboard. Bring the crowbar from your kit—we'll do it properly, in the daylight, like civilized people."

"It's one in the morning."

"Then we'll do it improperly," she said, and set off toward the stairs at a pace that dared the house to keep up, and I grabbed the crowbar and followed her down into the breathing dark, past the mirrors, past the walls with their surfacing handprints, toward the last honest floorboard in Winterhallow House—and beneath our feet, faint and patient, something began to hum the first verse back to us.
