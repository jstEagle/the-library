## 4. One Second Late

The house on Latch Street had a bathroom with a window that faced the school.

Sable noticed it the first morning, toothbrush in hand, watching the bell tower rise over the rooftops like a finger held to the town's lips. The glass was old and wavy, so that everything seen through it seemed to be underwater, waiting. The tower stood at the center of the distortion, and for a moment — just a moment — Sable thought she saw the bell swing.

No wind. No sound. She blinked and it hung still as a stone.

"First day jitters," she told her reflection, and spat foam into the sink.

Her reflection did not tell her anything back. It never did. That was the whole arrangement with mirrors: you performed, they copied, and neither of you acknowledged how strange the contract was. Sable had spent thirty-four years not thinking about it. She was good at not thinking about things. It was practically a professional skill — every teacher learned to file away the small wrongnesses of a school day and deal with them never.

She rinsed her mouth. The tap coughed before it ran, a little stutter of air in the pipes, like something clearing its throat.

* * *

It started on Thursday.

Sable knew the exact day because she'd written it on the lesson plan calendar in the corner of the bathroom mirror with a grease pencil — *Thurs: intro paragraphs, collect Wren's essay* — and later, when she tried to remember when the wrongness began, that smudged word *Thursday* would be her only witness.

She was brushing her teeth. Same window, same wavy glass, same tower. October had settled over Harrow's Bend like ash, the maples going red along Latch Street one by one, and the evenings came earlier now, so the bathroom light was on and the mirror behind it showed her the small nightly liturgy: woman, sink, toothbrush, tired eyes.

She brushed left to right. Her reflection brushed left to right.

Except.

Except there was a hitch in it, a stutter, the way a video call lags when the connection is bad. Her hand went up; the mirrored hand went up a beat behind, catching up smoothly, seamlessly, so fast she almost doubted it. Almost.

Sable stopped brushing. Foam gathered at the corner of her mouth.

She watched herself watch herself. The woman in the mirror stood perfectly still, matching her stillness exactly, and if there had been nothing else Sable would have gone to bed and blamed the light, the exhaustion, the second week in a new job, the way new places make your brain invent problems to solve because it hasn't mapped the real ones yet.

But then she raised her right hand.

And counted.

*One.* Nothing. Her arm up, mirror's arm up, perfect symmetry, boring as arithmetic.

*Two.* Still nothing. Her heart began doing something embarrassing against her ribs.

*Three.*

On three, she snapped her fingers — sharp, sudden, a movement designed to catch a copycat off guard.

The reflection's fingers snapped a half-second later. Alone. In an empty room. A snap with no cause, arriving after its echo should have died.

Toothpaste dripped off the brush onto the porcelain. Sable didn't hear it land. Her whole body had become a listening instrument, tuned to one frequency: the space between her and the glass.

"Okay," she said out loud, because saying things out loud made them smaller. "Okay. Old mirror. Old house. Bad silvering. Mirrors do weird—"

She stopped, because the reflection had stopped moving its mouth and she hadn't.

The lag was growing. That was the thing she understood then, standing in her bathroom in a towel with her hair dripping, the thing that turned unease into a cold clean wire down her spine: it wasn't a fixed delay, some mechanical quirk you could measure and dismiss. It was stretching. Like something waking up. Like an eye focusing.

She lifted her left hand slowly, watching.

The reflection waited. There was no other word for it. It held its position with a patience that no piece of glass had ever possessed, and then — a full second late, maybe more — it raised its hand too, copying her, but the way an actor copies, the way something mimics what it has studied rather than what it is.

Sable lowered her hand.

The reflection kept its hand up.

For three full seconds the woman in the mirror stood with her arm raised while Sable stood with both arms at her sides, and the bathroom was very quiet, and through the wavy window the bell tower stood black against a sky going the color of a bruise.

Then the reflection smiled.

Not Sable's smile. Not the tight, reflexive baring of teeth she gave parents at conferences, or the careful warmth she offered students who were struggling. This was wider. This was delighted. It was the smile of someone who has been holding very still for a long time and has just been given permission to move.

Sable got out of the bathroom. She did not run, which she would later count as one of the two or three genuinely dignified acts of her life. She walked. She closed the door. She stood in the hallway with her back against the wall opposite, breathing, listening to the house tick and settle around her.

From under the bathroom door, the strip of light lay flat and undisturbed.

Nothing knocked. Nothing scratched. The house simply continued being a house, which was somehow worse.

* * *

She didn't sleep much. When she did, she dreamed of counting doors — a corridor of them, each one slightly ajar, each one exhaling a smell of chalk dust and cold iron — and woke at 3 a.m. with the certainty that she'd left the bathroom light on.

She had. She always left it on; dark bathrooms in rented houses were a luxury of ignorance she'd abandoned years ago. But now the light seemed less like comfort and more like an admission: *I know you're in there. I'm watching you back.*

In the gray before dawn she sat on the edge of the tub and looked at the mirror the way you look at a dog that has bitten someone. Testing. Measuring distance to the exit.

"Do it again," she said. Her voice sounded scraped. "Go on."

The mirror showed her a tired woman sitting on the edge of a tub, and the tired woman in the mirror sat there too, and for a long moment everything was ordinary, and Sable nearly laughed at herself —

— and then the reflection tilted its head. Just slightly. Just enough. Before she moved.

That was the answer, then. That was the shape of it. Sometimes it copied her late. Sometimes it arrived early. And the space between those two failures — the gap where a real reflection would have no choice but to match her — was where the truth lived: whatever looked back at her from the other side of the glass was not a reflection. It was an occupant. It had been renting the far side of her mirrors since she moved in, polite as Mrs. Odum, patient as debt, and last night it had smiled because she had finally caught it working.

Sable Eriksen had taught middle school for nine years. She knew what it meant when a kid tested a boundary and watched your face while doing it. They weren't checking whether you'd notice. They were checking whether you'd do anything about it.

She stood up. She looked the occupant dead in its borrowed eyes.

"I see you," she said.

The reflection's mouth curved, small and satisfied, half a second ahead of any feeling she owned.

* * *

By seven she had done what Sable Eriksen did instead of panicking: she made lists.

On the back of a lesson-plan printout, in the blue pen she graded with, she wrote:

*Mirror facts.*
1. Lag began (noticed) Thurs 10/9, ~8 p.m., bathroom.
2. Delay variable. Growing?
3. Moves without me. Smiled. Head tilt BEFORE me this a.m.
4. Only bathroom? Or all?

Item four sent her through the house with her coffee going cold, checking every reflective surface like a woman taking inventory before a burglary. The hallway mirror: her own face, obedient, dull, exactly on time. She waved. It waved with her. She snapped her fingers. It snapped with her. Either the hallway mirror was honest or the thing in it was better at pretending.

The wardrobe mirror in the bedroom: fine. The dark screen of her laptop, dim and greenish with her own ghost: fine. The kitchen window over the sink, doubling her against the yard: fine, fine, fine.

Only the bathroom.

Which meant it wasn't the mirrors. It was the room. Or the angle. Or — and here her pen hovered, and her handwriting got smaller — *or it's where I'm most alone. Where I let my guard down. Where my mouth hangs open and nobody sees.*

Teachers knew about rooms like that. Every school had one — the supply closet, the boiler room, the stretch of hall where the cameras didn't reach. The place where the institution kept what it didn't want witnessed. Her bathroom had become that room, and she was the institution, and she was also the secret.

Under *Mirror facts* she wrote a fifth line:

5. Don't cover it. Watch it. Learn its schedule.

Because covering it felt right and was probably wrong. You don't put a sheet over something until you understand whether the sheet is a wall or an invitation. Mrs. Odum's warning about the mirrors — *never cover them at night, dear, never mind why* — had been rattling around Sable's head since move-in day, and it occurred to her now, coldly, that the landlady hadn't said *never cover them.* She'd said *at night.* As though daytime were safe. As though there were hours when the thing behind the glass slept, or was elsewhere, or was bound by some rule older than the house.

Everything in Harrow's Bend came with rules nobody explained. The kids went silent about 1974. Voss's eyes climbed to the bell tower mid-sentence. The ledger in the attendance office listed twenty-six students whose names the current children wouldn't say. And now her own bathroom had joined the conspiracy, keeping time badly on purpose.

Sable looked at the list. Then she added a sixth line, and the pen pressed hard enough to emboss the page beneath:

6. Find out what happened here. All of it. Tonight.

* * *

School was a mercy. School was forty-one seventh graders who needed her to be a person with answers, and there is no terror so persistent that it survives a student asking, in genuine bewilderment, whether a semicolon goes inside or outside the quotation marks. For six hours Sable lived in the ordinary world, and the ordinary world held.

Wren stayed after again. The girl had developed a habit of materializing at Sable's desk with the particular silence of a child raised to be invisible, and today she stood turning her essay over in her hands — the one about her mother leaving, which she'd written in sentences short as fence posts — and said, without preamble:

"You look like you didn't sleep."

"Teacher secret. We're powered by coffee and spite."

Wren didn't smile. She glanced at the door, then at the windows, where the bell tower stood in the last of the light, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into a register Sable was coming to think of as the Harrow's Bend register: low, flat, the voice people used here for true things.

"It starts with the bathroom," Wren said.

The room went very still around the words. Outside, a bus groaned away. Somewhere a janitor's keys chimed down a far corridor.

"What starts?" Sable asked, carefully.

"The looking." Wren set the essay down on the desk, squared its corners with two fingers, a small compulsive neatness that made Sable's chest ache. "My grandma says it used to start with the well. Before indoor plumbing. Now it's mirrors. It picks the place where you're alone with yourself." She looked up, and her eyes were steady and terribly young. "Did yours smile?"

Sable could have lied. Some part of her, the part trained by nine years of parent conferences, reached automatically for the soothing deflection, the *nothing to worry about,* the professional warmth that was mostly armor.

She didn't use it. Something about the girl's steadiness made lying feel like a desecration.

"Yes," Sable said. "Last night. When I wasn't smiling."

Wren nodded, unsurprised, and that was the worst part — not the mirror, not the smile, but a twelve-year-old nodding like a nurse confirming symptoms she'd seen a hundred times.

"It does that the first week," Wren said. "It's introducing itself." She picked up her backpack, slung it over one shoulder, and paused at the door. "Don't cover the mirror. Grandma says once you cover it, it stops pretending to be you."

"And that's bad?"

Wren looked back at her with an expression far too old for her face.

"It means it's done practicing," she said, and walked out into the orange failing light, toward the tower, toward home, leaving Sable alone in the classroom with twenty-six empty desks and one cracked bell shard of a thought ringing louder than any bell:

*It's practicing.*

* * *

That night she brushed her teeth facing the window instead of the mirror, watching the wavy glass distort the tower, refusing to turn around. In the mirror behind her — she could see it at the edge of her vision, a pale rectangle full of dim room — her reflection brushed its teeth too.

A half-second late.

Then a full second.

Then, in the corner of the mirror, in the background where she wasn't looking directly, the bathroom door she had closed stood open, and something small was standing in it, holding up its hand, counting.

Sable turned.

The door was shut. The mirror showed only the door, shut, and her own face, and her own face was smiling — wide, wide, delighted — a full second before she felt anything at all.

She wrote it down. Item seven. Then she packed a bag, because tomorrow she was going into the school basement, where the town kept its records, and she was going to read every page of 1974 if it took all night.

Behind her, unasked, the bathroom light clicked off.
