## 14. The Harvest Moon Rises

The moon came up wrong.

Sable had watched enough October moons in enough borrowed towns to know what a harvest moon was supposed to look like — pale gold, patient, a coin tossed high over the treeline. This one climbed out of the hills behind the school like something being *pulled*, huge and orange and swollen, so low it seemed to drag its belly along the ridge of Harrow's Bend. It filled half the sky before it had cleared the trees. It looked less like light than like an eye opening.

Sable stood on the porch of the house on Latch Street with a mug of coffee gone cold in her hands and counted doors one last time.

Front door: locked, salt line poured along the threshold in a seam so fine it looked like spilled sugar. Back door: locked, salted. The cellar hatch: nailed shut since Tuesday, Deke's hammer, Deke's nails, Deke muttering that nails wouldn't stop anything but would at least make noise doing it. Eleven doors in the house if you counted the little ones — pantry, closet under the stairs, the cupboard above the fridge where Mrs. Odum had kept her preserves in jars labeled by year going back to 1974. Sable had counted them all every night for thirteen nights. Tonight she counted them once more, gently, the way you'd touch a wound to check whether it still hurt.

It hurt.

Inside, the mirrors were already awake.

She'd wrapped them all in black cloth days ago — bedsheets cut into strips, pillowcases, the black T-shirt Wren donated without being asked. But cloth wasn't silence. All evening the shapes beneath the fabric had been moving, the way a body moves under a blanket when it isn't asleep yet. And now, as the moon dragged itself fully clear of the hills, every wrapped mirror in the house began to glow.

Not reflect. *Glow.* Warm light bleeding through black cotton from the inside out, gold as lamplight through a church window, and beneath the light, small shapes moving. Small. Upright. Walking slowly, unhurried, the way children walk when they know exactly where they're going and have all night to get there.

Sable set down the cold coffee. Her hand was steady. That surprised her; she'd expected shaking. Thirteen days of preparation had burned the shaking out of her somewhere between the salt lines and the council meeting where seven adults had blinked one second late and told her, politely, that she should really consider taking some personal time.

She pulled the cloth off the hallway mirror.

The schoolhouse looked back at her.

Not the hallway. Not her own face, not the stairwell, not the coat hooks with Wren's backpack hanging crooked off one of them. The mirror showed the interior of Harrow's Bend Elementary School, seen from somewhere near the ceiling of the main corridor, and it was full of light — warm, honey-colored, sourceless light, the kind that doesn't come from any lamp because lamps aren't invited. And moving through that light, small shapes. Twenty-six of them. She didn't count them; she knew the number the way she knew her own name now. They walked the corridor in no particular order, some holding hands, some alone, and their faces were turned up toward wherever the mirror was looking, toward her, and they were smiling.

They were smiling the way children smile in photographs from 1974. She'd spent hours with those photographs in the basement archive. Class picture, spring of '74, twenty-six faces arranged on bleachers, and the yearbook committee had caught them mid-laugh, mid-life, mid-everything. In the mirror now they wore those same smiles, preserved like flies in amber, and the light moved across them and none of them blinked.

"Bait," Sable said aloud, to the mirror, to herself, to the house. "That's bait. You know it's bait."

Her reflection — the real one, the one that answered on time — nodded grimly back at her, and for one merciful second the mirror showed both things at once: the lit schoolhouse full of small shapes, and superimposed over it, faint as breath on glass, her own tired face. Then the schoolhouse won. The light thickened. One of the small shapes stopped walking and raised its hand and waved.

Slow. Friendly. Patient as the moon.

Sable covered the mirror again and went to wake the others.

* * *

Wren was already awake. Of course she was. She sat cross-legged on the living room floor in the circle of candlelight they'd agreed on — no electric lights tonight, nothing plugged in, nothing humming, because humming was a sound and sounds could be copied — with the skipping rhyme written out on three-by-five cards spread around her like a tarot deck. She'd memorized it weeks ago, but she liked having the cards. "In case I forget myself," she'd said, and nobody had asked what she meant by that, because everybody understood.

"It started early," Wren said before Sable reached the bottom of the stairs. "The moon. It's supposed to crest at 9:41. My mom's almanac says—" She stopped. Corrected herself, jaw tight. "The almanac says 9:41. It crested at nine o'clock even. Like it couldn't wait."

"Things that are invited don't wait," Sable said. "They arrive."

Down the hall, a door opened and closed — counted, Sable noted automatically, door six, the bathroom — and Deke came out with his jacket already on and his keys in his fist, which he did every night now even though he hadn't driven anywhere in two weeks. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, gray-stubbled, with the permanent flinch of someone who'd spent fifty years being blamed for something he hadn't done. He looked at the glowing shape under the hallway mirror's cloth and said, quietly, "They're showing the school."

"They're showing the school."

"Then we're past the part where we get to be smart about this." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I've been dreading this night since 1974. Turns out dreading it doesn't make it any lighter."

The third knock came at ten minutes past nine, soft and rhythmic, and Sable let them in: Marcus and Odile, the other two marked students, fourteen and fifteen, wrapped in coats too heavy for the temperature because their families' houses had gone strange this week — doors appearing where doors hadn't been, parents blinking late, the smell of harvest bread baking in kitchens where nobody was baking. Marcus's family had fled on Tuesday night, leaving him asleep on Sable's couch with a note that said *we're sorry* and nothing else. Odile's family hadn't left. They'd simply stopped speaking to her, politely, completely, as if she were already gone. Both kids had moved into Latch Street like it was the most natural thing in the world, and maybe it was. Maybe found family was just the family that stayed when counting mattered.

"Mirrors?" Odile asked, shrugging off her coat. Fifteen years old and she led with logistics. Sable loved her ferociously for it.

"All showing the same thing. School's full of light. Shapes moving."

"The missing class," Marcus said. His voice cracked on *class*, and he pushed through it. "Showing us the missing class."

"Showing us what they want us to see," Sable said. "There's a difference. Remember that. Whatever you see tonight, there's always a difference between what's shown and what's true."

Deke snorted softly. "Fifty years in that town and I never heard anyone say it that plain." He picked up the duffel bag by the door — salt, hammers, the mirrored shards wrapped in black cloth, the crowbar he'd carried since the day Voss finally admitted to his face that Deke hadn't taken those children. "We going, then?"

Sable looked around the room. Candlelight on five faces. Wren with her rhyme cards squared into a neat stack. Marcus chewing the inside of his cheek. Odile checking the salt in her pockets like ammunition. Deke standing between the kids and the door like furniture that had decided to be a wall.

Outside the window, the orange moon hung directly over the schoolhouse bell tower, perfectly centered, like a target painted behind it.

"We're going," Sable said.

* * *

Harrow's Bend at night had always been quiet, but tonight it was *held* quiet — the silence of a town holding its breath, or being held. Every streetlight worked. That was the first wrong thing; half of them never worked. Every porch light was on. Every window glowed warm, and Sable, walking fast down Latch Street with four shadows strung out behind her, made the mistake of glancing into a window as they passed the Hutchins place, and saw the Hutchins family sitting motionless at their dinner table, plates untouched, all five of them facing the direction of the school. Not staring. Facing. The way Mrs. Odum had faced it in her garden, weeks ago, before they'd found her and brought her inside and she'd wept and apologized and said she hadn't known her feet were walking.

Every house they passed, the people inside were facing the school.

"Don't look," Sable said. "Eyes forward. Count your steps if you have to."

"I'm at forty-one," Wren said immediately.

"Good girl."

"Teacher," Wren corrected, and there was almost a smile in it, and Sable held onto that almost-smile like a coal cupped in both hands.

The town square opened up ahead of them, and the schoolhouse rose beyond it, black against the orange moon, and the bell tower stood up out of the roofline like a raised finger. Sable had taught in that building for three months. She'd graded papers under that tower. She'd drunk burnt coffee in the lounge beneath it. It had never once looked like this — never looked like a throat waiting to make a sound.

They crossed the square. And the mirrors found them.

There weren't supposed to be mirrors outside — but shop windows, car windshields, the dark glass of the bus shelter, the old display case outside Fenwick's Hardware: every reflective surface they passed lit up with the same honey-gold glow, the same sourceless light, the schoolhouse interior rendered in each one, and the small shapes closer now in every reflection, nearer to the glass, near enough that Sable could see details she wished she couldn't. Pigtails. A cowlick. Overalls with a patch on the knee. Children dressed in 1974, walking toward the surface of the glass from the inside, smiling, waving, and — this was new, this was worse — some of them were mouthing words.

Odile stopped dead in front of the hardware store window. "Miss Eriksen—"

"Don't read their lips."

"It's not them." Odile's voice had gone thin and precise, the voice she used when she was frightened and refusing to show it. "Look at Wren's."

Sable looked, and her stomach dropped through the pavement.

In the window's glow, among the twenty-six small shapes, there was a twenty-seventh. A girl Wren's height, Wren's build, Wren's red jacket — except Wren was standing right beside Sable, solid and breathing and real, and the girl in the glass was not copying her. Wasn't mirroring her at all. The reflection-Wren was pressed against the inside of the window with both palms flat against the glass, and her mouth was moving fast, urgent, shaping the same syllables over and over, and though no sound came through, Sable had spent enough evenings watching Wren say these exact words over flashcards to read them plain:

*Don't ring it. Don't ring it. Don't ring it.*

"Wren," Sable said. "Don't look."

But Wren was already looking, and whatever she saw in her own false face, she went white to the lips and stepped back and grabbed Sable's sleeve with fingers that shook. "That's not me," she whispered. "Miss Eriksen, that is *not me*. Mine doesn't do that anymore. Mine stopped copying me days ago and I didn't—I thought if I didn't say it out loud it wouldn't be real—"

"It's real," Sable said. "And it's trying to help. Or it wants us to think it's helping. Either way—" She turned to the group. Four faces, moon-orange, terrified, determined. "Either way, we keep moving. The reflection knows something we don't, or it's a lure wearing Wren's face. We find out which after dawn. Right now the only thing that matters is the bell."

They moved. Behind them, in every window they passed, the small shapes pressed closer to the glass, and the false Wren kept mouthing her warning, and Sable counted steps — sixty-two, sixty-three — and told herself the tightness in her chest was only the cold.

* * *

The schoolhouse doors stood open.

Both of them. Wide. No lights on inside that Sable could see from the steps — the honey-gold lived only in the reflections, in the mirrors and windows, while the actual building crouched dark under the swollen moon. But the doors were open, and the doors were never open, and propped against the doorframe, neat as a note on a kitchen table, was a single folding chair. The kind from the auditorium. On it sat a class roster — Sable's own attendance ledger, the one from her classroom, the one listing twenty-six students who hadn't existed since 1974 — and clipped to it, a handwritten card in careful, old-fashioned penmanship:

*You are expected.*

"Okay," Deke breathed. "Okay. That's — okay." He rolled his shoulders like a man stepping under a weight bar. "I've waited fifty years to walk into that building on purpose. Everybody stay behind me till we're past the office."

"You don't have to come," Sable said, and hated how much she hoped he'd ignore her.

"Fifty years," Deke repeated. "They took those kids and they hung it on me. Hung it on me so long I half believed it myself." He hefted the crowbar. "I'm not staying on the porch. Move."

They went in.

The corridor swallowed them, and the moment the doors swung shut behind, the light arrived — not gradually, not flickering up, but simply *present*, as if the honey-gold had been here all along waiting for witnesses. The whole main hall glowed. Lockers gleamed. The floor shone like water. And at the far end of the corridor, where the trophy cases stood, the glass was bright as a movie screen, and behind it the small shapes had stopped walking and were standing in rows, facing out, twenty-six children in clothes from another century, smiling their preserved smiles.

Waiting for the bell.

Sable felt it before she heard it — a pressure in the sternum, a hum in the fillings of her teeth, a tremor in the floor that traveled up through her boots and settled somewhere behind her ribs. Beside her, Wren gasped and clutched her stack of rhyme cards. Above them, up through three floors and the tower shaft, something enormous began to swing.

"No," Marcus whispered. "No, it's early, the moon hasn't even—"

The first toll hit the night like a fist on a table.

The sound rolled through the building and through the ground and through the soles of Sable's feet, deep and bronze and ancient, and every mirror in the corridor flared white with it, and out in the town — she could feel it rather than hear it — every wrapped mirror, every dark window, every sleeping household snapped one degree further awake. Somewhere out in the dark, doors were opening. Somewhere out there, people who had spent fifty years looking away were turning, slowly, in unison, to face the school.

One toll.

Twelve remained.

Sable stood in the glowing corridor with her found family gathered around her — a janitor with a crowbar, three children with salt in their pockets, a girl clutching a rhyme like a shield — and she felt the fear rise in her the way floodwater rises, total and cold. Twelve tolls until the moon set. Twelve tolls and a door held open by bronze and rope. Somewhere upstairs, whatever the founders had invited in was putting on its welcome.

But underneath the fear, steady as a second pulse, was something else. Something that had been growing in her since the first lagging mirror, since the wet ink in the ledger, since twenty-six names spoken aloud in a basement archive. It wasn't hope, exactly. It was harder than hope.

It was purpose.

"Thirteen tolls," Sable said aloud, and her voice didn't shake. "Before the moon sets. And we only need to stop one of them."

Above them, the bell swung back for the second.

Sable started running for the stairs.
