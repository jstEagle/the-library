## 18. The Bell Tower

The twelfth toll caught them at the base of the tower stairs, and Sable felt it in her teeth.

Not in the floor. Not in the air. In her *teeth* — a low, wet vibration, like something chewing slowly behind her jawbone. She grabbed Wren's wrist and pulled her into the stairwell, and the door swung shut behind them on its own, gentle as a hand laid over a mouth.

"Deke," Wren said. Her voice was small and flat, the way voices go when they've been wrung out and hung to dry. "He was just — he was right there."

"I know." Sable's throat worked. There was nothing else. *I know* was all there was, and it wasn't enough, and she said it anyway because Wren needed to hear a human voice say something true. "He bought us the stairs. We don't waste them."

Above them, the stairwell spiraled up into darkness that wasn't quite dark. The narrow windows let in the harvest moon's light, orange as a coal, and every window had a reflection in its glass — but the reflections were wrong tonight, lagging or leading, Sable couldn't tell anymore which. One of them showed her face turned toward the stairs below, watching something climb.

She didn't look down. Looking down was how hallways got you.

They climbed. The stairs were old oak, bowed in the middle from a century of feet, and they creaked in a rhythm that almost matched footsteps — almost, but not quite, one beat off, like everything else in Harrow's Bend tonight. Sable counted under her breath. Not doors now. Steps. Numbers were anchors; Deke had taught them that with the last of his time. As long as you were counting, you were somewhere. As long as you were somewhere, you could be found.

Forty-one steps. Forty-two.

"Sable." Wren's hand tightened on hers. "Listen."

Below them, faintly, through the closed door: children's voices. Singing. Not the rhyme — something older, wordless, a melody like a lullaby remembered by someone who'd never been sung to. And underneath it, footsteps on the stairs they'd already climbed, patient, unhurried, keeping perfect time with the song.

"It's coming up," Wren whispered.

"It always was," Sable said. "That's what the tower's for. Come on."

* * *

Sixty steps. The air changed.

Sable felt it before she understood it — a thinning, the way air thins before a storm, except this was the opposite of weather. This was the feeling of standing in a doorway between two rooms where one room is your life and the other is something wearing your life's clothes. The moonlight through the windows had gone thick, syrupy, and it moved when she moved, sliding along the walls like it was following her.

Wren stopped on the landing and pressed both palms against her ears. "It's saying my name."

Sable heard it then too. Under the lullaby, under the footsteps: *Wren. Wren. Wren.* Dozens of voices, all the missing class speaking together, sweet and terrible, the way a chorus of small bells can sound beautiful until you realize they're ringing for a funeral.

"Don't answer," Sable said. "Don't say it back. Names are handles. It's trying to get a grip on yours."

"How do you know?"

"Because it got a grip on mine an hour ago, and I'm still prying loose." She took Wren's hands away from her ears and held them. They were freezing. Everything about the girl was freezing except her eyes, which were burning. "When we get to the top, whatever you see up there — whatever it shows you — you remember what you did in the gymnasium. You refused it once. You can refuse it again. Refusal isn't a thing you do one time, Wren. It's a thing you keep doing."

Wren nodded. Then, so quietly Sable almost missed it: "What if I can't keep doing it?"

"Then I'll do it for you until you can. That's the deal. That's been the deal since the casserole."

Something cracked in Wren's face — not breaking, more like ice shifting on a river, the water moving underneath. She squeezed Sable's hands once, hard enough to hurt, and let go.

They climbed.

* * *

Eighty-eight steps, and the bell tower opened around them like a throat.

The bell hung above them in the dark, huge and black and wrong — not bronze, not anymore, or maybe never. Its surface moved like the surface of deep water, slow swells passing across it, and where the moonlight touched it the metal shone the color of the moon itself, orange-white, hungry. The rope hung down through the center of the tower, thick as a man's arm, braided from something that wasn't hemp. It looked wet. It looked warm.

And the rope was moving. Swaying. Gently, gently, back and forth, though no wind reached this height and no hand held it.

Waiting to be pulled.

"The thirteenth toll," Wren breathed. "If it rings thirteen times—"

"The door stays open forever." Sable stepped onto the platform beneath the bell, her shoes sticking slightly to boards that were damp with something. "That's the whole ritual. That's what the council has been doing for fifty years. Every generation, some keeper of the count rings it home."

Below them, the singing stopped.

In the silence that followed, Sable heard the footsteps arrive at the top of the stairs. Not entering. Arriving. Standing just past the threshold of the tower room, in the dark where the moonlight didn't reach, and then — slowly, shyly, the way children come to a teacher's desk when they've done nothing wrong but fear punishment anyway — they came in.

There were twenty-six of them.

They looked exactly as they had in the mirrors. Plaid skirts and corduroys and bowl cuts and bell-bottoms, 1974 preserved in flesh that hadn't aged a day because it hadn't lived one either. Their faces were clean and open and hopeful. Their eyes were the worst part. Not black, not hollow — *full*. Full of longing, full of hunger wearing longing's face, full of fifty years of waiting for someone to come up these stairs and finish what the town started.

One of them stepped forward. A boy, maybe ten, freckles, a cowlick. He'd been the first name in the ledger. Sable knew it the way she knew her own name now.

"Miss Eriksen?" he said. His voice was soft. Kind. A child who wanted to be liked by his teacher. "We saved you a seat."

"We've been so good," said a girl beside him, pigtails, a gap where a tooth had been. "Fifty years, and we never once rang it early. Even when we were so lonely. Even when we could hear everyone downstairs having recess without us."

"You'll stay, won't you?" Another voice, another face. "You won't leave like the others did? The teachers always leave. But you're different. We can tell. You count things. Counters belong here."

"You could be our family," the freckled boy said, and his face began to change as he spoke, the features sliding like wax, becoming older, becoming kind, becoming a face Sable had seen in her own mirror every morning for thirty years — her mother's face, dead eleven years, smiling at her from a child's skull. "We know you're tired, sweetheart. We know nobody ever kept you. But we will. Nobody leaves here. Nobody ever has to leave again."

The tower filled with their wanting. It pressed against Sable like warm water, like a bath drawn just for her, and God help her, some exhausted cellar of her heart leaned into it. A class that would never leave. Students who would love her forever, literally forever, whose attendance would be perfect unto eternity. Every door she'd ever counted had been a door she might be shut out of, and here was a door that would never close—

*Sable.*

Wren's voice. Not spoken — shouted, torn out of her, and Sable realized distantly that Wren had been shouting for a while.

"*Sable, the rope!*"

Sable's hand was on it.

She didn't remember reaching for it. Her fingers were wrapped around the braided wet warmth of it, and it pulsed against her palm like a throat swallowing, and above her the great black bell was trembling with anticipation, humming a note below hearing, leaning toward her like a dog toward the hand that feeds.

She let go. Staggered back. Her hand came away glistening.

And in the half-second her mind cleared, she saw it.

Not the bell. What the bell was *for*.

All these weeks she'd thought the bell summoned the thing — rang out across the valley each October, calling it home like a farmer calling a dog. That's what the town believed too. That's why they feared it, why they flinched at the tower, why Voss's eyes kept drifting up to it. Everyone in Harrow's Bend, guilty and innocent alike, thought the bell was the creature's dinner-bell.

But the bell wasn't calling anything.

The bell was *holding the door open.*

She saw it now with terrible clarity, the way you see the trick in a magic show only after it's done. The founders hadn't invited the thing in and built a bell to summon it. They'd invited it in, and discovered it would leave — that bargains lapse, that hungers wander, that even monsters grow bored of starving towns — and so they'd built a latch. Each toll was a hinge swinging wider. Twelve tolls and the door stood ajar between the world and wherever the thing waited, wide enough for it to lean through, to wear the faces of the drowned class, to walk the halls of the school and stand in gardens facing the schoolhouse. The thirteenth toll would swing it wide forever — and the keeper of the count, the one who pulled the rope, would be bound inside as the doorkeeper. Forever. That was the fine print Voss had never read aloud. That was why the position came with a raise.

The bell didn't call the monster.

The bell kept it *here*. Kept it fed. Kept the bargain running like a machine no one dared turn off, because the last people who tried were twenty-six children who'd learned too much, and the town had paid for its silence with them and called it fate.

"Oh," Sable said aloud. Her voice sounded very small in the tower. "Oh, you bastards. It's not a summons. It's a *lock*."

The twenty-six faces went still.

The freckled boy's smile faded, and beneath it, for just a moment, something else looked out — vast, cold, and suddenly attentive, the way the ocean looks when you realize how far from shore you are.

"You weren't supposed to see that," it said, in twenty-six voices, in her mother's voice, in Deke's voice, in a voice like the space between tolls. "No keeper ever sees that."

"No," Sable agreed. Her hand found Wren's shoulder and pushed the girl behind her, back toward the stairs, and her other hand found the shard of mirrored glass wrapped in black cloth in her pocket — Deke's last gift, still there, still cold. "That was the design flaw."

Above them, the bell began to move on its own.

Slowly, hugely, gathering itself for the thirteenth toll with no hand on the rope at all — because it had never needed a hand, not really, the rope was just tradition, just the ritual's costume, just the way the town liked its horrors dressed up in ceremony. The bell swung back. Held. Trembled at the top of its arc like a breath held before a scream.

"One second late," Sable said, and bared her teeth at it. "Everything in this town runs one second late. So here's what happens next, you son of a bitch—"

The bell began to fall.

"—we count."

* * *

The bell fell, and Sable did not run.

She stepped forward instead, into the shadow of its descent, and unwrapped the mirror shard, and held it up — and in its silvered surface, for one full second, the reflection arrived *early*, showing the bell already struck, already silent, already cracked, showing a dawn that hadn't happened yet, showing twenty-six children standing in ordinary light looking confused and free.

The entity saw it too.

For the first time in fifty years, the thing that wore the missing class made a sound that was not a lure, not a promise, not a name spoken sweetly. It made a sound like a door beginning to close.

And Sable Eriksen, standing under the falling bell with a child's chalk drawing folded in her pocket and a living child's hand gripping the back of her shirt, opened her mouth to speak the count — all twenty-six names, then the living, then the truth of what the bell was, out loud, into the record of the world, where no bargain could bury it —

— as the twelfth toll's echo finally died, and the silence after it stretched, one second, two, three, the whole town holding its breath beneath the setting moon,

and the bell, inches from her head, stopped.

Hung there. Motionless. Waiting — the way everything in Harrow's Bend waited — to see if anyone would finally, after fifty years, refuse to ring it.

Sable's lips parted.

"Wren," she said. "Get ready to count with me."
