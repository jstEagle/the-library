## 7. Jude

The boy in the photograph had a face. Jude wanted that said plainly, first of all, before anything else was understood. The boy in the photograph had a face, and the face had a name, and the name was Jude Aldery, and he had been nine years old in the autumn the village took everything, and he was nine years old still, because that was the age the road had kept him at.

He did not know how the camera had failed him. He only knew that it had, and that it was the last of a long failing. The parish register had failed him. The county map had failed him, its dotted line drawn through the middle of the kitchen where his mother had kneaded bread. The bell had failed him most of all, though the bell had not wanted to; the bell had been dragged out of its yoke screaming in a voice of bronze, and men with crowbars had stood in the tower and sweated and sworn at it, and still it had gone, because a thing that is pulled hard enough goes, no matter what it was for.

Jude had watched the truck go from the top of the field. That was the last thing he had watched with his living eyes, and so it was the thing he watched longest. Fifty years of watching it. The truck's back gate rattling. The bell lashed down under a tarpaulin that flapped like a bird trying to get free. His father's hat in the cab window, one hand raised—not waving, Jude had understood even then, not waving, just raised, the way a man raises a hand when he is holding on to something he cannot name.

Then the truck had gone round the bend by the hawthorn, and the sound of it had gone on much longer than the sight of it, and when the sound finally died the field had made a noise Jude had never heard a field make before. It was not wind. It was the field clearing its throat, or trying to. It was the sound of a debt being written down.

He had been standing there with his sister Meg's hand in his. She was six. She had asked him where they were going, and he had said to the next farm over, to the cousin's place, just for the winter, and she had believed him because she was six and he was nine and nine was the oldest thing either of them could imagine.

They had not gone to the cousin's place. None of them had gone anywhere. That was the part the village had never managed to write down, though God knew they had tried, with their registers and their purchase orders and their neat little stamps, Ada Voss's stamp among them, the stamp coming down on the paper like a small dark moon.

* * *

Jude remembered the harvest of 1974 the way the drowned remember water: completely, and from underneath.

It had been a good year. That was the cruelty of it, the part that had made his father go quiet in a way that frightened his mother more than shouting would have. The barley had stood thick and gold to the very edges of the land, and the apples had come in heavy, and his father had walked the rows in the last week of September with his hands open at his sides, palms out, the way he did when the land was giving and he wanted it known that he was receiving, that the account between them was good.

The bell was rung on the first morning of harvest. That was the old way, older than the church, older than the village, older than the names on the deeds. You rang the bell and the field heard it, and the field understood that its account was being called—that everything it had given would be answered, that the debt ran both directions, that no one who took would leave without paying. His father had said it so many times that Jude had it in him like a bone: *The bell doesn't call the workers, lad. It calls the account. A field that's never had its account called goes strange in the end. Goes looking for it.*

His father had rung the bell that morning. Jude had stood in the tower with him, small hands over his father's big ones on the rope, and the note had gone out over the barley and over the hedges and over the village below, where the houses had looked up, and some of the faces had smiled, and some of the faces had not.

Jude could still name the faces that had not. He had fifty years to practice.

The papers had come the next week. Compulsory purchase, the envelope said, in letters that pretended to be polite. The reservoir was coming, or the road was coming, or the development was coming—the reason changed depending on who was asking and when, and that was how Jude had learned, early, that reasons were like coats, changed to suit the weather. What did not change was the order itself. The land was wanted. The land would be taken. The price offered was a third of what the land was worth, and the letter said *fair* twice.

His father had fought it. Everyone always assumed, later, when they assumed anything at all, that the Alderys had gone quietly, sold up fair, taken their money down the road like sensible people. But his father had written letters until his hand cramped, and walked into the council office in his good coat, and stood in front of men at desks while they explained to him, slowly, as if he were simple, that the decision had been made, that his signature was a formality, that the formality could be dispensed with if he preferred.

It was dispensed with. The stamp came down. Ada Voss's hand on the stamp. Jude knew her name the way he knew the names of the men who had come with the crowbars, and the name of the man who had signed the removal order for the bell, and the name of the man who had driven the truck.

That last name he had learned only recently, and learning it had been like finding a coin in the pocket of a coat fifty years hung up. *Marchetti.* He had said it to himself in the dark of the field, over and over, tasting it. Marchetti. A foreign name, an outsider's name, and still the truck had been his, and still his hands had been on the wheel, and still he had taken the bell and driven it away and never once, in fifty years, come back.

Jude was not angry about it in the way the living were angry. The living got angry hot and then it cooled and then they made peace or they didn't, and either way they slept at night. Jude's anger was the other kind. It was the kind that is patient because patience is all it has. It was the kind that does not need to be fed. It was the kind that can wait under a field for half a century and come up with the same face it went down with.

* * *

His father died on the road. That was the sentence the village never said, because saying it would have required saying what came before it.

They had left on the last day of October, the day after the seizure took legal effect, because his father had refused to leave one hour before he had to. They had loaded the lorry his father had borrowed from the cousin—furniture, the good tools, his mother's sewing box, the crate of apples from the tree his grandfather had planted—and his father had driven, and his mother had sat beside him with Meg on her lap, and Jude had knelt up in the back seat looking at the farm getting smaller in the rear window, the barley stubble gold even now, the tower standing up black against the sky.

The tower was empty. He could see it even at that distance, the way you can see a tooth missing in an old man's smile. The bell had been gone a week. The field had been ringing without it ever since—not sound, exactly, but the place where sound should be, a hole in the air over the land shaped like a note. Jude had heard it every day. He had not told anyone, because he was nine and he already understood that there were things the grown world had agreed not to hear, and that a boy who heard them anyway would be looked at strangely, and looked at strangely was the beginning of being looked at the way they had begun to look at his father.

The road out of the valley was wet that night. There had been rain all day, harvest rain, the kind that comes too late to help and only makes the ground heavy. The lorry's headlights made two shaking cones in the dark. His father was driving carefully. He always drove carefully. But the road was wet and the load was heavy and the bend above the quarry came faster than a careful man could take it, and afterward—afterward, in the long years of the long waiting—Jude had come to believe that the road itself had been part of it. That a land whose account is never called does not only go strange inside its hedges. That the wrong travels. That the wet road and the quarry bend and the borrowed lorry were all, in some way Jude could not prove and did not need to, the field's unanswered question, asked one more time, in the only language left to it.

The lorry went through the barrier. His mother's scream lasted exactly as long as the fall and no longer, and Jude had time, in the air, to think one complete thought, which was: *the apples.* The crate had come loose and the apples were everywhere, turning in the dark with them, and he thought: the apples will be wasted, Dad picked them for keeping, they'll all be wasted—and then the ground.

The ground. He knew the ground. He had been born on ground like it, worked on it, slept on it in the summer when the house was hot. And the ground, meeting them, was not cruel. That was the thing no one would ever believe. The ground was not cruel. The ground took them the way a mother takes a child out of the rain—too late, but gently, and with the terrible tenderness of a thing that has been waiting.

They did not all die at once, and Jude did not stay to count. What he knew was what he knew when he knew it, which was this: his father was done, his mother was done, Meg was done, and Jude Aldery, nine years old, was standing in a wet field two miles from the quarry, whole and unhurt and cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature, looking at his own hands in the moonlight and finding that the moonlight went through them.

The children stayed in ways the living don't. That was the truth of it, and it was not a horror, whatever the village had come to believe in its locked houses with its curtains drawn. It was not a horror. It was a family that would not be separated. Their father had taught them that much, without ever meaning to teach it: you do not leave the land, and you do not leave each other, and you do not leave an account uncalled. His father had died with all three debts on him, and his children had died with the same three, and the dead are not bound by the rules of roads or councils or stamps on paper. The dead can keep what the living are made to give up.

So they had walked back. It had taken them most of the night, two children on a wet road, and no car had stopped for them, and no lighted window had opened, and that was the village's second sin and in some ways its worse one: not the taking, but the not-stopping. The not-seeing. Jude had stood in the doorway of the Fox and Hounds while the men inside talked about the reservoir, and not one of them had looked up, and he had understood then that he could stand there until the end of the world and not one of them would look up, because looking up was the one thing the village could not afford to do.

He had walked home to the empty house with Meg's hand in his, and they had gone into the field, because the field was the only thing left that would have them, and they had lain down in the stubble at the edge where the hawthorn grew, and the field had closed over them the way it closes over seed.

* * *

Fifty years. Jude had learned the arithmetic of waiting the way other children learned their times tables.

He had learned that the living thin out. The men with the crowbars went one by one into the churchyard with proper stones and proper dates, and Jude had stood at the hedge and read their names as they went down, and had not hated them, not even then, because hate was a fire and his was not a fire. He had learned that the village's memory was not empty but full of held breath—full of things everyone knew and no one said, and that held breath makes a sound of its own if you listen long enough, a thin whistle like wind under a door.

He had learned that the bell had been melted. He had felt it happen, or something like feeling it—had been in the field on the day in 1911, no, that was wrong, the melting was later, 1975, the spring after, when the council had ordered the bell destroyed because a lawyer had said a bell was a claim-mark, that a bell was a thing a land was *answered by*, and as long as it hung anywhere it hung over them. Jude had felt the day it went into the furnace the way you feel a word you were about to speak get swallowed. The note had died in the middle. The account had been called off mid-sentence.

And the field had gone strange, as his father had promised it would. Not cruelly. The field did not do cruelty; cruelty was a living art. But the field went *insistent*. The dragging footsteps in the lane at night—Jude's doing, and Meg's, their feet learning again and again the weight they used to have, practicing the walk home from the quarry over and over, because the walk home was the unfinished thing, the sentence they had never gotten to end. The wassail verse that stopped in the middle—because the verse's last lines were the lines that named the debt, and Ada Voss had never been able to sing them since the day she stamped the paper, and Jude had sat under her window more than one night listening to her try, and had not hated her either, and had wondered, in his patient way, whether a woman could carry a debt fifty years and still refuse to pay it, and what it would take to make her set it down.

And the bell. The bell that wasn't there. Once a year, on the eve of harvest, the empty tower rang—one note, clear and wrong, the note of a bell that had been melted and a claim that had not been. Jude did not ring it. He wanted that understood too. He had no hands for ropes anymore. The field rang it. The land itself, keeping its own books, striking its own hour, saying into the dark the only word it had left: *still owed. still owed. still owed.*

The living heard it and locked their doors. The living heard it and called it pipes, wind, bad wiring. One of them—a new one, a young one, a man with a recorder and a soft way of standing at hedges—had heard it and climbed the tower with a torch, and Jude had watched him from the shadow of the belfry arch, watched him put his hand on the scorched beam where the yoke had been, watched him find the date scratched in the plaster and stand very still under it.

Jude had stood close enough to touch him. He had thought about it. Fifty years of patience, and the thought had come anyway: *touch him, make him feel it, make him know.* But his father's voice was in him like a bone, the same as the verse was, the same as the account was, and his father had never once in his life taken a thing that was not answered for. You did not frighten the debt out of people. You named it to them, and you waited, and you let them choose.

So Jude had only stood beside the young man in the dark of the tower, close as a coat, and breathed the words into the space between them, words with no sound in them, words the living feel rather than hear, the way you feel weather coming:

*You hear it. Good. Now find out why.*

And the young man had shivered, and looked over his shoulder at nothing, and gone down the tower stairs too fast, and Jude had returned to the field, to the hawthorn, to Meg, who still asked him sometimes where they were going, because she was six, and he still said *just for the winter,* because he was nine, and both of them knew better, and neither of them could stop.

But things were moving now. That was the other thing Jude had learned about waiting: it does not go on forever, because the world does not hold still around it. There were men coming with measuring wheels and contracts, men who would pave the field and pour the foundations and put the debt under concrete where no bell could reach it. There was Ada Voss with a pen in her drawer and a blank line waiting for her name, one signature away from burying them a second time, deeper, permanent, official. And there was the young man with the recorder, walking the hedges, asking his questions, carrying a name in his pocket that he did not yet know the weight of.

*Marchetti,* Jude said to the field, in the dark, in the voice of a boy and the patience of a stone. *Marchetti. Come and see what your grandfather hauled away.*

The hawthorn stirred, though there was no wind. Down in the village, behind its curtains, the living slept badly and told themselves it was nothing. And at the field's edge, in the stubble where four Alderys lay unmarked and uncounted, the ground went on keeping its books—every entry, every year, every name—waiting, with a patience fifty years deep and fifty more if it needed, for someone to call the account at last.
