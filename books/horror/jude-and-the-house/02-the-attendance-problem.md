## 2. The Attendance Problem

The trouble began, as most troubles at Hollowmere did, with paperwork.

Xanthe had been at the school for eleven days when she finally understood that the attendance sheets were not a formality but a kind of liturgy — recited each morning in the same flat cadence, answered by the same careful silences, folded and filed by the front office with the reverence other institutions reserved for legal documents or relics. She had taught for fifteen years across three districts and had never once seen teachers take attendance the way Hollowmere took attendance: standing, facing the class, reading each name slowly enough to give it room.

She'd assumed it was tradition. Old schools loved their traditions the way old houses loved their drafts — thoughtlessly, structurally, without any memory of why.

On the twelfth morning, Wren Osei raised her hand during third period and asked the question that would follow Xanthe down every corridor of her life afterward.

"Ms. Villalobos? Why do we have kids on our roster who were never here?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped glass. Twenty-two students found urgent business in their notebooks. The radiator ticked. Outside the tall windows, October light lay flat and colorless on the courtyard, and somewhere below, the excavation crew's generator droned behind the west wing — they'd been digging for three days now, tearing up ground that everyone pretended not to watch from the windows.

"I'm sorry?" Xanthe said.

Wren didn't lower her hand. She was a small girl with close-cropped hair and the particular stillness of children who have learned to observe rather than be observed. "Our roster. It has thirty-one names. There are twenty-two desks." She said it the way you'd point out that two plus two was four — patiently, as if the confusion were hers to apologize for. "Nine kids who don't exist."

"They exist," said Devon Marsh from the back, not looking up. "They're just —"

"Just what?" Xanthe asked.

Devon's mouth closed. He became very interested in his pencil.

Wren lowered her hand at last, but she kept her eyes on Xanthe, and there was something in them that Xanthe recognized with a small cold shock: the look of a child who has already been told *don't ask* by every adult in her life, and who has decided, against all instruction, to ask anyway.

"Could I see the roster?" Xanthe said. "After class?"

* * *

The registrar's office occupied a narrow room on the first floor of the original building, all oak paneling and brass fixtures gone green at the edges. Behind the counter sat Mrs. Aldous, who had held the position, according to the faculty lounge gossip Xanthe had been carefully collecting, since roughly the invention of paper. She was a thin woman with white hair pinned so precisely it looked architectural, and she received Xanthe's request with an expression of mild, geological patience.

"The roster for 3-B," Xanthe repeated. "I just want to check something about my enrollment numbers."

"Enrollment is maintained centrally," Mrs. Aldous said. "Discrepancies are handled centrally."

"There aren't discrepancies. There's a student question. A good one, actually." Xanthe smiled the smile she used on skeptical parents. "I'd hate to tell her the school can't account for its own students."

Something moved behind Mrs. Aldous's eyes — too quick to name, gone before it arrived. She turned without a word, took a key from a ring of perhaps forty, and disappeared into the records room behind her. Xanthe heard drawers opening, closing. A long pause. More drawers.

When Mrs. Aldous returned, she carried a ledger bound in green cloth gone soft as felt with age, and she set it on the counter between them with both hands, the way you set down something that might be listening.

"You may look," she said. "You may not remove it. You may not photograph it. And Ms. Villalobos—" she paused, and her voice dropped into a register Xanthe hadn't heard from her before, dry and level as a spirit gauge, "—you may find that the ink hasn't dried."

"I'm sorry?"

Mrs. Aldous opened the ledger to a ribbon-marked page and stepped back.

It was, at first glance, exactly what a registrar's ledger should be: columns of names in a fine copperplate hand, dates of enrollment, dates of — Xanthe's eye snagged on the column header, which read simply *Departure*, with no further specification — and a final column for notes. The current year's page held thirty-one names. She counted twice. Thirty-one.

Twenty-two of them matched her students. She knew their handwriting on quizzes, their faces in the second row, the way Amara chewed her pen cap and Devon slouched like a question mark. Nine names remained, and those nine were crossed out.

Not struck through in red, the way schools marked withdrawals. Not annotated with *transferred*, which Xanthe had already learned was a word that made Hollowmere's staff flinch like a struck nerve. These nine names were crossed out in black ink — a single clean horizontal line through each — and the ink was wet.

She stared at it. The lines glistened faintly under the fluorescent light, catching it the way fresh ink catches light, with a slight raised sheen, as if the pen had lifted from the page seconds ago. She touched the margin beside one name — careful not to touch the line itself; she couldn't have said why — and the paper was bone dry. Room temperature. Ordinary.

"Mrs. Aldous," Xanthe said slowly, "when were these entries made?"

"The ledger is updated nightly."

"By whom?"

"The registrar."

"You're the registrar."

"Yes," Mrs. Aldous said, in the tone of someone confirming that water was wet.

Xanthe looked down at the nine names. She read them once silently, then aloud, quietly, because some part of her wanted to hear how they sounded in her own voice: *Tomas Reyes. Priya Chandrasekaran. Elliot Fenn. Grace Odum. Marcus Bell. Sofia Lindqvist. Daniel Achebe. Ruth Kowalski. James Whitlock.*

Nothing happened when she said them. No answering silence, no shift in the air. But Mrs. Aldous went very still, the way animals go still, and Xanthe stopped.

"These kids," Xanthe said. "Do they exist anywhere else? Transfer records? State enrollment data?"

"The ledger is authoritative."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only answer this office provides." Mrs. Aldous extended her hand for the ledger, palm up, patient as a toll booth.

Xanthe didn't give it back. Not yet. She bent closer over the page instead, and that was when she saw it — the thing that would keep her awake that night and many nights after, though she would spend years building explanations against it, load-bearing rationalizations, a whole architecture of *pipes* and *old buildings* and *coincidence*.

The crossings-out were dated. Each line through each name had been made on a different day — she could see it because the ink varied subtly, some lines darker, some feathered where the paper had absorbed it differently — and the dates ran backward through the semester. September. August. One in July, before the school year had even begun.

But the ink was wet. All of it. Every line, including the one drawn in July, glistened as if written this morning.

"Ink doesn't stay wet for months," Xanthe said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, and she was grateful for fifteen years of practice at sounding calm while the floor tilted. "That's not — chemically, that isn't possible."

"No," Mrs. Aldous agreed. "It isn't."

She let that stand for a moment, and Xanthe understood, with the vertigo of a trapdoor opening, that the old woman was not going to explain. That no one in this building was ever going to explain. That explanation itself might be the forbidden thing, the door everyone walked past without looking at, and that Mrs. Aldous had just shown her more honesty than anyone at Hollowmere had offered in eleven days precisely because showing the ledger was not the same as explaining it.

"Why show me at all?" Xanthe asked.

"Because you asked." Mrs. Aldous reclaimed the ledger with gentle firmness and closed it. Her hands rested on the cover. "And because you'll ask again, and next time you won't come to me. They never do. Better you see it here, in daylight, with me standing here, than—" She stopped herself. Recalibrated. The geological patience reassembled itself over whatever had briefly surfaced. "Better in daylight," she finished.

"Than what?"

"The bell is about to ring, Ms. Villalobos."

* * *

Xanthe spent her free period in the faculty lounge telling herself stories.

This was her oldest skill, older than teaching, older than anything: the making of reasonable accounts. She was, she admitted privately, very good at it. Fifteen years of practice. When the scratching had started in the walls of her childhood home — but no, not that story, not now, that one stayed locked in its room — when, for instance, a ledger behaved impossibly, there were always explanations. Archival restoration chemicals. A prank by a bored office aide with a fountain pen and a sense of humor. Condensation; old buildings sweated; paper hygroscopic in October damp. She stacked these reasons like sandbags and stood behind them and waited for the flood to pass, and it always passed, because floods always pass, because the alternative was admitting that the water was coming from inside the house.

The lounge was empty except for Vice-Principal Okafor, who sat by the window grading with the grim efficiency of a man defusing something. He glanced up when she entered, noted her face, and returned to his papers.

"The ledger," she said.

He didn't look up. "Mm."

"Mrs. Aldous showed me the ledger. Nine names crossed out. Kids who were never enrolled — or enrolled and never existed — and the ink is wet, Mr. Okafor. All of it. Entries from July. Wet."

"Mm," he said again, and made a small red mark on a paper.

Xanthe stood in the middle of the lounge, holding her sandbags, feeling the first one shift. "You're going to tell me it's nothing."

"I'm going to tell you," Okafor said, still writing, "that I've worked here nineteen years, and in nineteen years I have learned which questions get answers and which questions get you a meeting with the board, and the board will be very kind to you, and nothing will change, and you will feel foolish for asking." He set down his pen and looked at her then, and his face was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "You seem like a good teacher, Ms. Villalobos. The students like you already. So I'll say this once, kindly: teach your classes. Take your attendance. File your sheets. Let the school handle its own arithmetic."

"It can't," Xanthe said. "That's the problem. It can't do its own arithmetic. There are nine children on my roster who don't exist, and somebody crosses them out every night in ink that doesn't dry, and everybody in this building knows, and nobody blinks."

Okafor was quiet for a long moment. Through the window behind him, she could see the excavation site behind the west wing — the yellow machine parked and silent now, the trench half-full of shadow, a single worker in a hard hat standing at its edge looking down at something she couldn't see.

"Wren Osei asked you," he said at last. Not a question.

"How did you know?"

"She asks everyone, eventually. She asked me in her sixth-grade year." He gathered his papers and stood, squaring their edges against the table with unnecessary precision. "What did you tell her?"

"The truth. That I didn't know."

Okafor nodded slowly, as if this were the correct answer to a different question entirely. At the door he paused, his back to her.

"When I was new here," he said, "a teacher named Halloran told me the same thing you just said. Everybody knows, nobody blinks. And then he told me the rest of it, and I wished for twenty years that he hadn't." His hand rested on the door frame. "Some questions, Ms. Villalobos, are doors. You don't have to open every door you find."

He left. The lounge settled around her, warm and ordinary and smelling of burnt coffee, and Xanthe sat down in a chair worn smooth by decades of tired teachers and tried to rebuild her sandbags.

They wouldn't stack. Every explanation she reached for slid off the fact of it: *wet ink*. She could explain a lot of things away — she had a lifetime's evidence of her own talent for it — but she could not make July's ink wet in October, and the failure sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Because that was the crack, she understood later. Not the ledger itself. Ledgers could lie, could be pranked, could be mad. The crack was that for the first time in fifteen years, an explanation refused to arrive. The machinery of reasonable accounts, so well-oiled, so reliable, jammed — and behind the jammed gears she glimpsed something patient and enormous waiting to be acknowledged, and it was not in the walls of her apartment or the walls of her childhood house.

It was in the attendance.

* * *

Last period. The students packed up in their usual clatter, and Xanthe dismissed them, and they filtered out into the corridor's noise — all except Wren, who materialized at her desk the way she seemed to do everything, quietly and already there.

"You looked," Wren said.

"I looked."

"And?"

Xanthe pulled her chair around to face the girl. Up close, Wren had ink stains on her fingers and a notebook whose margins were full of tiny, precise drawings — floor plans, Xanthe realized. Little rooms, corridors, staircases, annotated in handwriting far too controlled for a twelve-year-old.

"Nine names," Xanthe said. "Crossed out. Wet ink. Nobody will tell me anything except to stop asking."

Wren nodded, unsurprised, and Xanthe felt something twist — guilt, maybe, or its cousin, the recognition that this child had been carrying the same stone and had carried it longer.

"My mom says the school enrolls ghosts to keep the numbers up," Wren said. "For funding. That's her theory. My dad says it's a clerical error that got copied forward for fifty years." She shrugged. "I think they're both wrong. I think the school thinks the kids are real. I think that's worse."

"Why worse?"

"Because if it's a scam or a mistake, it ends when somebody fixes it." Wren looked at her with those observer's eyes. "If the school believes it, it never ends. It just keeps needing new ones."

The radiator ticked. Down the hall, a janitor's cart squeaked away toward the west wing, toward the digging, toward whatever the man in the hard hat had been staring at in the bottom of the trench.

"Wren," Xanthe said carefully, "why does this matter to you? Really."

The girl was quiet for a moment. Then she opened her notebook to a page near the back, tore it out along the perforation, and folded it once.

"Because last Thursday," she said, "I counted the desks in our classroom during study hall. There were twenty-three." She put the folded page on Xanthe's desk. "There are supposed to be twenty-two. And the extra one wasn't empty."

She left before Xanthe could answer.

Alone in the emptying classroom, Xanthe unfolded the page. It was a map — the whole school, hand-drawn, obsessively detailed, every room labeled in that strange precise hand. Some rooms were crossed out. Three were circled. And one, in the oldest part of the building, in the wing that had been sealed since 1974, was circled twice, the ink pressed so hard it had nearly torn the paper, with four words beneath it:

*this one counts wrong.*

Outside, the light was failing early the way October light fails, gray sliding into darker gray, and somewhere below the window — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable to anyone who had spent fifteen years pretending not to recognize it — something began to scratch.
