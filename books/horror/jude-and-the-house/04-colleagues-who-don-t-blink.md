## 4. Colleagues Who Don't Blink

The knock came at 4:47 on her second Friday, and Xanthe almost answered it.

She had been erasing the board — her own handwriting this time, a lesson on unreliable narrators that had gone better than she'd expected — when the sound reached her through the door of Room 12: three slow raps, evenly spaced, patient as a metronome. Not urgent. Urgency would have been easier. Urgency would have sounded human.

Her hand stopped with the eraser still raised.

*Pipes,* said the practiced voice in her head, fifteen years old now and smooth as river stone from use. *Old buildings settle. Old buildings talk.*

But pipes didn't knock. Pipes groaned, hissed, ticked as they cooled. Pipes did not rap three times on a classroom door at ten to five, when every student had been gone for an hour and the corridor outside had gone the particular color of quiet that Hollowmere did so well.

The knocking came again. Three raps. The same interval. The same pressure, if pressure could be judged by sound — no harder, no softer, as though whoever stood out there had all the time in the world and wanted her to know it.

Xanthe set down the eraser. She looked at the door. It was a plain door, institutional gray, with a wire-glass window that showed only the empty hallway and, beyond it, the long amber windows of the east corridor going gold with late afternoon light. Nothing moved in the frame of that window. No shadow pooled at its bottom edge where feet would be.

*Don't answer it.*

The thought arrived whole, unbidden, in a voice that was not hers — or was hers, but older, younger, the girl's voice from before she'd learned to explain things away. She stood very still in the middle of her classroom, twenty-nine desks arranged in rows around her like an audience, and listened to her own heart count out the seconds.

A third round of knocking. Then nothing. Then footsteps — no, not footsteps, exactly; more a receding displacement of air, the way a house sounds when it exhales — moving away down the corridor toward the west end of the building, toward the wing that had been closed since before she was born.

Xanthe did not leave through that door. She gathered her bag, walked to the far exit by the supply closet, and let herself into the stairwell with hands that were not quite steady, telling herself the whole way down that she had imagined the interval between knocks, that intervals couldn't actually be identical, that human knuckles never repeated themselves that precisely.

She knew, even as she thought it, that she was doing the thing again. The thing she had done since she was nineteen years old. Building the explanation first and standing behind it like sandbags against a flood.

In the main hall, Vice-Principal Okafor was waiting by the trophy case, and he was watching her with an expression she would come to know very well: the look of a man confirming something he had already paid for.

"Ms. Villalobos." He said her name carefully, the way you handle a document that might still be wet. "You're here late."

"Not that late."

"The bell rang at four." He smiled. It was a good smile, warm at the edges, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "You heard it?"

"I heard a bell."

"Mm." He glanced past her, up the stairwell she'd descended, then back. He was a tall man, silver at the temples, with the kind of stillness that some people mistake for calm. Xanthe was beginning to suspect it was something else entirely — the stillness of someone who has learned that movement attracts attention. "Walk with me a moment. I'll walk you to your car."

"It's daylight," she said, and immediately regretted how it sounded — like a child insisting there was nothing under the bed because the light was on.

Okafor's smile thinned by a degree. "Daylight ends early this time of year. And Hollowmere is an old building. Old buildings make noise after hours." He began walking, and she fell into step beside him because it seemed rude not to, and because some part of her — the part that had knocked on her own front door at nineteen and gotten no answer and run anyway — wanted very badly to hear what he would say next.

They passed the trophy case, the display of yellowed newspaper clippings about regional spelling bees, the framed photograph of the class of 1961 smiling in front of the west wing, which loomed behind them in the picture, its windows dark even in full sun.

"You've settled in well," Okafor said. "Mrs. Adeyemi speaks highly of your lesson plans."

"Thank you."

"The students like you. Wren especially, I'm told."

Something in the way he said the name made Xanthe glance at him. "Is that a problem?"

"No." Too quickly. "No, of course not. Wren is..." He searched for the word the way a man searches his pockets for keys he suspects are lost. "...attentive. The attentive ones notice things. That can be difficult, their first year. Or yours." He stopped at the double doors that led to the faculty lot and turned to face her fully, and for a moment the professional warmth dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was tired — profoundly, structurally tired, the tiredness of load-bearing walls. "Ms. Villalobos. A word of advice, offered informally, colleague to colleague."

"All right."

"After the last bell — four o'clock, every day — if you hear knocking on your door, do not answer it."

The words landed in the space between them and just sat there, absurd and enormous, like a piano delivered to the wrong address.

"I'm sorry?"

"Don't answer it. Don't call out. Don't ask who's there. Finish what you're doing, leave by the east stairs, and go home." He held her eyes, and his were dark and level and utterly without embarrassment, which was somehow worse than if he'd laughed. "You'll want to answer it. Whatever form it takes — a student who forgot a book, a custodian, a colleague. There won't be anyone there. But the wanting will be very strong. Stronger than curiosity. It will feel like remembering something you've lost." He opened the door for her. Cold air came through, smelling of dead leaves and diesel from the excavation equipment parked somewhere behind the west wing. "It isn't memory. It's bait."

"Mr. Okafor." Xanthe heard her own voice come out flat and reasonable, the voice of a woman presenting evidence. "That's a strange thing to say to a new teacher."

"Yes," he agreed. "It is."

"And yet you said it."

"And yet I said it." He looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him decide something — watched the decision move through his face like weather. "You've heard it already. Haven't you. Before today, even. Somewhere else."

The cold went through her coat and found the place beneath her sternum where she kept the locked room of herself, the one with the scratching in it, the one with a nineteen-year-old girl pressing her back against a bedroom door while the wall beside her head whispered and scraped like something reading her name off a list.

"Pipes," she said. "My last apartment had bad pipes."

Okafor nodded slowly, and the nod was the saddest thing she'd seen all week. "Of course it did," he said gently. "Good evening, Ms. Villalobos. Go straight home. And Ms. Villalobos—" She paused on the top step of the entrance. "—the lounge portraits. You'll see them eventually. Everyone does. Try not to touch them."

He closed the door before she could ask what he meant.

* * *

She lasted until Monday.

It wasn't deliberate. She hadn't planned to seek out the portraits; she hadn't even known, consciously, that she was looking for them. But the faculty lounge occupied the corner of the second floor above the main office, and on Monday at lunch she carried her coffee in there to escape the noise of the cafeteria, and the portraits were simply *there*, along the north wall between the bulletin board and the window, and once seen they could not be unseen.

There were eleven of them. Oil paintings, mostly, in heavy gilt frames gone dull with age, hung in a row like a jury. Teachers, presumably — the placards beneath each gave names and dates of service. *Eleanor Marsh, 1948–1963. Thomas Greer, 1963–1974.* Dates of service, Xanthe noticed, and nothing else. No *in memoriam*, no retirement dates, no fond epithets. Just names and spans, the way you'd date a geological stratum.

And the faces were wrong.

Not grotesque. Not distorted. Wrong in a subtler and more terrible way: they were *smooth*. All eleven of them. Where eyes should have caught the light, there were soft blank hollows, like thumbprints pressed into clay. Mouths had faded to suggestions. One woman's face — Marsh, according to her placard — retained only the faintest ghost of features, a pale oval with the barest shadows where a nose and brow had once been painted, worn away as completely as the lettering on a centuries-old gravestone.

Xanthe set down her coffee and stepped closer, and the smell hit her — faint, organic, unpleasantly familiar. Old varnish, yes, but under it something else. Skin oil. The unmistakable faint sourness of many, many human hands.

Someone had touched these paintings. Someone had touched them thousands of times.

She looked over her shoulder. The lounge was empty except for Mrs. Adeyemi, the math teacher, grading at the corner table with her back half-turned. Xanthe lowered her voice anyway.

"These portraits. They're—"

"Worn," Adeyemi said, without looking up. Her red pen kept moving. "Yes."

"They look like they've been handled. For decades."

"They have." The pen paused. Adeyemi was perhaps sixty, small and precise, with reading glasses pushed up into gray curls. When she finally turned, her expression was pleasant, competent, closed. A door with no handle on the inside. "New teachers always ask. And we always give the same answer, so I'll save us both time: the frames are original to the building, the paintings have deteriorated naturally, and the school has neither the budget nor the mandate to restore them."

"That's not deterioration. Varnish doesn't wear *selectively*. Faces don't fade while clothing stays crisp." Xanthe heard herself pushing and couldn't stop. "Look at Marsh. Her collar is perfect. Her face is *gone*."

"Isn't that interesting," Adeyemi said mildly, and turned back to her grading.

The dismissal was so complete, so serene, that Xanthe felt heat climb her neck. "Mrs. Adeyemi. With respect. Something happened to these women."

The pen stopped.

For a moment — just a moment — Adeyemi's shoulders changed. The precision went out of them, and underneath was something bent and weary, a woman carrying weight in places weight shouldn't sit. She spoke without turning around, quietly enough that the words barely crossed the room.

"When I started here, thirty-one years ago, there was a portrait of a man named Calloway. Service dates 1974 to 1989. His face was still a face. You could see he'd been handsome, and kind, and tired." The pen resumed its motion, steady as a pulse. "I watched it fade. Year by year. Like frost melting off a window, except backward. And every year I told myself the same story you're about to tell yourself, and every year I touched my own collar and was grateful it wasn't me up there yet." She flipped a page. "Eat your lunch, Ms. Villalobos. The period ends at 12:40."

"Adeyemi—"

"There's a staff meeting Thursday," Adeyemi said, in a voice that had reassembled itself into pleasantness, brick by brick. "Attendance is mandatory. Mr. Okafor will discuss the west wing excavation and scheduling disruptions. Do bring your questions there. He's very good at answering them."

The way she said *answering* told Xanthe everything about how good he actually was.

* * *

She should have left it there. She knows that now. But isolation does something to a person — it makes the smallest acknowledgment feel like water in a desert, and Xanthe had spent fifteen years rationing herself, and Okafor had broken the ration on Friday, and Adeyemi had cracked the door another inch on Monday, and by Wednesday she was thirsty enough to do something foolish.

She asked Daniel Reyes, the young history teacher, over copies in the workroom. Casual. Conversational. *Hey, weird question — the portraits in the lounge, has anyone ever—*

Reyes didn't even finish stapling his packet. "Portraits?" he said brightly. "Haven't really looked at them."

"You've worked here two years."

"Have I? Feels like one." He smiled, gathered his packets, and left, and his smile stayed exactly the same size the entire way out the door, and Xanthe stood alone among the copy machines understanding that she had just watched a man perform normalcy the way a drowning swimmer performs calm — technically, desperately, with every muscle committed.

That afternoon she tried the registrar's office, and got a cheerful woman named Dot who had worked at Hollowmere for forty years and who answered questions about transcripts and bus schedules with genuine warmth and who, when Xanthe mentioned the names on the portrait placards, developed a sudden fascination with her filing cabinet that did not end until Xanthe left the room.

By Thursday morning, Xanthe had compiled the data without meaning to, the way you compile a diagnosis: everyone knew. Every single adult in the building knew *something*, and every single one of them had a polished surface where the knowing should show, and the surfaces were maintained — actively, daily, at cost — the way you'd maintain anything essential. The flinch at the word *transferred*. The ledger with its wet-ink crossings. Wren's questions that no adult would answer. Okafor's warning. The portraits with their touched-away faces.

Everyone knew, and no one would say, and the silence had a shape, and the shape was a wall, and Xanthe had lived inside walls like that before.

* * *

The staff meeting was held in the library, because the auditorium, Okafor explained, was being prepped for inspection. Twenty-three teachers sat in folding chairs between the shelves. Through the tall windows, Xanthe could see the excavation site behind the west wing: the orange excavator frozen mid-reach, the plastic sheeting over something at the bottom of the trench, two workers standing at the edge looking down and not moving, in the posture of people at a graveside.

Okafor stood at the front with a laser pointer and a slideshow of scheduling diagrams, and he was magnificent. He discussed bus rotations. He discussed standardized testing windows. He discussed the temporary closure of the west-wing restrooms and the rerouting of foot traffic, and his voice never wavered, and his slides were immaculate, and Xanthe watched her colleagues' faces and saw the same performance reflected twenty-two times over: attentive, agreeable, vacant. A congregation at a service they had all attended so many times the liturgy had worn smooth as a portrait's cheek.

Then Okafor advanced to a slide showing the trench, and the room's attention shifted — subtly, collectively, the way a herd shifts — and he said, "As most of you are aware, the foundation work behind the west wing has uncovered some original stonework of historical interest. The district has engaged a preservation consultant. Access to the rear grounds remains restricted. Questions?"

Silence. Total, rehearsed silence.

Xanthe raised her hand. She felt twenty-two pairs of eyes arrive on her with actual physical weight, and she felt, beneath that, something else — not hostility, but fear wearing hostility's clothes, the fear of a person watching someone stand up during the quiet part.

"What was uncovered?" she asked. "Exactly."

Okafor regarded her for a long, level moment. Around the room, teachers studied their agendas, their shoes, the ceiling.

"Stonework," he said. "Original to the 1890s construction. The consultant will advise on whether it can remain in situ." He clicked to the next slide. "Now, regarding testing windows—"

"Was it a door?" Xanthe said. "I heard the crew talking. In the parking lot. They said it was a door, and that it was sealed from the inside."

The silence that followed had texture. It had grain. Xanthe sat in the middle of it and felt it press against her eardrums like depth, like water, and she understood suddenly and completely why no one in this building ever said anything: not because they didn't know, but because saying was a door, and doors, here, opened from both sides.

Okafor's face did not change. That was the terrible part. Twenty years of practice had sanded him as smooth as any portrait, and behind the smoothness something moved — briefly, like a fish under ice — and was gone.

"Rumors travel fast," he said pleasantly. "We'll have accurate information after the consultant's report. Testing windows, everyone — page three of your agenda."

The meeting resumed. Xanthe took notes she didn't read. And when it ended and the room emptied and she was gathering her things alone between the shelves, she found that a slip of paper had been tucked into her agenda — folded once, unsigned, in a precise architectural hand:

*Not here. Not ever in the building. My office, Saturday, 9 a.m., when the halls are empty. Come by the east entrance only. And Ms. Villalobos — whatever you heard the crew say, don't say it aloud again. Names spoken in this building get heard by things that collect them.*

She read it three times. Then she looked up, scanning the library, the stacks, the tall gold windows — and caught, at the far end of the room, Wren, standing between the shelves with her backpack on, watching Xanthe with an expression no child should have to wear: the expression of someone checking whether the one honest adult had just been warned, or claimed.

Behind Wren, through the window, the excavator's engine started up with a cough and a roar, and the two workers at the trench edge stepped back, back, back, as if the ground itself had asked them to.

And in the walls of the library — faint, familiar, horribly patient — Xanthe heard the first scratch of the afternoon begin.
