## 10. The Humming Well

Dara Osei had worked enough bad sites to know the sound a dig made when it was wrong. The groan of over-loaded scaffolding. The wet suck of ground that wanted to swallow your trench. The particular silence a crew went into when they'd found something the survey hadn't promised. She'd catalogued them all across eleven years and four countries, filed them under *costs of doing business*, and slept fine.

The hum coming up through the farmhouse floorboards at three in the morning fit none of her categories.

She lay in the narrow bed in the site office—she'd claimed it on night one, a foreman's prerogative—and listened. It wasn't machinery. There was no machinery running. It wasn't wind; the night was dead still, the kind of stillness that made you check the window for fog even though the forecast had been clear. It came from below, from the direction of the well, a low sustained tone that sat just under the threshold of hearing, so that she couldn't say when it had started, only that it was there now, filling the room like water filling a glass.

She got up, pulled her boots on without lacing them, and stood in the dark kitchen with her arms crossed, listening properly.

It was melodic.

That was the word her mind kept rejecting and re-serving. A hum could be a frequency, a resonance, groundwater moving through limestone chokes—she'd heard all the rational versions, had written half of them herself in the daily log to keep the crew steady. But this rose and fell. This had *phrasing*. And underneath the drone, if she held her breath and stopped her own pulse in her ears by sheer force of attention, there were intervals. Notes. A tune.

It was badly sung. That was the worst part. It wandered off pitch and corrected itself, hesitated at the turns of the phrase like a student sight-reading, repeated passages with small variations—as though whatever was producing it was working something out. Learning it. Practicing.

Dara stood in the cold kitchen of a rented farmhouse in a village that didn't want her there, and listened to the well sing a song it did not know yet, and understood, with the flat certainty that arrives ahead of fear, that it would learn.

* * *

Morning did what morning does: made everything negotiable. In daylight the hum was barely there, a vibration in the water tank more than a sound, and Dara almost managed to file it under pipes. Almost. Then she walked the site perimeter before the crew arrived and passed the wellhead—the old stone ring out on the common, roped off since day two at the villagers' polite insistence—and heard it clearly, rising out of the shaft like breath from a throat.

She crouched at the rope line. Below, far down, the black water she'd measured yesterday—higher again, another handspan past the nineteenth-century notch cut into the inner stone—was moving. Not rippling. Moving in time. Swell and fall, swell and fall, and each swell pushed the hum a fraction louder, the way a singer pushes a note from the diaphragm.

"Boss?"

Pell stood behind her, hands in his pockets, twenty-two years old and the best junior she'd ever hired, with a habit of reading over her shoulder and an unshakeable conviction that old things were friendly things. He was looking down the shaft with an expression she couldn't read in the early light.

"You hear that?" she asked.

"Hear what?" But he said it strangely, tilting his head, and then, quietly, almost to himself: "It's better today. It got the third phrase right."

Dara turned slowly. "Pell. What did you just say?"

He blinked at her, surfacing from somewhere. "Nothing. Sorry. Didn't sleep." He smiled the smile he used when he'd been caught napping in the finds tent. "What's on today? Crypt extension?"

"Survey only," she said. "Nobody goes near the door."

"It's not going anywhere," Pell said, and laughed, and the laugh had a hum in it, a half-second of tone at the bottom that hadn't been there last week, and Dara felt the skin of her arms tighten and said nothing, because what was there to say? *You're resonating, kid*? She wrote it in the log instead. She was starting to understand why the villagers wrote everything down and sang everything twice to no one.

* * *

By afternoon the hum had words.

Not words—syllables. Vowel-shapes riding the drone, rising out of the wellhead and carrying across the common so clearly that the whole crew stopped work and stood in the trench in a row, faces turned toward the sound like iron filings. Dara counted heads twice. All six present. Good. All six listening. Bad.

Because she knew the shape of those phrases. She'd spent two evenings with Noor Lockridge's field recordings—the folklorist had lent them freely, too freely, with a look Dara had taken for academic eagerness and now understood as warning—and the melody leaking up out of Hollowmere's well was the melody on those tapes. The song Edith Marr had refused to finish. The covenant, the ledger-song, the thing Noor had explained in a voice gone carefully flat: *each verse is a lock, and every household here has held one shut for longer than there are records.*

The well was singing it back. Slightly wrong. Getting less wrong.

"It's learning it," Noor had said, ten days ago, standing at the crypt threshold with their recorder hanging forgotten at their side. "From us. From the village singing it nightly for centuries—it's been taking dictation through the stone. And now—" They hadn't finished. Now the door was found. Now the bolts were photographed. Now there were six strangers camped a field away, breathing its air, humming its homework back to it without knowing.

"Dara." Rennie, her second, at her elbow, voice low. "We should pull out. Tonight. I can have the trucks loaded by dark."

"And go where?" Dara said. She meant it as strategy talk—site security, contract obligations—but Rennie's face told her how it had landed, and she realized she didn't have an answer that wasn't superstition wearing a hi-vis vest. "Load the sensitive kit anyway," she said instead. "Just in case."

Rennie nodded and went, and Dara stayed at the trench edge a moment longer, listening to the well practice, and thought: *it isn't singing at the village. It's singing at us. We're the audience it wants.*

* * *

They found Pell at dusk.

Not at the farmhouse, not at the mess tent, not in the portacabin where he should have been cleaning finds. Rennie raised him on the radio—no answer—and they split into pairs with torches, calling his name across the darkening common, and it was Dara who cut toward the chapel grounds because some instinct older than protocol told her where a boy who hummed in his sleep would go.

The chapel was a roofless hip of stone against the last grey light. She came around the collapsed nave wall with her torch beam jumping, shouting his name, and the beam caught the crypt stairway—open, the tarpaulin they'd rigged over it hanging in shreds—and she took the steps three at a time with her heart going like a drill motor.

He was kneeling at the door.

The stone door with no handle, the door whose bolts had been driven home from the inside by hands unknown, the door she'd ordered photographed and never touched—he was pressed against it, forehead to the stone, both arms wrapped forward around its edge as far as they'd reach, and his fingers were moving. Tracing. Following the lines of the bolts, around and around, tracing them the way a child traces letters, and in the torchlight she saw the streaks on the pale stone, long brown smears, and saw his fingertips, split and bloody, worn down past the nail on two of them, and he was not stopping.

"Pell!" She grabbed his shoulder and hauled.

He came away with terrible ease, like something set down rather than something holding on, and that ease frightened her more than resistance would have. His head lolled, then lifted. His eyes were open and focused on nothing—or on something behind the door, which amounted to the same thing. His lips moved. Sound came out: the fourth phrase of the covenant song, pitched perfectly, sung correctly, in a voice that was his and was not, and Dara slapped him—open-handed, hard, once, twice across the face, the way you'd shock a man out of gas fumes—and on the second blow the song broke and Pell shuddered and looked at her and said, in his own ordinary cracked young voice:

"Dara? Why are we—my hands hurt."

His hands hurt. His fingertips were ruined. He had no memory of walking a mile across open country in the dark, down a crypt stair, past a barrier tape, to bleed on a sealed door while he learned a dead woman's verse. On the walk back, supported between Dara and Rennie, he asked twice what time it was and twice whether the trucks were fueled, and both times the questions sounded rehearsed, borrowed, like phrases picked up from someone else's mouth.

In the mess tent, under full lights, Dara checked his fingers herself, wrapped them, and watched his eyes. They were his eyes. Mostly. At the edges, when he stopped talking, something in them waited.

"Radio the others," she told Rennie quietly. "Everyone sleeps in the farmhouse tonight. One room. Doors barred. Nobody outside alone—not for a piss, not for anything."

Rennie looked at her. "You think it'll come for him again."

"I think," Dara said, "that it already has him part-time, and I intend to make the hours difficult."

* * *

They barricaded in at nine. Six bodies, one room, the farmhouse's ancient oak door barred with a table shoved under the handle and the windows latched. Dara sat nearest the door with the site radio on her knee, charged, antenna up, because the nearest help was forty minutes away by road and roads could be watched, and because the radio was the one thread connecting them to a world that still ran on normal rules.

At midnight the hum started again beneath the floor, louder than before, confident now, the third and fourth phrases clean, the fifth stumbling but recovering. Pell, wrapped in a sleeping bag between Rennie and Okonkwo, slept with his bandaged hands curled against his chest and hummed along in his sleep, a half-beat behind, correcting himself.

Dara keyed the radio.

"Control, Control, this is Osei, Hollowmere survey. Do you copy? Over."

Static. Then, warm and familiar and impossible:

*"Control, Control, this is Osei, Hollowmere survey. Do you copy? Over."*

Her own voice. Her exact intonation, the little upward catch she put on *copy*, the way she clipped *Osei*. Returned to her a half-second late, riding the static like a reflection off deep water.

She stared at the radio. Around the room, five sleepers breathed. Under the floor, the hum reached the end of the fifth phrase and began, patiently, to learn the sixth.

Dara keyed it again, and made her voice flat, and gave it nothing to copy. "Control, acknowledge. Say again your last. Over."

A pause. Static. Then her own voice again, half a second late, but this time it added a word it had not been given:

*"Over."*

And then, softer, in her voice, in the tone she used for reassuring crews at the end of bad days, the tone that said *we're nearly done, hang on*:

*"Come and sing the rest. You know how it ends. Everyone here knows how it ends."*

Dara set the radio down on the floor very gently, the way you set down something that might be sleeping, and looked around the barricaded room at her crew—at Pell's bandaged hands, at the dark windows, at the floorboards trembling faintly with a song getting closer to right—and understood that the farmhouse was not a shelter. It was a waiting room. Whatever came next would not come through the door or the windows.

It would come up through the ground, or down from the sky, or out of the mouths of the people she loved most on this job, asking politely, in voices she trusted, for the last verse.

Outside, far off across the common, the well hummed on—and beneath it, thin and clear and horribly patient, a new sound joined in: footsteps on the lane, unhurried, many, keeping perfect time.
