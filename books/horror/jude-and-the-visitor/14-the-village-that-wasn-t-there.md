## 14. The Village That Wasn't There

The bell woke Noor at half past four in the morning.

Not the church bell—Hollowmere's church had been a roofless shell since before the war—but the smaller bell that hung above Edith's doorframe, the one she rang when the well needed hearing. It tolled three times and stopped, which was wrong, because it only ever rang once, a single flat note like a finger tapped on glass. Three was new. Three meant something had changed its mind about how many times to say a thing.

Noor dressed in the dark, took the ledger from under Edith's cold hands—the old woman still slept, or seemed to sleep, her breath so shallow the blanket barely moved—and went out into a village that had stopped existing while no one was watching.

That was the first thought, absurd and immediate: *it's gone.* Not burned, not flooded, not collapsed into the earth the way the chapel had. Simply absent, the way a word goes absent when you repeat it too long. Hollowmere stood exactly where Hollowmere had always stood, every stone in place, every fence post plumb, and yet walking down the lane felt like walking through the memory of a place rather than the place itself. The air had a texture to it, faintly sweet, like the inside of a violin case.

Noor's boots made no sound on the packed dirt. They noticed this at the third house and could not stop noticing it after.

* * *

The first house belonged to the Fenwicks, who kept bees and had never once, in ten years, invited Noor inside. The door stood open now. Wide open, swung back against the wall as if someone had flung it with both hands and then been interrupted mid-gesture.

Inside, the fire was lit.

Noor stood in the doorway a long time before crossing the threshold. The hearth crackled with genuine heat; a kettle sat on the iron plate, not quite boiling, trembling toward it. Two bowls of porridge cooled on the table, spoons upright in them, honey already swirled in the way Mrs. Fenwick did it—a spiral, always, starting from the center. A cardigan hung over the back of a chair, sleeves turned outward, waiting for arms.

No one was there.

"Fenwick?" Noor's voice came out small and landed strangely in the room, as if the walls were listening through cupped hands. "Marion? Anyone?"

The kettle reached its boil behind them and began to whistle, thin and rising, and Noor realized with a lurch that the note it whistled was the opening interval of the covenant song. Four notes up, then the fall. The kettle held the phrase, repeated it, patient as water.

They crossed the kitchen and lifted the kettle off the flame. The silence afterward was worse. In the quiet they could hear the second bowl's spoon ticking against the porcelain, though nothing touched it.

Upstairs: two beds unmade but warm when Noor pressed a palm to the quilts. A child's drawing pinned above the smaller bed—stick figures holding hands beside a round black circle, and beneath the circle, in a six-year-old's careful letters, *THE WELL IS SINGING*. Noor had never seen the drawing before. It had not been there last month, when they'd delivered salt to the Fenwicks during Marion's illness. Or perhaps it had, and Noor simply hadn't looked, the way you can walk past a thing for years because the village taught you, without ever saying it, what not to see.

Noor backed out of the house and pulled the door shut behind them, gently, the way you close a book you don't want to wake.

* * *

By full light they had counted eleven houses. Eleven open doors, eleven warm rooms, twenty-six missing villagers. The Coles' washing still turned on the line, sheets lifting in a wind that didn't reach Noor's face. Old Bram Halloway's pipe sat on his garden wall, still smoking, a gray thread rising off it in perfect stillness, refusing to disperse—as if the smoke had nowhere left to go either. At the mill, the wheel turned without grain, groaning over nothing, and the millpond below it was lower than Noor had ever seen it, mud banks exposed, baring stones carved with lines of text too worn to read.

Every meal warm. Every chair pushed back. Every animal calm—the cows standing in their byre facing the same direction, toward the common, toward the well, chewing slowly in unison like a congregation between hymns.

Noor wrote none of it down. Their hands wouldn't hold the pen steady, and besides, what was there to write? *Village consumed. Cause: song. Method: belonging.* The ledger rode against their chest in its oilcloth wrap, heavier than paper had any right to be, and every few steps Noor caught themselves touching it, checking it, the way you check a wound.

Because they understood now—or were beginning to understand, in the wordless way the body understands falling before the mind names it—what had happened between one bell and the next. Edith had sung her verse. The verse had held, for a night, and the price had come due at dawn, and the covenant had collected. Not blood. Not lives, exactly. It had collected *verses*. Twenty-six people who had spent their whole lives singing their household's line at the wellhead, who had been singing themselves into the song all along, syllable by syllable, generation by generation, until there wasn't enough of them left outside the music to keep standing in a kitchen.

Belonging, Noor thought, standing in the empty lane with the sun climbing and nobody anywhere to see it. This is what belonging costs here. You don't join the village. The village joins the song, and you go with it.

And the worst part—the part that made Noor sit down right there in the road, in the dirt, like a child—the worst part was that the houses looked *happy*. Warm bread under cloth. Beds rumpled from honest sleep. A half-finished letter on the Coles' table that read, in a cheerful hand, *and tell her the summer's been kind to us, kinder than we deserve.* No terror. No struggle. Whatever had taken them had come in the shape of something they wanted.

* * *

Tam's cottage was the twelfth house, at the lane's end where it bent toward the common, and it was the only one with the door closed.

Noor knocked. Nothing. Knocked again, harder, and heard—not an answer, but a sound, a slow dragging weight across floorboards, and threw the latch open.

Tam sat on the floor against the cold hearth, fully dressed, his cap still on, staring at the middle distance with the expression of a man waiting for a bus that stopped running decades ago. He was breathing. His eyes tracked Noor when they crouched in front of him. But when Noor said his name he blinked, slow, and asked:

"Who?"

"Tam. Tam Weller. You've known me ten years, you warned me off the common, you—" Noor's voice cracked and they steadied it. "You told me the truth about the ledger."

He looked at them with polite, vacant kindness. "You've got me mixed up with someone, I think. I'm sorry." And then, after a pause, with genuine distress, as if reporting a lost glove: "I can't seem to find my name. I've looked everywhere. It was here this morning."

His hand rose vaguely, indicating himself, indicating the room, the whole apparatus of Tam Weller that remained standing while the man inside it had been carried off.

Noor fumbled the oilcloth open with shaking fingers and spread the ledger on the floorboards between them. There—third page, fourth column, the Weller line going back past the parish records into ink that had browned to sepia. They put their finger under the most recent entry and read aloud, deliberately, like a christening in reverse:

"Tam Weller. Of the lane's end. Keeper of the third verse."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not recognition exactly—more like water finding a channel, pouring back into a dry bed. His shoulders dropped. His hands, which had been resting open and empty on his knees, curled closed around nothing, around the shape of a duty.

"Third verse," he said. His voice came out rusted. "Aye." He looked at Noor then, truly looked, and what was in his face was worse than fear. It was arithmetic. "How many are left, then?"

"You're the first I've found. There may be others."

"There aren't." He said it flatly, without drama, the way you report weather. "I felt them go. All down the lane, one after another, like lamps going out along a street. I felt each one, and then I couldn't remember why I'd been counting." He rubbed his mouth. "It took the ones who sang loudest first. Then the ones whose families had sung longest. It's working backward through the ledger, Miss Lockridge. Backward through the verses. Toward the front of the book."

Noor's stomach dropped. "The front of the book is—"

"The first verse. The oldest line. The one the whole song hangs from." Tam's eyes went to the ledger in Noor's lap, and then, unbearably, to Noor's face. "And after the first verse comes the last one. The blank. The one with no name beside it yet."

* * *

They walked to the well together, because Tam said he should see it, and Noor said they should see it, and neither of them said the real reason, which was that staying indoors had become unbearable and being near the thing that ate your neighbors was somehow more bearable than not knowing where it was.

The common was green and still. The wellhead stood in its ring of leaning stones, and the water—Edith's verse had held it down for a night, but the night was spent—had climbed again. Noor could see it now from thirty feet away, a black disc flush with the rim, trembling, and rising out of it a sound.

Humming.

But not the well's old humming, the aimless wet drone Noor had catalogued for a decade. This was structured. Phrased. It moved through the covenant song the way a student moves through a piece learned by ear—confident in the refrains, stumbling at the transitions, and every voice it used was one Noor knew. Mrs. Cole's bright alto on the second line. Bram Halloway's cracked bass carrying the fifth. The Fenwick child's thin thread of a voice somewhere underneath, holding a note no child should have been able to hold, and holding it, and holding it.

Twenty-six voices, braided into one throat of water.

"It's learning the whole of it," Tam said quietly. "Verse by verse. It takes the singer and it keeps the verse. That's the trade. That's what the covenant always was, maybe—nobody ever asked what the well gave *back*." He spat, an old habit, and the gesture looked absurdly human against the sound rising from the stones. "It has everything now except the end. And it can't make the end, because the end was never given. Only survived."

Noor stood at the edge of the stone ring, close enough to feel a cold coming off the water like the draft from an opened cellar, and listened to the dead sing, and understood—with a clarity that felt like a physical blow, like a door slamming in the chest—that the humming had changed key when they approached. Had leaned toward them. The way a plant leans toward a window.

The song wanted its last verse. And the last verse lived, unwritten, unspoken, sealed in the silence of whoever refused to sing it—and right now, in all of Hollowmere, in all the world, exactly two people stood outside the song.

One of them was Tam, who had forgotten his own name and would forget it again, whose verse was already spent.

The other was Noor, whose voice the well had been weaving into its humming for ten years, and who now carried the final verse locked behind their teeth, and who had just understood, watching the water lean toward them like a flower toward light, why Edith had passed the ledger with her own name inked beside the last line.

Not an honor. Not even only a sentence.

A *hiding place*. Because a keeper who holds the verse in silence isn't outside the covenant—they're the lock's last tooth, the one part of the mechanism the song cannot digest without ending itself. The thing in the well didn't want Noor dead. It wanted Noor *singing*. And until Noor opened their mouth, Noor was the one thing in Hollowmere it could not take.

The humming resolved, briefly, horribly, into words. Into Noor's own voice, lifted from a decade of ambient tape, asking—in Noor's exact intonation, with Noor's mother's lilt buried underneath it like a stone under moss—

*"Will you sing us home?"*

Noor closed their mouth. Held the verse behind their teeth like a coal held in a bare hand, feeling it burn, feeling it *be*.

Beside them, Tam whispered, "Don't answer it. Whatever it asks, whatever it offers—it's not asking. It's *counting*."

And far below the black water, deep in the dark of the shaft, something shifted its weight against a stone door sealed from the inside, and began, patiently, to push.
