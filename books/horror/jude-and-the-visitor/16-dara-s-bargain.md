## 16. Dara's Bargain

Dara Osei had run crews through rockfalls in Cumbria and a flooded adit in the Pyrenees that had swallowed two men whole, and she had learned one thing about disaster: it always arrived politely. It knocked first. It gave you a moment to decide who you were going to be when it came through.

The thing at the well had been knocking for three days.

It started with courtesy. That was the part she couldn't shake—the *courtesy*. On the second night after Pell was lost, after the radio had begun returning her own voice to her like a mockingbird with a grudge, a sound had come up out of the wellhead that stopped her mid-stride in the farmyard. Not the humming. Not the wrong-sung covenant. A voice, clear as a bell over still water, saying:

*"Dara."*

Just her name. Nothing else. The way you'd call across a site to a colleague. The way her mother used to call her in from the garden.

She had stood in the dark with her fists in her pockets and not answered, and the well had waited—a long, patient silence, water lapping somewhere far down the shaft—and then it had said, almost gently, *"That's all right. I'll wait."*

* * *

By the third morning, the farmhouse felt like a submarine. They'd nailed boards over the windows on the ground floor, though Dara wasn't sure anymore what they were keeping out. The thing didn't need doors. It needed only ears, and it had theirs.

Marek hadn't slept in thirty hours. He sat at the kitchen table with his hands flat on the wood, as if the table might lift off without him, and every few minutes he said, quietly, "It knows my daughter's name," and then said nothing else for a while. It did know. It had said the name twice in the night, in Marek's own voice, pronounced slightly wrong in a way that made it worse—like hearing your own face in a funhouse mirror.

Yusuf had stopped talking altogether. He'd taped over the microphone of the site recorder and then, an hour later, peeled the tape off and held the recorder against his chest like something he was trying to keep warm. Dara had watched him do it and understood, with a cold sliding feeling, that Yusuf was no longer sure which side of the glass he was standing on.

And Pell—

They didn't say Pell's name. But every night, faintly, up from the well, came a young man's voice asking, in tones of mild professional curiosity, whether anyone had seen his gloves. He'd left them by the crypt door. He was nearly sure he'd left them by the crypt door. Could someone check?

Dara had gone out once, at two in the morning, meaning to shout at the well, to tell it whatever you told a thing like that—*leave him alone, he's dead, let him be dead*—and instead she had found herself standing at the wellhead listening to Pell's voice describe his gloves in loving detail, and she had thought, with a clarity that frightened her more than anything the well had done: *he isn't suffering. He sounds happy.*

That was the bargain already being made, she realized. It just hadn't been offered to her yet.

So she decided to offer one first.

* * *

She went to find Noor at dusk, because if she was going to do something this stupid she wanted a witness who'd spent ten years learning the rules.

Noor was in Edith Marr's front room, which had become, over the past week, a kind of war office. Papers everywhere. The ledger open under a lamp. Noor looked like a person who had been crying in a dry way—red-rimmed eyes, no tears, the grief of someone who has run out of the luxury of tears. When Dara knocked, Noor didn't startle. Just looked up with those tired folklorist's eyes and said, "You've decided something."

"How can you tell?"

"Everyone who comes to that door now has decided something. It's the only reason anyone leaves their house after dark." Noor closed the ledger but kept a hand flat on it, the way you'd keep a hand on a dog that might bolt. "Sit down, Ms. Osei."

"Dara."

"I know." A pause. "I know a lot of things now. That's the trouble with Hollowmere."

Dara stayed standing. She laid it out plainly, the way she'd lay out a change of plan to a crew: the well was rising faster than any hydrology could explain; the door in the crypt was going to open whether they liked it or not; the thing below wanted the song finished, and it was collecting voices to finish it with; and every hour they spent hiding in the farmhouse was an hour it spent getting better at sounding like people they loved.

"So here's my offer," she said. "I go to the well tonight. I tell it: you want the last verse? Fine. Open the door, take your—whatever's down there—and leave. Leave Hollowmere, leave the county, leave. And when it's gone, I sing the verse myself. Freely given. Deal closed, everyone walks away."

The silence that followed had texture. Noor sat very still, and Dara watched the color drain out of their face in stages, like a photograph developing in reverse.

"No," Noor said.

"You haven't even—"

"*No.*" Noor was on their feet now, and there was something in their voice Dara hadn't heard before—not fear exactly, or not only fear. Authority. The voice of someone reading terms and conditions aloud at a funeral. "Dara, listen to me. I've spent ten years transcribing this song and the last month learning what it *is*. Every verse is a lock. That's not poetry—that's mechanics. Each line is a tooth in the mechanism, sung by the named, in order, nightly, for four hundred years, and the mechanism holds the door shut. The song doesn't end when it's finished. The song ends when it's *refused*. Edith told me. The last verse is only a lock if it's withheld."

"And if it's given?"

"Then it's not a lock anymore." Noor's hand pressed harder on the ledger. "It's a key. A verse sung willingly, by choice, offered freely to the thing behind the door—that's not sealing anything. That's *inviting*. You wouldn't be paying a toll. You'd be opening the gate and handing it the deed."

"It wants out," Dara said. "It told me it wants out. If I give it what it wants, it has no reason to take anything else. No more names. No more—" She heard her own voice crack on the next word and hated it. "Pell is down there. Whatever's left of him. Maybe this ends it."

"Maybe." Noor came around the table slowly. "Or maybe a thing that has spent four centuries learning to sound like us hears *willingness* in your voice and learns the most important lesson of its long life: that we can be talked into singing. Do you understand what that would teach it? Right now the only thing stopping it is that the verse has to be refused. Refused by a person, freely, forever. That's the whole architecture. That's why Hollowmere exists—generation after generation of people agreeing to carry a line they never finish. You'd be proving the wall is made of paper."

"The wall is already paper," Dara said. "The village is empty, Noor. I walked through it yesterday. Warm meals on tables. Doors open. Where did everyone *go*?"

Something moved behind Noor's eyes—grief so large it had gone geological, settled into strata. "Into the song," they said quietly. "Every one of them. Absorbed. Sung." They looked at their own hands. "I think some of them may come back. Tam did, mostly. But yes. The village paid. The village has been paying since before there was a village."

"Then what's one more voice?" Dara heard herself say it and flinched, because it wasn't her voice—it was reasonable, it was calm, it was *persuasive*, and she knew, she absolutely knew, where persuasive calm came from in Hollowmere these days. She clamped her mouth shut.

Noor heard it too. Their eyes went sharp. "That's it talking. Through the shape of your own logic. It does that. It did it to me—I nearly answered it last night. In my mother's voice, Dara. It offered me my mother." They took a breath. "Here's what I actually believe. I believe you're half-convinced you've already lost, and a person who's already lost will sign anything, and it knows that, and it is *patient*. So no. You don't get to make that deal. I'm sorry. I know how that sounds. But you don't."

Dara stood there in the lamplight, fists clenched, and felt the terrible fairness of it land in her chest like a dropped drill bit.

"What am I supposed to do instead?" she asked, and her voice came out small. "Stand at the well and hold my breath until the water reaches the churchyard?"

"Yes," Noor said simply. "More or less. There's a version of your idea that works. Not the verse itself—you never sing the verse—but the *line*. The obligation. Edith carried hers sixty years. Someone has to carry the position now. Stand at the wellhead. Be named. Be *present*. Let it know there's a keeper who won't sing, and let it hate that, and hold anyway." Noor met her eyes. "You wanted a bargain. That's the only one on the table. Hold the door line, and when the night comes—and it's coming, soon, the water's past the third notch—we stand there together and we refuse it out loud."

"And if refusing isn't enough?"

"Then we find out what the alternative costs," Noor said, "and we pay it in the right currency, not the one it's asking for."

* * *

She went to the well anyway. Of course she did. Not to sing—she'd given Noor her word, and strange as it sounded, her word to a folklorist in a lamplit parlor was the only solid thing left in her possession. She went because she needed to look at it. To put a face on the negotiation, even if the face was a hole in the ground full of black climbing water.

The moon was down. The wellhead stood in the common like a throat, and the water was high now—she could hear it, a soft wet breathing far closer to the surface than it had any right to be. The old notch-marks on the inner stones, the ones the survey had dated to the fifteenth century, were disappearing one by one beneath the rise.

"Dara," the well said.

Her name again. Patient. Almost fond.

"I brought you an answer," she said. Her voice didn't shake. She was proud of that, later, in the way you're proud of small survivals. "No deal. Nobody sings your verse. Not me, not anyone. We're going to stand here and refuse you, every night, for as long as it takes, and you can wear whatever voices you like, and it won't matter, because the last line stays *ours*."

The silence stretched. Long enough that she thought it had gone. Then, up from the water, gentle as tide:

*"Pell asked about you today. He wanted to know if you'd found his gloves."*

"Don't."

*"He's cold, Dara. He says the water is warm but everything above it is cold, and he keeps waiting for someone to come down and explain the survey numbers to him. He trusted you with the levels. He said—" the voice caught, artfully, expertly, "—he said you never leave a man underground."*

Dara's knees nearly went. She locked them. She thought of Noor's hand flat on the ledger, holding the dog. She put her own hand flat on the cold stone of the wellhead and held.

"He's dead," she said. "Whatever's wearing him, he's dead, and I will grieve him properly, in daylight, with his mother on the phone, and you will not use him to sell me a key. Goodnight."

She turned and walked away, one step at a time, not running, counting her steps like a rosary, and behind her the well laughed—no, not laughed. *Applauded.* A soft patter of wet hands on wet stone, patient as rain.

But underneath the applause, as she crossed the common, she heard something new. The humming had changed key. It had stopped rehearsing the old verses in the vanished villagers' voices.

It was practicing the space where the last verse would go. Holding the silence open. Shaping the absence of her voice like a socket waiting for a plug.

And far off toward the chapel, faint in the dark, came a sound that turned her blood to well water: stone grinding on stone. One bolt. Withdrawing.

Only one. But the first.
