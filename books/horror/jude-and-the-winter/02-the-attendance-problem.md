## 2. The Attendance Problem

The register was waiting for him on his desk the next morning, exactly where Mrs. Okafor had promised it would be.

Isandro stood in the doorway of Room 12 with his coat still on, one hand on the strap of his bag, and looked at it the way a man looks at a snake coiled on his kitchen floor — not moving, not blinking, calculating the distance between himself and the door.

It was a green hardbound book, the kind he hadn't seen since his own elementary years, with a cloth bookmark and a gold-stamped title that had faded to a ghost of itself: *HOLLOW CREEK SCHOOL — ATTENDANCE*. The spine was cracked and mended with yellowed tape. Someone had written *Thornbury — Grade 6* on a fresh label and pressed it over whatever name had been there before.

He crossed the room and picked it up. The cover was cold. Not room-temperature-cold, not book-left-in-an-unheated-classroom-overnight cold. Cold like a stone pulled from a river in November. Cold like something that had been sitting in the dark with the windows open, waiting.

He opened it to the current week.

The pages were ruled in faded blue, the columns hand-drawn decades ago and photocopied, apparently, onto fresh paper — someone had been maintaining this book faithfully for a very long time. Each day had its grid. Each student had a row. And down the left-hand column, in a careful, old-fashioned cursive that was not his predecessor's handwriting — he'd seen Mr. Aldous's scrawl on the lesson plans he'd inherited, a cramped, impatient script — the names ran in tidy alphabetical order.

Bram, Colton. Delgado, Marisol. Finch, Dana. Grier, Thomas. Hollis, Abigail.

Twenty-seven names. Twenty-seven rows. He counted twice, because the number mattered to him now, the way the number of desks had mattered to him on his first day — thirty desks, twenty-seven children, three surplus seats that he had told himself was simple bureaucratic drift, a district too poor to replace furniture that outlived its students.

He checked the attendance marks for yesterday, his first day. Twenty-seven *P*s in a neat column, each one a small, precise circle with a tail, like a string of pearls.

And then his eye caught the three rows that didn't fit.

They weren't marked absent. They weren't marked at all. Instead, in each of the three rows — *Kettering, Ruth. Lassiter, Owen. Mott, Peter* — the space for yesterday's attendance held a single word, written in the same careful cursive as the names, in ink so fresh it still had a faint sheen to it, as if it had dried only minutes before he picked the book up.

*Present.*

Not *absent*. Not *excused*. Not the clinical *N/A* that schools used for long withdrawals. *Present.* Three children who, according to Mrs. Okafor's own careful briefing on his first afternoon, had been out of her school for months — transfers, she'd said, paperwork pending, don't trouble yourself — marked present in a book that no one had touched since he'd set it down at four o'clock yesterday.

Isandro stood very still.

Outside the frost-rimed windows, the playground sat empty under a sky the color of dishwater. Snow had fallen again in the night — it seemed to fall every night in Hollow Creek, a thin, grudging dusting, as if the sky couldn't quite commit — and the flag rope clinked against its pole in a wind that had no other sound to carry. Somewhere down the hall a radiator knocked twice and went quiet.

He turned back one page. Monday. The same three names. *Present.* He turned back another. The previous Friday, which had been his last day of interviews, his first day of signing papers in the district office — he hadn't been in the building at all. The column was filled in. Twenty-four *P*s. Three words.

*Present. Present. Present.*

He kept turning.

The ink changed as the weeks went backward — the ballpoint blue of recent months giving way to a purplish fountain ink, then to something brownish and iron-gall that had bitten into the paper itself. But the pattern held. Week after week, month after month, all the way back to September, and then to the previous September, and the one before that — three names, three words, written in a hand that never varied, never hurried, never smudged. Whoever wrote those entries had been writing them for a very long time. Whoever wrote them had, Isandro realized with a slow, cold settling in his chest, been writing them in a book that had been locked in an empty classroom every night, in a building that emptied at four o'clock, under a roof that creaked and settled and — he had heard it himself, his first evening, unpacking boxes in the empty room — hummed.

He closed the register.

The sound of it closing seemed too loud, a flat clap that bounced down the empty corridor, and he had the sudden, irrational conviction that somewhere in the building, something had heard it and turned its attention toward him — a slow, patient turning, like a head on a stiff neck.

He put the book in his desk drawer. He locked the drawer. Then he stood there with the key in his fist and understood, with the weary clarity of a man recognizing an old scar in a mirror, that locking a drawer against this was like hanging a curtain over a window to stop the winter from coming in.

*Three years of rationalized noises,* he thought. *Three years, and you still think a lock means anything.*

* * *

Mrs. Okafor's office smelled of coffee, lemon polish, and something else underneath — something faintly mineral, like the air in a cave. Isandro noticed it the way he'd noticed the chalk dust arranged like footprints under the floorboards of Room 12: a small wrongness filed away in the back of his mind, joining a collection that was growing faster than he liked.

She was a broad, handsome woman in her fifties with reading glasses on a beaded chain and a desk so tidy it looked staged. Her diploma hung behind her from a university Isandro had never heard of, dated nineteen years ago. Her nameplate read *PRINCIPAL V. OKAFOR* in brass, and beneath the nameplate, half-hidden by a pen organizer, was a small framed photograph of a boy of about ten, gap-toothed, holding up a fish.

"Mr. Thornbury." She didn't look up from her computer. "How's week one treating you?"

"I have a question about the register."

"Ah." She looked up then, and her face did something interesting. It didn't close, exactly — it was already closed, Isandro realized; it had been closed from the moment he walked in, and what it did was *arrange itself*, the way a salesperson arranges a smile. "The green book. Yes. Old-fashioned, I know. The district keeps threatening to move us to the software, and every year the funding falls through. Honestly, it's a blessing. Paper doesn't crash."

"The attendance marks," Isandro said. "For Kettering, Lassiter, and Mott."

The smile stayed exactly where it was. Nothing in her face moved. But something behind her face moved — he could feel it, the way you feel a draft from a door you can't see — and her hands, which had been folding a printout, went still.

"What about them?"

"They're marked present. Every day. Going back months." He laid it out flatly, the way he'd rehearsed in the car, no accusation in it, just data, because he had learned — three years ago, in a different town, in a different life — that the fastest way to get shut out was to lead with the thing that made you sound unwell. "I understand they've transferred. But someone's still filling in their rows. Every morning. In ink that isn't dry when I open the book at seven-forty."

Mrs. Okafor set down the printout. She removed her glasses, polished them on her cardigan with great deliberation, and put them back on. The whole performance took perhaps eight seconds, and it was the most eloquent eight seconds Isandro had witnessed in a decade of staff meetings. It said: *I have done this before. I have done this many times. I will do it again after you, and after you, and after you.*

"Ruthie Kettering's family moved to her aunt's in Cedar Falls," she said. "Owen Lassiter — the Lassiters are itinerant, they follow the road work. The Motts..." She paused, and for one unguarded instant something moved behind the arrangement — not guilt, Isandro thought. Something older and more tired than guilt. Something like a woman standing at a window watching weather come. "The Motts had a hard year. Peter's staying with family too, while things settle."

"Then the register should say so."

"The register is a formality, Mr. Thornbury." Her voice stayed pleasant, level, almost warm. "The state count is done electronically. The green book is — tradition. The district likes tradition. Some of those entries go back a hundred years and the historical society would have an aneurysm if we digitized and lost them." She spread her hands, a gesture of reasonable men defeated by reasonable systems. "Sometimes the office enters the marks late. Sometimes the sub before you made a mess of it and we had to reconstruct. I'll have Mrs. Pell in the front office reconcile it. Consider it handled."

"Reconstructed from what?"

The question landed in the room and sat there.

Mrs. Okafor looked at him for a long moment. Outside her office window, snow ticked softly against the glass. Down the hall, faintly, the radiator knocked — twice, pause, twice — and Isandro noticed that her eyes flicked toward the sound and away again, quickly, the way a person flicks their eyes toward a creaking stair in their own house at night and pretends they didn't.

"From the enrollment files," she said. "Which is what they're for." She picked the printout back up and unfolded it, and just like that the window of the conversation closed, the way a shopkeeper turns a sign. "Is there anything else? I want to get down to the gym before the boiler people come — they only ever come when it's snowing, of course, because why would anything in this town be simple."

"One more thing." Isandro stood. He hadn't planned to say it. It came out of him the way water finds a crack. "Mrs. Okafor — the three desks in my room that don't have students. Third row, by the windows. They're not the same as the others. They're older. And the floor under them—" He stopped, because her head had come up, and for the first time the arrangement on her face cracked clean through, and what was underneath was fear. Real fear, old fear, the fear of a woman who has been carrying something heavy up a long flight of stairs for a very long time and has just heard a step creak behind her.

"Mr. Thornbury." Her voice was still level. It cost her something; he could see it cost her something. "You're a substitute. A good one, I'm told, and I'm glad to have you, and I'd like to keep you through the spring if the district agrees. So I'll give you the same advice I give every new teacher here, and I'll give it to you once." She folded her hands on the tidy desk, on top of the tidy printout, and looked at him with eyes that were, for one bare second, pleading. "Teach the children on your roster. Twenty-seven of them. Take attendance on the twenty-seven. Grade the twenty-seven. Worry about the twenty-seven. The book takes care of the rest. It always has."

*It always has.* Not *we* take care of the rest. Not *the office*. *It.*

Isandro thanked her and left, and in the corridor he realized his hands were shaking, and he wasn't entirely sure it was fear.

* * *

He found the custodian at the end of the day, in the long basement corridor that smelled of floor wax and wet wool, running a push broom along the baseboards with the slow, metronomic patience of a man who has swept the same floor for forty years.

His name, according to the laminated badge pinned to his coveralls, was E. Sugg. He was small and dry as kindling, with a white mustache the color of the snow outside and eyes that had the particular red-rimmed look of a man who spent his days in fluorescent light and his nights not sleeping.

"Mr. Sugg." Isandro crouched slightly, the way you do with old men and horses. "Got a minute?"

Sugg didn't stop sweeping. "If it's about the heat in your room, it's the radiator, and the radiator's older than my mother, and my mother's dead."

"It's about the attendance book."

The broom stopped.

It stopped the way a heart stops — not gradually, not winding down, but all at once, mid-stroke, the bristles flat against the tile. For a moment the only sound in the corridor was the tick of the pipes and, from somewhere above them, from the direction of Room 12, a faint settling groan of the building, wood and plaster shifting like a sleeper turning over.

Sugg looked at Isandro. His eyes were pale, almost colorless, and very tired, and there was something in them that Isandro recognized with a jolt, because he'd seen it in his own bathroom mirror for three years running: the look of a man who knows exactly what is wrong and has decided, for the sake of getting through the day, to know nothing at all.

"You're the new one," Sugg said. "The sub."

"Isandro Thornbury."

"You'll be gone by June." It wasn't a prediction. It was a comfort — the kind of thing you say to a man at a funeral, or a man at a bedside. "They all are. Aldous lasted four years, and he was the longest since—" He stopped himself, jaw working, and resumed sweeping, one slow drag of the broom, the bristles hissing on the tile. "What do you want to know about the book for?"

"Three names get written in it every morning. Present. Every morning for years. Those kids aren't in the building, Mr. Sugg. I've checked the enrollment files, I've driven past two of their houses. So either someone's coming into my locked classroom every night at seven to write three words in a dead woman's handwriting—"

"It's not a dead woman's handwriting." Sugg said it flatly, to the floor, to the baseboard, to the wax. "That's the thing nobody says out loud, so I'll say it once, and then you and me are done talking about it. It's not anybody's handwriting. It's the *book's* handwriting. The book writes itself the same way the wall writes on the chalkboard and the floor writes its little footprints in the chalk dust, and if you go telling Okafor I said that, I'll call you a liar, because I'm too old to be let go and too poor to quit."

The corridor seemed to lean in. Isandro was aware of the pipes ticking, of the snow against the high, grimy windows, of the long dim hallway stretching away behind him into a dark that the fluorescents didn't quite reach.

"The seated ones," he said quietly.

Sugg's broom stopped again. When he looked up this time, the tiredness in his eyes had a different texture. Pity, maybe. Or recognition.

"Where'd you hear that?"

"You said it. First week, in the boiler room, to the other custodian. I was getting boxes."

For a long moment Sugg said nothing. Then he leaned the broom against the wall, took a folded bandana out of his coveralls, and blew his nose with tremendous thoroughness, buying himself time the way old men do. When he spoke again, his voice was lower, and he didn't look at Isandro. He looked down the corridor, toward the stairwell that led up to the main hall, toward Room 12, toward the green book locked in a drawer in a desk in the third row.

"My grandmother cleaned this school," he said. "Her mother before her. There's a thing in this town, Mr. Thornbury, older than the school, older than the county, and it doesn't take. It *seats*. That's the word the old ones used and it's the right word, because taking is quick and this—" his hand made a small, helpless motion, like a man describing water, "—this is slow. It sets a place for you. And it waits. And it's *patient*, and it's *polite*, and one morning the book says you're present, and the book's never once been wrong."

"Present where?"

Sugg picked up his broom.

"Go home, Mr. Thornbury," he said. "Grade your papers. Drink your supper. And when you hear the humming — and you will, everybody hears it eventually, it's just most folks have the good sense to call it the pipes —" He turned back to his baseboard, shoulders hunched, an old man making himself small inside his own life. "Don't hum back."

Isandro walked up the stairwell into the cold. Behind him, the broom resumed its slow, metronomic hiss, sweeping and sweeping a floor that no one would walk on until morning.

* * *

That night, in his grandmother's house on Fenwick Lane — her house, though the lawyer's letter about the estate sat unopened on the kitchen table, because he could not yet make himself open it — Isandro sat at the kitchen table with the attendance register he had very deliberately not returned to the office, and a legal pad, and a pen.

He had told himself he was only borrowing it. He would copy the data, return it in the morning, say nothing. He was a professional. He was a rational man. He was a man who had spent three years teaching himself, line by line, to stop explaining away the sounds in the walls, and the first rule of that hard curriculum was *write it down, because the thing that explains itself away always counts on your memory being soft.*

The house ticked and settled around him. Snow ticked against the window. Somewhere in the walls — and he made himself notice it, made himself write it down, *9:40 p.m., faint, melodic, stops when attended to* — something hummed.

He turned to the earliest pages of the register. 1971. 1958. 1946. The ink went brown, then sepia, then faded to the color of old tea. The names changed; the hand did not. Page after page, decade after decade, the same patient cursive, the same small circles with their tails, and — he checked, and checked again, and made himself believe it — every few years, a name that stopped being a *P* and became, instead, a single word.

*Present.*

He counted them. He made himself count them, all the way back to the first page, to a date — September 14, 1891 — written in ink that had gone the color of dried blood.

Forty-one names. Forty-one words. One hundred and thirty-three years of a book writing itself in an empty room, patient and polite, marking children present in a place none of them had ever been.

Isandro set down his pen. His hand was steady, which surprised him. Outside, the snow fell on Fenwick Lane, and the humming in the walls had stopped, the way it always stopped when he paid attention — and in the silence it left behind, he found himself thinking, with a calm that frightened him more than any noise could have, of the three empty desks by the windows in Room 12.

They were older than the others. He'd noticed it his first day and filed it away. Old-fashioned, wooden, wrong.

And this morning, when he'd unlocked his classroom at seven-forty, he had noticed something else, something he had told himself was nothing, something he had very nearly explained away — and now, sitting in his grandmother's silent kitchen with a hundred and thirty-three years of fresh ink open on the table in front of him, he stopped explaining.

The three desks by the windows had been warm.

All three of them. On a morning when the room itself was cold enough to see his breath.
