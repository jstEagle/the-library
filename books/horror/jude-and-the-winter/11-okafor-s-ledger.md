## 11. Okafor's Ledger

The key to Mrs. Okafor's office was not a key at all but a habit. Isandro had watched her for three weeks now — the way she turned the lock with her left hand while her right hand held her coffee mug against her hip, the way she always gave the handle one downward tug after locking, as if testing whether the building itself might disagree. He had watched her long enough to know that she left at 4:40 on Fridays, that the janitorial staff went home at five, and that the school's night lights — those sad amber bulbs in the hallway sconces — were on a timer that failed every third week and had not been repaired since before he arrived.

He waited for the third week.

Friday came down over Hollow Creek like a lid. By six o'clock the parking lot was a field of gray ice crusted with new snow, and the school stood dark against it, its windows reflecting nothing but themselves. Isandro sat in his car across the road for forty minutes, engine off, hands wrapped around a thermos of coffee gone cold, telling himself the same lie he'd told himself three years ago in another town: *I'm just checking something. Just looking. This is reasonable.*

Reasonable men did not break into their principal's office. But reasonable men also did not watch four children vanish from a roster of twenty-seven and hear the word *seated* spoken by a custodian who crossed himself with a mop handle. Reasonable men did not inherit photographs with an extra chair in them, a chair that appeared in pictures taken in 1941, in 1953, in 1968, patient as furniture, waiting as furniture.

He got out of the car.

The cold hit him like a slap from a stranger. Snow squeaked under his boots — that particular dry winter squeak, like styrofoam being murdered — and each step toward the front entrance felt louder than it could possibly have been, as though the town had gone quiet specifically to listen. The humming had been worse this week. It lived in the walls of his grandmother's house on Fenwick Lane, and lately it had learned a new trick: it stopped when he stopped walking, started when he started, matching him stride for stride like something practicing companionship.

The front doors of Hollow Creek School were locked, of course. But the east door, the one by the gymnasium where the teachers slipped out for cigarettes, had a deadbolt with a worn strike plate, and Isandro had discovered in his second week that a firm shoulder and an apology to no one would open it. He hated himself a little for knowing that. He hated the school more for making the knowledge necessary.

Inside, the darkness was institutional — that specific flavor of empty-school dark, all linoleum and locker metal and the faint chemical ghost of floor wax. His footsteps echoed down the corridor past the trophy case, past the faded mural of the founding class of 1891 (thirteen solemn children arranged around a bearded man with kind eyes — Elias Wren, the nameplate said, *Founder & First Teacher*), past the water fountain that dripped with metronomic patience.

Mrs. Okafor's office was at the end of the administrative hall, behind the reception desk, beside the display case of student art that never changed because no one changed it.

Isandro tried the door first. Locked, naturally. Then he knelt and examined the frame the way he'd seen done in films, feeling foolish, feeling like a burglar in a story told about him later, and found what he'd hoped he wouldn't find: nothing clever, no spare key taped above the door, no combination. Just wood, paint, and a cheap interior lock older than he was.

But the ceiling tiles above the reception desk were the drop-ceiling kind, and one of them sat crooked, and custodians — Isandro had learned this from the custodian who muttered about the seated ones — custodians kept everything. He dragged a chair over, pushed the tile aside, and there, wedged between a junction box and a nest of yellowed wiring, was a tin box with a picture of a lighthouse fading on its lid.

Inside: rubber bands, a dead flashlight battery, a birthday card someone had never delivered — and a ring of keys, seven of them, unlabeled, heavy with use.

The third one opened Okafor's door.

* * *

He did not turn on the light. He stood in the doorway for a full minute, letting his eyes adjust, letting the room introduce itself. A desk too large for the space. A wall of framed certificates — *Principal of the Year, Tri-County District, 2011* — hanging slightly askew, as if even her achievements couldn't quite commit to straightness. A window with blinds drawn against the snow. And filing cabinets, four of them, gray and dented, standing along the back wall like witnesses who'd agreed on a story.

He started with the obvious. Student records — Dana Whitlock's file was thin as a pamphlet, her emergency contact form half-filled-out, her last attendance entry dated the day before she stopped coming, marked simply: *excused.* Excused by whom? There was no signature. There was no signature on any of the four missing students' final entries, just that same typed word, *excused*, printed in a font that looked almost apologetic.

Then the bottom drawer of the filing cabinet nearest the window stuck when he pulled it, and he pulled harder than he meant to, and it came out all at once with a groan of old metal, and the folder inside — not a student folder, not district letterhead, just a plain manila envelope gone soft and brown at the corners — slid forward and fell open across his knees.

A ledger.

Not a modern ledger. Not a spreadsheet printout or a district logbook. A real ledger, bound in leather the color of dried blood, its spine cracked into segments like the shell of some molting animal. The cover bore no title, only a stamped impression, so worn it took him several seconds of tilting it toward the window's ambient glow to read:

*FENWICK LANE — RESIDENTS & WITNESSES*

His mouth went dry. Fenwick Lane. His grandmother's street. His grandmother's house — *his* house now, if the lawyer's letters were to be believed, though he hadn't signed anything yet, hadn't been able to make himself sign anything, some animal part of him balking at putting his name to paper in that place.

He carried the ledger to the desk and set it down gently, the way you set down something sleeping. Then he turned on the small brass lamp on Okafor's desk — a risk, but he needed light, real light — and opened the cover.

The first page was dated 1891.

*Elias Wren, teacher, builder. Residence accepted. Signed, Elias Wren.*

Below the signature, in different ink, different hand, someone had written: *First winter. One child seated. The song holds.*

Isandro stared at the words until they stopped being words. *One child seated.* He thought of the custodian's mop-handle crossing. He thought of thirty desks and twenty-seven students. He turned the page.

The handwriting changed every few years — different principals, he realized, different keepers of the book, generation after generation of them, each inheriting the duty along with the office. The entries were terse, clerical, horrifying in their calm:

*1893. Abigail Marsh, widow. Residence accepted. Signed, A. Marsh.*

*1894. Winter hard. Two children seated. Song holds.*

*1901. Thomas Greer & family. Residence accepted.*

*1902. Spring. The Greer girl, aged nine, seated. Parents departed town. Song holds.*

Page after page. Decade after decade. Every resident of Fenwick Lane since the county existed, listed, dated, signed — and beneath nearly every signature, within a year, sometimes within months, the same quiet notation. *Seated.* Sometimes one child. Once, in the terrible winter of 1918, five. The influenza year, Isandro thought numbly; the town had blamed the fever, and the fever had taken plenty on its own, but the ledger recorded the difference with a bookkeeper's precision: *Three seated. Not fever. Song holds.*

And then, near the middle of the book, a name that stopped his breath.

*1962. Eleanor Thornbury. Residence accepted. Signed, E. Thornbury.*

His grandmother. Young then, a young mother, moving into the house on Fenwick Lane with a family he barely knew from photographs. Her signature was small and careful, the loops disciplined, a woman writing her name the way she did everything — properly, thoroughly, without complaint.

Beneath it, dated fourteen months later: *One child seated. The Halloway boy, aged ten. Song holds.*

Isandro had never heard the name Halloway. He searched his memory of his grandmother's stories, her long Sunday phone calls, her occasional letters — nothing. No Halloway boy. No mention of any child lost on her street, in her care, in the years she lived in that house. Either she had never known, or —

Or she had known, and the knowledge had lived in her the way the humming lived in the walls, swallowed daily, never spoken, passed down not as information but as silence.

He kept turning pages. His own parents' names were not there — they had moved away before he was born, escaped, though his grandmother had stayed, had signed and stayed for fifty years, and no child had been seated on Fenwick Lane since 1963. Fifty years of nothing. Fifty years of the song holding, whatever that meant, whoever had been singing it.

Because someone had been singing it. He understood that now with the certainty of a man reading his own diagnosis. The ledger wasn't a record of crimes. It was a maintenance log. Sign the house's residents, note the feeding, confirm the song. The institution wasn't complicit in the disappearances.

The institution was the apparatus that kept them orderly.

The last entries were in handwriting he recognized — the careful, tired script he saw on every memo and permission slip. Mrs. Okafor had inherited the book, as her predecessors had, as perhaps she had inherited it unwillingly, the way Isandro had inherited a house full of humming.

*2009. Adaeze Okafor. Residence transferred to keeper of records. Signed, A. Okafor.*

She didn't live on Fenwick Lane. He knew that; her address was in his employment file, out on Route 9. But the entry said *transferred to keeper of records,* and beneath it, in her hand, smaller than the rest, pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss the next page:

*I don't want this. I don't know how to put it down.*

And below that, dated each year since, a single line, repeated like a prayer or a sentence:

*Song holds. No child seated. God forgive me for keeping count instead of keeping them.*

Isandro sat very still in the dark office, the lamp buzzing faintly, snow ticking against the window glass like fingernails — no, he corrected himself, like chalk. Like chalk dust settling. Like footprints made of dust under floorboards.

There was one more thing in the drawer. Beneath where the ledger had lain, a photograph, old and curled, and he lifted it with fingers that no longer felt entirely his own. It showed the school's founding class — the same thirteen children from the mural in the hall, posed on the steps of the original one-room schoolhouse, Elias Wren standing behind them with his kind eyes and his beard. But this version of the photograph was different from the mural. In this one, at the edge of the frame, half-cropped by the camera's limits, stood a wooden chair.

Empty. Old-fashioned. Wrong.

The same chair. The chair from his grandmother's photographs, from every photograph, patient at the edge of things since before anyone alive could remember.

On the back, in pencil, in a hand that shook — Wren's hand, he somehow knew, the founder's hand — three words:

*It wants singing.*

Down the hall, somewhere beyond the closed door, a sound moved through the darkened school. Not footsteps. Softer than footsteps. A dragging, sliding whisper, like a chair being drawn across a floor — once, then again, closer, unhurried, patient as furniture.

Isandro closed the ledger, tucked it inside his coat against his chest where he could feel its weight like a second heartbeat, and killed the lamp.

In the blackness, through the gap beneath Okafor's door, he watched a thin line of frost crawl across the hallway linoleum toward him — slow, deliberate, the width of a chair leg — and stop, precisely, at the threshold.

Something on the other side of the door waited with him in the dark. And it hummed.
