## 24. The Hospital Vigil

The hospital sat at the edge of Hollow Creek like something the town had built out of obligation and then tried to forget — a low brick building with frosted windows and a parking lot that hadn't been resurfaced since Isandro was a child. Snow had drifted against the emergency entrance in long, unbroken curves, and no one had shoveled it. The only footprints leading in were his own.

He had not slept. The house on Fenwick Lane had made sure of that. All night the humming had moved through its walls like water through pipes, and somewhere around three in the morning he had realized it wasn't one voice anymore. It was many. Layered. Patient. A congregation humming in the dark, each voice slightly out of sync with the others, so that the melody never quite resolved — never quite let him go. He had lain in bed with his coat on, keys in his fist, listening to his grandmother's house sing to him in voices he did not recognize and one or two, horribly, that he almost did.

Maren Voss had suffered her stroke on a Tuesday. By Wednesday morning half the town had already folded the news away into that soft place where people keep things they've decided not to think about. *Old age,* they said. *It happens fast, at her age.* As if Maren Voss were a thing that could simply be allowed to happen to, like weather.

Isandro signed in at the front desk. The woman behind it barely looked up. Room 114, she said. Long-term care wing, down the hall, past the vending machines that hummed their own small tuneless hum.

He heard her before he saw her.

Not singing — not exactly. He stood in the doorway of room 114 with his hand still on the frame and watched an old woman lie against white pillows, her mouth moving in slow, deliberate shapes, silent as a fish in winter ice. Her eyes were open. They were fixed on the ceiling, or on something just above the ceiling, and they were bright — too bright for a woman two days past a stroke, bright the way frost is bright, the way a window is bright when there is nothing on the other side of it but cold.

Her lips shaped the melody over and over. He knew it now the way you know a scar. The welcome. The first verse. *This house is warm, this house is yours* — the words Maren had never voiced, only mouthed, only traced in the air between them like a woman handling a live wire through thick gloves. She was mouthing them now without pause, without breath, cycling through the verse and beginning again before the last syllable had finished, a wheel turning and turning.

"Maren." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Maren, it's Isandro."

She didn't stop. Her mouth kept moving. But her eyes came down from the ceiling and found him, and what he saw in them stopped him where he stood.

She was afraid.

Not of him. Not of the room. Afraid the way a person is afraid who has been trying to shout for hours and cannot make a sound. Her hand twitched on the blanket — the left one, the good one — and her fingers curled inward, once, twice, a gesture he couldn't read until he leaned closer and saw that she was trying to point. At herself. At her own throat. Then her fingers spread wide, flat, pressing down against the air: *stop. Quiet. Nothing.*

*I can't stop singing,* she was telling him. *And I can't make a sound.*

He pulled the visitor's chair to the bedside and sat, and took her hand, and told himself the chill in her fingers was poor circulation, was medication, was anything other than what it felt like — the cold of a house that had gotten into a person and settled there, the way damp settles into stone.

"I'm here," he said. "I'm not going anywhere."

Her mouth kept moving. The melody cycled. And Isandro Thornbury sat in the plastic chair and held an old woman's hand and listened to silence shaped like a song, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the entity had found a way to keep her singing even here. Even behind hospital walls. Even with her body broken. It had taken the one weapon she had — her silence, her discipline, sixty years of never letting the song pass her lips — and turned it inside out. Now the song lived in her and would not come out, and could not be put down.

She was trapped mid-verse. Forever, maybe. Or until someone finished it for her.

A nurse came in around noon to check the IV line — a young man with tired eyes and a name tag that said DEV. He worked efficiently, spoke softly, and paused at the door on his way out.

"You're family?" he asked.

"Friend. Neighbor." Isandro hesitated. "Did she say anything? When she came in?"

The nurse glanced back at the bed. Maren's lips moved on, serene, tireless. Something flickered across his face — the particular discomfort of a medical professional who has noticed something he'd prefer to file under *not my department.*

"She sings," he said quietly. "All night. I mean — you wouldn't know it standing there. It's like..." He searched for the word. "It's like she's singing under the floor of her voice. You feel it more than hear it. We asked her to stop, the first night, because the woman in 112 said it was keeping her awake. Mrs. Voss looked right at me—" he rubbed his arms, remembering, "—and shook her head. Just once. Like she was saying, *I wish I could.*"

"Did anyone else visit her?"

"A few townsfolk, first day. Flowers, cards, that kind of thing." He shifted. "There was one visitor late. Night shift logged it. Older lady. Didn't stay long."

"Do you remember her name?"

"It'll be in the book." He nodded toward the nurses' station down the hall. Then, because he was young and kind and had not yet learned which questions cost too much, he added: "Funny thing, though. She signed in, but nobody remembers seeing her leave. And the camera by the east exit — maintenance checked it, some mix-up with the tapes. Whole hour's blank." He smiled the thin smile of a man backing away from his own sentence. "Hospitals, you know. Things fall through cracks."

He left. Isandro sat a long moment with Maren's cold hand in both of his.

Then he went to find the visitor log.

* * *

The logbook lay open on the nurses' station counter, a plain black ledger with a pen chained to it — and Isandro felt the bottom drop out of his stomach before he even touched it, because he knew ledgers now. He knew what this town did with ledgers. He had seen Okafor's book, page after page of Fenwick Lane residents signing themselves into a covenant they didn't understand, each signature a door opening, each date followed within the year by a child going missing.

This was a different book. Smaller. Innocent. A hospital guest register, the kind every waiting room in America kept without thinking, because signatures were how institutions proved to themselves that things happened.

He turned back through the pages. Tuesday: neighbors, church friends, the pastor from St. Brigid's whose handwriting slanted like a man in a hurry. Monday, the day of the stroke: blank except for the admitting staff. Sunday: blank.

He turned forward instead. Wednesday. Yesterday.

One entry.

**Edith Marsh** — 11:47 PM — relation: sister.

Isandro stared at the name until the letters seemed to swim. Edith Marsh. Maren's sister. The sister from the tape — the recording Maren had played for him in her kitchen with hands that shook, the sister who had sung along in 1968, whose voice on the reel-to-reel had grown warmer and happier and closer to the microphone with every bar until the tape ended mid-breath and Edith Marsh had never been seen again. Sixty years dead, or seated, or whatever word applied to a girl who sang a welcome to a hungry house and was welcomed back.

Dated 1968.

That was what the entry said. Not yesterday's date. **1968**, written in the little column meant for the day's date, in the same careful hand as the name. And the ink —

Isandro touched the page and snatched his finger back.

The ink was wet.

Not smudged-wet, not spilled-wet. Fresh. Glistening, faintly, the way frost glistens, the way the signatures on the deed crawled and shone when the light crossed them at the right angle. He looked up. The nurse at the station was sorting charts ten feet away. Other visitors passed with coffee and balloons and get-well cards. Nobody else saw anything wrong with the ledger, because there was nothing wrong with it. It was a perfectly ordinary book containing one impossible line.

*She signed in,* the nurse had said. *Nobody remembers seeing her leave.*

The entity was collecting signatures at the hospital. While Maren lay mouthing the melody it had trapped in her, something wearing her sister's face had walked into this building at 11:47 on a Wednesday night and signed a book that had been waiting fifty-seven years for that name. Signing in. Checking in. Taking up residence — because that was all a signature ever was, in this town. That was all it had ever been. A way of belonging. A way of being owned.

And if Edith Marsh could sign in, then Edith Marsh could sign other things.

His eyes went down the page again, hunting, dreading. No other entries. No fresh ink beneath his own name from yesterday's visit — he checked three times, heart hammering, and found his own signature dry and ordinary and alone.

But the book was not full. There were pages and pages left. Blank lines waiting, each one a chair at a table, each one patient.

Behind him, faint and tireless, from room 114, Maren Voss went on mouthing the welcome to a house that had sent its first collector to fetch her home.

* * *

He stayed through the afternoon. He read to her — the newspaper, badly, skipping the obituaries because he couldn't bear to check whether Maren Voss's name was already in them, pre-written, waiting. He opened the window blinds so the winter light fell across her blanket. He talked about nothing: the school, the snow, the way the town looked from the ridge road. Her mouth moved its silent round. Once, only once, her rhythm broke — the melody stuttering, faltering, her eyes dragging themselves from the ceiling to his face with an urgency that made his chest ache — and her good hand lifted off the blanket and pressed two trembling fingers against her own lips.

*Don't listen.*

Then, slowly, deliberately, those same fingers turned and pointed at him. At his mouth.

*Don't you dare.*

"I know," he whispered. "I know, Maren. I haven't sung. I won't."

Her hand fell back. The melody resumed, smooth as water finding its level, and Isandro understood that whatever was holding her — whatever had coiled into the pause between her heartbeats and set itself humming — it was using her own discipline against her. She would never voice it. She would die mouthing it. And the house could wait. The house had waited since 1891.

But the visitors' book could not. Books wanted filling. Ledgers wanted names. And somewhere in this building, in a room with a blank security tape and a night nurse who remembered nothing, something old and patient had already begun to write.

At dusk he gathered his coat. At the door he stopped and looked back. The light had gone blue and low, and Maren Voss lay in it like a figure in one of her own photographs — still, framed, an empty chair beside her bed where no chair belonged, and Isandro stood very still for a long moment before he admitted to himself that he might have imagined it, and then a longer moment before he believed that he had.

In the hallway, the vending machine hummed. Under it, beneath it, threaded through the building's bones the way the melody threaded through Fenwick Lane's walls, he heard — or felt, the way Dev had said, more than heard — the faintest echo of a welcome, sung by many voices, growing by one.

He walked out past the nurses' station without looking at the book. He failed. At the last second his eyes flicked down, the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth.

Below *Edith Marsh — 1968*, in ink that had not been there at noon, a second line was drying.

**Isandro Thornbury** — relation: *pending*.
