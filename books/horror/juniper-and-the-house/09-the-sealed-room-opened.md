## 9. The Sealed Room, Opened

Ivo could no longer remember her mother's face.

She knew she had one—knew it the way you know a room you've never entered, from the shape of its door—and she stood in the upstairs corridor of Juniper House turning that absence over in her hands like a stone found in a pocket. There had been a woman who braided her hair too tight. There had been a kitchen that smelled of burnt toast and dish soap. The facts were there, filed and labeled, but the living warmth of them had been scooped out, and in the hollow where a childhood should have been there was only a clean white silence, and Wren's voice saying *thank you* in a whisper made of chalk dust.

Half of her was gone already. She could feel the edges of herself like the edges of a wet page.

But Maren's face remained.

That was the strange arithmetic of it, the one mercy buried in the bargain: everything else had gone soft and unmoored, drifting loose like silt in dark water, but Maren's face had anchored itself somewhere below memory, somewhere deeper than the house could reach without tearing Ivo apart entirely. Perhaps because it wasn't a memory at all anymore. Perhaps because Ivo had held onto it so fiercely on the stairs, while the years peeled off her like bark, that it had stopped being something she remembered and become something she *was*. A girl with a gap in her teeth and a scab on one knee, laughing at nothing, laughing at Ivo, laughing the way only someone who loves you laughs—without any reason at all.

Ivo walked toward the sealed room on legs she was no longer entirely sure belonged to her.

The corridor was longer than it should have been. It had been longer than it should have been for fifty years, probably; she simply hadn't been invited to notice. Wallpaper patterned with faded roses repeated and repeated, and between the repeats hung doors—oak, pine, painted, bare—all of them closed, all of them breathing. She did not count them. Counting was how the house held you; Maren had told her that with one written sentence across half a century, and Ivo had spent a lifetime obeying a compulsion she'd mistaken for a quirk, ticking doorframes and window latches and stair treads like a woman keeping score in a game whose only prize was staying owned.

So she didn't count. She put her hand flat against the wallpaper and walked, and the doors muttered behind her in voices like furniture settling, and she did not turn around.

The sealed room was at the end of the corridor, where it had always been, where the chalk drawings said it would be. The door was small—not child-sized exactly, but smaller than a door had any right to be, as if the house had built it in a hurry and been ashamed of the proportions. The seal across it was not wax or paint or boards. It was handwriting. Rows and rows of tiny looping script covering every inch of the wood, written in something brownish that Ivo refused to look at too closely, and she recognized the loops now, recognized them with a sickness that lived in her teeth: they were her own letters, her six-year-old cursive, the alphabet she'd invented for Wren's name because real letters couldn't hold it.

*I am not supposed to open this,* the six-year-old had written, over and over, sealing her own guilt into the grain. *I am not supposed to open this. I am not supposed to open this.*

"You're right," Ivo said aloud, to the ghost of herself, to the house, to no one. "I'm not."

She opened it anyway.

The hinges made no sound at all, which was worse than screaming. Cold air came out—not cold like winter, cold like the inside of a mouth—and it smelled of pencil shavings and rain on hot pavement and the particular sweetness of a sister's breath when she falls asleep against your shoulder. Memories that weren't hers. Air that remembered being loved.

Inside, the dark was total. Not shadow—totality. And in the dark, something waited that had once been a voice.

Ivo stepped through the doorway that was too small for her, and it let her anyway, because doors in this house were only ever opinions.

* * *

The room was the size of a cupboard and the size of a cathedral, depending on which way she looked at it, and she tried very hard not to look at it either way. Her hands found shelves, a child's desk, a wall covered in drawings—hundreds of them, thousands, every drawing she'd ever made of Wren pinned up in careful rows, and beneath them, sitting on the floor with her knees drawn up, was the shape of a sound.

It had no body. That was the first thing. It sat like a body, hunched like a tired child, but it was only the idea of one, an outline drawn in the negative space of the room, and when Ivo knelt in front of it, the outline turned its head, and she heard—faint, frayed, worn down to almost nothing after fifty years of captivity—a hum.

Maren's hum. The tuneless little song Maren used to make while coloring, three notes wandering in circles, the soundtrack of every afternoon of Ivo's erased childhood. Even stripped of everything—even reduced to this, a hum in a sealed room—the sound went through Ivo like a hook through cloth.

"Oh," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Oh, Maren. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I didn't know what I was buying."

The hum faltered. Turned toward her. Waited.

Wren had explained it on the porch, in the last hour of coherence either of them had, her small hands pressed to Ivo's cheeks like the world's most terrible benediction: *A voice can't be carried. It has to be given back out loud. You have to speak it home. But Ivo—Ivo, listen—it will unmake the room, and the room is me, and when the count breaks the house will fall on whatever's inside it.* Wren had smiled then, that chalk-bright smile, unchanged since 1974. *So don't be slow.*

Ivo cupped her hands around the shape of the hum, the way you'd cup water for a bird.

"I don't have much left of me," she told it quietly. "I want you to know that. I gave almost all of it away to get here, and I don't regret it, and I can't remember why I shouldn't, which is probably the same thing." She laughed, or something adjacent to laughter, wet and thin. "But I kept your face. Whatever else they took, I kept your face. So I know exactly who I'm doing this for."

She drew a breath that hurt all the way down, past the places where a childhood used to be.

And she spoke her sister's voice back into the dark.

She didn't have words for it—that was the cruelty and the mercy both. A voice isn't words. So she gave it everything she had that wasn't words: she hummed the tuneless three-note song, badly, a fifty-year-old woman humming a six-year-old's nonsense in a sealed room at the end of a haunted corridor. She laughed the way Maren laughed. She said *Maren* the way Maren's name sounded when it was called across a backyard at dusk, when it meant *come inside, dinner, bedtime, I'm still here.* She poured out the face she'd kept—the gap teeth, the scabbed knee, the laugh with no reason in it—and she spoke it all into the shape on the floor until her throat tore and her eyes streamed and there was nothing left in her hands.

The hum rose to meet her.

It started small, catching at her notes like a fire catching at kindling, and then it grew, and it was Maren, it was wholly Maren, the whole drowned voice coming up out of fifty years of dark with all its music intact, and the sealed room began to scream.

Not the voice. The *room.* The walls buckled like a chest trying to exhale. The drawings of Wren curled and blackened at their pins. The tiny door slammed behind Ivo and vanished, and where it had been there was only corridor, and the corridor was convulsing, and somewhere far above and far below, Juniper House understood—in the way a trap understands, in the way a debt understands—that it had just been robbed.

The count broke.

Ivo heard it before she saw it: a door slamming open. Then another. Then another, each one further away, each slam a syllable in a number being spoken wrong on purpose, and the whole house shuddered like a animal throwing off a harness. Every door in Juniper House was opening at once—she felt it in the floor, in her remaining bones—and every open door was a hole in the house's arithmetic, and the house had been nothing *but* arithmetic, and it was coming apart.

She ran.

She ran down a corridor that rippled like water, past doors flung wide onto rooms that were emptying themselves of fifty borrowed years—rooms full of other people's Christmases dissolving into dust, other people's griefs going up like smoke, the house's hoarded memories streaming out through every broken count like light through shattered glass. The wallpaper roses bled. The stairs moved under her feet and she refused to count them, refused, refused, taking them three at a time into the dark.

Something was holding the house still.

She felt it as a pressure at her back, gentle as a hand between the shoulder blades—holding the collapsing walls apart, holding the falling ceiling up, holding a corridor of screaming architecture open long enough for one woman to run through it. And she knew, without turning, what it cost. She could feel Wren fraying through the pressure itself, coherence spending itself like a candle burned at both ends, the made thing choosing, with the last of what Ivo's loneliness had dreamed into her, to be a doorframe instead of a girl.

"Wren—" Ivo gasped, still running. "Wren, stop, you don't have to—"

*Yes I do,* said the voice in her own six-year-old timbre, everywhere and nowhere, thinner now, unraveling at the edges of every word. *You made me to keep you company. Let me do it one last time.*

The front door stood open ahead of her, and beyond it the porch, and beyond the porch the blue hour and the real world, and Ivo ran through the threshold with Maren's voice singing against her sternum like a second heart—

—and at the last moment, she turned.

She knew she shouldn't. She knew what looking back had cost people in every story ever told. But Wren had been hers—her invention, her friend, her sin and her shelter both—and Ivo Halloran had abandoned her once already, at six years old, by the simple act of growing up. She would not do it twice.

On the stairs behind her, holding up the house with arms made of chalk and borrowed memory, Wren smiled at her. She was coming apart even as she smiled—lines of her lifting off like ash, her edges going soft and bright—but the smile itself was whole, was perfect, was exactly as drawn.

"Goodbye," Ivo said, and her voice broke on it. "Thank you. I'm sorry. Goodbye."

*Counted,* said Wren, fading, radiant, *and let go.*

The house came down behind her like a held breath released.

Ivo stumbled out into the blue dark of the porch with the voice pressed safe against her heart, and behind her Juniper House groaned, settled, and went silent at last—every door hanging open, every window empty, nothing inside it counting anything ever again.

Somewhere across town, in a quiet room in a care home in Fenwick, a woman in her sixties who had not spoken since the autumn of 1974 opened her mouth.

And called her sister's name.
