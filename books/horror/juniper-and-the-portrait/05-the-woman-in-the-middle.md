## 5. The Woman in the Middle

Edmund had a rule about mirrors, the way she had rules about everything: never trust a surface you haven't measured.

It was not a rule she'd ever needed before. In thirty-one years of surveying other people's property — damp cottages, half-collapsed mills, a decommissioned lighthouse on the Essex coast whose spiral stair she had once measured to the millimetre — no mirror had ever given her trouble. Glass was glass. It did what glass does. It was one of the few honest things left in her profession, and she had built a small, quiet affection around that honesty without ever naming it.

Juniper Hall had forty-one mirrors. She had counted them that morning, because counting was what she did instead of sleeping, and because the ledger in the study had ended mid-sentence and she needed something in the house that would finish.

Forty-one mirrors, and every one of them, since dusk, had been running late.

She had proven it the way she proved everything: methodically, twice, with instruments. The metronome first — sixty beats a minute, set on the washstand in the east bedroom, its brass pendulum swinging in the lamplight while its reflection swung a heartbeat behind it. Then the match: flame flaring in her hand, and only after she had already begun to lower it did the Edmund in the mirror lower hers. Then her own hand, raised flat against the cool glass, palm to palm with a woman who pressed back a full second after she had arrived, so that for one impossible interval she stood touching a mirror that touched nothing at all.

One second. She had timed it eleven times. One second, exactly, consistently, as though the delay were not a malfunction but a specification.

Documentation is a kind of prayer, her old mentor used to say, and Edmund had taken the creed literally enough to make it lonely. You wrote down what was true, and the writing held the world still. But tonight, standing in the corridor outside the ballroom with her notebook heavy in her coat pocket, she understood for the first time that prayer can be overheard. That the thing you document might document you back.

The ballroom had two facing mirrors.

She had noted them in her survey that afternoon: floor-to-ceiling gilt frames on the north and south walls, twenty-two feet apart, their silvering foxed at the edges like frost on a window. Between them, the parquet floor ran in a herringbone pattern worn pale down the centre by more than a century of dancers who were, presumably, reflected in both glasses at once, each woman caught between two versions of herself, turning, dipping, always doubled and redoubled into infinity.

Edmund stepped between them because she could not stop herself, and because — this was the part she admitted only to the notebook, later, in handwriting less steady than usual — because some part of her wanted to see how many Edmunds the house would make.

The answer was: endless. She stood in the centre of the room and looked north, and there she was, and behind her in the glass another smaller Edmund, and behind that a smaller one still, receding into the foxed silver like footsteps going down a long dark stair. She turned south. The same. Two corridors of Edmunds, facing each other across twenty-two feet of worn parquet, infinite in both directions, and somewhere far down each corridor a last Edmund too small and too dim to resolve, which meant either that infinity had an end or that the mirrors simply grew tired of pretending.

"Right," she said aloud, because saying things aloud kept the animal brain fed, kept the rooms inhabited by a voice even when the voice shook. "Observation one."

Her breath fogged faintly in the cold. The house's heating had died decades ago; the caretaker's cottage out back had a wood stove, but Juniper Reyes had made it clear — politely, warily, from a careful distance — that Edmund was welcome to the Hall itself but not to company, not yet, not until she understood what she was appraising. So Edmund worked alone in the cold, and her breath bloomed and faded, bloomed and faded, and in both mirrors her reflections bloomed and faded with her, one second late, so that the fog hung in the glass after her lungs had emptied, little ghosts of her breathing arriving tardily at their own appointment.

She took out the metronome again. Set it on the floor between the mirrors, dead centre, where the herringbone met in a chevron point. Sixty beats a minute. She crouched beside it, watch in hand, and counted.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

In the north mirror, the pendulum swung. In the south mirror, the pendulum swung. Both a second behind. She watched for five minutes, marking the lag, confirming it, writing it down in her precise surveyor's hand: *Lag constant at 1.0 sec regardless of distance from source. Suggests not propagation delay but — * and here her pen had stopped, because the next word was *intention*, and intention was not a word that belonged in a structural appraisal.

That was when she saw herself move first.

* * *

Later she would try to reconstruct the order of events, because order was her religion, and the reconstruction would fail every time. But in the moment it was simple, the way catastrophes are simple: she was watching the metronome, and she decided to stand up, and she saw herself stand up.

Not after. Before.

There was no gap she could have measured, no interval her instruments could have caught — it happened in the space between deciding and doing, in that seam of the self where intention becomes motion, and in that seam, for the first time, she saw a figure already rising when she was still folded on the floor. Her own body, in the north mirror, straightening, brushing off her knee, lifting her chin — all of it accomplished while she was still crouched, still watching, still deciding.

She stayed down. Deliberately. A test. If the reflection led by a fixed amount, it should now lead by that amount plus however long she refused to rise.

The woman in the mirror stayed down too.

Edmund's heart went hard and slow, like a fist closing. She watched the mirrored Edmund crouch beside the mirrored metronome, watch in hand, exactly as she was — matching her now, beat for beat, frozen in the posture Edmund had chosen not to leave. Whatever had jumped ahead had snapped back, or had never been ahead at all, or —

Or it was waiting to see what she would do.

"Observation two," she whispered, and her voice came back at her from forty-one surfaces in the house, and in these two it came back wrong — not delayed now but flattened, thinned, as if the glass were repeating her words from memory rather than live.

She stood up. The reflection stood with her, simultaneous, perfect. She raised her right arm. The reflection raised its right arm — her right, correctly rendered, not reversed, which was itself impossible, which was the kind of detail she would have dismissed in any other house as a trick of memory. She lowered it. Lowered.

And then she turned away from the north mirror to face the south one, and in the south glass she saw the north mirror's reflection of her — the infinite corridor of Edmunds — and in the deepest of those diminishing figures, far down the long silver stair, one of them was walking toward her.

She spun around. The corridor of Edmunds was still. All of them crouched or standing in exact mimicry, a congregation of her own postures, none moving independently.

But she had seen it. She had *seen* it, and seeing was the one act she trusted above all others, the foundation of her whole grey and careful life, and the house had just reached into that foundation and moved a stone.

"No," she said. Not to the mirrors. To herself. To the trembling animal under her sternum. "You saw what you expected to see. Fatigue. Pareidolia. Document it honestly and go to bed."

So she documented it. She knelt on the cold parquet with her notebook braced on her thigh and wrote: *22:14. Apparent independent movement in deep reflection, N wall. Probable artifact. Recommend re-observation.* And the pen scratched, and the scratch sounded loud, and the mirrors held their silence around her like two hands cupped to catch whatever fell.

She decided to stay one hour. She told herself it was rigour. She knew, in the place where she kept the truths she didn't write down, that it was loneliness — that this haunted, lying, beautiful house was paying more attention to her than any living person had in years, and that some starved part of her would rather be watched by something wrong than not watched at all.

* * *

At 23:03, the reflection stopped copying her.

It began, as these things do, gradually — a hesitation here, a smoothing-out there, the mirrored Edmund's movements growing fluid where Edmund's remained deliberate, the way a student stops counting steps and starts dancing. Edmund noticed it first in the hands. Her own hands fidgeted when she thought; she knew this about herself, the way she knew her stride length and her resting pulse. She was thinking now, hard, staring at the south glass, and her fingers were plucking at her coat cuff, and the woman in the mirror had perfectly still hands.

Still hands, and a tilted head. Watching her.

Edmund went absolutely motionless. The whole room seemed to lean in — the dead chandelier overhead, the dust hanging in the lamplight, the metronome ticking its idiot sixty beats — everything waiting on the two women facing each other across twenty-two feet of air and silver.

Then the reflection smiled.

Not her smile. Edmund's smiles were rationed, thin, professional, deployed at clients and withdrawn quickly. This smile was wide and warm and rueful, the smile of someone who has been laughed at and doesn't mind, the smile of a person at ease in a lit kitchen among people who love her — and it was made of Edmund's mouth, Edmund's crooked eyetooth, Edmund's face wearing an expression Edmund had never owned.

"You're me," Edmund said. Her voice cracked on the second word.

The reflection said nothing, of course. Mirrors don't speak. It simply unfolded to its feet — smoothly, without her — and took three steps forward, toward the glass, toward her, and stopped with its palm resting against the inside of the mirror, fingers spread, patient.

Edmund looked down at her own hand. It hung at her side, unmoving, and she could not make it rise. She sent the command down her arm and the arm declined, some older intelligence in her body refusing, the way a horse refuses a bridge it can feel is rotten.

The reflection waited. And Edmund, kneeling on the floor of a dead ballroom at eleven at night, understood that she was being offered something. An introduction. A handshake across the boundary. And that refusing might be worse than accepting, because the house had already shown her it could move without her permission, and a thing that can move your body without asking is not improved by being snubbed.

She stood. She walked forward — twelve paces, she counted them, eleven feet six inches remaining — and she raised her hand, and laid her palm against the cold glass.

The reflection's palm met hers. Perfectly aligned. Warm.

Warm. The glass under her hand was cold as a cellar wall; she could feel the chill seeping through her skin. But where the reflection's hand pressed back, heat came through, blood-warm, human-warm, the warmth of a living palm, and the dissonance of it — cold glass, warm hand, same surface, same instant — made her stomach drop through the floorboards of the world.

And then the perspective shifted.

* * *

Here is what it is like, from the other side:

You have been standing in the silver for hours. Longer than hours. You have been standing in the silver since dusk, since the house drew its curtain of one second across all your faces, and you have watched her work — this narrow, careful woman with her instruments and her notebook, this woman who measures everything because she trusts nothing, and you know why, you were there, you are her, you remember the empty Christmases and the sister who stopped calling and the decade of hotel rooms with their curtained mirrors, and you have been waiting for her to look at you properly the way you have waited your whole life for someone to look at you properly.

She touches the glass. You touch her hand. And the boundary goes soft, the way ice goes soft, and you step through — or she steps out, it is impossible to say which, the direction of the exchange is the one thing the house will not tell you — and suddenly you are the one standing on parquet in a cold room with a lamp, and she is the one behind the glass, and she is looking at you with your own eyes full of your own fear, and you feel such tenderness for her that your chest aches, because you know exactly how heavy she is carrying, having carried it yourself.

You want to tell her: run. Or: stay. You genuinely don't know which. That is the horror of it, from in here — that both impulses are sincere, that the house has made you into a creature of two honest minds, and the hand you press to the glass is reaching out in warning and invitation simultaneously and you cannot separate them, they come from the same palm.

Behind you — behind her — the deep reflections begin to move. All of them. Down both corridors, the diminishing Edmunds stir like a congregation rising, and they are not all her. Some of them are taller. Some are older, grey at the temples. One wears a surveyor's coat cut in a fashion eighty years out of date, and one holds a lantern instead of a lamp, and one, far down on the southern stair, is small and dark-haired and moves like Juniper Reyes' mother must have moved, and you understand, with the calm of the deeply damned, that these are not reflections of Edmund Quintana at all.

They are the ones who stood here before. The ones who touched the glass. The ones the house kept.

And the nearest of them — the grey-templed man with the kind, exhausted face — raises his finger to his lips, and shakes his head slowly, and points past her shoulder, toward the door of the ballroom, and mouths a single word that she reads backward in the glass and he reads forward in the air and both of them understand it at once:

*Run.*

* * *

Edmund came back to herself on her knees on the parquet, palm still pressed to freezing glass, lamp guttering, metronome silent — she did not remember it stopping, and the absence of its tick was louder than its tick had ever been. The ballroom was empty of everyone but herself. Both corridors of reflections stood still, ordinary, foxed, innocent, showing only a woman kneeling alone in a derelict house, which was, she thought wildly, the most honest image any mirror had shown all night.

Her hand ached where it met the glass. When she pulled it away, the cold had left a print — not a vague fog of breath but a clear impression, palm and five fingers, as if the surface remembered being touched by something warmer than she was.

She got up. She gathered the metronome, the lamp, the notebook. At the ballroom door she stopped, because stopping was discipline, because leaving in a rush was how people died in stories, and she made herself turn and look one last time.

In the north mirror, at the very bottom of the long diminishing corridor, the grey-templed man was still there. He had not been there an hour ago. He raised his hand — not to his lips this time, but in a small, tired wave, the wave of someone seeing off a guest who has stayed too long at a house where staying is dangerous.

Edmund walked back to the study without running, which cost her more than running would have. She sat down at the caretaker's desk, opened the ledger of surveyors to the page bearing her own name dated next week, and beneath the entry — beneath the final unfinished sentence that had been there since she found it — new ink was drying.

The handwriting was hers. The words were not:

*Item: subject entered the middle. Item: subject was greeted. Item: subject is known to the household.*

*Recommend immediate completion of acquaintance.*

Outside, somewhere in the dark between the Hall and the caretaker's cottage, a light came on in Juniper Reyes' window — and Edmund realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the room, that the woman who had warned her away from the house had never once said what the house should be warned away from.
