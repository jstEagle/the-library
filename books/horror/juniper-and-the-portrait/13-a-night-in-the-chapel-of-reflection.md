## 13. A Night in the Chapel of Reflection

The door closed behind Edmund with the sound of a book shutting.

She had not opened it herself — that was the first thing she made note of, because making notes was how she kept her hands from shaking. She had been following the lag. That was all. The mirrors had been running four seconds behind since sundown, and she had learned to use the delay like a blind woman uses a cane: step where the reflection stepped, trust the room that arrived a moment after you did. It had led her down a corridor that wasn't on her survey, past a door that wasn't on the plan, and through it into a chapel that existed only in glass.

Now the door was gone.

Not locked. Not barred. *Gone* — the wall behind her seamless plaster, cool and slightly damp, like the skin of something sleeping. Her flashlight beam found no seam, no hinge, no scar in the paint. She pressed her palm flat against it and felt, faintly, a pulse that was either the house's or her own and she could no longer say which.

"Documentation is a kind of prayer," she said aloud, because saying it helped. Her voice came back to her wrong — half a beat late, as if the chapel were repeating her before she finished speaking.

She turned and looked at what had caught her.

The chapel was small, smaller than any room the Hall's footprint could contain, and every surface of it was mirror. Not panels, not glass set into frames — the walls themselves were reflective, curved subtly inward the way the inside of an eye is curved, so that wherever she stood she stood at the center of herself. A dozen Edmunds looked back at her from a dozen distances, each one fractionally delayed, so that when she raised her light they raised theirs in a slow ripple, a wave of her own gesture rolling outward and back like something breathing.

At the far end, where an altar should have been, there was a font. Stone, shallow, dry. Above it hung no cross but a frame — an empty mirror-frame, ornate, silvered with age, holding nothing but the wall behind it. Except the wall behind it was not the wall behind it. In the empty frame she could see the chapel as it might have been: candlelight, garlands, nine figures standing in a crescent with their heads bowed. A memory playing on a loop too worn to hold its edges anymore.

Edmund checked her watch. 11:47 p.m. Sunrise at 6:12. She had done the arithmetic of trapped-in-the-dark before, in other houses, other lifetimes, and the arithmetic always helped. Six hours and twenty-five minutes. She sat down against the font, back to cold stone, and opened her survey notebook across her knees.

That was when the reflections began to arrive.

* * *

It started with the candles.

In the frame above the altar, the remembered ceremony flickered, and one of the nine bowed figures lifted its head. A woman, gray-haired, wearing a cardigan Edmund recognized from a photograph in the caretaker's ledger — Ada Reyes, Juniper's grandmother, younger here than in any photo, her face smooth with the terrible youth of the dead. She stepped out of the frame the way a person steps out of a doorway, without drama, without light bending, and stood in the chapel looking at Edmund with an expression of profound, exhausted kindness.

"You're the surveyor," Ada said. Her voice had the texture of a recording played once too often. "The one who measures."

"I document," Edmund said. Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "Who are you?"

"Ada. I kept the east rooms for thirty-one years." The reflection — Edmund could not make herself think of her as anything else — folded her hands. "You've met my daughter's handwriting in the ledger. You'll meet my granddaughter's face tomorrow, if you're still you by then."

"What do you mean, if—"

"We're the others." Ada gestured, and the chapel filled.

They came out of the walls the way images come out of developing fluid — gradually, all at once, faces surfacing through the silvering. A man in a surveyor's coat from a century ago, his brass instruments still slung across his chest. Two sisters in matching aprons. A boy of perhaps fourteen holding a lantern that cast no light. An old man whose mouth moved continuously, silently, counting something. Nine of them, then more than nine, then dozens — caretakers going back and back, generations deep, their clothing cycling through decades like a film run fast. They arranged themselves in the crescent shape from the frame above the altar, facing her, and the arrangement was so practiced, so worn into the room, that Edmund understood they had stood this way ten thousand times.

"We are what the house keeps," Ada said. "What it reflects instead of releases."

Edmund wrote in her notebook: *Reflected chapel contains accumulated reflections of former caretakers. Est. 40+ individuals. Coherent. Communicative.* Her hand was steady. She was grateful for the steadiness, even if it was a lie she was performing for an audience of herself.

"You're trapped," she said. "In the mirrors."

"We're *held*," said the old surveyor, and his voice was different from Ada's — harder, angrier, worn down to wire. "Held is not trapped. Trapped implies someone lost the key. We were put here. Deliberately. By people who thought hunger could be housebroken if you fed it slowly enough."

"The ritual," Edmund said. "The covenant in the cellar."

"Yes." Ada again, gentle, steering. "You've seen it. Nine names, nine duties. The founders meant it as a covenant of care — a household given freely, feeding the house willingly, so the house would never need to take. And for a while it worked. For a while this place was—" she smiled, and the smile cracked down the middle like old varnish, "—it was *loved*, surveyor. Do you understand? Some houses are only built. This one was loved into being. That's why it can't simply fall down. Love doesn't permit it."

"But the ninth line went blank."

"The last founder died before naming her successor, and the ones after — the descendants — they stopped living here. Stopped *staying*. A household needs presence, not paperwork. So the house began to starve, and starving things don't keep covenants. They keep larders." The old surveyor spat, though nothing came. "We're the larder."

Edmund looked at them properly then. Up close, in the curved light, she could see what they were made of. Not flesh. Reflection-stuff — the same delayed silver as the mirrors, moving a half-beat behind their own gestures, so that Ada's smile arrived slightly after Ada decided to smile. They were lag incarnate. People who had become the second between a thing and its image.

"How long have you been here?" she asked.

The boy with the lantern spoke for the first time. "I don't remember outside," he said. "I remember there was an outside. I remember grass being a color I liked."

Something in Edmund's chest, long boarded over, shifted on its nails.

* * *

They told her their stories in overlapping pieces, the way water finds every channel at once. The surveyor from the brass instruments — Halloway, 1911 — had come to appraise the Hall and been kept because the house liked having its measurements admired. The apron sisters had been hired cooks who stayed one winter too many. The counting man had been a caretaker who tried to leave in 1953 and had been walking toward the front door, inside the mirrors, ever since; he counted his steps to keep from screaming, and he was somewhere past four million.

"They beg," Halloway said flatly, watching her face. "I want you to know that going in. We're going to beg, and it's going to be awful, and some of us mean it kindly and some of us mean it selfishly and after enough years in here you can't tell the difference yourself. But hear the truth under the begging: finishing the ritual frees us. All of us. Every name written, every duty bound, and the house eats a household instead of hoarding us like coins in a dead man's pocket."

"And the household it eats," Edmund said quietly, "is a person. One name on the ninth line."

Silence. Even the counting man stopped counting.

"One name," Ada agreed softly. "Given freely. Bound gladly. That was always the terms. The founders wrote them that way so it would be a gift and never a theft." She knelt beside Edmund, and up close her reflected eyes were full of something so like love that Edmund's throat closed. "You've been alone a long time, haven't you. We see it. The house sees it. You measure everything because if you're measuring, no one asks why your emergency contact is blank."

"That's not in any record you could have read."

"The house reads deeper than records." Ada's hand hovered near Edmund's knee, not touching. Reflected things couldn't touch; Edmund understood that now, understood it as mercy. "There's a sister. There's a phone call you draft every Christmas and delete every Boxing Day. There's a flat you've lived in six years and never hung a picture in, because pictures make a place a home and homes can be taken away. Oh, surveyor. We know what you'd trade. We know because we traded it too, every one of us, and we told ourselves it was sacrifice and it was, it truly was, and we are *still here*."

"Finish it," said the boy, urgent now, coming close, his lantern-lightless lantern swinging. "Write the name. Free us. Please. Please, I just want to see if grass is still the color I liked."

"Finish it," said the sisters, in unison, sweetly, terribly.

"Finish it," said the counting man, breaking count for the first time in seventy years, and the silence of his stopping was louder than all their voices.

And Edmund sat in the middle of them, in the eye of their need, and felt the thing she had feared feeling since the ledger showed her own name dated next week: *compassion, weaponized*. Because they weren't lying. That was the horror of it. Every word was true. They were held, and hungry-for-release, and suffering in a way she could measure — she could *measure* it, God help her, she could quantify their captivity in seconds of lag and decades of service — and the only key was a name, and the name everyone wanted was Juniper's, and Juniper had brought her tea two nights ago with both hands and laughed at her joke about laser levels like it was the first laugh anyone had given her in months.

"She's nineteen," Edmund said. "She's a child."

"She's a Reyes," said Halloway. "The line was chosen before she was born."

"Chosen by whom?"

"By us. By the tired. By everyone in this room who wanted it to be someone else's turn." His anger collapsed all at once into something older and worse. "That's the inheritance, surveyor. Not the duty. The *cowardice*. Each generation passes the cup along because drinking it ourselves was unthinkable, and the unthinkable gets more unthinkable every year it goes undrunk, until a girl grows up knowing the exact date her life ends and calls it family tradition."

Edmund closed her notebook. Her hands were shaking now; she let them.

"If I finish it," she said, "with her name — I free you. And I spend her."

"Yes."

"And if I don't, you stay here."

"Yes."

"And the house stays hungry."

Ada's smile returned, cracked and kind. "And the house stays hungry. And it will keep offering, surveyor, because it likes you. It has been showing you nurseries. It thinks you'd be a wonderful mother to it."

* * *

The night wore on the way nights do in places without windows: not passing but accumulating. The reflections did not sleep. They circled her in slow rotations, taking turns, each one kneeling to plead in turn — and the pleading changed register as the hours deepened, from eloquence to plainness to the naked animal simplicity of the boy, who eventually simply sat beside her and leaned his weightless shoulder against hers and whispered, over and over, *please, please, please,* in the voice of someone who has forgotten that please is a word and not a heartbeat.

Edmund documented. It was all she had. She filled eleven pages: names, dates, duties, the geometry of the chapel, the behavior of the frame above the altar, which now showed not the founding ceremony but something else — a possible future, rendered in the same worn silver. In it, a woman with Edmund's posture signed a document while another woman with dark hair watched from a doorway, and the watching woman's face was doing something Edmund refused to interpret.

Around three in the morning, Ada sat down across from her.

"You're going to refuse," Ada said. Not accusing. Reading, the way Edmund read rooms.

"I'm going to find a third option."

"There is no third option. There's the cup or the thirst."

"Every survey I've ever done, someone told me the boundary was fixed. Fences, deeds, easements — the whole grammar of land says *this is where things end*. And every survey worth the name finds the line drawn wrong." Edmund tapped the notebook. "Your covenant assumes the house must eat a household. Nobody ever surveyed *that* assumption. Nobody asked what else a house built on love might consent to accept, if someone finally asked it honestly instead of feeding it people."

Ada studied her for a long time. Around them the other reflections had gone quiet, listening with the hunger of the long-confined.

"You want to renegotiate with a starving thing," Ada said.

"I want to reclassify it. There's a difference between a home and a grave, and it isn't the building. It's what the living owe it." Edmund heard the words leave her and knew they were true and knew they were insane and knew, with the cold professional certainty that had carried her through every haunted appraisal of her career, that they were the only true insane thing in the room. "A grave doesn't get tenants. It gets mourning. Maybe that's all this house has ever needed — not a family to eat. Someone to grieve it properly. To mark it. To say: *here lies a thing that was loved, and the loving killed it, and we will not pretend otherwise.*"

The counting man began to count again. But slower. As if considering.

Ada reached out her weightless hand and, for the first time, touched — barely, like frost forming — the back of Edmund's wrist. Where her fingers landed, the skin showed a faint silvering, one second of lag written into living flesh.

"Sunrise is soon," Ada said. "The door will open, because the house wants you to go and carry our begging to the girl. It knows you'll tell her. It's *counting on* you to tell her." Her eyes were unbearably sad. "So here is my last gift, surveyor, freely given, no strings: when you speak to Juniper Reyes, and she looks at you with those eyes her mother had, and you feel what you're feeling right now — remember that we felt it too. Remember that compassion is exactly how this house feeds. It has never once used force. It only ever shows you someone suffering and puts the key in your hand and lets your own heart do the turning."

The first gray suggestion of dawn seeped into the mirrored walls from nowhere, from everywhere, and the reflections thinned with it like breath fading off glass. The boy faded last, still whispering *please*, and then the chapel was only a chapel of mirrors, and the wall behind Edmund softened, and a door appeared in it — the same door, the corridor beyond, the honest world with its honest lag.

Edmund stood. Her legs had gone to wood. In the nearest mirror her reflection stood a half-second behind her, and as she gathered her notebook and her light, she saw the reflection do something she did not do.

It looked back at the altar. At the empty frame. And it nodded — once, slow, deliberate — at whatever stood in the frame's silvered depths, nodding agreement to terms Edmund had not yet heard offered.

Then the lag closed, and the reflection was only her, and the door stood open, and somewhere upstairs Juniper was waking in a house that had already begun, gently, patiently, to make its case.

Edmund walked toward the door and did not run, because running was what the house wanted — running proved fear, and fear proved she could be steered. But at the threshold she stopped, and took out her pen, and wrote one line in the margin of page eleven, pressing hard enough to score the pages beneath:

*Note to self: the most dangerous sentence in any negotiation is "they wouldn't lie about suffering." Suffering lies. Suffering lies beautifully.*

Then she went to find Juniper, and to tell her everything — including the part Edmund was already afraid to admit aloud:

that some traitor corner of her had spent the whole night wanting to say yes.
