## 16. The Reflected Wing

The door to the reflected wing had not existed that morning. Edmund was certain of it, and certainty — her own, her hard-won, instrument-calibrated certainty — was the one thing she could no longer trust in this house. But the door was there now: a tall thing of blackened oak at the end of the east corridor, its frame hung with juniper berries gone grey as ash, and it stood open onto a darkness that breathed.

Beyond it, faintly, she could hear Juniper's footsteps. Not ahead of her. Behind her. A full second behind, the way everything in this wing worked.

"Juniper!" Her voice went into the dark and came back wrong — delayed, flattened, as if the house were repeating her to itself, tasting the words.

She stepped through.

* * *

The reflected wing did not obey the survey she'd spent two weeks drafting. That was the first thing she understood, standing in a corridor that should have been the butler's pantry and was instead a hallway lined with doors — forty of them, fifty, their number shifting when she wasn't counting directly. The wallpaper was the same faded rose as the front hall, but the pattern moved if she looked at it too long, small botanical figures turning their heads like horses at a fence line.

She raised her laser level out of habit more than hope. The beam found the far wall and held there, steady red, and then the dot slid — not because her hand shook, but because the wall arrived late. She watched the reading settle a full second after the wall had visibly stopped moving. The house here was not lying about its dimensions. It was simply telling the truth one second behind itself.

*Documentation is a kind of prayer,* she thought, and almost laughed. Well then. Pray your way through.

She began to map by lag. If a room existed only in reflection, then the reflection was the room's most current self, and the room was its past — and Edmund Quintana had built a career on reconciling past and present states of a property. She would walk toward where Juniper's echo sounded, and when the sound arrived, she would know the space between them was exactly one second wide. The whole wing became an instrument. The whole wing became legible.

The first door opened onto the nursery.

She hadn't meant to open it. The handle had been warm under her hand, warm as a living cheek, and then she was inside a room lit by low amber light with no visible source. A crib stood against the wall. A rocking chair moved gently, empty, keeping time with no one. On the floor, blocks spelled out letters she didn't look at too closely, because the arrangement kept changing, and she suspected the room was trying to spell her name.

In the mirror above the dresser, the nursery showed a woman sitting in the rocking chair. Dark hair pulled back. Reading glasses on a chain. Her mother's glasses; her mother's posture; her mother's habit of tucking one foot beneath her. The woman in the mirror was reading aloud from a picture book, and though the glass gave no sound, Edmund knew the cadence of it, the rise and fall, the little pause before the page turn. She had fallen asleep to that cadence thirty years ago and had spent every year since trying to hear it again.

The reflection's mother looked up. Looked at Edmund. Smiled the smile that had been turned away from her on the platform at Paddington, all those years ago, when her mother had put her sister on the train instead and waved until the carriage disappeared.

"No," Edmund said aloud, and her voice came back a second later, thinner.

The nursery dimmed around her like something exhaling. She walked out without running, because running felt like what the house wanted, and shut the door behind her, and stood in the corridor with her heart going like a hammer on sheet metal.

It knows, she thought. It has been appraising me for weeks. Of course it knows what I'd trade.

Her hand was still on the door handle. Warm. Patient. Like a hand holding hers.

She let go and walked on.

* * *

The corridor of doors delivered her, eventually, to the chapel — or rather, to the chapel's reflection, which was larger than the chapel itself, the way a grief is larger than its cause. Candles burned in ranks along the walls, hundreds of them, each flame perfectly still, burning without consuming, and the light they gave was the color of old photographs. In the center of the floor lay the covenant stone, and beside it, dropped as if in flight, Juniper's canvas jacket.

Edmund picked it up. The fabric was cold, but the pockets were warm — keys, a folding knife, a stub of pencil, a bus timetable for a route that hadn't run since 1987, kept the way other people keep saints' medals. She held the jacket against her chest for a moment, absurdly, the way you'd hold a child, and then folded it over her arm and kept going.

Behind her, in the chapel's great dark mirror, the candles had begun to go out. One by one, in order, like a congregation bowing its head.

* * *

She found the dining room next, and this was where the house made its real offer.

The table was set for six. Candles lit. Food steaming — actual steam, actual smell, roast meat and rosemary and the burnt-sugar edge of a pie cooling somewhere out of sight, and Edmund's stomach clenched with a hunger that had nothing to do with food and everything to do with it too. She hadn't eaten a meal with anyone in longer than she could comfortably calculate. She ate standing up, over the sink, in rented rooms, documenting her own life in triplicate because no one else would witness it.

And around the table, in the tall mirror on the far wall, sat her family.

Not the family she'd had. The family she'd never gotten. Her mother at the head, older, softer, the estrangement never having happened. Her sister beside her, laughing at something, wearing the easy affection she'd only ever shown in photographs. And two empty chairs — no. Not empty. As Edmund watched, the chairs filled: a man with kind eyes and flour on his hands, and a girl of perhaps ten who turned toward the glass and mouthed, clearly, deliberately, *Mum.*

The reflection ran a full second ahead of the room. She understood that now. Here, in the wing's deepest rooms, the lag had inverted — the mirror showed what was coming, what could come, what the house was prepared to serve. The girl in the mirror said *Mum* again, and a full second later Edmund heard it, small and clear, in a voice like a bell over water.

"You're not real," Edmund said. "None of this is real."

"It could be," said the man with flour on his hands, and his voice came from the empty chair, warm and reasonable. "That's rather the point of the place. A household, held forever. Nobody leaves. Nobody dies on a platform waiting for someone who doesn't come." He gestured at the food. "You've been hungry a long time, Edmund. We've read your ledger."

"I know you have." Her voice was steadier than she felt. "You wrote half of it in my handwriting."

"We only transcribed what was already true." He smiled. Somewhere in the walls, paper rustled — pages turning, entries being made. "Sit down. Eat. Stay. The ninth name doesn't have to be Juniper's. It could be yours. You could be the one who holds the family together. Isn't that what you've wanted? To matter to a household? To be load-bearing?"

*Load-bearing.* The word went through her like a spike. Yes. God help her, yes — she had wanted exactly that, all her life, to be the beam that held something up, to be necessary to a structure that noticed.

And Juniper's jacket was folded over her arm, and Juniper was somewhere in this wing alone, believing — having seen Edmund's face across a table while descendants offered her name like currency — that Edmund had considered the trade.

Because she had. For four seconds, maybe five, she had let the promise move through her like warmth through cold hands. And the house had recorded those seconds. The house recorded everything.

"I'm looking for Juniper Reyes," Edmund said. "Where is she?"

The man's smile didn't falter, but the candle flames all leaned, together, away from her, as if a draft had passed through the room's opinion of her.

"She's where people go," he said, "when they stop being useful to a story."

* * *

She left the dining room and its warm dead air behind and walked faster now, jacket clutched to her chest, following the lag. Juniper's footstep-echoes had gone quiet, but the house itself had begun to leak — small sounds arriving a beat late through the walls: a sob with no crier, a door slamming somewhere a second after the slam, and once, unbearably, her own voice saying *I wouldn't trade her*, words she hadn't yet said, arriving early, the house showing her its hand or begging her to say them aloud so they'd be true.

She said them anyway. Into the corridor. Loudly. "I would not trade her. Write that down. Enter it in your ledger. Edmund Quintana, appraiser, finds the subject property morally insolvent."

The walls went very quiet. Then, somewhere deep in the wing, a door opened — and stayed open, the sound of it hanging in the air like an invitation or a wound.

She followed it through three more rooms that existed only in reflection: a laundry where sheets dried on lines strung between decades; a schoolroom with nine desks and nine names carved into the wood, eight of them worn smooth by centuries of thumbs, the ninth freshly cut and raw; a stairwell that descended into the cellar she knew, except the covenant stone down here was blank, all nine names erased, waiting, and she understood with a lurch that this was the version of the house that wanted to start over with new blood entirely.

Hers. Or Juniper's. The house wasn't particular. Hunger rarely is.

At the bottom of the stairwell, the air changed. Colder. Wetter. Realer — she could taste the mineral truth of actual stone, actual groundwater, and she knew she'd crossed back out of the reflected wing into the house's honest bones. Ahead, under the single bulb, the true covenant stone waited, and beside it stood Juniper.

She was facing the wall, and she was crying, and in her right hand, held like a weapon or a lifeline, was the founders' pen.

"Juniper—"

"Don't." Juniper didn't turn. Her voice was scraped raw. "I saw your face. In the dining room. Don't tell me I'm wrong, because I watched you *consider* it, and I've spent my whole life watching people consider it and then do it anyway, so just—" Her shoulders shook. "Just let me finish it myself. My name. My choice. At least it'll be mine."

The pen hovered over the blank ninth line. The stone seemed to lean toward it, the way a starving animal leans toward a hand.

Edmund set the canvas jacket down on the floor between them, gently, the way you'd set down something breakable, and stepped forward, and reached out — not for the pen, not for Juniper, but for the surveyor's case she'd carried through every room of this house, through every reflection, the one thing the mirrors had never managed to copy.

She opened it. She took out her own pen.

"Before you write anything," Edmund said, "I need you to witness a document."

Juniper turned at last, tear-tracked, furious, hopeful, and Edmund uncapped her pen over the covenant stone, over the blank ninth line, over the whole starving architecture of the ritual — and began to write.
