## 4. The Extra Chair

The photographs lived in the front bedroom, in a cedar chest that smelled of mothballs and old varnish and, beneath both, something sweeter — cedar oil, maybe, or the ghost of a sachet Bram had tucked among the linens forty years ago and never replaced. Juniper knelt on the floorboards with the chest open before her like a mouth, and she began, methodically, to empty it.

She had made herself a system by now. It was the third day in the house, and systems were how she kept from thinking too hard about the tapes in the basement, about the voice on the last one saying *not yet* in a whisper that had followed her up the stairs and into her dreams. Sort first. Feel later. That was the deal she'd struck with herself, and so far she was honoring it.

Photographs went into one of three piles: *keep*, *donate*, *burn*. The burn pile was smallest. The donate pile was mostly landscapes — Bram had taken dozens of pictures of the lake at every hour of the day, gray water, silver water, black water, as if she were trying to catch it doing something. The keep pile grew slowly: Juniper's mother as a girl, gap-toothed on the porch steps; Juniper's grandfather in a uniform she'd never heard described; Juniper herself at seven, missing a front tooth, held aloft in Bram's thin arms while they both laughed at something outside the frame.

It was good work, in its way. Grief work, Marcus would have called it — no. Not Marcus. She hadn't met Marcus yet. Grief work, her therapist would have called it, back when she'd still been able to afford a therapist. Handle each image. Let it pass through you. Don't grip.

She gripped anyway, sometimes. She couldn't help it.

The afternoon light came through the west window in long amber bars, and dust turned slowly in it, and for an hour or more the house felt almost kind. Almost ordinary. A dead woman's house full of dead women's faces, but kind.

Then she found the parlor photographs.

There were more of them than she expected. The back parlor — the small room off the kitchen with the tall windows and the wallpaper of faded blue roses — had apparently been the stage for half a century of family life. Birthdays. Christmases. Easter mornings with the table dragged in from the dining room. Someone's graduation, someone's engagement, someone's new baby passed from arm to arm like a collection plate. In every picture, the whole family arranged itself against the parlor's far wall, below the window, faces tilted toward the camera with that particular squint people get when they're pretending not to blink.

And in every picture, there was a chair.

Juniper noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a song you've known all your life — not immediately, but as a slow accumulation of unease, a itch between the shoulder blades. She'd gone through perhaps thirty photographs before she stopped, went back to the first one, and looked properly.

A wooden chair. Straight-backed, plain, the wood dark with age, standing against the wall to the left of the window. Just a chair. The kind of thing a house accumulates and nobody thinks about.

Except.

Juniper set the photograph down on the floorboards, walked out of the front bedroom, down the hall, through the kitchen, and opened the door to the back parlor.

The room was empty.

Not empty of furniture entirely — there was a sideboard against the east wall under a sheet, and a stack of folding chairs leaning in the corner, aluminum, the cheap kind used for funerals and garage sales. But no wooden chair. No straight-backed, dark-wooded, plain old chair anywhere along the left wall, or the right wall, or any wall. The floorboards were bare where the photographs showed the chair standing. Bare, and lighter than the boards around them, as if something had stood there long enough to keep the sun off — but nothing stood there now.

Juniper stood in the doorway a long moment, listening to the house breathe around her. Then she went back to the chest.

* * *

By evening she had pulled every parlor photograph from the piles and laid them out across the front bedroom floor in chronological order, which was easy enough to determine because Bram had written dates on the backs in her small, upright hand — the same hand that had labeled fifty years of cassette tapes. 1961. 1963. 1968. 1971. The dates marched forward across the floorboards like footprints, and in every single image, the chair was there.

Same chair. Same position. Same angle of light falling across its seat.

Juniper told herself the obvious explanations first, because she was a rational person, because rationality was a rope and she intended to hold onto it. The chair had existed once and been removed. Fine. People got rid of chairs. The photographs simply recorded a piece of furniture that had since gone to the dump or the church sale, and there was nothing strange in that, nothing at all, except —

Except that in the 1972 photograph, someone had hung a wreath on the parlor door, and the doorframe was painted green, and the wallpaper showed blue roses in a pattern she could match, rose for rose, to the actual paper on the actual walls. Except that the room in the photographs was *this* room, exactly this room, down to the hairline crack above the window that Juniper had noticed her first morning and privately thought looked like a river delta seen from very high up. The crack was in the photographs. The crack was in the wall. Everything matched.

Everything matched except the chair.

She sat cross-legged on the floor amid five decades of family joy and studied the chair the way she'd once studied a painting in a seminar, looking for what it was hiding. It never changed. That was the thing that began to crawl up the back of her neck. Chairs wear. Chairs get moved — two inches here, a foot there, turned to face the tree at Christmas, pushed aside to make room for the high chair when the babies came. This chair did none of that. In 1961 it stood against the left wall below the window, and in 1998 it stood against the left wall below the window, and in every year between, identical, patient, its seat catching the same bar of window-light as if the sun itself had agreed to hit the same spot forever.

Nobody ever sat in it.

That realization arrived quietly, without drama, and then refused to leave. She went through the photographs again, deliberately, counting bodies. Birthday parties with twelve people crammed shoulder to shoulder. Christmas mornings with wrapping paper drifts on the floor. And always, always, the crowd arranged itself to the right of the chair, around the chair, in front of the chair — but never on it. Children who climbed on everything, who perched on arms of sofas and mantelpieces and each other's shoulders, children who in one 1985 snapshot were using the dog as a footstool — even those children gave the chair a wide berth. Their bodies angled away from it. Their eyes, if you looked closely, if you cropped in close the way she was cropping in close now with her phone's camera, their eyes were never quite pointed at it.

As if it weren't there.

Juniper lowered the phone. Her hands had gone cold, though the room was warm.

Because that was the other thing, wasn't it. The thing she'd been circling since the moment she'd stood in the parlor doorway and seen bare floorboards. In the photographs, everyone behaved as though the chair were invisible. They smiled past it. They reached across it to hand dishes. In one picture, a man — great-uncle somebody, his name written on the back in Bram's hand — leaned back casually against the wall directly beside it, his elbow hovering in the space just above the chair's armrest, touching nothing.

They couldn't see it. The chair was in every photograph and no one had ever seen it.

Juniper gathered the pictures into a careful stack, oldest on top, and carried them to the kitchen table, where the light was better and where she could hear herself think over the tick of the hallway clock. She made tea she didn't drink. She laid the photographs out again in a long line down the table's length, and she walked beside them like a general reviewing troops, and she let herself say the sentence she'd been refusing to say:

The chair is not in the room, and the chair is in every photograph, and those two facts cannot both be true unless one of them is lying.

* * *

She didn't sleep much. When she did sleep, near dawn, she dreamed of the basement wall of tapes, each spine labeled in Bram's handwriting, and in the dream the labels were rearranging themselves into a single word repeated down the entire wall, and she woke before she could read it.

Morning came gray and slow. Juniper made coffee and carried her phone into the back parlor, because there was one more test she hadn't run, and she wanted it done in daylight, with coffee in her, with the windows open to birdsong and the neighbor's distant lawnmower. Ordinary sounds. Proof of an ordinary world.

She stood in the center of the room, facing the left wall below the window, the spot where the floorboards were pale. She raised the phone. Framed the shot the way the old photographs framed it — wall, window, crack in the plaster like a river delta.

She took the picture.

For a moment she just stood there, thumb hovering, letting herself enjoy the last second in which the result could still be nothing. A normal room. An empty wall. Dust motes and morning light and the pale patch of floor where a chair had once, decades ago, stood.

Then she looked at the screen.

The chair was there.

Dark wood, straight-backed, plain. Standing against the left wall below the window, its seat catching the light, occupying the exact footprint it occupied in every photograph since 1961. In the photo it was unmistakable, solid, *present* — rendered in the flat honest pixels of her phone's camera with all the authority of a document. Behind her, in the actual room, the wall was blank. She turned and checked. Blank. She turned back to the phone. There.

Her breath came out in a sound she didn't recognize as coming from her own throat.

She took another photograph. The chair again. Another. Again. She photographed it from the doorway, from the corner, from directly in front of it — and in the last one, shooting straight down the chair's sightline, she saw that the seat was worn smooth in two places, symmetrically, the way wood wears under hands gripping armrests, or under — she stopped the thought — under a body sitting and rising, sitting and rising, for a very long time.

She backed out of the room. She stood in the kitchen with her heart going like a trapped bird, and she scrolled back through yesterday's photos, the ones she'd taken her first evening in the house — snapshots of boxes, of the kitchen, of the view from the porch — and found the one she'd taken of the parlor through its open door, casual, barely looking, just documenting the state of the rooms for the estate lawyer.

In that photograph, taken two days ago, the chair was there too. Far away, small, half-hidden in shadow at the edge of the frame. But there.

It had been there the whole time. Since the night she arrived. Since before.

Juniper braced both hands on the counter and made herself breathe. Outside, the lawnmower droned on, indifferent. The clock ticked in the hall. The world continued to be the world.

*One more,* she thought. *One more, and then I stop.*

She didn't know why she needed one more. Maybe because terror and compulsion are twins; maybe because some part of her already understood that the photographs were a language and she was being taught it word by word. She went back to the parlor doorway. She raised the phone. She took a final photograph of the empty-seeming room, and lowered the phone, and looked.

The chair was there, as it would always now be there.

But it had moved.

Not far. Two feet, perhaps three — out from the wall, into the room, angled slightly toward the doorway. Toward her. Its front legs rested on the border where the pale patch of floor met the darker wood, as if it had stepped to the very edge of its own outline and paused there, considering.

And it faced the camera.

Every photograph for sixty years, the chair had faced the wall, or the window, or the family — indifferent, decorative, furniture. In this one, in the newest one, in the one Juniper Hale had taken ninety seconds ago on a gray Tuesday morning, the chair faced the lens directly, squarely, the way a person turns to look at you when they've finally decided you're worth turning around for.

Its seat was empty. It was waiting to be filled.

Juniper's thumb was moving before she knew it, scrolling, comparing, confirming — the old photos, the new ones, the line of them down the kitchen table like evidence at a trial. Sixty years of birthdays and christenings and graduations, and in every frame, patient as a debt, the chair. Her grandmother in every era of her life, laughing beside it, leaning near it, never once touching it, never once sitting in it — and Juniper understood, with a certainty that arrived whole and cold like ice water in the veins, that Bram had known. Bram had spent her whole life knowing, taking her family's pictures in that room year after year, smiling beside the thing only the camera could see, and never, not once, telling anyone.

On the counter, Juniper's phone buzzed. Once. Twice.

She looked down. The screen showed a notification from her photos app, cheerful and automatic, the way machines are cheerful about the worst things: *You have a new memory from one year ago today.*

She hadn't been in this house a year ago. She'd never taken a photograph in this house before this week.

With a finger that did not feel like her own, she tapped it.

The memory opened. A single image, timestamped one year ago today, 2:14 p.m. The back parlor, in daylight. The wooden chair against the left wall below the window, facing the wall, minding its own business, waiting.

And seated on the bed of the front bedroom behind her — visible through the parlor's open door, small in the background, blurred as if caught mid-turn — a woman with dark hair, sitting on the edge of the mattress with her head bowed, as if she'd just woken, or was about to pray.

Juniper zoomed in until the pixels broke apart.

The woman had her face.
