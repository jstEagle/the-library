## 9. The Girl Who Stayed

Juniper did not sleep.

She sat at Bram's kitchen table until the windows went from black to the color of dishwater, the torn ledger pages spread before her like a wound she couldn't stop looking at. *Lorraine Dunmore — paid in full.* The ink had gone brown with age, but the strike-through was still violent, still deliberate, a single hard line drawn by a hand that hadn't trembled. Bram's handwriting was beautiful. That was the worst part. Even the erasure of her sister had been done with care.

The house creaked around her in the early light, settling into its bones, and Juniper found herself listening to it differently now. Not as a grieving granddaughter cataloguing an inheritance, but as something closer to a detective standing in a room where a crime had been committed fifty years ago and never reported. Every sound had a second meaning. The tick of the hallway clock wasn't just a clock; it was a metronome counting toward something. The draft under the parlor door wasn't weather; it was breath.

She made coffee she didn't drink and thought about Odile Vasseur.

*Don't sit in the parlor after dark,* the old woman had said through the fence, her face going the color of candle wax at the word *chair*. *And don't ask me about the girl who stayed.*

But that was the thing about people who said *don't ask me* — they were always, always telling you exactly where the answer lived.

* * *

Odile's house sat two doors down, set back from the road behind a hedge of forsythia gone woody and half-dead, the way hedges do when no one has loved them in decades but no one can bear to cut them down either. The paint was the pale green of old medicine bottles. A wind chime hung from the porch eave — small copper fish on lengths of thread — and it made almost no sound at all in the morning air, just a faint dry clicking, like teeth.

Juniper stood at the gate for a long moment, the ledger pages folded into her coat pocket against her ribs, warm as a held bird. She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it three times on the walk over, and all three rehearsals collapsed at the same point: how do you ask a stranger to confirm that your grandmother destroyed someone?

In the end she didn't knock so much as raise her fist and let gravity do the rest.

Footsteps inside, slow and deliberate, the particular cadence of someone who had decided years ago never to hurry for anyone. The door opened on a chain, then fully, and Odile looked out at her with eyes the gray of river stones.

"You found something," Odile said. It wasn't a question.

"Can I come in?"

The old woman considered her for a long time. Somewhere behind her, deep in the house, a radio murmured — a talk station, voices layered over each other like sediment. "You look like her," Odile said finally. "Bram. When she was young. That same way of standing like you're braced for a door to hit you." She unhooked the chain. "Come in before the neighbors see you. They already think I'm strange enough."

* * *

The interior of Odile's house was dim and dense with objects, but unlike Bram's house it didn't feel curated. It felt *kept*, the way a nest is kept — everything arranged by use, by habit, by decades of small adjustments. Doilies under lamps. A wall of photographs going back so far they'd faded to ghosts: sepia families, stiff-collared children, a wedding party squinting into a sun that no longer existed anywhere but in that frame.

There were no photographs of the parlor. Juniper noticed that immediately, the way you notice a missing tooth in a familiar smile. Fifty years of a neighbor's life documented on these walls, and not one image of the room next door.

Odile settled into an armchair by the window and gestured Juniper into the one across from her, positioning them knee to knee like a confession booth. On the table between them sat a teapot under a knitted cozy shaped like a chicken, which under any other circumstances would have been funny.

"I'll make tea," Odile said, and then didn't move. "No. I won't. Because if I get up, I'll find a reason not to tell you, and I've been finding reasons for fifty years, and my knees are tired, and so is the rest of me." Her hands were folded in her lap, knuckles like tree roots. "Ask me."

"Lorraine Dunmore," Juniper said. The name came out of her mouth and seemed to change the pressure in the room. The copper fish clicked once against the window glass, though there was no wind.

Odile closed her eyes. When she opened them again, they were wet, but her voice was level, worn smooth like a stone she'd been turning over for half a century. "You said the name like you know it. So you found the ledger." Juniper must have flinched, because Odile nodded slowly. "I thought you would, eventually. Bram was too careful to leave it where anyone could find it, but careful people die too, and dead people can't be careful anymore."

"She was my grandmother," Juniper said. It came out smaller than she intended. "She raised me summers. She taught me to can tomatoes. She read me *The Wind in the Willows* four times. And there are pages in her own handwriting saying she *paid* for something with—" Her voice snagged. She pushed through. "With a person. With her sister."

"Yes," Odile said.

Just that. No softening, no preamble, no *let me explain what your grandmother really meant*. Just yes, laid on the table between them like a bone.

Juniper gripped the arms of the chair. "Tell me everything."

* * *

What Odile told her took two hours, and she told it in the flat, unhurried voice of a woman who had rehearsed this testimony alone in the dark for so many years that the horror had been sanded off it, leaving only the facts, which were somehow worse.

Lorraine Dunmore had been the younger sister by six years. Bram and Lorraine had grown up in this house — the Hale house, the one Juniper now owned — after their parents died in a collision on Route 9 in 1961. Bram had raised her, more or less. Fed her, clothed her, signed her report cards. "Bram was mother, father, and warden all in one," Odile said. "She loved that girl the way a lighthouse loves a boat. From a distance. With a beam. Never letting her drift, never letting her get close enough to touch."

In the spring of 1974, Bram had started doing the radio show. Everyone thought it was a lark — the Night Shift, midnight to dawn on the little AM station out past the quarry, taking calls from insomniacs and truckers and lonely people. But Odile had known even then that something else was happening. "Bram changed," she said. "She got thin. She got *watchful*. She started keeping odd hours and talking to herself in the parlor, and the parlor—" Odile stopped. Her jaw worked. "The parlor was always thin. Even when we were girls. You didn't go in there. Children know things like that without being told. There was a cold spot by the east wall, and if you stood in it too long, you'd hear your own name said in a voice that wasn't quite yours. We thought it was a game. We thought it was fun. Children are idiots."

That June, Lorraine had turned nineteen. She'd wanted to leave — college, or at least the city, anything — and Bram had refused. Refused to sign the financial aid forms, refused to let her take the car, refused and refused and refused, and Odile had heard them fighting through the hedge more than once, Bram's voice low and iron-hard and Lorraine's rising and cracking. *"You need me here,"* Lorraine had shouted, one night in late June, loud enough to carry over the forsythia. *"Why do you need me here?"*

And Bram had answered, and Odile had never forgotten the words because they had made no sense at the time and perfect sense ever since:

*"Because the chair is empty."*

On the third of July, 1974, Lorraine Dunmore walked out of the house and was never seen again. The sheriff's office called it a runaway. Nineteen years old, restless, a fight with her guardian — open and shut. There was a search, briefly. There was a newspaper item, briefly. Then nothing, because runaways who stay run aren't news, and nineteen-year-old girls leave all the time.

"But she didn't take anything," Odile said. "Not her coat, and it was a cold July that year, if you can believe it. Not her purse. Not the photograph of her parents she kept under her pillow since she was eight. Girls who run away take their purses, Mrs.— Juniper. Girls who run away plan to arrive somewhere." She looked down at her folded hands. "Bram never spoke her name again. Not once, not in fifty years. I tried, early on. I said, *Bram, do you think Lorrie's all right?* — we all called her Lorrie — and Bram looked at me the way you'd look at someone who'd brought a snake into your kitchen, and she said, *We don't discuss her.* Not *her disappearance*. Not *the past*. *Her.* Like the name itself was a door you didn't open."

"And you believed the runaway story?" Juniper asked. "All this time?"

Odile was quiet for a while. Outside, a car passed, tires hissing on asphalt, and the ordinary sound of it felt obscene.

"I believed what I could live with," she said at last. "For about ten years, I believed it. And then one night in — oh, '84, '85 — I woke at two in the morning, which I never did, and I heard music coming from the Hale house. Singing. A woman's voice, very faint, singing a lullaby I knew from church when we were girls. And I got up and I looked out my window, and the light in the parlor was on. Just the parlor. And there was a shape in the window. Sitting. Very still." Her voice didn't change at all, which was somehow the most frightening thing yet. "I told myself it was Bram. Bram sitting up at night in her own parlor, listening to the radio. Of course it was Bram. What else would it be?"

"What else indeed," Juniper whispered.

"After that I stopped asking questions, and Bram stopped answering them anyway, and we became the kind of neighbors who wave over the fence for forty years and never once say a true thing." Odile's eyes came up, and they were fierce now, burning through the age. "I failed her, you understand. Lorrie. I was right next door and I chose comfort. I chose not knowing. Don't you dare do the same. Whatever you're thinking of doing — and I can see it in your face, you have her jaw when you're stubborn — you finish it. You don't leave that girl in there another fifty years because it gets hard."

"In there," Juniper repeated. "You said *in there*. You believe the room is real."

"Mrs. Hale—" Odile caught herself, corrected. "Juniper. I have lived beside that house for seventy-one years. I have watched eleven people live in it and every single one of them grew strange in the same direction. I have seen lights in a sealed room. I have heard a dead woman sing a lullaby through a closed window. And last week, when I saw you carrying boxes up those steps, I counted the chairs in your parlor window." She leaned forward, and her grip on Juniper's wrist, when she took it, was shockingly strong. "How many chairs are in that room?"

"One," Juniper said. "One armchair by the fireplace. That's all."

"There were two when Bram died," Odile said. "There were two for fifty years. Two chairs, side by side, and Bram dusted them both and never sat in either one, and everyone thought it was grief." Her hand tightened. "Now there's one. Now the room is missing a piece. Now it's *furnishing itself* for you."

* * *

Juniper walked home under a sky the color of pewter, the ledger pages a hard rectangle against her heart, and the family myth came apart in her hands as she walked, quietly, completely, the way wet paper comes apart.

There had been a myth. She could see its whole architecture now, elegant and load-bearing. Bram Hale, the eccentric, beloved broadcaster who talked to lonely strangers at night. Bram Hale, who never married, who never spoke of family, whose griefs were private and dignified. Bram Hale, who left everything to the one grandchild who still visited, because Juniper was the favorite, because Juniper was chosen.

Chosen. The word turned over in her mind and showed its underside. Not the favorite. The *heir*. The next keeper of whatever debt ran beneath that house like a buried river. Bram hadn't left her a house. She'd left her a position.

And Lorraine — Lorrie, who sang, who wanted the city, who kept a photograph under her pillow — Lorraine hadn't run away at nineteen. She had been *spent*. Handed over in a parlor that was thin, in a chair that was empty, by a sister who signed the transaction in beautiful handwriting and then spent fifty years documenting it, praying over it, paying interest on it, and never once saying her name.

Juniper stopped at the gate. The house looked back at her, windows dark in the gray morning, and for the first time she saw it clearly: not a home, not an inheritance, but a machine, patient and well-maintained, with one part currently missing.

Her phone buzzed in her pocket. She almost ignored it — she had been ignoring most things for days — but the number was Priya's, and Priya had been cataloguing the tapes with Marcus all week, and something in Juniper had learned, without deciding to, that these two were hers now. Found, not born into. Chosen back.

"We finished the 1974 reels," Priya said, no hello, her voice tight and fast. "Juniper, there's a call on the last one. July third, 1974, 2:14 a.m., the final night of the show. A caller who doesn't give a name. She's crying, and she says she's somewhere with wallpaper that breathes, and she says—" Priya's voice broke, steadied. "She says, *tell my sister the chair doesn't have to stay full.*"

Juniper stood at the gate with the phone pressed to her ear, and above her, in the upstairs window of the parlor, the curtain moved — though every window in the house had been shut since she arrived.

Three days, she thought. She didn't know yet how she knew, but she knew: the anniversary was coming, and the room was expecting payment.

She opened the gate.
