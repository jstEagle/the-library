## 1. The Song Sung Once

The bus let Emrys off where the road gave up on itself, a gravel shoulder dissolving into a cart track, and for the first mile into Juniper's Rest he heard nothing but his own boots and the tick of his recorder cooling in its case. That was how he would remember it afterward — the silence first, and then the village arriving around it like water filling a bowl.

He had been chasing songs for eleven years. Laments that survived their languages, lullabies older than the words inside them, work chants that had outlived the work. He had learned to hear a song's age the way a forester reads rings: in the worn places, the repetitions gone smooth as river stone. The letter from Juniper's Rest — unsigned, postmarked from a town forty miles away — had promised him something he'd never been permitted to record: a harvest song sung once a year, by every voice in the village at once, and never again until the following autumn.

*Never twice,* the letter said. *We ask that you respect this.*

He respected it the way scholars respect all prohibitions: by wanting to know what it was protecting.

The hollow opened before him without warning, the track dipping between two shoulders of wooded hill, and there it was — two dozen houses with slate roofs furred green, a church whose tower leaned like a man listening, and fields of barley already cut to stubble, gold going gray at the edges. Smoke stood straight up from the chimneys. No wind reached the bottom of the hollow; the hills held it out.

A woman was drawing water at the well when he came down the lane, and she looked at him the way people look at weather they've been told to expect.

"You'll be the recordist," she said. Not a question.

"Emrys Oyelaran. I wrote ahead—"

"We know why you're here." She set down her yoke with care, as if the buckets were sleeping things. "Maren says you can stay at the mill house. There's bread on the step if it hasn't been taken." She hoisted the yoke again and paused, and her eyes went past him, up the hillside, to where the trees stood dark against the sky. "You'll want to be at the standing stone tonight. But you'll want to be far from it, too. Both are true."

"How far is far?"

She considered him. She had a face like a closed door, though not an unkind one — more like a door someone had decided, long ago, to stop opening.

"Further than your microphone reaches," she said, and walked on toward the church.

* * *

They gathered at dusk, and they did not gather the way harvest crowds gather anywhere else he'd been. There were no lanterns strung in the barley stubble, no trestle tables, no cider. Perhaps sixty people walked up the hill in loose procession, oldest at the front, children carried or led by the hand, and no one spoke above a murmur. Emrys followed at a distance with his bag over his shoulder, feeling like a thief who has been invited to watch the robbery.

The standing stone stood alone near the crest of the western hill, taller than a man, its face worn featureless except for long vertical grooves, as if rain had been falling on one side of it for a thousand years while the other stayed dry. Around it the villagers arranged themselves in a rough ring, facing inward, and Emrys found a fold of ground thirty yards off, behind a hawthorn, and knelt there with his recorder cradled like something newborn.

An old woman stepped to the stone. He would learn her name later — Odile — but that night she was only a straight-backed silhouette against the last of the light, hair white as ash, hands folded. She did not raise them. She did not conduct. She simply began, low, almost conversational, and the ring of voices rose to meet hers like water finding its level.

Emrys had recorded choirs on four continents. He knew what voices did when they joined — how they fought for the interval, settled, swelled. This was different. Sixty voices moved as one voice, not because they'd rehearsed but because, he thought, they could not do otherwise. The melody descended where melodies rise. It circled a small cluster of notes the way a hand circles a wound, never quite touching it, and the words — he caught fragments only, the dialect thickened by generations of singing it half-understood — were about the cutting of grain, the filling of the storehouse, the closing of doors.

*Closing of doors.* He noted it and moved on. Harvest songs everywhere sang of closing things: barns, coffers, the year itself.

Then the verse turned, and the whole ring of voices dropped at once to something below speech, below whisper, a register he felt in his sternum rather than heard, and every hair on his arms lifted. It lasted perhaps ten seconds. In those ten seconds the sound stopped being sixty people and became one thing wearing sixty mouths, and Emrys understood, with the cold clarity of the professional mind doing its work, that whatever this song was for, the villagers were not performing it. They were maintaining it. The way you maintain a wall. The way you keep a fire banked so it does not go looking for wood.

It ended as simply as it began. Odile's voice settled to silence, the ring followed, and then—

And then they scattered.

Not dispersed, not mingling, not walking home in twos and threes the way any congregation does. They scattered, quickly, silently, each person peeling away from the circle at a different angle like iron filings released from a magnet, and within two minutes the hilltop held nothing but the stone, the darkening sky, and Emrys kneeling in the grass with his heart going hard against his ribs.

He stayed another hour. Nothing else happened. The moon came up gibbous and yellow over the eastern ridge, and the stone threw a shadow that seemed, in that light, to point down the hill rather than away from it.

* * *

Back in the mill house, he played the tape back through headphones because the village had no electricity worth trusting and his battery rig was precious. The recording was good — better than good. Every voice distinct and blended, the acoustics of the hollow folding the sound back on itself. He made his notes in the margin of his field book: *descending contour, unusual; cadence unresolved — deliberately?; final register sub-phonational, cf. throat-singing traditions, but untrained performers...*

Then he heard the second voice.

At first he took it for tape hiss, a flaw in the machine. But it moved. It sat beneath the choir the way a stone sits beneath moss — present, supporting, wrong. It was a single voice, deep, unhurried, and it was singing the same melody a half-beat behind the villagers, the way a child echoes a lesson. Except the timbre was not a child's. It was dry, and vast, and it sounded — he searched for the word and disliked every candidate — *buried*. As if the voice were coming up through soil.

He rewound. Played it again. The voice was still there, patient under the sixty, keeping its terrible time.

He checked the machine. He checked the tape for splices. He walked outside into the cold blue night and stood in the lane listening to real silence, and told himself what folklorists have always told themselves: that documentation is harmless. That a recording changes nothing. That to write a song down, to fix it in wax or oxide, is not to wake it but to honor it — is, in fact, the only prayer scholarship knows how to say.

He believed it, mostly. Belief is a door you can hold shut with your shoulder, if you're strong enough, if you don't look too closely at what's pressing on the other side.

When he came back in, he did not play the tape again. But the mill house was very quiet, and quiet, he was learning, was not the same as empty. Somewhere below the floor — below the millstone, below the foundations, below, he thought, any depth a mill house had any business having — something hummed four descending notes, softly, and stopped.

Emrys lay down in his clothes with the recorder on the chair beside him, its red light off, its reels still.

In the morning, he would learn that the excavation crew had arrived on the far hill, and that Maren's men had struck iron in the barrow they'd been clearing, and that the door they found there was bolted from the inside.

But that night he only listened to the humming fade, and slept, and dreamed of a melody he could not quite place, sung in a voice that was patiently learning his own.
