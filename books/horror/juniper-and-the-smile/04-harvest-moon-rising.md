## 4. Harvest Moon Rising

The moon came up the color of a peeled egg, and Emrys stood at the edge of the barley field with Maren's hand gripping his sleeve like a tourniquet.

He could no longer remember why he had come to Juniper's Rest. That was the first line of the verse, and he had paid for it already: *the reason, the road, the name of the town that raised you.* Somewhere behind his eyes there was a city with trams and rain and a mother's kitchen — but when he reached for it, he touched only smooth stone. He knew he had lost things. Knowing was all he had left of them.

"It's opening," Maren said.

The barrow hill stood black against the harvest moon, and down its flank the iron door — bolted from the inside for two hundred years by hands they had never found — swung outward with a sound like a held breath released. No light came out. What came out was worse: a voice.

It sang in Emrys's own recording. His tape machine's hiss, his footfall on gravel, the cough he'd made at the second verse — all of it woven through a melody that climbed the hillside note by note, patient as water rising in a cellar. The thing below wore his fieldwork like a skin. It had learned the song from him, and now it was coming up the dark to return it.

The villagers had gathered without being called, silent along the fence lines, their faces turned toward the hill. Not one of them opened their mouth. This was not their night. The song renewed each year needed only one voice tonight, and every eye among them found Emrys as if they'd always known it would.

"Odile said—" Maren began.

"I remember what she said." He did; it was nearly the last thing he owned. *Sing it alone. Sing it until there is nothing left of you to sing with. The door wants a price, and the price is a self.*

The thing crested the hill.

It moved wrong — too tall, unfolding as it descended, wearing the shape of a person the way a coat is worn by something that has never had shoulders. And it sang with the buried voice, the faint wrong voice beneath the choir on his tape, except now it was not faint. It filled the valley. It sang his recording back at him, complete with the wind-gust on verse three, and where the final verse should have been, it left a hole — a silence shaped exactly like the words Odile had taught him, waiting to be filled.

Emrys stepped forward into the barley stubble.

"Don't," Maren whispered. "We could run. The others—"

"The others ran once." His voice sounded strange to him, stripped of provenance. "Two hundred years ago someone sealed that door from the inside, and the village has paid the interest ever since. I'm settling the account."

He began to sing.

The first line cost him his mother's face — what remained of it. He felt it go like warmth leaving a cup, her features dissolving into a kindness without features, a love without a body. He sang anyway, louder, because the thing on the hill had stopped and tilted its head, listening, hungry for exactly this.

The second line took the city. Trams, rain, the smell of wet asphalt, a river he'd loved — gone, gone, a blank where a skyline had been. He staggered. Maren caught his elbow and he shook her off gently, because the third line required both lungs and nothing held back.

The third line took his name.

*Emrys* went out of him like a lamp pinched between wet fingers. He kept singing because the melody remembered itself even when he did not; the song lived in his throat now independent of its owner, and that was the whole terrible design of it. A song needs a singer. The door did not care which.

The thing on the hill had begun to sink, folding down into itself, the borrowed recording fraying at the edges as his living voice overwrote it. Its mouth — when it had one — was still open on the empty space where the last verse lived.

So he gave it the last verse.

Line by line he unlearned himself. The university. The reason folklore mattered. The face of the woman he'd almost married, whose name he surrendered mid-syllable so that it became only a shape of tenderness, orphaned, belonging to no one. The color of his childhood bedroom. Whether he had been happy as a boy. Each line a coin off his tongue, each coin swallowed by the dark under the hill, and the iron door drawing shut by degrees with every payment, groaning home along its ancient track.

On the final line he had almost nothing left. He was a warm throat and a melody and a man-shaped absence standing in dead barley under an enormous moon. He searched himself for anything worth keeping and found one small stubborn coal: the memory of this valley, these fields, these faces along the fence — the only home he had left, because it was the only one remaining.

He sang the coal into the last line too. Willingly. Because belonging, he understood now, was the one thing the door could not take — it could only be given.

The door closed on silence.

Not echo, not aftermath. Silence, total and clean, the way snow silences a field. The thing beneath the hill had his recording no longer; it had nothing but the verse, and the verse was spent, and spent verses do not repeat.

* *

Maren walked him back along the lane as the villagers emerged from the fences, and here was the miracle he would never be able to explain to anyone outside the hollow: they smiled at him. They knew him. Hands on his shoulders, bread pressed into his palms, a child taking his finger in her fist as if he'd always been there.

"You'll stay at the mill house," Odile said, ancient and dry-eyed, squeezing his arm. "It's got your chair in it already."

"But I've only just—"

"You've always been here," she said, and the strange thing was that the words settled into him like truth, like a key into a lock. He could not remember otherwise. There was no otherwise to remember.

They fed him at long tables under the waning moon. Someone played a fiddle. A woman with flour on her hands laughed at something he said and he realized, with a fullness in his chest he had no history to explain, that he was happy — happy the way stones are settled, the way walls are load-bearing. He belonged here. Everyone agreed. Even the part of him that might have objected had been sung away, and what remained fit the hollow of Juniper's Rest perfectly, the way water fits a cup.

Later, walking Odile home, he found himself humming.

She stopped on the bridge and looked at him sharply. "Careful."

"It's only the tune," he said, surprised. "I don't know any words. Isn't that odd? I feel like I should know the words."

Odile studied him a long moment — the last willing singer, looking at the newest keeper — and whatever she saw, she must have judged it safe, because she took his arm again and they walked on.

"You'll learn them if you ever need to," she said. "That's how it works. The song keeps its singers."

He hummed as they crossed the bridge, the melody rising sweet and wordless over the water, and far below the hill, behind the iron door, something that had once worn his voice lay listening — patient, learning, keeping time.

Waiting, as songs do, for the next voice that would need to sing it twice.
