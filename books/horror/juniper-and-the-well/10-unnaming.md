## 10. Unnaming

The first morning of the protocol, Odile burned the porridge.

Not because she was distracted—she was never distracted; twenty-two years of nursing had trained distraction out of her like a bad habit—but because Mr. Fenn stood in her kitchen at six a.m. and told her that from this day forward, no one in the house would speak anyone's name aloud.

"You want me to feed four children breakfast without calling them to it," she said. She kept her voice level, the way she'd once kept it while a doctor explained that the medication error had been *systemic*, actually, and wasn't it interesting how blame moved through hospitals like water finding cracks.

"I want you to keep them off the count," Fenn said. He turned his teacup a quarter-turn on its saucer, then another quarter, as if calibrating something. "The well takes what's named. Names are handles. We take the handles off."

"And how do I call Tam down for school? Whistle?"

"Tap on his door. Point. Children are adaptable."

"Children," Odile said, "are not adaptable. Children are resilient, which is worse. They bend and they remember the shape."

Fenn looked at her over his spectacles with the mild patience of a man who had survived something she hadn't yet seen, and she hated him a little for it, and loved him a little too, which was the whole trouble with old men who repaired clocks: they made you feel that time itself was on their side of the argument.

She burned the second pot of porridge anyway. Call it protest.

* * *

By Wednesday, the house had developed a language of gestures so elaborate that Odile privately thought of it as their private sign system, though she'd have died before saying so out loud, since saying anything out loud had become suspect.

Lior tapped twice on the table for *sit*. A flat palm for *quiet*. Two fingers walked across the tabletop for *go upstairs now, don't ask*. Tam, who was eight and took to silence the way other children took to puddles, invented a flick of the chin that meant *I heard something again* and used it roughly forty times a day, most of which turned out to be the pipes, or the wind, or Mr. Fenn's clock workshop doing whatever clock workshops did at night—though after the third night of footsteps overhead when everyone was downstairs, nobody joked about the pipes anymore.

Sef did not invent gestures. Sef sat at the far end of the table with her arms crossed and her headphones on—not playing anything, Odile noticed, just wearing them like armor—and communicated exclusively in eye-rolls of escalating sophistication. At fifteen, Sef had perfected an eye-roll that could convey *this is stupid*, *you are stupid*, *the entire concept of household safety is stupid*, and *I am hungry*, sometimes all four at once.

Odile covered the mirrors on Monday. Every one: the hall glass, the bathroom mirror, the little round one in Lior's study that Lior had been testing with a metronome all week, click-click-click, watching their reflection raise its hand one full second late, like a student who knew the answer but couldn't quite get there in time.

Sef refused to cover hers.

"It's my mirror," she said, when Odile came at it with a pillowcase. "It's my face."

"The rule is no reflective surfaces uncovered."

"The rule is *stupid*." She said it loudly, deliberately, and Odile watched her glance toward the stairs—the cellar hatch at the end of the hall—as if checking whether the word carried. "You can't hear me if I'm quiet, right? That's the theory? The well's got ears now?"

"We don't know what it has," Odile said. "That's rather the point."

"It's a *hole in the ground*, Odile. It's a hole in the ground under our house and we're all tiptoeing around it like it's a sleeping baby." Her voice cracked on the last word, and Odile saw it then, underneath the armor: the fear, big as the girl herself, wearing anger as a coat two sizes too small.

Odile set down the pillowcase. "When you're frightened," she said quietly, "you get loud. I know. I've done it in front of better doctors than you'd believe."

Sef stared at her. For one moment the coat slipped entirely, and Odile saw a child standing in a doorway of some group home years ago, watching a new foster placement decide whether she was staying or going, learning not to hope either way.

Then the armor came back up. "Whatever," Sef said, and put her headphones on, and Odile left the mirror alone, because some battles you lose on purpose to win the war, and some wars you lose on purpose because winning them costs more than the children can pay.

* * *

The hush frayed by Friday.

It frayed the way things do—not all at once but along the seams, in small ugly places. Tam stopped singing. That was the thing Odile noticed first and would remember longest: Tam, who hummed constantly, who narrated his own life in tuneless melody the way other children chewed gum, went silent. Not because anyone had told him to stop singing. Because he'd understood, faster than any of them, that songs were made of names, and names were dangerous, and so he'd folded up the music and put it away somewhere, and the house felt the loss like a missing tooth.

Mr. Fenn grew stranger in his silences. He began leaving notes instead of speaking at all—little slips of paper in his precise clockmaker's hand: *Milk's gone off. —F.* and *Don't let the boy near the hatch. —F.* Once, just once, a note that said only *Nineteen. Watch the nineteen,* with no signature, and when Odile asked him about it he looked at her for a long moment and said, "Did I write that?" and she couldn't tell if he was lying.

And Lior—Lior worked. That was their answer to everything, Odile had learned in five years of sharing a roof with them: when the world became unbearable, Lior became methodical. They filled a notebook with parish records and frost counts and verses of the lullaby transcribed in their careful folklorist's hand, and they spoke less each day, and their eyes got brighter and more exhausted in equal measure, until Odile wanted to grab them by the shoulders and say: *you cannot archive your way out of grief, I've tried, the filing system collapses.*

Instead she made tea and left it beside the notebook without a word, because words were rationed now, and somehow the rationing had made every unspoken thing heavier.

Saturday evening, the seams gave.

* * *

It started, as these things did, with potatoes.

Dinner was stew, eaten in the gesture-language quiet that had become normal, six people making the small sounds of cutlery and chewing and nothing else, and Tam reached for the salt and knocked his glass over, and milk spread across the table in a slow white flood, and Tam's face crumpled—he'd been so *careful* all week, so perfectly silent, and now he'd made noise, real noise, the wet slap of spilled milk, and he looked at the adults with genuine terror, waiting to be counted.

"Oh, love," Odile said—which broke the rule, *love* wasn't a name exactly but it was close enough that Fenn flinched—and she grabbed a cloth, and Tam burst into tears, and Sef pushed back her chair so hard it went over backwards.

"That's *enough*," Sef said. Loudly. Wonderfully, terribly loud. "Look at him. Look at him crying over *milk*. You've all lost your minds. It's a well. It's a dirty old well full of rainwater and probably rats, and we're sitting here eating dinner like hostages, like—if we're just quiet enough, if we just don't say our own *names*—" She laughed, high and wild. "My name is Sef!"

"Sef—" Lior started, rising.

"My name is *Sef*!" she shouted, louder, and she was already moving, out of the kitchen, down the hall, and Odile was moving too, and Fenn was shouting *no, no, girl, not indoors* in a voice cracked clean down the middle, and Sef reached the cellar hatch and dropped to her knees in front of it, both palms flat on the wood, and screamed into the gap beneath the door:

"*Come and take it, then!*"

The house went still.

Not quiet—still. There is a difference, and everyone in that hallway learned it at the same moment. Quiet is absence of sound. Stillness is presence of attention. The floorboards stopped their settling. The pipes stopped their ticking. Even the draft under the door seemed to hold its breath, and Odile had spent twenty-two years around dying people and knew, with the certainty of the professionally familiar, what a room sounded like when something in it was listening.

Then, from below—from below the floor, below the cellar, below the foundations, from somewhere the architecture of the house could not account for—came a sound like a long, slow swallow.

Sef scrambled backward. All the armor gone, all the anger gone, just a girl on a hallway floor with her hands pressed over her mouth, eyes enormous.

Tam picked up the overturned chair and set it gently upright, the way you do when the world needs one small ordered thing. Nobody said anything. Nobody needed to. The rule had been tested, and the test result was lying on the floor in the shape of a fifteen-year-old who suddenly believed.

Odile helped her up. Sef didn't cry—that would come later, privately, into Tam's shoulder, because those two had formed an alliance years ago that ran deeper than blood and deeper than sense—but she leaned into Odile's side, briefly, hard, the way a struck dog leans, and then pulled away and went to her room and shut the door.

In the kitchen, Lior stood at the window with the notebook forgotten in their hands, looking at the glass.

"Odile," they said. Their voice was very even, which from Lior meant the opposite of calm. "Come look at this."

* * *

The frost bloomed across the kitchen window sometime in the night.

Odile woke at three—her body still kept hospital hours, some habits outliving their reasons—and came downstairs for warm milk and found the kitchen silvered with moonlight that shouldn't have been there. July outside. Frost inside, feathering across the pane in branching white ferns, delicate as lace, growing even as she watched.

Third pattern. She counted the branches the way she counted pills, twice, because accuracy mattered and mistakes were paid for by others: one, two, three, four, five, six—

Seven.

No. She counted again. Eight.

Eight branches, fanning out from a single stem at the bottom corner of the glass like a hand pressed against the window from the outside. Six people in the house. Last week there had been seven branches and six people, and they'd told themselves—she had told herself, firmly, in the voice she used on panicking relatives—that the seventh meant nothing, was miscounted, was a trick of the light.

Now there were eight. And the eighth branch was longer than the others, reaching up toward the top of the pane, still growing, its tip curling like a finger beckoning.

Behind her, the floor creaked. Lior stood in the doorway in their dressing gown, notebook already open, pen already moving—they'd come down the moment the cold reached the bedrooms, Odile realized; they'd been counting too.

"Eight," Lior said.

"I know."

"Last week seven. This week eight." Lior's pen didn't waver. Only their voice did, slightly, at the edges. "It's counting ahead. Or it's counting things that aren't people yet." They looked up from the page, and in the frost-light their face looked older, carved. "Or Sef's shout added something. Named something. Opened an account."

From above, faintly, came the sound of Tam's bed frame shifting—a child turning over in sleep, ordinary as bread—and then, beneath it, softer, from the direction of the dry bathroom, the unmistakable sound of water running where no water should be.

Odile poured two cups of the warm milk she'd come down for, and handed one to Lior, and they stood together in the frozen kitchen drinking it in silence, because silence was the protocol, silence was the shield, silence was the whole desperate strategy—and because neither of them could think of a single true thing left worth saying quietly.
