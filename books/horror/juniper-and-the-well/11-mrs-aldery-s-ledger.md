## 11. Mrs. Aldery's Ledger

The village archive smelled of beeswax and old paper and, faintly, of the lavender sachets Mrs. Aldery tucked between the parish registers like little purple sentries. Lior had been coming here for twenty years—first as a nervous postgraduate with a dictaphone held together by tape, later as a fixture, someone whose tea appeared without asking. In all that time they had never once considered breaking in.

Now they stood outside it at half past midnight with a coat hanger bent into a shape that would have embarrassed every locksmith who ever lived, feeling like a burglar in a pantomime.

"You're doing it wrong," Tam whispered from the shadows by the drainpipe.

"I'm doing it *delicately*."

"You're doing it like you're trying to fish the lock out through the keyhole."

"Go home, Tam."

"No." He said it simply, the way he said most true things. He was fourteen and built like a heron and he had appointed himself lookout with the solemnity of a boy who had read too many books about spies. "If you get arrested I have to be able to tell Odile exactly what happened so she can decide how angry to be."

"That's not how being a family works."

"It's absolutely how our family works."

Lior couldn't argue. Their household ran on a constitution of negotiated chaos: Sef's curfew was theoretical, Mr. Fenn's clock parts colonized every horizontal surface, and Odile's authority rested entirely on the fact that she could make rice pudding that silenced rebellion. They were not a system with rules. They were a raft with opinions.

The lock, insultingly, gave up on the third try. The hanger wasn't even the trick; the trick was that Hollowmere had never needed real security. Nobody stole from the archive because nobody wanted what was in it, and the one person who did want what was in it had spent two decades being *granted* access, politely, cup by cup of tea, and had never once thought to ask why the access came pre-filtered.

That was the thought that had kept Lior awake for three nights running. Not the frost on the windows in July. Not the eight branches where there should have been six. The *tea*. Twenty years of tea, always poured by Aldery herself, always served in the reading room, never once had Lior been left alone with the back shelves.

Invisibility, they had written in their own notebook, in the margin next to the chalk tally mark they'd copied off their gatepost, is a service provided. Someone is providing it. Which means someone can withdraw it.

Inside, the moon through the tall window laid pale stripes across the floor. Lior knew the room by heart—the reading table, the map chest, the shelf of tithe records nobody had opened since the war—and walked straight past all of it to the back wall, to the shelves Aldery's body had always angled toward whenever Lior drifted near.

Tam clicked on his torch, cupped his hand around it. "What are we looking for?"

"A ledger. Something that isn't in the catalogue." Lior ran their fingers along the spines. "Aldery catalogues everything. It's her religion. So whatever she keeps off-catalogue is whatever she thinks is hers alone."

"Like when Sef hides her phone inside the hollowed-out dictionary?"

"Exactly like that, except Sef's dictionary doesn't decide who lives."

They found it behind a false row of vestry minutes—a slim book bound in green leather gone soft with handling, no title, its spine unbroken by any stamp. It fit in one hand. It weighed like a stone dropped down a well.

Lior carried it to the reading table, set it under the torch, and did not open it for a full minute, because some part of them already knew, the way you know the shape of a room behind a door before you turn the handle.

"Read it out loud," Tam said. "I don't want to just watch your face do things."

So Lior opened it, and read.

* * *

The handwriting changed across the pages the way handwriting does across generations—flourished copperplate giving way to a cramped Victorian slant, then a schoolroom roundness, then, in the last third, a small precise script Lior recognized instantly, because it was the same hand that had addressed twenty years of Christmas cards to a house at the edge of the moor.

But the format never changed. That was the horror of it. The format was *stable*. Two hundred years of stable.

Each entry had a date. A description, brief, almost clerical. And a word in the margin, repeated over and over down the decades like a refrain:

*Satisfied.*

Lior read the first page aloud, voice steady because they made it steady, because Tam was watching.

*"Michaelmas 1803. A tinker, name unknown, taken at the crossroads. Fed at dusk. Well satisfied. Frost count restored to seven."*

"Fed," Tam repeated. "Fed at dusk. Like—it says it like feeding a fire."

"It says it like feeding a fire because that's what it is." Lior turned the page. The entries came faster now, clustered in certain years, and Lior's folklorist brain, that traitorous efficient machine, began sorting them automatically. Famine years. The hard winter of 1841. The year of the fever. *"A vagrant man, drowned, given whole." "A child, unwanted, the Hartley girl's, no name given, no name asked." "Two Irishmen passing through, lodged one night at the Bell, gone by morning, well satisfied."*

Gone by morning. Lodged one night. Lior had recorded songs about men like these—ballads where the traveler never reached the next town, where the innkeeper's wife wept for reasons the verse never explained. For twenty years they'd filed those songs under *motif: the vanishing guest*, a type-scene found across three counties, charming, archaic, harmless.

Harmless. God. They had put a little smiley face in the margin of one of them, in 2009, because the tune was pretty.

"There's a section by years," Tam said. He was pointing, not touching. He'd learned early in this house not to touch other people's paper. "Look—there's gaps. Big ones. Then a bunch all at once."

"Nineteen years," Lior said. Their mouth had gone dry. "Look at the clusters. Count between them."

Tam counted under his breath, finger hovering above the dates without landing. "Nineteen. Nineteen again. Nineteen." He looked up. "It's the frost thing. The lullaby. *Count the ferns upon the glass, count them slow, count them fast.*"

"The well gets hungry on a schedule." Lior heard their own voice go flat and academic, the tone they used for lectures, the tone that was actually a lifeboat. "And the village fed it. On time. For two hundred years. Vagrants, travelers, people nobody would—" They stopped. Started again. "People nobody would come looking for. That's the whole mechanism. That's the entire law of this place. The founding families didn't build a church and a court. They built a *feeding schedule*."

The torch trembled slightly in Tam's grip. He steadied it against the table edge. "Keep reading," he said, which was brave, and Lior loved him for it, and hated that love was happening in a room like this, at a table like this, over a book like this.

* * *

They worked through the ledger the way you work through anything unbearable: in sections, with pauses, Tam timing Lior's silences and nudging them onward. Somewhere past midnight Lior stopped reading aloud and started just turning pages, and Tam let them, because he understood that some things you need to take in silently first.

The entries got shorter as the centuries went. The village got better at it. By the twentieth century the language was almost bureaucratic: *"One male, transient. No inquiry followed." "Female, itinerant laborer. Satisfied."* The word *offering* appeared, then stopped appearing, replaced by nothing at all—the entries simply became lists of descriptions, the way you'd list stock.

Then Lior turned a page and their own surname looked up at them.

*Oyelaran.*

Not in the left-hand column. Not among the fed, the given, the satisfied. In the right-hand column, where the ledger recorded something else entirely—a second register Lior hadn't noticed until now, sparse, a name every generation or two, written larger, underlined.

*1948. Keeper: E. Oyelaran. Received the office from her mother. Sound judgment. No irregularities.*

Lior sat very still.

"Elsie," they said finally. "Elsie Oyelaran. My grandmother." The woman who had sung them the lullaby. The woman whose notebook waited upstairs in Lior's attic with its marginalia—*the well counts by frost, it takes the unnamed first*—written in a hand that had known, that had *managed*, that had chosen which strangers' names went into the left-hand column.

"She was a keeper," Tam said slowly, working it out. "Like—like a job?"

"Like an office. Like a *title*." Lior's laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "She used to tell me the lullaby was just a counting song. She taught me to collect the other songs, the safe ones. She steered me, Tam. Twenty years before I was born she was steering me. She made sure I'd be the kind of person who loved folklore but never asked about *this* one twice." They pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes. "She sang me to sleep with the inventory of a slaughterhouse and I thought it was sweet."

Tam said nothing for a moment. When he spoke, it was careful, the way he was careful with Sef after her bad nights. "Maybe she was trying to keep you away from it."

"Maybe." Lior lowered their hand. "Or maybe she was training her replacement. Keepers pass by blood. If it passes by blood, then there has to *be* blood to pass to. She needed me ignorant enough to stay, curious enough to stay useful—" They stopped, because the sentence ahead of that one was a cliff, and they weren't ready to go over it yet.

The next keeper entry was worse. Of course it was. There was a whole line of them, Oyelaran after Oyelaran going back past Elsie, past the copperplate, back nearly to the beginning of the book, and the earliest entry—the first right-hand column entry, in the oldest ink—read:

*Keeper: the founder's daughter. Chosen because she wept for the first one and could not be comforted. Office created so that the weeping would be orderly.*

Lior read it four times. *Chosen because she wept.* Two hundred years ago, somebody's daughter had cried for a murdered stranger, and instead of the crying changing anything, the village had built a job around it. Had made grief itself into the instrument. Handed the weeping girl the pen.

"They didn't just feed people to it," Lior said. "They domesticated the conscience. They took the one person who objected and made her responsible for doing it properly." The nausea arrived then, right on schedule, hot and climbing, and Lior gripped the table edge and breathed through it while Tam hovered, torch shaking, whispering *are you okay, are you okay* until Lior waved him quiet.

Because here was the thing, the thing that would not wash out: Lior understood her. Understood the founder's daughter, understood Elsie, understood the whole grim inheritance. Understood it from the inside, sitting here in the dark with a stolen book, because hadn't Lior done the same arithmetic for twenty years? Kept the strays. Kept the silence. Never asked the questions that might bring the village's attention down on the house full of people they loved. Had called it discretion. Had called it keeping everyone safe.

It had been granted to them, all of it—the house, the peace, the invisibility. Granted, the way you grant a dog the run of the yard. And the fence had always been there.

"Lior." Tam's voice, very small. "There's more pages. At the back. Loose ones."

* * *

The loose pages were newer, unbound, tucked into a pocket inside the back cover. Notes in Aldery's precise hand—drafts, revisions, a keeper rehearsing her own record. And one page that stopped Lior's heart with the gentleness of it, which was somehow the worst cruelty yet: a page headed, in Aldery's writing, *For whoever comes after me,* followed by instructions. Dutiful. Thorough. Almost tender. How to choose. How to keep the count. How to spot the hungry years early. How to manage a folklorist—"let them sing everything except the ninth verse; curiosity is a leash if you hold the end of it."

At the bottom, added recently, ink still confident though the letters wavered with age:

*The last Oyelaran will inherit whether they wish it or not. Blood is the only qualification the well recognizes. Prepare them kindly. Kindness costs nothing and buys compliance.*

Lior closed the ledger.

The moon had moved. The stripes on the floor lay differently now, and somewhere out in the village a dog was barking at nothing, or at something, and Tam was looking at them with an expression Lior recognized from mirrors—the look of a kid recalibrating everything he believed about an adult he trusted.

"So," Tam said. "Mrs. Aldery's been managing you. This whole time. The tea, and the—she's been *keeping* you. Like a—"

"Like a keeper keeps," Lior said. "Yes."

"And tomorrow you're going to go ask her about it."

Lior looked at the green book, soft as an old glove, heavy as a headstone. They thought about walking out of here, driving to the coast, burning it, never singing verse nine. They thought about the frost ferns blooming on the kitchen glass in July, eight branches, six souls, and about Sef shouting her own name at the cellar hatch like a dare, and about a founder's daughter weeping two hundred years ago because nobody would let her grief mean anything.

"No," Lior said, and picked up the ledger, and felt it settle into their arms with the horrible rightness of an heirloom. "Tomorrow I'm not going to ask her anything. Tomorrow she's going to find the ledger gone, and she's going to know exactly who took it, and she's going to come to *me*—because that's how keepers work, Tam. We're about to find out what my grandmother's friend does when the leash runs the other way."

Tam clicked off the torch. In the dark, his voice: "Is that a plan or a threat?"

"It's an inheritance," Lior said. "Apparently those run in my family too."
