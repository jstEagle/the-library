## 2. Doors Counted Twice

The ritual had a name, though Cyrus never said it aloud. He called it *the circuit*, and he ran it every night before sleep: fourteen doors in the house above ground, seven locks on the exterior, three windows that actually opened. Fourteen, seven, three. The numbers were old friends. They had never once lied to him.

He started the circuit at the top of the stairs, as always, left hand trailing the banister's worn groove—his grandfather's groove, polished by forty years of the same palm. Bedroom door, closed. Bathroom door, closed. Linen closet, closed. He touched each one with two fingers, a tap light enough to leave no mark but firm enough to mean something. Down the hall. Spare room. His grandfather's room, still sealed since the funeral, still smelling faintly of pipe tobacco and camphor.

Fourteen.

He was halfway down the staircase when the number snagged in his mind like a fishhook.

Fourteen doors. He'd counted fourteen. But he had counted them going up this morning too, and this morning there had been thirteen, because the door to the cellar coal chute—the little painted-over one under the main stair—had been missing. Not locked. Not closed. *Missing*, as in absent from the wall, as in drywall where a door should be, and Cyrus had stood in front of it for four minutes with his phone flashlight, telling himself he'd misremembered which wall it lived in.

Now it was back. Painted green, hinge-side to the left, exactly as it had always been.

Cyrus sat down on the seventh step and did not move for a long time. The house ticked around him, settling heat into cooling joists, the ordinary language of an old building at night. He made himself breathe. He made himself run the count again, out loud this time, because saying numbers aloud made them real in a way thinking them didn't.

"Bedroom. Bathroom. Linen. Spare. Grandfather's. Hall closet. Hall closet—" He stopped.

There were two hall closets.

There had only ever been one.

* * *

He told himself, in the morning, that he had been dreaming on his feet. Sleepwalking-adjacent. A fugue of exhaustion; he'd been mapping tunnels until 3 a.m., after all, descending into the dark with a laser measure and a headlamp like some kind of professional mole, and brains did strange things when you fed them that much darkness. He wrote it all down in the survey log the way he wrote everything down—*anomalous door count, second floor, resolved by morning*—because writing things down was its own ritual, and rituals were how you kept a mind like his from eating itself.

But he started photographing the doors. Timestamped, geotagged, uploaded to cloud storage within seconds of capture. If the house wanted to play games with its architecture, it would have to do so against a documented record.

For six days, the record held. Doors stayed where photos said they were. Locks stayed locked. He almost relaxed.

On the seventh day, the photographs disagreed with each other.

He found it at 2 a.m., scrolling back through the gallery looking for a shot of the kitchen doorway he'd taken for the insurance file. The photo was there. Taken June 3rd, 11:47 p.m. In it, the kitchen door stood open, and beyond it the kitchen was visible—cabinets, the window over the sink, the humming refrigerator.

Except the window over the sink looked out onto black water.

Not night. Water. A flat, drowned darkness pressed against the glass from outside, filling the frame edge to edge, and something about its surface suggested depth rather than height—as if the kitchen window opened not onto the yard but onto the side of a submerged shaft, and the camera had caught the moment the water noticed it was being seen.

Cyrus checked the metadata twice. Then three times. Then he went downstairs and stood in the kitchen and looked at the actual window, which showed the actual yard, moonlit grass, the neighbor's fence, nothing else. He took another photo. Grass. Fence. Moon.

He deleted nothing. Deleting felt dangerous, like tearing a page out of a book that might be reading him back.

* * *

The rearrangement, when he finally caught it happening live, was almost gentle.

He was running the evening circuit, tapping doors, when he reached the linen closet on the second floor and found it ajar—which was wrong, because he'd tapped it shut ninety seconds earlier, and no one else lived in the house, and the hinges didn't drift. He pushed it open with the flat of his hand.

Inside were towels. Towels, and the vacuum, and the box of Christmas lights, and a stack of his grandfather's old magazines. Everything normal. Everything exactly where it should be.

Except the closet was deeper than it had been yesterday. Deeper by a good three feet, its back wall now farther away than the exterior wall of the house could possibly allow. Cyrus measured it with his eyes, then with his arm, then with the tape measure he carried everywhere these days. Five feet, eight inches. The house was twenty-two feet wide. The closet shared a wall with the bathroom. The math didn't just fail—it failed *insultingly*, the way a lie fails when the liar has stopped bothering.

And at the back of the closet, behind the Christmas lights, there was a seam in the plaster. A vertical line, hairline-thin, running floor to ceiling, with a faint rectangular ghost around it—the shadow of something that had once been mounted there, or bricked over there, or hidden.

Cyrus stood in his hallway holding a tape measure and understood, with the calm clarity that sometimes arrives instead of panic, that the house was not malfunctioning.

The house was *offering*.

* * *

He came back with tools. That was who Cyrus Marchetti was, when anything in the world went wrong: he came back with tools. Headlamp, pry bar, dust mask, the reciprocating saw he'd bought for tunnel survey work. He cleared the closet shelf, cut the power to that circuit so he wouldn't find live wiring with the blade, and set the saw's shoe against the seam.

The plaster came away in slabs. Behind it: lath, and behind the lath, brick—not the soft yellow brick of the house's bones but hard, dark, vitrified brick, the kind fired for furnace linings. Foundry brick. His grandfather had built a wall inside a wall, and the space between them was eight inches deep and stuffed with newspaper.

He pulled it out in handfuls. The paper was brittle as autumn leaves, browned to the color of weak tea, but the interior sheets had been folded against each other and the print survived. The *Evening Sentinel*, March 1974. And folded inside the outer pages, protected like a relic, a single clipping with its headline facing outward:

**SIX STILL MISSING IN FOUNDRY SHAFT COLLAPSE; SEARCH SUSPENDED**

Cyrus lowered himself to sit cross-legged on the closet floor, dust sifting off his shoulders, and read.

The story was short. The city's oldest bell foundry—a business older than the street it stood on—had suffered a catastrophic collapse of its casting shaft, which had flooded, the article said, "in circumstances the company declines to describe." Six workers had been below when the shaft gave way. Recovery efforts had been suspended after nine days. The names were listed in a single grim column, small type, as though even the newspaper had been asked to keep it brief:

*Vittorio Sartain. Emil Brant. Tomas Reyes. Daniel Okafor. Peter Lunn. Augustin Vey.*

Daniel Okafor. Cyrus mouthed the name without meaning to. Ruth Okafor next door had a father who'd gone missing before she was born—he knew this the way you know things about neighbors, half-heard across fences and decades. He filed the thought away and kept reading.

There wasn't much more. A quote from the foundry owner calling the men "lost to structural failure." A line about the shaft being sealed. And then, in the final paragraph, a sentence that made the hairs on Cyrus's arms rise through the dust:

*"A bronze bell cast in the foundry's final commission was reportedly removed from the flooded shaft by unknown parties prior to the sealing. Its whereabouts are not known."*

Unknown parties. Cyrus looked at the dark brick surrounding the cavity, at the careful double wall his grandfather had mortared into place, and thought: *you knew exactly where it was. You put it there.*

The clipping trembled slightly in his hand. He told himself it was the cold draft moving through the open wall—and then he realized there was no draft. The air in the cavity was perfectly, unnaturally still, the way air is still underwater.

Behind him, in the hallway, a door clicked softly shut.

He turned. Every door on the second floor stood closed. He had left them all open. He had left them all open because he was mid-circuit, and the circuit required—

Fourteen, seven, three.

He counted anyway, voice thin in the empty hall. "One. Two. Three..."

At eleven, he stopped. Because the door to his grandfather's room—the room that had been sealed since the funeral, the room whose handle he had not touched in eight months—was standing open six inches, and from inside it came a sound he recognized from the tunnels below, from the flooded corridors where his flashlight beam died too fast:

The slow, patient drip of water falling a very long distance.

And beneath it, almost too quiet to catch, something else. A rhythm. Metallic, deliberate, unhurried.

Someone—or something—was ringing a small bell, one note at a time, in the dark of his grandfather's room.
