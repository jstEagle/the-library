## 4. Frost on the Inside

The heat wave broke over the city on a Tuesday and never really landed on the house.

Cyrus noticed it first as a wrongness in the air when he came up from the tunnels at dawn—July pressing against the windows from outside, and something cooler, patient, breathing back from inside the glass. He stood in the kitchen in his boots still crusted with tunnel clay and watched his breath fog in a room where the thermostat read eighty-one degrees.

The frost came that afternoon.

It started on the bathroom mirror, the same one that had lagged behind him, and spread the way ice spreads on a pond in time-lapse: a bloom of white feathering out from the center, delicate as lungs, until the whole pane was cased in crystal. Except it wasn't cold to the touch. He pressed his palm flat against it and felt only glass, only the warm July room behind him, while the surface wore winter like a mask.

He told himself it was condensation. He told himself a lot of things that week.

By Thursday, every mirror in the house had bloomed—the hallway cheval glass his grandfather had never let anyone move, the little round mirror in the entryway, the vanity upstairs with its three panels. And the windows. That was the part that made no sense, the part his rational mind kept circling like a tongue probing a broken tooth: the frost was on the *inside* of the glass. He wiped the kitchen window and watched the crystals regrow behind his hand, reforming in seconds, in patterns too deliberate to be chance.

Ferns of ice. Spirals. And then, as he watched, the frost stopped being abstract.

It resolved.

The kitchen window stopped showing the yard—the overgrown lilac, the fence, the neighbor's garage—and showed instead a vertical shaft dropping into darkness. Brick walls slick with water. A ladder rung vanishing down into black. The perspective was impossible, from above and inside at once, the way dreams show you a place you've never been and somehow know.

Cyrus stepped back. The image held. Water moved down there, in the image, in the frozen glass—water that rippled without sound, that caught a light from somewhere below, a bronze-colored glow like lamplight through deep lake water.

He went room to room. Every pane gave him the same view from a slightly different angle, the way a security system gives you cameras. The hallway mirror showed the shaft from the bottom, looking up, and at the top of that image—far, far up, a coin of gray light—he thought he saw a shape. Small. Standing at the rim. Looking down.

He did not go closer to look.

* * *

He stopped sleeping in the house. He told himself it was the heat, and rented a room two neighborhoods over, and spent his days in the tunnels instead, which should have been worse and somehow wasn't. Down there the air was a constant fifty-four degrees and the dark was honest about what it was. Upstairs, the house lied to him with frost and silence and the slow rearrangement of its rooms when he wasn't counting.

On the fifth day, he came up through the coal chute at dusk and found a woman standing at his back door.

She was maybe sixty, silver hair cropped close, wearing a cardigan despite the heat and holding a canvas tote like a shield. She didn't flinch when the door opened. She'd been waiting, he realized—had probably been knocking, and he'd been underground, deaf to the world.

"You're the Marchetti grandson," she said. Not a question. "Cyrus."

"And you're trespassing."

"Ruth Okafor." She said it like an introduction he should already have. "I live at the end of the block. The yellow house. I've lived there since 1968, which means I've been your neighbor longer than you've been alive, so let's not start with trespassing."

He should have sent her away. Every instinct he'd built over forty years—the instincts his grandfather had praised, *good boy, keep to yourself, the quiet holds*—said to close the door, to be strange and closed and safe. But through the kitchen window behind him, the frost had begun to bloom again, and he saw her eyes go past his shoulder and fix on it, and everything in her face changed.

"That's it," she said quietly. "That's what it looked like. In the end."

* * *

She wouldn't come inside at first. She stood on the back step and looked at the window the way people look at an open grave.

"In '74, the summer my father didn't come home," she said. "I was nine. The foundry flooded, six men went into the shaft and never came out, and the company called it structural loss and paid out nothing because there were no bodies to pay out for. And that whole week before they sealed it up—" her hand made a small, involuntary motion toward the frosted glass, "—every window in our house did that. Every mirror. My mother covered them with bedsheets and we lived in the dark for eleven days, and then it stopped, and everyone pretended it had been a cold snap. In July."

"Your father," Cyrus said. "He was one of the six."

"Emmanuel Okafor. He poured the molds." She looked at Cyrus directly then, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "I've spent fifty years thinking I was the only one who remembered the frost. The company bought the widows' silence. The city buried the report. And your family—" she stopped, and something in her voice went flat and careful. "Your family bought something too. Didn't they."

It wasn't a question either.

He thought about denying it. He thought about the journals hidden in the attic, about his grandfather's handwriting growing shakier over the years, the entries that had stopped saying *the bell* and started saying only *it* or *the weight*. He thought about the last entry, from three months before Aldo Marchetti died, the one Cyrus had read forty times and still couldn't finish without his hands shaking:

*Ruth's boy used to wave at me from the fence. I stopped waving back. That was the price. You don't get to keep the quiet and be a neighbor too.*

"You'd better come inside," Cyrus said.

* * *

She walked the house like a surveyor, like someone returning to a burned-down childhood home. She didn't touch anything. She stood in the kitchen and watched the frost bloom and reform on the window, watched the drowned shaft swim into focus inside the ice, and she nodded slowly, as if confirming a diagnosis she'd carried since she was nine years old.

"The bell," she said. "They pulled it up out of the flooded shaft before they sealed it. My mother told me—once, drunk, once only—that the foundry men said the bell was the *throat* of the place. That when it rang, the whole street heard their names in it. Your grandfather's crew hid it in the walls instead of returning it to the water, and the frost stopped. Eleven days, and it stopped." She turned to face him. "Because the family paid. Not with money."

"With silence," Cyrus said.

"With *names*." Her voice cracked on the word for the first time. "Six men went into that water and the city wrote *structural loss* on all of them. No plaque. No funeral with the names said out loud. My mother couldn't even put Emmanuel's name on a stone because there was no body and the company's lawyers—" She pressed the back of her hand to her mouth, mastered herself. "Fifty years. And now your house is doing it again. Which means the bargain is failing. Which means it was always going to fail, because you can't hold a door shut with a dead man's silence forever."

Cyrus looked at the window. In the ice, at the bottom of the shaft, the bronze glow had brightened. And this time, when he looked, he could almost resolve it into a shape—not a lamp, not a reflection. A bell, hanging in black water, swinging without moving the water around it. Waiting to be found. Waiting to be put back.

Or waiting to be answered.

"Ruth," he said. "I need the six names. All of them. I only have your father's."

She looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide—watched fifty years of careful, armored grief tip over into something else, something that looked almost like relief.

"The city archive keeps the misfiled reports," she said. "I checked, twenty years ago. They're filed under structural loss, all six, like they were bricks that fell." She pulled a pen from her tote and wrote an address on the back of a receipt, her hand steady. "Tomorrow morning. They open at nine, and I've been waiting fifty years to walk through that door with a reason."

She paused at the back door, the heat rolling in around her, and looked back at the frost one last time.

"My father used to sing while he worked," she said. "The whole street could hear it. If that bell is still down there, Cyrus—if it kept anything of them—then it's been waiting fifty years for someone to say their names back."

She left. The door closed. And in the frosted glass, deep at the bottom of the drowned shaft, the bronze glow pulsed once—slow, patient, like a held breath finally beginning to end.

Cyrus stood alone in the kitchen, in the summer heat, shivering, and understood that he had already decided to go down.
