## 4. The Missing-Class Problem

There is a particular silence a classroom makes when a name goes unanswered.

Most teachers never learn to hear it. They mark the absence, move on, and the silence closes over like water. But I have spent my whole life in silences—the ones people leave around me, the ones they don't notice they're making—so I heard it immediately, that gray October morning, the way you'd hear a tooth drop into a drain.

"Ellery," I said again, to the room. "Tomas Ellery?"

Nothing. Twenty-two faces looked back at me with the mild, patient attention of children waiting for an adult to catch up. In the third row, Priya Nair had gone very still, her pen hovering over her notebook like a small bird deciding whether to land.

I looked down at the register. The school had given me a paper one—actual paper, in a leather folder soft with age, which should have been my first clue that Hollowmere did things differently—and there, in the printed list of Year Nine, between *Farrow* and *Gunnarsson*, there was no Ellery at all.

I flipped back a page. Forward again. Ran my finger down the column like a man checking his pockets for keys he's certain he had a moment ago.

"Tomas Ellery," I said. "He was here yesterday. Second row, by the window. Tall, freckles, chewed the end of his pencil like it owed him money."

A boy near the front—Okafor, I'd learned his name because he was one of the few who looked at me directly—frowned. "Tomas Ellery?"

"Tomas. Ellery." I spelled it, because that's what you do when the world refuses to hold still: you slow it down, letter by letter.

"He's not in this class," Okafor said, not unkindly. "Is he?"

And here is the thing I want you to understand about that moment, the thing that made the back of my neck prickle in a way I couldn't yet name: he said it the way you repeat a stranger's mistake. Not *I don't know him*. Not *has something happened to him?* Just—*he's not in this class, is he?* The tone of a boy correcting a substitute's arithmetic.

"Yesterday," I said. "Period three. We did the Battle of Hastings. He drew an extremely rude Norman on the back of his worksheet. I confiscated it."

Someone laughed—a short, uncertain laugh, the kind that checks the room first. Priya Nair's pen came down onto the page. She wrote something, small and quick, and covered it with her hand.

I opened the register again, more carefully this time, and read the class list aloud in my head. Twenty-three names. I counted them twice, because counting is a thing I do, a thing I've always done, the way other people bite their nails. Twenty-three. And yesterday, when I'd taken the register for the first time, there had been twenty-four, and the twenty-fourth had answered *here* in a voice that cracked on the vowel because he was thirteen and his voice was mid-collapse, and I had smiled at the sound because it was the most ordinary sound in the world.

"Right," I said, and heard my own voice do the thing it does—go smooth, go pleasant, go nowhere. "Perhaps he's been moved. Admin mix-ups happen. Let's carry on. Page forty-one."

They carried on. That's the terrible thing about classrooms; they carry on the way weather does. But Priya Nair did not look at her page forty-one, and I did not look at her either, and between us, unspoken, sat a small cold fact like a coin on a table.

* * *

Verity Crane's office smelled of pencil shavings and, faintly, of something floral and old—lavender water, maybe, the kind that comes in a brown glass bottle and belongs to a decade nobody alive remembers fondly. The headmistress herself sat behind a desk so tidy it looked staged, a woman of perhaps fifty-five with silver-streaked hair pulled back so severely it seemed less like a hairstyle than a statement of policy.

She was reading when I knocked. She did not look up for a full three seconds, and I understood that this was not rudeness. It was a measurement. Everything about Verity Crane was a measurement—of you, of your nerve, of how long you'd last.

"Mr. Okonkwo." She set down her pen. "How is Year Nine?"

"They're very good. That's actually—" I held up the register. "There's a discrepancy. Tomas Ellery. He was in my class yesterday. Today he's not on the list. Not marked absent. Not marked anything. As far as the register is concerned, he never existed."

I had rehearsed this in the corridor, the way I rehearse everything, sanding the edges off my own alarm so it would present as competence. *A discrepancy.* A nice administrative word. A word that keeps its shoes on.

Verity looked at the register for a long moment. Then she took it from me, turned the pages with one finger, and made a small sound—not surprise, not concern. Something closer to a sigh you'd give a photocopier.

"Transferred," she said.

"Transferred. Overnight? Without paperwork? His parents didn't—"

"The family relocated. It happens, Mr. Okonkwo, particularly at the start of a term. Families in this area are... mobile." She wrote something on a memo pad, a single line, and I noticed her handwriting was beautiful, upright, unhurried. "I'll have the records amended. Thank you for flagging it."

"Flagging it," I repeated. "Right. Only—it's odd, isn't it? That the register wouldn't show it. Registers show transfers. There'd be a code. There'd be—"

"Mr. Okonkwo." She looked up then, and her eyes were gray and steady and not unkind, which somehow made it worse. "You've been with us three days. You're doing well. The students like you—more than they like most, which I'll admit surprised me." A pause, thin as paper. "Hollowmere is an old school in a remote place. Things here are done by hand, and hands make errors. My advice—offered kindly—is not to build mysteries out of clerical mistakes. It's a long winter. Mysteries are exhausting."

She handed the register back. Our fingers didn't touch. I noticed that; I notice everything, it's the curse of the overlooked. She had passed the folder the way you'd pass something you didn't want on your skin.

"Of course," I said. "Clerical mistake. Thank you."

"And Mr. Okonkwo—" I was at the door when she said it, and I turned, and she was already looking back down at her papers as though she hadn't spoken. "Keep your door closed after dark. It saves on heating."

* * *

I did what I always do with a wrongness: I put it in a box and carried it around, weighing it at intervals like a suspicious parcel. I taught Year Seven. I taught Year Eleven, who were preparing for exams and had the grateful, hunted look of the besieged. I ate lunch in the staff room, in my usual chair—my usual chair being whichever chair nobody else wanted, a talent of mine—and listened to the mathematics teacher complain about the boiler and the French teacher complain about the road, and I said nothing about Tomas Ellery, because I am very good at saying nothing.

It was Bram Oduya who broke the box open, and he did it without ceremony, the way he seemed to do everything.

I found him that evening in the east corridor, up a ladder with a screwdriver, replacing a bulb in one of the wall sconces. The corridor lights at Hollowmere were always slightly too warm, slightly too many, as though the building were wearing a coat in mild weather. I'd noticed it my first night. I'd assumed it was inefficiency.

"Mr. Oduya," I said. "Got a minute?"

"Bram." He didn't look down. He was a broad, gray-templed man with forearms like dock rope and a face that had decided, sometime in the last century, to stop performing emotions it didn't feel. "Everyone calls me Bram. Mr. Oduya is the man they'll name the boiler after, if it keeps behaving."

"Bram, then. Quick question. Tomas Ellery. Year Nine. Do you know him?"

The screwdriver stopped.

It stopped for maybe two seconds—two seconds is nothing, two seconds is the gap between heartbeats—but I've built a life out of noticing gaps, and that one had a whole winter in it.

"Ellery," Bram said, and resumed turning the screw. "Reddish hair? Big lad for his age?"

"That's him. He's left, apparently. Transferred."

"Mm." The screw went in. The bulb glowed. Bram climbed down the ladder with the unhurried economy of a man who had climbed a great many ladders, and looked at me, and I watched him decide something behind his eyes the way you watch weather decide. "Third one," he said.

"Third one what?"

"Third one this term." He coiled the ladder's cord with two practiced loops. "Kids gone. Register forgets them. Headmistress says *transferred.*" He said the word the way you'd say the name of a medicine that stopped working. "You're new, so you'll do what the last three subs did. You'll shrug. That's the sensible thing. Shrugging's free."

"And if I don't shrug?"

He looked at me properly then, and there was a long moment where I felt—this will sound strange, but I felt *seen*, which for me is roughly the sensation of being caught in a spotlight I'd spent thirty years learning to step around. Bram Oduya looked at me the way you look at a wall to check whether it's load-bearing.

"You're the one who counts the doors," he said.

I hadn't told him that. Verity had told me, my first evening—*count the doors each night, Mr. Okonkwo, it's an old fire-safety habit of ours*—and I had assumed it was an eccentricity, one of those rituals remote institutions develop, like ringing a bell nobody needs. I hadn't mentioned it to anyone. I certainly hadn't mentioned it to the caretaker.

"How did you—"

"Fire-safety habit," Bram said, and something moved at the corner of his mouth that was not a smile but was paying rent in the same building. "That's what she tells everyone. And everyone believes her, because everyone wants to." He picked up his toolbox. "Twenty-three doors in this building, Mr. Okonkwo. That's what the plans say. That's what they've always said. You count them tonight, and you tell me tomorrow what you get. And then—" he paused at the end of the corridor, where the light from the sconce he'd just fixed fell just short of the stairwell, leaving that particular dark the way it was, "—and then we'll have a conversation about Tomas Ellery. A real one. Bring nothing. Tell no one."

He walked off, and I stood there under the too-warm light, holding my suspicious parcel of a wrongness, which had just grown heavier.

* * *

That night I counted the doors.

I want to be honest about this: I almost didn't. I lay in my room in the small staff accommodation—narrow bed, narrow window, a radiator that ticked like a patient clock—and I told myself the sensible things. *Clerical error. Mobile families. Long winter.* I told myself that a substitute teacher's contract does not include mysteries, that my entire life had been an exercise in not being where trouble was, that invisibility was not a burden but a craft, and that craftsmen do not smash their own work for the sake of a boy who drew a rude Norman.

Then I got up, because there was a sound in the walls.

Not a voice. Not yet. Just the pipes, ticking and settling, the way old buildings do. Except old buildings settle in random, and this was not random. It had a shape. Four sounds, then a pause, then the same four sounds, patient as a tide. I stood with my hand flat against the radiator and felt the rhythm come up through my palm, into my wrist, into the place behind my sternum where a person keeps the things they're afraid of, and I thought: *that's a melody. That's four notes.*

I got my coat. I took the torch from the drawer—everyone at Hollowmere had a torch in their drawer; I'd thought it was a power-cut thing—and I went out into the corridor.

The lights were on. All of them. Every sconce, every bulkhead, every yellow bulb burning in its fitting at eleven at night, the whole school lit up like a ship that has decided, against all evidence, to keep sailing.

I walked the ground floor and counted as I went. It steadied me, the counting. It's the oldest thing I know how to do. Classroom doors, storeroom doors, the fire doors with their green signs glowing like exits from a different world. I counted them twice, walking the loop twice, because I trust a number only when it agrees with itself.

Twenty-three, said the first loop.

Twenty-three, said the second loop.

And then I stood at the end of the east corridor, where Bram's new bulb shed its warm and insufficient light, and I looked at the door at the very end—the one I had walked past three days running without seeing, the way you can walk past a picture in your own hallway for years—and I understood that I had not counted it. That my eye had slid off it both times, the way people's eyes slid off me, my whole life, and that this door and I had something in common, and that the thought was so absurd and so cold that I laughed out loud, alone, in a lit corridor, and the laugh came out wrong.

I counted it. Twenty-four.

The door was old, older than the others, iron-banded, with a lock the size of a fist. There was no sign on it. There was no handle on the inside of it, I noticed—no handle at all, just a keyhole, and the keyhole was warm.

I put my hand near it, not touching. Warm, in a cold corridor, at the end of October, in a building where the radiators sang four notes to nobody.

Down the corridor, behind me, a radiator ticked. Ticked again. And then—softly, under the ticking, under the wind at the windows, under the sensible explanations I was still building like sandbags against a flood—came the melody. Four notes. A pause. Four notes. Patient as a tide. Patient as something that has waited a hundred years and can wait a hundred more, and is, even now, checking its list.

I thought of Tomas Ellery, cracking on the vowel of *here.*

I took my hand away from the warm keyhole, and I went back to my room, and I did not sleep. And in the morning, before first period, before the lights had even finished pretending it was daytime, I found Bram Oduya in the boiler room, standing among the pipes with two cups of coffee and the expression of a man who has been waiting for someone to ask.

"You counted," he said.

"Twenty-four," I said.

Bram nodded slowly, and set one of the cups down on a crate in front of me, like an offering, or a beginning.

"Right," he said. "Sit down, Mr. Okonkwo. And I'll tell you about the ones who transferred."
