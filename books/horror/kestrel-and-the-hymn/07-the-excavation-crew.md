## 7. The Excavation Crew

The trucks arrived on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays are the day the world does its maintenance—bins collected, deliveries made, men in hi-vis vests descending on places that had been quietly minding their own business. Hollowmere had been minding its own business for a hundred and thirty years, and now three white lorries were grinding up the mountain road at seven in the morning, trailing diesel fumes and a smell of thermos coffee and certainty.

I watched them from my classroom window while pretending to prepare a lesson I'd already prepared. This is one of my oldest skills: looking busy enough that nobody asks you anything. In thirty-eight years of being overlooked, I have refined it to an art form. There is a particular way of arranging papers so that your face becomes furniture. I could teach a masterclass. Nobody would attend, which would suit me fine.

"Sir?" Priya Nair was at my desk. She had a way of appearing without arriving, which I respected professionally. "Are those the diggers?"

"Excavation crew," I said. "For the new boiler room."

"They're digging *behind* the east wing." She said it the way other people say *there's something on your shoulder*—flat, factual, and loaded with everything they're not saying.

"That's where boiler rooms go. Behind things. It's the law."

Priya looked at me for a long moment with the patience of someone deciding whether the adult in front of her was worth the trouble of honesty. I've been on both sides of that calculation. Usually I lose.

"My dad says ground remembers," she said finally. "He's a surveyor. He says if you dig somewhere old, the ground tells you what it's been." She hitched her bag higher. "Just—when they start, watch the machines. Not the men. The machines will tell you first."

Then she left, and I stood there with my beautifully arranged papers, feeling like a man who has just been handed a note in class and told not to read it until he gets home.

* * *

The foreman was called Dennis Achebe, which I learned because he shouted his own name into his phone approximately forty times before nine a.m., as though the world kept forgetting it. He was a broad, sunburnt man with the permanent squint of someone who has spent decades arguing with terrain, and by ten o'clock he was standing in the middle of the churned brown rectangle behind the east wing, hands on hips, wearing the expression of a man who has just discovered the terrain is cheating.

I was watching from the staff room window with a mug of tea I had no intention of drinking. Bram Oduya stood beside me, arms folded, radiating the specific unhappiness of a caretaker watching strangers touch his building.

"They'll be through the clay by Thursday," Bram said. "Then they'll hit the old foundations and stop, and Verity will sign whatever form makes them go away, and we'll all get on with it."

"You've seen this before?"

"I've seen crews come before." His voice did something careful around the sentence. "They don't always leave for the same reasons."

Below us, a mini-digger swung its arm and bit into the earth, and even through the double glazing I heard the sound it made—not the clean rip of bucket through soil but a wet, reluctant tearing, like the ground was being peeled rather than dug. The machine lurched. The driver revved, tried again, and this time the bucket came up trailing earth that steamed.

Steam. In October. On a mountainside where the frost had already started sketching its early drafts on the windows every morning.

"Bram," I said. "Is there geothermal activity here? Hot springs? Anything?"

"There's a reason the original builders put the school here," Bram said, which was not an answer, delivered in a tone that made clear it was never going to become one.

Dennis Achebe had noticed the steam too. We watched him crouch, press his palm flat against the exposed earth, and then stand up very quickly and wipe his hand on his trousers with the brisk disgust of a man who has touched something he'd rather not have touched. Then he pulled out his phone again and shouted his name into it.

"He'll ring the office," Bram said. "They'll tell him it's a coal seam. There's always a coal seam."

"Is there?"

"No." Bram picked up his keys from the windowsill. "But there's always a story, and stories are cheaper than stopping work." He paused at the door. "Xanthe. When they dig deeper—and they will, because men like him don't stop at the first wrong thing—the hymn will notice. Things that sleep shallow sleep light. You understand me?"

"I genuinely don't."

"Good," said Bram, and left, which seemed to be his preferred conclusion to any conversation involving information.

* * *

At lunchtime I took my sandwich out to the low wall by the car park, ostensibly to eat it, actually to get closer to the pit. Being unnoticed has practical applications; I can stand within earshot of almost anything, because people edit you out of their awareness within seconds. I once listened to an entire divorce being planned in a supermarket queue. The couple never once glanced at me, though I was holding a pineapple.

Dennis Achebe was holding court by the spoil heap, and his crew were giving him the particular silence of men who want to quit but haven't found the words yet.

"It's not right," Achebe was saying, to no one and everyone. "You don't get soft ground at depth on a mountain. Ground compacts. That's what ground *does*. This—" he kicked the spoil heap, and the clods fell apart too easily, like stale bread, "—this is like digging through something that's been turned over. Recently. And it's warm. It's warm all the way down, and it shouldn't be, and the smell—"

Nobody asked about the smell. That was the interesting part. Four grown men, professional diggers, and not one of them wanted the smell described.

"The survey said clay over shale," said the youngest one, whose hi-vis vest still had its creases.

"The survey," said Achebe, "was done by a man with a map and a pen, and I'd like to know what he knew that we don't, because nobody surveys a site this thoroughly and then doesn't mention that the ground is *breathing*."

A pause. One of the older lads spat thoughtfully into the pit.

"It moves," the older lad said. "Just a little. Like it settles when you look away and then looks innocent when you come back. My grandad worked the quarry at Nant-y-Glo and he used to say some ground holds its breath. You dig till it breathes out, and then you stop, and you don't ask why."

"And what do we do?" said the young one.

"We keep digging," said Achebe, "because I've got a contract and a mortgage and a reputation, and because it's *dirt*, Kieran, it's dirt and it's warm and warm dirt is a plumbing problem, not a haunting." He said it loudly, the way people say things they need to believe more than say. "Now get back down there."

I finished my sandwich. The crusts went to the kestrel that sometimes hunted the playing field—a small, fierce, russet-backed thing that saw everything and owed nothing to anyone, which I have always considered the correct way to live. It took the bread without thanks, which I also respect.

When I walked back toward the east wing, I passed close to the pit's edge, and I felt it.

Not heard. Felt. The way you feel a conversation stop when you enter a room—the sudden self-consciousness of air. The hum that lived in the school's pipes, the four-note figure I'd been telling myself for two weeks was just old heating doing old-heating things, went quiet. Not faded. Stopped. The way a singer stops mid-phrase when they hear a floorboard.

And underneath the silence, faint as a pulse through a wrist, something listened back.

I know how that sounds. I'm a substitute teacher, not a mystic; my relationship with the supernatural previously extended to an irrational dislike of taxidermy. But I stood at the edge of that warm, breathing hole in the world, and I had the absolute, skin-crawling certainty that something below the school had paused its song to pay attention to the men with the machines, and that it was deciding—patiently, musically—what to do about them.

The frost ferns on the east wing window above the pit, I noticed, had changed overnight. They'd been random before, or I'd assumed they were. Now they curved. Now they repeated. Four marks, over and over, in patterns like the notation I'd half-remembered from a music lesson in 1993.

Four notes. The same four notes the pipes hummed.

I got back inside and did what any rational man does with an impossible observation: I wrote it down, dated it, and hid it in the false bottom of my pencil case, because a lifetime of foster homes teaches you that documentation is survival and also that documentation gets stolen.

* * *

By Thursday, the pit was eleven feet deep, and Dennis Achebe had stopped shouting his name into his phone, which somehow frightened me more than the shouting had.

The crew had found the edge of something. Not foundations exactly—Bram had been wrong about that, or hopeful. It was a curve of dressed stone, dark and dense, sweeping across the whole width of the excavation like the rim of something enormous buried face-down. The stone was warm to the touch. Moss grew on it in winter-green velvet, lush and wrong, in a season when everything else on the mountain had given up and gone brown.

Achebe stood at the lip of the pit with a clipboard he wasn't writing on.

"That's not in the survey either," said Kieran.

"No."

"What is it?"

"It's a wall," said Achebe. "Or part of one. Curved wall, dressed stone, pre-Victorian by the tooling." He said it in the tone of a man reciting facts to keep from saying other things. "Which means there was a building here before the school, and the school records say there wasn't, and somebody paid for a survey that didn't find a wall the size of a cathedral floor."

"Ring the office?"

"I rang the office." Achebe looked at the stone for a while. "You know what the woman said? She said, 'Oh yes, that'll be the old chapel, carry on.' Just like that. 'Carry on.' Like she'd been waiting for the call." He rubbed his jaw. "Thirty years I've done this job. You develop a nose for jobs that go wrong. This one went wrong before we ever unloaded the digger. I just didn't smell it."

"So we stop?"

Achebe didn't answer immediately. Below him, two of his men were brushing soil off the curved stone with trowels, and the steam rose around them gently, like the ground exhaling after a long time held.

"We dig to the contract depth," he said at last. "We document everything. And Friday night, when we knock off, we take our photos, and Monday I make calls, and if anyone official tells me to carry on, they can carry on without me." He folded the clipboard shut. "My mother sang in a church choir in Enugu for forty years. She used to say there are songs you sing and songs that sing you, and you'd best know which is which before you open your mouth." He snorted at himself, embarrassed. "Superstition. But she also said trust the gooseflesh, and I've had gooseflesh since Tuesday."

I withdrew before my invisibility wore thin. It doesn't, usually—it's less a cloak than a fact about me—but standing near Achebe, I'd felt oddly *seen*, the way you do around honest men. They look at everything, including the unremarkable.

* * *

That night I counted the doors.

It had become ritual without my quite agreeing to it. Twenty-three doors on the main corridor—I'd checked, rechecked, and checked again during my first week, after Verity Crane's instruction, counting twice nightly like a man defusing a bomb by habit. Twenty-three doors, twenty-two classrooms and stores and cupboards, one of which was locked and had been locked, according to Bram, since before his time.

Twenty-four doors tonight.

I stood in the corridor with the lights burning—all of them, per instructions—and counted again, slowly, touching each doorframe like a blind man reading a line of text. Twenty-four. The extra door sat between the music room and the stairwell, a plain door in plain paint, with a brass number that read 12, which was wrong, because number 12 was four doors back, and I was looking at it.

The pipes hummed their four notes. And beneath them, far below, faint as a pulse under floorboards, came a second sound. Rhythmic. Warm. Almost like breathing.

Almost like singing, very softly, to itself, the way you sing when you think no one is listening—and have just realized that someone is.

Outside the window at the end of the corridor, the frost was writing its four curved marks across the glass, row upon row, patient as homework. And past the east wing, down in the floodlit pit, I could see the little yellow digger parked for the night beside the curve of dark stone, and the steam rising off the warm earth around it like incense.

The hymn had stopped humming to itself.

It was listening to the digging.

And tomorrow, I understood, with the clarity that arrives at 2 a.m. and refuses to leave—it was going to start answering.
