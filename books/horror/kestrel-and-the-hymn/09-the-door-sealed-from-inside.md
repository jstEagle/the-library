## 9. The Door, Sealed From Inside

The morning the excavation crew broke ground behind the east wing, I was teaching Year 9 the difference between a metaphor and a simile, which is the sort of thing that sounds simple until you try to explain it to fourteen-year-olds who have decided, collectively and without discussion, that nothing before lunch is worth their attention.

"Simile is when you *say* it's like something," I told them. "Metaphor is when you just commit to the lie."

Tomas Ellery, back among us for a week now and smiling his even, unblinking smile, raised his hand. "What's it called when something *is* the thing?"

I opened my mouth to say that wasn't a literary device, that was just being, and then the ground shuddered under the school. A low, grinding tremor, like the building clearing its throat. The windows rattled in their frames. Somewhere down the corridor, a stack of exercise books slid off a shelf with a papery sigh.

The class went still. All of them. Not the ordinary stillness of teenagers hoping a distraction will eat the rest of the lesson — a different quality entirely, a held-breath, listening stillness, like a congregation waiting for the organ to start.

Then the moment passed, the way such moments do, and Marcus Adeyemi said, "Sir, is that the boiler?" and I said, "Probably," and we went back to similes, and I let the lie stand because the alternative — *that was the school, and it was paying attention* — was not a sentence I was prepared to say out loud.

But I noticed that Tomas had not flinched. He'd leaned, very slightly, toward the east-facing window. Toward the sound.

I marked that down in the growing ledger of things I would rather not know.

* * *

The crew had arrived three days earlier: four men and a woman in high-vis jackets and a digger the colour of an old bruise, sent by the diocesan trust or the county council or whichever body had decided that Hollowmere's heating system, installed sometime during the reign of a monarch whose face I'd have to look up, finally needed replacing. They were meant to dig a new boiler room behind the east wing, in the strip of ground between the building and the boundary wall where nothing grew but nettles and a single ash tree that had been dying for as long as the school had photographs of itself.

I'd watched them from the staff room window on the first morning, coffee going cold in my hand, and I'd felt something I couldn't name — a wrongness, a held note. The hymn in the pipes, my four-note lodger, had gone quiet. Not silent. *Quiet.* The way a conversation goes quiet when someone new walks into the room.

It had been listening.

Bram Oduya had appeared at my elbow, also watching, also not speaking. After a while he said, "Ground's wrong back there."

"Wrong how?"

"Soft." He turned his mug in his hands. His knuckles were the colour and texture of old rope. "You'd think a hundred years of school foundations would pack it down, but it gives. And it's warm. Digger man said so yesterday. Warm ground in October, under frost every night. He reckons it's a spring." He drank. "It's not a spring."

"What is it?"

Bram set his mug down and looked at me with the flat, tired patience of a man deciding how much of a truth a person can carry. "Ask me again in a week," he said, "if it's still just a boiler room."

* * *

It was not still just a boiler room by Thursday.

I know because Priya Nair came to find me at the end of last period, which she had never done before. Priya did not do things like come to find teachers. Priya did things like leave annotated notebooks on desks and vanish around corners with the efficiency of a rumour. So when she appeared in my classroom doorway, backpack over one shoulder, face set in the expression of someone about to deliver bad news she'd rehearsed, I knew it was serious.

"They've found something," she said. "The diggers. Everyone's down there. Even the Year 7s are sneaking out."

"Everyone's *where*?"

"East wing. Come on, sir. You'll want to see it before they put a fence up."

I want to say I hesitated. I want to claim the dignity of reluctance, the measured caution of a responsible adult weighing the wisdom of following a fifteen-year-old toward an unsecured excavation site. What I actually did was grab my coat and follow her, because Priya Nair had been the only person in this building to tell me the truth about anything, and when the only honest person you know says *come and see*, you come and see.

* * *

The strip behind the east wing had become a small amphitheatre of mud. The digger sat idle at the edge, engine ticking as it cooled, and the crew stood in a loose cluster at the lip of the trench they'd opened, not working, not talking, just looking down into it the way people look at things they wish they hadn't found.

The students hung back by the boundary wall in a ragged, whispering crescent. I counted them without meaning to — it's a habit, counting things; doors, windows, exits, children — and the number came back wrong, or rather the number came back *right* and that was the wrong part. Every returned student was there. Tomas. The two girls from Year 10 whose names I'd learned from the register and nowhere else. The little boy from Year 8 who'd come back in September. All four of them stood slightly apart from the other students, slightly forward, faces tilted down toward the trench like compass needles.

Priya and I pushed through.

The trench was maybe eight feet deep, cut into earth that did look wrong — dark and loose and faintly steaming in the cold air, as if the ground itself had a fever. And set into the floor of the trench, flush with the earth, level with it, so that you might have walked over it your whole life and never known, was a door.

Iron. Black with age, but not rusted — that was the first thing I noticed, standing at the edge with my heart doing something arrhythmic. A century in the ground and the metal was smooth as a church collection plate, unblemished, as though the earth had been keeping it polished. It was broad, broader than any domestic door, arched at the top in a style I recognised from the oldest photographs in the school corridor, from the chapel that appeared in the 1890s class pictures and then, after 1921, appeared in nothing at all.

And it was shut. Not buried shut — the crew had cleared the earth from its face, and I could see the whole of it, hinges and all. It was shut the way a door is shut when someone has closed it carefully from the other side and thrown the bolts.

Because the bolts were visible. Three of them, heavy slide-bolts set across the door's inner face — no, wait. I stood there for a long moment, cold air in my lungs, trying to make the geometry behave. The bolts were on *our* side of the door. I could see them. But they were thrown — shot home into their catches — and the catches were mounted on the door itself, on the side that faced down, into the dark.

Which meant the bolts had been thrown from below. From inside.

The door was sealed from within, by the people who had gone down into it, who had locked themselves in, who had — and this thought arrived with the gentleness of a blade — wanted to make sure nothing got out. Or wanted to make sure nothing got *in*. I genuinely could not decide which was worse.

"Foreman says it's a cellar," said a voice beside me. Bram had materialised, as he did, with the quiet of a man who'd spent thirty years learning how buildings move. "Says old chapels had crypts. Says they'll have it open by Friday."

"Do you believe him?"

Bram was looking at the door with an expression I'd never seen on him — not his usual weary vigilance but something rawer. Recognition, maybe. Or grief. "I believe," he said, "that them bolts are on the wrong side, and I believe that foreman believes his own story because the other story's got no pension attached to it." He shifted. "And I believe that ground's been warm for a hundred years, and warm ground means something's been breathing down there."

"Breathing."

"Or singing." He said it lightly, the way you'd say anything unbearable. "Same difference, maybe, for whatever it is."

* * *

The frost came at dusk, and it came wrong.

I was still at the trench, because I had developed the specific coward's courage of a man who cannot look away from the thing that frightens him. The students had been herded inside by the deputy head, the crew had knocked off for the day — the foreman had made some joke about knocking off early that nobody laughed at, including him — and I'd told myself I was staying to *supervise*, which was a lie so thin you could read through it.

The air temperature was mild. Six, maybe seven degrees. The kind of grey, damp October evening where frost is a rumour you don't believe. The grass was wet but unfrozen. My breath didn't even cloud properly.

And across the iron door, frost began to bloom.

It didn't creep. It didn't spread from the edges. It *bloomed* — that's the only honest word — opening across the black metal in ferns and feathers and branching, fractal lace, white on black, gorgeous, the way a virus under a microscope is gorgeous. It grew in seconds. Where there had been bare iron there was winter, a whole winter, laid out in patterns too intricate for accident.

I should have stepped back. Any reasonable person would have stepped back. But the patterns had a rhythm to them, a repetition, a structure, and I have spent my whole life noticing structures — the architecture of rooms that let a boy become furniture, the grammar of being overlooked — and I saw, with the particular horror of a man whose only talent is attention, that the frost was not decorating the door.

It was *writing* on it.

Curves and stems and hooked marks, arranged in lines, in phrases, in something with the cadence of language and the discipline of music. The same shapes, recurring. The same phrase, over and over, with variations, the way a melody returns.

The way a verse returns.

"Sir." Priya's voice, behind me, very quiet. I hadn't heard her come back out. I was starting to suspect that Priya Nair could move through that school without being seen, and I meant to ask her about that someday, when asking questions was less expensive. "Sir, you shouldn't touch it."

"I wasn't going to touch it."

"You were leaning."

I was leaning. I looked down and found my right hand extended over the trench, over the frost-laced iron, fingers out, the way you reach for a door handle in your own home, the way you reach without thinking because the body knows doors are for opening.

I pulled my hand back. I said, "Go inside, Priya."

"Are you coming?"

"In a minute."

She didn't go. She stood at the trench edge with her arms crossed and her breath finally clouding — hers clouded, I noticed; the cold was coming off the door, pooling in the trench, spilling up over the lip like water finding its level — and she watched me with the flat, unimpressed gaze of a girl who had already decided that adults who said *in a minute* meant *never* or *always*, and she was going to find out which.

I should have gone inside. I want that on the record. Whatever happened next, I want it understood that I knew better, that some cold, sensible Xanthe Okonkwo in the back of my skull was saying *step away, count the doors, keep the lights on, be furniture, be forgotten, that's what you're for* —

I touched the door.

Just my fingertips. Just the heel of my palm, briefly, the way you'd test an iron gate for rain. The frost was cold but not biting; it felt almost *eager*, the cold equivalent of a handshake held a beat too long.

And the hymn completed itself in my skull.

I don't know how else to say it. For weeks the melody had been four notes, looping, patient, a phrase that never resolved, a sentence without its ending. I'd hummed it to myself in the shower without realising, caught myself doing it, stopped. Four notes, over and over, like a knock that never gets answered.

The moment my skin met the iron, the fifth note arrived. And the sixth. The melody turned, climbed, found its cadence, and closed — *closed*, the way a door closes, the way a lock closes — and I heard, for one full, impossible second, the whole verse, beginning to end, sung in a register below hearing, in a harmony of more voices than there are people in the world.

It was beautiful. That's the part I keep failing to convey, the part that shames me. It was the most beautiful thing I have ever heard, and it sounded like *home*. It sounded like being known. It sounded like a room where everyone turns when you walk in, where your name is the first word of every sentence, where you could never, ever be overlooked again.

I wanted to sing it back. My throat actually moved. My lungs filled.

And underneath the beauty — this is the only reason I'm writing this, the only reason I'm not standing at that trench still, palm on iron, frost climbing my sleeve — underneath it, in the foundations of the sound, I heard the voices. Not one voice. Hundreds. Children's voices and old voices and voices that had been singing so long they'd worn down to their outlines. And they weren't singing *with* the melody.

They were the melody.

The verse ended. The door hummed under my hand like a struck bell, once, fading. The frost on its face settled into stillness, a finished page.

I took my hand back. I fell backward onto the wet grass, which was the least dignified thing I'd done since the incident with the fire drill at St. Aldric's, and I lay there looking up at the grey sky, breathing, while Priya Nair stood over me with her arms still crossed and said, in the voice of a girl who has been warning adults for years and is tired of being right:

"I *told* you."

* * *

I don't remember walking back to the main building. I remember the grass, and the sky, and then I remember the corridor outside the staff room, my hands wrapped around a mug of tea I had no memory of making, Bram sitting across from me watching me with those old-rope hands flat on the table like a man pinning something down.

"You touched it," he said.

"It touched me first."

"What did it sound like?"

I looked into my tea. "Finished," I said. "It sounded finished. Like the whole time it's been in the pipes, it's been holding the last note, waiting." I swallowed. "Bram, it's not a song they're singing. It's a song they're *building*. And they only need one more part."

Bram was quiet for a long time. The radiators ticked. Somewhere far off in the building's iron throat, faint and patient, the four notes began again — and this time, for the first time, they were followed by a fifth, held, questioning, like a hand extended.

Like an invitation.

Bram stood, went to the light switch, and turned on every light in the staff room, and then, through the little window in the door, I watched him move down the corridor flipping every switch as he went, floor by floor, until the whole east wing blazed against the dusk like a ship signalling desperately to shore.

"They only need one more part," he said from the doorway, not looking at me. "Then we'd best make sure it's not yours."
