## 11. Frost Writing Itself

I have never been a morning person. This is not a personality quirk so much as a survival strategy—mornings are when the world insists on looking at you, and I had built an entire life on the principle that being looked at was dangerous. So when I woke at 5:47 a.m. to a silence that felt *wrong*—the particular wrongness of a room where something has been happening while you slept—I lay in my narrow bed in the staff quarters and listened.

The pipes were quiet. That was the first thing. After two nights of that four-note thread humming through the radiators like a child practicing under its breath, the silence should have been a relief. It wasn't. Silence after a sound like that isn't peace; it's a held breath.

The second thing was the light. My window faced west, toward the quad, and even before dawn there should have been the orange smear of the security lamps on the glass. Instead the window glowed a pale, bruised white, like moonlight with an agenda.

I got up. I did not put on my dressing gown immediately, which for me constitutes reckless abandon. I crossed the little room and stood in front of the window, and my breath fogged the glass, and beneath my breath's fog there was already something else written.

Frost. In October. In a building whose heating Bram Oduya maintained with the devotion of a man tending a sacred flame—and I mean that literally now, though I didn't yet know how literally.

But it wasn't the frost itself that made me stand very still and think about the door of my room and whether locking it from the inside would accomplish anything. It was the shape of it.

* * *

Here is what I knew about frost by then: it doesn't draw. It blooms, it feathers, it spreads in the fractal way of all cold things—beautiful, yes, but random. Nobody looks at frost on a window and thinks, *ah, penmanship.*

This frost had penmanship.

Curved marks marched across the lower pane in careful rows, each one a small economy of line—a hook, a loop, a stroke that bent back on itself like a question mark that had given up asking. They were spaced evenly. They were grouped. And groups, as any substitute teacher who has ever squinted at a whiteboard knows, imply language.

I pressed my fingertip to one of them. The glass was so cold it burned, a bright white ache, and I snatched my hand back and left a clear print in the frost like a signature on someone else's document.

Behind me, someone knocked.

I want to note, for the record, that I did not scream. What I did was make a short, strangled noise that in a braver man might have been called a shout, and then I said, "Yes?" in the level voice of a man who has absolutely been awake and composed for hours.

"It's Ada." A pause. "Lindqvist. Music." Another pause, longer. "You're the only other light on."

I looked at my window—the pale glow I'd taken for pre-dawn. It wasn't coming from outside at all. The frost was catching the security lamp and throwing it back, every curved mark lit up like a filament.

I opened the door. Ada Lindqvist stood in the corridor in a cardigan over her nightclothes, holding her phone in both hands the way you hold a bird you've just rescued from a chimney—not sure whether it's grateful or about to panic.

"You've seen it," she said. Not a question.

"I've seen something."

"The east wing," she said. "All of it. Every window." Her voice was steady the way a tightrope is steady. "Xanthe, I need you to come and look at it before the sun comes up. Frost melts. Whatever this is—" she lifted the phone slightly, "—it's been writing all night, and I don't think it's finished."

* * *

The east wing at six in the morning is a corridor of pale fire.

Every window, floor to ceiling, the old Victorian panes with their leaded seams—all of them furred white, and all of them marked. Not identically. That was the detail that turned my stomach from unease into something colder. Each window carried a different passage of the same script, the way pages of a book carry different passages of the same story. If you stood at the far end of the corridor and looked down its length, the marks ran window to window to window, unbroken, a single text laid out across forty feet of glass.

Ada walked slowly along it, photographing pane after pane. Her hands were shaking—I could see it in the way the images stuttered on her screen—but she kept going, methodical, a woman doing her job while her job tried to become something else.

"How long has it been doing this?" I asked.

"I noticed the first window around three." She didn't look up. "Classroom 4E. I'd gone down for water. By four it was this whole floor. By five—" she gestured with the phone at the windows above us, visible through the stairwell's open well, "—it went upstairs too."

"Did you wake anyone?"

"Bram. He came with a hairdryer."

"A hairdryer."

"He melted one window clean." She finally looked at me, and her face in the frost-light was older than it had been at the staff meeting, or maybe just more honest. "By the time he'd found an extension cord long enough for the second window, the first one had filled back in. Same marks. Exactly the same marks. Like it remembered what it had written and was annoyed at being interrupted."

"Annoyed."

"I'm a music teacher, Xanthe. I spend my life listening for intention in sound. Intention is not a mysterious concept to me." She turned back to the nearest pane. "This has intention. Look at the spacing. Look at the phrasing."

"Phrasing."

She gave me a look that said she was going to run out of patience at roughly the same rate the sun was rising, and then she held up her phone beside the window, and I saw what she'd done.

She'd transcribed some of it. On her notes app, in careful thumb-typing, she'd drawn the curved marks as best she could—and next to them, in standard notation, she'd written what they *were*.

"They're not letters," she said. "Or—they're not only letters. Neume shapes. Medieval notation, the kind they used for chant before anyone thought to put music on staves. A hook means rise. A loop means hold. These little ticks are liquescents—notes that slide, that aren't quite pinned down." Her finger hovered over the glass without touching it; she'd learned that lesson already, apparently, or watched me learn it. "Someone wrote music on these windows. Someone with a very old education and a great deal of patience."

"And no hymnal contains it," I said, because she'd gone still in the way people go still when they're deciding whether to say the frightening thing.

"No hymnal contains it." She exhaled. "I own four hundred hymnals. I'm not exaggerating—it's a problem, my husband used to say, until he stopped saying anything, but that's another story. Gregorian, Anglican, Shape Note, Finnish Lutheran—my grandmother's tradition, all the grim minor-key stuff about winter and drowning. I know the repertoire. This isn't in the repertoire." She scrolled through her photos. "And it's not random, either. Random doesn't repeat motifs. Look—this figure here, the double loop with the descending tick. It appears in window seven, window twelve, window nineteen. Always at the start of a group. Always followed by the same rising phrase." She looked at me. "It's a verse. Or verses. It's structured like verses, Xanthe. With a refrain."

The word went through me like the cold had. Refrains repeat. Refrains are what you remember. Refrains are what get stuck in your head, what you hum without knowing you're humming, what—

"What does it sound like?" I asked.

"That's the wrong question."

"It's the only question I have."

Ada lowered the phone. For a moment she just breathed, and the frost-light moved faintly across her face as clouds passed over the security lamp outside.

"When I transcribed the refrain," she said quietly, "I could hear it. You understand? Just from the notation. That's what notation is *for*—it's a machine for storing sound in ink. And I sat in classroom 4E at four in the morning with my phone in my hands, and I read those curves, and I heard four notes." Her voice dropped further. "The same four notes that have been in the pipes."

The corridor seemed to lean in.

"But slower," she said. "Slower, and lower, and—complete. The pipe-hum is a fragment. An announcement. This is the announcement's full text." She hugged the phone to her chest. "And here's the part I haven't said out loud yet, because saying it makes it real: the last group of marks, the ones on the top-floor windows that appeared around five—I can't transcribe them. I've tried. The shapes won't resolve. Either whoever wrote this ran out of alphabet—" she laughed, a small terrible laugh, "—or the ending hasn't been decided yet."

* * *

We heard Bram before we saw him, because Bram Oduya moved through this building like weather—you registered the pressure change first. His boots on the stairs, heavy and deliberate, and then his voice, pitched low, talking to someone.

He came around the corner with Priya Nair beside him, and I want to say I was surprised, but I wasn't. Priya had the look of a girl who had stopped sleeping at roughly the same time I had, and Bram had the look of a man who had decided that if the building was going to do things at night, he was going to be present for them, professionally, with his keys.

"You're both here," Bram said. He surveyed the corridor of burning-white windows with the expression of a caretaker seeing thirty years of maintenance insulted in a single night. "Good. Then everyone can hear this once." He planted himself in the middle of the corridor, hands in his pockets, and addressed the frost directly, which told me something about how his night had gone.

"I've worked here since I was twenty-three," he said. "My predecessor, Mr. Fenn, worked here forty years before that. First week on the job, Fenn tells me: keep the lights on, count the doors, and if frost ever comes to the glass in October, you get everyone out of the east wing and you don't ask why." He looked at me. "I asked why, obviously. I was twenty-three. Fenn took me down to the boiler room, poured me a whiskey I wasn't legally old enough to drink, and told me about the year the school went quiet. Nineteen fifty-something. A term where the heating failed and nobody could say why, and the windows frosted from the inside, and eleven children were 'transferred' before Christmas." His jaw worked. "Fenn said the frost is the school thinking out loud. Said it writes what it wants sung. Said the last time, they brought in a man from the diocese to look at the windows, and the man copied down every mark, took it home, and sang it—took it to his church choir, if you can believe, thought it was a curiosity, an old chant, a treasure."

Priya had gone very still beside him.

"He harmonized it," Bram said. "Him and his choir. And the following spring, his whole village had a bad year. Bad the specific way Hollowmere has bad years. Children walking in their sleep. Doors counted wrong." He shrugged, a big slow shrug that didn't match the size of what he was saying. "Fenn said the frost isn't decoration. It's a score. And scores want performing."

Nobody spoke. Down the corridor, the frost-light flickered as the security lamp cycled, and for half a second every curved mark on every window seemed to shift—seemed to *lean*—and I honestly couldn't tell you afterward whether it happened or whether the dark rearranged it in my memory.

"So we melt it," I said. "Bram, you have a hairdryer and an extension cord. We get every heater in the building, we—"

"We tried that in '54," Bram said. "Fenn tried it. You can't melt a thought off a window, son. It comes back. It comes back *exact*." He rubbed his face with one broad hand. "No. What you do is you keep people away from it. You keep them from reading it. Because reading is halfway to singing, and singing—" he stopped.

"Singing is the rest of the way," Priya finished.

We all looked at her. She stood there in her coat over pyjamas, thirteen years old and holding herself together with visible effort, and she pointed at the nearest window—at the double-looped figure with the descending tick, the one Ada had called a motif, the one that opened every group.

"That one," Priya said. "Tomas hums it. Since he came back. Under his breath, all the time, in class. I thought it was nothing. I thought it was a habit." Her voice cracked and she pushed through it. "It's not a habit. It's homework. They sent him back knowing it, and he's been teaching it to us without knowing he's teaching it, and now it's on the windows because—" she swallowed, "—because it's nearly time, isn't it? It's revising."

Ada made a sound I'll remember for a long time—a music teacher hearing a student describe a composition with terrible accuracy.

Outside, the sky was graying toward dawn, and the frost began, gently, to fade. Not melt—fade, the way a screen dims. The marks thinned to ghosts, to suggestions, to nothing, leaving behind ordinary glass and an extraordinary chill, and the four of us stood in the emptying light like witnesses after an accident, none of us sure whether we'd imagined the speed of the car.

"Photograph everything," Ada said at last, hoarse. "Every window, every mark, before it goes. I'll transcribe what I can. And Xanthe—" she caught my sleeve. "There's a hymnal. My grandmother's. Hand-copied, 1893, from a congregation that isn't in any archive I've ever searched. I brought it to Hollowmere because the binding matched the chapel pews and I thought it was a nice coincidence." Her eyes were wet and furious. "I don't believe in coincidences anymore. There's a final verse in that book. Torn out. And I think I need to go find out why."

* * *

I spent my free period that day in the east wing classrooms, opening every window and running my palm an inch from every pane, feeling for cold. The glass was warm. Ordinary. October-mild.

But in classroom 4E, on the board, in the tray of dried-out whiteboard markers, someone had left a single marker uncapped overnight, and the nib had grown a fine rime of frost, and when I picked it up, the four notes hummed through the radiator beneath the window—once, softly, like a throat cleared before speech.

I capped the marker. I put it in my pocket, where it stayed cold against my hip all day, patient as a promise.

At lunch, Ada found me in the staff room and set a slim, cracked-leather book on the table between us, wrapped in a tea towel like something wounded. Her grandmother's hymnal. The torn edge of the missing page showed at the gutter, ragged, old.

"There's something else," she said. "Something I photographed this morning and haven't shown anyone. The last legible group of marks—the one on the top floor, right before the script stops resolving." She turned her phone toward me. "I ran it through the transcription twice. It's not neumes at all, that last bit. It's a different hand. Smaller. Shakier."

On the screen, magnified, the frost-marks resolved into something I recognized in my bones before my brain caught up: not notation. Letters. Curved, hurried, scratched into the frost from the *inside* of the glass.

Three words, written by someone who had been running out of time:

**DO NOT LEARN IT WHOLE.**
