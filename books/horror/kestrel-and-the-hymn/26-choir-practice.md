## 26. Choir Practice

The frost finished its work at 4:17 in the morning. I know because I was awake to see it, and because Ada Lindqvist, who had not slept either, called my room at 4:19 and said, in the voice of a woman reading her own autopsy, "It's done. Every window. Come look."

I came and looked.

The east wing's windows had been filling with notation for three days—curves and stems and rests blooming like frost ferns, one bar at a time, patient as ivy. Now every pane in the building held its completed fragment, and when I stood in the corridor and turned slowly, the whole school became a single score wrapped around me, a hymn written in ice on every surface that could hold it. Even the glass in the fire doors. Even, when I pressed my thumb to it, the small window in the door of the staff lavatory, which felt like a personal remark.

"It's the whole thing," Ada whispered. She had her grandmother's hymnal clutched to her chest like a life vest. "Every verse. The one from 1893, the fragments Bram's predecessor wrote down, all of it—completed. Except—" Her finger traced the final line of frost on the nearest pane. "Except the last verse is blank. There's a rest written where the melody should go. A held rest, a whole line of it." She looked at me. "It's waiting for someone to fill it in."

"Then it can keep waiting," I said, with more confidence than I felt. Confidence, in my experience, is mostly a costume you wear so fear doesn't recognize you.

* * *

By seven, Verity Crane had done the thing I'd been dreading: she'd cancelled lessons.

"Assembly at nine," she announced over the tannoy, her voice carefully level, the voice of a woman walking a tightrope over a canyon and pretending it's a hallway. "Years Seven through Eleven. Staff to attend. The hall is warm. The hall is safe."

Nobody believed the last two sentences. I could tell because the staff room, when I entered it, had the atmosphere of a lifeboat discussion. Bram sat with his arm bandaged and his hands wrapped around a mug of tea he wasn't drinking. Ada had the hymnal open on her lap. Priya sat on the radiator—still warm, still mercifully silent since the night the hymn had stopped pretending to be plumbing—and watched me with the flat, furious calm she'd worn since the night of the Calling, when we'd stood on the stairwell singing "Wonderwall" at the top of our lungs while six children walked past us like sleepwalkers with somewhere to be.

"So," Bram said. "She's gathering them."

"She's herding them," Priya said. "There's a difference. Herding's what you do when you've stopped pretending they're people."

"Priya," Ada said gently.

"No. She wrote the letter. We saw the letter. *Dear Choir, I beg you to consider an extension of terms.* She's been feeding them students for thirty years and now she's calling an assembly because the bill's come due and she wants everyone in one room where the singing can reach them all at once." Priya's voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it for cracking, and I pretended not to notice because that's what you do for people you love, you let them keep their dignity like a coat they can put back on.

"We don't have to go," Ada said. "We could take the kids we can reach and barricade the west wing—"

"The road's gone," Bram said. "Snow's ten feet up the pass. And you'd be penning ourselves in with the windows. With the score." He nodded at the frosted glass of the staff room door, where the notation gleamed. "It doesn't need a road. It needs voices."

I looked at the frost. And here is the thing I have not told you yet, the thing I'd been carrying since the night before, since I'd stood in the dark corridor with my palm flat against the cold and felt—not heard, *felt*, the way you feel a word forming in your own mouth—the hymn turn its attention toward me like a head turning.

It had stopped humming through the pipes. It was humming through *me*.

Four notes, over and over, in the marrow of my arms. And beneath the four notes, patient as erosion, the shape of the fifth thing it wanted. The held rest. The blank line.

"Okay," I said. "We go to assembly."

They all looked at me.

"We go, we sit near the doors, we watch who sings and who sways and who's already gone. And we do not—*any* of us—hum, tap, whistle, or so much as think in four-four time." I looked at each of them. "If this is a trap, I want to see the shape of it. I've spent my whole life watching rooms without being seen in them. It's the one skill I've got. Let me use it."

Priya's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "You're going to lurk at your own school's assembly."

"Lurking is a discipline," I said. "I have a black belt."

* * *

The hall at nine o'clock was warm, exactly as advertised, and the warmth was the worst part. Outside, the temperature had fallen so far that the snow squeaked underfoot and the flagpole wore a sleeve of ice. Inside, the radiators roared, the overhead lights blazed, and the air had the thick, sweet, slightly wrong quality of air that's been breathed too many times by too few lungs.

Verity stood at the lectern. She'd put on her good blazer. Her hands, folded on the wood, were steady in the way of hands that have been gripping something underneath the lectern, and I would have bet my contract they were gripping it hard enough to splinter.

The returned ones sat in the front two rows. Tomas Ellery, and the two girls, and the others—the ones from the Night of the Calling, the six who'd walked through our nonsense songs with their eyes shut and their faces smooth as eggs. Eleven of them now. Eleven children who came back wrong, sitting with their spines straight and their hands in their laps, and when Verity said "Good morning," they answered "Good morning, Miss Crane" in a unison so perfect it made the hair on my arms stand up and salute.

The rest of the school filed in behind them. Two hundred students, stamping snow off their boots, whispering, elbowing, being gloriously, ordinarily adolescent. I sat at the end of the back row with Priya on my left and Ada on my right, Bram standing by the double doors because he said his leg needed the wall, and I did what I have always done: I watched, and made sure nothing watched me back.

Verity read the notices. Fire drill rescheduled. The library would close early. The excavation works were suspended until spring. Each announcement landed in the hall like a stone dropped down a well, and the well swallowed it, and the returned children in the front rows did not fidget, did not pass notes, did not exist in the same acoustic space as the announcements at all.

And then Verity said, "We'll end, as always, with the school hymn."

The organist—poor Mr. Fenn, who had taught here longer than Verity and played with his eyes shut—struck the opening chords. And two hundred children stood, and opened their mouths, and sang.

I'd heard the school hymn a hundred times in my weeks at Hollowmere. Dull Victorian thing, all hills and duty and shadows of the evening. I'd hummed along in the back row with the sincerity of a man who'd learned early that not singing draws attention.

Except it wasn't the school hymn.

The words were the same. The tune had *moved*. It had shifted by intervals I didn't have names for, slid sideways into a shape I'd heard threading through the radiators at two in the morning, and the children sang it without hesitation, without a single stumble, as though the hymn had always been this, as though the hymnals were the ones that had drifted. Half the hall swayed. Not the returned ones—they sat rigid, mouths moving, eyes forward. It was the *ordinary* children who swayed, the ones with snow in their hair and chewing gum under the pew, swaying in rows like wheat, like reeds, like anything that bends when the wind asks it to.

Ada's hand found my sleeve. Her knuckles were white around the hymnal.

"It's the same descant," she breathed. "From last assembly. But it's in the *melody* now. It's climbed."

Beside me, Priya's lips had parted. I saw the first vibration start in her throat—that awful involuntary hum, the hymn reaching into her chest and plucking—and I gripped her wrist, hard, and she gasped and bit down on the sound, and her eyes when they met mine were terrified and furious and alive.

"I've got you," I said, which was a stupid thing to say, because I didn't, nobody had anybody, but her jaw clenched and she nodded and the hum died in her throat like a swallowed ember.

The hymn rolled on. And underneath the children's voices—underneath, in the floor, in the radiators, in the frost-scored glass of the high windows, which had begun, very softly, to *sing along*, a sound like a wet finger on a wineglass played by a thousand invisible hands—the deeper melody rose to meet them.

The ground shook.

Not dramatically. Not the movie earthquake, no chandeliers swinging. It was a tremor, low and sustained, the kind of shudder a building gives when something very large and very patient turns over in its sleep. Dust sifted from the ceiling roses. Mr. Fenn's organ wavered on its foundations and produced a chord no organ should know. The frost on the high windows flared white, every line of notation burning bright for a moment, and the whole hall—the walls, the floor, the air itself—became a resonating chamber, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Hollowmere School had been built as an instrument. Every corridor a pipe. Every hall a soundbox. A hundred years of children's voices, term after term, assembly after assembly, tuning it.

The hymn ended. The children sat. The ordinary ones blinked and looked around, puzzled, the way you do when you come out of a daydream, and a boy in Year Eight said "what verse was that, miss, it's not in the books," and Verity said, in a voice like a cracked bell, "It's an old arrangement. Sit down, Daniel."

And the eleven in the front rows turned their heads.

All of them. Together. A movement with no beginning and no end, one motion shared by eleven necks, and they looked—not at Verity, not at the doors, not at Bram standing rigid by the exit with his bandaged arm pressed to the wall like a man holding back a tide.

They looked at me.

I have been overlooked my entire life. It is the one thing I am good at. Teachers marked me absent while I sat in the third row. Foster mothers set a place for me and walked past me to answer the door. I once spent an entire weekend in a house where the family had, without noticing, locked me out, and I sat on the step until Monday and no one ever knew. Invisibility is not a superpower. It is a scar tissue, and it has kept me alive in rooms where being seen would have cost me everything.

Eleven children looked directly at me, in a hall of two hundred people, and every one of them smiled.

It was the smile Tomas had worn when he came back. Even, complete, arriving all at once like a light switched on. And their mouths moved, all eleven, in perfect unison, no sound coming out, and I read the words as easily as if they'd been shouted, because I had seen them already, written in frost across every window in the school:

*The rest. The held rest. Sing.*

"No," I said. Out loud. In a silent hall. Two hundred students turned to look at the strange substitute teacher at the back who had just said no to nobody, and I didn't care, because eleven faces in the front rows had turned back around with the smooth synchrony of a flock, and the organ had begun, all by itself, to sound the four notes.

The four notes. Over and over. And beneath them, in the shaking floor, the shape of the fifth thing.

"Out," Bram said at the door, not loudly, and it carried anyway, the voice of a man who'd spent thirty years keeping lights on. "Ada. Priya. Out now."

But I stayed one second longer, because I needed to see it, and I did see it, the last piece, the thing that turned dread into certainty. The frost on the high window above the lectern flared again, and in the blank line of the held rest, in the space where the final verse's melody should have been, a single note appeared—written in ice, blooming like a fern—and it was a note I recognized, because it was *my* range, my tessitura, the pitch my voice had fallen into on the stairwell the night I'd shouted myself hoarse singing nonsense at the dark.

The hymn had been auditioning me for weeks. The pipes, the door, the calling in my name. It hadn't been gathering children. The children were the *choir*. A hymn a century unfinished, a congregation sealed behind its own door, a final verse with a lead line written into it—and a school built as an instrument, waiting for a voice.

Waiting for the voice of a woman it had caught and could never make sing. Waiting for her blood. My grandmother had been preserved, unheard, as punishment—or as *stock*. And I was the harvest.

The ground shook again, longer this time, and somewhere beneath us, behind the iron door with its bolts thrown from the inside, something vast drew breath.

"Mr. Okonkwo," Verity said from the lectern. Her face was grey. Her eyes were wet. And her voice, when it came, was the voice of a woman reading terms and conditions she'd signed thirty years ago. "They've asked that you stay behind after assembly."

The doors boomed shut behind Bram, Ada, and Priya. All of them. Every exit. The frost-scored glass of the fire exits bloomed white, and through the white I could see, faintly, the notation completing itself around the doorframes—bars and rests and a long, held, final line.

Eleven children rose from the front rows and began, softly, to sing.

And the last verse—the one torn from a hymnal in 1893, the one hidden in a foundation wall, the one I had read aloud in a library while the lights guttered—rose in my skull, complete, in my own voice, already sung.

The trap hadn't sprung. The trap had been sprung for a hundred years. I had simply, finally, walked into it.
