## 30. His Grandmother's Voice

The stairs went down farther than the school had foundations.

I counted them out of habit—counting being the one prayer I'd ever been any good at—and lost the number somewhere past a hundred and forty, somewhere around the point where my breath started coming back to me warm and tasting of iron and old candle smoke. The dark below was not empty. It was *occupied*. You could feel it the way you feel a full church before you see it: that pressure of held attention, of many bodies arranged toward something.

Warm dark, I'd thought from the top of the stairs. I'd been right, and I hated being right. Cold makes sense. Cold is just weather. But this warmth rising out of the earth like breath through floorboards—that was the warmth of a room where people have been sitting for a very long time, breathing, waiting, *singing*, and the walls have absorbed all of it and can't let any of it go.

My grandmother's name was Comfort Okonkwo.

I know. Comfort. Her own mother had named her that in 1931, in a house with a leaking roof and too many mouths, and she had apparently decided early on that if her name was going to be an instruction, she might as well follow it. She was the only person in my entire tangled foster-file history who had ever been described in writing as "a joy to have." One social worker wrote it. One. I've had sixty years of paperwork and exactly one sentence of praise, and I could recite it under torture.

She died—or the file said she died—in 1968. I was four. I don't remember her. What I remembered instead was a smell of nutmeg and coal smoke, which is not a memory, it's a rumor of a memory, and I had built half my personality on it anyway. That's what orphans do. We take the smallest splinters of evidence and build whole cathedrals on them, because the alternative is standing in an empty lot.

I was about to walk into her cathedral. Literally, as it turned out.

* * *

The stairs ended at an archway, and the archway opened into a hall that should not have fit beneath Hollowmere School, because Hollowmere School sat on a hill, and hills do not generally come with vaulted ceilings ribbed like the inside of a throat.

Candles burned along the walls. Not recently lit candles—candles that had burned down to pools of wax and kept burning anyway, flames standing on nothing, on faith, on whatever this place ran on instead of oxygen. The light they gave was the color of old photographs, sepia and forgiving, and it fell on rows of figures.

They were kneeling. Hundreds of them, arranged in a horseshoe facing a raised stone dais at the far end, and every one of them was singing.

That's what I understood first, before I understood anything else: they were singing. The hymn—the hymn, the four notes that had threaded through Hollowmere's pipes since before I arrived, the melody that had pulled Tomas Ellery and six others down corridors in their sleep—came off these figures in slow waves, and it was *beautiful*, and I want you to understand how much I hated that. It was beautiful the way a trap is beautiful when you're looking at it from outside. Four notes, then a fifth that resolved them, then silence, then again. A century of patience compressed into eight seconds of music.

And the singers were stone.

Not statues. Statues are honest about what they are. These were people who had been caught mid-hymn by something slower than death—a petrification that had taken its time, working outward from the voice. Their mouths were open. Their throats were ridged and gray, and the stone there was different from the stone of their hands, finer-grained, almost crystalline, as though the song itself had mineralized inside them and spread. Some were more finished than others. A woman near the front still had the soft look of weathered limestone in her cheeks; you could believe she'd been singing yesterday. A man three rows back was nearly all granite now, his cassade of robes fused to his body, only his eyes—

His eyes were wet.

Stone does not weep. Whatever was left of him did, two bright tracks cutting channels through the mineral crust of his face, refreezing, cutting again. He was aware. They were all aware. That was the punishment, I realized, and my stomach turned over slowly, like something rolling in its sleep. The hymn hadn't killed them. The hymn had *kept* them. Preserved them as instruments, tuned and strung and unable to stop playing, conscious behind the stone for a hundred years while their voices poured out of them without permission.

I had spent my whole life wanting to be unheard. These people had been heard so completely that hearing was all they had left.

"Hello," I said, because I am, at core, a substitute teacher, and substitute teachers address rooms even when the rooms are wrong.

The hymn faltered.

Just for a beat—one skipped rest, one held breath across two hundred stone throats—and in that gap I felt the whole hall *notice* me. Not see. Notice. The way a net notices the shape pressing against it. The candles leaned, all together, like grass under wind. Somewhere above me, faint through a hundred feet of earth and frost-riddled masonry, I could hear the school's own answering hum stutter and search for its place, like a choir whose accompanist has stood up and walked out.

Then the hymn resumed, and I understood that my noticing had been noted, catalogued, and filed for later. There would be a later. There was always a later, here. Later was the entire business model.

* * *

I walked the rows.

I'm not brave. Let's be clear about that. My knees were doing a percussion section of their own, and every instinct I had honed over fifty-eight years of being overlooked was screaming at me to flatten myself against the wall, to become furniture, to be no one until the danger got bored. But that was the thing, wasn't it? That was the whole inheritance, laid bare at last. My invisibility wasn't a shield anymore. Down here, in a hall built entirely out of listening, being no one was the same as being raw material. The Choir didn't overlook people like me. It *collected* us. Grandmother Comfort had been marked 'unheard' in the registers, and 'unheard,' I now understood, was not a compliment. It was a label on a jar.

So I walked the rows, and I looked at faces, because someone had to, and because some stubborn Okonkwo part of me refused to let these people be scenery.

They were villagers. That was the detail that broke me a little, quietly, row after row. Not monsters, not martyrs in robes—just people. A man with a farmer's sun-cracked hands frozen mid-clasp. A girl of maybe sixteen whose stone lips were parted on a high note, a ribbon still in her hair, gone brittle as autumn leaf. An old fellow whose hymnal lay open on the floor before him, its pages turned to stone mid-flip. They had come down here in their Sunday clothes in 1923 or whenever it was, to sing, and the song had closed its hand.

I found myself doing the thing I do with registers. Reading names that weren't written anywhere. Making up lives for them—*you grew beans, you argued with your sister, you liked the descant best*—because the alternative was to see them as what the Precentor saw them as, which was parts.

And then, in the fourth row back from the dais, on the left side, near the aisle—

A woman knelt alone.

Not with the others. Set slightly apart, the way you seat someone at a wedding whom the families are feuding over. She was further along than most of them—the stone had reached her shoulders, her jaw, the delicate architecture of her ears—but her face was still hers, still flesh enough to hold an expression, and the expression was the worst thing in that entire underground country:

She was listening *disapprovingly*.

Head tilted, brow faintly furrowed, mouth pressed to a line beneath the creeping gray. Even entombed in a century of enforced harmony, this woman looked like she was mentally correcting everyone's pitch and finding them all wanting.

I knew her.

I had never seen her, and I knew her. I knew her the way you know a face from the other side of a mirror—the set of the mouth that was my mouth, the heavy brows that were my brows, the particular stubborn angle of the jaw that every foster-mother had once described, in my files, as "defiant." The nutmeg-and-coal-smoke rumor of memory stood up inside me and put on its coat.

"Comfort," I said. My voice came out cracked down the middle. "Comfort Okonkwo."

The hymn stumbled again. This time it didn't recover cleanly. Around the hall, stone heads began—slowly, with the grinding patience of glaciers—to turn. Not toward me.

Toward *her*.

Because I had said her name, and names, down here, were still currency, and I had just spent one loudly in a room full of creditors.

Her eyes moved. Just her eyes, in the stone cage of her face, and they found mine, and I watched a hundred years of solitary confinement reorganize itself around a single point of contact. Her eyes were brown. Mine are brown. Hers had lines around them from squinting into West African sun and then Yorkshire rain, and they were wet, and the tears cut little clean channels down through the mineral bloom on her cheeks, and I thought: *she has been crying in the dark since 1954 and nobody has been able to see it, and the first person who sees it is a substitute teacher who never existed.*

"I'm—" My voice failed. I tried again. "Xanthe. I'm—I'm Ruth's boy. Your daughter's son."

Something happened in her face. I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe it. Grief and relief and fury and love, all trying to use the same eleven remaining muscles at once, all of them fighting the stone that had taken her jaw and her throat and everything she would ever need to answer me.

She couldn't speak. Of course she couldn't. That was the design. The unheard are preserved unheard; the hymn had taken her voice the day she refused to give it, and had kept her here ever since as a warning exhibit, a lesson in consequences, propped in the fourth row where the Precentor could gesture at her during sermons. *This is what refusal looks like.* A hundred years of being made an example of.

But her mouth moved.

The stone had her jaw, but it had not quite taken her lips—they were gray and stiff as old rubber, but they moved, with the agonizing slowness of a mechanism running against its own corrosion, shaping soundless syllables at me. I crouched. I knelt in the dust of a century in my good trousers, close enough to see the candlelight moving in her wet eyes, and I read her lips the way you read a register, carefully, twice, because getting a name wrong is unforgivable.

First she said—shaped, rather—my name. *Xanthe.* Testing it. And then her face did something complicated and fierce, and she shaped it again, and I realized she wasn't saying it, she was *correcting* it, the way she must once have corrected my mother's pronunciation, the way grandmothers correct everything, and I laughed, one wet broken note of laughter in a hall of stone singers, and several candles guttered.

Then her lips moved again, and this time it was one word, and she gave it to me the way you press a key into a child's hand before the soldiers reach the door—fast, hard, final, with everything behind it.

*Broken.*

I frowned. "Broken? Broken what, I don't—"

Her eyes flicked upward. Toward the surface. Toward, I understood dimly, the east wing, the frost, the windows writing their notation, Ada Lindqvist's photographs, the torn page Priya had pulled from the library wall wrapped in oilcloth. Then back to me. And she shaped the word a third time, slower, and added a second word after it, and the second word was:

*Learn.*

Learn it broken. The marginal note in Ada's grandmother's hymnal, 1893, in faded copperplate: *Do not learn it whole. Learn it broken.* I had thought it was advice. A caution. A health-and-safety notice for the soul.

It wasn't advice. It was a message. It was a message sent forward through seventy years by a woman who had known she would never get to deliver it herself, tucked into a torn-out page and a marginal note, addressed to whoever came after her—whoever inherited the unhearing. She had known someone would come. She had spent a century in stone betting on it.

"You're telling me," I said, and my voice shook, and I let it, because there was no one down here left to perform steadiness for, "that the verse has to be sung wrong. On purpose. That's the way through. That's what you did. That's why you're—" I gestured helplessly at her, at the stone, at the fourth-row exile. "That's why they couldn't take your voice properly. Because you learned it broken, and it couldn't harmonize with you, so it *kept* you instead."

Her eyes closed. Opened. One tear, fresh, cutting a new channel.

Yes. It was the most expensive yes in the history of communication.

Around us the hymn swelled, and I felt the hall begin to pay attention again—the candles straightening, the pressure returning, the great patient net drawing its fibers taut. Up on the dais, half-glimpsed in the candle-shadows, something tall shifted its weight. I did not look at it. Looking at it, I suspected, was a transaction, and I was not currently in a position to negotiate.

"Listen to me," I said to my grandmother, fast, low, leaning in until my forehead nearly touched the cool gray of her brow. "I'm going to get you out. All of you. I don't know how yet, and honestly the odds are terrible, and I've never successfully rescued anyone in my life—I once lost a class on a museum trip—but I have a girl upstairs who reads everything, a music teacher with a contraband hymnal, a caretaker who fights with electricity, and between us we have misfiled more paperwork than God. So you hold on. Do you hear me? You have held on for seventy years; you can hold on for one more night. Hold on, and sing nothing, and stay unheard, and wait for me."

Her lips moved one last time. Not *broken*. Not *learn*.

Three words, shaped slowly, deliberately, each one placed like a stone in a wall:

*You. Sound. Like. Sunday.*

Four words. Fine. Grandmothers don't count syllables, they count meanings. And I don't know what it means to sound like Sunday, except that I suddenly, desperately wanted to, and except that I was crying, openly, in front of two hundred petrified congregants, and not one of them judged me, because they had all been crying for decades and knew exactly the technique.

I pressed my hand flat against her stone shoulder. It was warm. Everything down here was warm, and that remained the single most horrifying fact of the entire establishment.

"Sunday," I promised. "Got it."

* * *

I was three steps back toward the stairs when the hymn stopped altogether.

Not faltered. Stopped. Two hundred stone throats fell silent at once, and the silence was worse than the singing, the way a held breath is worse than a scream, and in that silence every candle in the hall bent toward me like a jury leaning forward.

From the dais, a voice—not a voice, a chord pretending to be a voice, many voices braided into one shape—said my name. Both of them. The one I use, and the one on the 1950s register, and then, softly, almost tenderly, a third name I had never heard before but recognized instantly, the way you recognize a family resemblance:

My grandmother's name for me. The one she'd have used. The one no document recorded.

The chord said it like an invitation. Like a seat being pulled out at a table.

And behind me, in the fourth row, I heard—not heard, *felt*—Comfort Okonkwo begin, against every rule of her imprisonment, against a hundred years of enforced silence, to move her lips one more time.

Not at me.

At *them*.

She was singing, silently, soundlessly, in the only way left to her—and from the twist of her gray lips and the furious light in her wet brown eyes, I could tell it was gloriously, magnificently, deliberately *wrong*.
