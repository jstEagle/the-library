## 33. The Unmaking of the Verse

The hymn broke against us the way a wave breaks against a sea wall—enormous, furious, and, in the end, merely water.

I didn't understand that yet. Standing there in the vaulted dark beneath Hollowmere School, with Priya's lullaby cracking in the air beside me and my own voice doing violence to a verse that had waited a century for its perfect completion, I understood only one thing: it was working. The wrongness was working.

The Precentor had stopped conducting.

That was the first sign. For all the time I'd been below—minutes or hours, I genuinely couldn't say—it had stood at the head of its congregation with its arms raised, half shadow and half score, fingers moving through the cold like a man stirring soup. Every gesture had pulled sound out of the stone-singers, out of the taken children swaying on the stairs behind me, out of the very pipes in the walls above. The hymn hadn't been sung *by* anything. It had been conducted *through* everything.

Now its arms hung at its sides. Its face—if you could call that arrangement of frost and intention a face—was turned toward me, and it was listening. Actually listening. Trying to find the place in its harmony where my voice fit.

It couldn't find one.

Because there wasn't one. That was the whole point. Ada had taught me the final verse broken: intervals flattened where they should have soared, rests held so long they became their own kind of note, a melody that kept promising resolution and then shrugging and wandering off into the wrong key entirely. My grandmother's word. *Broken.* The hymn could absorb any voice that tried to join it. It could not absorb a voice that refused to be absorbed—a voice that knew the song and sang it wrong on purpose, the way you might know someone's name and refuse to say it.

"You want the last verse," I sang, off-key, holding a rest three beats too long, "but you can't have it whole."

The congregation shifted. Stone faces cracked—not breaking, but moving, the way old paint moves when the wood beneath finally breathes. And from somewhere inside those cracked faces came a sound I will hear until I die, and I mean that as a compliment.

They were laughing.

* * *

You have to understand what laughter means when it comes from people who have been singing for a hundred years.

The stone-singers—the original congregation, the ones who had built this vault and sealed the door from the inside and given themselves to the song until the song gave them back as architecture—they were still in there. That was the horror of it, and also, it turned out, the hope. Sustained by borrowed voices, held in a state of half-sung, half-stone, they had been aware the entire century. Aware and unable to stop. Every child Verity fed them, every 'transfer,' every borrowed voice had kept them conscious inside their own petrification, singing harmony they never chose.

And now here was a man singing their sacred verse like a drunkard mangled "Auld Lang Syne," and something in them—something that had been waiting since 1893 for exactly this—recognized freedom when it heard it.

The laughter started low, a grinding in the stone like a glacier clearing its throat. Then it spread. Face after face cracked wider, and the sound came up out of them, and it was terrible and joyful and completely unharmonized. Laughter is like that. You cannot conduct laughter into four parts. It refuses arrangement. It is the most human noise there is precisely because it cannot be made to behave.

The Precentor's frost-face turned among its congregation, seeking the source, and found all of them, everywhere, laughing.

"No," it said.

It had a voice. Of course it did. It sounded like a choir trying to speak as one person, which is to say it sounded like nothing you'd ever want to hear alone in a room. But underneath the braided voices, for the first time, I heard something else. Something thin and frightened.

A single voice. The first singer, maybe. The one who had started it all, who had taught the congregation the verse that would make them eternal and discovered too late that eternity, done properly, requires an audience that never gets to leave.

"No," it said again, and raised its arms, and pulled at the hymn with everything it had.

* * *

Above us, faintly, through a hundred feet of earth and stone and bad Victorian brickwork, I felt the school answer.

The pipes caught the hymn and carried it. The radiators in the east wing began to thunder with it. Somewhere overhead the frost on the windows must have been completing itself—I'd learn later that every pane in the building had written its final notation at once, every curved mark sliding into place like a lock accepting its key—and the whole structure of Hollowmere groaned toward the vault below, offering itself, offering its children, offering its substitute teacher who had spent his whole life learning how to disappear.

The hymn came down around us like weather.

I felt it try to take my voice. That's the only way I can describe it—a pressure at the back of the throat, a sweetness, a promise. *Sing it right,* it said, in the voice it had used at the door, the voice that knew my name and my grandmother's name. *Sing it true and you'll never be overlooked again. Every room you enter will know you. Every register will hold your name. We will write you into the music permanently.*

And God help me, some part of me—that part that had spent forty years being forgotten by foster mothers and registers and colleagues who looked straight through him—some part of me leaned toward it like a plant toward a window.

Priya hit the note that saved me.

Not literally—she wasn't singing the hymn, she'd never sing the hymn—but she was standing next to me singing her mother's lullaby, the one in her grandmother's language, the one about a river that carries things home, and the melody collided with the hymn's pull and shattered it around me like ice underfoot. She grabbed my hand. Her palm was freezing and her grip was fierce.

"Keep singing badly!" she shouted over the rising sound. "It's the worst thing I've ever heard! Don't you dare improve!"

"I would never," I sang, flat where I should have risen, and the hymn shuddered.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing Ada must have known when she copied her grandmother's marginal note into her own memory: the hymn was not a creature. It was a *structure*. A chord that had learned to hunger. And a structure can withstand assault, siege, silence even—but it cannot withstand a voice inside it that belongs to no part of it. I was not attacking the hymn. I was *dissonance*. I was the wrong note sustained past all endurance, and the longer I held it, the more the whole architecture strained to accommodate me, and the more it strained, the more it revealed the cracks that had always been there, hidden under a century of perfect harmony.

The cracks were where the stolen voices lived.

* * *

Tomas Ellery was the first to come out of one.

He was standing three rows back among the taken, smiling his even smile, humming his descant, when the dissonance reached him and the smile simply fell off his face like a mask slipping its wire. He looked down at his own hands. He looked at the stone-singers. He opened his mouth and what came out was not the hymn.

"Mum?" he said.

Just that. One ordinary, cracked, terrified, thirteen-year-old syllable, and it did more damage than everything I'd sung combined. Because the hymn could harmonize with anything sung *to it*, but Tomas hadn't sung to it. He'd spoken *past* it, to a woman in a house in town who thought her son had transferred, and the hymn had no part written for maternal love, no rest long enough to hold it, no interval wide enough to contain it.

He said it again, louder. "MUM!"

And the other returned ones broke like ice on a pond in spring—one, then two, then all of them, the even smiles collapsing into the raw faces of children waking from a dream they can't describe, children who want their parents, who are cold, who are *scared*, and whose fear is the most gloriously unmusical sound in the world.

The hymn lurched. I felt it stumble the way you feel a staircase missing a step.

"Keep going!" Ada's voice, from the top of the stairs—she'd come down, brave mad woman, folk tune in her throat, some Swedish thing about a summer meadow that had no business in a frozen vault and therefore worked perfectly. Behind her, Bram's football chant, slurred and triumphant, echoing down the stairwell off-key in six different directions at once because Bram Oduya had never once in his life found a key, let alone stayed in one.

The single voice became a ragged chorus. Lullaby and folk song and football chant and Priya's river-song and my broken verse, all of it colliding, none of it agreeing, and the hymn—perfect, patient, hungry hymn—found itself trying to harmonize with chaos and failing, failing, failing.

* * *

The Precentor screamed.

I want to get this right, because it matters. It did not scream in rage. Rage has a direction; this had none. It screamed the way a building screams when its foundations give—not because it chooses to, but because everything it is made of is coming apart at once. And because it was made of voices, it screamed in four-part harmony: soprano wailing above, alto keening, tenor pleading, bass roaring underneath like a cave-in. Four parts, perfectly balanced, perfectly tuned, screaming *in agreement*, and somehow that was worse than any discord. Even its agony had to obey the rules. Even its death was arranged.

The frost that composed it began to go.

It started at the edges—the hem of it, if hem is the word, the trailing fringe where robe became air—and sublimated upward, not melting but *unwriting*, turning to glittering dust that rose instead of fell, drawn up toward the vault ceiling like snow falling in reverse. Underneath the frost, I glimpsed things I won't fully describe: a suggestion of vestments, a suggestion of a face, a suggestion—worst of all—of relief.

Because here is what I saw in the last moment before the dust took the face entirely. The Precentor looked at its congregation. At the stone-singers, laughing. At the children, crying for their mothers. At me, singing wrong. And the braided voice, unraveling thread by thread into its component singers, said—not to me, maybe not to anyone—

*"We only wanted the verse finished."*

And then it was frost, and then it was dust, and then it was nothing at all, and the hymn collapsed inward like a star giving up its argument with gravity.

* * *

The sound of a hymn dying is not silence.

It's the opposite of silence. Every note it had ever sustained came due at once—a century of borrowed voices, all called home simultaneously, pouring back out of the structure and into the world. The vault filled with them. Voices in languages nobody at Hollowmere spoke, voices singing snatches of songs from 1893, a woman's voice calling a name—*Edith, Edith, supper's on*—a man laughing at a joke a hundred years stale, a child practicing scales badly. All of it released, all of it rising, passing through us like wind through wheat, and I stood in the middle of it with tears freezing on my face and my heart breaking in the good way, the way it does when something long imprisoned goes free.

The stone-singers came apart gently.

That's the truth, however strange it sounds. As the hymn's hold dissolved, the stone left them the way frost leaves a window when the sun finds it—from the edges inward, softly, without violence. What remained were not bodies, exactly. A century is a long time. What remained was what the hymn had no use for and therefore never touched: the mortal parts, the tired parts, the parts that had wanted supper and sleep and their children's faces. Dust, mostly. Dust and, here and there, a wedding ring. A hairpin. A child's shoe, small enough to cup in one hand.

One figure lasted longer than the others.

She was near the front, where the honored places were, and as the stone slid off her I saw her clearly for the first time—an older woman, gray-haired, with cheekbones I had seen every morning of my childhood in the bathroom mirror and never known why they haunted me. Her eyes found mine across the vault. They were not afraid. They were not even sad, exactly. They were the eyes of someone finishing a very long shift.

Her mouth moved. No sound came out—her voice had been taken a lifetime ago, the price of being 'unheard'—but I read it the way you read lips across a noisy room, easily, instantly, the way family reads family.

*Take me up,* she mouthed. *Into the light.*

And then she, too, was dust, settling softly onto the vault floor in a shape that was almost, almost a resting person, and I knelt down in it, still singing—because I couldn't stop, because the broken verse was the only prayer I knew now—and gathered what remained of my grandmother into my hands.

Behind me, the iron door began to scream.

* * *

I've heard hinges complain before. Everyone has. This was different. This was a door that had been sealed from the inside for a hundred years, bolts thrown by desperate hands, hinges fused by frost and time and the sheer weight of everything it had been keeping in—and now, with the hymn dead and the pressure of a century's held breath suddenly released, the door wanted *out* the way a lung wants air.

The hinges shrieked louder than the Precentor had. Louder than the hymn. A long, rising, metallic scream that went on and on, and underneath it I swear I could hear the door itself—iron doesn't have a voice, but iron remembers stress, and this iron had been holding its breath since Queen Victoria's funeral—groaning out its century of tension in one enormous exhalation.

The bolts drew themselves back. I watched it happen, kneeling in my grandmother's dust with Priya's hand on my shoulder, and the bolts moved slowly, grinding, reluctant even now, as if the door couldn't quite believe it was allowed.

Then it swung open—all the way, wide, hospitable, an ordinary door doing an ordinary thing—and cold clean air poured down the stairs from the world above, smelling of snow and diesel and, faintly, impossibly, the canteen's burnt porridge.

Bram appeared silhouetted in the doorway, backlit by the emergency lights he'd rigged, one arm hanging wrong and his grin absolutely intact.

"Well," he said, surveying the vault, the dust, the weeping children, the substitute teacher kneeling in ashes. "About time somebody cleaned this place out."

And above us, through the open door, through a hundred feet of school and stone, the first gray light of dawn was beginning to touch the windows of the east wing—and the frost on every pane was already, quietly, melting away.
