## 4. Descent Protocol

The recorder went in first.

Wendell lowered it on a length of paracord, down through the gap behind the boiler room wall where the survey gap opened like a throat, and let it hang there for a full minute before she touched anything. Old habit. Ten years of urban exploration had taught her that buildings lie with their surfaces and tell the truth with their air. She watched the little red light pulse in the dark, listened through her headphones to the sound of her own breathing amplified into something tidal, and told herself this was procedure. This was protocol. This was a woman doing her job.

She had spent three days building the protocol. That was the part she didn't say out loud, even to herself. Three days of not going back, which was three days longer than she'd ever stayed away from the Kestrel sub-basements since she'd found the unmapped tunnel two weeks ago. Three days of telling herself the fresh airflow meant nothing — a cracked foundation vent, a thermal differential, some mundane plumbing of the city's breath through old concrete. Three days of sleep interrupted by the sense of a question being asked somewhere below her floorboards, patient as water finding a crack.

On the third night she'd caught herself standing at her apartment window at 2 a.m., looking east toward the Kestrel Building's silhouette against the harbor haze, and understood that she wasn't staying away out of caution.

She was rationing it.

So: the protocol. Headlamp primary, backup lamp clipped to her harness, chemical light sticks — six of them, cracked and dropped at intervals like breadcrumbs, because batteries died but phosphorescence was honest. The recorder on its cord. A second recorder in her jacket pocket. A paper map, hand-drawn, because she didn't trust her phone down there anymore; the compass needle had begun to wander near the tunnel's far end, sulking like a cat that wanted to be elsewhere. Chalk. Water. Her multi-tool. And taped inside her left wrist guard, where no one would ever think to look, a folded page torn from the 1974 probate index — the Kestrel trust, the sealed file number, the name Ada written in a clerk's faded ink.

She hadn't decided why she'd brought that yet. She told herself it was reference material.

* * *

The tunnel took her the way it always did, swallowing sound until her own footsteps arrived late, like an echo embarrassed to be associated with her. Forty feet past the boiler room the modern building ended and the older one began — brick footings, hand-hewn timber, a section of wall that predated every map Wendell had ever pulled from the city archive. The airflow came from ahead, steady and cool, carrying something faintly sweet underneath the mineral smell of wet limestone. Not decay. Sweeter than that. Like apples going soft in a cellar.

She marked her first light stick at the junction where the tunnel forked — except it hadn't forked before. On her previous visits the passage had run straight for sixty yards and ended in a collapse of rubble. Now the left branch opened onto a corridor lined with glazed brick, greenish-white, the kind you saw in hospital basements from the forties. The right branch was new too, narrower, sloping gently downward.

Wendell stood at the fork for a long time, listening to the recorders hum.

"Okay," she said aloud, because saying things aloud kept them ordinary. "Okay. I missed a turn. People miss turns."

She had not missed a turn. She had mapped this passage twice, once by laser measure. There had been no fork. But the alternative explanations were queuing up behind that one, and none of them were better, so she took the wider branch — the glazed-brick corridor — and dropped a second light stick at its mouth.

The corridor descended in a long, gentle ramp, and the sweetness thickened. Her headlamp picked out details she catalogued compulsively, the way she catalogued everything: mortar joints finger-smooth with age, a rusted conduit running along the ceiling into darkness, a doorframe without a door, then another, then a third, each frame bricked over but painted — actually painted, in enamel the color of cream, with numbers stenciled above them. 4. 5. 6. Rooms. Sub-basement rooms, numbered, finished, intended for use.

No survey showed sub-basements here. No survey showed this building having more than two levels below grade. And yet someone had numbered doors down here, and painted them, and hung conduit for lights that were long gone.

At doorframe seven the corridor turned, and the room was there.

* * *

It wasn't hidden. That was the thing she would keep returning to afterward, turning it over like a stone with something alive under it. It wasn't concealed behind a loose panel or down a crawl she'd overlooked. The doorway simply stood open at the end of the ramp, and warm light came out of it — actual light, yellowish, lamplight-colored — and the wallpaper ran right up to the frame as if the room had been built into the corridor rather than found behind it.

Wallpaper. Flocked, cream and rose, a repeating pattern of small birds. A baseboard. A braided rug just inside the threshold, lying flat, corners square, as though someone had smoothed it down that morning.

Wendell stopped ten feet away and made herself do the inventory, because the inventory was real, the inventory was measurable, the inventory was the rope she could hold onto while everything else went sideways.

A bed, single, iron-framed, with a chenille spread the color of butter. A dresser, walnut, doilies under a hairbrush and a jewelry box. A desk with a child's schoolwork squared on its surface, pencil laid parallel to the paper's edge. A bookshelf: Nancy Drew, a Bible, a yearbook. A rocking horse by the window — a window, a real sash window with curtains, and beyond the glass not earth or concrete but a soft amber glow, sourceless, like afternoon light through honey.

1974. Every object said 1974. Not reproduced, not staged by some artist with a grant and a sense of humor. *Kept.* The difference was in the wear — the hairbrush's bristles bent in the arc of a girl's hand, the spread's corner turned back as if someone had meant to get in and changed her mind, the pencil sharpened down to a stub and still in use.

And arranged. Everything arranged neatly, facing the door, angled toward it, the whole room oriented like a face waiting to be looked at.

Wendell's mouth had gone dry. She became aware of her own heartbeat the way you become aware of a stair that isn't where your foot expected it.

"This isn't on any survey," she whispered. Her voice fell into the room and did not echo. The room received it. Held it.

She should leave. Every protocol she'd written, every rule of a decade underground, said the same thing: when a space contradicts the record, when a space is *finished*, you document from the threshold and you go. She raised the camera anyway, hands steady in the way of hands that have decided to be steady, and took four frames. The shutter clicked. In the viewfinder the room looked almost tender. Almost safe. A museum diorama of a childhood, lovingly lit.

Then she noticed the desk drawer was open an inch, and inside it, visible in the lamplight, was a spiral notebook — and propped against the notebook, facing outward, was a photograph of a girl about nine years old, dark-haired, squinting into summer sun, and beneath the photograph, in a child's careful cursive, a single line:

*for the lady who keeps asking questions.*

Wendell did not remember deciding to run. She registered it afterward in fragments: the camera strap jerking at her neck, the braided rug sliding under her boot, the amber light stretching long and wrong down the corridor behind her, the light sticks glowing where she'd dropped them like a trail of small cold fires. She ran uphill through the glazed-brick corridor, past the numbered doorframes, past the fork, and the whole time the sweet apple smell followed her, close, attentive, and underneath her own ragged breathing and the slap of her boots there was another sound keeping pace.

Not footsteps. A voice. Dry, patient, unhurried — the voice from her sleep recordings, the voice that answered questions in her own cadence — and this time it wasn't answering anything.

This time it said, clearly, distinctly, at the exact moment her hand closed on the boiler-room ladder:

"Wendell."

Just her name. Nothing else. Said the way you'd say the name of something that already belonged to you.

She climbed. She hauled herself through the gap behind the boiler, dragged the panel shut, and sat on the concrete floor of the present day, gasping, alive, above ground level, in a building that was only a building. It took her several minutes to notice what was missing.

The recorder. The one on the paracord — she'd never reeled it back in. Somewhere between the fork and the room, the cord had gone slack, cut or released, and the little machine with its pulsing red light was still down there. Still hanging in the dark of the corridor. Or worse — she thought of the desk, the notebook, the photograph placed carefully facing outward — still *arranged*, somewhere, in the room with the birds on the wallpaper, set neatly among a dead girl's belongings, angled toward the door.

Kept.

Wendell got home past dawn. She showered until the water went cold. She did not sleep; she sat at her kitchen table with her laptop and the backup recorder from her jacket pocket and told herself she would review the audio in daylight, rationally, and find the flutter of a draft, the tick of cooling pipe, the ordinary machinery of an old building settling.

The file was intact. Two hours and eleven minutes of her own footsteps and breathing, the chalk marks, her whispered inventory at the threshold.

And then, forty seconds after the sound of her running, after the ladder, after the panel closing — a pause, and the dry patient voice, recorded clean, close to the microphone, as if it had been brought up the ladder with her, as if it had been speaking directly into the machine the entire climb:

"Wendell. You forgot something."

A silence, five seconds long.

Then, very softly, in a cadence that was almost — almost — her own:

"Come back for it. I'll keep the light on."

The recording ended there. Wendell sat in the gray morning light with her headphones on, listening to the last five seconds again, and again, and understood with a clarity that felt like falling: the room hadn't taken the recorder by accident.

It had accepted the offering.

And it knew her name.
