## 5. The Keeper's Terms

The first time it came to her in a reflection, she was brushing her teeth.

Ordinary Tuesday. Ordinary bathroom light, the bulb over the mirror that buzzed when the humidity rose. Wendell had stopped sleeping at her apartment — she'd been rotating between Mara's couch and a motel on Delancey for six days — but she'd come back to shower and change clothes, because a person couldn't live out of a duffel forever, and because some stubborn part of her still believed the apartment was hers.

She spat foam into the sink and looked up, and the woman in the mirror was still brushing.

Not moving. Not mimicking. Just standing there with the brush against her teeth, looking back at Wendell with an expression of mild, terrible patience, like someone waiting for a bus that was always late.

Wendell didn't scream. That surprised her later. What she did was raise one hand, slowly, and watch the reflection not raise its own. The fluorescent light stuttered. When it steadied, the mirror held only her — gaunt, unwashed, holding a toothbrush like a weapon.

"You're not real," she said to the glass.

The drain gurgled. It sounded almost like laughter.

* * *

After that, it stopped pretending to be rare.

It appeared in the dark window of the laundromat while she waited on a load of clothes, standing behind her reflected shoulder though no one stood behind her actual one. In the black screen of her phone when it powered down. Once, horribly, in the polished toe of her boot as she sat on Mara's couch, a small warped face looking up at her from the floor.

Mara noticed the weight loss first. Then the way Wendell flinched at windows.

"You look like you haven't slept in a month," Mara said, setting down two mugs of tea Wendell wouldn't drink. "And don't tell me it's the building again."

"It's the building," Wendell said.

"Wendell."

"Something is in the building, and now something is—" She stopped. There was no sentence that ended well. "I found something under it. Something from 1974. And I think it wants me to fix it."

Mara stared at her for a long moment. Her sister had always been the practical one, the one who paid bills on time and called their mother every Sunday, and Wendell watched her decide, visibly, between alarm and patience.

"Fix what?"

"I don't know yet." That much was true. "But it's teaching me."

* * *

The dreams started three nights later, and they were not dreams so much as screenings.

The first one: a cellar, low-ceilinged, lit by a single bulb on a drop cord. Three men stood around a table covered in tools nobody was using. One of them was young Harold Finch — she recognized him from the archive photographs, the trust filings, the grainy news clipping about the Kestrel estate — but younger, thirty maybe, with a mustache he'd lose by 1980. He kept wiping his hands on his trousers even though his hands were clean.

The second man was heavyset, sweating through his shirt, saying, "We move her tonight. We agreed. Tonight."

The third man stood apart, near a door with a padlock on the outside of it, and did not turn around, and said nothing, and somehow his silence was the loudest thing in the room.

Behind the locked door, something small made a sound. Not crying. Worse than crying — the patient, rhythmic tapping of a child who has decided that if she just keeps making noise, someone will remember she exists.

Wendell woke with her hand pressed flat against the motel room door, as if she'd been trying to hold it shut from the inside.

The second dream was the same cellar. Same men. But the padlock was open, hanging loose, and the door stood ajar onto darkness, and Harold was saying, "Nobody has to know. That's all I'm saying. Nobody ever has to know," and the third man finally turned around—

—and his face was the face from her bathroom mirror. Her face. Patient. Mild. Waiting.

She woke up tasting concrete dust.

* * *

On the ninth night she didn't fight it. She lay down in her own apartment for the first time in weeks, recorder running on the nightstand out of pure habit, and let the dream take her.

This time there were no men. There was only the room — her room, the wallpapered one beneath the Kestrel Building, the one that hadn't existed until it did — and the room was talking.

Not in words. In arrangements. The child's belongings shifted while she watched: a tin whistle moved two inches left; a hair ribbon coiled itself tighter; a small leather shoe rotated so its toe pointed at the wall where, if walls could be read like documents, something was written.

"What do you want?" Wendell asked aloud, in the dream, and heard her own voice come out flat and doubled, the way voices sounded on her corrupted recordings.

The room answered with a date. She felt it rather than saw it: *1974*, pressed against her like a hand on the back of her neck. Then another sensation, colder: *return*. Then, last, laid over her like a shroud, so gently she almost missed its meaning:

*or stay.*

She woke at 4:17 a.m. with the terms fully formed in her mind, the way you wake remembering a phone number. Return what was taken. Or become what waits.

"That's it?" she said to the dark ceiling. "That's the whole deal? No instructions?"

The radiator ticked. Somewhere below her floor, impossibly far below, a tin whistle played three notes.

* * *

She tried to negotiate. She wants that on the record, whatever record survives. She was an urban explorer; exploration had rules — never go alone, tell someone your route, leave nothing, take nothing. Rules were how you stayed alive in places that wanted you dead. Surely the thing under the Kestrel Building understood rules.

She went down on the eleventh day, wired for light and sound, backup batteries, a paper map in a ziplock bag. She'd replaced the recorder the room kept with a cheap one from an electronics shop, and she carried it like a talisman.

The tunnel to the room was longer than before. She measured it against her pace count and came up forty meters of extra corridor that had not existed in any survey, any memory, any prior visit. The wallpaper in the room was unchanged — faded roses, water stain shaped like a reaching hand. The child's belongings sat in their neat arrangement, and now, among them, there was a new object.

Her missing recorder. Sitting in the center of the child's quilt like an offering returned, or a claim staked.

"Okay," Wendell said. Her voice worked. That was something. "Okay. I know what you want. Ada Kestrel. Taken in 1974. You want her back — her remains, or the truth, or both. Fine. I'm working on it. I found the probate file. I found the withdrawal signature. I have a name, Harold Finch, and I will find the other two—"

The lights dimmed. Not flickered — dimmed, evenly, like a held breath.

"—but I need terms," she pressed on, because stopping felt fatal. "Real ones. If I bring her back, I walk away. Both of us. You release me. You release anyone connected to me. My sister stays out of this. Agreed?"

Silence. The good kind or the bad kind, she couldn't tell.

"And I want the ten years back," she heard herself say, and hated how fast it came out, how greedy. "My archive. Everything the room took. That's my price for doing your digging."

The temperature dropped so fast her breath bloomed white. On the quilt, the objects began to move — not toward her, just rearranging, the shoe turning, the ribbon uncoiling, the whistle sliding — and Wendell understood, with the cold clarity of someone reading a contract written in a language she'd only pretended to know, that she had just asked the wrong question in exactly the right way.

The room showed her a fragment. Quick, brutal, like a slideshow frame: the cellar, 1974, the third man finally speaking, saying *we protect our own names first, we always have,* and the other two nodding, and the padlock clicking shut.

Then another fragment: herself, eleven days ago, standing in the archive, feeling the thrill of discovery pulse through her like a drug, thinking *nobody else knows this, nobody else can touch this, it's mine* —

"No," she said. "That's not the same thing."

The room rearranged itself around her disagreement. The wallpaper pattern shifted; the roses turned, very slightly, all at once, like heads.

It was not the same thing. That was the lesson. It was exactly the same thing. A decade underground, hoarding maps and recordings, keeping the Kestrel tunnels secret even from the city, even from Mara — *mine, mine, mine* — she had been practicing for this room since before she knew it existed. The room hadn't chosen her at random. It had chosen the only person in fifty years who kept coming back.

"Fairness," she said aloud, and her voice cracked on it. "You don't do fairness."

The tin whistle played three notes. It sounded, this time, less like a threat than like agreement.

* * *

She climbed out into the November afternoon shaking, and sat on the loading dock steps behind the building until her legs worked, and called the county archive from a payphone she found on Halsey Street because she no longer trusted her cell phone's black screen.

"Probate division, this is Dolores."

"Dolores. Wendell Voss. I was in last week, the Kestrel file. I need everything you'll give me on the sealed probate — and I need to know if there's a second copy of the missing-person report. The withdrawn one. Filed February '74, pulled within the week."

A pause, papers rustling. "Honey, that file's sealed by court order. Signature of the presiding—"

"I know whose signature. I've seen it." She closed her eyes. Behind her eyelids, the cellar. The padlock. "Dolores, the man who signed that withdrawal still owns the building. He's alive. And I think there are things buried under his property that a nine-year-old girl's family has been owed for fifty years."

Another pause, longer. When Dolores spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice of a woman deciding something.

"There's a second copy. Intake clerks keep carbons, always did. Mine's in a box in my garage, and I've been wondering for fifty years why I never threw it out." A breath. "The name on the report isn't just Ada Kestrel. There's a witness line. Somebody reported her missing before the family did. Somebody who withdrew it the next day, scared."

"Who?"

"A boy. Sixteen years old. Housekeeper's son." Papers again, then stillness. "Name's Ellis Voss."

The payphone's cord swung slightly in the wind, though there was no wind.

"My father," Wendell said.

"He worked for the Kestrels, honey. Until February of '74. Then the whole household scattered like birds." Dolores's voice gentled. "I always wondered what scared them all so bad."

Wendell hung the receiver on its hook without hanging up, and stood a long time in the cold, listening to the dial tone, which sounded — she would swear this later, and Mara would swear she was lying — like breathing.

Her father had been in that house. Her father might have been in that cellar. Her father had died four years ago believing, apparently, that some things stayed buried.

The room had known. The room had been showing her the third man's silence all along, patient as a mirror, because the silence was hers by inheritance.

She picked the receiver back up.

"Dolores. I'm coming by tomorrow. And then I'm going to find Harold Finch."

From somewhere inside the payphone's dead handset, faint and dry and satisfied, a voice that was not Dolores said:

*Terms accepted.*
