## 2. Doors Counted

The funeral director had offered Silas a box for her grandmother's effects—"whatever you don't want to take home, we can arrange disposal"—as if Astrid Eriksen's entire life could be summarized in a single cardboard cube and hauled off like yard waste. Silas had declined with a politeness she didn't feel. She would sort the farmhouse herself. It was the last thing she could do for either of them: the grandmother who had raised her through summers of rhubarb pie and hard rules, and the brother who was currently lying in a casket wearing a stranger's face.

She started in the parlor on Tuesday morning because it was the least haunted-looking room, which is to say the curtains were open.

The house had that particular silence of places where someone has recently stopped being loud. Astrid had been a hummer—hymns mostly, off-key, with conviction—and the absence of the humming sat in every room like unclaimed luggage. Silas moved through it with a garbage bag in one hand and a growing suspicion in the other that she was not so much cleaning as trespassing.

The journals were in the cedar chest under the window seat, wrapped in a flour-sack towel embroidered with a bird that might have been a kestrel or might have been a very ambitious chicken. There were eleven of them. Cloth-bound, water-stained, dated from 1961 to 2021—the year before last. Sixty years of handwriting, starting in careful schoolteacher cursive and ending in a scrawl that looked like it had been written during an earthquake.

Silas made coffee, sat on the floor with her back against the chest, and opened the earliest one.

*March 4, 1961.* The first entry was almost domestic. *Baked. Lars fixed the fence. Counted doors: 14.*

Counted doors. She flipped forward. *April 12. Counted doors: 14.* *May 30. Doors: 14. Chairs: 6.* Every entry, without exception, ended with a count. Doors. Chairs. Sometimes names.

*Doors: 14. Chairs: 6. Names: 3 (Astrid, Lars, Wren Sr.).*

Her grandfather had been Wren too. Her brother had been named for him, which everyone said was sweet and which now felt like handing a kid a lightning rod and calling it a family heirloom.

By 1974 the counts had grown teeth. The entries got shorter, harder, written in a hand pressed deep enough to emboss the next three pages.

*Doors: 15. One extra. Did not open it. Chairs: 7. Names: 2.*

Silas read that one four times. *One extra.* Not "I miscounted." Not "Lars added a door to the barn." *One extra. Did not open it,* as though the door were a dog that had wandered onto the porch and the correct response was to pretend it wasn't there until it went away.

She kept reading as the light moved across the floorboards. The counts became the whole entry, then the whole page, then pages of nothing but numbers in columns, dates marching down the margin like a metronome nobody wanted to keep time with anymore:

*Doors: 14. Chairs: 6. Names: 1.*
*Doors: 14. Chairs: 6. Names: 1.*
*Doors: 14. Chairs: 6. Names: 1.*

For years. Decades. A woman alone in a farmhouse, counting her own life down to its smallest safe number, every single day, like a prayer or a password or a spell against something that only understood arithmetic.

The last journal ended in October of 2021. The final entry was three words long, and Silas sat looking at them for a long time after she'd finished reading everything else.

*It knows Wren.*

* * *

She called the county clerk's office at noon to ask about the body again and got the same cheerful non-answer she'd gotten twice already. "Misidentification happens more than you'd think, ma'am. We're reviewing." Reviewing what, she'd asked. The paperwork? The corpse? The man at the morgue had looked at her with the specific exhausted kindness of someone who has seen something he plans to drink about later, and said, "Ma'am, I've worked here nineteen years. That body came in with your brother's fingerprints."

Fingerprints. Dental records. The scar on his chin from falling out of the hayloft when he was nine. Everything matched Wren. And yet the face—that was the part she couldn't say out loud without sounding insane—was wrong in a way that no document could capture. It was Wren's face rendered by someone who had only ever heard him described. All the parts present and accounted for, assembled by a committee that had never seen him laugh.

She put the phone down and told herself, firmly, that grief did strange things to perception. Then she counted the doors in the downstairs hallway. Fourteen. She wrote it on the back of her hand in pen, felt immediately ridiculous, and did not wash it off.

* * *

Mrs. Halvorsen arrived at four o'clock with a covered dish and the expression of a woman delivering both.

"Lefse," she announced, setting it on the kitchen table like a peace treaty. "You'll eat. You've been living on coffee and spite since you got back, I can tell."

"Mostly coffee," Silas admitted.

Greta Halvorsen was seventy-some years old, built like a root cellar, and had known Silas since before Silas had opinions. Her farm shared a property line with the Eriksens' going back further than anyone bothered to remember. Her husband Torvald waved from the truck—a man of few words, most of which were "yep"—and their granddaughter Petra, fifteen and all elbows and dark suspicious eyes, stayed in the cab with a book, watching the house the way cats watch vacuums.

"You shouldn't be staying out here past dark," Mrs. Halvorsen said, not looking up from arranging the lefse with unnecessary precision. "Not this week."

"It's my house, Greta."

"It's a house," Mrs. Halvorsen allowed. "That's different."

Silas laughed, and Mrs. Halvorsen didn't, and the laugh died in the room like a bird hitting a window.

"I found my grandmother's journals," Silas said, because the silence needed filling and because some part of her had been waiting two days for permission. "She counted things. Doors, mostly. Every day for sixty years."

Mrs. Halvorsen's hands went still over the dish.

"Do you know why?" Silas asked.

"I know your grandmother," Mrs. Halvorsen said carefully, "was a careful woman. Careful people count." She straightened up, wiped hands that weren't dirty on an apron that didn't need it, and looked at Silas with an expression that was almost—almost—pity. "Lock your bedroom door tonight. And if you hear counting, don't count along."

"What?"

"Petra!" she called toward the truck, voice suddenly bright and normal. "We're leaving! Sun's getting low!"

At the door she paused, half-turned, and said, quietly, without turning all the way around: "Your grandma never once let the sun set while she was alone in that house. Sixty years. Think on that." Then she was gone, and the taillights crawled down the drive, and Petra's face in the rear window watched Silas until the trees took the truck.

* * *

Night came the way it does in October—suddenly, and with intent.

Silas locked the bedroom door as instructed, mostly to prove she wasn't scared, and lay on top of the covers in her clothes listening to the house settle. Old houses talk. She knew this. This house talked more than most, but she'd spent childhood summers here and had catalogued its vocabulary: the furnace's asthmatic wheeze, the kitchen chair that sighed when the temperature dropped, the attic boards that popped like knuckles.

What she hadn't catalogued was the sound coming from downstairs at eleven forty.

Click.

Soft. Deliberate. A latch.

Click. Another one.

She lay rigid, doing math she didn't want to do. Click. Click. Click. Steady as a heartbeat, working its way through the house—through the hallway, past the parlor, along the back rooms—and Silas realized with a cold, crawling certainty that she was listening to doors opening. One after another. In sequence. Like someone walking the house and politely letting each room out.

She should stay in bed. Every instinct honed by every horror novel she'd ever edited said stay in bed. But Wren had been the brave one and she'd been the stubborn one, and stubbornness got her feet on the floor and her hand on the lock before her fear finished filing its objection.

The hallway was dark. Moonlight came through the stairwell window in a pale wedge, and in that wedge she could see, at the far end of the hall, the bathroom door standing open.

That was fine. Doors stood open. Houses were full of open doors; it was practically their whole personality.

Except she had walked this hallway a hundred times in four days, and the bathroom door stuck—it had stuck since 1998, Astrid had complained about it every summer, *that door wants to be closed, Silly-girl, let it have its way*—and it was open now, wide, easy, swinging gently on hinges that had apparently been oiled sometime between sunset and now.

She checked the others. The linen closet: open. The spare room: open. Downstairs, every door in the house hung open exactly the width of a hand—not flung, not ajar, but *positioned*, each one held at the same precise angle, as if the house were taking a breath and holding it.

Fourteen doors. She counted them twice, walking the whole circuit with her phone flashlight shaking in her grip. Fourteen, and every one of them open by the same exact measure, and when she got back to the foot of the stairs she stood there breathing hard and understanding, finally, what her grandmother had been counting all those years.

Not how many doors there were.

How many doors were supposed to be there.

Because she'd read the 1974 journal. *Doors: 15. One extra. Did not open it.* And standing in the moonlit hallway, Silas turned slowly and looked at the end of the corridor, where the wall met the wood, and let her flashlight find it.

There was a door there.

There had never been a door there. She had walked past that wall four days running; it was plaster and wallpaper and a framed photo of Grandpa Lars holding an enormous fish. The photo was gone. The wall was gone. In its place stood a door—tall, dark, older than any other door in the house, its wood grain swirling in patterns that hurt slightly to look at directly.

It was closed. Unlike the others, it was closed, and it was the only thing in the silent house making any sound at all, because from behind it, faint and patient and horribly familiar, came humming.

Off-key. Hymns. With conviction.

Grandma Astrid's tune—but slowed down, stretched, sung by something that had learned the melody from outside a window and thought it had the shape of it right.

Silas backed up the stairs without turning around, the way you back away from a dog you've decided not to trust. In her bedroom she locked the door, put her back against it, and slid down to the floor with the journals hugged to her chest like armor made of paper.

On the back of her hand, the ink had smudged. She looked at it anyway. Fourteen doors, she'd written.

Below it, in her own handwriting—which she had definitely not written—someone had added, in small, neat letters:

*Fifteen now. You counted along. They always count along eventually.*

Down the hall, unhurried, the humming continued. And somewhere beneath it, so soft she could almost pretend she'd imagined it, something began to count names.
