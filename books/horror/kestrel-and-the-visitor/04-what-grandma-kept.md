## 4. What Grandma Kept

The funeral was postponed twice, which should have been my first clue that Wren's death was going to be as uncooperative as his life. He'd been the kind of kid who hid from the school photographer, who once faked a fever for three days because he'd promised a girl he'd take her to a dance and lost his nerve. Even dead, he was dodging me.

The county finally released the body on a Tuesday, and I spent Wednesday in the funeral home's back office with a man named Dale who kept touching his tie like it might apologize for him.

"Miss Eriksen, I want to be upfront with you," Dale said. "The identification was made by dental records. Your grandmother's dentist, actually—Dr. Pruitt, retired, but he kept the files. And then the family, uh. The family requested we hold off on viewing."

"The family being my grandmother, who has been dead for six years."

"Your brother, then. Before."

"Wren requested we not view his own body."

Dale's tie got a lot of touching. "The county can't explain it," he said finally, in the tone of a man who had rehearsed this in the shower and still hated it. "The dental work matches your brother. The DNA—" He turned his monitor toward me like he was showing me a puppy. "The DNA doesn't. It's not a match to your family line at all. Not a relative. Not anyone in the system."

I looked at the screen for a long time. The stranger's profile sat there, patient as a spider.

"So the man in the casket," I said, "is nobody."

"Is nobody," Dale agreed, relieved to have it said out loud.

"And he has my brother's teeth."

Dale did not touch his tie so much as cling to it. "We've had, uh. We've had stranger requests for the embalming, honestly. You wouldn't believe—"

"I believe everything now, Dale. It's my whole personality."

I signed the papers. I took custody of my brother's body, which was not my brother's body, and drove it out to the farmhouse in a rented van with the radio off because every song sounded like it was being sung at me.

* * *

The farmhouse had been sealed since Astrid died—windows papered with sun-bleached newspaper, the porch swing still swaying in a wind I couldn't feel. I'd spent the first two nights in the guest room because my old room's door wouldn't stay shut, and I'd told myself it was the foundation settling. Houses settle. Houses are allowed.

On the third day I started on the attic, because grief is just housework with worse music, and someone had to decide what to do with sixty years of a dead woman's belongings.

The journals were in a cedar chest under the window, under a quilt I recognized from every winter of my childhood. There were dozens of them. Composition books, mostly, the cheap kind, their covers soft as cloth from handling. I opened the nearest one expecting recipes or a garden log.

*Doors: 9. Counted twice. The back porch door is not a door. Do not count it. Chairs: 7. Names: 4. Astrid. Henrik. Wren. Silas.*

I sat down on a trunk that groaned like it had opinions.

Every journal was the same. Decades of them. Doors, chairs, names—counted and recounted, the numbers sometimes struck through and rewritten, sometimes underlined so hard the pen had torn the page. In the margins, in Astrid's tight, upright handwriting, little notes accumulated like frost:

*Do not let them learn their names are counted.*

*The chair is not furniture. Stop looking at the chair.*

*Henrik says I'm being morbid. Henrik has never been hungry in November.*

I laughed. It came out wrong, thin and high, the laugh of a woman alone in an attic with a cedar chest full of her grandmother's madness. Except it wasn't madness, was it. Because last night I had counted nine doors in a house that had eight when I was a child, and this morning the count had been ten, and the extra ones were always open exactly one inch, breathing out cold air that smelled like apples going soft.

The earliest journal was dated 1974. Its cover was water-stained, the pages swollen, and the handwriting inside was younger—looser, still carrying whatever accent Astrid had worked so hard to sand off her English. I carried it downstairs, built a fire in the parlor stove because the house had been cold in a way that felt structural, and read it while the light died outside.

* * *

*March 1974.*

*The blizzard took Henrik's mother on the 9th and the twins on the 11th and Papa on the 14th, and by the 20th we had buried four and could not dig the fifth grave because the ground was iron. I have not slept. The house makes sounds like a man chewing.*

*March 23rd.*

*I went out to the orchard because I could not be inside one more hour. The trees were white to the top and every branch held snow like it was holding its breath. I stood among them and I said, out loud, that I would do anything. I said it to God first, and when God did not answer I said it to the orchard, which was foolish, and it answered.*

*It said: I do not want your anything. I want a schedule.*

*March 24th.*

*I have made a bargain and I am writing it down so that some future fool—I, I mean, I am the future fool—cannot pretend she did not know the terms.*

*It keeps the house standing. It keeps the cold off the land, keeps the well sweet, keeps the wolves in the far hills. In return: one hidden, one given. Harvest moon to harvest moon. One of us it will not touch—the hidden one, the kept one. The other we bring to the orchard when the moon is full and fat, and we give them over, and the year turns, and we live.*

*I asked it what happens to the given one. It said: they are not wasted. I asked it again and it said, in my mother's voice, Sila, do not be ungrateful.*

*March 30th.*

*Henrik says I dreamed the whole thing. Henrik says a lot of things. Henrik is sleeping with the axe by the bed now, so. Draw your own conclusions.*

*I am with child. We will name it for whichever of the dead we miss most. And when it is born, we will know which one it is—the hidden or the given—and we will love it exactly the same, because it will not know until it is old enough to count.*

*God forgive me. The orchard says God is not a party to this arrangement.*

* * *

I read that last line four times. The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, a door that I had personally closed swung open with its one-inch sigh, and I found I didn't have the energy to be startled anymore. The house and I had reached an understanding: it would do its noises, and I would do my reading, and we would both pretend this was normal until one of us broke.

The journal kept going. Astrid's handwriting aged with the pages—looser, then tighter, then cramped into the counting that filled the later books. I skipped ahead, hunting. The births. My father, Tomas, born 1976. My aunt Lise, 1979. And then the entries where the counting stopped being about doors.

*September 1981.*

*The moon rose full at 7:41. Tomas was the hidden this year. Lise was the given. I walked her out myself because Henrik could not. She held my hand the whole way and asked me if it would hurt and I lied to her, which is the one kindness I had left to give.*

*The orchard took her gently. I will say that. It is not cruel. It is only hungry, and hunger is not the same as cruelty, though I could not explain the difference to Lise and I cannot explain it to myself.*

*One hidden, one given. Tomas lives. The farm lives. Next spring we plant.*

*September 1990.*

*It is Tomas's year and he ran. He got as far as the county road before the truck he'd flagged down brought him back, driver white as milk, saying the boy had simply vanished from his passenger seat at the property line. The moon set. The count is short.*

*I paid it myself. I am too old to be the given and it knows it, and it took me anyway, or tried to. I woke in the orchard at dawn with frost on my eyelashes and a year taken off the far end of my life, and the orchard said, in my mother's voice: we will hold the debt. We are patient. But the next one who runs pays double, and the next after that pays for everyone.*

*So. We hold the debt.*

That was the phrase. *We hold the debt.* It appeared again in 2003, again in 2011, again in the last journal—the one from Astrid's final year, the handwriting gone thin as thread:

*Wren and Silas are grown and gone to the city, God keep them there. The debt is held. The orchard reminds me every autumn, standing at the fence line in the dark, patient as winter. It has started calling them by name.*

*If I die before I settle this, and one of them comes home—the count will come due. One hidden, one given, and the house will decide which is which. It has always decided. I only ever did the paperwork.*

I closed the journal. The fire had burned down to a red, thoughtful glow. Outside, full dark, and the orchard at the edge of the yard stood in it like an audience.

Wren had come home in April. Wren had run, once, years ago—God, he'd run from everything, that was the whole shape of him—and now the debt was held no longer. Now the count was overdue. Now there was a stranger in a casket wearing my brother's teeth, because something had come back in his place, or something had been sent, or—

The kitchen door, at the back of the house, opened its one inch.

And from the direction of the orchard, faint but unmistakable, carried on air too still to carry anything, I heard someone humming. A tune I knew. The stupid song Wren used to hum when he was nervous, the one from the cereal commercial, the one I'd never once heard Astrid sing.

Except this was Astrid's voice. Older, and patient, and coming closer along the fence line, one slow step at a time, like it had all the time in the world and every year of mine besides.
