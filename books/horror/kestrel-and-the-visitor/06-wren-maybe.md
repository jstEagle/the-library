## 6. Wren, Maybe

I had started talking to the house. That's what four days alone with the journals will do to a person. I'd catch myself narrating—*okay, kitchen door, you stay shut now*—and then apologizing to the door when it obeyed. Mrs. Halvorsen had taken to calling every evening at six to check that I was still vertical, and I had started to look forward to those calls the way sailors used to look forward to lighthouses, which tells you something about the state of me.

So when I saw the figure at the orchard fence on the fifth evening, standing in the long gold light like he'd been carved out of it, my first feeling was not fear.

It was joy. Stupid, total, whole-body joy, the kind that starts in the sternum and takes the legs with it.

"Wren," I said, and I was already moving, already crossing the yard with my boots half-unlaced because I'd run out of the house the way you run toward a fire you've been told is impossible.

He was on the other side of the fence, hands hooked in the wire, and he looked—God, he looked *good*. Sunburnt and thin, a scrape healing along his jaw, wearing the flannel shirt he'd left crumpled in the truck in April. His hair had gone shaggy. His smile was the crooked one, the one where the left side got there first.

"Hey, Sil," he said. His voice was rough, like he'd been shouting into wind for a long time. "You look terrible. Have you been sleeping in grandma's house? Because you look like you've been sleeping in grandma's house."

I laughed. It came out of me like something breaking loose, and I pressed both hands over my mouth because I was suddenly, stupidly close to crying.

"Where have you *been*," I said. "Wren, they buried somebody. They buried somebody with your *face*. I identified you. I signed papers. I picked a—Wren, I picked a *casket*—"

"I know." His smile faded into something apologetic and tired. "I know, and I'm sorry, and I'll explain everything, I swear. But I can't do it out here. I need you to open the gate. I need to come inside."

My hand was already on the latch. Of course it was. Twenty-eight years of muscle memory, of opening gates for him, of letting him in—into rooms, into trouble, into my life. My fingers were actually on the cold iron when the light shifted.

The sun was dropping behind the orchard, and his shadow came out long across the grass, ahead of him, toward me.

Except it wasn't ahead of him.

The shadow was behind him. Sitting.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left now. The shadow of a standing man, thrown by a low sun, should have been a standing shadow, stretched long and thin across the yard. Wren's shadow was folded. Seated. Elbows on knees, the posture of someone settled comfortably, patiently, in a chair.

There was no chair.

There was a high-backed chair in the photographs, though. The one that hadn't existed, that crept closer every time I took a picture of a picture, that Mrs. Halvorsen had burned her copy of with hands that shook so badly she'd scorched her own thumb and hadn't noticed. The chair where Wren used to sit.

The shadow sat the way that chair would hold someone.

My hand came off the latch like the iron had bitten me.

"Wren," I said, and my voice did something I didn't authorize—it stayed warm. Steady. Big-sister warm, the voice you use when your little brother has climbed somewhere he shouldn't and you need him to come down slowly. "Why don't you tell me how you got out here first. Walk me through it. You were in Bellingham in April, and then—"

"Sil." He laughed, and it was his laugh, the exact pitch of it, the little snort at the end when he found something funnier than he wanted to admit. "Come on. You're doing the thing. The interrogation thing. You used to do it when I came home past curfew, stand in the doorway with your arms crossed like a tiny cop."

"Answer the question, tiny cop's jurisdiction."

"Fine." He rolled his eyes—his eyes, that exact shade of river-mud brown—and leaned on the fence. "I got a ride as far as Colfax with a guy hauling seed potatoes. Walked the last six miles. My phone died outside Endicott, which is why nobody could reach me, which I know sounds like the world's flimsiest alibi, but you *know* me. When have I ever been where I said I'd be?"

That was true. That was so true it hurt. Wren had the punctuality of weather.

"Okay," I said. "Okay. And the funeral home, Wren. The body. The man with your face. Explain that."

Something flickered across his expression—there and gone, fast, like a bird past a window. Not guilt. Something older and more patient than guilt.

"I don't know," he said simply. "I swear I don't. I heard about it from a guy at a truck stop, that they'd found me, that there was going to be a funeral, and I came as fast as I could. Sil, I've been walking for *days*. I'm so tired. Please open the gate. It's getting dark."

It was getting dark. The light was going amber to copper to something with rust in it, and the orchard behind him had gone quiet in the way the orchard had been going quiet all week—no birds, no wind, the trees holding their breath.

And his shadow still sat in a chair that wasn't there.

I made myself smile. I made my voice do the warm thing again, because whatever was at that fence, it wanted to be let in, and I was not going to let it know that I knew.

"You look like you've lost twenty pounds," I said. "Come around to the porch, I'll make you something. There's soup. Grandma's recipe, which means it's mostly butter, but—"

"I can't come around," he said, and the patience in his voice had a hairline crack in it now, something moving underneath. "The gate, Sil. Open the gate."

"The gate's not latched," I said.

He looked down at the latch. It wasn't latched. It had been swinging open all week because the hinges were rusted through and I hadn't fixed them because fixing things felt like agreeing to stay.

He looked at the open gate. Then he looked at me, and the smile came back, and it was his smile, perfect, and something in my chest went cold all the way through, because a thing that could not cross an open gate did not need the gate open. It needed *me to open it*. It needed the invitation. It had always needed the invitation. Astrid's journals were full of doors standing open by exactly one, and I finally understood that the house hadn't been counting doors.

It had been practicing.

* * *

I kept talking. I don't know how I kept talking. My whole body wanted to run and my whole heart wanted to stay, and those two things had gotten into a fight somewhere behind my ribs and were wrecking the place.

"Tell me something," I said. "Tell me something only you'd know. So I can—so I can be sure. You understand, right? After everything. I have to be sure."

"Sure," he said, easy, warm. "Ask me anything."

"The summer I broke my arm."

He didn't even blink. "You were nine. We were climbing the big maple behind the Halvorsens' place, the one with the tire swing, and I dared you to go out on the limb over the creek, and you fell, and I carried you home on my back the whole way even though you were heavier than me because you wouldn't stop crying and Petra was screaming for her mom." He shook his head, fond and rueful. "Mom was so mad. Not about the arm—about me carrying you. She said I could've hurt us both worse. You got the cast signed by everybody at school. You cried when they took it off."

Every word true. Every word a knife, because I had chosen that memory on purpose, and I'll tell you why, and then you can judge me.

There was a second version of that day. The real version. In the real version, I hadn't fallen off the limb over the creek. I'd fallen off the *lower* limb, the boring one, because I'd slipped on wet bark like an uncoordinated nine-year-old, and I had been so humiliated by the lameness of it that I'd begged Wren—*begged* him, made him swear on the tire swing—to tell everyone I'd fallen from the high one. The dramatic one. He'd kept the secret for nineteen years. He'd told it wrong at every party, every campfire, every time, the *right wrong way*, because that was the deal, that was our thing, the lie we'd built together and maintained together, the one memory in the world that existed only because the two of us had agreed to falsify it.

Anyone could know the true story. Grandma's journals, family lore, God knew what else. But only Wren knew the lie. Only Wren would tell it wrong in exactly the right way.

He had.

He passed. He passed perfectly, and I stood there in the dying light with my hand nowhere near the latch, and I understood that this was the worst thing that had happened to me yet, worse than the casket, worse than the doors. Because I had built a test to tell me whether to hope, and the test said *hope*, and the shadow said *no*, and one of those was lying, and I could not for the life of me figure out which.

Unless.

Unless it had gotten the memory out of him. Out of the real Wren, wherever he was—out of the man in the orchard, held since spring, every secret poured out slow. Unless the thing at the fence was wearing my brother's memories like the flannel shirt, like his face, like his laugh, everything but the one thing it couldn't fake.

The shadow shifted. Crossed its seated legs. Waited.

"Sil," the thing said gently, kindly, in my brother's voice, with the sun going down behind it and the orchard silent as a held breath. "It's cold out here. Open the gate."

And I did the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than the casket, harder than anything that came after. I looked at my brother's face and I said:

"Not tonight."

The smile stayed exactly where it was. That was the worst part. It didn't fall. It didn't twist. It just stayed, patient as furniture, and behind it the seated shadow leaned back, comfortable, unhurried, a thing that had been fed on this family for a hundred years and knew exactly how long a person can stand in a yard saying no.

"Tomorrow, then," it said. "I'll be here."

And it turned and walked back into the orchard, and the trees closed behind it like water, and the shadow stayed a moment longer than the man did—sitting, waiting, in the chair that was not there—before the dark took that too.

I stood in the yard until full dark, and then I walked to the porch, and I picked up the phone, and I called the Halvorsens, and when Mrs. Halvorsen answered I said, "It knows my brother's secrets. All of them. Even the ones only he and I knew."

There was a long silence on the line. Then, very quietly, in the voice of a woman who had been forbidden to warn me her whole life and had warned me anyway:

"Then it's been talking to him, Silas. Which means he's still alive. And which means, God help us, it's getting ready to make you a deal."
