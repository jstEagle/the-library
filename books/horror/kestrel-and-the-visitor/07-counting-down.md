## 7. Counting Down

Petra counted the family at dinner the way she'd counted them her whole life: four. Dad at the head of the table, Mom to his left, little Anders across from her building a fortress out of mashed potatoes and peas, and Petra herself by the window, where she could see the Eriksen farmhouse's porch light burning through the dusk.

Four. She said it in her head like a prayer, because for three generations her family had said it in their heads like a prayer, and because lately the number kept being wrong.

"Pass the gravy," Dad said.

Petra passed the gravy. In the window's reflection, five people sat at the table.

She didn't scream. Halvorsens didn't scream; they witnessed. But her hand shook hard enough that the gravy boat chattered against the plate, and Mom looked up from her plate with that tight, careful expression she'd worn since September—the one that meant *I saw it too, and we are not going to say so.*

"Anders," Mom said brightly, "eat your peas."

"There's somebody behind Petra," Anders said.

The fork in Petra's hand went cold. "Anders—"

"In the mirror," he clarified, pointing with his spoon, unbothered, seven years old and mercifully literal. "Not *behind* behind. Just in the glass."

Everyone looked. Everyone saw. The fifth figure sat where the reflection of the empty chair was, a shape like a person drawn from memory by someone who hadn't met many people—right proportions, wrong everything else. It had no face worth describing. It was holding a fork anyway, mimicking them, patient as furniture.

"Well," said Dad, setting down his napkin with the deliberate calm of a man who had decided, sometime in the last week, that he was done pretending this was normal. "It can't have any. It doesn't have a mouth."

"Dad," Petra whispered.

"It's true. Look." He turned in his chair, actually turned, and addressed the mirror directly, which no Halvorsen had done in living memory. "You're not getting any gravy. House rules."

The figure tilted its head. Somewhere upstairs, every door closed at once—a sound like a book snapping shut—and then, one heartbeat later, opened again.

*One more than there should be,* Grandma Ruth used to write in her journals, back when the witnessing was still a duty and not yet a dread. *Always count. Always be polite. Never warn.*

Petra thought about Silas Eriksen, alone over there in that house full of open doors, and felt something crack down the middle of her, clean as ice on a pond.

Grandma Ruth would have understood. That was the problem.

* * *

The lights failed at 9:14 p.m., and they failed politely, all at once, like someone flipping a master switch—which was worse than flickering, because flickering implied struggle, and this implied permission.

Dad got the lanterns down from the cellar while Mom lit the good candles, the beeswax ones saved for power outages and baptisms, and Anders trailed after everyone asking if the dark people were afraid of the light people, and nobody answered him because nobody knew.

"The well pump's electric," Mom said, doing inventory in her head the way she did. "So's the freezer. If this lasts—"

"It won't last," Dad said. "It's not weather."

He was right, which was the terrible part. Weather wanted something. This just *was*, the way a wall is, the way a debt is.

Petra stood at the front window with the lantern held high, looking down the quarter mile of gravel road toward the county blacktop, and watched her father's truck—parked at the end of the drive since Tuesday, gassed and pointed toward town, because Dad wasn't a fool—sit exactly where it was while the road itself did something roads should not do.

The road bent. Not curved. *Bent*, the way a straw bends when you pinch it, the far end swinging around until the county blacktop's distant glow appeared to sit directly behind the barn. Then it swung back. Then it did it again, slower, like something turning the road over in its hands, learning it.

"Mom," Petra said. "Come look at the road."

Mom came. Mom looked. Mom's face did the thing where it went very still and very old, the face from the photographs of her own mother standing witness at the orchard fence in 1974.

"That's new," Mom said quietly. "That's never—" She stopped. Started again. "Your grandmother said the property only ever needed to keep what was inside it. It never needed to reach."

"It's reaching," Petra said.

And then, because she was seventeen and tired and the covenant had been sitting on her chest like a stone since the day Wren Eriksen died, she said the other thing. The forbidden thing. She said it to her mother's face in lantern light with the road bending outside the glass:

"We have to warn her. We have to get her out. Both of them, whatever's left of both of them, before the moon comes up tomorrow."

Mom didn't answer right away. Outside, the fifth chair scraped faintly in a room where no fifth chair existed, and Anders laughed at something only he could hear, and the road bent and bent and bent.

"You know what happens to witnesses who warn," Mom said finally. It wasn't a refusal. Petra had grown up fluent in the language of her mother's refusals, and this wasn't one.

"Yeah," Petra said. "I know."

Mom closed her eyes. When she opened them, she looked less frightened and more furious, which Petra recognized with a jolt as the family face—the real one, under the witnessing, the one that had been waiting three generations for permission.

"Get your boots," Mom said. "Anders! Boots! We're going next door."

* * *

They went as a convoy of lanterns across the stubble field, because the road couldn't be trusted and the field, apparently, still could. Dad carried Anders on his shoulders even though Anders was nearly too big for it, and neither of them mentioned why. Mom walked with the shotgun she'd never once fired at anything but clay pigeons, broken open over her arm like a lady at a garden party, and Petra carried the thing that mattered most, which was a thermos of coffee and her grandmother's last journal, because if you were going to break a covenant you might as well bring the paperwork.

The Eriksen farmhouse glowed ahead of them, every window lit, and through those windows they could see Silas moving room to room with a roll of masking tape and a marker, labeling doors.

Numbered doors. Numbered *chairs*. Through the kitchen window they watched her stand in front of the table counting under her breath, lose count, start over, and put her hand flat on the wood like she was taking its pulse.

Silas saw them coming up the porch steps and opened the door before they knocked, and the look on her face was so nakedly relieved that Petra's throat hurt.

"I numbered the doors," Silas said, by way of hello. "There were eleven yesterday. There are twelve now. I've taped numbers on all of them and I check every hour and I want you to know that I understand how that sounds."

"It sounds like Tuesday," Dad said, stepping inside. "Anders, don't touch anything with a number on it."

"Can I touch the unnumbered things?"

"There are no unnumbered things," Silas and Petra said together, and despite everything—despite the twelfth door, despite the road bending, despite the extra place setting at the Eriksen table that Silas had clearly stopped trying to remove—something almost like laughter moved through the room.

Mom set the shotgun by the door, leaned the journal on the table, and got down to business the way Halvorsens did when the business was unbearable: briskly, with coffee.

"Here's what we know," she said. "The count completes at moonrise tomorrow. Everyone the house knows by name has to be off the property or gets added to it. And the house," she glanced at the twelfth door, "has been learning names."

"It knows yours," Petra told Silas. "It's known Eriksens forever. But it's been watching us too. Three generations of witnesses—it knows our names better than the church does."

Silas looked at the four of them—at Dad with Anders on his hip, at Mom already scanning the room for exits, at Petra—and Petra watched her do the math. Watched her arrive at the sum. Watched her try to hide it.

"No," Petra said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're going to say something about how you'll stay and we'll go, and I'm telling you now, pre-emptively, absolutely not, we didn't walk across a haunted turnip field to leave without you."

"It's a beet field," Silas said automatically, and then pressed both hands over her face. "Sorry. Sorry. It's just—you sound like Wren. He corrected me about the beets for twenty years."

The lanterns flickered—not failing, just flinching—and in the hallway, softly, all twelve doors drifted an inch wider, like the house drawing breath.

Dad cleared his throat. "Ma'am," he said to Silas, formal as a deacon, "your grandmother and my grandfather had an arrangement, and that arrangement was garbage, and I'd like to formally announce that we're canceling it. Effective tonight. Witnessed." He nodded at his wife, his daughter, his son. "All four of us. No takebacks."

Something in the walls made a sound—not a groan, not a creak, but a kind of *attention*, the whole frame of the old house leaning in to listen.

Mom started pulling curtains closed. "We sleep here tonight, all six of us, in one room, and at first light we load your truck and ours and we drive out together, and if the road won't let us, we drive through the beet field, and if the beets object, the beets can take it up with the shotgun."

"You've never fired that at anything but skeet," Petra said.

"Then the beets will be the second thing."

Silas laughed—a wet, cracked, real laugh—and for one moment the kitchen felt like a kitchen instead of a countdown. They made plans on the back of a feed store calendar. They drank the coffee. Anders fell asleep sitting up against Dad's ribs, mid-sentence, defending his theory that the dark people were probably just shy.

And at midnight, when Petra took her hourly turn checking the doors, she found the twelfth one standing wide open onto darkness that smelled like ripe apples, and written inside the doorframe in handwriting she recognized from a hundred years of journals—her grandmother's hand, and her great-grandmother's, and now, freshly, wetly, its own—

*a name.*

Not hers. Not Silas's.

Wren's.

The count wasn't waiting for moonrise anymore. It had started early, and it was starting with him.
