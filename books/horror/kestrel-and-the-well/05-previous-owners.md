## 5. Previous Owners

The county records office smelled of dust, damp cardboard, and the particular despair of a photocopier that had been asked to do too much for too long. Xanthe stood at the front desk while a woman named Mrs. Okonkwo — her nameplate said so, along with a small sticker of a smiling sun that seemed to be apologizing for the rest of the building — looked up "Hollowmere" in a ledger older than both of them combined.

"Hollowmere House," Xanthe said. "I'm doing a valuation survey. I need the ownership history, any deeds of transfer, and — if you have them — death certificates connected to the property."

Mrs. Okonkwo's pen paused mid-scratch. She looked up over her glasses with the expression of someone who had worked in this office long enough to know which requests were normal and which ones came back to visit later.

"Death certificates," she repeated.

"For the previous owners. It's standard due diligence." This was not entirely true. It was perhaps fifteen percent true. The other eighty-five percent was that Xanthe had spent three nights in Hollowmere House watching their own reflection raise its hand a full second after they did, and they had decided that if the house was going to keep secrets, Xanthe would go find its paperwork. Houses, like people, left trails. Houses, unlike people, could not lie on forms.

Mostly.

"Due diligence," Mrs. Okonkwo said again, in the tone of a woman filing the phrase away for future use. "Well. Deeds are in the green cabinets, second floor. Death certificates are held by the registrar's office, but we have microfiche copies of the parish returns up to 1974. You'll want bay six. Mind the lightbox, it bites."

"It *bites*?"

"It sparks. Same thing, morally speaking."

Xanthe liked her immediately, in the way one likes anyone who is honest about machinery.

* * *

Bay six turned out to be a carrel wedged between a broken radiator and a window that looked out onto a car park, as though the architects had understood exactly how much joy this room would need to leach out of its occupants and had planned accordingly. The lightbox did, indeed, bite; it gave Xanthe a static shock on the first sheet they fed into it, as if announcing its terms.

Xanthe counted the sheets as they loaded them. Forty-one fiche cards for the Hollowmere parish returns, 1901 through 1974. Forty-one was prime, indivisible, trustworthy. They said so under their breath, because saying numbers aloud had become, over the past week, less a habit than a load-bearing wall.

The deeds came first. Xanthe spread them across the carrel and made notes in the margins of their survey folder, the professional part of their brain settling gratefully into routine:

**Hollowmere House, parcel 114-Kestrel Road.**

- Built 1848, Aldous Wren, merchant. Sold 1893.
- 1893–1927: the Fenwick family. Sold 1927.
- 1927–1961: **the Marlow family**, deed of purchase signed by Thomas Marlow, choirmaster.
- 1961: transfer of title to **the Hollowmere Trust**, no named trustees, consideration listed as *"one pound and other considerations."*
- 1961 to present: nothing. No sale, no mortgage, no probate. Sixty-three years of silence in the ledger.

Xanthe stared at that last line for a long time. Property changed hands. That was what property *did*. Even derelict houses got bought and sold by speculators, inherited by feuding cousins, seized by banks. A house that sat untouched on the books for sixty-three years wasn't abandoned. It was *held*.

"One pound and other considerations," Xanthe murmured. "What other considerations?"

The lightbox sparked, conversationally.

* * *

The parish returns gave them the choir.

It took an hour of squinting at microfiche — the machine's lens had a scratch across it that put a faint comet through every face — before Xanthe assembled the picture. The Hollowmere parish newsletter, *The Kestrel & Vale Advertiser*, had been dutifully preserved, and dutifully it recorded everything worth recording in a village where nothing much happened and everything mattered.

March 1959: *"Congratulations to the Hollowmere Parish Choir, winners of the County Choral Festival! Under the direction of Mr. Thomas Marlow, the seven-voice ensemble brought the hall to its feet with 'The Well-Spring Carol,' an old local air revived by Mr. Marlow himself."*

Seven voices. Xanthe wrote down the names from the accompanying photograph caption, the letters slightly warped by the scratched lens:

**Thomas Marlow, director. Margaret Marlow (contralto). Ruth Ellery (soprano). Samuel Voss (tenor). Peter Voss (baritone). Anne Quill (mezzo). Edith Marlow (treble).**

Edith Marlow. The surname matched the deed. A daughter, then. A treble — a child's voice, high and clear, the kind of voice that gets given the descant because it can go places grown throats can't.

There was more. There was always more; villages kept receipts.

June 1960: *"The Choir sang at the Well-Dressing, continuing our beautiful local custom. Miss E. Marlow's solo was much admired."*

September 1960: *"A farewell social for the Choir at Hollowmere House. Mr. Marlow announced the group will begin recording a collection of regional airs this winter."*

And then — nothing. Not a gap, exactly. Newspapers never printed gaps; they printed the absence of things in the shape of other things. The Christmas 1960 issue mentioned the choir's carol service only in passing, in a list. By spring 1961 the Well-Dressing article said the ceremony went ahead *"without music this year, owing to circumstances."*

*Circumstances.* Xanthe had read enough survey reports to know a euphemism when it sat down across from them and refused to make eye contact.

They fed the registrar's index fiche into the biting lightbox and began working through deaths registered in the district, 1961.

Six certificates.

Xanthe laid them out in a row across the carrel, and the row had a weight to it that went beyond paper. Six death certificates, all dated within eleven days of each other, March 1961. All certified by the same district registrar, a Mr. H. Cobbleigh, whose signature deteriorated visibly from the first certificate to the sixth, starting as confident copperplate and ending as something closer to a spider drowning in ink.

**Marlow, Thomas, b. 1901. Cause of death: asphyxiation. Found at: Hollowmere House.**
**Marlow, Margaret, b. 1904. Asphyxiation. Hollowmere House.**
**Ellery, Ruth, b. 1932. Asphyxiation. Hollowmere House.**
**Voss, Samuel, b. 1929. Asphyxiation. Hollowmere House.**
**Voss, Peter, b. 1935. Asphyxiation. Hollowmere House.**
**Quill, Anne, b. 1938. Asphyxiation. Hollowmere House.**

Six. Seven minus six equals one. Xanthe did the arithmetic twice, then a third time, because arithmetic had been the one thing in Hollowmere House that still behaved, and they needed to know it hadn't followed them here.

One name missing. One treble.

They searched the 1961 register forward and backward. Nothing. They searched 1962. Nothing. They pulled the 1960 fiche and found her there instead — not a death certificate but a *correction slip*, filed in March 1961, the same month as the six deaths. It was a thin strip of paper photographed grey against grey, and Xanthe had to lean close to the lightbox to read it, breath fogging the glass.

> **CORRECTION TO REGISTER OF BIRTHS.**
> **MARLOW, EDITH JUNE, b. 1949, KESTREL ROAD DISTRICT.**
> **STATUS AMENDED FROM: LIVING.**
> **STATUS AMENDED TO: REMOVED.**
> **AUTHORITY: HOLLOWMERE TRUST.**
> **COUNTERSIGNED: H. COBBLEIGH, REGISTRAR.**
> **NOTE: ENTRY CLOSED BY AGREEMENT OF ALL PARTIES. NO FURTHER RECORD TO BE KEPT.**

Xanthe read it four times.

*Removed.*

Not deceased. Not missing. Not transferred, emigrated, or lost at sea. *Removed* — as though she were a stain, a listing error, a line item struck through by agreement of all parties. And the authority amending a human being out of the register of births was not a court, not a coroner, not the Home Office. It was the *trust that owned the house*. The house had edited its own paperwork. The house had walked into the county records office — or sent something wearing a coat and carrying a pen — and a man named Cobbleigh had stamped it, because that was his job, and stamping things was easier than asking why.

"No further record to be kept," Xanthe read aloud, just to hear how it sounded in a living voice.

It sounded like a door closing.

* * *

Mrs. Okonkwo found them twenty minutes later, still sitting in bay six with six death certificates fanned out like a losing hand of cards.

"You've gone pale," she observed. "That's the lightbox. I told you it bites."

"Mrs. Okonkwo — do you know anything about a registrar called Cobbleigh? District registrar, March 1961?"

She considered. "Before my time. But there'll be a logbook. Registrars kept day-books — appointments, fees, remarks. Grey cabinet, bottom shelf, the ones with the cracked spines. 1961 will be in there somewhere." She peered at the certificates spread across the carrel, and her eyebrows climbed slowly toward her hairline. "Good Lord. Hollowmere. My mother used to say you could hear singing off that road some nights, when she was a girl. We all thought it was the church practicing."

"Was it?"

"The church closed at nine." She said it mildly, the way one says a thing one has decided not to think about for fifty years, and went back to her desk.

The day-book was where it should be, fat and cracked-spined, its pages the colour of weak tea. March 1961. Xanthe ran a finger down the entries, past christenings and marriage notices, and stopped.

> **14 March 1961. 11:40 p.m.**
> **Caller at private entrance: T. Marlow, Hollowmere.**
> **Business: registration of six deaths. Fee paid in full. Cash.**
> **Remarks:** *Mr. Marlow in poor condition. Would not come inside. Stood at the step. Said the others were waiting and he mustn't keep them. Asked me to enter cause as asphyxiation for all six and to say no inquest required, family declining. I advised him an inquest is mandatory in such cases. He said —*

The handwriting stopped. Then resumed, smaller, pressed hard into the page:

> *He said, "You don't understand, they're still singing. If I stop writing it down it stops being true, and if it stops being true the well opens." I entered the deaths as instructed. What else does one do?*
>
> **15 March 1961.** *Correction slip re: E. Marlow executed per instruction of Hollowmere Trust. Fee waived.*
>
> **16 March 1961.** *No callers. Heard singing on the walk home. Told myself it was the church. The church closes at nine.*

Xanthe sat very still.

Somewhere behind them, the photocopier groaned through a long job, its rhythm almost — but not quite — like breathing. Almost, but not quite, like a held note. Xanthe counted the copier's cycles to steady themselves. Eleven. Eleven was fine. Eleven was two fives and a one, and fives were friendly.

*If I stop writing it down it stops being true.*

So the opposite must also hold. If it was written down, it stayed true. Somewhere in this building, in these grey cabinets, in these tea-coloured pages, the truth about Edith Marlow existed — filed, stamped, countersigned, and ignored for sixty-three years. Not hidden. *Archived*. The crime hadn't been covered up; it had been *processed*. Given a reference number. Placed in a drawer where it could be technically available to anyone and actually seen by no one, which was the same thing as invisible, which was the same thing as safe.

Xanthe thought of the mirror in the hallway, raising its hand one second late. Of the appraisal figures rewriting themselves in the margins. Of a house whose value kept changing because the house was still owed something, and debts, unlike property, appreciated.

They photocopied everything. The certificates, the correction slip, the day-book pages, the photograph of seven smiling faces outside a church in March 1959, Edith Marlow in the front row with her mouth open mid-note, a girl who could go places grown throats couldn't. The photocopier jammed twice on the page with her face. On the third attempt it went through clean, and Xanthe chose not to have an opinion about that.

At the desk, Mrs. Okonkwo stamped the request form without looking at it. "Anything else?"

"One thing." Xanthe hesitated. "The Hollowmere Trust. Is there a file? Trustees' names, correspondence, anything?"

She checked the index drawer, ran one finger down a card, and frowned. "There's a reference number. H-T dash one. But the file itself—" She pulled the drawer fully open, tilted her head, and pulled it again, harder, as though the drawer might be hiding it out of spite. "That's odd. The card says bay twelve. Bay twelve has no such file. It has drainage plans for the leisure centre."

"Could it be misfiled?"

"Everything in this building is misfiled, love. But it isn't *missing*. Missing is different. Missing means it was here once." She shut the drawer with a snap and looked at Xanthe over her glasses, and for a moment the smiling-sun nameplate seemed very far away. "You'll be going back out there, will you? To the house?"

"I have to finish the survey."

"Mm." She handed back the form. "My mother said the singing stopped in '61 and started again around about now-ish, if you follow me. Village talk. Probably nonsense." She smiled, warm and brief. "Count your fingers when you get home. Both hands. It's what my mother always said, and she lived to ninety-four."

Xanthe walked out into the November afternoon with a folder full of photocopies pressed to their chest like a breastplate, and told themselves the cold coming off the car park was just weather.

In the folder, between the sixth death certificate and the correction slip, the photocopier had left a smudge — a grey streak across the registrar's drowned-spider signature, as if the ink had been wet sixty-three years and had finally, patiently, begun to run.

That night, back at Hollowmere, Xanthe pinned the photocopies to the kitchen wall and counted the doors aloud — twenty-one, twenty-one, twenty-one, all present, all accounted for — and did not notice until morning, when the first frost ferns had bloomed across the inside of the glass in tight ironwork spirals, that the window above the sink now faced the well.
