## 7. What the Mirror Keeps

Xanthe Vasquez had a system for the mirrors, because Xanthe Vasquez had a system for everything.

It had started on day three, after the hallway mirror raised its hand a full second behind them like a shy child at a party. The system was this: every mirror in Hollowmere House got a sticky note. Not to cover it — covering felt like an invitation, somehow, like knocking — but labeled, numbered, and logged in a small green notebook that had once been for square footage calculations and was now mostly for survival logistics.

*Hallway (stairs), 1 sec. Bathroom (up), 1 sec. Dressing room, 1 sec, cracked, do not look at crack.* Eleven mirrors total. Twelve if you counted the hand mirror in the sewing box, which Xanthe did not, because the sewing box stayed shut and the shut-ness of the sewing box was one of the few things in the house that behaved.

"Good morning, eleven," they said aloud, tapping each note in turn, because counting aloud was armor and armor worked better when you could hear it clanking. "One through eleven. All present. All one second late, which is rude but consistent."

Consistency. That was the word Xanthe clung to the way other people clung to banisters. The house lied about its doors, its dimensions, its heating bill, and quite possibly its own existence, but the mirrors had been *reliably* wrong. One second. Every time. You could set your watch by a lie if the lie kept regular hours.

On the morning of the ninth day, the hallway mirror took two.

* * *

Xanthe noticed it the way you notice a friend has changed their hair — a wrongness without an immediate name. They were carrying two mugs of tea up from the kitchen (one for themselves, one for the ritual of setting a mug on the hall table, which was not superstition, thank you very much, it was *occupancy signaling*, letting the house know someone lived here who paid attention). They passed the tall mirror at the stair landing, and their reflection passed it too, and then —

They stopped. The reflection stopped. But between the stopping and the stopping there was a gap, a little seam of time where Xanthe stood still and the glass held someone who hadn't caught up yet.

They counted it out loud, because that's what they did now. "One... two."

The reflection lifted its mug on *two*. It should have lifted on *one*. Or rather — it should have lifted when Xanthe lifted, which was zero, which was how mirrors worked, which was the entire social contract between a person and their reflection since the invention of polished metal.

"Okay," Xanthe said to the empty hallway, in the bright, reasonable voice they used for difficult clients and worse houses. "Okay. We're going to test this."

Testing took the rest of the morning. Xanthe went mirror to mirror with the notebook, performing the same sequence: raise right hand, count one-Mississippi two-Mississippi, note the lag. The bathroom mirror: two seconds. The dressing room mirror: two seconds, and the crack had grown a centimeter overnight, branching like frost or lightning or a river delta seen from very high up. The little oval mirror in what had probably been a servant's room: two seconds. Even the sewing-box hand mirror, when Xanthe finally, reluctantly, opened the box — two seconds, and something else besides, something Xanthe wrote down as *texture?* and underlined twice before deciding not to investigate texture today. Today was for data. Texture was tomorrow's problem.

Every mirror. Two seconds. Uniformly, precisely, as though the house had issued a memo.

"That's not drift," Xanthe told the notebook. "Drift is gradual. This is a policy change."

The question — the question they circled around all day while measuring rooms that measured differently depending on which wall you started from — was: *where does the second go?*

Because a lag isn't nothing. A lag means the mirror is showing you something that happened a moment ago, which means somewhere in the glass there is a version of you that is always slightly in the past, always slightly behind, walking through a hallway that no longer exists. And if the mirror can hold onto a second of you, hold it, keep it, replay it —

Then what else was it keeping?

* * *

They found out at four in the afternoon, in the long grey light, and afterward Xanthe would think: of course it was the landing mirror. Of course it was the big one, the one with the carved frame of oak leaves gone black with age, the one positioned so that anyone standing on the landing saw the whole upper corridor reflected behind them, doors and walls and dimness, a second hallway made of silver.

Xanthe stood on the landing with the tea mug (theirs; the ritual mug lived downstairs) and did the thing they'd started doing every evening: checking the reflection for changes. Inventory of self. Hair: present, chaotic. Face: present, tired, a new crease between the brows that Priya would have called "stress" and Xanthe called "professional development." Coat: still on, because the house's cold had opinions and Xanthe had stopped arguing.

Behind them, in the mirror, the corridor stretched away, empty.

They counted the lag. Raised a hand. "One Mississippi. Two Mississippi."

The reflection raised its hand on two. Confirmed. Xanthe exhaled, wrote *2s, all units, uniform*, and was capping the pen when they noticed that the reflection had not lowered its hand.

Xanthe lowered theirs again. Counted. The reflection followed, two beats late, obedient. Fine. Fine. Except that during those two beats — during the gap, the seam, the stolen second-plus-one — the corridor behind the reflection was not empty.

There were people standing in it.

Seven of them. Standing in a loose line along the upper corridor, spaced evenly, like a choir waiting for a downbeat. Long coats, dark dresses, a man in a waistcoat with his hands folded. Clothes from another century's middle, or the middle of another century — 1961, said the part of Xanthe's brain that had spent a week in the county records office and couldn't unsee it. Six adults. And at the end of the line, half a step apart from the others, a girl who could not have been more than fourteen, her hair dripping flat against her head, her dress clinging to her the way wet things cling.

Their mouths were open. All seven mouths, open in the same shape, mid-vowel, mid-note. Singing nothing. The corridor made no sound at all. The house was silent as a photograph.

And then the two seconds closed, the way a door closes, and the reflection caught up, and the corridor behind Xanthe — the real one, the one they could turn and check, and they did turn, they turned so fast the tea slopped over their wrist — was empty. Just corridor. Just doors. Just the twenty-two (or twenty-one, or whatever the number was today) and the smell of cold dust.

Xanthe stood very still on the landing, heart going like a woodpecker, and made themselves say it out loud, because saying things out loud made them smaller:

"You are a lagging mirror. You show me two seconds ago. Two seconds ago I was alone. Therefore what I saw was not two seconds ago."

The logic wobbled. Xanthe looked at it the way you look at a chair with a broken leg.

"Unless," they said, and stopped, because *unless* was a door and they'd had enough of doors.

* * *

That night they didn't sleep so much as patrol. Up and down the upstairs corridor with a torch, counting doors aloud — "one, two, three..." — and checking mirrors at intervals like a nurse checking a patient's pulse. The frost had come back inside the windows again, ferning across the glass in spirals that matched the ironwork pattern from the wellhead diagram in the old survey documents, and the temperature inside was dropping faster than outside, which violated several laws and all of Xanthe's professional training. They wore their coat indoors. They wore a second coat indoors. They drank tea until their teeth floated.

At some point past two in the morning, standing in front of the bathroom mirror with the torch held under their chin like a child telling ghost stories to themselves, Xanthe saw their reflection move first.

Not much. Not dramatically. The reflection simply began to speak — mouth moving, jaw working, lips shaping words — while Xanthe stood silent, mouth firmly shut, watching.

The lag meant the reflection should have been *behind*. Two seconds in the past, always. But this mouth was moving ahead of them, or beside them, or nowhere near them at all, shaping syllables Xanthe couldn't hear through the glass. The words had a rhythm. A meter. Da-da-DUM, da-da-DUM, with pauses in places that felt deliberate, like verse.

"No," Xanthe said clearly. "No, we are not doing this. You are my reflection. You say what I say. That's the deal. That's the oldest deal there is."

The reflection's mouth kept moving. And here was the thing that made Xanthe's skin try to leave their body: the shape of the words wasn't angry, wasn't menacing. It was *patient*. The reflection looked like someone rehearsing. Like someone learning a part, mouthing lines over and over in a dressing-room mirror, getting the phrasing right, waiting for the cue.

Waiting for the music.

Xanthe backed out of the bathroom, torch beam shaking, and checked the landing mirror. The reflection there was also mouthing. The dressing room mirror: mouthing. Eleven mirrors, eleven versions of Xanthe Vasquez's face, all silently singing something Xanthe didn't know yet.

*Yet.*

They sat down on the stairs, in the dark, in both coats, and let themselves shake for exactly sixty seconds — they counted them, of course, counted them like a rosary — and then wiped their face and did the only thing that had ever helped: they got the notebook and they wrote it down.

*Day 9, night. Lag now 2s everywhere. In the gap: seven figures, corridor, mouths open, no sound. Girl at the end, wet. Matches records: Hollowmere Choir, six deaths 1961, one "removed," E. Marlow, age 14.*

*Reflections have begun speaking independently. Content unknown. Rhythm suggests song. My face is learning words I haven't learned.*

Underneath, after a long pause, in smaller handwriting:

*I don't think the mirrors are showing me the past. I think they're showing me rehearsal.*

* * *

Morning came grey and thin. Xanthe made tea, tapped the eleven sticky notes — "one through eleven, good morning, behave" — and tried to feel like a property surveyor instead of a character in something. They had work to do. There was a valuation due. There were measurements to take that would refuse to stay taken. There was, technically, a job.

But standing in the hallway, mug in hand, they caught themselves humming.

Three notes. Four. A rising phrase with a hitch in the middle, da-da-DUM, da-da-DUM, the exact meter their reflection had been mouthing. They stopped. Blinked. Tried to remember where they'd heard it, and couldn't, because they hadn't heard it anywhere — it had arrived the way the frost arrived, from the inside of the glass outward.

"That's not mine," Xanthe told the hallway. "I don't sing. Priya says I sing like a filing cabinet falling downstairs."

The hallway offered no opinion. But at the end of it, the tall mirror at the landing caught the morning light, and in it — just for the length of a breath, just inside the two-second seam — Xanthe saw the seven figures again, closer now, arranged not along the corridor but *behind their own reflection*, crowded at its shoulders like an audience leaning toward the stage.

And at the end of the line, the girl with the wet hair was looking directly out of the glass. Not at the choir. Not at the house.

At Xanthe. With an expression that was not haunting, not hungry, not any of the things ghosts were supposed to be.

She looked, Xanthe thought with a lurch, like someone waiting for a debt to be acknowledged. Like paperwork that had learned to stare.

Her mouth moved — one word, maybe two, shaped slow and clear through the silver — and though no sound crossed the glass, Xanthe's lips, entirely without permission, began to shape it back.
