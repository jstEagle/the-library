## 9. The Song Without a Last Verse

The third night, the house began to hum.

Not metaphorically — I checked. I got up at 2 a.m., put my palm flat against the bedroom wall, and felt it: a vibration too low to be plumbing, too regular to be wind, and far too melodic to be anything a building had any business doing. It came up through the plaster like a hymn remembered by someone who'd rather forget.

I did what any reasonable property surveyor would do at two in the morning in a haunted house with an appraisal problem.

I made tea.

There's something about a kettle that restores the natural order of things. The click of the switch, the tick of the element, the small domestic roar of water deciding to boil — these are sounds from a universe where houses are houses and songs stay inside people. I stood in the kitchen with my hands around a mug of tea that tasted faintly of iron, and I listened properly for the first time.

It was a choir. That was unmistakable once I stopped flinching. Six voices, maybe seven, singing in close harmony, the kind where each part leans against the others so none of them can fall. Except these voices weren't leaning on anything. They were suspended, held in the walls the way frost is held in glass, and they were singing a melody that circled and circled and never landed.

I know music the way I know most things: by counting. Four beats to the bar, eight bars to a phrase, thirty-two to a verse. This song kept promising a resolution — you could feel it reaching for its final note the way a hand reaches for a light switch in a dark room — and then starting over. Phrase after phrase after phrase, all approach, no arrival.

It was, I thought into my tea, the most frustrating piece of music ever composed. And then I thought: *or it's not finished.*

That thought arrived with the specific cold weight of a truth I didn't want. I drank my tea anyway. The kettle clicked off. Somewhere upstairs, a door counted itself shut — twenty-two, always twenty-two — and the humming went on.

* * *

In the morning I did the thing I'd been avoiding since the mirrors started lagging: I treated the house as evidence.

This is the part of surveying nobody warns you about. People think we measure rooms. Really, we build arguments. Every valuation is a case: here is what exists, here is what it's worth, here is my reasoning, sign below. A house like Hollowmere doesn't want to be argued about. But I had spent eleven years learning to write down what was true until the untrue things got embarrassed and left, and I wasn't going to stop now just because the untrue things had started harmonizing.

I set up at the kitchen table with manuscript paper I'd bought from the village shop — the only stationery they stocked besides birthday cards featuring tractors — and a pencil worn down to a stub I'd found in a drawer with a 1974 coin and a dead moth. Then I waited for dark, because the song only came at night, and I transcribed.

It took three nights. I'll spare you the details except the ones that matter:

The melody sat in a minor key that kept trying to be major, like a person smiling through a headache. It moved in sevens — seven-note phrases, seven phrases to a section, sections built like staircases descending. Each phrase ended one note short of rest. Not unresolved in the interesting, modern-composer way. Unresolved in the *amputated* way. You could hear exactly where the last note should go. The whole song leaned toward it. Every voice strained toward it. And then silence, and the beginning again, over and over, a sentence cut off mid-word by a throat that wouldn't stop trying.

By the fourth night I had forty-one bars written down, and the shape of the thing was clear enough to make my hands shake. It wasn't a performance. It was a plea. It was six or seven voices asking the same question for sixty years: *where does it end?*

And underneath the notation, in the margins of my careful little manuscript paper, my handwriting had started doing the other thing. The thing it had been doing to my square footage since day one.

*One voice,* my pencil had written along the bottom margin, in letters not quite mine. *One voice owed.*

I crossed it out. Underneath, in smaller letters: *one voice owed, held in trust.*

I closed the notebook and went to bed and did not sleep, which at this point felt less like insomnia and more like a scheduling decision.

* * *

Here is what I should have done, and what I told myself I would do, and what lasted approximately nine hours: nothing. Don't sing it. Don't hum it. Don't tap it out on the kitchen table while waiting for the kettle. A song without an ending is a trap with the door propped open, and I had read enough folklore between surveys to know that the door being propped open is the entire design.

But here is what actually happened, on the fifth night, and I'm telling you honestly because if I soften it you won't understand how it almost went.

I was checking my transcription against the wall-hum — holding the page up near the plaster like a customs officer comparing a passport to a face — when I noticed I'd gotten bar nineteen wrong. The third note was a step lower than I'd written. Trivial. A correction. And because I am a person who checks her work twice and counts her doors aloud every morning like a rosary, I sang the corrected bar to confirm it.

Four notes. Half a second. Barely a sound — more breath than voice, the kind of noise you make finding a pitch.

The temperature dropped so fast my tea steamed.

Frost came across the kitchen floor like spilled milk running uphill. I watched it happen — genuinely watched, standing on a chair within four seconds of making four notes, which tells you how fast my survival instincts have sharpened — as the white ferns raced from the window, across the flagstones, up the table leg, and stopped exactly one foot from my left slipper. One foot. Precisely. As if measured.

As if *appraised.*

The wall-hum stopped. All six voices, mid-phrase, gone — and the silence they left behind was worse, because it was attentive. The whole house had turned its face toward me the way a room turns toward an open door.

"Sorry," I said to the kitchen, because apparently my policy in supernatural crises is British politeness. "My mistake. Won't happen again."

The frost stayed where it was, one foot away, all night, arranged in spirals I recognized from the wellhead ironwork in the mirror-room-that-wasn't. I sat on my chair until dawn with my knees pulled up, and the frost sat with me, patient as a creditor.

Lesson learned, you'd think. And mostly learned! Mostly. But here's the thing about temptation that nobody puts in the pamphlets: it isn't loud. It's reasonable. It sits beside you at breakfast and says, *you already know half the song; knowing the rest would at least tell you what you're dealing with; knowledge is your job.* It says, *the song wants an ending, and you are extremely good at endings — you close out valuations for a living.* It says, very quietly, in your own voice: *wouldn't it be a kindness?*

Because that was the worst discovery of the week, worse than the frost, worse than the margins. When I'd sung those four notes, the wall-hum hadn't stopped in anger.

It had stopped in *hope.*

* * *

On the sixth morning I called the office from the landline in the hall — mobile signal at Hollowmere was theoretical — and asked Priya to look up something for me, keeping my voice light and professional and absolutely free of the words *choir*, *well*, or *frost*.

"Hollowmere," she said. Papers rustling at the other end. My sister has the comforting sound of a well-organized filing system. "You've been there three weeks, Xan. Your expense reports read like a hostage note. 'Tea, tea, candles, more candles, manuscript paper.'"

"The house has unusual acoustics."

"The house has unusual *everything*, according to your invoices." A pause, and her voice dropped into the register she uses when she's about to be right at me. "Come home for a weekend. Just one. The figures will keep."

"They're rewriting themselves, Priya."

"Then they can rewrite themselves while you eat actual food." Another pause. Longer. "Xan. Are you okay?"

I looked down the hallway. At the end of it, the mirror hung on the wall, and in the mirror my reflection stood a full second behind me, watching me with my own face, wearing my expression of tired patience like a borrowed coat. Behind the reflection — faintly, in the glass's grey depth — shapes that might have been people, might have been coats on hooks, might have been nothing.

"I'm fine," I said. "Counting everything twice, drinking too much tea, nearly done. I'll call Thursday."

"You hate counting."

"I'm growing as a person."

She laughed, and the laugh did what her laughs always do, which is make the world briefly warmer than the evidence supports, and I hung up and stood in the hall feeling the shape of the lie I'd told. Not the content — the *shape*. Because I hadn't lied to protect myself. I'd lied to keep her out. And some cold, precise part of me noted that the house approved of exclusions. The house liked doors that kept people on one side.

The frost in the kitchen had melted by noon. It left no water. It left, instead, on the flagstones where it had lain, a faint white residue in a pattern I refused to look at directly, though my eyes kept snagging on it: a spiral, tightening inward, ending at a point roughly where a wellhead might be, if a kitchen were a parlor, and a parlor existed, and the center of this house could be found by anyone.

* * *

That night I made a decision that felt like the first smart one in weeks: I stopped listening.

Easier said than done when the song comes through the walls, but I had equipment. Surveyors travel with ear defenders — you spend enough time on construction sites and you acquire a pair — and I dug mine out of the kit bag, put them on, and lay in bed in padded silence while the house hummed its unfinished question into the muffled dark.

It didn't work, is the thing. Of course it didn't. The song wasn't coming through the air. The ear defenders muted the walls and left the melody perfectly intact inside my skull, where it had apparently been renting a room since the first night, paying its rent in fragments. I lay there with my own pulse in my ears and the choir circling their missing note somewhere behind my eyes, and I understood, with the calm of total exhaustion, that I could not unlearn this song. It was in me now. Transcribed twice: once on paper, once in bone.

So I did the next best thing. I bargained.

"All right," I said aloud, to the ceiling, to the walls, to whatever kept the books here. "Terms. I don't sing. In exchange, you leave the kitchen alone and stop writing in my margins."

Silence. Then, from the direction of the stairs, the soft click of a door settling — twenty-two, faithful, terrible — and beneath the floorboards, so low I might have invented it, a single harmonic swelled and faded. Not agreement. Acknowledgment. The difference matters. A signature acknowledges terms; it doesn't promise to honor them. I know this professionally.

I slept, eventually, in the way you sleep in cold rooms — lightly, in shifts, surfacing often. And at some hour past three I surfaced into a silence that was different from the others, and lay still, and heard why.

The song had changed.

Not louder. Not closer. *Further along.* The melody that had circled its missing note for six nights now reached the edge of the amputation and — hovered. Held there. All six voices balanced on the lip of the final phrase like divers on a board, not falling, not retreating. Waiting.

Waiting the way a form waits for a signature.

I sat up in the dark with my heart going like a metronome set by someone spiteful, and I knew — the way you know a figure doesn't add, instantly, in the gut before the brain catches up — what had happened. My four notes. My stupid, careful, half-second correction. The song had taken them the way a lock takes a key's first teeth. I hadn't sung the melody *to* it. I'd sung the melody *into* it. And now it had my voice on file.

The frost bloomed up the bedroom window as I watched, ferning outward from the corners, and in its white lacework — fine as engraving, deliberate as typesetting — lines appeared. Not spirals this time. Staves. Five thin lines, repeated, panel after panel, filling with marks: notes, rests, dynamics, all rendered in ice with a copyist's precision.

The last verse. Being written. One line per night, maybe. A song completing itself now that it had a living mouth on record — not asking me to sing it, not yet. Just showing me the score. Letting me watch the ending arrive at its own pace, the way you let a debtor watch the interest accrue.

I got my notebook. I copied down what the frost had written — one line, eight bars, ending on a note that resolved everything, the arrival the whole song had been starving toward for sixty years — and my hand shook so badly the transcription looked like it was written during an earthquake, which, spiritually, it was.

At the bottom of the line, in the frost, small and neat, the ice added a footnote. Performance instruction. The kind conductors write in red.

*For the final verse: five voices sing. One holds silent. The song completes only through the silence.*

And beneath that, in letters that formed slowly, like breath on glass:

*We have yours on file.*

I sat on the edge of the bed until the frost faded with the dawn, taking its notation with it, leaving the window wet and ordinary. Downstairs, I found my manuscript pages fanned neatly across the kitchen table, which I had definitely left closed in the hall. On top, square in the center, lay the county archive photocopy I'd brought back from the records office — the Hollowmere choir roster, seven names, six death certificates, one girl listed as *removed* — and across Edith Marlow's name, in pencil in my own handwriting, someone had drawn a single line of musical stave.

Eight bars. The line the frost had shown me. The first line of the ending.

Underneath it, in letters not quite mine, small and patient as a stamp:

*Received with thanks. Balance outstanding: one voice, held. Final settlement pending review.*

I put the kettle on. My hands were steady enough for that, barely. Outside, the November sky pressed down grey as ledger paper, and the first flakes of snow came down — early, too early, weeks early — and did not melt when they touched the windows.

They melted when they touched the ground. Only the windows kept them, spiraling inward, and I understood that winter here wasn't weather. It was a deadline, and it had just begun to count itself down, and somewhere in the post, already signed and stamped and traveling toward me with bureaucratic inevitability, was a document I had sent away thinking it was about the house.
