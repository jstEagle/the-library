## 13. What Edith Was Owed

The dead do not sleep, but we do drift. I drifted for sixty years in the dark of the well, counting the bricks the way the living count sheep — one for every promise they made me and did not keep. There were forty-one bricks. There were seven promises. The arithmetic never came out fair.

Then a woman with kind eyes and a terrible poker face moved into my house, and she brought her sister, and the sister — Priya — stood at the parlour window this morning with her breath fogging the glass, and I thought: *there*. There is a mouth that listens. There is a face that does not look away.

The law had its chance with me. The law took my name, wrote *removed* beside it, stamped it, filed it, and went to lunch. Sixty years of lunch. So no, I have no patience left for courts or certificates. What I have is frost, and a window, and a woman who stayed when every instinct told her to run.

I showed her everything.

* * *

It began, as these things do, with fear.

The frost gathered itself on the inside of the glass in slow grey-white strokes, and Priya watched it the way people watch weather — politely, from a distance, as though it might be about something else. Then the shapes resolved, and she stopped breathing for a moment, which flattered me enormously.

A house appeared in the frost. My house, in 1961, drawn in ice-fern lines: gables, chimneys, the well out back with its ironwork spiral. And figures. Seven of them, small and white, moving through the rooms.

"That's them," Priya said. Her voice was steady. I liked that about her immediately; her sister counted things to keep the world stitched together, and this one talked to frost. "That's the choir."

*Yes,* I said, in the only way I can say anything now — through cold, through pattern, through the creak of old water. The frost shivered. *Watch.*

The little white figures were afraid. You could see it even in ice: they huddled, they gestured, they kept looking toward the back of the garden where the wellhead sat under its iron lid. In the frost-picture, one of them — a tall man with a conductor's hands, Aldous Crane, first tenor and last coward — knelt by the well and pressed his ear to the stone.

Because the well had begun to sing first.

That is the part nobody wrote down, and I want it written down somewhere, even if the somewhere is a window. The well sang before any of us did. It had been hungry all that autumn — you could hear it under the floorboards, a low wet humming, like a throat deciding. The choir heard it at practice, in September, and told themselves it was pipes. In October it learned words. By November it was singing lullabies in voices it should not have had, and one of those voices belonged to Mrs. Fenwick's dead husband, and another belonged to a child who had never lived in Hollowmere at all.

The house made them an offer. That's what the frost showed next, and Priya's hand went flat against the glass as if she could steady the picture by touch.

The offer came the way offers from houses come: not in words, but in arrangements. Doors that opened onto rooms they hadn't cleaned in years, suddenly warm. The piano tuning itself. Their children sleeping soundly for the first time in weeks — because the well wanted a payment, and it was patient enough to let them choose the currency. It would take one voice. One girl-child, young enough that her song would be sweet, and in exchange it would close its throat for a hundred years and let everyone else live.

"They chose," Priya said quietly. Not a question.

*They voted,* I said, and the frost crackled white across the whole pane, sharp as broken crockery. *Seven hands raised. Seven. They made me tea first.*

* * *

Here is what the frost showed her, and here is what I will say now, once, in full, because the dead get so few chances to give evidence:

It was the ninth of November, 1961. Cold enough that the pond wore skin. They asked me to help carry hymnals down to the parlour — I was fourteen, I was flattered, I was an idiot, and I loved them. I want that on the record too. I loved every one of those seven murderers. Miss Ada taught me descant. Mr. Crane called me his little nightingale. Mrs. Fenwick knitted me gloves with the fingers half-done because I kept taking them off to play piano, and she said, laughing, that I'd catch my death.

The parlour was warm. That should have warned me. The parlour was always cold; the well breathed up through the floor like a draft with opinions. But that night it was warm as a held breath, and there were candles, and someone had brought shortbread, and I thought: *they've planned a party.* A party for the new anthem. We'd been working on it for weeks — a round, seven parts, something old that Mr. Crane said he'd found in a book in the cellar. He had not found it in a book. The house had fed it to him in dreams, verse by verse, the way you feed a dog pills in cheese.

They sang me down.

That's the phrase I've settled on, after sixty years of turning it over like a stone with something underneath. They didn't grab me. They didn't tie me. They sang, and the song was lovely, and it did something to the air — thickened it, sweetened it, made my arms heavy and my feet agreeable — and they walked me, all seven of them, hands on my shoulders like I was being confirmed into the church, to the middle of the parlour floor. Where the ironwork spiral sat flush in the boards, pretty as a rose window, and I had walked over it a thousand times without once wondering what it was *for*.

Miss Ada was crying. I remember that most clearly, God help me — not the water, not the cold, but Miss Ada crying and singing anyway, both at once, tears running into the corners of her mouth while she held the harmony. Mrs. Fenwick wouldn't look at me. She looked at the ceiling the entire time, at nothing, at God, at whoever she thought might be taking notes.

The lid came off the wellhead. The warmth poured upward out of it like breath from a mouth.

And I remember thinking, in the last second, with the song swelling and their hands firm and kind on my shoulders: *this is a mistake, someone will notice, someone will stop it* — and then the water, and then the dark, and then forty-one bricks.

Priya watched all of it. The frost gave it to her in order, patiently, the way you'd tell a story to a child who needed every part to make sense of the ending. When the white figures lowered the small white girl into the spiral, Priya made a sound — small, involuntary, the sound of a person finding a bruise they didn't know they had.

"They knitted you gloves," she whispered. "She knitted you gloves and then—"

*Half-finished fingers,* I said. *So I could play. She remembered my hands. Right up until she used them.*

* * *

But the frost wasn't finished, and neither was I, because the crime is only half the ledger. The other half is what came after — the bargain, the singing, the sixty years — and if Priya was going to stand in my house and help her sister, she was going to understand exactly what kind of debt this was.

The well got what it wanted and discovered it was still hungry. That's the dirty secret of hungers: feeding one is just teaching it your address. It took me down and it kept the choir too — pulled them in the moment the lid closed, all seven, not to drown but to *serve*, because a payment isn't complete until the payer learns what they paid for. The house offered them terms, and this time there was no vote. Sing forever, and the well stays shut. Stop singing — falter, rest, grow old, die — and it opens again, wider, for the village, for the county, for whoever's nearest.

Six of them are still down there. Or in the walls. The distinction stopped mattering around 1974. Mr. Crane lasted longest in body — died at his piano in 1968, mid-phrase, and the house simply kept using him. Death, it turns out, is not a resignation the house accepts. You sign on for eternity here whether you meant to or not.

"And Edith?" Priya asked. Her breath fogged the glass and my frost drank it, took the warmth of it, used it. "Where were you, all this time? Why didn't you—"

*Sing?* The frost laughed, if frost can laugh — a spatter of white like sneezed flour. *Oh, I sang. Sixty years, I sang. Down in the dark, under the choir, under the floor, I sang the verses they taught me while the water went into my lungs and stayed there, and do you know what I learned?*

The frost drew it for her: the wellhead, the spiral, and beneath it — deeper than brick, deeper than the county's maps went — a shape like a throat, and in the throat, a knot. A knot of song, wound tight, holding the hunger shut the way a cork holds a bottle. Seven voices wound around it. Mine underneath, oldest and deepest, the keystone.

*I am the cork,* I said. *They drowned me to plug the hole, and the hole has been plugged with me ever since. Every note they sing presses me tighter. And every year the hunger grows around me, learning my shape, wearing me smooth.*

Priya had gone very pale. Outside, actual weather — real weather, the honest grey kind — pressed against the other side of the glass, and between the two weathers, real and remembered, her reflection looked thin as paper.

"So it never ends," she said. "Unless—"

*Unless,* I agreed. *Unless someone settles the account properly. Not the way the law did — a stamp, a file, a word like 'removed,' as if I were a stain. Not the way the house did — a bargain, a leash, a song. Properly. With the truth of it spoken out loud by living mouths, and the debt assigned where it belongs, and the last verse sung by everyone except—*

I stopped. Even frost can hesitate.

"Except?" Priya prompted.

*Except one,* I said. *You'll see. Your sister will see. Ask her sometime why she counts doors. Ask her what she thinks counting is *for*.*

Because here is a thing I have watched from the bottom of a well for two months now, watching Xanthe Vasquez pace her corridors with her lips moving through numbers like a rosary: the house chose her. Before the bank sent her, before the appraisal, before any of it — the house read her name somewhere in its endless paperwork and went quiet with satisfaction. A counter. A keeper of figures. A person who believes, down in her bones, that if the numbers are right, the world holds.

The house respects exactly one language. And it has spent sixty years making sure the only person fluent in it would be standing in the parlour when the frost finished writing.

*One more thing,* I said, and the frost turned gentle, which cost me more than the drowning did. I showed her the gloves — Mrs. Fenwick's gloves, half-finished fingers, grey wool gone white in the ice. *I don't want them punished. Understand that. Punishment is just another filing system, and I've been filed enough. I want it *said*. Out loud. What they did, and why, and what it cost. I want it witnessed by someone warm. That's all justice ever was, before the lawyers got hold of it — somebody willing to look.*

Priya put her palm flat on the glass. Her hand steamed faintly against the cold, a little ghost of a handprint, and I pressed the frost up to meet it, careful as a handshake.

"I'm looking," she said.

*Good,* I said. *Because tonight it starts spelling.*

She frowned. "Spelling?"

*Music,* I said, *is just arithmetic that learned to hurt. And the last verse has to come from somewhere. The house is going to write it out — one line a night, in the frost, while the cold lasts. Copy it down, Priya. Every note. And when you see what the final line requires—*

The frost thinned suddenly, all across the pane, my strength guttering like a candle in a doorway. But I had time for the warning, and I spent it all:

*—don't let her open her mouth.*
