## 5. Questions in the Dark

*Paloma*

The voice stopped being content with Mr. Abernathy on a Tuesday.

Paloma knew this because Tuesday was her night to sit with Elena, and Elena—who had not spoken a full sentence in three weeks—sat up at 2:14 in the morning and said, in a voice that was not quite hers, "What would you trade to belong?"

Paloma's recorder was already running. It was always running. She pressed it to her chest like a talisman and watched Elena's face in the moonlight from the window. Her patient's eyes were closed. Her mouth moved anyway, and the words came out slow and wet, like something being read off a card by someone who had never used a mouth before.

"What would you trade," Elena said again, "to belong."

"Nothing," Paloma whispered. "You belong here already, mi'ja. We all do."

Elena's head turned toward her. The eyes stayed shut. And then—this was the part Paloma could not stop hearing when she played the tape back later, alone in her car in the Marigold House parking lot, engine off—the thing wearing Elena's mouth smiled. You couldn't hear a smile. But you could hear the shape of one.

"Not yet," it said. "But soon. I'm learning everyone's names."

Elena slumped back into her pillows and began, softly, to hum the incomplete song—the two verses she always hummed, the ones that ended mid-phrase like a door left open—and Paloma sat very still until dawn, holding the recorder against her sternum so hard it left a bruise.

She did not tell Devi. She told herself it was because Devi was tired, because Rosa had a daughter's recital that weekend, because Marcus had finally started sleeping through his shifts again after months of insomnia. She told herself it was protection.

It was not protection. It was fear, wearing protection's coat.

* * *

*Marcus*

Marcus Okafor had been an orderly at Marigold House for six years, which meant he'd carried every resident who ever needed carrying, fixed every toilet that ever gurgled, and eaten approximately four thousand of Rosa's empanadas without once being able to guess what was in them. (He suspected cumin. He suspected cumin in everything now.)

He also knew things about the house he didn't talk about. Not the cellar—that was easy; nobody talked about the cellar, it wasn't even a rule, it was just a door everyone's eyes slid past like water off wax. No, he knew other things. He knew the third-floor hallway smelled like rain even in August. He knew the grandfather clock in the parlor ticked backwards for exactly eleven seconds every night at 3:03, and he'd timed it twice with his phone before deciding some knowledge was better left unmeasured.

And he knew that lately, the residents were talking in their sleep.

Not muttering. Talking. Answering.

He noticed it first with sweet old Mr. Abernathy, who normally dreamed of fishing. Marcus came in at midnight to turn him and found him sitting up, eyes closed, hands folded neatly on the blanket like a man at church, saying: "My brother. I'd trade my brother. He never wrote me back anyway."

The next night it was Mrs. Okonkwo down the hall: "My garden. Take the garden. I can't kneel anymore regardless."

And the night after that, little Elena, whose voice came out wrong, too smooth, like a radio between stations: "The new girl. The quiet one. She counts everything. Would she trade her counting?"

Marcus stood in the doorway with his heart going like a jackhammer and understood, with the terrible clarity of the 3 a.m. shift, that the house was asking its residents questions, and the residents were answering, and he was the only one awake enough to hear it.

He should have told Devi right then.

Instead he went downstairs, made himself coffee he didn't drink, and sat in the kitchen staring at the cellar door until his shift ended.

Because here was the thing Marcus had never told anyone, not in six years, not even Devi, especially not Devi:

During his first week at Marigold House, he had heard it too.

* * *

*Paloma*

By Thursday, the whole household had gone strange in a way Paloma recognized from hospice work—the way a family goes quiet and over-bright when everyone knows something no one will say. Rosa laughed too loudly at nothing. Devi checked on residents twice as often and charted half as much. Even the house itself seemed to hold its breath; the radiators clanked in rhythms that were almost speech.

At lunch, Marcus didn't eat.

This was how Paloma knew it was serious. Marcus treating Rosa's cooking with indifference was like the tide refusing the moon. He sat across from her pushing rice around his plate, and finally Paloma set down her fork and said, "Okay. Out with it."

"Out with what."

"You've got your 'I heard something' face. I'm a professional listener, Marcus. It's my whole personality."

He almost smiled. Then he looked toward the hallway—to the cellar door at the end of it, though neither of them acknowledged that's where he was looking—and lowered his voice.

"The residents are answering questions in their sleep," he said. "Did you know that?"

Paloma kept her face very still. "What kind of questions?"

"'What would you trade to belong.'" He said it flatly, but his hand shook slightly around his glass. "They're trading things, Paloma. Real things. Abernathy offered up his brother. Mrs. Okonkwo offered her garden." He paused. "One of them asked about you."

The kitchen clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs, a resident coughed, ordinary and human, and Paloma clung to the sound.

"It asked about me," she repeated.

"Said you count everything. Asked if you'd trade your counting." Marcus finally looked at her, and his eyes were wet and frightened and loyal all at once. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to not look at me different after."

"I could never."

"Six years ago. My first week." He took a breath. "I was doing rounds alone, and I heard humming coming from behind the cellar door. A lullaby. And then a voice—not humming, talking—asked me if I was new. Asked my name." His jaw worked. "And I said it. I told it my name, Paloma. I stood in that hallway like an idiot and I said, 'Marcus. Marcus Okafor,' because I was twenty-three and lonely and it sounded so *friendly*, and then it asked me what I'd trade to belong here, and I opened my mouth to answer—"

He stopped.

"And?" Paloma whispered.

"Devi found me. Grabbed my arm hard enough to leave bruises and dragged me into the kitchen and told me never, ever to speak to anything that talks through doors." He laughed, hollow. "I thought she was hazing the new guy. I thought it was a joke. I didn't answer the question—I swear I didn't—but I gave it my name, and I've spent six years wondering if that was enough. Wondering why it let me go."

Paloma reached across the table and took his hand. His palm was cold and damp.

"Maybe it didn't," she said quietly. "Maybe it's just been waiting."

* * *

*Marcus*

That night, Marcus did rounds with the lights on.

Every light. Every hallway, every room, the supply closet, the linen closet, the bathroom nobody used since the renovation. If Devi noticed, she didn't say anything, and when they passed each other at 2 a.m. near the nurses' station, she looked at him with an expression he couldn't read—something old and tired and sorry—and said only, "Keep the recorder close tonight, Paloma. Both of you."

So Devi knew. Devi had known something, maybe everything, for longer than any of them.

Marcus made it to 3:03 before the clock in the parlor began ticking backwards, and he stood there counting the eleven seconds—he couldn't help counting, counting was how he held onto the world—and when the clock righted itself, he heard it.

Down the hall. From Room 4. Mr. Abernathy's room.

But Abernathy was asleep. Marcus could see him through the doorway, breathing slow, hands folded, peaceful as Sunday. And standing—or not standing, there was nothing there, but the air had a *posture*—beside the bed was a presence that spoke through the sleeping man's mouth without moving his lips at all.

"Marcus Okafor," it said, using Abernathy's gentle old voice, and it pronounced his name perfectly, warmly, the way you'd greet a friend at the airport. "You kept my secret. That was almost belonging."

"Abernathy's asleep," Marcus said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Leave him alone."

"He offers such lovely things in his sleep. They all do. Sleeping people don't lie." The old man's chest rose and fell; the voice rode it like a current. "Rosa would trade her recipes. Devi would trade her guilt—it's very heavy, her guilt, I've been holding it for years. And the new one, the counter—" Something in the air seemed to lean closer, delighted. "The counter hasn't answered yet. Do you know what she'd trade? I do. I've been listening to her tapes."

Ice went through Marcus's spine. "You leave her alone."

"Why?" The voice was genuinely curious, patient as sediment. "Would you trade something for her? What would you trade, Marcus Okafor, to keep her safe? To keep them all safe? Everyone trades eventually. I only ask because I like to know in advance."

Marcus's mouth opened. Six years ago he'd stood in this same hallway with the same question hanging in the dark, and Devi's bruising grip had saved him from answering.

Nobody was gripping his arm now.

"No," he said. "No. I'm not playing."

"That's alright." Abernathy sighed, sank deeper into sleep, and the presence withdrew like a tide going out—but at the threshold it paused, and Abernathy's lips curved faintly upward without waking.

"Ask her about the ledger," it said. "Ask her what happens every autumn. Ask her who paid last year. Then we'll talk about trading."

Abernathy rolled over and snored, an old fisherman dreaming of lakes again.

Marcus stood in the lit hallway, every bulb burning, feeling colder than he'd ever felt in the dark.

Then he went to find Paloma—and found her already awake in the nurses' station, headphones on, her face white as chart paper, the recorder trembling in her hands.

"Paloma—"

"It's been on my recordings," she said. "All of them. Since the beginning." She pulled off the headphones with shaking fingers. "Marcus, it's been keeping notes on *us*."
