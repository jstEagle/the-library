## 8. Elena's Last Verse

Elena stopped eating on a Tuesday, which was how everyone at Marigold House knew the end had begun in earnest. Rosa took it personally. She spent the whole afternoon in the kitchen making caldo de pollo from scratch, simmering it down to something golden and fragrant enough to raise the dead, and carried it up to Elena's room on her best tray with a cloth napkin folded into a swan.

Elena ate three spoonfuls and pronounced it "almost as good as my mother's, which is a lie, but you looked so hopeful."

Rosa came back downstairs crying and laughing at the same time, which was more or less her resting state anyway.

"She's got maybe two days," Devi said quietly, catching Paloma by the medication cart. Her voice was steady the way it always was—head nurse steady, the voice that could talk a panicking family off a ledge—but her hands were busy straightening cups that didn't need straightening. "Hospice says tonight or tomorrow night. I want someone sitting with her around the clock."

"I'll take the nights," Paloma said.

Devi looked at her for a long moment. There was something in that look—something that wanted to be said and couldn't find a door out—and then Marcus wheeled the linen cart past singing badly to himself, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

* * *

The house began counting at nine o'clock.

Paloma was sitting beside Elena's bed with her recorder running—old habit, old armor—when she realized the sound wasn't coming only from Elena's room. It was coming from everywhere. From under the floor. Through the vents. Out of the walls themselves, the way heat comes out of them in winter, patient and diffuse and wrong.

She stepped into the hallway.

Mr. Abernathy stood in his doorway in his pajamas, eyes open but unfocused, his lips moving. Down the hall, Mrs. Okafor's door stood ajar, and through the gap Paloma could hear her too, her voice thin and papery, keeping perfect time. Even the residents who hadn't spoken clearly in months were counting. The whole house was counting, backwards, in a dozen cracked and trembling voices, all of them synchronized like they'd rehearsed.

And beneath them, holding the tempo like a conductor, was *the other one*. Not whispering anymore. Not hiding between counts. Speaking aloud, in a voice like cold water finding cracks in stone:

*Nine. Eight. Seven.*

The residents' voices followed it down.

Paloma's first instinct was to run door to door and wake everyone, shake them out of it—but when she touched Mr. Abernathy's shoulder, he kept counting without pausing, without blinking, and his skin was cold as cellar stone. So instead she did what she always did. She documented. She noted the time, 9:14 p.m., the number the count had started from, the way the voices braided together. Her handwriting shook. She made herself write anyway. *If I write it down,* she thought, *it stays outside me. If it stays outside me, it can't get in.*

Then she heard Elena.

Not counting. Elena's voice, reedy and furious, cut across the count like a knife through rope: *"Oh, hush. You never learned any manners."*

The whole house went silent at once. Every voice stopped mid-number. The silence rang.

Paloma went back into Elena's room and shut the door.

* * *

"You heard it," Elena said. It wasn't a question. She was propped up against her pillows, small as a bird in the big bed, her eyes bright and terribly clear—the midnight lucidity, except it wasn't midnight yet. Dying people got their clarity early sometimes, like they were packing. "You've been hearing it for weeks. Don't look surprised, mija. You carry that recorder everywhere like a rosary. You think I haven't noticed you flinching every time the furnace kicks on?"

Paloma sat down slowly in the chair by the bed. "How long have you known?"

"Since the second week you worked here." Elena smiled, and there was mischief in it even now, even tonight. "You hum when you chart. Did you know that? You hum when you're nervous. And last month you hummed a song you couldn't possibly know unless you'd been listening where listening isn't polite."

"The song you hum," Paloma said. "The incomplete one."

"Mm." Elena's smile faded into something older. "I've been humming it for sixty years, waiting for someone to ask me about it. Isn't that funny? All this time I thought I'd take it to the grave unfinished, and then God sends me a nurse who records everything." She held out her hand, papery and light. "Come here. We don't have much night left, and less of me."

Paloma took her hand. It was like holding a sparrow.

"It has three verses," Elena said. "You know the first two—you've heard me hum them, and I expect you've heard *it* hum them too, though I imagine it does it prettier. The third verse no one living knows. No one alive now, anyway. I learned it from my grandmother, who learned it from the woman who sang the thing to sleep the first time, back before this house had a name. A lullaby, mija. That's all it ever was. Somebody's baby, once. Something that got sung to, and loved, and then forgotten, and forgetting is what curdled it."

"And the third verse?"

"The third verse is the binding. The first two verses are a cradle. The third is the lid." Elena's grip tightened, surprising strength in it. "But listen to me now, because this is the part that matters, the part my grandmother made me swear on her Bible: you sing it with your mouth *closed*. You mouth the words. You let no breath pass your lips. Because the verse is an answer, do you understand? Every word of it is an answer to its questions. And if you answer it aloud—if you give it your voice—it doesn't bind you to it. It binds you *into* it. You'd belong to it forever, and belonging is all it wants, and it will make belonging feel so sweet you'll thank it while it swallows you."

Paloma's mouth had gone dry. "Why are you telling me this? Why not Devi? She's been here twenty years—"

"Devi knows *about* it. Knowing about it and knowing the verse are different animals." Elena coughed, wet and long, and Paloma reached for the basin, and Elena waved her off with the imperiousness of a queen. "I'm telling you because you're the one it talks to. Don't argue. It said your name in your recordings—I know it did, because it said mine fifty years ago, the autumn I worked here as a girl. And I answered nothing, and I stayed silent, and I watched two others answer, and I have carried that silence like a stone in my chest ever since." Her eyes filled, but her voice didn't break. "Silence keeps you safe, mija, but it doesn't keep you *good*. Tonight I get to put my stone down. Take the verse. Take it and finish what my grandmother's friend started."

* * *

She taught it in fragments, because that was all dying left her—pieces between coughs, between drifts, between the moments when her eyes rolled back toward something Paloma couldn't see and she'd snap back and say, sharply, "Don't listen when I do that. It's rude to eavesdrop on both sides."

The melody Paloma already knew; Elena had been humming it into the house for decades like a stitch through fabric. But the words of the third verse came slow, in Spanish first, then translated, each line repeated until Paloma could hold it:

*Lo que fue cantado debe ser callado* — what was sung must be silenced—

No. Not silenced. Elena corrected her, gripping her wrist hard enough to bruise. *Guardado.* Kept. Guarded. Held safe. "It's not a punishment, the verse. It's a promise. You're promising to keep its song for it, so it can finally rest. Singing it aloud is refusing the promise. Mouthing it is making it."

Line by line. Fragment by fragment. Outside, the house had begun counting again, softer now, almost shy, numbers seeping up through the floorboards: *five... four...*

"Again," Elena whispered. "From the top of the verse. And close your mouth this time. Show me."

Paloma mouthed it. Felt the strange pressure of unsung words, the shape of them pushing at her teeth like birds at a window. When she finished, the counting below stuttered—just for a heartbeat—and resumed.

Elena laughed, a dry rustle of delight. "It felt that. Sixty years I waited to feel it flinch." Then her face grew grave, and she pulled Paloma close, close enough that Paloma could smell lavender and something underneath it like pond water. "One more thing, and then you'll let me sleep, because I intend to die rested and beautiful. The harvest moon is tomorrow night. It will come to you—it always comes to whoever holds the verse—and it will offer you whatever you're hungriest for. For me it offered my mother, back from the dead, asking me one question. I nearly answered. God forgive me, I nearly answered." Her thumb traced a small circle on Paloma's knuckles. "What will it offer you, I wonder?"

Paloma didn't answer. She didn't have to. They both knew.

"Yes," Elena said softly. "That. Be careful, mija. Being seen is a lovely thing and a terrible price, and sometimes they're the same thing." She settled back into her pillows, suddenly exhausted, suddenly small again. "Now sing me the first two verses. Aloud—that one's allowed, that one's just a lullaby. Sing me to sleep like I'm somebody's baby. I'd like to go out being somebody's baby."

So Paloma sang, her voice cracking on the second line, and Elena's breathing slowed and deepened, and somewhere below them the counting wound down through *three*, through *two*, and stopped—not finished, Paloma noticed. Stopped. Waiting.

* * *

Elena died at 4:17 in the morning, between one breath and the next, so gently that Paloma only knew because the room felt suddenly larger. Paloma closed her eyes, kissed her forehead, and sat with her hand until the sky went grey.

Then she opened her recorder, and spoke the time, the date, the name, in her documentation voice, her armor voice. And when she played it back, beneath her own words, faint as a memory, something in the cellar was humming the first two verses of the lullaby.

Waiting for the third.

Tomorrow was the harvest moon.
