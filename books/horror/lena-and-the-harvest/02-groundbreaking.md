## 2. Groundbreaking

The Marchetti field had lain fallow for sixty years, and Dane Voss felt it watching him before the first bucket ever bit the earth.

He stood at the edge of the property at seven in the morning, coffee cooling in a thermos cup, and looked out over the wheat gone wild—knee-high, silver-headed, leaning in long waves whenever the wind moved, which was often, though the morning was otherwise still. The field sat in a shallow bowl of land two miles east of Hollow Creek's dying main street, ringed by a fence that had been new sometime during the Eisenhower administration and had surrendered to rust shortly after. Beyond it, the hills rolled brown and gold toward the county line.

Sixty years fallow. In farm country, that wasn't rest. That was neglect, or superstition, or both. Every other acre within sight of the water tower had been turned and planted and turned again since before Dane was born. But not this one. Old Man Alder used to say the Marchettis kept it like you'd keep a grave—mowed but never plowed, tended but never touched. And when the last Marchetti besides Felix had died, nobody had taken up the habit. The wheat just kept coming back on its own, thick as carpet, year after year, asking nothing from anyone.

"Daylight's burning," Ray Voss called from the cab of the excavator. Dane's uncle—his father's younger brother, foreman of Voss Excavation for going on twenty years—sat with his arm hooked out the window, squinting against the low sun. "You gonna stand there admiring the scenery or are you gonna work?"

"I'm appreciating it," Dane said. "There's a difference."

"There's a paycheck difference." Ray grinned, all teeth and sun-creased leather. "Get your ass on the skid steer."

Dane drained his coffee and went.

The crew was small that morning—seven men, which was everyone Voss Excavation had left after the layoffs. Ray on the excavator. Dane running the skid steer. Marty Kowalski and his son T.J. on the grade stakes. Old Pete Hargrove, who'd been digging holes in Hollow Creek since before Dane could walk, walking the perimeter with a dowser's seriousness he claimed was a joke. The Delgado cousins, Ruben and Sam, hauling fence line. And at the far edge of the lot, parked crooked because its driver never bothered, the pickup belonging to the seventh man, who hadn't shown yet.

Dane knew why he'd come to this job. Everyone on the crew knew, though nobody said it. The county had finally released the Marchetti parcel for development—a distribution warehouse, of all things, forty thousand square feet of concrete block and loading docks, the kind of building that could be dropped onto any flat piece of ground between here and Denver. The money was decent. The work was steady. And the field was the last big undeveloped parcel in the county, which meant it was the only reason a company like theirs would still be breathing.

But Dane had another reason, one he kept folded up tight behind his ribs like a letter he couldn't bring himself to open. Felix. Fourteen months since they'd spoken—no, longer, he'd stopped counting, which was its own kind of counting—and the estrangement had calcified into something neither of them knew how to break. Their mother's funeral had been the last time they'd stood in the same room, and even then they'd managed it only by standing at opposite ends of the gravesite, each pretending the other was a stranger the cemetery had hired for atmosphere.

*You bury people for a living,* Dane had told him once, in the last real fight, the one that ended everything. *That's all you've ever done. You bury them and you file them and you drive home and you sleep fine.*

*I keep them comfortable,* Felix had said. *There's a difference.*

*There's a paycheck difference,* Dane had said, throwing their uncle's own cynicism back at him like a stone, and Felix had looked at him then with an expression Dane still thought about at three in the morning—not anger, not hurt, but something worse. Recognition. Like Dane had confirmed something Felix had always suspected about him and about himself and about the distance between what people do and what they mean.

Now here Dane was, about to dig up the one piece of ground in Hollow Creek that his brother's family had kept untouched for six generations, and he couldn't decide if that made him a traitor or just a man with a mortgage.

"Voss!" Ray again. "Quit daydreaming."

*

The first hour was almost pleasant. Late September, air cool enough to work in, the diesel rumble settling into a rhythm Dane's body knew better than his own heartbeat. He cut fence line with the Delgados while Ray walked the site with the surveyor's map, frowning at it, turning it sideways, frowning again.

"Something wrong?" Dane asked.

"Stakes don't match the plat." Ray tapped the paper. "County says the buildable footprint starts forty feet further east. Says there's a drainage easement through here." He gestured at the exact center of the field, where the wheat grew thickest, tallest, as if the middle of the bowl were drinking deeper than the edges.

"So we shift east."

"We shift east." Ray pocketed the map, but he kept looking west, at the dense center of the field, for a beat too long. Then he shook it off and hollered for the crew to set new stakes.

By nine they were cutting the first pass. The excavator's bucket swung out and dropped and bit, and sixty years of undisturbed soil came up in dark, heavy curls. It smelled rich, almost sweet—the smell of really good dirt, blacker than anything else in the valley, and Pete Hargrove stopped his perimeter walk to stand over the spoil pile and crumble some of it between his fingers.

"This ain't right," Pete said.

"It's beautiful is what it is," Marty Kowalski called from the stakes. "Topsoil like that, you could sell it by the bag."

"That's what I'm saying." Pete let the soil run through his fingers. "This ground hasn't been farmed in sixty years. Nothing's been added to it. No manure, no compost, nothing. Dirt gets tired, boys. Dirt gets thin. This dirt—" he held up a fistful, black as coffee grounds, wriggling faintly with worms, fat white ones, more of them than Dane had ever seen in one handful— "this dirt has been *fed*."

"You're a poet, Pete," Ray said. "Dig."

They dug. By ten they had a trench twenty feet long and three feet deep along the eastern stake line, and Dane had settled into the work enough to stop thinking about Felix, mostly, except every time the excavator's bucket came down he thought about how his brother would have hated this—not the noise, Felix didn't care about noise, but the violation of it. Six generations of Marchettis leaving one field alone, and here came the Voss family with machines to crack it open inside a single morning. The Marchettis and the Vosses had been neighbors since the town was founded; their grandfathers had traded seed corn and borrowed each other's combines. Dane wondered if old Manfred Marchetti, Felix's namesake grandfather, dead now thirty years, was somewhere in the ground under this wheat, disapproving.

At eleven-fifteen, they hit stone.

Ray felt it before anyone heard it—a shudder through the whole machine, a hard refusal, the bucket bouncing instead of biting. He killed the hydraulics and climbed down and stood at the lip of the trench looking at what the bucket had exposed, and he didn't say anything for a long time, and Ray Voss not saying anything was louder than most men shouting.

"What've we got?" Dane asked, dropping into the trench beside him.

"Stone," Ray said. "Four feet down."

Dane looked. The trench floor, where the bucket had scraped it clean, was exactly that: stone. Not boulders, not rubble, not glacial junk. A surface. Flat, dressed, fitted. Pale gray slabs laid edge to edge in a pattern too regular to be natural, running away under the unexcavated ground in every direction like the top of an enormous table buried beneath the field.

"Bedrock?" T.J. Kowalski asked, climbing down to look. He was nineteen and eager and hadn't learned yet to be quiet around his elders' silences.

"At four feet?" Ray crouched and put his palm flat on the stone. "Son, bedrock in this valley is eighty feet down. I know because I've drilled through it four times. This isn't bedrock. This is *laid*."

Marty came down into the trench, knelt, and ran a hand along the seam between two slabs. "Fitted joints. Look at this—there's no mortar. They're cut to fit. Somebody spent some serious money cutting these." He looked up. "Quarry work. Good quarry work. This is older than the town."

"The town's a hundred and fifty years old," Dane said.

"Then this is older than a hundred and fifty years," Marty said, "because there's no record of any quarry within fifty miles that cuts stone like this. I did two years of geology before I quit school. This is dressed granite. There is no granite in this county. Nearest source is the front range, ninety miles east, and hauling this much of it here with horses would've cost a fortune." He stood, wiping his hands on his thighs. "Somebody built a floor under this field. A big one. And then buried it."

"Maybe it's a foundation," T.J. offered. "Old homestead?"

"A foundation for what? There's no structure on any map of this parcel going back to the 1870s. I checked—I check everything before we dig, that's my job." Marty's voice had gone careful, the way voices go when a man is trying very hard to sound reasonable. "And foundations sit at frost depth. This is four feet. You don't pour a footing at four feet in freeze country unless you want it heaved to pieces by spring."

"Root cellar," said Pete, from above. He hadn't come down into the trench. He stood at its edge with his hands in his pockets, looking down at the stone the way a man looks at a dog that has just shown its teeth. "Or a cistern. Or a crypt. Towns used to have family crypts."

"On a farm field? Under wheat?" Ray shook his head. "Pete, you got a theory, share it."

"My theory," Pete said, "is that the Marchettis knew this was here, and that's why nobody plowed this field for sixty years, and that's why I think we should load up and call the historical society and let somebody else's crew find out what's under it."

"Historical society doesn't sign our paychecks," Ray said. "The developer does. Developer wants a warehouse. Warehouse wants footings at eight feet." He looked down at the stone slab under his boots, and Dane watched his uncle arrive at the same arithmetic every man in the trench was doing: eight feet minus four feet of stone equals four feet of granite to break through, across the entire footprint, and granite doesn't care about deadlines.

"Hydraulic hammer'll take it," Dane said. "Rent the big one from Billings."

"Renting the big one costs four hundred a day plus bits. Bits on dressed granite, we'll chew through two a week minimum." Ray rubbed his jaw. "I gotta call the developer. Everybody smoke 'em if you got 'em."

The crew climbed out of the trench, and Dane was last, and at the top he turned and looked back down at it. The trench ran straight and clean through the black soil, and at its bottom lay the pale fitted stone, and the noon light fell into the cut at an angle that made the shadows of the wheat lean across the exposed surface like fingers.

It was then he noticed the marks.

They were scratched into the tops of the slabs—shallow, deliberate, spaced with a regularity that made his skin prickle before his mind caught up to why. Groups of four vertical strokes with a fifth struck across them, the way prisoners count days. Group after group after group, covering every slab the trench had exposed, hundreds of tallies, maybe thousands, worn soft at the edges by time but unmistakable.

"Marty," Dane said. His voice came out strange. "Come look at this."

Marty came back and looked, and the color left his face in a way Dane had never seen happen to a grown man outside of a hospital waiting room.

"Somebody was counting something," Marty said quietly. "Down there. In the dark. For a long, long time."

Above them, the wind moved through the wheat, and the whole field leaned and whispered, and Dane had the sudden irrational certainty that it was whispering about them.

*

Ray got the developer on the phone by one o'clock and got an answer Dane should have expected: core it, sample it, keep moving. Time was money; the warehouse had tenants lined up; the stone was probably just some old settler's folly and could be hammered through like anything else. Ray relayed all of this standing beside the trench with his back pointedly turned to the tally marks, as if the marks could hear him.

So they stayed. And the afternoon went long and strange.

The hydraulic hammer arrived on a trailer at two, and by three it was mounted and screaming, and the sound it made against the buried stone was unlike anything Dane had heard in fifteen years of excavation. Steel on dressed granite should ring—a bright, hard, bell-like concussion. This didn't ring. It *answered*. Each blow landed with a flat, dead thud, as if the stone were absorbing the impact rather than resisting it, and after the first ten strikes Dane noticed the crew drifting away from the trench one man at a time, finding reasons to be at the trucks, at the porta-john, at the far fence line, until only Ray remained at the controls, jaw set, feeding the hammer bit after bit, refusing to look at whatever it was he refused to look at.

At four-thirty the hammer broke a bit. At five it broke a second. Ray shut it down, and the silence that followed was so total that Dane could hear the wheat moving a quarter mile away.

"Four feet of stone in three hours," Ray said, climbing down. His voice was level but his eyes weren't. "We've moved maybe two square feet. At this rate the footings alone are a season's work. I'm calling it. We regroup tomorrow."

Nobody argued. Nobody ever argues with quitting time, but today nobody even joked about it, and the trucks pulled off the parcel in a loose convoy with their lights already on although the sun wouldn't set for two hours.

Dane was last out again. He paused at the gate with his hand on the latch and looked back at the field—at the trench lying open in the evening light like a wound, at the pale stone showing in its floor, at the wheat standing thick and silent around it all.

His phone buzzed. A number he knew by heart, though he'd deleted it twice.

*Hear you're digging up the field.* Felix. First words between them in fourteen months, sent like a stone through a window. *Don't.*

Dane stared at the screen. The wind moved. Somewhere behind him, down in the darkening trench, in the space between the fitted slabs, something shifted—a sound like a slow exhalation, like a held breath finally released after a very long time.

He typed back: *Since when do you care what I dig?*

The reply came fast, faster than Felix ever texted, and it was four words long:

*Since I counted them.*

Dane stood at the gate until the light failed, and the field waited with him, patient as a held breath, and far below the wheat, below the black fed soil, below the dressed and numbered stone, something that had been counting for a very long time felt the first cold thread of daylight touch its ceiling, and adjusted its arithmetic.
