## 10. The Field at Night

The fuel gauge on the Voss pickup had been lying to them for two days.

Dane knew it the way you know a man is dying—by small signs, by the way the needle trembled where it should have held steady, by the smell of something underneath the smell of gasoline. The generator at the farmhouse drank diesel like a man dying of thirst, and the reserve cans in the barn were down to fumes. The nearest station with a working pump was the Co-op on Route 9, four miles east, and between here and there lay the Marchetti field.

Nobody said *the Marchetti field*. They said *out that way*, or *around the back forty*, or nothing at all, which was what most people chose.

Dane stood in the barn doorway at eleven o'clock at night with a flashlight in one hand and a list in his head: three men, two trucks, the long way around on the county road, no cutting through, no stopping, no matter what they saw or heard. He'd made the plan twice over in his head and then a third time just to have something to do besides listen to the wheat.

Because the wheat was talking again.

It wasn't words. It had never been words, not exactly. It was the sound the field made when the wind moved through it—or would have been, if there'd been any wind. There hadn't been wind for three days. The stalks stood straight as nails under a fat white moon, and still they rustled, low and continuous, like a crowd murmuring behind a closed door, like his mother's hospice ward in the last hour before visiting ended.

"You don't have to go," Felix said behind him. His brother had come out without a coat, arms crossed against the October cold, and he looked older than fifty-two. He looked like their father at the end.

"Somebody's got to." Dane checked the flashlight again. "Ruthie's on oxygen. If the generator quits in the night—" He didn't finish it. Ruthie Voss was nine years old and counting backward from thirty in her sleep, and every number she lost was a little more color gone out of her face. "I'm not letting her die because we were scared to drive to the Co-op."

"It's not the driving I'm worried about."

"I know what you're worried about."

"Then say it."

Dane turned and looked at him. In the moonlight Felix's face was all hollows and stubbornness, the same face he'd been making across kitchen tables for thirty years, through funerals and foreclosures and the slow estrangement of two brothers who loved each other in the way that men of Hollow Creek loved anything—silently, badly, too late.

"The field moves," Dane said. It sounded insane in the open air, and he was glad Ruthie couldn't hear him, glad none of the families could. "You've seen it. I've seen it. The rows don't go where they went yesterday. So we take the road. We stay on gravel the whole way. Gravel doesn't move."

Felix didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice was very quiet. "The ledger doesn't end at the town line, Dane. Whatever's out there—it counts names, not miles."

"Then it can count me driving." He shouldered past his brother, and that was the last thing either of them said about it.

* * *

They took the gray F-250 and left the Suburban for the women, though nobody called anyone a woman anymore; they were all just *the ones inside now*. Dane drove. Ray Okonkwo rode shotgun with a crowbar across his knees, and Marty Pell sat in the back seat with the empty fuel cans rattling around his boots, humming to himself the way he always hummed when he was frightened, tuneless, a habit from the crews, from the good years when fear had been about rebar and OSHA fines.

The county road ran straight and pale under the moon. On both sides the fields lay flat and silvered—soybeans to the north, the Marchetti wheat to the south—and Dane kept his eyes on the centerline and his speed at fifty-five and told himself the story he'd been telling himself since the door came up out of the ground: *stone at four feet, an oak door banded in iron, sealed from the inside, tally marks still being added.* A hole in the world dressed up as a construction problem. He'd spent twenty years solving problems with a backhoe and a level. This one wouldn't grade.

"Radio's dead," Marty said from the back.

"It's off. I turned it off."

"It was on. I heard it." Marty's voice had gone thin. "Static. Then somebody counting."

Dane's hands tightened on the wheel. Behind them, in the mirror, the farmhouse shrank to a yellow square, then a spark, then nothing. Ahead, the Co-op sign glowed its tired green half-mile out, and he felt something in his chest unclench—one errand, one run, one small proof that the world outside the field still worked on ordinary rules.

Ray laughed, sudden and loud, the way he did to break tension. "Four miles. Four goddamn miles and we're acting like it's the Bataan death march. Fill the cans, get some jerky, come home. That's the whole mission."

"That's the whole mission," Dane agreed.

The Co-op was dark but the card reader worked, and the pump ran rough and coughed air before it caught, and they filled six five-gallon cans while Marty stood watch at the edge of the lot, facing west, toward home. Dane paid with cash because the register clerk—a kid, maybe nineteen, who'd slept through the whole thing apparently, who thought the excavation crew had just quit—rang him up yawning. Ordinary. It was so ordinary it hurt. A bell over the door. A rack of lottery tickets. A poster for the harvest festival, CANCELLED scrawled across it in marker.

He was carrying the last two cans to the truck when Marty started shouting.

Not words. Just his name, over and over—*Dane, Dane, Dane*—and pointing west, and Dane turned with the cans still in his hands and looked down Route 9 toward Hollow Creek and felt his understanding of the night tilt sideways.

The field was closer.

He knew it the way you know your own kitchen in the dark. The Marchetti field began a quarter mile past the Kessler fence line—that was surveyor's fact, that was his own crew's stakes, he'd set them himself in April. But the wheat now came right up to the shoulder of the county road, a black wall of stalks taller than the truck, swaying without wind, and beyond it, where the road should have run on toward the farmhouse, there was only more wheat. The road didn't end. It was *buried*. Rows of it lay across the gravel like fallen timber, like water closing over a stone, and even as he watched, another row slid out of the mass and laid itself down across the asphalt with a dry whispering sound, patient as a tide.

"Get in the truck," Dane said.

"Dane—"

"*Get in the truck.*"

Ray was already moving. Marty scrambled around the bed and dove into the back seat, and Dane threw the cans in the bed, slammed the tailgate, and cranked the engine, and the headlights swept the wall of wheat and showed him—just for a second, just at the edge of the beams—the stalks leaning toward the light. Not falling. *Leaning.* Like grass under a hand.

He put the truck in gear and drove at the buried road.

The first rows were loose, dry, and the tires chewed through them, wheat thrashing the undercarriage, stalks whipping the mirrors. The third row was different. It lay across the road like a cable, dense as rope, and the truck hit it and bucked and stalled, and in the silence after, every man in the cab heard the field settle around them. Not rushing. Settling. The way a body settles into a chair it has decided to keep.

"Reverse," Ray said. "Back up, back up—"

Dane turned the key. The engine caught, and he backed twenty feet, and the wheat behind them had already closed. There was no road back. There was no road forward. There was the truck, and the moon, and on every side the field standing in ranks, tall and silent, and the rustling had stopped, which was worse. Silence meant listening.

"We go around," Marty whispered. "There's the Kessler track, the old farm road, it comes out—"

"The Kessler track goes through the middle of it." Dane's voice came out flat and strange, calm in the way of a man reading his own autopsy. "Everything goes through the middle of it. Look."

And they looked, all three, and understood at the same moment that the field was no longer a field with edges. To the north, where the soybeans had been, wheat. To the east, back the way they'd come, wheat rolling low and endless under the moon. The Co-op's green sign still glowed behind them, impossibly close, impossibly far away, a lighthouse on a sea that had swallowed the land it stood on.

Ray picked up the crowbar. "We drive until we're through. It's wheat. It's *plants*, Dane. It's not—"

A stalk tapped the driver's side window.

Once. Politely. The way a teller taps a glass.

Nobody spoke. Nobody breathed. The stalk withdrew, and then, further out, another tapped the bed of the truck, and another, moving away from them in a slow line into the dark, tap, tap, tap, going somewhere, and Dane understood with a certainty that lived below thought: it was showing them the path. It wanted them to follow.

"Don't," he said aloud, to all of them, to himself. "Whatever you see. Don't follow it."

But the truck wouldn't start again. And the tapping went on, patient, receding, and the moon was sinking, and they had hours yet until dawn, and the cold was coming up out of the ground like breath.

* * *

They walked.

There was no decision in it that Dane could remember making—only the fact of it, later, the three of them shoulder to shoulder in the narrow lane the wheat had opened, walls of stalks eight feet high on either side, the lane barely wide enough for two. The tapping led them. Tap, tap, tap, ahead and to the left, unhurried, and behind them the lane closed as they passed, softly, the way water closes, and Dane made the mistake once of turning to look and saw the wheat seamless behind them, unbroken, as if no three men had ever walked there, and after that he kept his eyes forward.

Ray held the crowbar like a rosary. Marty had stopped humming, which frightened Dane more than the tapping.

"How long since we left the Co-op?" Marty asked.

"Twenty minutes," Dane said.

"It feels like—"

"I know how it feels. Don't say it."

Time had gone soft. The moon hung where it had hung an hour ago, or a year ago. The lane bent, gently, the way paths bend, and each bend seemed designed—not random, never random—to bring some new thing into view and then take it away. Once it was the farmhouse, whole and lit, close enough to see Ruthie's window, and then the lane curved and it was gone. Once it was the excavation site, the pit, the door standing upright at the bottom of it, iron-banded oak, and the tally marks on its face catching moonlight like scars. And once—

Once it was his father.

Standing off in the wheat, ten rows deep maybe, in his good funeral coat, hands at his sides, face turned toward Dane with an expression of mild expectation, the expression of a man waiting to be handed something. Their father had been dead eleven years. Dane knew this the way he knew the surveyor's stakes. He also felt, with equal force, that if he stepped off the lane—just four steps, just five—his father would explain everything. Would make it make sense. Fathers did that.

"Dane." Ray's hand on his arm, hard. "Eyes forward."

"He's—"

"He isn't. Eyes *forward*."

Ahead, the tapping stopped.

The lane opened into a clearing that had no business existing, a circle perhaps thirty feet across, packed earth, and at its center stood the door.

Not in a pit. Not in a frame. Standing upright in the bare dirt like a headstone for a giant, oak and iron, and the tally marks covered it now from edge to edge, thousands of them, and as Dane watched, a new mark scored itself across the wood with a sound like a fingernail on a coffin lid. The marks near the bottom were old, weathered gray. The marks near the top were fresh-cut and pale. And above them all, at the height of a man's raised hand, the wood was blank—waiting, Dane thought, for the last rows. For the names still living. For theirs.

The door was open.

Only a crack. Six inches of darkness between the oak and its frame, and the darkness did not look like night or like shadow or like the inside of anything. It looked like attention. It looked like a room that had been watching them since April, since the first stake went into the ground, since the first Marchetti fed the first name to the count two hundred years ago, and it was not angry and it was not hungry, or if it was those things, they were such old hungers, such patient angers, that they had gone quiet as furniture.

Something waited behind it. Dane could not see it and knew everything necessary about it anyway: that it was vast in the way geology is vast, that it had counted every soul in Hollow Creek backward to zero and had never once been in a hurry, and that it was looking at him now with the mild interest of a farmer checking a crop coming in.

Marty made a sound. Small, wet, involuntary.

"No," Dane said. He didn't know who he was saying it to. "No. We're not—we're not doing this. We're going home."

He grabbed Marty's collar and Ray's sleeve and walked them backward, away from the door, and the door did not pursue them, did not swing wide, did nothing at all, and somehow that was the worst of it. It didn't need to do anything. It had all the time that the ledger had names.

The lane was still there. It led them away, and the walls of wheat closed behind them like water, and the tapping resumed somewhere ahead, courteous, guiding, and Dane walked with his eyes fixed on the backs of his own eyelids if he could manage it, on the gravel when it finally came, real gravel, real road, the farmhouse rising out of the dark with its yellow windows and its generator drone and its nine-year-old girl counting backward in her sleep.

* * *

They came in at dawn.

Felix met them at the porch steps, took one look, and asked no questions, which was its own kind of mercy. Ray set the crowbar against the wall and sat down on the step and put his face in his hands. Marty walked past all of them into the house and did not stop until he reached the far bedroom, and he did not come out that day, or speak, and in the weeks after he would sit with his back always to the windows, facing the interior walls, as if the middle of a house were the only direction left that was safe.

Six cans of fuel stood in the truck bed. Dane carried them in one at a time and stacked them by the generator, and his hands were steady, and that steadiness frightened him more than anything in the field, because it wasn't calm. It was the stillness of a man who has seen the shape of his own death and found it, horribly, orderly.

Felix followed him out on the last trip. The sun was coming up red over the tree line, and the field lay flat and innocent in the early light, dew on the stalks, edges exactly where the surveyor's stakes said they should be.

"You saw it," Felix said. Not a question.

Dane set down the last can. He looked at his brother, at the man who had spent a lifetime explaining away the voices of the dying, and heard himself say the thing he had sworn in the field he would never say:

"It was open, Felix. The door was open. And whatever's behind it—" His voice failed and restarted, quieter. "It wasn't waiting for us. That's the thing. It wasn't even interested. We're not due yet."

Felix went very still. And Dane watched something move across his brother's face—something that looked, terribly, like recognition, like a man hearing his own name read off a list—and Felix said, in a voice Dane had never heard him use:

"There's a ledger in the county archive. I need to show you something about our family."

Behind them, inside the house, faint through the walls, Ruthie Voss was talking in her sleep.

She was at seven.
