## 12. The Count Quickens

The night it happened, Dane Voss was standing in the kitchen of his family's farmhouse with a shotgun he didn't know how to use and a pot of coffee nobody would drink, when the first scream came from the parlor.

He knew before he moved. He knew the way you know a storm is coming by the smell of the air. Two nights ago there had been eleven names left on the ledger with dates that hadn't happened yet, and Felix had gone through them one by one with a pencil, crossing off nothing, because nothing could be crossed off. The count only went one direction.

Marta Reyes was in the parlor. Her husband Emilio had been one of the three who vanished at the excavation—Dane had seen the boots, arranged in their neat row like shoes outside a temple—and she had not slept since. She sat in the rocking chair by the cold fireplace every night with her daughter's hand in hers, refusing to close her eyes, as if wakefulness were a wall the thing beneath the field couldn't climb.

Dane came through the doorway in time to hear her say "nine."

She wasn't talking to anyone. She was looking at the window, at the black glass where the wheat stood beyond the yard, and her voice had changed—had dropped into a register no living throat should own, layered, like several people speaking through one mouth in imperfect unison. Her daughter Ana was shaking her shoulder, saying *Mama, Mama*, and Marta's eyes slid past the girl without catching on her, the way your eyes slide past furniture.

"Eight," said Marta Reyes, in the voice of a dead woman Dane had never met.

"Get Felix," he said. "Get Felix *now*."

* * *

Felix arrived in nine minutes, driving too fast down the farm road with his headlights cutting cones through the dark, and by the time he came through the door Marta was at four. He knelt in front of her chair and took her hands the way he must have taken a hundred dying hands, gentle, professional, and Dane watched his brother's face do something it almost never did: break open.

"Marta." Felix's voice was low and even, the hospice voice, the voice that had talked a dozen people across the threshold in Hollow Creek alone. "Look at me. You're not counting. You're listening to something count. There's a difference."

Her eyes found him. For one second—one terrible, human second—she was Marta again, Emilio's wife, a woman who grew tomatoes too big for her windowsill and laughed with her whole body. "It has my date," she whispered. "It's been reading my date all week. Like a man reading a menu."

"Three," said the other voice, from underneath hers.

"We believe you're going to live." Felix gripped her hands harder. "Do you hear me? Belief slows it. That's the rule. That's the only rule we've got. Say it. Say you believe."

"I believe," Marta said, in her own voice, and then, in the other one: "Two."

Ana was screaming now. Dane pulled the girl back against his chest and held her there, her heart hammering against his forearm like a bird in a box, and over her head he watched his brother fight the only war Felix Marchetti had ever trained for and lose anyway.

"One," said Marta Reyes.

And then she smiled. That was the worst part, the part Dane would carry into every sleepless night that followed. She smiled—warm, relieved, the smile of someone setting down a heavy bag after a long walk—and said, in a voice that was entirely her own and entirely finished, "Oh. There you are."

She stopped. Not died—stopped, the way a clock stops, mid-breath, mid-smile, her hands still warm in Felix's grip when he checked for a pulse that wasn't coming. Outside the window, far out in the field, something moved the wheat in a long slow wave, north to south, like a breath drawn across three acres.

Nobody spoke. Then, from upstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, they heard the girl.

Holly Brandt was seven years old and sleeping in the room the Voss cousins had given up for her, and her voice came down through the old house clear as a bell:

"Four."

* * *

By dawn there were twenty-three people in the Voss farmhouse, and by noon there were none of them speaking to each other.

The second zero had come at 4:17 in the morning. Old Tom Brandt—Holly's grandfather, who had farmed the east parcel for fifty years and had come to the house only because his son-in-law refused to leave Holly anywhere he couldn't see her—had gone out to the porch for air and simply hadn't come back. They found him sitting on the steps, upright, hat still on, facing the field. His mouth was open around the end of a number no one had heard him say. Felix closed the old man's eyes and wrote the time in his logbook with a hand that shook so badly the ink tore the paper.

Two names in one night. The ledger was accelerating, and everyone in that kitchen understood what acceleration meant: the harvest was being resisted, and resistance had a price, and the price was paid per soul.

The fracture started small, the way cracks in foundations start. Ruth Voss—Dane's aunt, seventy-one years old, a woman who had survived two husbands and one tornado—said quietly, over the untouched coffee, "I'm leaving."

"You can't leave," said Cal Mercer, whose brother was among the missing behind the sealed door. "The truck that ran didn't make the county line. You *saw* what came back in that truck. Or what didn't."

"The truck that stayed is still here," Ruth said. "I'd rather die running than die sitting in my sister-in-law's kitchen waiting for my turn on some dead man's list."

"It's not a list, it's a *count*—" Cal began, and then Deke Hollis, the youngest man on the crew, slammed his palm flat on the table hard enough to jump the cups.

"Then we burn it." His eyes were red-rimmed, wild; he hadn't slept since the boots. "The field. Tonight. Diesel, the hay barn's full of it, we burn three acres of wheat down to the clay and we find out if whatever's under there likes smoke. It's a *plant*. Plants burn. I don't care what else it is—it's growing in dirt, and dirt burns everything eventually."

"And if the fire doesn't reach it?" Felix asked. He was standing by the sink, gray-faced, having not slept either. "If it's forty feet down behind an oak door? You'll have burned the only cover between us and it, and shown it exactly how scared we are. Fear feeds the count. The farmer told me that. Belief slows it. Fear *feeds* it."

"Then what's your plan, nurse?" Deke spat the word. "Pray at it? Hold its hand? Tell it about its dignity?"

"I think," said a new voice, "we should open the door."

Everyone turned. It was Sarah Voss—Dane's cousin, quiet Sarah, who had barely spoken in six days except to ask whether anyone wanted more coffee. She was holding her own hands on the table like a woman in church.

"They took Emilio and Ray and Curtis," she said. "They're not dead. We don't know they're dead. The door opened once for them. Maybe it opens for us. Maybe it wants to *talk*. Everything else in this town has rules—the counts, the ledger, the tally marks. Things with rules can be bargained with. My grandmother used to say the Marchettis fed it so the rest of the town could live. Maybe we just need to feed it *something*. Something smaller than a person. Something it'll take instead."

"Feed it," Ana Reyes repeated. Her mother had been dead eleven hours. "You want to *feed* it."

"I want my neighbors back," Sarah said, and her voice cracked, and Dane saw that half the room had leaned toward her without meaning to. Bargaining. Of course bargaining. People would rather kneel than run; kneeling felt like doing something.

"That's enough." Dane hadn't planned to speak. He heard his own voice come out flat and hard, a stranger's voice, his father's voice, God help him. "Everybody sit down."

They sat. That surprised him most—not the argument, not the fear, but that they sat. Twenty-three frightened people in a kitchen that smelled of cold coffee and the copper tang of the day's first grief, and they sat because a thirty-four-year-old excavation worker told them to.

"Here's what I know," Dane said. "I know the county line didn't save the second truck. I know fire is a maybe and a bargain is a prayer and running is a coin flip, and I know that while we stand in this kitchen deciding which maybe to bet on, that thing downstairs—" he pointed at the floor, toward the girl's room, toward the small voice counting them all toward morning— "is at four. Four, folks. That's not a someday problem. That's a tonight problem. So here's what happens today. Nobody leaves this property alone. Nobody goes near the field. Deke, you fill every vehicle we've got and park them nose-out at the barn—you want to run, we run *together*, all of us, at once, in daylight, and we accept what happened to the last truck. Sarah—" her face fell and he gentled, because gentling was Felix's gift and not his but he could borrow it— "nobody's saying we won't go to that door. But we go when we choose, with a plan, not because it snapped its fingers. And Felix—"

His brother looked up. In the gray kitchen light Dane could see how much the last weeks had carved out of him. Felix looked like a man who had been read his own obituary and was still deciding whether to contest it.

"Felix works on the girl. That's the job. That's everybody's job, protecting him doing it. Because if belief slows the count, then she's not just a kid in a bedroom. She's the front line. And we hold it."

Silence. Then Ruth Voss, arms crossed, chin up, said, "And when she hits zero? What's the plan then, boy?"

Dane looked at his aunt, and at the window beyond her, where the wheat stood in the morning light like ten thousand soldiers at attention, and he told the truth because it was the only thing he had left in quantity.

"Then I'll figure it out," he said. "Between now and then, I'll figure it out. That's the plan."

* * *

He found Felix in the girl's room at dusk.

Holly Brandt lay small and pale under a quilt two sizes too big for her, and Felix sat in the desk chair beside her, holding her hand, and Dane stopped in the doorway because the scene was so quiet it felt like trespassing.

"Three," the girl murmured, asleep, in that layered voice. Then, softer, in her own: "Tell him the story again. The one about the dog."

"There's no dog in it," Felix said gently. "But I'll tell it anyway." And he did—some rambling thing about a golden retriever and a stolen Thanksgiving turkey, invented on the spot, his thumb moving in slow circles on the back of the child's hand—and Dane watched the cadence of it work on her breathing, slowing it, steadying it, and understood that his brother had been doing this all day. Not fighting the count. Drowning it out. Filling the girl's ears with the sound of a living voice telling a story about a living world, so the other voice had less room.

"How long can you keep that up?" Dane asked from the doorway.

Felix didn't turn around. "Until my voice gives out. Then I'll whisper." A pause. "Dane. When it gets low—when she gets to one—I need you to take everyone into the cellar. All of them. No arguments, no heroics. If the count finishes and it comes for her, I don't want an audience. I want witnesses *gone*."

"Why?"

"Because I've been reading the confession again. The founders' pages." Felix finally turned, and in the failing light his face was unreadable, which from Felix, who wore everything, was itself a kind of alarm. "There's a line in it I haven't told anyone. About what the count does when it reaches the last name on a page. About what the ledger requires to turn the page." He looked back down at the girl, at his thumb circling her small hand. "Go eat something, Dane. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow we're going to need you to be the one who holds them together."

"I'm not good at that," Dane said. "That's your job. You're the one people listen to."

Felix smiled then, tired and crooked, the old smile from a childhood that seemed to belong to two different boys in two different towns. "People listened to me because I told them what they wanted to hear—that death was natural, that it was okay, that the numbers meant nothing. I'm done lying. From here on out, they're going to need somebody who tells them the truth and stands there while they hate it." He squeezed the girl's hand. "That's you, little brother. Always was."

Downstairs, the kitchen radio—batteries only, nothing broadcast anymore but static—hissed softly to itself. And beneath the static, so faint that Dane would spend the rest of his life unsure whether he'd actually heard it, something in the walls, or under the floors, or out in the wheat, was keeping time.

The girl stirred in her sleep.

"Two," she said.
