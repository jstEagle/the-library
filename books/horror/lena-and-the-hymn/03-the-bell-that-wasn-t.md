## 3. The Bell That Wasn't

Edith Marsh had been vicar of St. Aldhelm's for eleven years, and in that time she had learned three things about the job that no seminary had thought to mention.

First: the tea urn in the vestry would only work if you spoke to it. Not prayers — it ignored those — but a firm, conversational "come on, old girl" did the trick every time.

Second: the flower-arranging rota was a more volatile document than the parish accounts, and twice as likely to cause a schism.

Third: at dusk, on certain evenings, the bell rang.

There was no bell to ring. This was the difficulty. The tower of St. Aldhelm's had stood empty since 1911, when the old bell — Old Margaret, as the village had called her, though Edith suspected the name predated any actual Margaret by a century or two — had been taken down, melted, and poured into the foundation stones of the chapel itself. There were photographs in the county archive of the day they lowered her: men with their hats off, standing very still, as though at a funeral. Which, Edith supposed, it had been.

And yet.

She heard it now, as she heard it perhaps eight or ten times a year: a single tone rolling down from the empty tower, round and bronze and patient, the way a large animal is patient. It came at dusk, always at dusk, never twice on consecutive evenings, and it did not echo the way a real bell echoes. A real bell's sound bounces off roofs and hillsides and comes home to you. This sound went out and did not come back. It simply stopped, as if the air had decided to keep it.

Edith set down her pen over the parish log — the ordinary one, births and deaths and the scandalous business of the Hendersons' christening being booked for the same Saturday as the fete — and looked out the study window at the tower against the bruised evening sky.

The bell rope hung in the ringing chamber, frayed and grey with dust, and had not moved since 1911. She had checked. Early in her tenure, in her first flush of rational competence, she had climbed the ninety-three steps on four separate evenings when the ringing was due, and each time she had arrived to find the rope hanging dead still, the chamber cold, and the sound already fading below her — which was impossible, because the sound came from above, except that there was nothing above, nothing but the empty frame where the headstock used to sit and, on one memorable occasion, a pigeon who regarded her with what she could only describe as pity.

The pigeon, at least, had been explicable.

* * *

"You'll want to write it down," said Mrs. Tilly Sowerby, appearing in the vestry doorway with the particular briskness of a woman who has been delivering bad news for so long that it has become a kind of hospitality. She held a covered dish. "I've brought you a cottage pie. You get thin when it rings."

"I do not get thin."

"You get thin in the face. It's the listening." Tilly set the dish on the vestry table with the confidence of someone who knew exactly where things belonged in this church, having dusted them for forty years. "Have you written it?"

"Not yet."

"Well, don't leave it till morning. Your Mr. Prowse — the vicar before your predecessor, before my time — he left it overnight once, 1953 it would have been, and come morning he couldn't recall whether it had rung once or twice, and he wrote *once*, and his hand shook doing it, Mrs. Marsh told me, and she'd have known, she cleaned for him." Tilly folded her arms. "Once or twice matters."

"Why does once or twice matter, Tilly?"

Tilly considered her for a moment with the weary patience of the long-serving. Edith liked Tilly enormously; she was the closest thing Hallowmere had to a second opinion.

"It matters," Tilly said, "because if it rings twice, somebody's been asked twice. And nobody gets asked twice unless they didn't answer the first time." She untied her scarf with a snap. "Cottage pie's still warm. Eat it while it's hot, and write it down after."

"Tilly—"

"And don't go asking me what it means, because I've lived here seventy-one years and I know precisely what it means, which is that I don't ask, and neither should you, and we both sleep better for it." She paused at the door. "Onions are underneath the potato. Some people like them hidden."

And she was gone, into the blue evening, walking quickly, the way everyone in Hallowmere walked at dusk — not running, never running, but with the unhurried urgency of people who had somewhere indoors to be.

Edith ate the cottage pie. The onions were indeed hidden. Then she sat down at the desk in the vestry, where the light was worst and the draught was best, and opened the lower drawer.

* * *

The ledger did not have an official name. In the parish inventory it was listed, in her predecessor's handwriting, as *Old Register — misc. ecclesiastical records, do not discard*, which was the sort of catalogue entry that made archivists weep. It was a fat book bound in green leather gone black at the corners, and its first dated entry was 1832, though the paper stock suggested the habit was older than the dating.

Every vicar of St. Aldhelm's had kept it. That was the thing Edith had understood slowly, then all at once, in her third year: this was not a quirk of one nervous man or one superstitious woman. It was a discipline, handed over like the keys. When Reverend Anselm Prowse had retired in 1988 — he of the shaking hand, though that story had reached her garbled through two generations of cleaning ladies — he had put the ledger in her hands along with the keys to the vestry safe, and he had said, "You'll want to keep the record current," in exactly the tone he used for the boiler maintenance schedule, and she had thought nothing of it until the first time she heard the bell, six weeks into her incumbency, and had gone looking through the old registers for some precedent and found instead this book, waiting, with blank ruled pages and a pen laid across them as if the previous hand had only just lifted it.

The entries were simple. A date. A time — dusk, variously rendered as *at evensong*, *as the light went*, *6:47 p.m.* And a single word, entered with terrible consistency:

*Rang.*

Sometimes a little more. *Rang. Long.* Or, in 1940, in a hand pressed hard enough to tear the page: *Rang during the raid warning. God forgive us, it rang anyway.* Or, in 1974, an entry Edith had read so many times she could recite it: *Rang thrice. Unusual. Note appended re: Crane.*

Note appended re: Crane. She had never found the note. There was a loose leaf missing from that year, torn close to the spine, and every vicar's instinct in her body said *find it*, and every other instinct she possessed said *leave it be, Edith, you've seen how the dog reacts to that corner of the crypt.*

But tonight there was no note to find, no mystery beyond the standing one. Tonight the entry was the plain kind.

She dipped the pen. Outside, the last light was going copper behind the yew trees, and the swifts were doing their evening screaming over the churchyard, and everything was so ordinary that it took an act of will to write what she was about to write, because writing it made it part of the record, and the record was two hundred years long, and two hundred years is long enough for a lie to become a liturgy.

*14th September,* she wrote. *Rang. Once.*

Her hand was steady. Eleven years of practice. She blotted it, closed the ledger, and sat for a moment in the cooling vestry with her hands flat on the green leather, feeling — as she felt every time — like a woman keeping books for something she had never signed a contract with.

Because that was the truth of it, wasn't it. She was complicit. Not in anything she could name, not in any act she could confess at a synod or explain to a bishop without being quietly retired on grounds of health. Her complicity was smaller and stranger: she kept the record. She watched. She wrote *rang* in a book that no one had ordered her to write in, in a parish where no one else admitted hearing anything at all.

That was the other thing about Hallowmere. She was the only one who logged it, but she was not the only one who heard it. She knew this the way you know the shape of a thing from the way people move around it. Tilly knew. Silas Fen knew, though Silas's knowledge came out sideways, pickled, usually at closing time, in fragments that the publican swept up like broken glass. The Crane family had known — old Mr. Crane, Lena and Oscar's grandfather, had once stopped her in the lane, years ago, gripped her wrist with shocking strength, and said, "When it asks, Vicar, you make sure the book says what happened. Whatever they tell you. You write what happened." And then he had let go, touched his hat, and talked about the weather for a full four minutes until she wanted to scream.

He was dead now. His granddaughter was being buried Saturday, God rest her, drowned in the mere in June, a terrible accident, everyone agreed, everyone saying it a half-beat too quickly, the way people say things they've agreed to say.

Edith had taken the funeral herself, of course. Well — she would, on Saturday. The banns of grief, as she privately called them, were all in order. And tomorrow the coffin would arrive at the church, and she would stand in the porch and receive it, and she would not think about the fact that the bell had rung last Tuesday, and again this evening, and that in eleven years it had never rung twice in one week.

Never, in fact, so close together. Never, she realized, sitting up straight in the draughty vestry with the ledger under her palms, in living memory — and she had two hundred years of living memory right there in the drawer — so soon after a death.

She pulled the ledger back open and turned the pages backward, fast, her finger running down the column of *rang*s like a dowser's rod. Deaths in the parish register, deaths in the burial book, dates matched against dates. Most years: nothing, or a ring months removed from any funeral, unmoored, gratuitous. But sometimes — 1931, 1962, 1974 — the ring fell within days of a burial. Within hours, in '74. She found the entry for the week of the Crane funeral in '74 — the grandmother's, the one Oscar and Lena had been too young to remember — and there it was: *Rang. Once. Day of interment.* And beneath it, in different ink, added later, cramped into the margin as if the writer had been afraid to give it a line of its own:

*It does not attend funerals. It takes attendance.*

Edith sat back. The swifts had gone quiet. Somewhere out in the churchyard a rook shifted on a branch with a sound like a door deciding.

"That's not comforting," she informed the empty vestry. "In case you were aiming for comforting."

The vestry, as usual, declined to answer. But the tea urn, unplugged, ticked once in the silence, and Edith chose — deliberately, professionally, the way she chose to believe in a good God in a world with plenty of evidence otherwise — to interpret it as agreement rather than appetite.

* * *

She locked the church at nine, which was late for her, and stood a moment in the porch with the key cold in her fist. The village lay below in its shallow bowl of hills, a scatter of amber windows, chimney smoke going up straight in the windless dark. From here she could see the Cranes' house, dark. The boy — the young man, Oscar, he must be thirty now — had arrived today; Tilly had reported it within the hour, in the tone of a war correspondent filing dispatch. Staying at the family house. Refusing the hotel. Wanting to see his sister before the funeral, alone, which was natural, which was right, which was the sort of request that in any other parish would mean nothing at all.

Edith looked at the tower. It stood against the stars, square and blind, its louvred openings black as held breath.

"One week," she said to it. "Whatever you think you're owed, we buried her on Saturday, and that's an end of it, and I will not have you making a spectacle at a funeral. I have enough trouble with the flowers."

The tower said nothing, in the way that it said nothing, which was somehow louder than the rooks.

She locked the porch, pocketed the key, and walked down through the churchyard, past the yews, past the fresh-dug grave waiting under its board, and she did not hurry, because hurrying was a language this place understood, and she refused to speak it. At the lychgate she stopped, once, and listened. Nothing. Only the mere, away to the east, holding its water flat and black under the rising moon, and — she would tell herself later she imagined it, and mostly succeed — only, from far off, from underneath everything, a hum. Not a ring. A hum. The sound a bell makes in the moment before it is struck, when the ringer's hand is already on the wheel.

Edith Marsh went home, put the kettle on, and did not speak to it.

Then she got the ledger out of her bag — she had begun carrying it home, these last two years, which she told herself was about damp in the vestry — and turned to the page for 1974, and looked for a long time at the torn stub where the note about the Cranes had been ripped away, and at the words in the margin, *it takes attendance*, and at the date she had written that evening, still faintly glistening.

Saturday, she thought. A funeral. A mourning brother, alone in a house full of doors.

She reached for the pen, hesitated, and in the space beside her own neat entry added five words in small, defiant handwriting — the first editorial remark any vicar of St. Aldhelm's had risked in two hundred years:

*If it comes to the funeral, I want it written down that I objected.*

She blotted it. Outside, far off, thin as wire, something began — very softly, very patiently — to count.
