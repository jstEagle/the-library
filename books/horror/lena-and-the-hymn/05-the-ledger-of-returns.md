## 5. The Ledger of Returns

Edith Marsh had been vicar of St. Brannoc's for eleven years, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things about the parish of Hallowmere: that the tea always went cold before anyone finished saying anything important, that the damp got into everything eventually, including — she was fairly certain — her knees, and that there were certain questions a vicar did not answer until she was quite sure she wanted to.

Oscar Crane had been asking questions since the funeral. He asked them politely, which was worse. He asked them with the particular steadiness of a man who had decided that if he stopped being steady, even for a moment, something in him would come apart like wet paper. She recognized the look. Her predecessor, old Canon Weller, had worn it for the last twenty years of his life, right up until the morning they found him in the vestry with his prayer book open to a page he had underlined so many times the paper had given out entirely.

So when Oscar appeared at the vicarage door on Thursday evening — the fourth evening of the bell, though he didn't know yet that she counted — she had already made her decision. She'd made it, truthfully, the moment she'd seen him lift the coffin lid. She'd been standing by the lychgate pretending to fuss with a wreath, and she had watched his face do the thing faces were not supposed to do at funerals: go *still*. Not grieving. Still. The stillness of a man doing arithmetic he did not want to do.

"Miss Marsh," he said now. "Vicar. Sorry — I'm not sure what to call you."

"Edith is fine. Everyone calls me Edith. The last person who called me 'Vicar' with that tone was a man from the diocese asking why our maintenance budget had a line item for 'structural listening,' and I'd rather not relive it."

That earned her the ghost of a smile. Good. She ushered him into the vicarage parlour, which was warm in the way old rooms are warm — reluctantly, and only in a small circle around the fire — and poured him tea she fully expected him not to drink. There was a plate of biscuits too, because her mother had raised her to believe that no terrible conversation should be conducted without at least the *offer* of a custard cream, and because she had a feeling this was going to be a very terrible conversation indeed.

"You heard it," he said, before he'd even sat down properly. "Last night. The bell."

Edith sat across from him and folded her hands. "I hear it every year. Usually just the once, at dusk, in the last week of October. This year it's been — talkative."

"Talkative." He said the word as though testing whether it would hold his weight. "The church hasn't had a bell since 1911. The tower's empty. I looked."

"I know you looked. Mrs. Petchy told me you climbed halfway up the tower steps on Tuesday and came back down looking like a man who'd seen his own funeral. Which, given the circumstances, I imagine was an uncomfortable sensation."

"Vicar—"

"Edith."

"*Edith.* My sister is missing. The body in that coffin was not my sister. Nobody in this village will say a word about it that isn't warm and vague and wrong, and last night a bell that was melted down sixty-three years ago rang in an empty tower, and you're offering me biscuits."

"They're custard creams," Edith said. "The good ones. I want that on the record."

He stared at her. And Edith, who had spent eleven years perfecting the art of the gentle deflection — the vicar's whole trade, really, was the gentle deflection — felt the deflection die in her throat. Because here was the thing about Oscar Crane that she had not expected: he wasn't going to go away. The others always, eventually, went away. Grief wore them down, or the village's warm vagueness wore them down, or the years wore them down, and they left, and she logged the date in a ledger she kept in a locked drawer, and she lit a candle, and she hated herself a little more efficiently each time.

This one had his sister's chin and his grandmother's stubbornness, and he was not going away.

"Wait here," she said.

* * *

The ledger lived in the vestry, in the bottom of the old iron safe that had been installed in 1890 and had defeated every locksmith since, on the grounds that it was easier to simply keep the key in the parish than to fight the thing. Edith carried it back to the parlour wrapped in a linen cloth, the way you'd carry something that might be sleeping.

It was not a beautiful book. People expected ledgers of this sort to be beautiful — leather, gilt, a clasp — but the truth was that whoever had first made it had wanted it to look as boring as possible, and had succeeded magnificently. It was a plain parish account book, the kind sold by the dozen to churches up and down the country, with a spine of cracked brown cloth and pages the colour of weak tea. The only remarkable thing about it was its age, and the fact that the pages had never once, in a hundred and forty years, been allowed to run out.

"Every parish keeps registers," Edith said, setting it on the table between them. "Baptisms, marriages, burials. This one keeps a different kind of record. We call it the Ledger of Returns."

She opened it to the first page and turned it toward him.

The heading, in a copperplate hand gone brown with age, read: *An Account of Those Returned, Being the True and Faithful Record of St. Brannoc's, Hallowmere.* Below it, the entries began.

1832. *M. Ashcombe.* Struck through with a single clean line.

1833. *T. Hedger.* Struck through.

1834. *R. Ashcombe.* Struck through.

Page after page, year after year. One name, written in ink that always looked freshly dried no matter how old the entry, and struck through with a single line — and beside each struck name, in the same copperplate, the same small notation: *returned.*

"What does 'returned' mean?" Oscar asked. His voice had gone very quiet, the way voices go in hospitals.

"It means what it says. Each year, one name is written in this book. And each year, that name is — given back. To whatever it is that asks for it." She turned the pages slowly, letting him see the unbroken rhythm of it. Wars came and went in these pages; she could show him the year of the influenza, when two names were written and the book had — she chose her words with care — *permitted* it. Plague, famine, the long grey decades of the twentieth century marching past, and every October, one name, one line, one small word: *returned.*

"Who writes them?" Oscar asked. "The names."

"The vicar writes them. It's always been the vicar." She heard how that sounded and didn't flinch from it. "Yes, Oscar. Me too. Last week. I wrote a name, and then I sat in this room and drank two glasses of sherry and hated myself in a historically thorough manner, and then I wrote to the diocese requesting a transfer, and then I tore up the letter, because I already know what the reply would be, because Canon Weller wrote the same letter in 1958 and the reply is filed in that safe, and it says, in essence: *the parish of Hallowmere is a settled matter, do not disturb it.*"

"Whose name did you write?"

She looked at him for a long moment. "You're not ready for that answer, and I'm not ready to give it, and I've learned to respect that kind of mutual unreadiness. Turn to 1974. It's the page you came for, even if you don't know it yet."

He turned the pages faster now, his finger running down the margin of years, and Edith watched his face and thought, not for the first time, that the worst moments in a life rarely announce themselves. They arrive quietly, in a warm room, with tea going cold.

He found the page.

The entry for 1974 read, simply: *Crane.*

And it had been struck through. Twice.

Two lines, crossing and recrossing, the second one dug so deep into the paper that it had nearly torn — the handwriting above it pressed hard, furious or frightened or both — and then, beside it, in a different hand, a smaller, meaner, more cramped script, a name Edith had read so many times she could see it with her eyes shut:

*E. Merrick. Returned.*

"Crane," Oscar said. "That's — that's my family. My grandfather's name was Crane. This is 1974. That's the year my grandparents were married, the year before my mother was born." His finger hovered over the double strike-through without touching it, the way you'd hover a hand over a stove you'd already been burned by. "It was struck through twice. Why twice? The others are once."

"Because the first line didn't take," Edith said.

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled with a sound like a small, tired sigh.

"The book writes a name," she said. "The name is returned. That is the order of things; it has been the order of things since 1832, and I promise you, Oscar, that everyone in this village who is old enough to have an opinion about it has an opinion, and every one of those opinions is *keep your voice down.* But in 1974, your grandfather — Thomas Crane, a good man by every account, and I say that with the bitter respect of someone who has had to clean up after good men her whole career — your grandfather decided that the order of things had gone far enough. The book wrote *Crane.* And Thomas Crane took his pen — or took something, we've never known exactly what he did, and the one person who might tell us drinks himself insensible every night rather than say — and he crossed it out."

"He refused."

"He refused. And the book wrote it again — or he wrote it again, the accounts differ, and the accounts differ because the accounts were kept by frightened people — and he refused again. Twice. No one in the history of this ledger has refused twice. And then the name was changed. Not by the book, as far as we can tell. By a human hand. The hand that wrote *E. Merrick* is not the hand that wrote *Crane.*"

"E. Merrick." Oscar said the name slowly, tasting it. "I don't — I've never heard that name. Not in my family, not in the village—"

"No," Edith said gently. "You wouldn't have."

She turned the ledger around to face herself and ran her thumb down the column of years, all those struck names, all that tidy, terrible bookkeeping. "Here's the thing I've come to understand about this book, in eleven years of keeping it and a lifetime of dreading it. Everyone assumes it's a record. That it writes down what has happened, or what will happen, the way a register records a burial. But registers don't *choose*, Oscar. Registers don't have a hand in it. And I have watched this book — I have sat with it at two in the morning with the fire down to embers, and I have watched the pen I left resting on the page — and I have come to a conclusion I would give a great deal never to have reached."

"Which is?"

"This book is not a record of the debt." She closed it, softly, the way you close a door on a sleeping thing. "This book is the *creditor.*"

Oscar had gone the colour of the tea. "And my grandfather crossed out its entry. Twice. And then — what, paid it with someone else? With this Merrick woman? And it *accepted?*"

"It accepted," Edith said. "For fifty years it accepted. And I will tell you what I think, though thinking it has kept me up more nights than the damp has. I think a debt deferred is not a debt forgiven. I think whatever waits under St. Brannoc's has been counting — patiently, the way old things count — and it has been waiting for a Crane to walk back through the lychgate. And this year, when you and your sister came home, the book got *hungry.*"

She hadn't meant to say the sister's name. She watched him flinch anyway, at the shape of the thing even without the word.

"The bell's been ringing four nights," she said. "It's never rung four nights. And today I opened this ledger to make the year's entry, and the ink on the 1974 page — the *Crane* —" She stopped. Started again, more quietly. "The old strike-through is fading, Oscar. The two lines your grandfather dug into the paper. Fifty years they held, and this week they've begun to lift, like a scab coming away. And underneath them, the name is still legible. It's as though the book has decided the entry was never properly closed."

Oscar stood up. He stood the way people stand in films about men about to do stupid, necessary things, and Edith felt a pang of something between pity and respect, because she had buried — in the ordinary, merciful way — enough of these stubborn Cranes to know the family face when it was deciding to be brave.

"Where is my sister?" he asked.

"That," said Edith, "is the question this village has been not-answering since 1832. And I'll tell you the only answer I have for free: whatever was given back in 1974 — whatever *E. Merrick* was, and was not — it was not enough. It was never enough. It was a substitute, a stopgap, a coin clipped at the edges. And whatever is under the chapel has spent fifty years learning the difference." She wrapped the ledger back in its linen cloth, and her hands were steady, which she counted as the day's one small victory. "Your grandfather stole something, Oscar. From something that does not forget. And the interest has come due, and the bell is ringing it out, one night at a time, and I do not think it intends to stop at four."

He was already at the door.

"Crane," she called after him — his name, the family name, the name in the book, and he stopped with his hand on the latch. "The house you're staying in. Your grandparents' house. Go up to the attic. Whatever your grandfather did in 1974, he was the sort of man who would have needed to *explain himself* to someone, even if only to paper. Men like that always leave letters."

"How do you know?"

"Because," Edith said, "Canon Weller left me one. And the first line of it reads: *If you are reading this, the Crane debt has come back round, and I am sorry, and whatever you do, do not let them read the ledger before they've read the letters — the book lies, and the truth is heavier.*"

She let that sit.

"And I gave you the ledger first," she said, "because I have decided I don't much care what Canon Weller would have wanted."

The door closed behind Oscar Crane. Edith Marsh sat alone in the warm parlour with the wrapped ledger on the table, and after a while she poured herself the two fingers of sherry she'd been saving, and drank them looking at the fire, and did not hate herself at all, which frightened her more than anything else that had happened that week.
