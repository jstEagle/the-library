## 9. The Erasure Begins

Edith Marsh had been vicar of Hallowmere for thirty-one years, and in that time she had learned three things about the parish: that the flower rota would always be a battlefield, that Mr. Tulliver would fall asleep during every sermon but wake precisely at the blessing, and that the ledger in the vestry safe was never, under any circumstances, to be left open overnight.

She had assumed the third rule was superstition. Vicars, she had come to understand, were not immune to superstition — they simply dressed theirs in cassocks and called it tradition.

On the morning after Oscar Crane's visit, she opened the funeral register and found the ink lifting off the page like steam.

* * *

It began subtly, which was how these things always began. The entry for Lena Crane — dated, signed, witnessed by two wardens — had gone grey at the edges, as though the page itself were exhaling. Edith ran her thumb along the line of text and watched the letters blur, then fade, then vanish entirely, leaving behind a pale groove where a name had been pressed into paper and then unpressed, like a footprint retreating from sand.

"Well," she said, to the empty vestry, because there was no one else to say it to. "That's not ideal."

She was a woman who coped with catastrophe through mild understatement. It had served her well at her mother's funeral, at the flood of '68, and during the unfortunate incident with the travelling puppet ministry in 1981. It served her now. She did not panic. She fetched her reading glasses, put them on, took them off again, cleaned them, and put them back on, and by that time the entire entry was gone — not smudged, not water-damaged, but *unwritten*, the way a dream is unwritten by nine o'clock in the morning.

The register did not look tampered with. That was the worst part. There was no blank space, no gap in the sequence. The entries before Lena Crane's and after it sat flush against one another, comfortable, as though they had always been neighbours. The book had healed over the wound.

Edith poured herself a small sherry from the bottle she kept for "communion emergencies" — a phrase that had come to cover an increasingly generous range of situations — and sat down to think.

The bell had rung last night. She had recorded it, as she always did, in the ledger: date, time, duration, and, in the column her predecessors had labelled simply *Conditions*, the word *clear*. Two hundred years of entries. Two hundred years of vicars sitting in this same chair, hearing the same impossible sound, writing it down with the same tired hand. She had once believed they kept the record out of duty. Now, watching a dead woman's name dissolve off a page in front of her, she understood they had kept it for the same reason you keep a fire lit in a house surrounded by wolves.

Not because it helps. Because stopping is worse.

* * *

By noon, the erasure had spread.

Edith went to check on Mrs. Pargiter, who had arranged the flowers for the funeral and had wept so movingly at the graveside that Edith had given her an extra hymn to recover. She found the old woman in her garden, deadheading roses with the contented violence of the truly retired.

"Mrs. Pargiter. A sad question, I'm afraid. Do you remember the funeral? Tuesday last?"

The secateurs paused. Mrs. Pargiter frowned — not with grief, but with the particular irritation of a woman being asked to recall something that had never happened. "Whose funeral?"

"Lena Crane's."

"Crane." The frown deepened into genuine effort. "There was a Crane family. Up at the old house. Years ago. Twins, weren't there?" Her eyes went vague, reaching. "I don't recall a funeral, Vicar. Did I miss one?"

"You were there. You did the lilies. You cried at the second verse of the hymn and told me afterward it was the cold wind."

"I said what?" Mrs. Pargiter laughed, a bright, untroubled sound, and Edith felt something in her chest go cold and quiet. There was no grief under the laughter. No confusion. Only a smooth, seamless absence, like a tooth socket long since healed. "You do tell stories, Vicar. Was there ever a Crane girl at all? I'd have remembered."

*You wouldn't,* Edith thought. *That is rather the point.*

She made her excuses, walked back down the lane at a pace she told herself was dignified, and let herself into the vestry. The parish register of burials — the legal one, the one that went to the county — lay on its shelf. She opened it with hands that were almost steady.

Lena Crane's entry was gone there too. And beneath the absence, faintly, in pencil, someone had written — decades ago, judging by the faded hand — *count it, copy it, keep it*.

Her predecessors had known. Of course they had. All those careful ledgers, all those columns in fading iron-gall ink: not bureaucracy. Ballast. They had been copying things down before the thing below could take them, racing an appetite that ate names, and they had passed the habit down like a family recipe for survival.

Edith took out fresh paper, her good pen, and the last sherry, and got to work.

* * *

Copying by hand is slower than forgetting. That is the whole secret, and it is a terrible one.

She worked from memory first, because memory was all she had left of some details. *Lena Crane*, she wrote, *daughter of Margaret Crane, sister of Oscar Crane, born—* and here she stopped, pen hovering, and felt the horror of it properly for the first time. She could not recall the birthdate. She had buried people in this parish for thirty-one years; she could recite half their birthdays if pressed, along with their preferred hymns and their grudges against the flower rota. But Lena's dates slid away from her like fish in dark water.

She tried the civil registry instead. The clerk in Ashford was a pleasant young man named Dominic who thought Edith was calling about a christening, and when she gave him the name he was silent for a moment, then apologetic. "Nothing coming up, I'm afraid. Are you sure of the spelling? Sometimes they're filed under—"

"C-R-A-N-E," Edith said. "Like the bird. Like the river."

"Nothing under either, I'm sorry to say. Could it be under a married name?"

"She wasn't married."

"Adopted, perhaps? Records before 1970 can be patchy—"

"She was born in 1951 in this parish," Edith said, more sharply than she intended, "and I have held her baptismal certificate in my own hands."

A pause on the line. Then, gently, the way one speaks to the confused elderly: "Vicar, is everything quite all right?"

She thanked him, hung up, and sat for a long moment listening to the tick of the vestry clock. Then she wrote, at the top of her fresh page, in letters large enough to be read by candlelight or by desperation:

*REMEMBER THIS OR IT IS LOST.*

And beneath it, everything she could hold onto. Not just facts — facts were going first, she could feel them going, like ice melting from the edges inward — but textures. The way Lena Crane had hummed while choosing hymns, absent-minded, a tune Edith had never placed. The exact grey of her coat at the graveside. The way her brother had stood apart from the mourners, too still, like a man listening for something under the ground. The smell of the lilies Mrs. Pargiter no longer remembered arranging.

Names are hooks, Edith thought, laying down line after line of cramped, stubborn handwriting. Facts are hooks. But memory keeps best when you salt it with particulars. Her predecessors had understood this. That was why the ledger's *Conditions* column didn't just note weather. Old Reverend Cobbett, 1849: *bell rang eleven minutes; my dog would not enter the churchyard; the smell of the river came up through the vestry floor.* You couldn't erase a detail like that without erasing the man who noticed it, and apparently even appetites have limits of patience.

Or perhaps they don't, she thought, and write faster.

* * *

By evening, the strangeness had grown teeth.

Oscar Crane arrived at the vicarage door at dusk — dusk, Edith noted, glancing at the empty tower with an expression she hoped conveyed professional calm rather than dread — looking as though he hadn't slept, which she suspected was true, and carrying a photograph, which she suspected was worse.

"They've taken her from this," he said, without preamble, holding it out. "Look at the corner. My mother wrote *Lena, Whit Monday 1963* on the back. Look at it now."

Edith looked. The back of the photograph was blank. Worse than blank — the paper there was smooth and slightly younger-looking than the rest, as though the inscription had been absorbed rather than removed. And the girl in the picture — Edith squinted — the girl's face had gone soft at the edges, blurred, like a thumbprint on wet paint. Not erased. *Smearing.* As though whatever was taking Lena Crane had to work gradually, loosening each memory before pulling it free.

"I went to the pub," Oscar said. His voice was flat, controlled, the control costing him visibly. "Six people in that pub knew my sister. Six. This morning. By evening, four of them had never heard of her, and the other two remembered a Crane family but couldn't say why. One of them — Mrs. Aldous — she taught Lena piano for six years. She looked straight at me and asked if I was the postman's boy."

"The drunk one," Edith said. "Fen. What about him?"

Oscar blinked. "He remembers. Perfectly. He remembers too much, if anything. He said—" Oscar stopped, jaw working. "He said she's not dead yet. That's the problem."

Edith closed her eyes briefly. *Silas Fen,* she thought, *you absolute fool, and thank God for you.* "Come inside," she said. "I'll make tea. And then I'm going to show you something, and afterwards neither of us is going to sleep well, and I want it noted that I offered the tea first."

* * *

Over tea — strong, over-brewed, the vicarage way — she showed him her copy page. He read it twice, his lips moving slightly, and when he looked up his eyes were wet but level.

"You're transcribing her. By hand."

"I'm salting her," Edith said. "Into paper. It slows the process. I don't know why — ink and attention, perhaps. Perhaps the thing below can take what's stored in heads and hearts and registers, but a page written in defiance, witnessed, argued over — that costs it something. My predecessors seem to have believed so. They built an entire library of defiance in that safe." She nodded toward the vestry. "Two hundred years of it. Dates, conditions, complaints about dogs. I used to think it was morbid bookkeeping. Now I suspect it's the only reason Hallowmere remembers anything at all."

"And it's working? The copying?"

"It's slowing it." She hesitated, then decided he deserved the truth; he was paying for it, after all, in the hardest currency. "Mr. Crane — Oscar. This morning I could not recall your sister's birthday. An hour ago I could not recall her face without the photograph. Tonight—" she touched the page, gently, the way one touches a sleeping child, "—tonight I can still see the grey of her coat. But I'll be honest with you, because you strike me as a man who has had enough kindness this week and might prefer the alternative. Whatever is doing this is patient, and thorough, and it has been fed on this parish for a hundred and forty years. Copying buys days. Perhaps weeks. It does not buy her back."

"Then what does?"

"That," said Edith, "is the question I have been asking for thirty-one years, and the answer, as far as I can determine, is in that safe, in a ledger I am going to show you whether or not it's wise, because wise stopped being an option for either of us the moment that bell started ringing early."

Oscar set down his cup. "Early?"

"Last night it rang at dusk. For a century and more it has rung on the eve of the return — one night, one hour, one tolling. This year it began eight days early." She met his eyes. "Eight days, Mr. Crane. As though it's hungry. Or impatient. Or—" she chose the word carefully, having spent the afternoon learning exactly how much weight words could carry, "—as though it knows someone has come asking questions, and it wants to finish the meal before he finds the recipe."

The colour left Oscar's face, but he did not flinch, and Edith found herself revising her opinion of him upward. Grief made most men brittle. This one was becoming load-bearing.

"The ledger," he said. "Show me."

"One condition." She stood, took the lamp from the sideboard, and held the door. "When I show you the entry for 1974 — your family's entry — you will not touch the book. Whatever you feel. Whatever you see. Ink in that volume has been known to move, according to Cobbett, and Cobbett was a man who recorded the smell of rivers through floors, so I believe him implicitly."

They crossed the churchyard together in the last light. Above them the tower stood empty and silent, and Edith caught herself counting the seconds between heartbeats, timing the silence, waiting for it to break.

Inside the vestry, she unlocked the safe and lifted the ledger out — heavy as a paving stone, bound in leather gone black with age — and laid it on the table before Oscar Crane.

"Page one thousand seven hundred and six," she said. "The year of your grandfather. Read it. And then, God help us both, we're going to talk about what we intend to do about it — because tonight, while I was copying, I checked the current year's page."

Oscar looked up. "And?"

Edith's hand was steady on the lamp. Her voice was not quite.

"This year's entry wrote itself sometime today. Fresh ink. No hand that wrote it." She turned the ledger toward him, to the page for 1974, and beside it, already curling at the edge like something newly born, the page for this year, where two words stood in wet, glistening, self-satisfied ink:

*CRANE TWIN.*
