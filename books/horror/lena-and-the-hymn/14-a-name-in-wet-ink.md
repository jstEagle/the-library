## 14. A Name in Wet Ink

The vicarage kitchen smelled of toast and old paper, which struck Oscar as the most honest smell in Hallowmere. Everything else in the village had a smell underneath its smell — the chapel's candlewax over damp stone, the pub's ale over something mineral and cold, the family house's dust over dust. But Edith Marsh's kitchen was just a kitchen, with a kettle ticking on the hob and a loaf under a cloth, and for one absurd moment Oscar wanted to sit down at her scrubbed table and ask if he could stay forever.

He did not sit down. He stood at the table's edge while Edith laid the ledger open between them like a surgeon preparing a patient.

The book was enormous — bigger than a Bible, bound in leather gone black with handling, its corners worn round as river stones. Two centuries of entries lay inside it in a dozen different hands: spidery copperplate, blunt Victorian block letters, a wartime scrawl that pressed so hard it had torn through three pages beneath.

"Every year," Edith said. "Since 1832. One name, written in, struck through when it's been returned." She turned pages with the care of someone turning bandages. "I've kept it eleven years now. My predecessor kept it forty-three. He used to say it was the only honest book in the parish."

"Honest," Oscar repeated. The word tasted wrong.

"Everything else lies, Mr. Crane. Birth registers, marriage records, gravestones. They all pretend this village is ordinary." She stopped at a page near the end and smoothed it flat with both palms. "This doesn't pretend anything."

Oscar looked down.

The entry read **1974** in faded brown ink, and beneath it, **CRANE**, and beneath that, two lines struck through the name — not once, as every other entry had been, but twice, hard enough to score the paper — and then, squeezed into the margin in a different hand, smaller and hurried, another word he couldn't quite make out. It looked less like a name than like a smudge, as though someone had tried to write *water* or *nothing* and given up halfway.

"Twice-struck," Oscar said. "No one else is."

"No."

"What does that mean?"

Edith was quiet long enough that the kettle came to a boil behind them and neither of them moved to take it off. When she spoke, her voice had dropped into the register people use in hospitals.

"It means they cheated. Your grandfather wrote Crane, and the thing below took the grandmother instead of the twin, and the ledger knew. Books know, Mr. Crane. It struck the name once for the offering and once more because the offering was false. And then your grandfather tried to write what he'd actually given it, and the ink wouldn't hold him." She touched the smudge in the margin with one fingertip, gently, the way you'd touch a bruise. "Fifty years it's been waiting for the difference. Fifty years, and now the bell has stopped being patient."

Oscar stared at the double-struck CRANE until the letters seemed to swim. Somewhere in the walls of his skull, his grandfather's voice was reading those attic letters again — *the thing below wants a Crane twin* — and somewhere beneath that, colder, was the memory of Lena's face in the photograph, smearing like wet ink, and the phone call, and *come find me, follow the sound*, and the terrible arithmetic of it all.

"Then it wants me," he said. "Or it wants Lena. Those are the options."

"Those are the options it has shown you." Edith closed the ledger partway, then seemed to think better of it and opened it again. "But the book has always allowed for substitution. That's the whole horror of it, if you want my opinion, and nobody ever does. It doesn't demand a particular soul. It demands *a* soul. A name strong enough to be remembered. The vicars have always chosen who gets returned, and every vicar before me chose someone whose absence would trouble the village least." Her mouth went thin. "That is not a sentence I say with pride."

"You're saying I could write anyone."

"I'm saying the pen works for whoever holds it." She slid the ledger fully toward him, and from her apron pocket she took a pen — an old dip pen, black-handled, its nib stained the color of dried blood — and set it beside the book with a small click that sounded, in the quiet kitchen, like a lock turning. "And I am saying that a man who loves his sister might find that a mercy. Or a curse. I've never decided which."

Oscar didn't touch it. Not yet.

"Who would I even write?" he asked, and heard how hollow the question was even as it left him. Because there was a village full of names outside that window — Silas Fen stumbling home along the lane with his bottle, Mrs. Aldous who'd described Lena with a stranger's face, the postman, the schoolteacher, fifty warm bodies who each remembered his sister differently and none of them correctly — and every one of them was a candidate now, wasn't it? Every one of them was a door he could open instead of the one marked with his own name.

"That," said Edith, "is the question every vicar has answered badly."

* * *

He thought about Silas first, which shamed him, and thinking about it shamed him more, and thinking about *that* made him laugh — a short, ugly sound that startled a pigeon off the windowsill.

Because Silas Fen was right there, wasn't he? Silas, who had carried the bundle down into the dark in 1974. Silas, who had heard the bell every year since and drunk himself deaf against it. Silas, who had told Oscar the truth when no one else would — *she's not dead yet, that's the problem* — and who had led him to the crypt entrance and shown him the carvings and the footprints going in and none coming out, and who had wept afterward, actually wept, big silent tears running into the grey stubble of his chin.

Silas owed a debt too. Wasn't that almost justice? Wasn't there a version of this where Oscar stood in the kitchen and told himself a story about fairness, about the man who helped carry the baby down paying for it fifty years later, and slept fine after?

He picked up the pen.

It was heavier than it looked. Cold, too — cold as the water in the flooded crypt must be, cold as the stranger's face in the coffin — and the nib left a small dark bead of ink on his thumb when he tested it against the air. Edith watched him without expression, but she had gone very still, the stillness of a woman watching weather come in.

"The book will help you," she said quietly. "That's the thing no one tells you. You don't have to know how to write a life away. You only have to want it."

Oscar turned to the last blank page. The paper was thick and yellowed, ruled in faint lines, and it seemed to breathe very slightly under the lamplight — no, not breathe. *Wait.*

He wrote: **SILAS FEN.**

The ink went down easily, eagerly, the nib gliding as if pulled. And then Oscar lifted the pen, and sat back, and looked at what he'd done, and felt nothing — no thunderclap, no tolling bell, no change in the pressure of the air. Just two words in wet ink on old paper, and the kettle ticking, and Edith's hands folded on the table.

"Is that it?" he asked. His voice came out smaller than he intended.

"It's never 'it' immediately," Edith said. "There's a night. There's always a night. The bell rings at dusk tomorrow, and whatever name stands unstruck on that page is the name it calls for." She reached across and turned the ledger toward herself, reading his handiwork, and her brow creased. "You've written it clearly. That matters. Sloppy names slip free; clear ones hold."

Oscar nodded. He found he couldn't look away from the page. There was something about the wetness of the ink — it should have been drying by now, should have dulled and set, but it gleamed, it *moved*, catching the lamplight the way water catches it, and as he watched, the S in SILAS seemed to lean slightly to the left.

"Edith," he said. "Is the page supposed to—"

The ink ran.

Not smeared — ran, deliberately, the way a snail draws a line, the two words dissolving and re-forming, the letters crawling across the ruled lines like insects deciding on a new direction. Oscar's stomach dropped through the floor of him. He gripped the table's edge. Beside him Edith had risen halfway out of her chair, one hand pressed to her mouth, and she was saying something — *no, no, it's never done this, it's never* — but Oscar barely heard her, because the ink was forming letters now, deliberate and unhurried, one by one, in a hand that was not his and not his grandfather's and not any human hand at all:

**L**

The letter bloomed dark and glistening in the center of the page.

**E**

Oscar's heart was slamming. He knew — some animal part of him knew exactly what was happening, knew it the way you know a dream is a dream — but his body wouldn't move. His hand still held the pen. The pen wanted to be writing. He could feel it pulling at his fingers like a dog on a lead.

**N**

"Don't finish it," Edith whispered. "Mr. Crane. Don't let it finish."

**A**

The word sat there, complete, shining: **LENA**. Written in ink that was still moving, still wet, still hungry, and Oscar understood — finally, wholly, in the marrow of him — what Edith had been trying to tell him since the beginning. This was not a record. Records are passive. Records remember. This book *chose*. This book *wanted*. Every year for two hundred years it had lain open on tables like this one while frightened men and women bent over it with pens, believing themselves the authors, believing they were choosing, and every year the book had gotten exactly what it was owed.

It hadn't accepted Silas. It hadn't even considered Silas. Silas was a scrap; Lena was a feast. Or Oscar was. The book would take whichever of them it could get, and it had simply written down its preference, the way you'd mark an order at a shop counter.

*Come find me,* Lena's voice had said on the telephone. *Follow the sound.* And he had wondered, in his worst hours, whether that voice was really hers or something wearing her. Now he understood it was neither. It was the book. It was the appetite, learning to speak in the voices of the things it wanted, patient as water, finding the shape of every crack in him and pouring itself in.

His sister's name gleamed up at him from the page, and beneath it, faintly, he could hear — not with his ears, with something older than ears — the first far-off note of a bell that had been melted down sixty years ago.

"No," Oscar said aloud. His voice cracked on it. "No. You don't get her."

He slammed the ledger shut.

The sound was tremendous — a boom like a door in a deep cellar, like a coffin lid, like the last ring of a great bronze bell cut short — and the lamp guttered, and the kettle shrieked, and somewhere below the floorboards, below the foundations, below the chapel and the stones that held the recast metal of the old bell, something vast shifted its weight and went still.

Then silence. Ordinary kitchen silence. The pigeon resettled on the sill. Edith took the kettle off the flame with shaking hands and poured two cups of tea neither of them would drink.

Oscar looked down at his thumb. The bead of ink from the nib was still there, a small dark crescent, and it would not wash off — he tried, later, at the pump in the yard, scrubbing until his skin was raw. It stayed. A mark. A receipt. The book's way, perhaps, of noting that a debt remained outstanding.

He stood in Edith's kitchen with his stinging thumb curled in his fist and made himself say the thing he had spent the whole evening refusing to say.

"There's only one name left that it'll take. And it isn't hers, and it isn't Silas's, and God help me, Edith, I don't know if I'm brave enough to write it."

Edith set down her cup. For the first time since he'd known her, the old woman looked afraid — not of the book, or the bell, or the thing below the chapel, but of him, or for him, he couldn't tell which.

"Then don't write it," she said. "Go down and give it back in person. There's rope in my shed, and Silas knows the way, and the key to the crypt is in the vestry drawer where it's been since 1911." She met his eyes. "The book takes, Mr. Crane. But a man who walks down there on his own feet — that's not a taking. That's a giving. And I have a feeling, from two hundred years of that ledger, that the difference matters more than we know."

Outside, the light was failing toward dusk, and far off — impossibly, terribly, for the first time in daylight — the bell began to ring.
