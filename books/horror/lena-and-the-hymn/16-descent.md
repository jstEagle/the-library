## 16. Descent

The key weighed more than it should have.

Oscar had stolen it from Edith's desk at half past nine in the evening, which felt like the sort of thing a man should be able to do with a certain elegance — a sleight of the hand, a distraction, maybe a cough. Instead he had knocked over a jar of pens, said "sorry" to no one, and stood in the vestry with his heart going like a rabbit's while the bell above them rang and rang and did not stop.

It hadn't stopped since sundown. That was the new thing. For two hundred years, according to Edith's ledger, the bell had contented itself with a ring at dusk — a single toll, patient as a landlord. Now it rang continuously, a bronze heartbeat over the whole village, and every dog in Hallowmere had gone silent, and every light in every window had gone out except one: Edith Marsh's study lamp, where she sat copying pages by hand into a notebook, because she said the book remembered better than paper did.

She hadn't tried to stop him taking the key. That was the part that sat strangely in his chest. She had looked up from her copying, seen his hand on the ring of keys, and said only, "The iron one. The rest are just doors." And then, as he'd turned to go: "Oscar. The water will be warm. Don't let that comfort you. Warm water down there is not kind water."

He'd wanted to ask what she meant. He hadn't. Some questions you save like the last sweet in the bag, hoping you'll never have to eat them.

* * *

Silas Fen was waiting at the waterline with a coil of rope over his shoulder and a lantern in his fist, and he looked, Oscar thought, like a man who had dressed for his own funeral and gotten the venue wrong. He'd shaved. That was somehow the most alarming part. In all the days Oscar had known him, Silas had worn his beard like a fortification, and now his jaw was bare and pale and human, and it made him look younger and more frightened and more honest all at once.

"You came," Oscar said.

"I said I would." Silas handed him one end of the rope. "Don't make it sentimental. I've had two pints to keep my hands steady and if you hug me I'll drop the rope and we'll both have to go home and think about what we've done."

"I wasn't going to hug you."

"You had the face. My brother had the face. He hugged me at our mum's funeral and knocked the collection plate out of the vicar's hands." Silas tested the knot at Oscar's harness — a harness he had built himself, out of a climbing rope, a leather belt, and what appeared to be two curtain rings, and which Oscar had decided not to inspect too closely, on the principle that a bad plan held together with love was still better than a good plan held together with nothing. "Right. The entrance is under the chapel floor. There's a hatch beneath the third pew from the back, left side. The Ashcombes used to sit there, when there were Ashcombes. The vicar before Edith — old Mr. Pellow — had it boarded and blessed and boarded again. The blessing's worn off. The boards, you'll find, have opinions."

"Opinions?"

"You'll see." Silas lit the lantern and held it up, and the light made his face into something carved and old. "Oscar. Before we go down. There's a thing I've carried fifty years and I'd rather set it down here, in the air, where it can be a weight, than down there where it can be a hand on my ankle."

Oscar waited.

"I carried the bundle," Silas said. "1974. Your grandfather walked in front of me with the lantern and your grandmother — the other one, the one nobody remembers, God forgive us all — she walked behind, and I carried the bundle, and it was warm. That's what I never told anyone. Everyone said the baby was cold, dead, a sad little thing. It wasn't cold. It was warm like a loaf out of the oven, and it was humming. Not crying. Humming. A tune I didn't know then." His voice dropped. "I know it now. It's the hymn. The one your sister sings. The one the bell plays if you stand under the tower long enough with your mouth shut."

The bell rang, above them, through them, in the soles of their feet.

"Then let's go give it back," Oscar said, and heard how thin his voice was, and said it again, louder, to hear it be less thin.

* * *

The chapel at midnight was a different building than the chapel by day. By day it smelled of polish and old flowers and the particular dust of hymnals. At midnight it smelled of the river, of green water and iron and something underneath the iron that was older and wetter, like the inside of a shell. The bell rang without pause, and Oscar understood now that it wasn't ringing *in* the tower. There was no tower left to ring in — the belfry had been empty since 1911, a stone tooth with nothing in it. The sound came up through the floor. Through the walls. Through the pew where he sat down, hands shaking, to pull up the boards.

Silas had been right about the boards. The first two came away easily. The third groaned, resisted, and then came free with a sound like a long indrawn breath, and beneath it was the hatch — black iron, no lock, only a ring — and beneath the hatch, stairs, going down into a dark that the lantern light fell into and did not so much illuminate as apologize to.

"Rope first," Silas said, and paid it out, and tied his end around the pulpit rail, which was oak, and which had held worse, he said, though he didn't say what.

Oscar went first. He didn't decide to; his body simply went, the way water goes, and Silas followed with the lantern, and the hatch came down above them with a boom that the bell swallowed whole.

The stairs were stone and slick with a soft black growth that wasn't moss and wasn't mold and gave, very slightly, underfoot, like skin. Oscar counted the steps because counting was what he and Lena did, counting was the ritual, doors and steps and bells and verses — and at forty steps the stairs ended in water.

It was black and utterly still and it reflected the lantern perfectly, so that it looked less like water than like a hole in the world with a light burning at the bottom of it.

"Warm," Silas said, and put his hand in, and pulled it back as if scalded, though it wasn't. "Warm as a bath. Don't let that comfort you. Edith said."

"She told me too."

"She tells everyone. It's her one kindness." Silas tied the rope off on an iron ring set into the wall — a ring worn smooth, polished by decades of other hands, other ropes, other descents — and looked at Oscar with his bare, honest, terrified face. "The crypt runs under the whole chapel. The water was waist-deep in my day. The air pocket where the twins are — the old men called it the Casting Room, because that's where they poured the bell, before it was a bell, when it was just metal and a name. You follow the left wall. You'll know the room when the roof opens up. And Oscar —"

"I know. Don't answer it."

"Don't answer *her*," Silas said. "If you hear her. If you hear her singing, or calling, or saying your name in that voice she had when you were children and she wanted the last biscuit — you keep walking. Because the thing down there has learned her. It's been learning her since the day they took the baby, and it's had fifty years of practice, and it is very, very good now."

The bell rang. The water shivered, all across its black surface, in rings that moved outward from the center, as if something far below had turned over in its sleep.

Oscar stepped down into the warm.

* * *

The water came to his chest. It was blood-warm, bath-warm, the exact temperature of the bath Lena used to run when they were children and she'd sit in it for hours until her fingers went to prunes, reading, singing, refusing to get out. The thought of it hurt him in a place below grief, some older floor of the heart, and he made himself keep walking, one hand on the left wall as Silas had said, the rope trailing behind him through the water like a lifeline in more than metaphor.

The wall under his palm changed as he went. First it was dressed stone, blocks fitted by masons who had loved their work. Then it was rougher. Then it was not stone at all.

It was bronze.

Great seams of it, green-black with age, running through the stone like veins through a body, and where the lantern light touched them they gleamed dully, and Oscar remembered the archive, the county records, the photograph of the foundry yard in 1911: *metal recast into the chapel's foundation stones.* Not melted down and discarded. Melted down and *built in.* The bell was not gone. The bell was the building. He had been walking inside its throat since the stairs.

"Silas," he said, and his voice came out strange, flattened, the way voices sound in a large empty room, except the room was not empty, the room was a bell, and every word he spoke was a small strike against its inner wall.

"I know," Silas said behind him. "I felt it on the way out, fifty years ago. I've felt it every day since. You live your whole life inside a bell, boy, you learn to talk quiet."

They went on. The water deepened, then shallowed, then deepened again, and once Oscar's foot came down on something that shifted and rolled and was smooth and long and he did not look down and he did not stop walking. Once the lantern guttered and in the dark he heard, very close to his ear, a voice humming — Lena's voice, the childhood hymn, the one about the doors and the counting — and his whole body turned toward it like a plant toward light, and the rope went taut against his harness, and the humming stopped, and in the silence he heard the water settle, patiently, the way a listener settles.

"Don't answer it," he said to himself, out loud, and the bell above took his words and rang them back to him in bronze, and he walked on.

* * *

The roof opened.

That was the only way to describe it. The low crypt passage suddenly rose into a vaulted chamber, and the vault was ribbed with bronze, and the ribs converged on a point above the black water where a shape hung — not the bell itself, not any bell that had ever hung in any tower, but the *negative* of a bell, a bell-shaped absence, a place where the air did not move, and the continuous ringing came from everywhere and nowhere and most of all from inside Oscar's own ribs, which he was beginning to suspect had been bronze all along.

The Casting Room. The air pocket was a shelf of stone along the far wall, above the waterline, dry and pale as a skull.

And on the shelf, two figures sat.

The Ashcombe twins. The foundry girls, drowned in 1911, the bell's first returns. They were not dead and they were not alive and their faces were like wax that a sculptor had abandoned halfway — one had an eye finished, gray and lovely and terribly patient, and the other eye only suggested, a smooth blank. Their hands were folded in their laps. Their dresses were dry. Their feet, where the water lapped the shelf, were wrinkled as if from a very long bath.

The older one — or the one that seemed older, though they must have been the same age, twins, like him and Lena — turned her finished eye toward him.

"You're the Crane one," she said. Her voice was the voice of a bell struck very softly, very far away. "The one that's still whole. We wondered if they'd send you. They usually send the guilty one first. The guilty ones run."

"I'm not guilty," Oscar said, and then, because the room was bronze and bronze rang true: "I'm guilty. My family cheated you. My grandfather wrote a name over a name and we've all been living in the space between them ever since."

The twins looked at each other, in the way of people sharing a joke fifty years old.

"He's honest," said the one with no face yet. "The honest ones get to the end. The honest ones are the ones the bell likes."

Oscar waded toward the shelf, and the water was warm, and beneath him — not far beneath him, ten feet, twenty, a distance that was also no distance at all — something vast shifted. He felt it in the water first, a slow displacement, a tide with intention. Then he felt it in the bronze walls, a hum that was almost a purr. It was not rising. It was not coming for him. It was *turning over*, the way a great dog turns over by the fire, and settling again, and the settling said, more clearly than any voice: *I have waited two hundred years. I can wait a little longer. Tell me why you've come.*

Oscar pulled himself up onto the stone shelf, into the dry air, beside the unfinished girls, and the rope paid out behind him across the black water, and above, far above, through stone and bronze and the whole patient body of the chapel, the bell rang on.

He looked at the twins, and they looked back with their one and a half eyes, and he said the thing he had carried down all forty steps and through all the warm water:

"Tell me what it wants. Not a life. You said — everyone says it takes a life. But that's not it, is it. That was never it."

The finished-eyed twin smiled, and her smile was beautiful and terrible, like a hymn sung at a funeral.

"No," she said. "It wants a *memory*. A memory strong enough to hold a name. A name is a heavy thing, Crane. It sinks. Someone has to carry it." She lifted one wax-smooth hand and pointed, past him, down, into the black water, to where the vast thing lay listening. "And down there, at the bottom, there is a name that no one has carried for fifty years. A baby's name. Your family's theft. And it is *so heavy*, and it is *so cold*, and it has been lying at the bottom of our bell all this time, waiting for someone to come and pick it up."

The water moved. The whole chamber of it, moving at once, a slow swell from the center outward, and in the swell Oscar heard — not Lena's voice, not the hymn, but something older, something patient, something that had been counting doors in the dark since before there were doors —

— and the bell rang, and the shelf of stone trembled, and the unfinished twin said, in a voice like a struck match:

"It's waking up. You'd better say what you came to say."
