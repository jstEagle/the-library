## 18. What Was Taken in 1974

The air pocket was the size of a confessional, and Oscar had been sitting in it for what felt like a liturgical age, listening to the water breathe.

That was the only word for it. The flooded crypt inhaled and exhaled around him, slow and tidal, and somewhere beneath that rhythm something vast turned over in its sleep — or its waking; he could no longer tell the difference — and the bell above rang on, muffled now by stone and water, a sound he felt in his teeth more than heard.

The Ashcombe twins sat across from him with their unfinished faces.

He had stopped flinching from them. That was perhaps the most horrifying part. Two hours ago — or two minutes, or two days; time down here moved like syrup poured uphill — he had nearly screamed himself hoarse at the sight of them: two girls of perhaps nineteen, dressed in the fashion of 1911, their skin smooth and blank as candle-wax, their features suggestions rather than facts. Eyes like thumbprints in dough. Mouths that opened when they spoke, but never quite in the same place twice.

Now he found them almost companionable. They were, after all, the only people in Hallowmere who had told him the truth without contradiction.

"You said," Oscar said carefully, "that it doesn't want a life."

The twin on the left — he thought of her as Edith-before-Edith, though she predated every Edith in the parish register — tilted her head. The gesture was gentle, birdlike, and wrong in a way he couldn't catalogue.

"It wants to be *held*," she said. Her voice was like water finding its way through gravel. "A name is heavy, Mr. Crane. Did you know that? Heavier than bronze. A name has to sit inside someone, or it sits nowhere at all."

"And if it sits nowhere—"

"Then someone must carry it." The second twin smiled, or performed the shape of smiling. "It has been carrying us for sixty-three years. We were very tired, you see. The foundry. The water. Our father's hands on the mould, and the pour going wrong, and the ringing, the ringing, the *ringing*—" She stopped herself with visible effort, the way a person stops a sneeze in church. "Forgive us. Old grief. It pools."

Oscar hugged his knees. The water lapping at the ledge where he sat was warm — blood-warm, bathwater-warm — and he tried very hard not to think about why. Above him, faint through forty feet of stone and flood, the bell tolled again. Once. Patient. Like a hand knocking on a door it knew would open eventually.

"Lena," he said. "My sister. What does it want with her?"

The first twin reached out — her fingers left faint ripples in the air itself, which was a thing Oscar decided he would deal with later, in a better century — and touched his wrist. Her hand was cool and dry as paper.

"It doesn't want to eat her, if that is your fear. It wants to *wear* her. A name needs a body to ring in. Your sister's name is being drawn up out of everyone who holds it — out of photographs, out of registers, out of the warm rooms of memory — and pulled down here, into the dark, where it can be wound around the clapper like wire." She paused. "But names are stubborn things. They fray. They need constant holding. And we—" the twins exchanged a look that contained an entire conversation's worth of exhaustion— "we are so old now, Mr. Crane. Even our own names have gone soft in our mouths. Say yours to yourself tonight and see how long it takes to feel strange."

He didn't say it. He wasn't brave enough for that experiment.

* * *

It was the second twin who mentioned the coffin.

"The one they buried yesterday," she said, apropos of nothing, in the tone of someone remarking on weather. "In your churchyard. With the locket."

Oscar went very still. "You know about that."

"We know everything the water knows. The water runs under the whole village." She folded her wax-smooth hands. "That woman in the box. Do you know who she is?"

"A stranger. Someone wearing my mother's locket. The undertaker swore it was Lena, and I—" His voice cracked and he hated it. "I've spent four days being told my living sister is dead and my dead-looking sister is alive, and nobody in that village can hold both ideas in their head at once except me and a drunk."

The twins made a sound between them — not laughter exactly, but the memory of laughter, preserved like a flower in a book.

"She is not a stranger," the first twin said softly. "She is the opposite of a stranger. She is a person made entirely out of substitutions. Think, Mr. Crane. What happened in 1974? What did your grandfather do?"

And Oscar, sitting in the warm dark with the drowned dead of 1911, finally let himself think it all the way through.

His grandfather's letters, found in the attic behind the water tank, written in a hand that deteriorated across eleven pages from confident copperplate to a scrawl like a man writing during an earthquake. *The thing below wants a Crane twin. It has always wanted a Crane twin; the debt names our blood. But it will accept a substitution if the substitute is unclaimed — if no one else will miss the name.* And then the terrible arithmetic of that October: his grandmother's sister, arriving with a newborn, the baby's father gone, the mother already half-decided to give her up. *We took the child before the papers were signed. Mother says God will forgive an arithmetic of mercy. One life given so that Lena may keep hers.*

One life given. And then never named. Never claimed. Never entered into any register, because a registered child leaves records, and records are memories, and memories are exactly what the family could not afford. So the infant had been carried down into the flooded crypt by Silas Fen and two other men whose names Oscar had found scratched out even in his grandfather's letters, and given to the dark as *the Crane debt*, nameless, and the bell had been quiet for fifty years.

Fifty years. A nameless infant, kept — not killed, *kept*, the way the twins were kept — growing in the dark on substituted names. Fed on scraps of identity that belonged to others. Wearing, eventually, a face assembled from borrowed features, because a person cannot go fifty years without a face.

A person who, when the bell finally called in the debt and the village needed a body to put in Lena's coffin, had risen — or been raised — shaped like a woman, wearing his mother's locket because the locket was the closest thing to a true possession she had ever been permitted.

"Oh," Oscar said. The word came out very small. "Oh, you poor woman."

"Yes," said the twins together, and there was something in their ruined voices that might have been relief — the relief of witnesses finally believed.

* * *

He asked them how she had lived down here, and the twins looked at him with their thumbprint eyes, and the second twin said: "How did we live? The water keeps what it is given. It does not let go. She grew the way a pearl grows, Mr. Crane — around an irritation. The irritation was the empty space where her name should have been. Fifty years of wanting to be someone. And then your bell began to ring again, and the ledger wrote CRANE TWIN, and the debt came due, and the water gave her up — gave her *up*, lifted her, sent her walking — because the debt required a body, and she was the nearest unclaimed thing."

"So the coffin—"

"The coffin was her first bed with a lid on it that anyone ever provided." The first twin's voice gentled further. "She lay down in it because she was told it was hers. Has anything ever been hers, do you think? Have you considered what it is to be a person assembled entirely out of other people's discards? A locket that isn't yours. A face stitched from strangers' memories of a girl who isn't you. A grave dug in someone else's name." She tilted her head again. "And underneath all of it, one true thing. One small, buried, genuine fact."

"What fact?"

"That she exists. That she was born. That in October of 1974 a woman named Margaret Vane gave birth to a daughter in the cottage hospital at Ashford, and that daughter weighed six pounds two ounces, and cried, and was real." The twin leaned close, and her breath smelled of river stones. "Your grandfather stole many things, Mr. Crane. But the theft was not of a baby. Babies are not property. The theft was of a *story*. Her beginning. Her name. The right to be remembered as having happened."

The bell rang again, closer somehow, though nothing had moved.

"And the debt?" Oscar whispered. "The ledger says CRANE TWIN. It wants Lena. Or it wanted me — Edith offered me the pen. How does giving this woman her story back pay anything?"

The twins were quiet for a long moment. In the silence the water breathed, and far below, the vast patient shape shifted, listening, always listening.

"Because," the first twin said at last, "it was never hungry for flesh. Flesh is cheap; the water makes flesh. It is hungry for *holding*. For a name held so firmly, so completely, that it rings true all the way through — the way a bell rings true when the metal is honest. Your family cheated it with a hollow substitute: a name with nothing inside, a story with no keeper. Of course it rang. Of course it has rung for fifty years. You gave it an empty bell and expected it not to notice the lie in the tone."

"But a true story—"

"A true story is a bell cast properly. If the unclaimed one is *claimed* — truly claimed, named aloud, remembered, given back every stolen fact of her beginning, held in memory by someone who means it — then the debt is paid in the only coin it ever actually wanted. Not a death. A *recognition*." The second twin smiled her waxen smile. "It does not want your sister, Mr. Crane. It wants to stop being lied to."

Oscar sat with that. The warm water lapped. Somewhere above, dusk was coming to Hallowmere, and Edith Marsh was presumably watching the empty tower with her weary complicit face, and Silas Fen was presumably drinking himself toward courage, and Lena —

Lena was somewhere below, in the flooded dark, singing fragments of a hymn to keep her name intact, forgetting a word per verse, dissolving by syllables while he sat in an air pocket having revelations.

"How do I find her true name?" he asked. "Margaret Vane — is she alive? Are there records? The hospital, the birth—"

"The water remembers what the village chose to forget," said the first twin. "Ask it kindly. Ask it *by what it lost*. And Mr. Crane—" both twins leaned forward now, and their unfinished faces were suddenly terrible and tender at once, like the faces of saints in old, worn paintings— "when you speak her name, mean it. Hold it. Names are heavy, and the holding must be honest, or the bell will know. The bell always knows. It knew in 1911. It knew in 1974. It has had fifty years to learn the sound of a lie, and it is *very* well educated."

* * *

He climbed back up through the dark with Silas's rope wet and heavy in his fists, and the words the twins had given him turning over and over like a stone in a tumbler, wearing smooth, becoming something he could carry.

Grief was still there — grief for his grandfather's cowardice dressed up as mercy, grief for the woman in the coffin who had lain down in a stranger's grave because she'd never been offered any other, grief for fifty years of a hunger nobody fed. But underneath the grief, something new was setting like cement.

Purpose. The specific, load-bearing kind.

Because he understood now, climbing toward the thin grey line of the crypt entrance, what he had to do. Not a substitution — substitutions were the family disease, three generations of swapping one name for another and calling it survival. No more arithmetic of mercy. No more hollow bells.

Somewhere in the county records, in a hospital archive, in the fading memory of a woman named Margaret Vane — alive, the twins had implied, *ask the water by what it lost* — there was a true name. Six pounds two ounces. A cry in October. A beginning that had been stolen and could be returned.

And on the final night, when the bell rang its last, Oscar Crane would stand in the flooded dark and give the unclaimed one back her story — every fact, every date, spoken aloud and meant — while Edith read it from the ledger above and Lena sang the hymn complete.

Not a sacrifice. A restitution.

His boots found the ledge. Grey light. Cold air. Above him, through the chapel floor, the bell was ringing still — but differently now, he thought. Less like a demand.

More like a question, waiting for an answer he finally knew how to give.

He put his shoulder to the trapdoor and pushed, and as it opened, the tolling swelled to fill the whole tower, and beneath it — unmistakable, threaded through the bronze like a signature — he heard a second sound. Faint. Wet. Human.

Someone below was humming the hymn.

And she was missing fewer words than she had been yesterday.
