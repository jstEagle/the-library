## 1. Dead Air

The red light above the studio window had been burning for eleven years, and Oscar Ashgrove had long since stopped seeing it as anything but a heartbeat. ON AIR, it said, in letters that had faded to the color of an old wound, and at 11:47 on a Thursday night in October, it glowed the way it always glowed—steady, patient, indifferent to whatever came through the headphones.

"Three minutes to the top of the hour," said the board, in the voice of Dev Ramanathan, who was not really the board but was sitting behind the glass in the producer's booth with his feet up on a filing cabinet and a cup of vending machine coffee balanced on his knee. "You've got the weather, the dedication line, and then we're wide open. Full moon tonight, by the way. You know what that does to your callers."

"It makes them poetic," Oscar said, adjusting the microphone an inch closer, a ritual so worn into his hands that he did it without thinking, the way other men checked their watches or touched their wedding rings.

"It makes them *weird*," Dev said, but he was smiling. He was always smiling at this hour, when the station emptied out and it was just the two of them and the insomniacs and the grieving and the lonely, all of them stitched together by a signal that reached, on clear nights, all the way to the coast.

Oscar had inherited the show the way he had inherited everything else in his life—sideways, from a dead woman, without instructions. Aunt Ruth had hosted *The Night Desk* for thirty-one years before her heart quit during a reading of the shipping forecasts, and when Oscar slid into her chair at the age of twenty-six, the listeners had accepted him the way a house accepts a new tenant: cautiously at first, then completely. Fifteen years now. Fifteen years of reading dedications to the dead, of taking calls at 2 a.m. from truckers and nurses and widows, of saying *you're not alone, you're not alone, you're not alone* until the words wore smooth as river stones.

He believed every word. That was the thing nobody understood about Oscar Ashgrove. He wasn't performing comfort. He meant it. The night was enormous and cold and full of people lying awake in houses that felt too big, and if a voice on the radio could make the dark feel furnished, then the dark was a little less dark. That was all. That was everything.

"Caller on line one," Dev said. "Edie Marsh, if you can believe it. Says she wants a dedication."

Oscar's hand was already moving toward the fader. "Edie Marsh," he repeated, and something flickered at the edge of his attention—a moth against a screen, there and gone. The name was familiar the way all his regulars' names were familiar, worn into him like the groove in the studio chair. Edie Marsh, Tuesday nights, always requesting the same Patsy Cline song for her husband Walter, who had died in 2011 and whom she missed with a devotion that had long since stopped being sad and become almost architectural, a monument she visited weekly.

He faded up the line. "You're on the air, this is *The Night Desk*. Who's this?"

"Oscar." The voice came through warm and slightly rough, the way it always did, with that faint rasp of a woman who had smoked for twenty years and quit for twenty more. "It's Edie. Edie Marsh."

"Evening, Edie." He settled back, and the chair received him with its familiar creak. "Let me guess. Patsy Cline?"

A small laugh. "You know me too well. But—not tonight. Tonight I wanted something different." A pause, and in the pause Oscar heard something he couldn't name, a quality in the silence like the silence itself was listening. "I wanted to tell you about the funeral."

"The funeral," Oscar said, gently. Walter's anniversary was in the spring. He kept a ledger in his head, a calendar of griefs, and this wasn't on it. "Whose funeral, sweetheart?"

"Mine."

The word arrived without drama. That was what he noticed later, when he lay awake replaying it—the complete absence of drama, no tremor, no joke in the voice, no catch where a punchline would live. Just a woman stating a fact about her week, the way she might have said *mine* if he'd asked whose car was blocking the driveway.

Oscar laughed. It was the professional laugh, the one he kept holstered for exactly this kind of call, the 2 a.m. callers who said strange things to see if the night was safe for strangeness. "Well," he said, "I hope it was a good turnout. You deserve a packed house, Edie."

"It was," she said. "Forty-one people. I counted."

The laugh died somewhere in his chest, quietly, without ceremony, and he found himself sitting forward in the chair with his hand flat on the console as if the desk might need steadying.

"You counted," he repeated. His voice was still level. Fifteen years of practice will do that; fifteen years of taking calls from people at the end of their ropes had taught him that the voice was a rope bridge, and if it swayed, the caller fell. So he kept it taut and level and said, "Tell me about it."

And she did.

She described the church—St. Brigid's, on the east side, where the radiator knocked like a polite ghost all through the winter service. She described the flowers, white chrysanthemums because her daughter Karen had always said her mother deserved something clean after a lifetime of clutter, and how the chrysanthemums smelled faintly of nothing at all, the way flowers smell when they've been refrigerated. She described the organist fumbling the transition into the hymn and the congregation covering for him, forty-one voices finding the key by feel. She described Karen's coat, the green one with the missing button that Edie had been meaning to sew on since March, and how Karen had worn it anyway, and how Edie had wanted, more than anything, more than she had ever wanted anything, to reach out and fix that button, and couldn't.

"Six rows back," Edie said, "there was a man nobody recognized. Tall, gray coat. He stood for the whole service and left before the graveside. I don't think he was there for me. I think he was there because he didn't know where else to go. I wanted to tell Karen to ask him to lunch. That's the kind of thing you think of too late."

Oscar's eyes had gone to the window. The studio was on the second floor of the old broadcast house, and beyond the glass the parking lot lay under the full moon, every puddle a small white coin. He was aware, distantly, of Dev behind the glass, of the coffee cup, of the ON AIR light. He was aware of all of it the way a man is aware of the surface of the water when he is sinking through it.

"Edie," he said, and his voice was still level, still the rope bridge, and he marveled at it from a great distance, at the calm of his own instrument. "When did this happen? The funeral."

"Last Friday," she said. "The twenty-fourth."

The twenty-fourth. He knew the twenty-fourth. He had dedicated Patsy Cline to Walter Marsh on the twenty-fourth, because Karen had called the station that afternoon, her voice small and sanded down, and asked if he would play it, because it had been her mother's favorite and her mother had passed on Tuesday and they were burying her Friday and she just—she just wanted the song to be somewhere in the world, on the air, at the hour her mother used to listen.

Oscar had played it. He had said *this one's for Edie Marsh, who kept the light on for Walter for thirteen years, and who we'll miss*—and the words had felt right at the time, the way tribute words do, smooth and final and safe.

"Edie," he said. The console clock read 11:53. The moonlight lay on the parking lot like a sheet pulled up to the chin. "Edie, I'm going to ask you something, and I want you to answer me straight."

"Ask me anything, Oscar. You always did."

"Where are you calling from?"

A pause. Not a long one. But in it, he heard the silence change again, that listening quality, and beneath it now something else—something like wind moving through a house with all the windows shut.

"That's the funny part," Edie said, and there was warmth in it, real warmth, almost amusement. "I've been trying to figure that out all week. There's no cord. There's no phone. I'm just—here. And the show was on, and I've called every Tuesday for thirteen years, so I called. It didn't seem like a thing that needed explaining. It seemed like a thing that needed *doing*."

"Edie—"

"You always say it at the end of every show." Her voice was softer now. "You say, *keep a light on for somebody, and somebody's keeping one on for you.* I kept mine on for Walter for thirteen years. Nobody kept one on for me. Except you, I think. I think that was you, Oscar. So I wanted one more dedication. Not Patsy Cline this time. Play something for Karen. Something about buttons, or coats, or—no. Just something kind. She'll be listening. She doesn't sleep anymore."

The clock read 11:56. Oscar's hand was still flat on the console. He could feel his own pulse in his fingertips, quick and stubborn and alive, and he fixed his eyes on it, on his own living hand, like a man gripping a rail.

"Edie Marsh died on Tuesday the twenty-second," he said, and his voice cracked on *died*, just a hairline fracture, and he heard it and hated it and could not fix it. "I played her song on Friday. Her daughter called. I'm sorry, but I—Edie, whoever this is, this isn't funny. This is cruel, actually. This is a cruel thing to do to a man who—"

"Who talked to the lonely every night for fifteen years and never once believed any of it was real?" No anger in it. Only gentleness. "Oscar. Look at the window."

He looked. The parking lot, the moon, the puddled coins of light. And in the reflection of the glass, faint as breath on a pane, the studio behind him—console, chair, the red glow of the light. And in the chair, himself, hand on the desk, exactly as he was.

Except that the reflection was a half-second behind. He knew it the way you know a note is flat, some animal part of him registering the wrongness before his mind could name it. He blinked, and the reflection blinked, and the blink arrived late, like thunder after lightning.

"Edie," he said, and now the levelness was gone, and what was underneath it was very young, twenty-six years old, sitting in a dead woman's chair for the first time. "Edie, I don't understand."

"You will," she said. "That's the hard part. You're a good man, Oscar Ashgrove, and you'll understand it, and it won't be easy, but you won't be alone. We're—well." A breath, or the memory of a breath. "We're keeping the light on for *you* now. All of us. That's what the show is, sweetheart. It always was. It's a porch light. And porch lights don't go out just because the person who lit them comes inside."

The clock read 11:59.

"Play something kind for Karen," Edie said. "And Oscar? Thank you for the song. It reached me. I want you to know it reached me."

The line went to a soft, clean tone, the dial tone of a call ended, and then to nothing, and the nothing had a texture he had never heard before, a held-breath quality, as though the whole frequency were listening.

* * *

"Oscar." Dev's voice, behind the glass, careful. "Oscar, you've got dead air, buddy. Thirty seconds to the hour. Oscar?"

Oscar looked at him. Dev's face behind the glass was ordinary and worried and alive, coffee cup in hand, eyebrows up, and the sight of it was so solidly, stupidly real that Oscar felt something in him unlock.

"—coming back," Oscar heard himself say, and his hand found the fader, and the needle of the studio clock swept to the hour, and he was talking, the voice doing what it had been trained to do for fifteen years. "You're listening to *The Night Desk*. It's the top of the hour, the moon's full, and I've just been reminded of something I'd like to say to all of you, so forgive an old man his sermon."

He talked. He talked about porch lights. He talked about how a light left burning in a window is a promise made to the dark, and how the dark always keeps the receipt. He introduced a song—something kind, something about coats, he couldn't even have said what it was afterward—and faded it up, and the music filled the studio the way water fills a room, gently, from the bottom up.

Then he took off the headphones, and the music vanished, and the silence came in behind it like a tide.

Dev was saying something through the glass. His mouth was moving. Oscar raised a hand—not a wave, just a *give me a minute*, and Dev, to his credit, nodded and turned back to his board.

Oscar sat in the chair. The ON AIR light burned. The song played for Karen, wherever Karen was, awake in a house that was too big, not sleeping, just as her mother had said.

"Six days," Oscar said aloud, to the empty studio, in the voice he used for no one, the voice that was only his. "She's been dead six days."

The light did not answer. The moon did not answer. But in the window, in the dark glass, the reflection of a man sitting alone in a chair raised its hand a half-second after he did, and Oscar Ashgrove, who had spent fifteen years explaining away every strange and tender thing his aunt's house had ever shown him, sat very still and told himself, in a calm and level voice, that it was the glass. Old glass. Warped glass. Nothing more.

He told himself that all the way home, through the moonlit streets, up the drive, to the dark porch of the house he had inherited from a dead woman who had left no instructions.

And it was not until he was inside, with the door locked and the lights on and his hand pressed flat against the cool hallway mirror—until he saw his own palm meet itself a heartbeat too late—that Oscar Ashgrove finally stopped talking.
