## 2. The Second Behind

The drive home from the station took eleven minutes, the way it always did, and Oscar used every one of them to rebuild the wall between himself and the broadcast.

This was a discipline he had perfected over fifteen years, the way a surgeon perfects the washing of hands—ritualized, automatic, never spoken of. The show ended at four. By four-oh-five he was in the car with the windows cracked and the radio deliberately off. By the first stoplight he had begun the sorting: the night's calls went into a drawer in his mind labeled *work*, and the drawer closed, and whatever had been said in them—the funeral of Edie Marsh, described with loving precision, the lilies, the casket, the way her sister had wept into a handkerchief embroidered with her own initials—stayed in the drawer. By the second stoplight he was thinking about whether he had milk.

It worked. It had always worked. That was the whole point of the method.

The house was dark when he pulled into the drive, the way houses are dark at five in the morning, but not empty-dark. It had the particular darkness of a place that has been waiting up. Oscar had lived with that darkness for fifteen years and had long ago stopped interpreting it as anything but the ordinary personality of an old building settling into its own weight. The porch light was on—he always left it on, a habit from Aunt Ruth, though he'd stopped being able to say whether it was for visitors or for the house itself—and it threw his shadow long and thin across the boards as he climbed the steps.

Inside, he did the things he did every morning at this hour. Coat on the hook. Keys in the bowl. Kettle on. He moved through the kitchen in the half-dark without turning on the overhead light, because the overhead light in the kitchen had a stutter to it that he'd been meaning to fix for six years, and because there was something about the house in the early hours that made him want to keep it dim, the way you keep your voice down in a church or a hospital.

The tea steeped. He carried the mug upstairs.

The bathroom mirror was the last thing he looked at every night and the first thing he looked at every morning, which meant he had looked at it perhaps ten thousand times, and it had never once looked back at him strangely.

Tonight it did.

He noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a song you've known all your life—before he understood what was wrong, he understood that *something* was. He was setting the mug down on the sink's edge, and in the glass, the man in the mirror was setting down his mug too, and there was—there was a hitch. A stutter. The mug in the mirror touched the porcelain of the reflected sink a fraction after his own mug touched his own sink, a delay so small that a saner man would have blamed his tired eyes, blamed the hour, blamed the four hours of dead air still humming in his ears.

Oscar did not blame his eyes.

Oscar raised his hand.

The man in the mirror raised his hand—one beat later. A full, unmistakable, countable beat. The way an echo follows a shout. The way a shadow follows a body only if the light is willing.

Oscar stood in his bathroom at ten past five in the morning, tea going lukewarm at his elbow, and watched his own reflection arrive late to its own life.

He did not scream. He did not drop the mug. What he did, because he was who he was—a man who had spent fifteen years building a fortress of reasonable explanations brick by brick, *the house settles, old wiring, grief does strange things to the living*—was what he always did with the unexplainable.

He got out a notebook.

* * *

The metronome was in the study, in the bottom drawer of the desk, beneath a sheaf of old show notes and a birthday card from Dev that said *TO THE ONLY MAN ON RADIO WHO MAKES INSOMNIA SOUND COZY*. He'd bought it years ago for a segment he'd never aired—a bit about the tempo of the city at night, three a.m. as a piece of music—and it had lived in the drawer ever since, a small brass pyramid of unused ideas. He wound it. It ticked. He carried it upstairs like a man carrying a loaded question.

He set it on the sink, facing the mirror, and stood back, and watched.

*Tick.* His hand went up. The reflected hand went up.

*Tick.* His hand came down. The reflected hand came down.

*Tick. Tick. Tick.* And on the fourth beat, he saw it clearly, indisputably, with the cold clarity of the very tired and the very frightened: the reflection was not synchronized. It was following. His hand moved, and the mirror's hand moved after, a sixteenth of a second behind, maybe an eighth, small enough to miss if you blinked, large enough to catch if you knew what to look for—and now he knew, and he would never unknow it.

He tried the other hand. Same result. He tried a slow movement, lifting the metronome itself, and watched the mirrored metronome lift in the mirrored hand a beat late, so that for one strange instant there were two metronomes in the room, out of phase, ticking against each other like two hearts in one chest disagreeing about the time.

Oscar sat down on the closed lid of the toilet and wrote in his notebook, in the small tidy hand he used for show prep:

*Observation one: lag is consistent. Not random. Not intermittent. Consistent.*

*Observation two: lag is uniform. All objects, all movements, delayed equally. This suggests a systemic cause, not a surface defect.*

*Observation three: the glass is not the problem.*

He underlined that last one twice, and then sat looking at it, because he didn't actually know what it meant. If the glass were the problem—if the mirror were simply old, silvered badly, warped by decades of steam—then the reflection would be distorted, not delayed. Distortion lives in space. Delay lives in *time*. And a mirror, Oscar's reasonable mind insisted, has no business holding time at all. A mirror is a surface. A surface cannot lag. A surface cannot *remember*.

And yet.

He thought, briefly, wildly, of calling Dev. Dev would laugh. Dev would say, *Ashgrove, you've been talking to dead people on the radio for fifteen years, it was only a matter of time before your own house started calling in.* And Oscar would laugh too, and the laugh would be the wall, and the wall would hold, the way it always held, and tomorrow night he'd be back in the booth with his calm voice and his drawer full of things that didn't happen.

But Edie Marsh had described the lilies. Edie Marsh had said *the organist played the hymn too fast, you know how she rushes*, and Edie Marsh had been dead six days, and Oscar had said, on air, in his warmest voice, *thank you for sharing that with us, Edie*, and the whole listening city had heard a dead woman's voice and thought nothing of it, because grief makes the bereaved credulous and the rest of the city was asleep.

The wall had a crack in it. He could feel the draft coming through.

He went back to the mirror.

* * *

The sun came up while he tested, and the morning light changed nothing, which was somehow worse. He had half hoped—had *counted* on, in the superstitious corner of himself that he kept locked and denied—that daylight would dissolve the anomaly, that the lag was a creature of the dark hours, a trick of lamplight and exhaustion, and that at seven a.m. the mirror would snap back into honest, instantaneous obedience.

It didn't. At seven, in the flat gray light, the reflection still trailed him. At eight, when he brushed his teeth, the mirrored toothbrush followed his like a shy child imitating a parent. At nine he stood in front of the glass and simply breathed, watching his own chest rise and fall a beat behind his chest, and felt something in him tilt, quietly, like furniture sliding on a floor that is very slightly not level.

He made charts. This was the scientific curiosity part, the part he clung to, the part that let him keep functioning: he made a chart of the lag against the hour, and a chart of the lag against his distance from the glass, and a chart of the lag against which mirror he tested—the bathroom, the hallway cheval, the little round one in the entry that had come with the house and had never hung anywhere else in fifteen years.

The bathroom mirror lagged. The hallway cheval lagged. The little round mirror lagged.

Every reflective surface in the house was running one beat behind the world.

Every reflective surface *in the house*. He stood on the front porch at ten in the morning with his phone's camera facing the front window, and the window showed him a reflection in perfect time. He walked to the corner, to the big plate-glass window of the shuttered pharmacy, and his reflection there kept pace like a loyal dog. The world outside was synchronized. The house was not.

The house was not, and the lag was not growing—but it was not shrinking either. It was *holding*. Steady as the metronome. One beat. Always one beat. As if the house had settled on a rhythm and intended to keep it.

He sat on the porch steps with the notebook on his knee and the morning going warm around him, and he let himself think the thought he had been not-thinking since five a.m.

*The mirror isn't showing me late. The mirror is showing me what happened one beat ago. It's not delayed. It's recording.*

No. That was worse. He crossed it out.

*Alternative: the reflection is not delayed. The room is. As if the room itself is running slightly behind—*

He stopped writing. A car went by on the street, ordinary, a mother in a minivan, and he watched her pass and felt the enormous ordinary weight of the world outside the property line, the world that ran on time, and understood with a clarity that felt less like discovery than like remembering that the boundary of the anomaly was the boundary of the lot. The house was a place where time ran a beat behind. Everything else was fine.

Everything else had always been fine. That was the thing. In fifteen years, nothing in this house had ever been fine, and he had explained every single instance, and the explanations had held because he had built them to hold, load-bearing, lovingly maintained. The footsteps upstairs that matched no one's gait: the pipes. The smell of bread baking at three in the morning in a house where no one baked: the neighbor's kitchen, the wind. The way the third stair spoke in two voices: old wood. The callers on the show—no, that was the station, that was the city, that was grief and phone lines and coincidence, that had nothing to do with the house—

Except that Edie Marsh had died six days ago, and the house had started lagging, and he did not believe in coincidences that arrived in flocks.

His phone buzzed. Dev. *Great numbers last night. Weird numbers. You doing something different with the dead-air stretches? People are calling in saying the quiet parts are their favorite. Keep doing whatever it is you're doing.*

Oscar looked at the message for a long time. *The quiet parts.* The stretches when he let the silence run, when the open line breathed its soft room-tone hiss into ten thousand dark bedrooms. People loved the quiet parts. People had started calling specifically to sit in the quiet parts, he'd noticed, callers who said almost nothing, who just wanted to be on the line while the silence held.

He typed back: *Same as always.* Then, after a moment: *Dev, do you ever get the feeling the show is listening back?*

He deleted it. He sent instead a thumbs-up, which was the wall in its most portable form.

* * *

He did not sleep. He meant to, and he lay down in the afternoon with the curtains drawn and the house quiet around him in that particular way it had of being quiet, not empty-quiet but *attentive*-quiet, and he lay there and listened to his own heart and thought: one beat. Everything in this house is one beat behind. Which means that right now, somewhere, the room I am lying in is still catching up to the room I was in one beat ago.

He got up. He went to the bathroom, because it was the worst of them, the clearest of them, the mirror where he'd first caught the house in the act.

The light was fading toward evening. He stood before the glass and watched the man there—gray at the temples, tired in a way that sleep wouldn't fix, holding a notebook against his chest like a shield—and he raised his hand one more time, slowly, and the man in the mirror raised his hand one beat later, and this time Oscar did not reach for the metronome or the notebook or the wall of explanations.

This time he said, out loud, to the glass, in the voice he used on the air at three in the morning when a caller had said something true:

"Okay. I see you."

The reflection's mouth moved, one beat behind his. But the shape it made was not the shape *okay, I see you* makes. He would swear it, later, to himself, in the notebook, in the small tidy hand: the reflection's mouth made a different shape. A longer shape. Two syllables, maybe three, formed with a care that his own mouth had not used, as if the man in the glass were not echoing him but *answering* him—as if the one-beat-late room were not a recording at all but a place, and someone in that place had been waiting a very long time for the living man on the near side of the glass to stop explaining and start looking.

He could not read the shape. The mirror was old, the light was failing, and the mouth closed before he could parse it.

But his hands were shaking, and he set the notebook down on the sink, and in the mirror the notebook was still being set down, and in the silence of the house he heard—faintly, from below, from the kitchen, from the direction of the cabinets Aunt Ruth had filled with her rules and her tins and her terrible, patient silence—a sound like a drawer opening.

He had not opened a drawer.

He stood very still. The house held its breath, one beat behind.

Then Oscar Ashgrove, custodian of the reasonable, keeper of the wall, went downstairs to see what his aunt had left him besides the house.
