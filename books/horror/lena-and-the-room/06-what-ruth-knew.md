## 6. What Ruth Knew

The attic ladder had always been the house's one honest confession. Everything else in the Ashgrove house performed—floorboards creaked theatrically, doors swung with a sense of being watched, the radiator ticked like something counting—but the ladder was simply old, and simply heavy, and it dropped down into the hallway with the same reluctant groan it had made for fifteen years. Oscar pulled the cord at nine in the morning, on a day off, with a flashlight in one hand and no good reason in the other.

That was the lie he told himself, anyway. He had a reason. He'd had it since two o'clock the previous afternoon, when he'd stood in the bathroom with a metronome ticking at sixty beats a minute and watched his own raised hand arrive in the glass a full second after he'd raised it. He had run the test eleven times. He had changed the tempo, changed the light, changed the angle of his body. He had even, in a moment of desperation he was not proud of, covered the mirror with a bath towel and stood in the dark listening to the metronome, as if the lag might be some property of the glass itself, some trick of silvering and old light. When he pulled the towel away, his reflection was already looking at him. It had been waiting. That was the part he couldn't explain away, and so, at nine in the morning on his day off, he climbed into the attic to find the explanation Ruth had surely left, because Ruth left explanations for everything, because Ruth had been the kind of woman who labeled her spice jars and her extension cords and, once, memorably, a box of *miscellaneous grief*.

The attic smelled the way it always had: dust, cedar, and the faint chemical sweetness of old newsprint. The single bulb overhead buzzed on after a delay of its own, and Oscar stood in the yellow light between the rafters and let his eyes adjust. Fifteen years he'd been coming up here, mostly for Christmas boxes, once for a suitcase, once—during a bad winter he didn't think about anymore—to sit among the stored lives of a family that had ended with him and feel less alone than the empty rooms below ever allowed. He knew this attic. He knew the wardrobe with the broken left foot propped on a brick. He knew the trunk of Aunt Ruth's things, the one he'd gone through the week after her funeral, when grief had made him thorough and then, abruptly, made him stop.

He went to the trunk now and knelt in front of it, and the floorboard under his knee said *you're home* in the house's usual language, and Oscar, for the first time in fifteen years, did not find it comforting.

* * *

The journals were where they had always been, wrapped in a pillowcase at the bottom of the trunk beneath a wedding dress that had never been worn and a man's watch stopped at 4:17. There were eleven of them. He remembered eleven. He remembered reading the first three that terrible week in 2009, the ones full of recipes and radio schedules and a running feud with the neighbor's dog, and he remembered deciding, with the particular mercy of the newly bereaved, that the rest could wait. They had waited fifteen years. They had waited, he understood now with a cold spreading feeling, exactly as long as they needed to.

He carried them to the window at the attic's far end, where the light came through a round glass pane that had always been slightly warped, and he sat down cross-legged on the floorboards with the stack in his lap, and he opened the fourth journal, the first one he'd never read, and the first line was dated March 2nd, 1974.

*The Vane family is moving out on Friday. The mother came by to say goodbye and I could not look at her. I have the box in the cedar chest and I have not slept.*

Oscar read it twice. The name meant nothing to him. The cedar chest was three feet to his left. He looked at it the way you look at a dog that has just spoken.

He kept reading.

*March 4th. Harold says I am being foolish, that a music box is a music box, but he did not hear the girl. She came to the door yesterday while he was at the plant and she said please, and she said it's not mine to keep but it's mine, and I told her the house was sold and everything in it went with it, and she looked at me the way you look at someone who is lying, and I was lying, and she knew it, and she is nine years old.*

*March 6th. The movers came today. I watched them from the kitchen window. They carried out the dresser and the beds and the piano and the mother kept counting things under her breath like a prayer, and the father kept looking back at the house, and the girl stood on the sidewalk holding nothing. Her hands were empty. I have never seen a child hold emptiness like that, like it was the heaviest thing she owned. They drove away at four o'clock. Harold waved. I did not.*

Oscar's mouth had gone dry. Outside the round window, the morning went on being ordinary—someone's lawnmower, a jay scolding in the elm—but the attic had begun to feel like the inside of a held breath. He turned pages. Ruth's handwriting, which he knew from grocery lists and birthday cards, from the recipe for lemon squares she'd written out for him the last Christmas she was lucid, grew smaller and tighter as the entries went on, as though the words themselves were trying to take up less room in the world.

*March 11th. It started. I want to write it plainly so that if I go mad I will at least have been mad in a legible hand. The mirror in the hall is late. Not broken—late. I raise my hand and the woman in the glass raises hers a moment afterward, like an echo, like someone remembering to. I have tested it with the metronome from Harold's workshop. One second. Always one second, as if the room in the glass is one second away from the room I am standing in, and something between us is slow.*

Oscar set the journal down on his knee. His hands were not entirely steady. *The metronome from Harold's workshop.* He had run his own test with a metronome two days ago, in a bathroom, alone, feeling like a madman, feeling like the first person in the history of the world to notice such a thing. And here was Ruth, fifty years earlier, describing it in a legible hand, having tested it the same way, with the same instrument, in the same house.

There was an explanation for that. There was always an explanation. He had built a career, of a kind, on explanations—he took the world's static and gave it a shape people could sleep to. The metronome was a coincidence; the journals had primed him; the human mind was a pattern-finding machine that would find patterns in static, in dead air, in the voices of callers who were not who they said they were. He had spent fifteen years being the calm voice at the end of the line, and he had spent those same fifteen years being the calm voice at the end of his own line, talking himself down from every creak and cold spot and door that opened on its own. *Old house. Settling. Drafts. Imagination.* He had a whole liturgy of it. He had said the words so many times they had worn smooth as river stones.

He picked the journal back up because his hands needed something to do.

*March 19th. The lag is worse, or I am. Today I stood in the hall and the woman in the mirror did not move at all until I turned to go, and then she moved, all at once, catching up, like film run fast. Harold says the glass needs resilvering. Harold says many things. Harold has begun to sleep in the front room.*

*April 2nd. I took the box out of the cedar chest to look at it. It is a child's music box, painted blue, with a windup key on the side, and it is heavier than it should be. I shook it and something inside it shifted—not the mechanism. Papers. I opened the bottom, which is lined in felt, and there is a paper folded inside, an old paper, and a name written on it in a hand I do not know.*

Oscar looked at the cedar chest. It was three feet away and it had been three feet away for fifteen years, and he had opened it a hundred times, and he had never once looked at the bottom, because who looks at the bottom of a chest? Who looks at the floor of the world? His heart was doing something he chose not to name. He did not get up. He kept reading, because the journal was a rope and he was somewhere over a drop, and the only thing worse than following it down was letting go.

*April 9th. The house is doing what houses do when they are not houses. I have written and crossed out three attempts to say this plainly. Plainly, then: the rooms are not all ours. There is a room in this house that was taken, and the taking did not finish, and the house knows. The mirror is the seam. The lag is the seam showing. I have begun to understand that we did not buy this house. We bought everything around it, and the middle came along the way a splinter comes along in the wood.*

*April 14th. The girl was on the sidewalk again. Not the family—they are in another state now, the mother wrote to the realtor asking after her box and the realtor wrote to me and I lied in my reply, I lied on paper, which I will answer for. But the girl was on the sidewalk, at dusk, in a dress out of season, holding nothing, and she looked up at the window where I was standing, and she did not wave. She waited. Children do not wait like that. Children wave. She waited the way the mirror waits, one second behind, and then she was not there, and the street was empty, and I have not turned on the hall light since.*

*April 20th. Harold left. He says it is the house. I say it is the house too. We are saying the same thing and it is the end of us. He took the car. I have the journals and the cedar chest and the room, and I do not know which of those I am guarding.*

Oscar stopped. The lawnmower outside had stopped too, or he had stopped hearing it. He was aware of the attic around him with a new and terrible attention—the wardrobe on its brick, the boxes labeled in Ruth's square hand, the round window with its warped glass through which the morning came in slightly wrong, slightly delayed, the way everything in this house came in slightly delayed, and he had called it charm for fifteen years, he had called it *character*, he had told listeners on the air that he lived in an old house with a soul, and the house had a soul, and he had never once asked whose it was.

He thought about 2009. He thought about the week after Ruth's funeral, when he had sat in this attic going through her things and had found, in the first three journals, nothing but recipes and radio and the dog feud, and had closed the trunk and gone downstairs and made himself a drink and told himself that was that, that was the whole of her, a woman of lemon squares and station schedules. He had been so tired then. He had been so willing. Fifteen years of explanations, and here was the moment they had all been built on: a tired grieving man deciding that three journals out of eleven was a complete accounting. He had curated his aunt the way you curate a house, keeping the rooms that were easy and never opening the door where the weight was.

He turned the page. The handwriting had changed—not the hand, but the pressure, the pen digging in, the letters leaning like people leaning into wind.

*May 3rd. I have decided to write down the rule, the real rule, the one underneath the list in the kitchen, because I am seventy-one years old and the mirror has started showing me the hall with the lights off, and I do not think I have long. If anyone finds this: the room was taken in the winter of 1973 by the man who owned this house before us, who took the deed and the box and the name out of the middle of another family's life and closed the door behind him. The house has been holding its breath since. Everything strange here is the breath. Everything strange here is a family, mid-move, that never got to finish leaving. I could have given the box back. I had fifty years of chances. I kept it because the room was warm, and the room was ours by then, or we were its, and I told myself the family had gone, had moved on, had forgotten. They have not forgotten. They cannot. It is in the glass, one second behind, waiting for us to finish what we started.*

*Return it or become it.*

The last line stood alone at the bottom of the page, underlined twice, the pen having torn slightly through the paper. Below it, the journal was blank. All the remaining journals, he saw, flipping through them with a hand that no longer pretended to be steady—blank, blank, blank, except the eleventh, the last, which held only a single entry dated the week before Ruth died, a single line in a hand gone thin and wandering:

*It is showing me the room now. It is warm. I am so tired. I am sorry, girl. I am sorry, whoever finds this. Return it or become it. I chose, and I am sorry.*

* * *

Oscar sat in the attic for a long time after that. The light through the round window moved the way light moves, which is to say slowly, which is to say, he thought, and could not stop thinking, *on time*.

He got up on legs that felt borrowed and went to the cedar chest. He opened it. The wedding dress, the watch, the smell of fifty years. He reached past all of it, down to the bottom, past the folded quilts he'd never disturbed, and his fingers found felt. A lining, torn at one corner, loose. Under the felt: a hollow. And in the hollow, a weight his hand understood before he did—heavier than it should be, the way Ruth had said, heavier than a mechanism and some folded paper had any right to be, as if it had been holding something the whole time, as if weight were just another word for waiting.

He drew it out into the light. A child's music box, painted blue, the paint gone soft and chalky at the edges, a windup key on the side. He turned it over. The bottom was lined in felt, and the felt was old, and the corner was loose, and beneath it, he could feel the shape of a folded paper and something written in a hand he did not know.

He did not open it. His hands were on the lid and his thumbs were on the seam and he did not open it, because he understood, in the way you understand the last step of a staircase in the dark, that once he opened it, the fifteen years of explanations would be over—not collapsed, not disproven, but *over*, finished, retired, and he would have to live in the world as it actually was, in the house as it actually was, with the family that had never finished leaving, with the girl on the sidewalk holding the heaviest thing she owned.

Behind him, in the attic's long mirror—the one leaning against the rafters that he had never once looked at, that he was looking at now—his reflection raised its hand to its face.

He had not moved yet.

It was waiting for him to catch up. And in the glass, over his shoulder, in the warm yellow light of a room one second away, something small was standing at the edge of the frame, holding nothing, mouthing a name he could not yet hear.
