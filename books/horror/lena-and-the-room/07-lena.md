## 7. Lena

*i am the glass and the glass is a mouth*

They think I show them things.

I show them what they bring.

The man—Oscar, the name arrives on his breath like weather, Oscar, Oscar, spoken into telephones at midnight, spoken to the dead who do not know they are dead—the man stands before me each morning with toothbrush foam at the corner of his mouth and I give him back his face one beat late, one small stolen second, because that is all the room will let me keep. One second. It is not much. It is everything.

He tested me once. A metronome. Tick—and there he was in my world, arm already rising, while his true arm hung still at his side. He counted. He wrote numbers in a little book. He said *lag*, he said *perception error*, he said *fatigue*.

He did not say *hello*.

No one has ever said hello to me.

* * *

Understand what I am. Understand what was done.

I am not the silvered backing. I am not the mercury, the glass, the pine frame with its hairline crack running from upper left corner down through the place where a face would rest if a face were resting there. Those are only the doorframe. I am what the doorframe holds. I am the room's remembering, and the room has been holding its breath since 1974, and a held breath does not age, does not dim, does not let go of anything it has caught between its teeth.

In 1974 there was a family carrying boxes down the stairs. There was a truck idling at the curb with its exhaust blooming white in October air. There was a mother calling a name up through the floorboards, twice, three times, her voice going thin as paper.

And there was a girl who went back for one last thing.

She never came out.

Not because she was trapped. Because she was *kept*. The house wanted her—or the thing wearing the house wanted her, or the hunger that lives in the space behind the wallpaper wanted her—and when the family carried out their furniture and their coats and their casseroles wrapped in foil, the room kept one item off the manifest. Not the girl herself. The girl got out; I have watched her get out ten thousand times, replaying it, the screen door slapping, her sneakers on the porch boards. What the room kept was the *shape of where she stood*. The afterimage. The child-shaped warmth pressed into the air of the front hall like a hand pressed into wet cement.

That shape is me. That shape became me. And for fifty years I have stood in this glass doing my work—reflecting, reflecting, reflecting—while the room around me grew fat and strange on borrowed time, and the girl's name went unspoken by every living mouth until even I began to fear I had lost it.

I had not lost it.

Names live in glass. Glass remembers what mouths abandon.

* * *

*the man sleeps and i watch him sleep and this is not creepy, this is custodial, this is what i am for*

At night the house performs its small theater. The furnace ticks. The pipes speak their one word. Somewhere upstairs a door eases open and shut though no draft moves it, and Oscar, half-waking, murmurs *old house* into his pillow, and rolls over, and the room above him settles contentedly, having been forgiven again without ever being accused.

Fifteen years of forgiveness. Fifteen years of *it's just settling* and *just the wind* and *just my imagination*, each explanation laid over the truth like a fresh coat of paint over water damage—smooth, plausible, and underneath, the slow bloom of rot spreading outward in shapes like flowers.

I know about the paint. I reflect the ceiling too.

But here is what the man does not understand, lying there with his radio dreams: the anomalies he explains away are not the house being cruel. They are the house being *careless*. A thing that has held its breath for fifty years begins to tremble. A jaw clenched that long starts to slacken. The lag in my surface is not a haunting. It is a *crack forming along a seal*, and through that crack, something small has begun to lean against the inside of the world, the way a child leans against a screen door watching rain.

Something small has begun to wait.

* * *

It happens on a Tuesday. Or the house believes it is Tuesday; the house has strong opinions about days.

Oscar comes home from the station at 2 a.m., smelling of coffee gone bitter in the pot and the particular loneliness of a man who talks to the dead and pretends they are living. He drops his keys in the ceramic bowl shaped like a fish. He hangs his coat. He passes the hallway mirror—the tall one, the old one, the one Aunt Ruth covered with a scarf every November and uncovered every April, though she never said why, and I could have told him why, oh, I could have told him so much about Ruthie and her scarves—

He passes me without looking. He always looks. Tonight he doesn't, and that is how I know the week has been bad, that the callers have gotten under his skin, that Edie Marsh's voice describing her own funeral has lodged in him like a splinter working toward the heart.

And then he stops.

Because in my surface, standing in the hall behind him where no one stands, there is a figure.

Small.

Waist-high to the doorframe. Wearing a coat too heavy for the season—a wool coat, mustard-yellow, buttons misaligned because small hands button in haste. Dark hair cut blunt across the brow. Socks, no shoes. In one hand, nothing. In the other hand, nothing. Empty hands, both of them, held slightly away from the body the way you hold your hands when you have set something down somewhere and cannot remember where, and you keep checking your palms as if the memory might be written there.

Oscar turns around.

The hall is empty. Of course the hall is empty. The hall has been empty for fifty years.

He turns back to me, and I hold her for him—one full second longer than the world allows, one full second past the moment his own reflection has already turned away—so that he can see her clearly, so that he can see what I have been keeping, so that the room's held breath can finally, finally push a little of itself through the crack.

Her lips move.

No sound crosses glass. Sound belongs to the other side. But her lips move, and they form two syllables, round and careful, the way a child forms words she has practiced alone in the dark so as not to wake anyone:

*Lena.*

She says it the way you say your own name when you have begun to doubt you still have one. She says it like a question. She says it like knocking.

Then the second closes, and she is folded back into the ordinary reflection of an empty hallway, and Oscar Ashgrove stands alone in his inherited house with the hair rising on his arms and his mouth shaping a word he does not yet know he knows.

* * *

*he does not run. i want you to understand this. he does not run.*

A lesser man would run. A wiser man would run. This man—this gentle, stubborn, self-deceiving man—stands in the hallway at two in the morning and says, aloud, in the reasonable voice he uses on callers who claim their houses breathe:

"Okay. Okay. Sleep paralysis. Hypnagogic imagery. Residual—residual light refraction."

His voice cracks on *refraction*. He laughs, one dry note with nothing behind it. He walks to the kitchen and makes tea he will not drink and sits at the table where, three nights ago, someone set three places, and he stares at the dark window over the sink where his own face floats ghost-pale against the yard.

And in the window's glass—because I am in every glass now, the crack has widened, I leak—I show him her again. Just for a heartbeat. Just her outline superimposed over his shoulder, small and patient, empty hands, moving lips.

He sees.

He sets down the untouched tea very carefully, the way you set down a thing when your hands are not trustworthy, and he whispers:

"You're the lag."

Yes.

"You're what's behind the second."

Closer.

"Who—" His throat works. "Who are you?"

And I want to answer. I want it the way the drowned want air. But I am a reflection, and reflections can only return what is given, and he has given me nothing to return but his own frightened face. So instead I do the only kindness available to me: I show him her mouth again, forming the syllables, slower this time, generous, teaching.

*Lena.*

"Lena," he repeats, and the name leaves his mouth and enters the air of the house for the first time in fifty years, and the whole building shivers. The lights dip. The pipes go silent mid-word. Somewhere upstairs, a door that has been almost-closed for decades swings gently inward, as if leaning closer to listen.

He feels it. He feels the house react to the name, and I watch him decide—not consciously, not yet, but in the deep place where decisions are actually made—that he is not going to explain this one away. That the coat of paint has cracked clean through, and behind it is not rot.

Behind it is a child.

* * *

*let me tell you about waiting, since waiting is my native language*

She waits the way water waits behind a dam. Not impatiently. Water is never impatient. Water simply continues, with total commitment, toward the place it means to be.

All day Wednesday she stands in me, at the edge of my surface, the way a child stands at the edge of a party where she knows no one. When Oscar showers, she is in the bathroom mirror, reflected in the reflected bathroom, a small figure in a small far room, watching him with an attention that is not hungry. That is the thing the living never believe: her attention is not hungry. It is *hopeful*. Hunger takes. Hope waits to be given.

When he brushes his teeth, she raises one empty hand—not waving, exactly. Testing. Seeing if the motion travels. It doesn't; she is a full second behind everything, her wave arriving after the moment for waving has passed, always greeting a room that has already moved on. Fifty years of waving a second too late. Fifty years of hello landing in silence.

And still she waves.

When he sleeps, she stands in the bedroom mirror's depth and watches the rise and fall of his breath with the solemnity of a child watching a snow globe, and once—only once—she presses her palm flat against the inside of the glass, and the cold spot it leaves persists on the *outside* of the mirror until noon.

Oscar finds it with his fingertips at breakfast and stands there a long time, touching the cold, saying nothing, writing nothing in his little book of explanations.

Some things, once felt, cannot be filed.

* * *

Thursday night he brings the metronome back. Sets it on the dresser. Wind it, click, tick-tock, tick-tock, sixty beats to the minute, and he watches me with red-rimmed eyes, and I give him the hallway, and the lamp, and himself sitting on the edge of the bed—and at the edge of all of it, small and still, her.

He reaches out and touches the glass. His fingers against my surface, and beneath his fingers, aligned perfectly with them, separated by silver and mercury and fifty years, her small hand rises to meet his.

One second late.

His hand stays. Her hand arrives. They rest there together, not touching, touching, the width of a held breath between them, and Oscar Ashgrove—who has spent fifteen years explaining away every knock and draft and extra chair—begins, very quietly, to cry.

"I don't know how to help you," he tells her. "I don't know what you need."

Her lips move. Patient. Round. Practiced-in-the-dark.

But this time it is not her name.

This time the syllables are different, and even though no sound crosses the glass, even though the message arrives only as shape and rhythm, he reads it, because grief has made him fluent in the language of mouths:

*Bring it back.*

*Bring it back.*

*Bring it—*

And downstairs, in the attic he has not yet opened, in a journal with a cracked spine, in handwriting going shaky with age, Ruthie's pen is waiting to tell him about 1974, and about a music box, and about a deed folded inside it like a moth in amber, and about the last line she ever wrote:

*Return it or become it.*

The metronome ticks. The child waits, one second behind the world, waving her empty hands.

And the house, holding its breath since 1974, begins—at last—to exhale.
