## 10. The Knocking

The knock came at 3:04 in the morning, and it came in Oscar's own rhythm.

He knew it before he was fully awake—knew it the way you know your own name spoken by a stranger's mouth, the syllables right and the voice wrong. *Shave-and-a-haircut* was what everyone else did, the cheap universal knock, two brisk raps and a taunting pair. But Oscar had never used that. Since he was nine years old he had knocked with his own private pattern, one he'd invented on the porch of his mother's last apartment so she would always know it was him coming home: three slow, then two fast, then one that lingered—a knuckle held against the wood like a hand pressed to a forehead checking for fever.

Three slow. Two fast. One long.

Oscar lay in bed in the dark and listened to the echo of it fade down through the hallway, through the front door, out into the cold October night where no one should be standing. His heart performed its own percussion, arrhythmic, panicked. The digital clock on the nightstand glowed 3:04, and he thought—not for the first time this week, not for the tenth time—that 3 a.m. was when the house did its thinking. That was when Edie Marsh had called the station. That was when the metronome in the bathroom had started skipping, as if even time needed to clear its throat before speaking to him.

The knock came again. Three slow. Two fast. One long.

"Nobody knows that," Oscar whispered to the ceiling. "Nobody living knows that."

His mother had died in 1998. Ruth had died five years ago, and Ruth had never once knocked on his door in her life—she simply appeared in rooms the way weather appears, already there, already commenting. Dev didn't know the rhythm; they'd never lived close enough for knocking to be part of their friendship. And the dead callers—the dead listeners who had been phoning in all week asking him to keep the light on—they didn't knock. They arrived through wires and static, through the little speaker on his nightstand that sometimes, at 3 a.m., breathed without being asked.

The knock came a third time, patient now, almost gentle. And underneath it, faintly, Oscar heard something else: the sound of breathing that wasn't quite breathing, the wet-paper rustle of lungs remembering their job.

He got up because lying still felt more dangerous than moving. He took the baseball bat from behind the dresser—he'd owned it since college, never swung it at anything but a ball, and holding it now felt less like arming himself than like carrying a talisman from a saner life—and he went down the stairs with the lights off, because some animal part of him believed that if he couldn't see the hallway, the hallway couldn't see him either.

The house was cold in the way it had been cold all week, a cold with intention behind it, a cold like held breath. The streetlight through the front windows laid long amber bars across the floorboards, and Oscar noticed—stopped mid-stair, bat raised—noticed that the bars were trembling very slightly, the way light trembles through water. As if the whole house were submerged. As if he were the one underwater, looking up.

*Fifteen years,* he told himself, descending the last steps. *Fifteen years of pipes and settling foundations and raccoons in the crawlspace. Fifteen years of rational explanations, and every single one has held until this month.*

But that wasn't true anymore, and he knew it. The explanations had begun failing like old teeth—one loose, then another, then the whole jaw gone soft. The mirror lagged. The callers were dead. Ruth's journals sat open on the kitchen table upstairs, and the sketch of the music box stared up at the ceiling all night like an eye that wouldn't close.

The knock came again, and this time it was closer—or sounded closer—which made no sense, because there was only one door between the porch and the hallway, and Oscar was standing six feet from it.

Three slow. Two fast. One long.

And then, in the silence after, a voice. Small. A child's voice, or a voice doing its best impression of one, slightly too smooth, like a recording of a child played by someone who had only ever read about children.

*"Oscar."*

He did not answer. Rule seven. *Never answer if someone knocks using your own rhythm.* Ruth's handwriting, blue ink gone pale, taped inside the cabinet above the sink where he saw it every morning of fifteen years and had somehow never once *read*. He had obeyed the rule without knowing it, the way you obey a superstition you've forgotten learning. And now here was the rule arriving to collect on itself, standing on his porch at 3 a.m., wearing his own knock like a stolen coat.

*"Oscar, I'm cold."*

The voice was coming through the door, but it was also coming from everywhere else—from the walls, from the stairwell behind him, from the bathroom where the mirror hung lagging a full second behind the world. Oscar turned slowly, bat up, and looked down the length of the hall toward the bathroom doorway, where the dark was a shade darker than the rest of the dark.

*"Let me in, Oscar. It's me."*

"It's not," Oscar said, and hated how thin his voice came out, how much it sounded like a man negotiating with a dream. "There's nobody there."

*"Look,"* said the voice, and it was warm now, coaxing, familiar in a way that made his stomach drop, because it had found a register he recognized—it sounded like Ruth. Not exactly. Like Ruth remembered by someone who had only heard about her. *"Just look. You always look. You're a looker, Oscar. You look and look and explain. So look."*

Against every rule, against the blue-ink warning and the bat in his hands and the animal screaming in the oldest rooms of his brain, Oscar looked.

He crossed the hall to the bathroom. The door stood half open. The streetlight reached in far enough to silver the edge of the mirror, and the rest of the glass held the room in its gray-green memory: the sink, the towel rail, the window with its flaking paint, the metronome sitting on the shelf where he'd left it after the second night of testing, its brass arm frozen mid-swing like a conductor struck dead.

And in the mirror, standing on the porch that the mirror should not have been able to see—

Himself.

Not a reflection of Oscar standing in the bathroom. There was no version of the angle that could produce it; he checked, madly, stupidly, tilting his head, stepping left, stepping right, and the figure in the glass did not correspond, did not track, did not obey any law of optics that had governed his life until now. It simply stood there, outside the front door, seen from the porch's point of view, rendered in the mirror's dull mercury as clearly as if the glass were a window looking out over his own shoulder.

It was Oscar Ashgrove. Same height, same slouch, same bat hanging loose in one hand. But older. Grayer at the temples in a way the bathroom light exaggerated and the porch light confirmed. Thinner in the face, the way men get thin when they stop cooking for themselves. It wore a cardigan Oscar didn't own—mustard yellow, moth-eaten at the cuffs—and it stood on the porch with the terrible patience of furniture.

As Oscar watched, the figure raised its free hand and knocked on the inside of the glass.

Three slow. Two fast. One long.

The sound arrived a heartbeat later, muffled, from the front door.

"No," Oscar said. "No, no, no." He backed out of the bathroom, and the mirror-Oscar did not follow, did not move at all except to lower its hand, and that was worse—worse than pursuit, worse than a lunging horror-face, this calm duplication, this evidence that somewhere outside his door stood a man with his face who had been waiting long enough to grow old waiting.

The knock came again at the front door. Then the voice, and this time it wasn't Ruth's ghost-register or a child's approximation. This time it was Oscar's own voice, pitched low, tired, reasonable—the voice he used on air between calls, the voice that had talked four thousand insomniacs through their worst nights.

*"Oscar. Open the door. I've been out here a long time. I'm tired of waiting on porches. I just want to come home."*

Oscar stood in the middle of the hallway with the bat shaking in his grip, and the worst part—the part he would not tell anyone, the part that would sit in his chest like a swallowed stone for days—was that some deep, starving chamber of him wanted to open it. Because hadn't he been lonely? Hadn't he built a family out of dead voices on a phone line, out of ghosts who set three places at a table set for two? Wasn't the thing on the porch offering him the one thing no one else had ever offered: company that matched him exactly? A self that understood the silence of this house from the inside?

*That's how it gets in,* said the part of him that had finally, belatedly, started reading Ruth's rules as instructions rather than eccentricities. *Not through fear. Through recognition.*

He thought of Marisol Vane on his doorstep two nights ago, rain in her hair, holding a lawyer's letter like a lantern. *Something of theirs was never returned.* He had turned her away. He had stood in this exact hallway and lied to her with a straight face while the mirrors lagged behind him like witnesses taking notes.

*"Please,"* said his own voice through the door, and now it was fraying, and the fraying sounded real, sounded like grief, sounded like Edie Marsh describing her own funeral in that bright, bewildered voice. *"I don't want to be out here anymore. It's so quiet out here, Oscar. You know how quiet it is. You've always known."*

"I know," Oscar whispered. And then, louder, pressing his back against the wall opposite the door, putting the whole width of the hallway between himself and the handle: "I know how quiet it is. That's why I'm not opening it."

Silence. Long enough that he began to hope. The streetlight bars steadied on the floor. The cold loosened its grip by a degree, maybe two. Somewhere upstairs the metronome—no, the metronome was down here, on the bathroom shelf—but somewhere, something ticked once, evenly, like a heart resuming.

Then, softly, almost kindly, his own voice spoke one last time through the wood:

*"Friday, then. Before she signs. You'll figure it out by Friday—you always do. You're so good at figuring things out, Oscar. It's the doing them you were never any good at."*

And the footsteps receded down the porch steps—slow, unhurried, his own gait, he knew that walk, he'd seen it in shop windows his whole life—and faded into the street, and the house exhaled around him, and the clock downstairs that had stopped at 3:04 ticked forward to 3:05 with a sound like a small door closing.

Oscar slid down the wall until he was sitting on the cold floorboards, bat across his knees, and he laughed once—a short, cracked sound with nothing in it—and put his face in his hands.

When he finally lifted his head, the bathroom mirror was visible down the hall, a pale rectangle in the dark. He made himself look at it. Made himself raise his right hand, slowly, testing.

In the glass, the bathroom stood empty. No figure. No mustard cardigan. Just the sink, the towel rail, the frozen metronome.

But the reflection of his raised hand came down a full second after he lowered his own—as if the mirror were still watching him walk away, still standing on the porch, still keeping his place.

Oscar got up, went to the kitchen, and pulled Ruth's journals off the table. If the thing outside was going to be him, then he had until Friday to find out what Ruth had done in 1974—and what, exactly, she had left in the attic for him to undo.

Under the lamp, on the last page he hadn't dared read, Ruth's handwriting had grown small and hurried, pressed hard into the paper as though she'd been writing against a deadline of her own.

*Return it or become it.*

Outside, far down the street, faint as a radio between stations, someone was whistling his name.
