## 17. The Room Behind the Glass

The mirror in the upstairs hallway had been lagging for eleven days now, and Oscar had learned its schedule the way one learns the moods of a difficult animal: when it was slowest (just after three a.m.), when it caught up (never fully), and where the seam was—that hairline place at the lower left corner where the reflection didn't quite meet the glass, like a door that had been painted shut and then, over decades, begun to open again on its own.

He stood before it at 2:47 in the morning with Ruth's final journal page folded in his breast pocket, over his heart, the way you'd carry a letter from someone who died angry at you.

*Return it or become it.*

The house was quiet in the way it had been quiet all week—not empty-quiet but held-quiet, the silence of a room full of people trying not to breathe. He could feel them behind the walls. Edie Marsh's laugh, low and smoke-roughened, coming from the kitchen where she'd set out three cups of coffee no one drank. Mr. Okafor humming the station's old sign-off jingle in the parlor. The dead callers, his dead listeners, moving through the rooms with the gentle industry of family preparing for something—a homecoming, or a departure, or both.

They wanted him to stay. That was the terrible truth he'd finally let himself see last night, when the mirror showed him an older Oscar, gray at the temples, comfortable in a chair by the window, waving at him with an easy familiarity that made his stomach drop through the floorboards. They would keep him. They would keep him warm and fed and accompanied until he was one of them, until the lag swallowed him whole and he became another voice on the other side of the static asking someone—anyone—to *keep the light on*.

But there was the box. The music box, wrapped in a pillowcase at the bottom of Ruth's cedar chest, holding the original deed and a name no one had spoken aloud in fifty years. And somewhere on the other side of this glass, if Ruth's journals were true, there was a girl who had been waiting since 1974 with her hands empty.

Oscar raised his hand toward the mirror.

His reflection raised its hand—one second later. He watched it happen, watched his own arm move in the glass like a delayed broadcast of himself, and felt the vertigo of it, the sense of standing at the edge of a time zone drawn in silver.

"Okay," he said quietly. "Okay, Ruth. I'm doing it your way. The right way. Fifty years late."

The reflection mouthed the word back at him, late, and then—it did something new. It smiled first.

Not cruelly. Not the way mirrors smile in nightmares. It smiled the way you smile at someone about to come home after a long trip: recognition, relief, *finally*. And then the reflection stepped forward when Oscar hadn't moved, and the seam in the lower left corner widened like an eye opening, and the surface of the mirror went soft as the skin on warm milk.

Oscar Ashgrove, forty-six years old, host of the midnight hour, custodian of a stolen room, put his palm against the glass.

It gave.

* * *

Stepping through was not like falling and not like walking. It was like the moment between songs on the radio—the breath of dead air where anything could be, where the signal hasn't decided what it is yet. For one endless instant Oscar was nowhere, everywhere, a frequency without a body. He heard his own heartbeat played back to him on a delay. He heard, very faintly, a music box winding down.

Then his feet found floorboards, and he was standing in a living room.

Not *his* living room. Not quite. The bones were the same—the same load-bearing wall where the hallway met the parlor, the same window seat under the north-facing window—but everything else was wrong in the way a photograph is wrong when it shows you a place you know wearing clothes from before you were born. The wallpaper was a faded gold fleur-de-lis pattern he'd never seen but somehow recognized, the way you recognize a relative's face in an old picture. A console radio the size of a coffin squatted against the wall, its dial glowing amber. Doilies lay under lamps. A crocheted afghan draped over the sofa arm, mid-slide, frozen in the act of falling.

And the light. God, the light. It came through the windows at the angle of a late September afternoon, honeyed and long, and it did not move. Oscar watched it for a full minute, waiting for the shadows to creep, and they didn't. The sun hung in the sky like a note being held.

"Held breath," he whispered, and his voice sounded strange here, too close, as if the room were inside his own chest. "Ruth said it was like a held breath. She wasn't kidding."

Because that's what this was. He understood it all at once, the way you understand a dream only upon waking. This was 1974. This was the exact moment the Vane family had walked out their front door for the last time—or rather, the moment they had been *supposed* to walk out, the moment that had been lifted out of time along with the room itself, sealed up like a fly in amber. The family had been leaving. The room had refused to let the moment finish.

There were boxes everywhere. Cardboard boxes with rope handles, half-packed, their flaps open like mouths. A stack of newspapers—Oscar tilted his head to read a headline, *September 1974*, and felt the year go through him like cold water. A child's bicycle leaned against the wall, its training wheels still attached, though the girl who'd ridden it looked, in the photographs Marisol had shown him, to be far past training wheels. Someone had packed in a hurry. Someone had packed hoping to come back for the rest.

On the kitchen table—visible through the doorway, the same table, he was almost sure, the same table where Edie set out her three cups—a meal sat untouched. Plates for five. Meatloaf going gray at the edges, mashed potatoes skinned over, a pitcher of lemonade with the ice long since melted and refrozen and melted again, though nothing here ever actually changed. Five plates. Four chairs pushed back from the table as if their occupants had just risen.

The fifth chair still held a cardigan, folded neatly, waiting.

Oscar's throat closed. He knew whose chair it was. He knew without knowing how he knew, the way he'd known Edie Marsh's voice six days after her funeral, the way he'd known the rhythm of the knocking at three a.m. because it was his own.

"Lena," he said.

The name left his mouth and the room *heard* it. He felt it happen—a shiver went through the frozen light, the dust motes hanging in the air trembled like struck piano strings, and somewhere deep in the walls, beneath the plaster and lath, something exhaled. Fifty years of not-being-said, undone in a single syllable.

"She's in here," said a voice behind him—Edie's voice, kind and sad—and Oscar turned, but the room behind him was empty. Of course it was. The dead couldn't cross into the held breath; they could only press their faces to the glass of it, the way he had pressed his face to theirs for fifteen years. "Go on, sweetheart. She's been waiting so long."

* * *

He found her in the window seat.

She was small. That was the first thing, the thing that broke him open before anything else could: she was so *small*, seven years old maybe, eight, wearing a corduroy jumper and white knee socks with one fallen down around her ankle, and she sat with her back very straight and her hands turned up in her lap like two little cups, waiting to be filled.

Empty hands. Marisol had described them from the photograph—the girl clutching an empty spot where a music box should be—and Oscar had imagined it, but imagining was nothing against the reality of those small upturned palms, patient as prayer, holding fifty years of nothing.

She wasn't frozen. That was the second thing. Everything else in the room was stopped mid-motion—the afghan falling, the door swinging, the afternoon sun nailed to the sky—but Lena was alive in the way that a pilot light is alive. She breathed. Her eyes tracked him as he crossed the room, and they were brown and enormous and dry in the terrible way of eyes that have cried themselves out long ago and now simply watch.

"You're not her," she said. Her voice was small and clear and hoarse, a voice that had spoken to itself for half a century. "You're not Mama."

"No," Oscar said. He knelt down in front of her, knees cracking, making himself small, making himself harmless. "No, I'm not. My name is Oscar."

"You're the one who lives in our house." It wasn't an accusation. It was worse than that—it was simple fact, delivered with the flat patience of a child who has had infinite time to accept things. "You have my room. You sleep in my bed." She tilted her head, studying him. "You talk on the radio at night. I can hear you through the walls. You have a nice voice. You sound like the man who used to do the news."

Oscar's eyes burned. "You've been listening? All this time?"

"When the static is thin." She shrugged, a small, weary gesture far too old for her shoulders. "Mostly it's thick. But sometimes, late, when you turn the microphone up—I hear you reading the weather. I like the weather." A pause. Then, with the grave precision of a child stating the most important thing: "It always changes. Nothing else does."

He looked at her—at the dust-free shine of her hair, at the knee sock slowly, slowly sliding down a leg that never grew, at the room around her where meatloaf grayed and lemonade melted and refroze and a bicycle waited forever for a rider who had left in 1974 and never finished leaving—and grief hit him like a wave hitting a seawall, huge and useless.

"We're fixing it," he said. His voice cracked on the second word and he let it. Fifteen years of practiced calm, of smooth on-air reassurance, of rationalizing every anomaly in that house—and here, in front of an eight-year-old girl who had been waiting since before he was born, he let every bit of it crack wide open. "Lena, listen to me. I found the box. Your music box. My aunt took it—in 1974, when your family was moving out. She took it to keep the room, and I'm so sorry, I'm sorry, we didn't know, we didn't understand what it—" He stopped, dragged air into his lungs. "I brought it back. I'm giving it back. Today. Before Friday, before anyone signs anything, before anything else happens. Your family gets to finish leaving. All of you. Together."

Lena looked at him for a long moment. In the held light, her face did something complicated—hope and fear wrestling in a small frame, two animals circling each other.

"If I take it back," she said slowly, "the room lets go."

"Yes."

"And we leave. Mama and Papa and Tío Rafael and Abuela." She counted them off on her fingers, touching each fingertip with her thumb like a rosary. "We finish going."

"Yes."

"And you?" Her enormous brown eyes fixed on him, and there was something in them he hadn't expected—something beyond hope, beyond fear. Something like pity. "Where do you go, radio man?"

Oscar opened his mouth. Closed it. Because he had told himself the answer on the drive of these last weeks, had rehearsed it in the shower and on the air and in the dark: *I give the room back and I walk away and I find some small apartment and I learn to be alone honestly.* But kneeling here in the held breath of 1974, looking at the fifth chair with its waiting cardigan, hearing Edie's voice pressing against the glass of this moment from the outside—*go on, sweetheart*—he knew the truth he'd been carrying like a stone.

He didn't want to go back to the empty side. He had spent fifteen years on the empty side, talking to the dead through a microphone, and the dead had loved him better than the living ever had.

"I don't know," he said honestly, because she deserved honesty more than comfort. "Maybe nowhere. Maybe... wherever people like me go."

Lena considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then she reached out—one small hand leaving its post in her lap for the first time in fifty years—and touched his cheek. Her fingers were cool and real and impossibly light.

"The room likes you," she pronounced. "It kept you safe all this time even though you had my box. It only keeps what it loves." She withdrew her hand and returned it to its cupped waiting, but her chin lifted, and something in her spine straightened further, something gathering. "Bring it here. Bring it to me. I'll hold it, and you tell the room it can let go now. It listens to you. It's been listening to you for years—you just kept talking instead of listening back."

From outside the glass—from the hallway of the house that was and wasn't his—came a sound like a great intake of breath. The light in the windows flickered, the first change in fifty years, and the shadows leaned eastward, one degree, two, the afternoon resuming at last its long fall toward evening.

Somewhere below them, faintly, a car door slammed in 1974. Voices—Mama's voice, calling up the stairs, *Lena, mija, we're waiting, we're going to be late*—a call that had hung unfinished in the air for half a century, finally reaching its addressee.

Lena turned toward the sound. Her whole small body turned toward it like a compass needle finding north after decades of spinning.

"They're here," she whispered. "They're really here. Hurry, radio man. Before the door closes again."

Oscar reached into his coat, fingers closing around the pillowcase-wrapped shape of the music box, and felt it—felt it *answer*, warm as a held hand, ready at last to go home.
