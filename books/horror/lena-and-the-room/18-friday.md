## 18. Friday

Marisol arrived at the Ashgrove house at nine minutes past nine in the morning, and the first thing she noticed was that the door was already open.

Not unlocked. Not ajar. Open, the way a mouth opens before a word, six inches of darkness breathing out at her, carrying a smell she could not place until she did—dust, yes, and old paper, but underneath it something warmer, something like a room that had been sat in, lived in, kept. Like a lamp had been burning in there for a very long time, waiting.

She stood on the porch with her lawyer's leather folder under her arm and her grandmother's photograph in her coat pocket, and she did not go in. Not yet. Her hands were steady; she made a point of noticing that. Her hands had been steady all week, through the probate office, through the notary's raised eyebrow, through the phone call from her mother saying *you don't even know what's in that house, Mari, you don't even know whose house you think that is*—and Marisol had said, calmly, *I know exactly whose it is. It's ours. It's been ours for fifty years and nobody ever came to take it back.*

The dirt under her fingernails from Wednesday's dream was gone, but she could still feel the shape of it, the way you feel a removed splinter.

"Mr. Ashgrove," she called, and her voice went into the house and did not come back. No echo. The hallway ate it. "I'm here for the signing. I brought the documents."

From somewhere deep inside—upstairs, she thought, or under the stairs, it was impossible to tell with old houses—came a sound like a man setting down something heavy and hurrying.

* * *

Oscar had not slept.

He had spent the night in the attic with Ruth's journals spread around him like fallen leaves, reading her last entries until the words swam, until *Return it or become it* had stopped looking like handwriting and started looking like a verdict. He had taken the music box down from the shelf above the linen closet at four in the morning, and the moment his fingers closed around it the mirrors in the hallway had all shivered at once, a ripple running through the glass like wind over water, and from behind every one of them had come a sound he had spent fifteen years refusing to name: relief.

They knew. The household knew what today was. He had come downstairs at dawn and found the kitchen table set for three—no. Set for two, and then, as he watched, a third place assembling itself out of nothing: a plate sliding an inch, a fork turning, a napkin unfolding with the crisp precision of a hand that had done it ten thousand times. Edie Marsh's hand. He could almost see her, if he unfocused his eyes the way you do with those pictures of sailboats hidden in landscapes.

"Don't," he said softly, to the kitchen, to all of them. "Don't make this harder than it has to be."

The kettle began to boil on a cold burner.

The knock, when it came, was not his rhythm. It was a stranger's knock, brisk and official, and Oscar stood in the hallway with the music box in his coat pocket, feeling its weight the way you feel a heart that isn't yours, and he understood that the last hour of his life as a living man in a living house had just begun.

He opened the door. Marisol Vane stood on the porch with the morning behind her, and she looked, he thought, exactly like someone arriving at a funeral they had spent their whole life dreading and could not stay away from.

"You're open," she said. "The door was open."

"The house does that," Oscar said. "It's been doing a lot of things lately." He stepped back to let her in, and the hallway light flickered once, like a held breath being taken.

* * *

The signing was to happen at the kitchen table, because Marisol insisted. "Where they lived it," she said, laying out the documents with a notary's care. "Where my grandmother grew up. I'm not doing this in a lawyer's office like it's a car sale."

Oscar sat across from her with his hands flat on the wood, and under his palms the table was warm. It should not have been warm. The kitchen had been cold all week; the whole house had been cooling as Friday approached, drawing inward, the way a body draws heat to its center when the extremities are dying. But the table was warm, and the third place setting was still there, and Marisol kept glancing at it and then away, and finally she said, "Are you expecting someone?"

"Everyone," Oscar said. "Eventually. It's a long story."

"I've read the story." She tapped the folder. "Fifty years of it. My grandmother's family walked out of this house in the summer of '74 carrying everything they owned, and one thing they owned never made it out, and whatever it was, it's still here, and it's keeping—" she stopped, and he watched her choose her words the way you choose footholds on a cliff, "—keeping something here that should have left with them."

"A girl," Oscar said.

Marisol's pen stopped moving. "You know."

"Lena," Oscar said, and the name went through the house like a bell through water. Every mirror in the kitchen—there were two, the big one over the sideboard and the small one by the door—fogged at the same instant, and cleared.

Marisol stared at him. "My grandmother never said her name. Not once. Not in sixty years. She'd go quiet at birthdays. She'd leave the room when music boxes played." Her voice was very controlled and very thin, a wire holding weight. "How do you know her name?"

"Because she's been standing in my mirrors," Oscar said, "waiting a second behind everything, since before I knew to look. Because my aunt took something that wasn't hers in 1974 and spent the rest of her life writing about it in a journal she hid in the attic, and the last thing she ever wrote was *Return it or become it*." He reached into his coat, slowly, the way you move around a sleeping animal, and set the music box on the table between the documents and the third place setting.

It was small. Rosewood, brass fittings gone green, a keyhole worn silver by a child's fingers. It looked like nothing. It looked like the least important object in the world, and the whole house leaned toward it.

Marisol did not touch it. Her hand rose halfway and stopped, hovering, and Oscar saw her grandmother in the gesture—saw sixty years of learned avoidance living in a woman who had never met the reason for it. "That's it," Marisol whispered. "That's what she was holding in the photograph. The empty spot."

"I have to give it back to her," Oscar said. "Before you sign. That's the whole— that's everything. If you sign while it's still here, the house—" He stopped, because he did not actually know what the house would do, only what Ruth had written, and what the mirrors showed, and what the dead callers had begged him for in a chorus of static: *open the box before Friday, keep the light on, hold the door.*

"If I sign while it's still here," Marisol said, "the house what, Mr. Ashgrove?"

The kitchen answered for him.

* * *

It began at the edges.

The wallpaper at the far corner of the room puckered inward, gently, the way a cheek puckers before a kiss—or before a swallow. The doorway to the hall narrowed by a hand's width, so smoothly that for a moment Oscar's eye refused to register it, insisting the doorframe had always been that snug, that cozy, that *close*. The window glass thickened. The light coming through it went from morning-white to the amber of a room remembering lamplight.

Marisol stood up so fast her chair went over backward. "What is that. What is *that*—"

"The house is closing," Oscar said. He was calm. He was surprised at how calm he was, and then he understood: the calm wasn't his. It was being lent to him, poured into him like warmth into a cold room, by every dead listener who had ever asked him to keep the light on. *We have you,* the calm said. *We've always had you. Now move.*

"Marisol. The papers. Pick them up. Do not let them touch the table."

She grabbed the folder, clutching it to her chest like a child's schoolwork in a rainstorm, and the table beneath where the documents had lain was no longer wood. It was smooth and pale and slightly yielding, and when she snatched her hand back it bore the faint print of the tablecloth's pattern, pressed into her skin like a bruise from the inside.

The doorway was narrower. The window was smaller. The ceiling had come down—not dramatically, not with groaning and cracking, but with the patience of a throat, of a swallow, of something that had waited fifty years and could wait another fifty minutes if it had to.

"Give me the box," Marisol said. Her voice had gone high and hard. "Give it to me and I'll carry it out and this ends—"

"It doesn't end with you carrying it." Oscar was already moving, shrugging into the weight of the decision he had made in the attic at dawn. "It ends when *she* takes it. Her hands. Her name. The deed is inside the box, Marisol—your family's name is inside the box, the real one, the one that was erased—and it has to be *given*, freely, to the person it was taken from. That's the whole law of this place. That's the only law."

"Then where is she?" Marisol's eyes were wet and furious. "Where is my—where is *Lena*?"

Oscar turned to the mirror over the sideboard.

The glass had gone dark and deep, deeper than glass, and in it the frozen living room of 1974 hung like a wasp in amber: the half-packed boxes, the coats on the hooks, the door standing open onto a summer that had never finished happening. And in the center of it, small and still, a girl with her hands open at her sides, waiting, waiting, waiting, her mouth shaping a name that was not her own.

Oscar's name.

"She's in the room behind the glass," Oscar said. "And I'm going to go in and give it to her. And you—" he took Marisol's shoulder, and she was shaking, and he held on, "—you are going to call a number, and you are going to ask for the dead to hold the door."

"The *what*?"

"The radio station." He was already backing toward the mirror, and the mirror was already coming toward him, or the room was, the distinction had stopped mattering. "KRVL, 1310. Ask for the overnight line. Tell them Oscar Ashgrove is calling in one last time, and he needs every caller he's ever had to hold the door open. They'll know. They've been waiting for someone to ask." He managed a smile, and it was a terrible, wonderful, frightened thing. "I'm on the air in twenty minutes anyway. I never miss a Friday."

* * *

The mirror took him the way water takes a stone: without ceremony, without sound, the surface closing over his shoulders and knitting itself smooth, and Marisol Vane stood alone in a shrinking kitchen holding a leather folder and screaming at a house that had stopped pretending to be a house.

* * *

Oscar came through on the other side walking, because that was the rule of the room behind the glass—you arrived mid-step, mid-breath, mid-life, exactly as you'd been interrupted. The frozen summer of 1974 pressed against him from all sides: dust motes hanging like suspended constellations, a clock on the mantel stopped at 3:14, the smell of packing paper and cut grass and a lemon cake half-eaten on the counter. And everywhere, the held-breath quality of it, the sense of a family paused between one room and the next, between one life and the next, for fifty years.

Lena stood where she always stood, in the center, in the light.

She was smaller up close. Eight years old, maybe nine, in a sundress gone gray with waiting, her dark hair half-escaped from its braid. Her hands were open at her sides. They had been open for half a century, and Oscar thought: *that is the most terrible thing I have ever seen, and I have taken calls from the dead for six years and called it a radio show.*

"Hi, Lena," he said, and his voice worked, barely. "My name is Oscar. I'm sorry it took so long. My aunt Ruth—" his throat closed. He knelt, so they were eye to eye, and took the music box from his coat, and the weight of it had doubled, tripled, as if it had been drinking the house on the way down, as if it knew. "My aunt Ruth took this. She thought she was keeping the room safe. She was wrong, and she knew she was wrong, and she was too much of a coward to fix it, and then she died and left it to me, and I was a coward too, for fifteen years, because this house was the only family I had." He held the box out. "I'd like to give it back now. If you'll take it."

Lena looked at the box. Then she looked past him—at the mirror, at the shrinking throat of the kitchen on the other side, at the woman with the folder—and her open hands finally, finally closed, reaching.

And the whole room exhaled.

* * *

Marisol's fingers were dialing before her mind caught up—KRVL, 1310, the number she had found scrawled in her grandmother's album margin next to a photograph of the moving truck, *the night man, if anything ever—* and the line picked up on the first ring, which no radio station line had ever done.

"KRVL, overnight," said a voice made of static and patience.

"I need—" The doorway was a slit now. The window was a coin of light. The kitchen was a throat closing around a swallowed name. "I need the dead to hold the door. Oscar Ashgrove is calling in one last time. He needs every caller he's ever had to *hold the door*—"

The static on the line changed. It gathered. It became, unmistakably, a room full of people leaning toward a microphone, and then it became a sound Marisol felt in her sternum, in her teeth, in the dirt-memory under her fingernails: hundreds of voices, all the callers, all the dead, saying in unison something she couldn't parse—*we have him, we have him, we have him*—and through the last coin of window light she saw the walls stop.

Hold. Trembling, straining, like a jaw held open by fingers.

On the line, one voice separated from the chorus, warm and dry and familiar from a hundred sleepless nights of weather reports and dedications: *"This is Oscar Ashgrove, coming to you live from the other side. I'm going to need you all to do me one last favor."*

The mirror, in the dying kitchen, began to glow.

And Marisol's pen—the pen she had brought for the signing, the pen her grandmother had once used to sign her own name into a false deed's shadow—slipped from her fingers and rolled across a floor that was no longer a floor, and somewhere beyond the closing dark she heard the small, bright, unmistakable sound of a music box lid opening for the first time in fifty years.
