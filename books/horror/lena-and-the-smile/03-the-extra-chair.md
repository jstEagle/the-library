## 3. The Extra Chair

I want to say, for the record, that I am a careful man. A man of footnotes. A man who backs up his field recordings twice, once to a drive and once to a cloud that costs me eleven dollars a month and has never once been thanked. I date my notebooks. I label my cables. When I was nine years old I alphabetized my grandmother's spice cabinet and she said, "Lena, you are going to make some very tired children someday," and she was right about the tiredness if not the children.

So when I sat down on the seventh night to organize my photographs from the first week in Kessho, I did what I always do. I imported them. I renamed them. I sorted them by date, by location tag, by the time-stamped order in which I had pressed the shutter. A tidy little archive of a tidy little week. The house at dawn. The house at dusk. The kitchen table with its morning light. The kitchen table with its evening light. The kitchen table, because apparently I had been photographing the kitchen table a great deal, which in hindsight should have told me something. The camera notices what the eye refuses to.

The first anomaly I dismissed in under four seconds, which is the record for self-deception in the Nakamura family, previously held by my grandfather, though I didn't know that yet.

It was the photo from Monday morning. I'd taken it to document the light — the way it came through the east window and laid itself across the table like something poured. Four chairs. I remembered four chairs, because I remembered counting them on my second day, standing in the kitchen with my tea going cold, thinking: four. Family of four, growing up. Grandma, Grandpa, Mom, and the aunt who left before I was born. Four chairs, one for each, and the table worn pale in four places where four elbows had rested for fifty years.

In Monday's photograph there were five.

I zoomed in. The fifth chair sat at the far end of the table, near the wall, half in shadow, a plain wooden thing with a straight back — not one of the mismatched survivors around my grandmother's table but something simpler, older, the kind of chair that gets made rather than bought. It didn't look photoshopped in. It looked like it had been there all along and the other four had simply been polite about it.

I did what any rational folklorist does with an impossible image. I checked my other cameras. I have a backup body, a battered Fuji I take into the field because it survives rain and my own clumsiness. I'd taken maybe a dozen shots on it that same morning. Five chairs. Every one.

Then I did the thing I am slightly ashamed of. I walked downstairs, at eleven at night, in a house I had lived in for exactly seven days, and counted the chairs in my grandmother's kitchen.

Four.

I stood there in the dark with my phone's flashlight making the room look like a crime scene, counting four chairs, four worn elbow-places, four of everything, and I felt the specific relief of a man whose rational world has been restored to him. The camera, then. The camera was wrong. Some rolling shutter artifact, some double-exposure ghost, some — I don't know, the sensor was old, the light was strange, mountain light does things to sensors that city light doesn't.

I went back upstairs and I told myself a story about it, and the story was good, and I slept.

* * *

The trouble with being a careful man is that you build systems, and systems have a way of testifying against you.

By Wednesday I had a spreadsheet. I am not proud of the spreadsheet. The spreadsheet had columns: date, time, camera, file name, chair count, chair position. The last column was the one I added on Thursday, after I noticed that the extra chair was not only present in every image — it had begun to move.

Monday: far end of the table, against the wall, shadowed.

Tuesday: still at the far end, but pulled out from the wall by what I estimated — and I did estimate, I measured it against the table's known length, because of course I did — by eight centimeters.

Wednesday: twelve centimeters further. Now clearly visible in full light. Now, in one shot, catching a gleam on its backrest where the window's light struck it, the way wood gleams when it has been polished by hands.

I sat with my laptop in my grandmother's upstairs study, surrounded by her moth-eaten curtains and her collection of regional cassette tapes labeled in her cramped hand, and I watched a chair approach the head of a table one photograph at a time, and I thought: this is what it must be like to watch a tide come in if you are a sandcastle.

Because here is the thing I understood, sitting there, before I understood anything else. The chair wasn't moving toward the table. The table had a head — the seat where my grandfather had sat for forty years, the seat with the view of the door, the seat you sit in when you are the one who decides. The chair was moving toward the head. Toward the seat of the one who decides.

And the seat of the one who decides was empty, and had been empty for two years, and I had been sitting in it all week without thinking about it, because it was the chair nearest the kettle and I am a creature of convenience.

I got up and I moved to the other end of the table for dinner. I am not saying that helped. I am saying I did it, and I noted it in my notebook like a good little scientist: *Subject alters behavior in response to photographic evidence. Subject chooses seat farther from door.* The notebook did not record that my hands shook while I ate, because notebooks are brave and their keepers are not, always.

* * *

Friday it rained, and rain in Kessho is not weather so much as an argument the mountain is having with itself. I stayed in. I went through the rest of the week's images, and then, because avoidance has never once worked on me, I went through the older photographs — the ones I'd brought with me, scanned family prints, the digital archaeology of a family that had mostly scattered.

That's when I found the portrait.

It hangs — hung — on the stairwell wall, my grandmother at perhaps sixty, seated, hands folded, wearing the dark kimono she wore for funerals and photographs and, I suspect, for anything that required her to be more grandmother than woman. I'd walked past it forty times that week. I'd photographed it on day one as part of my "documentation of the house" pass, a dutiful sweep of rooms and walls that I always do on arrival, the way you'd photograph a rental car before driving it.

In the portrait she is looking slightly off-camera. That's how I remembered it, anyway. Slightly off-camera, gazing toward the kitchen, toward the sound of the kettle, toward the ordinary middle distance of a woman in her own house.

In the scan on my laptop, she was looking at me.

Not at the camera. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. The camera had been positioned to her left, at eye level, three or four meters back. In the original print — I went downstairs in the rain to check, I stood in the stairwell with water dripping off my jacket and compared the print to the scan on my phone — in the print, her eyes are aimed a degree or two off the lens, at nothing, at the photographer's shoulder. In the scan, they are aimed at the lens. At the viewer. At me, sitting in her study with her curtains going gray around me.

I checked the print again. Off to the side. I checked the scan. Direct.

The photograph had changed. Or the photograph had always been this way and I had changed — some perceptual gate in me opening, the way you can't hear the second voice on a recording until you've heard it once, and then you can never not hear it. I thought about the lullaby. I had not listened to that recording since the first night. I had told myself it was because I was busy. I opened the folder where it lived. I hovered over the file.

I did not play it. I closed the laptop instead, and the study went dark except for the rain-light, and I sat in my great-grandfather's chair and listened to the house.

Houses make noise. I have a catalog. Beams swell and contract; the house talks to itself in the temperature changes of evening, a language of ticks and groans that I learned as a child and never unlearned, the way you never unlearn a mother tongue. Pipes knock. Wind finds the loose shingle on the north slope and plays it like a bad reed. The floorboard at the top of the stairs sighs under no weight at all, has sighed under no weight at all for as long as I can remember, and as a child I had named the sound — called it "the old man walking," and Grandma had smiled and said, "Yes, the house is old, it has old bones," and I had accepted this, because children accept explanations the way the ground accepts rain.

The catalog was still there. Settling beams. Wind. Pipes. The old man walking.

But now, sitting in the dark, I noticed what the catalog had always smoothed over, the way a familiar voice smooths over the words it's saying. The noises had a direction. The beams settled from the kitchen outward. The pipes knocked in a rhythm that, if you were generous, if you were the kind of person who heard songs in everything because songs were your trade — the pipes knocked in fours. One, two, three, rest. One, two, three, rest. Like footsteps. Like something walking a circuit of the table, checking each place, and finding one empty.

I did not sleep much that night. When I did sleep, I dreamed of the kitchen table seen from above, from the ceiling, the way a camera sees, and the four chairs were arranged around it and the fifth was not at the table at all. The fifth was standing at the doorway. And in the dream I understood — with the dream's terrible fluency — that it was not coming to the table. It was waiting to be invited to the table, because it was polite, because it was old-fashioned, because it was my grandmother's generation down to its bones, and things of that generation do not sit until they are asked, and they do not leave until they are seen.

* * *

Saturday morning I did the thing I had been not-doing all week. I took a photograph of the kitchen table.

Not for the archive. Not for documentation. I took it the way you knock on a door. I stood in the kitchen with the morning light coming in and the four chairs sitting there in their four worn places, and I raised the camera, and my hands were steady in the way that hands are steady when they've stopped asking permission, and I pressed the shutter.

I looked at the screen.

Five chairs.

The fifth was at the table now. Not near the wall, not at the far end, not approaching. It was at the table, on the grandfather's side, one seat down from the head. Close enough that if the table had been set, its occupant could have reached the salt.

And it was turned slightly. Not square to the table like the others. Turned outward, angled toward the doorway, toward the part of the room where a person entering would stand. The way you turn a chair when you've just noticed someone come in. The way you turn a chair to say: *there you are. Sit down. We've been waiting.*

I stood in my grandmother's kitchen holding a camera that was telling the truth, and I finally let myself say the sentence I had been refusing all week, the sentence that the spreadsheet and the scans and the pipes had been building toward syllable by syllable like a verse that couldn't be stopped once begun:

Something is coming to dinner.

Not a ghost. I want to be clear that my mind did not leap to ghosts, because I am a folklorist and ghosts are, professionally speaking, the lazy answer. What my mind leapt to was the archive. Twenty years of fieldwork. Every village I'd ever recorded in had its thing — the thing the songs went around, the way water goes around a stone. In Iwakura it was a well you didn't draw from after dark. In the coastal villages it was a name you didn't say at sea. And in Kessho — I had grown up here and I had never once heard the thing the songs went around, and that absence, I realized, standing there with the camera heavy in my hands, that absence was itself the loudest thing in the village. You don't silence a song unless the song is true.

The chair had been coming all week, one photograph at a time, eight centimeters, twelve, a seat, and it had not once been in a photograph of any other room. Not the study, not the stairwell, not the porch. Only the kitchen. Only the table. Whatever it was, it was not haunting the house.

It was setting the table.

I put the camera down on the counter, and as I did, my eye caught the stairwell through the doorway — the portrait, small and dark at this distance, Grandma in her funeral kimono with her hands folded. And I could not swear to it, with the distance and the rain-light and everything else, but I would testify to it under oath if it came to that:

Her head was turned. Just slightly. Toward the kitchen.

Watching me watch the table. The way you watch from the head of a table when the last guest is finally, finally arriving — with that particular expression I knew from her face, from every photograph of her face, the one the village had called her smile and I had called, all my childhood, without knowing why, without having the words for it:

*the smile that waits.*

I went upstairs. I opened my notebook. And on the first clean page, under the date, I wrote the only honest sentence of the week:

*There are four chairs in the kitchen. There have always been five.*

Then, because the phone was in my hand anyway, because the dread had finally burned through the professionalism and left something rawer underneath — I called my sister.

It rang four times. Five. And on the sixth ring, just before voicemail, Mika picked up, and before I could say a word she said, in a voice I had never heard her use, flat and careful, the voice of someone reading from a letter:

"Lena. Why did Grandma write to me?"
