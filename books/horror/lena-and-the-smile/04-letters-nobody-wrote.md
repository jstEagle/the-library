## 4. Letters Nobody Wrote

### Lena

The morning Mika arrived, I was cataloguing the house's vocabulary.

That sounds precious, I know. But after a week of fieldwork you start to think of a house as an instrument — every creak a note, every rattle a rest. The beam over the kitchen doorway spoke in E-flat when the temperature dropped. The pipes behind the bathroom wall answered the wind in a kind of call-and-response, like two old men arguing about weather. I had recordings of all of it. I had *explanations* for all of it. That was the trick I'd learned at eight years old, lying awake while Grandma's house groaned around me: name the sound and it becomes furniture. Unnamed, it becomes company.

I was labeling files on my laptop — *beam_E-flat_0430.wav*, *pipes_wind_response_0515.wav* — when the gravel crunched under tires outside.

Nobody drives to Kessho without calling first. There's no signal worth mentioning, but there's a landline, and my sister knows I answer it the way other people answer fire alarms. So when I opened the front door and saw Mika standing in the yard with one suitcase and no rental car keys in her hand — she'd taken the bus up from Nagano, which meant four transfers and a walk — my first thought wasn't *hello*. My first thought was: *someone told her to come.*

She looked smaller than I remembered, which is a stupid thing to say about a thirty-four-year-old woman who once made a regional sales director cry with a spreadsheet. But grief had been sanding her down for two years, and the mountain air finished the job. She stood there in her city coat, city shoes ruined by the road dust, holding something in both hands like it might bolt.

"You didn't call," I said.

"The phone's dead," she said. "Yours. I tried the archive office too. They said you'd gone dark." She held up what she'd been carrying. An envelope, soft with handling, the paper gone the color of weak tea. "So I came."

"Come in before the mountain decides it owns you," I said, which is Grandma's line, delivered in Grandma's cadence, and we both flinched a little at hearing it come out of my mouth.

Inside, she did the tour without asking for it — the hallway, the kitchen, the table. She stopped at the table. Counted. I watched her lips move slightly. Four chairs, plus the fifth, the one that had been living at the edge of my peripheral vision all week like a debt collector waiting politely by the door.

"When did we get five chairs?" she asked lightly.

"We always had five," I said. "Storage. Spares."

"Lena." She set the envelope on the table, dead center, equidistant from everything. "We were four. You, me, Theo, Grandma. I used to count us at dinner because Theo ate so fast it felt like he might leave early even when he wasn't going anywhere."

I didn't have an explanation ready. That was new. For a week I'd been assembling explanations the way Grandma assembled bento — everything in its compartment, nothing touching. Settling beams. Wind. Pipes. A chair bought at some point and forgotten, because houses accumulate objects the way rivers accumulate stones. But Mika was looking at me with the particular patience of someone who has already done the math, and I found my compartments wouldn't close.

"Show me the letter," I said instead.

### Mika

Here's what nobody tells you about receiving mail from the dead: your hands know before your brain does.

I'd gotten the letter on a Tuesday, in my building's actual mailbox, which required an actual stamp, which was the first wrong thing. Grandma died two years ago — pneumonia, in a hospital three hours from this house, surrounded by machines that beeped in rhythms no folklorist would ever mistake for music. She had not, in her final years, written letters. She called. Loudly. On a phone with a handset heavy enough to use as a weapon, because touchscreens offended her the way automatic doors offend cats.

The handwriting was hers, though. That was the second wrong thing, and the bigger one. I know her handwriting the way you know a scar. The hard downstrokes. The way she crossed her *t*'s like she was disciplining them. I sat on my apartment floor with the envelope for forty minutes before I opened it, and I want to be honest here: part of that was fear, and part of it was that some small, shameful part of me wanted to stay in the moment where it might still be real. Once you open a letter from a dead woman, either she's alive or you're crazy, and neither option comes with instructions.

The letter said:

*Mika. Come home. The house needs counting, and you were always the one who counted. Don't tell your brother yet. He'll understand last.*

It was dated eleven days ago.

I did what any reasonable person does. I called the archive office where Lena works. Voicemail. I called his cell. Nothing — though his voicemail box, when I finally got through to it days later, was full, which is very him; the man archives everything including missed calls. I called Theo. Theo said, and I quote, "If Grandma's writing letters, ask her for money, because I'm broke." Then he laughed, then he stopped laughing, then he hung up fast, which is how Theo says *this scares me*.

Then I got on the bus, because the letter said *come home*, and here is the third wrong thing, the one I haven't told anyone: I wasn't surprised. Not really. Some part of me had been waiting for that letter since the funeral. At the funeral I'd felt watched — not mourned, not missed, *watched* — the way you feel eyes on the back of your neck in an empty room. I'd told myself it was grief. Grief explains a lot of things. It's the settling beam of emotions. Name it and it becomes furniture.

Except now I was standing in Grandma's kitchen, watching my brother not-answer a question about chairs, and the furniture was moving.

"Read it again," Lena said.

I read it aloud. He recorded it. Of course he recorded it — he records everything now, the whole house, a running audio diary of beams and wind and, apparently, correspondence from beyond. I watched him hold the microphone near the paper like the ink itself might hum.

"Dated eleven days ago," he murmured. "Postmarked Nagano. Handwriting?"

"It's hers."

"I believe you." He turned the envelope over. "But I want to check it against the cards she sent us. I kept them all."

"You keep everything."

"Somebody has to," he said, "or it gets rewritten."

There was something underneath that sentence. I let it go, because the fourth wrong thing happened right then: the kettle started boiling on its own. Not whistling — I mean the burner clicked, ignited, and the water began to heat, and neither of us had touched the stove. Lena looked at it. I looked at it. The kettle went on being reasonable and domestic and utterly impossible.

"Pilot light," Lena said.

"This stove is electric," I said. "I unplugged it when I came in. Habit. Grandma was paranoid about fires."

We stood there while the kettle boiled on an unplugged stove, and I swear to you the most frightening part was how quickly we both decided to accept tea from it. That's the thing about this house. It doesn't threaten you. It *hosts* you. And hospitality is very hard to refuse.

### Lena

Over tea that neither of us mentioned making, Mika filled me in on Theo — broke, between jobs, sleeping on a friend's couch in Osaka and pretending it was a lifestyle choice. She'd called him from the bus transfer in Ueda. He'd been strange on the phone. Stranger than usual.

"He asked if the house still had the sealed room," she said.

I put my cup down. "Grandma's sewing room? The one he wasn't allowed in?"

"He asked if it was still locked. Then he said, 'Never mind, forget it,' and hung up." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "Lena, why would Theo think about that room? We were kids. Grandpa died before I was born. Nobody ever talked about that room except as a rule, like 'don't swim after eating.' Rules you follow without knowing why."

I thought about the week I'd spent explaining this house to myself. Beams. Wind. Pipes. I thought about the fifth chair, which I had photographed twice without meaning to, and which had appeared in both photographs at slightly different distances from the head of the table, and which I had not yet shown anyone, because showing someone makes it shared, and shared things demand decisions.

And I thought about the letter. *Don't tell your brother yet. He'll understand last.* Last of what?

"Mika," I said carefully, "did the letter say anything else? Anything you left out?"

She hesitated. My sister lies beautifully and always has — corporate training — but she can't hold a lie over tea in Grandma's kitchen, where honesty soaked into the walls along with sixty years of miso steam.

"There was a postscript," she admitted. "On the back. I thought it was a stain until I got it under a lamp on the bus."

"Read it."

She recited it from memory, eyes closed, the way you recite something you've read too many times: "*Count the chairs. When they stop coming closer, it will be sitting down.*"

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere upstairs — the sealed side, the grandfather's side of the house — a floorboard settled. E-flat. Just the house talking. Just furniture.

Neither of us wrote it down. Neither of us needed to.

### Mika

I slept in my old room, the small one facing the valley, and I did not sleep well, and I want to be precise about why, because precision is the only weapon I've ever trusted.

At around three in the morning I heard the kitchen table.

Not a sound *from* the kitchen — I know what carrying sounds are, I've moved enough furniture. This was the table itself, or something at the table: the slow drag of wood across wood, a few centimeters, then silence, then again. Patient. Rhythmic. Like someone arranging a place setting by feel in the dark, checking the distance, adjusting.

I lay in bed and ran through the list. Drafts. Thermal expansion. A raccoon dog, somehow, indoors, dragging its dinner. I got to maybe seven explanations before I noticed I was whispering them out loud, the way Lena used to as a kid, and that the whispering wasn't calming me down, it was just keeping time with the scraping. Like a call-and-response. Like the pipes.

I got up. I'm not brave; I just couldn't stand being the person who stayed in bed.

The hallway was cold in the specific way hallways get when a door somewhere is open that shouldn't be. The kitchen was empty. Four chairs, tucked in. And the fifth — the spare, the storage, the whatever-it-was — stood at the corner of the table nearest the hallway, angled outward, as if someone had just risen from it. Or was about to sit.

On the table, dead center, lay a fresh sheet of paper. Grandma's handwriting, the disciplined downstrokes, the punished t's:

*Theo arrives tomorrow. Set another place.*

I did not sleep again. In the morning I showed Lena, and he photographed the note with shaking hands, and then he played me a recording he'd made overnight — he leaves the recorder running now, he confessed, he's been leaving it running for days — and on the recording, underneath the beams and the wind and the pipes, there was humming.

Low. Patient. Almost a lullaby.

"That's the song Auntie Sato wouldn't finish," I said.

Lena nodded slowly. "It's the verse the elders stopped mid-line at the festival. The one about the guest at the table." He looked toward the kitchen, toward the fifth chair, toward the note drying in the morning light. "Mika, whoever wrote that letter knew Theo was coming before Theo knew. And I checked the handwriting against Grandma's greeting cards last night."

"And?"

He turned his laptop toward me. Two images side by side. They matched perfectly — every stroke, every angle, every disciplined, punishing crossbar.

"They're identical," he said. "Which means either our grandmother taught someone to write exactly like her—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Or she's still writing. And I don't know which possibility I'm less equipped to record."

Outside, far down the valley road, a bus ground its way up toward Kessho, hours ahead of schedule, carrying exactly one passenger.

Theo had texted Mika from a rest stop an hour ago: *Got a job offer. Weird details. Coming to see you guys first.*

The address on the offer, he said, was this house.
