## 6. What the Village Won't Sing Twice

The community hall of Kessho smells like every community hall I've ever been in: old varnish, cold tea, and the particular staleness of a building that is used often but loved rarely. It sits at the crooked heart of the village, a low wooden structure with a roof that sags like a tired shoulder, and on the evening of my ninth day in Kessho it held perhaps thirty people, most of them older than the building itself.

I had been invited — or rather, I had invited myself with sufficient politeness that refusal would have required effort. Auntie Sato made the introduction for me, gripping my elbow at the door and announcing to the room, "This is Chiyo's grandson. The one who records songs." As if I were a device rather than a man. Several elders nodded at me the way you nod at a tape recorder: acknowledging its presence without expecting conversation.

I set up in the back corner, microphone small and unobtrusive, and for two hours I was happier than I'd been since arriving.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fieldwork: most of it is waiting through things that are not songs. Speeches. Announcements about drainage. A twenty-minute debate over whether the autumn festival should feature pickled plums or pickled radishes, conducted with a ferocity that suggested someone had died over this question before, possibly recently. I recorded all of it anyway. You learn early that the boring parts are where people stop performing, and the unperformed voice is the one worth keeping.

Then they began to sing.

Festival songs in mountain villages are not performances so much as weather. They happen because they have always happened, in the same order, at the same points in the calendar, and no one remembers who decided any of it. An old man named Goro — I learned his name later, from Sato — led the first one, a planting song with a rhythm like water being poured. His voice was astonishing. Not beautiful; astonishing. It had a grain to it, a texture like rope pulled taut, and when he hit the low phrases the windows buzzed faintly in their frames.

I watched the room while he sang. That's the other thing fieldwork teaches you: watch the listeners, not the singer. The singer gives you the song. The listeners give you everything else.

And the listeners were doing something strange. Every time Goro approached the end of a verse, the whole room leaned slightly forward — not toward him, but *with* him, the way passengers lean into a turn. And at the end of each verse there was a pause, a held breath of maybe two seconds, before anyone moved or spoke or poured more tea. Two seconds is nothing. Two seconds is also an eternity if you know what you're looking at. These people were leaving space for something. A call that never came, or came once, long ago, and was never answered again.

I noted the timestamp. Old habit. New paranoia.

* * *

They sang four songs in the first hour. A children's counting song performed by exactly zero children, which is its own kind of elegy. A wedding song so mournful I checked my notes twice to confirm I'd translated the title correctly. A harvest round that three women sang in interlocking lines, weaving past each other like birds, and this one was genuinely lovely, and I let myself forget, for almost four minutes, about chairs and photographs and the way my grandmother's portrait had begun to feel less like a portrait and more like a window.

Then Goro stood up again, and said, "One more," and the temperature of the room changed.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. Nothing visible happened. No one stood, no one objected, no faces did anything dramatic. But the quality of attention shifted — the way a forest goes quiet before rain, or the way a house goes quiet when someone bad-tempered comes home. Thirty elderly people became very interested in their tea.

Goro began a song I hadn't heard yet. Slower. The melody wandered in a way the others hadn't, circling back on itself, and the words were archaic enough that I caught only fragments as I scrambled to follow: something about the mountain road, something about setting a place, something about —

"The guest at the table," Goro sang, "waits with his hands —"

He got no further than that.

Three voices came down on his line at once, overlapping, loud, deliberately clumsy — the musical equivalent of a hand clapped over a mouth. One of the women launched into the harvest round again, mid-verse, out of nowhere, and the others seized it like drowning men seizing a rope, and within ten seconds the room was singing loudly and badly about rice, and Goro sat down with an expression I will be thinking about for the rest of my life.

He wasn't embarrassed. That's what struck me. He wasn't angry, either, or chastened, or any of the things a man might reasonably be after being publicly silenced. He looked *relieved*. He lowered himself onto the bench like a man setting down something heavy, and he rubbed his hands together, and he stared at the floorboards, and I swear to you — I have the recording, I've played it forty times — I swear the whole room exhaled at once.

Except me. I had stopped breathing somewhere around "the guest at the table."

* * *

Afterward, tea was pressed on me by a woman with a face like a walnut shell, who asked cheerful questions about Tokyo and answered none of mine about the songs. I had turned off my recorder during the interruption — reflexive courtesy, drilled into me by a decade of ethics boards — and I could have bitten my own hands off. But the recorder's little level meter has a memory buffer, and when I got home that night, hands shaking slightly in a way I told myself was the cold, I found that the machine had kept the last several seconds in its cache.

Four seconds of audio. That's what survived. Four seconds:

*"The guest at the table waits with his hands—"* and then the collision of voices, and underneath it, barely, Goro's line continuing for half a beat before he surrendered — *"open, and his chair pulled near—"*

His chair pulled near.

I sat in my grandmother's kitchen at one in the morning with headphones on, listening to four seconds of a dead tradition trying to stay buried, and I thought about the chair in my photographs. The chair that had been at the far wall the day I arrived, and by the table's corner by day four, and — I hadn't checked today. I realized I hadn't checked today, and that I didn't want to, and that not wanting to check was itself a kind of data.

Because here's the thing I couldn't stop turning over: silences like the one in that hall don't form around nothing. You don't get thirty people trained, reflexively, generationally, to smother a single verse unless the verse cost them something once. Songs don't get forbidden because they're wrong. They get forbidden because they're *accurate*.

* * *

I went back the next afternoon, ostensibly to return the tea things. Actually to find Goro.

He lived in a narrow house behind the shrine steps, in a room that smelled of cedar and kerosene, surrounded by the possessions of a man who has outlived everyone who gave them to him. He received me the way the others had — nodding at the recorder in my bag as if greeting a small animal — but when I mentioned the festival songs, something in his posture closed like a shop shutter.

"Old things," he said. "Nothing worth your archive."

"The fourth song," I said. "The slow one. I only caught a fragment."

"You didn't catch anything." He said it kindly. That was the worst part. He said it the way you correct a child who has misremembered a dream. "There is no fourth song."

"Goro-san, I have four seconds of it on—"

"There is no fourth song." Same tone. Same kindness. And then, quieter, pouring tea he clearly didn't want to pour, performing hospitality as a way of ending conversation: "Your grandmother knew the words too. She never sang them either. She was a good woman, Chiyo. She kept quiet."

*Kept quiet.* Not *she was loyal*, not *she was faithful*. Kept quiet. In Kessho, apparently, these were the same thing, and the equivalence was so old and so load-bearing that a man could praise his dead friend with it across sixty years and mean nothing but love.

I asked him, carefully, what the guest at the table waited with his hands open *for*.

Goro set down the teapot. For a moment — one moment — he looked at me with an expression of such naked, exhausted pity that I understood he saw exactly how far along I already was. How many days. Maybe how many chairs.

"Eat well while you're here," he said. "Sleep with the doors shut. And when the letters come for the rest of your family —" he stopped himself, jaw working, and I felt the floor tilt under the sentence, because I had sent no letters, and I had assumed no one else had either, and here was this old man speaking of letters plural, coming, future tense, as though it were a scheduled bus. "When they come," he finished, "tell them not to open them."

"What letters?"

He smiled at me then, and it was the smile you give at a funeral, warm and helpless and aimed slightly past you. "You're a good boy," he said. "Chiyo would be proud. Now go on. It'll be dark soon."

It was two in the afternoon.

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the terraced fields, telling myself stories. Goro was senile. The village was insular and superstitious and protective of its repertoire, which is common enough — sacred songs guarded from outsiders, folklore hoarded like seed corn. There were perfectly good anthropological explanations for everything I'd seen, and I catalogued them the way I'd catalogued the settling beams and the wind in the pipes of my grandmother's house, the way I'd been cataloguing rationalizations since I was six years old and something scratched at the sealed room's door and Father said *old houses talk.*

But catalogues have a failure mode, and I hit it halfway up the hill: the explanations explained the villagers. None of them explained the pause. Two seconds, at the end of every verse, in every song, from every throat in that hall — a space left open, uniformly, unconsciously, the way you leave space for a person who used to sit beside you. You can't learn that from superstition. You can only learn it from grief. The whole village was grieving something it refused to name, and feeding the silence instead of the song, and calling the silence loyalty.

My family called it that too. I recognized the accent.

At the house, I did check the photograph. I made myself. Day nine: the extra chair now stood at the table's edge, close enough that its carved back nearly touched the wood, close enough that in the image it seemed not placed but *seated*, patient, angled toward the head of the table where my grandmother's portrait hung on the wall behind.

And beneath the kitchen door, wedged in the gap where the draft comes through, was an envelope.

Grandmother's handwriting. My name.

I didn't open it. Goro had told me not to — though he'd meant the ones coming for Mika and Theo, hadn't he, the ones still traveling toward my brother and sister like slow arrows fired two years ago. Mine had apparently arrived early. Or had always been here, waiting in the house, the way everything here waits.

I left it on the mat. I went upstairs. I put on the headphones and played the four seconds again, and again, transcribing the fragment phonetically, archiving it against every instruction I'd ever been given, because that is the whole of my religion: what is recorded cannot pretend it never happened.

*The guest at the table waits with his hands open, and his chair pulled near.*

Somewhere below me, in the kitchen, a chair scraped softly across the floorboards — once, deliberate, like a throat cleared before speech.

I turned off the light and did not go down.
