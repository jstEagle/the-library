## 20. Signature

The pen was heavier than a pen should be.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was all I had left. It wasn't metaphor. It wasn't adrenaline sharpening my senses into poetry. The fountain pen lying on the table — my grandfather's pen, the one I'd found in the sealed room beside his unfinished confession, the one that should have been dry as a bone after fifty years in a drawer — weighed something like a hammer. When I picked it up, my wrist complained.

The thing at the head of the table watched me do it.

It wore my grandmother's face the way you'd wear a coat borrowed from someone smaller. The features were right — the soft fold of her cheeks, the mole above her left eyebrow, the reading glasses pushed up into her silver hair. But the smile was wrong in a way I couldn't have explained to a police officer and didn't need to explain to myself. Grandmother had smiled like she was letting you in on something. This thing smiled like it was letting you *out*.

"Lena." My grandmother's voice, but pitched slightly too low, the way a recording sounds when the tape has stretched. "You came to the table."

"I came to negotiate," I said.

Behind me, the doorway breathed. That's the only word for it. Since I'd sung the song whole in the square — since I'd stood on the cracked flagstones with my voice cracking worse and named what Grandfather had done in 1968, out loud, in front of whatever remained of Kessho to hear it — the house had been screaming. Not creaking. Screaming. A long, structural vowel of grief or rage that lived in the walls and in my teeth. And beneath the threshold, when I'd walked back up the path, the ground had opened — not dramatically, not a chasm with fire at the bottom, but a seam, a lip of dark earth parting like something exhaling after holding its breath for two generations.

I had stepped over it anyway. Because the alternative was leaving Mika and Theo inside it.

They were upstairs now. I'd made them promise — actually promise, hands held, the three of us in a knot like children playing tug-of-war against nothing — that they would stay in the sealed room until I called for them. Theo had said, "If this goes wrong, I'm coming down," and Mika had said, "When it goes right," and I had loved them both so much in that moment that it frightened me more than the table did.

Because love, I understood now, was the currency here. It had always been the currency. Grandfather had paid with his silence, and silence is just love with the sound turned off.

"Negotiate," the thing repeated. It turned the word over like a stone looking for the soft underside. "Your grandfather also came to the table."

"My grandfather came to the table to lie," I said. "He took Kirisawa money to write 'accident' where he should have written 'murder,' and then he sat in this house for thirty years listening to the chair get closer, and he told himself it was the pipes. He signed himself and everyone who came after him into being your witnesses. Your residents. Your leash-holders." I set the pen down. My hand was shaking; I let it shake. Honesty, apparently, worked better unsteady. "I'm not signing that."

The smile did not move, but something behind it adjusted, the way furniture shifts in an empty room when no one has touched it.

"Then you will leave," it said. "And I will call the others down. One by one. The sister first. She already knows the song."

"You will not touch her."

"I have never touched anyone," it said, and there was something almost wounded in it, which was the most horrifying thing yet. "I sit. I wait. They come to me. Your brother nearly sat last week. He saw himself in the glass and ran, but he *looked*, Lena. Looking is halfway to sitting. Your sister hums in her sleep. Your auntie Sato died with her hand open, reaching for a pen she was too honest to use. Everyone comes to the table eventually. I am very patient. Patience is the only thing I have ever been given."

I pulled out the fifth chair — the extra one, the one that had crept closer day by day through every photograph I'd taken, and I noticed, distantly, that it was warm — and I sat down across from my dead grandmother's stolen face.

"Then let's talk about what you've actually been given," I said. "Because I've been doing research. It's what I do. I'm a folklorist. I collect songs nobody wants sung anymore, and I've learned something about songs: they're contracts. Somebody promises something in a verse, and everybody who sings it afterward keeps renewing the promise without knowing they are. You've been living off a contract written in 1968 by a frightened man who wanted to protect his family. But contracts can be renegotiated. Even yours. *Especially* yours — because I don't think you ever liked the old terms either."

For the first time, the thing moved. Not much. Its head tilted, three degrees, like a bird considering a window.

"Speak," it said.

* *

Here is what I knew, laid out on the table between us alongside the deed and the pen:

I knew the Hara house had gone empty in 1969, six chairs photographed for a family of five, and then dust. I knew the clause in Sato's papers — *full transfer of all residents* — meant exactly what it sounded like. I knew Ayame Kirisawa cast no shadow because she wasn't carrying one around; she'd traded hers off decades ago, or been built without one, or was simply too old for shadows to bother claiming her. And I knew, from the letter in the sealed room, from the confession Grandfather never finished, that the arrangement had a shape: the family sang the song to keep the thing leashed, and the thing kept the village safe-ish, and the village kept its mouth shut, and round and round, a wheel greased with silence for sixty years.

But wheels wear. And the thing at the head of the table was starving. Not for blood — I don't think it had ever wanted blood, despite the mud on Theo's shoes and the blood under Mika's fingernails. Those were side effects. No. What it starved for was what it had been fed at the beginning, before 1968, before the surveyor, before any of it: it had been *remembered*. Sung to. Named. The lullaby I'd recorded in my first week, the one with the second voice humming underneath — that hadn't been a haunting. That had been a feeding. An old woman singing to something that hadn't been sung to in decades, and the second voice joining in because it couldn't help itself, the way a dog comes to a whistle it hasn't heard since puppyhood.

"You don't want residents," I said. "Residents are just hostages. Hostages make terrible company. What you want is what the song gave you before my grandfather turned it into a lock. You want to be *known*. Annually. Truthfully. By name."

"I do not have a name," it said. "Names were taken."

"Then we give you one back." My heart was going like a woodpecker, but my voice held, and I'll be proud of that on my deathbed, whenever the hell that turns out to be. "Here are my terms. Listen carefully, because I'm only saying them once, and then I'm writing them down, and once they're written, they're as binding on you as your old bargain was on us."

The house stopped screaming. Just — stopped. The silence rang.

"The Nakamura family keeps the story," I said. "Every year, on the night of the autumn festival, we come back to Kessho — or we gather wherever we are, if Kessho falls down around us, and honestly, buddy, the way these beams look, that could be soon — and we sing the song whole. Both voices. The verse about the guest at the table, the one the elders strangled mid-line in the community hall. We sing it truthfully. We say the surveyor's name — Hara Kenji, I found it in the newspaper, I checked the dates twice. We say what my grandfather did. We don't dress it up. We don't call it an accident. We tell the story the way it happened, every year, forever, as long as there are Nakamuras to tell it."

"And in exchange," the thing said, in my grandmother's voice, and for one awful second it sounded genuinely curious, genuinely *hungry*, the way I sounded asking Auntie Sato about the festival songs.

"In exchange, you release the residents. All of them. Full transfer, reversed. Nobody signs themselves to you. Nobody sits at this table unless they choose to visit, as guests, and guests leave. The house stands. You keep the house — I'm not asking you to go, I'm not stupid enough to think anything could make you go, and maybe I don't even want you to. You were here before us. You'll be here after. But you stop *collecting*. No letters in dead women's handwriting. No job offers dated from this address. No sleepwalking sisters. No chairs creeping closer in photographs. The chair stays at the head of the table, and it stays *empty*, and its emptiness is the point. Every year we sing, and every year the empty chair reminds us why. That's not servitude. That's memory. And memory, unlike a hostage, doesn't try to escape."

The thing regarded me for a long time.

Outside, wind moved through the valley, and I heard — faintly, from the direction of the village — someone's television, someone's dog, the ordinary machinery of a place deciding whether it still wanted to exist. Upstairs, floorboards settled under my brother's pacing. Everything alive was making small noises, and the thing across from me made none, and somehow that contrast was what steadied me. I was the noisy one. I was the living one. For once, that counted for something.

"You offer me a leash made of attention," it said finally, "to replace a leash made of silence."

"I offer you the difference between being locked up and being *commemorated*. Ask any folklorist which one lasts longer."

Something happened to its face then. I want to describe it accurately, because accuracy mattered to me even then, especially then. The smile stayed. But underneath the smile, the borrowed features went briefly transparent — not literally, or maybe literally, I was tired and terrified and my field notes from that night are mostly exclamation points — and I saw, or sensed, or was permitted to see, something vast and patient and terribly lonely, something that had been waiting at tables since before tables, something whose entire existence had been reduced by one man's cowardice to counting chairs in photographs and waiting for signatures.

It considered. That was the beat, if I'm using my own vocabulary: negotiation with the unknowable, and the unknowable taking its time.

"You understand," it said at last, "that stories change in the telling. Each year, less true. Details soften. Guilt wears smooth, like a stone in a river. In a hundred years your descendants will sing that a surveyor *went missing*, and it will be a pretty little ghost song, and I will have been cheated."

"Then I'll write it down too," I said. "The whole account. Sealed copy in the prefectural archive, open copy in the folk archive under my name, and I'll record the song with both voices and credit the second one properly. Paper doesn't soften. I've spent my career watching paper refuse to soften while people begged it to."

"And if your family fails? If they forget? If they decide one year that the festival is far, and the train is expensive, and the song is unpleasant?"

"Then you may remind them," I said. "One letter. One dream. One chair in one photograph, moving closer. Reminders only. Never collection. If I'm going to hand you a leash, I'm not pretending it isn't a leash both ways."

The smile widened. For the first time, it reached the eyes — my grandmother's eyes, wet and brown and suddenly, horribly, kind.

"My grandson," it said, and I flinched, and it noticed, and I think it enjoyed that, "always did drive a hard bargain."

"He drove a *bad* bargain," I corrected. "There's a difference. That's rather the whole point."

* *

I picked up the pen.

It was lighter now. I noted that too — noted everything, compulsively, the folklorist's reflex running even through terror like a spine. Whatever weight the pen had carried, it had been the weight of the old terms, and those were dissolving even as we spoke, or being rewritten, or being *considered*. The nib touched the deed where Sato's signature would have gone, where Theo's almost had, where the line waited with the patience of everything else in this valley.

Above the line, in the space where the transfer clause sat printed in its clean, murderous legalese, I wrote — aloud, because spoken words bound here, I knew that now, the whole village was built on spoken words nobody would repeat — the new clause:

*"The Nakamura family shall keep the story of Kessho: annually, truthfully, wholly sung, wholly named. In remembrance, not servitude. The resident signs as witness, not as payment. The chair remains empty, and the emptiness is honored."*

Then I signed.

*Lena Nakamura.* My handwriting came out steadier than I expected, which annoyed me — I'd have liked a dramatic flourish, something for the archive — but it came out true, and true was the whole assignment.

The moment the ink dried, three things happened, and I've never been able to settle on their order.

The house sighed. Not screamed — sighed, a long exhalation through every beam and pipe and settling joint, sixty years of held breath released at once, and dust sifted down through the kitchen light like the building was shedding a skin.

The chair at the head of the table — the extra one, the fifth, the fourteenth, the one that had been creeping toward us through photographs since the day I arrived — settled. Just settled, the way furniture does, finding its final position. It did not move again. It has not moved since.

And the thing wearing my grandmother's face looked down at the signature, and read it — I watched it read, lips moving slightly, my grandmother's lips, forming my words — and then it looked up at me, and the smile changed. Became, for one flickering instant, the actual smile. Hers. The one from the photograph on the stairs, the one from summers I barely remembered, the one that let you in on something.

"Well sung," it said.

And then the lights went out, and from upstairs, Mika screamed my name.

* * *

I was moving before the darkness finished landing. Stairs, three at a time, calling back "I'm here, I'm here, it's done, it's done" — though I didn't know if it was done, that was the thing about negotiating with the unknowable, you sign and you hope and you run toward the screaming regardless. The sealed room door stood open. Moonlight through the broken shutter. Mika pressed against the far wall, Theo in front of her with a chair leg raised like a sword, and both of them staring at —

Nothing. At the empty fourth place setting under its fifty years of dust. At the unfinished letter on the desk, except it wasn't unfinished anymore; I could see fresh ink on the final page, gleaming in the moonlight, though I hadn't touched it.

Mika grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise. "Lena. Lena, the letter —"

I crossed the room and picked it up. My grandfather's handwriting, the same hand that had written *accident* in 1968, had written one more line below where the confession broke off fifty years ago. Fresh black ink. Still wet enough to smudge.

It said: *"Forgive me. It says yes."*

Downstairs, very softly, from the kitchen, came the sound of a single chair scraping gently into place at the head of a table — and staying there.

And outside, far down the valley road, headlights swung toward the village. Slow. Unhurried. A car that cast, I would later confirm, no shadow at all, coming to learn that the property it had come to collect had just been sold to the best possible buyer: the truth, payable annually, in song.
