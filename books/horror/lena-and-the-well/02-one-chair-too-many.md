## 2. One Chair Too Many

The funeral home gave me a box. Not a box—a *receptacle*, the director had said, with the gentle diction of a man who handled grief the way I handled audits, as a matter of procedure. A banker's box, legal-size, with Elias Whitlock printed on a label in block capitals that weren't anyone's handwriting at all. Inside: a wallet, a phone with a cracked screen, a set of keys, a watch stopped at 4:17, and a shoebox of photographs that I had no memory of him keeping.

I sat at the kitchen table of Grandma's house with the box between my elbows and a legal pad to my left, because that is what I do. When the world stops making sense, I make lists. Assets. Liabilities. Outstanding obligations. It had gotten me through the phone call from the sheriff—*fell into the old well on the property, ma'am, we'll need you to come identify*—and it would get me through this. Grief, I had decided somewhere over the Appalachians, was mostly a paperwork problem. You file the death certificate. You close the accounts. You cancel the subscriptions. You do not, under any circumstances, open the emotional equivalents of unexplained line items, because unexplained line items are how companies collapse and how people do too.

The house hummed around me. Old houses do that—refrigerator, water heater, wind in the chimney flue. I told myself the hum was only that.

I started with the wallet. Thirty-one dollars, a library card expired in 2019, a photo of the two of us at Coney Island, sunburned and squinting, twelve years old and identical. I put it in the *keep* pile. The phone wouldn't charge. The keys were house, truck, a padlock I didn't recognize. Keep. Keep. The watch I held for a long time, thumb over the crystal, watching my own warped reflection swim in the glass. 4:17. The coroner's report said the fall happened sometime in the late afternoon. I put the watch in the keep pile face-down so I wouldn't have to look at the number again.

Then the shoebox.

It was a woman's shoebox, actually—some old brand, *Merrick's Fine Footwear, Hollow Creek*, the lid soft with age, the corners furred. I noticed the name without registering it, the way you notice weather through a window. Inside, photographs. Dozens of them. And this is where the ledger in my head began, very quietly, to stop balancing.

They were pictures of this house. All of them. Decades of them, arranged not quite chronologically—Elias had never been an orderly man—but spanning, by my estimate, from the early seventies to last spring. The same porch, the same sagging eave over the northeast corner, the same elm that had since been struck by lightning and half-removed. Elias had photographed this house the way other people photograph their children: obsessively, tenderly, from every angle, in every season. I hadn't known. We talked every Sunday. We talked about work, about Mom, about whether either of us was ever going to fix our posture. He had never once said, *I've been documenting the house where Grandma raised us like it's a crime scene.*

I laid them out on the kitchen table in rows, and the kitchen light buzzed, and outside the October wind moved through the dead soybean field with a sound like a crowd shushing itself.

Here is the thing I want to be precise about, because precision is all I have: in the oldest photographs—the seventies, the eighties—there is a chair on the porch. A plain wooden chair, ladder-back, no cushion, the kind of thing a farmhouse accumulates and never discards. It sits to the left of the door, against the siding, angled slightly outward, as if someone had just gotten up from it, or was about to sit down.

I have been coming to this house my entire life. Summers. Christmases. The year Mom's second marriage collapsed and we lived here for four months while she slept. I know this porch the way I know my own hands. There has never been a chair there. There is a fern stand, a boot scraper, a wind chime Grandma took down in 2003 because it annoyed her. There is *no chair*.

I did what I always do with an anomaly. I looked for the ordinary explanation.

*It was removed at some point.* Fine—but the photographs continued. Nineties, aughts, a 2011 shot with a dusting of snow, and the chair was in every one, same position, same angle, aging with the house the way the house aged—the paint on it going from white to gray to bare wood in perfect parallel with the porch boards. A chair that had been removed does not continue to appear in photographs taken after its removal. A chair that had been removed does not *weather*.

*It's a different porch.* No. The lightning-scarred elm. The eave. The boot scraper, present in every frame like a signature. It was this porch.

*Elias composited them.* My brother, who once returned a toaster because the manual confused him. My brother, whose idea of digital manipulation was a filter that made everything look like 1977. I turned the oldest photograph over. On the back, in pencil, in a woman's slanted hand that was not Elias's, not Grandma's, not anyone's I knew: *June 1974. Ada's chair.*

I sat with that for a while. The refrigerator cycled off, and the silence it left behind felt less like an absence than a held breath.

* * *

I told myself I'd stop at ten photographs. I went through all of them. That is the kind of accountant I am—I find the discrepancy and I cannot sleep until I have traced it to its source, even when the source is a hole in the ground and the money is grief.

Somewhere past midnight the rows on the table had become a timeline, and the timeline told a story I didn't want to read. Because the chair did something no piece of furniture should do. It *moved*.

Not much. Not between decades—between *visits*. In a photograph from 1998, taken from the yard, the chair sat flush against the siding. In another from the same summer—Elias had written *July* on the back—the chair had shifted perhaps a foot toward the door. In 2006 it was angled differently, facing the road instead of the fields. Small movements. The movements of something patient. I measured them with a ruler from the junk drawer, because of course I did, and logged the distances on my legal pad in a column, and then I looked at the column of numbers and understood that I had just created a spreadsheet tracking the migration of a ghost chair, and that if Elias were alive he would be laughing at me, and the laugh I imagined was so exactly his that I had to put down the ruler and press the heels of my hands into my eyes until the wetness there could be blamed on fatigue.

There were also, I noticed, photographs of the well. Elias had photographed the well more than the house. The well appeared in the background of half the porch shots, a low stone ring out past the fence line, capped—I now recalled—with a concrete lid and a steel plate, because Grandma had it capped the year I was born. Or so I'd always been told. In the earliest photographs the cap was there. In the most recent ones, taken last spring, the cap was there too, but the grass around it was wrong. Trampled. A dark circle of worn earth, like something had been pacing.

I was still staring at that when I remembered the last item in the box. Beneath the photographs, wrapped in a page torn from a phone book, a Polaroid camera. And a single developed Polaroid, still in its sleeve, dated on the back in Elias's handwriting: *Funeral. Today.*

I had not known he'd been to the house before I arrived. The funeral was three days ago; I'd flown in the day before, and he was already dead by then, so the date made no sense unless—unless he'd written it in advance, unless he'd meant to come, unless the handwriting was his and the date was a lie or a mistake or a message.

My hands were not entirely steady when I slid the Polaroid out of its sleeve.

It was this porch, at dusk, from the yard. The light in it was the bruised gold of the hour I'd arrived, three days ago, to a house I already hated. And there, against the siding, left of the door—

The chair.

Closer. Much closer. Not against the wall anymore but centered in the frame of the doorway, pulled forward to the very lip of the porch, facing out, facing the yard, facing the camera, facing *me*. Positioned the way you'd position a chair for a guest you were expecting. Positioned the way you'd position it at a table for someone who was late to dinner but who you knew, *knew*, was coming.

I did the rational things. I held it under the light to check for double exposure. I checked the film batch number against recall—Polaroid's later stock had a known flare artifact, a ghosting in long exposures, and grief plus exhaustion plus a low-ceilinged kitchen at one in the morning is a known recipe for pareidolia. I wrote all of it down on the legal pad. *Artifact. Suggestion. Prior owner's furniture, removed, photos staged or misdated.* The words looked correct. They looked like a balanced ledger. They balanced the way Grandma's books had balanced for fifty years, which is to say: perfectly, and on purpose, and hiding something.

Because here is what I could not explain away, what I keep coming back to now, writing this by lamplight with every door in this house locked and the kitchen chair—my chair, the one I dragged in from the garage—turned to face the room instead of the window.

In the Polaroid, on the porch, in the doorway of the house where I was at that moment standing, there is a second figure. Small. Blurred, the way Polaroids blur things at the edge of their depth of field. A child, maybe, standing beside the chair with one hand resting on its back. Not looking at the camera.

Looking at the door.

At the door I had walked through, three days ago, at dusk, in the bruised gold light, carrying a box of my dead brother's belongings.

I have an appointment tomorrow. There's an estate attorney in town, a Mr. Deering, executor of Grandma's will, and I have questions for him—about the property, about the well, about why Grandma Whitlock, who kept receipts for everything, kept a shoebox from a company called Merrick's Fine Footwear in a box of her grandson's most private possessions.

But tonight I went up to the attic, because the photographs had to have come from somewhere, and Elias's room yielded nothing, and the attic stairs were the only place left in this house I hadn't searched. There's a cedar chest up there under the eaves. Old, heavy, locked, its hasp fitted with a padlock gone green with fifty years of weather.

Scratched into the lid, small, in that same slanted woman's hand from the back of the June 1974 photograph, are four words:

*GIVE IT BACK, EDITH.*
