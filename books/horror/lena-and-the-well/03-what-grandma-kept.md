## 3. What Grandma Kept

The attic ladder was the kind of thing that should have killed somebody years ago—aluminum, sprung, a hinge that shrieked like a stepped-on cat when I pulled it down. I stood at the bottom of it for a long moment with the flashlight in one hand and my phone in the other, the phone's screen showing the checklist app I'd built that morning, because that was what I did. I made lists. I put things in columns. Death, in my experience, was mostly logistics with better lighting at the funeral.

ELEAS—ESTATE, the checklist said, misspelled because my hands had been shaking when I typed it and I hadn't fixed it, because fixing it would have meant looking at it.

*Inventory attic. Sort clothing. Cancel subscriptions. Find the deed.*

That last one mattered. The deed to the Hollow Creek property—eighty-one acres of timber and a house that had been trying to lie down into the earth for thirty years—was the only asset in my grandmother's estate worth the word. The funeral home wanted a signature. The county wanted a death certificate. The bank wanted everything else. I had come to Hollow Creek to close accounts, and I was going to close them in order, top to bottom, the way I closed every account, and then I was going to drive back to the city and never think about this house again except as a line item.

That was the plan. I want that on the record. I had a plan.

* * *

The attic smelled like every attic smells—dust and heat and old paper—and like one thing every attic does not: cedar. It came from the far corner, past the boxes of Christmas ornaments and the moth-eaten slipcovers and the exercise bike my grandmother had bought in 1988 and ridden exactly never, a low sweet smell that cut through the staleness like a knife through frosting.

The chest sat under the round window, the one that faced the well.

I want to say I noticed that immediately. I didn't. I noticed the chest—a cedar chest, knee-high, dark with age, its brass fittings gone green—and I noticed the padlock, which was the strange part. Everything else in this house had been open. Every door, every drawer, every box, left unlocked and unbothered for the fifty years my grandmother had lived here alone, as if she had nothing in the world worth hiding and wanted the world to know it. But this chest had a lock. A small brass padlock, tarnished, and no key hanging on the little nail beside it where she kept the keys to everything—the shed, the cellar, the lockbox where she kept her birth certificate and her savings bonds in a fireproof box she'd bought from a traveling salesman in 1969.

I stood there and did the thing I do. I thought about it like a ledger.

A locked chest in an otherwise unlocked house. A chest that smelled strong enough that the cedar had to be relatively recent, or recently opened—cedar scent fades, that's basic; the oil volatilizes; a chest sealed for fifty years smells like nothing but dust on the outside. So she had opened it. Not long ago, even. Within the last few years, probably. Within the last year.

My grandmother had been dying for eight months and she had climbed that shrieking ladder and knelt on these boards and unlocked this chest and then locked it again. What does a woman put back in a box, at seventy-nine, with maybe a season left, and lock?

I should have gone to find the bolt cutters in the shed. Instead I stood there, and the light through the round window—the window that faced the well, I noticed it now, I couldn't stop noticing it—fell across the lid in a long pale oval, and I had the distinct, unaccountable feeling that the chest was not locked *against* me.

It was locked *for* me. Waiting, the way everything in this house lately seemed to be waiting.

* * *

The padlock's hasp had been soldered and re-soldered; the screws holding the hasp plate were loose enough that I got it off with the flathead screwdriver I carry, because I am the kind of person who carries a flathead screwdriver, because I am the kind of person who believes every problem is a fastener. The lock fell into my palm, light as a bird's skull. The lid came up with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside: a quilt, folded in half, that I didn't look at yet. A shoebox. A legal-size envelope, yellowed, soft at the corners like fabric. And under all of it, flat and heavy, something wrapped in a dish towel.

I took the envelope first. Occupational habit—paper first, objects later. Inside were three things.

The first was a property deed. Deed of Conveyance, County of Hollow Creek, dated March 14, 1974. Eighty-one acres, more or less, being the parcel known to the grantors as the Merrick farm. I read it the way I read everything, top to bottom, and my eyes snagged on the grantor's signature the way a sock snags on a nail.

*Ada L. Merrick.*

I knew the name. Of course I knew the name—Merrick Road ran along the north edge of the property; the county had never renamed it, or couldn't afford to, or nobody had thought it mattered. I'd driven in on it two days ago. I'd assumed it was like Whitlock Hollow, a name that had outlived its family the way most names do.

But it was the signature itself that stopped me. I have spent eleven years looking at signatures. It's most of my job—forged endorsements, altered instruments, the small confident crimes people commit with a pen. And this signature was wrong in the specific, catalogued way forgeries are wrong. The letters were too careful. Real signatures are muscle memory; they trail off at the end, they get lazy. This one had been *drawn*, each letter constructed, the *k* in Merrick made in two separate strokes—you could see the seam where the pen had lifted and come back down, a hairline discontinuity no living person leaves in their own name.

Someone had practiced this. Someone had written *Ada L. Merrick* on a piece of paper until it looked right, and then written it on this deed, and then taken eighty-one acres from a woman who could not have known until the sheriff came.

The second thing in the envelope was a newspaper clipping. *HOLLOW CREEK WOMAN MISSING SINCE JUNE*, the headline said. *Search Suspended at Well Site.* The paper had gone the color of weak tea. The photograph was of a young woman with dark hair and a face that was—there is no other word for it—*familiar*, in the way a stranger on a bus is familiar, in the way the woman I'd half-seen at the kitchen sink at three in the morning two nights ago had been—

I put the clipping down on the quilt. My hands were very steady. I noted that they were steady the way you note a symptom.

*Ada Merrick, 24, last seen on the evening of June 9, 1974, near the Whitlock property on Merrick Road. Mrs. Evelyn Whitlock told investigators Miss Merrick came to the house that afternoon "in an agitated state" regarding a property matter, and left before dark. The well on the property, capped in the spring of that year, was opened and searched on June 14. Nothing was found. The cap was replaced.*

The clipping was dated June 22, 1974. The deed was dated March 14, 1974. Three months. She had taken the land in March and the woman had been at her door in June, agitated, asking about it. And the woman had walked down Merrick Road toward the house and had never walked back up it.

And my grandmother had lived here for fifty years. Alone. On land with a missing woman's name on the road that led to it, sleeping every night above a chest with the deed in it, and the clipping, and—

I took out the dish towel bundle last.

It was a child's shoe. Leather, small, the kind with a single button at the ankle, cracked with age, one lace missing. A girl's shoe, maybe a four-year-old's, maybe a five-year-old's. It had been wrapped with care, the way you'd wrap something breakable, the way you'd wrap something you couldn't throw away and couldn't look at.

I sat down on the attic floor with the shoe in my hand and I did the thing I always do, which is reach for the rational explanation, and for the first time in my life the rational explanations lined up like customers at a teller window and every single one of them was insolvent.

*A shoe the grandmother kept as a memento.* Of what? Of what child?

*Ada Merrick had a child.* The clipping didn't say it, but the clipping didn't have to; I could feel it, the way you feel a draft. A twenty-four-year-old woman in 1974, in a hollow like this, agitated, desperate enough to walk down to the house that had stolen from her—desperate because there was a child. And the shoe was in the chest with the forged deed like a receipt. Like *collateral*.

I am not a person who believes things. Belief is a rounding error; I deal in what reconciles. And nothing—nothing—about this reconciled. The deed was forged, that I could prove, that I could prove in any courtroom in the country, and the proof was sitting in my hands in my dead grandmother's attic, and the woman who had signed it had walked toward this house and vanished, and the well had been opened to search for her and *nothing was found*, and the cap was replaced, and the well stayed capped for fifty years until last spring, when—

When what? Who uncapped it? Why would you uncap a well that a missing woman had once been searched in, unless you had finally decided you needed to know what was down there? Or unless something had decided for you?

The light through the round window had gone the color of the clipping. I hadn't noticed the afternoon ending. I looked at my phone: 6:40. I'd been up here for three hours. Three hours, and it had felt like twenty minutes, and somewhere below me a floorboard settled with a sound like a footstep, and I did not flinch, because I was a grown adult and a forensic accountant and I did not flinch at floorboards.

But I did look at the shoe again, and I noticed what I should have noticed at the start: the sole was muddy. Dried to a crust, fifty years old, but mud—dark hollow-creek mud, the clay that stained everything here the color of rust. And the mud was on the *inside* of the sole too, pressed up through the stitching, the way it does when a shoe comes off a foot in soft ground.

When a shoe comes *off* a foot.

I put everything back. The shoe, the clipping, the deed, the quilt. I set the hasp plate back over its holes and drove the screws in with the screwdriver, and I locked the padlock through the hasp, and I sat there on the attic floor in the failing light with my back against the chest and my knees drawn up, and I thought: *the house is stolen.* Not a figure of speech. Not a feeling. The deed was forged; the estate I was here to settle was built on a forged instrument; every dollar this property had ever earned—every year of timber, every month of the pension that had kept my mother fed and my tuition paid—had compounded, fifty years of it, off a crime. I was the executor of stolen goods. I had been the beneficiary of them my whole life. Elias had died on stolen ground.

Elias had died in the well that they'd searched for her.

I got down the ladder. I don't remember climbing down. I remember standing in the upstairs hallway with the padlock still in my pocket, because I hadn't put it back on the nail, because I wasn't going to, and I remember looking down the stairs toward the kitchen, where the light was on, where I had not left the light on.

And from the kitchen, faint and unhurried, someone was humming. A lullaby. A tune I knew—knew the way you know a word you've never said aloud—and I stood on the landing with my hand around a brass padlock and understood, with the last of my skepticism going out of me like heat from an open door, that the chest had never been locked to keep me out.

She'd left it where I'd find it. She'd been waiting for someone to come and read the ledger.

The humming stopped. In the quiet that followed, from somewhere much deeper than the kitchen, much deeper than the house, I heard—or thought I heard, and the distinction no longer mattered—a sound like water shifting, far down, under the ground.

Tomorrow I would find Ruthie Merrick. Tomorrow I would knock on doors on Merrick Road until I found what was left of Ada's family, and I would hand them the deed, and I would pay back fifty years with everything I had.

But tonight the light was on in the kitchen, and the humming had stopped, and I had to walk past that kitchen to get to my room.
