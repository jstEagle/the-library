## 2. Rooms That Count Themselves

Lena had a system. She'd developed it over eleven years and four hundred-odd properties, and it had never once failed her: measure everything twice, photograph everything once, document in the order the rooms presented themselves, and never—this was rule one, learned from a collapsed farmhouse outside Cahors—never let the property know what you thought of it.

So she treated the Delacroix house like any other job. She arrived at eight with her kit bag and her thermos and her laser distance meter, and she began at the front door and worked clockwise.

The house was not large. From the outside—a full circuit on foot, tripod shots at each corner—it was a two-storey limestone farmhouse of perhaps two hundred and ten square metres, plus a cellar she'd locate later and an attic under a slate roof gone soft with moss. The notaire's file said built 1789, extended 1834, unoccupied since March 1974. The file was precise. Lena liked that about it. It was the last precise thing for some time.

She set her benchmark at the threshold, zeroed the meter, and started with the kitchen.

The kitchen measured six point one by five point four. She wrote it down. She shot the diagonals to check for square: both seven point nine, clean. She photographed the hearth, the stone sink, the door into the pantry, and moved on. Pantry: two point three by one point eight. Scullery. Back corridor. Front parlour. Dining room. Each room gave up its numbers without argument, and by eleven she had the ground floor done and was feeling the particular calm that came over her when a building cooperated—the sense, faintly ridiculous but real, of a conversation going well.

She ate lunch on the front step because there were no chairs inside worth trusting, and watched the village do its impression of being alive. Saint-Aubin-du-Mont had maybe forty residents left, most of them clustered near the mairie and the bakery that opened three days a week. The rest of the houses stood with their shutters closed like faces turned away. Above them all, on its bald hill, stood the stump of the bell tower—just the base, knee-high, overgrown, looking less like architecture than like something buried that hadn't finished sinking.

An old woman was watching her from across the lane. Lena had noticed her yesterday when she'd arrived: small, straight-backed, dressed in a man's wool coat buttoned to the throat, standing in a doorway with the stillness of furniture. Now the woman crossed the road at an unhurried pace and stopped at the low wall in front of the house.

"You're measuring," the woman said. Not a question.

"Good morning. I'm Lena Vasseur. I'm doing the survey for the sale."

"I know who you are." The woman looked past her, through the open front door, into the dark of the hallway, for longer than politeness allowed. "Madame Perrin. I'm the last one."

"The last—"

"Resident. Of the village proper. Everyone else lives around it now, along the road. My family kept the house keys since '74." She held out a hand, and only then did Lena see the iron key hanging from a loop of string, black with age. "You'll want this for the cellar. The notary will have told you the cellar door is locked. He will not have told you why."

"He didn't mention a cellar at all."

"No," Madame Perrin said, as if that confirmed something. She didn't offer the key yet. She looked at the laser meter clipped to Lena's belt the way you'd look at a knife someone was carrying point-up. "You'll do your work in daylight."

"That's the plan."

"Do it *only* in daylight." Her eyes came back to Lena's face. They were pale grey, almost colourless, and very steady. "Whatever the rooms tell you after dark, don't write it down. Writing makes it true. And whatever number the machine gives you that you don't like—" she tapped one finger toward the meter, not quite touching it, "—measure again in the morning before you believe it. Rooms lie in the evening. They can't help it. They're old."

"Is that a local saying?"

"It's a local everything." She set the key down on the wall, on top of the stone, deliberately, the way you'd feed an animal you didn't want following you home. Then she walked away, coat swinging, unhurried as ever, and did not look back.

Lena picked up the key. It was warm. That was the first thing she noticed and the first thing she decided not to think about. Iron left in October sun should be cool by late morning; this was warm the way a hand is warm, evenly, all over.

She put it in her pocket and went back to work.

* * *

The upper floor took the rest of the day. Four bedrooms, a linen cupboard, a landing wide enough to be called a gallery. All of it immaculate. That was the other thing she was keeping a list of, the things she wasn't thinking about: the house had been empty for fifty years and there was no dust. Not little dust. No dust. The floorboards gleamed as if waxed last week. A spider had spun a web in the corner of the third bedroom and the web was pristine, geometrically perfect, and contained no flies, no husks, nothing caught. As if even the insects understood the arrangement and stayed out.

By six the light was going copper and then grey, and Lena packed up, drove to the rented room above the bakery, ate tinned cassoulet standing at the counter, and spent the evening transferring her field notes into the survey software, drawing the floor plans properly. This was her favourite part, if she was honest—the part where chaos became geometry, where a day of crawling through other people's attics resolved into clean lines and labelled rectangles. Documentation as ritual. Her old mentor had called it that, half-joking: *the drawing is the exorcism, Liora. You put the ghost on paper and it stays there.*

She drew the ground floor. Kitchen, pantry, scullery, corridor, parlour, dining room, hall, stairs. She drew the upper floor. Four bedrooms, cupboard, landing. She checked every figure against her notes. Everything matched. She saved the file, backed it up to two drives, printed a copy because she liked the weight of paper, and went to bed with the printout on the desk beside her laptop, squared neatly, ready for tomorrow's cellar work.

She woke at 3:14 in the morning for no reason she could name.

Not a sound. Not a light. The village outside her window lay absolutely still, and the hill above it was a darker shape against the sky, and she lay listening to her own pulse and telling herself the feeling in the room was just disorientation, just waking in an unfamiliar bed.

Then she saw the printout.

She hadn't touched it. She knew she hadn't touched it, the way you know your own hands. But the top sheet—the ground floor—was wrong. The lines had shifted. The dining room was smaller. The corridor was longer. And between the scullery and the stairs, where her survey showed solid wall, there was now a rectangle drawn in fine, confident strokes, with a door symbol in its west wall and a label written across its centre in small, neat capitals:

**THE COUNTING ROOM**

It was her handwriting.

That was the thing that made her get out of bed. Not the impossible room, not the redrawn walls—those she could have filed under fatigue, under printer error, under some software glitch that corrupted the layer. But the handwriting was hers. The way she made her capital E's with the middle bar shortened. The way her R's kicked. She had written that label, or something had written it exactly as she would have, and either answer was worse than the other.

She stood over the page for a long time, bare feet cold on the boards, and did not touch it. Rule one: never let the property know what you think of it.

In the morning she compared the sheet against her field notes. Field notes: no such room. Wall between scullery and stairs, load-bearing, with a hairline crack she'd photographed. The printout disagreed with the notes, and the notes agreed with the photographs, and the photographs agreed with the house as she'd seen it—and yet when she returned to the Delacroix house that morning and walked the ground floor with her tape, the wall between the scullery and the stairs had a door in it.

A plain pine door, painted the same grey as the others, with a latch worn bright by use.

It was locked. She tried the Perrin key in it and the key turned—smooth, oiled, recent—but the door didn't open onto darkness. It opened onto a wall. Bricks, mortared, flush against the back of the door, laid with care, even lovingly. Someone had bricked up a doorway and then hung a working lock on the outside of it.

Behind her, in the scullery, her laser meter, sitting in its case on the windowsill where she'd left it, beeped once. A completed measurement. She hadn't touched it.

She stood in the grey morning light with her hand still on the warm key and made herself say it out loud, because saying things out loud made them smaller: "You're not getting in my notes."

The house said nothing. Houses don't. But the hallway behind her felt, for a moment, attentive—the particular silence of a room where someone has just stopped talking.

* * *

She worked the cellar that afternoon, because the alternative was driving back to the city and she was not going to be driven off a job by a locked door and a beeping machine. The cellar stairs went down further than they should have—she counted fourteen steps and the depth read three point nine metres, which meant steps of twenty-eight centimetres, which was possible, which was fine, stop counting.

The cellar was dry, whitewashed, and divided into three bays. Wine racks, empty. A coal chute, sealed. And in the third bay, shelving: rows of ledgers, account books, farm journals, decades of them, spines cracked, covers furred with mould that stopped abruptly at a clean line, as if the damp had been permitted everywhere except here.

And on the end of the shelving, a brass plate, tarnished but legible:

**COMPTE TENU — ACCOUNT KEPT**

Lena photographed it. She photographed the shelves. She did not take a single book down—not yet, not today; tomorrow, in daylight, with witnesses' phone numbers handy—and she climbed back up the too-long stairs and stood in the kitchen breathing carefully while the kettle boiled and refused to boil, sitting on the hob making a thin whining sound, until she turned the burner off and it stopped.

At dusk Madame Perrin was waiting at the wall. Lena hadn't seen her arrive. She seemed to operate on the assumption that appearing suddenly was normal, and Lena was beginning to suspect that for her, it was.

"You found the door," Perrin said.

"How could you possibly—"

"You've got brick dust on your sleeve." She nodded at it. "You tried to open it."

"I opened it. There's a wall behind it."

"There would be." Perrin folded her hands on the wall. Up close her face was a network of fine lines, like crazed glaze, and her eyes were the same steady grey. "My grandmother told me about that door. It opens when the house wants counting. It doesn't matter that they bricked it. Brick is just a suggestion, to that house."

"Wants counting? What does that mean? Counting what?"

Perrin looked at her for a long moment, and Lena had the distinct sensation of being weighed—of some calculation running behind those pale eyes, some arithmetic of risk and inheritance.

"Rooms," Perrin said finally. "Years. People. It keeps accounts. It has always kept accounts. That's what the ledgers are." She glanced toward the cellar hatch, invisible from here, beneath their feet. "You're a surveyor. You count too. That's why the house likes you. That's why—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening.

"Why what?"

"Why the door unlocked for your key." Perrin pushed off the wall and stepped closer, and her voice dropped, not to a whisper but to the register people use for truths they've carried a long time. "Listen to me, girl, because I'll only say it plainly once. Do your measuring in daylight. Be out of that house before the light goes. Don't write down anything the house shows you after dark—if a number comes to you at night, if a measurement arrives in your head whole and certain, you let it go. You don't record it. Recording is agreeing. And if you find yourself wanting to measure anyway—wanting it the way you want water—then you come find me, and we'll talk about whether you should finish this job at all."

"That's absurd," Lena said. "I've surveyed haunted-sounding houses before. Drafts, settling timbers, bad wiring. There's always an explanation."

"Yes," Perrin said. "There usually is." She turned to go, then paused, her back to Lena, and spoke to the lane rather than to her. "One more thing. The room on your plans. The new one."

Lena's skin went cold. "You've seen—"

"My grandmother drew the same room. In 1911. Same place on the plan, same label." Perrin's shoulders rose and fell once. "In her handwriting too. The house doesn't invent rooms, surveyor. It remembers them. And it's remembering you now."

She walked away down the lane, small and straight-backed, and the dusk took her a few metres at a time, coat first, then the white cap of her hair, until the lane was empty and Lena was alone with a warm key in her pocket and a house at her back that felt, unmistakably, like it was waiting for her to come inside and get to work.

She slept at the bakery again. She locked the printout in her car boot, under the spare tyre, and put the car keys under her pillow like a child guarding against ghosts.

At 3:14 she woke anyway. She didn't get up. She lay rigid under the blankets and listened, and from the direction of the Delacroix house—two hundred metres away, downhill, through walls—she heard a sound she had no business hearing: the soft, patient squeak of a drawer opening. Then another. Then the whisper of paper being counted, sheet by sheet by sheet, unhurried, thorough, all the way to dawn.

In the morning the printout was exactly where she'd left it, locked in the boot. When she opened the car, the spare tyre had been lifted out and set neatly beside it. On top of the plans, square in the centre of the ground-floor sheet, sat a single pressed flower—forget-me-not, blue gone grey—with a page number written beneath it in her own handwriting:

**p. 1 of 7.**

Seven pages. Seven nights. Nine days to the harvest moon, though she didn't know yet that the moon was counting too.

Lena stood in the cold morning holding the flower, and understood, with the flat clarity of a level bubble finding centre, that she had not been hired to appraise this house.

She had been engaged to finish its books.
