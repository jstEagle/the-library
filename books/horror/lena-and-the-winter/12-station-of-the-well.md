## 12. Station of the Well

The well sat in a hollow behind the church ruins, which was strange, because Lena had walked that ground twice already and there had been no well.

She noticed the absence of it before she noticed its presence. That was how Saint-Aubin worked on her now—everything arrived as a gap first. She had stood at the edge of the sunken chapel two nights ago, hands numb around Delacroix's ledger, naming her fear aloud to a door that drank the words, and when she turned back toward the village the road had been a road again, ordinary, gravelled, ending at Perrin's shuttered house. The well had not been there. She was certain. She was a surveyor; certainty about ground was the one professional religion she still kept.

And yet at four in the afternoon, under a sky the color of dishwater, she came down the hill path and the well was there, squatting in its collar of moss-eaten stone like it had been waiting since before the church, since before the founders, since before anyone thought to dig a hole in this particular earth and ask it for water.

The ledger's instructions were simple enough to be insulting. *The second station asks what you can spare. Bring nothing you are unwilling to lose. Speak into the dryness. Do not look down while you speak.*

She looked down immediately, because she was Lena Vasseur, and being told not to do something had always made her want to measure it first.

The well was perhaps nine meters deep by eye—a figure she distrusted instantly, having learned what eyes were worth in this village—and utterly dry. No black water at the bottom reflecting her face back wrong. Just pale stone, bone-colored, descending into a darkness that didn't so much swallow light as decline to acknowledge it. Someone had dropped things down there over the years. She could see the suggestion of them: a shoe, a birdcage, something round and white she decided was a gourd and refused to reconsider.

"Right," she said, to no one, because saying *right* out loud before doing something terrible was a habit from every difficult inspection of her career, and this was going to be the most difficult inspection of her life.

She set down her pack. Took out the ledger. The pages had continued writing themselves in the margins—not her handwriting, not Silas Delacroix's either, but something older, cramped, leaning left as if blown by a wind that existed only inside the book. Tonight's entry read:

*The well is dry because everything given to it is kept. Nothing here rots. Nothing here is returned.*

Comforting.

She heard him coming before she saw him—no footsteps, just the particular quality of silence changing shape, the way a room changes when someone who belongs in it enters. Étienne came down the path with his coat collar up against the wind, hands in pockets, and gave her the crooked half-smile that had already become, in six days, dangerously familiar.

"You started without me."

"I wanted to see if it would still be here if I came alone." She hugged the ledger to her chest. "It wasn't here yesterday."

"No," he agreed easily, crouching at the well's rim, running a finger along the moss. "It comes up when it's needed. Like everything else in this place." He glanced at her. "You don't have to do this one alone. I'm told I make adequate company for terrible decisions."

"Who told you?"

"The last person who stood here." His smile thinned. "In 1911. She said I talked too much, but that she was glad of it."

Lena felt the cold move through her that Étienne always brought with him—not malice, just temperature, the chill of deep water or deep stone. Six days ago she would have called it a trick of the wind off the hill. Now she knew better. She knew what he was, or suspected it with a completeness she couldn't yet afford to say aloud, and knowing hadn't made him less necessary. That was the trap within the trap: whatever he was, he was also the only warm thing in Saint-Aubin, and warmth was a currency here, and she was spending it fast.

"Tell me what happens," she said. "Exactly. No comfort."

He straightened. For a moment the humor went out of his face entirely and she saw underneath it something very old and very tired, and she understood that this too might be performance—but performance for whom? A house didn't need to rehearse sincerity for itself.

"You choose a memory," he said. "A true one, whole, yours. You hold it in your mind—all of it, every detail, the way you'd hold a coin between finger and thumb. And then you give it. You speak it into the well, all of it, beginning to end, and as you speak, it stops being yours. Not forgotten. *Unowned.* There's a difference." He looked down into the dark. "You'll know it happened because you'll feel the shape of it gone. A hole with edges. You'll remember that you remembered. You won't remember what."

"And it doesn't come back."

"It doesn't come back," he said. "Nothing here is returned."

*The bell,* she thought. *Melted down in 1911 and ringing anyway.* Nothing returned, but nothing ever quite destroyed either. Everything in Saint-Aubin was kept. She wondered, with a lurch, where the memories went—whether they pooled somewhere beneath the hill like rainwater in a cistern, whether something down there drank them slowly across a century, savoring.

"And if I refuse?" she asked, though Perrin had answered this already, three nights ago, in a kitchen that smelled of woodsmoke and old fear: *Refuse, and it wakes hungry. Feed it small, and it stays asleep. But the stations must be done, girl. Done by you. The house chose you when the deed changed hands.*

"Then we're all standing in the dark together," Étienne said quietly. "And some of us are much older than we look."

*

She sat on the well's rim for a long time before choosing.

That was the cruelty of it, she realized—the station didn't demand a random memory. It demanded she curate her own loss. Somewhere in the architecture of her mind she had to walk through the rooms of herself and decide which one to strip bare, and every candidate came with its own argument for why it should be spared.

Her father teaching her to read a floor plan at eight years old, his pencil tapping the load-bearing walls. Too central. Take that and the profession went with it, and the profession was the only self she trusted.

The night in Lyon, years ago, laughing until she cried with a friend whose name she suddenly couldn't—no, whose name she could, Margot, Margot was fine, Margot stayed. But the memory was load-bearing too. It held up her belief that she was capable of joy.

Her mother.

She flinched away from it and the flinch told her everything. Her mother had died four years ago—pancreatic, fast, the kind of death that leaves no time to rehearse goodbye—and in the four years since, Lena had carried the memory of her voice the way you carry an ember in cupped hands, careful, protective, feeding it silently. She played it sometimes on long drives. *Lena, ma chérie, tu travailles trop.* The slight rasp. The way she said Lena's name with the accent falling on the second syllable, a little song, a door always opening.

If she gave anything else, she reasoned, the ritual would take it clumsily. If she gave something peripheral, she'd spend the rest of her life wondering what hole she was standing next to. But if she chose—if she chose deliberately, knowingly, freely, the way the covenant demanded—then at least the loss would be hers, authored, not inflicted.

At least that's what she told herself, sitting on cold stone with the wind coming down off the hill carrying the smell of frost-to-come.

"I know what you're doing," Étienne said softly. He was sitting beside her now, close enough that their shoulders almost touched. "You're picking the wound yourself because you don't trust the knife."

"Is that wrong?"

"It's brave," he said. "It's also exactly what the thing under the hill wants. It wants the giving to be willing. Willing gifts taste different." He paused. "I'm told."

She turned to look at him. "Told by whom? Étienne—who tells you things?"

For a moment something moved behind his face, something vast and sorrowful turning over in deep water, and then it was gone and he was just a man on a well's rim with wind-reddened ears.

"Later," he said. "Do this first. While you still have the courage. Courage keeps badly here."

*

She closed her eyes.

Built the memory the way she'd build any survey: systematically, room by room. Her mother's kitchen in Cahors, yellow walls, the table with the wobble fixed by a folded cork. Sunday afternoons. Her mother at the stove singing—badly, cheerfully—old songs half-remembered from her own mother. And the voice: she reached for the voice, found it, held it. The rasp. The laugh that broke upward at the end like a bird taking off. The way she said *ma chérie* with the *r* soft as water.

Every detail. Beginning to end. A true memory, whole, hers.

She leaned forward over the mouth of the well, eyes still closed, and spoke.

She told the dark about the kitchen. About the songs. About the last phone call, four years ago, when her mother's voice had already begun to fail and she had sung anyway, one verse, breathless, and said *tu me manqueras déjà* — I will miss you already — present tense, preemptive, a woman arranging her own grief in advance so her daughter wouldn't have to carry it unprepared.

She told all of it. And as she told it, she felt it leave.

There was no tearing. That was the horror. It left the way water leaves sand—quietly, completely, without violence, the surface simply drying behind it. She narrated the final detail—the folded cork, the wobble fixed—and even as the words crossed her lips she could feel the meaning draining out of them, becoming sounds about people she did not know, and then sounds about no one, and then—

Silence.

She opened her eyes.

The well was there. The hill was there. Étienne was beside her, watching her with an expression she couldn't read, and her face was wet, and she had no idea why.

That was the first thing. Not the absence—the absence took a moment to find, like reaching for a stair that isn't there in the dark. The first thing was that she was crying without cause, tears moving down her face with the dumb persistence of weather, and she wiped them and said, "Something's—" and stopped, because there was no word for it.

Grief with no object. Mourning with no corpse. She knew, with absolute certainty, that she had lost something enormous, something structural, something that had held up part of the sky of her life—and she could not remember what. She cast through herself the way you pat your pockets for keys, checking: father, yes. Margot, yes. Lyon, yes. Career, fear, ambition, the smell of rain on hot pavement, all present, all accounted for. And yet somewhere in the inventory there was a gap, a clean-edged absence, and the edges of it ached like a pulled tooth.

"There was—" she began. "There was someone. Wasn't there. Someone who—"

"Yes," Étienne said gently.

"Don't tell me." Her hand came up fast. "Don't. If you tell me, does it—"

"It won't come back." He said it without pity, which she was grateful for. Pity would have broken her. "Knowing you lost something isn't the same as having it. You'll just hurt twice."

She laughed—a cracked, awful sound. "That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in this village."

"I try." He took her hand.

His fingers closed around hers and the cold of them shot up her arm like well water drawn in January—that specific cold, mineral and patient, cold that had never known the sun. She should have pulled away. Every instinct that had kept her alive through six days of impossible rooms and rewriting floor plans said *pull away, he is not a man, he is the house wearing a man the way the hill wears grass.*

She didn't pull away. She held on. Because his hand was the only hand there was, and grief needs somewhere to go, and hers had nowhere—no grave to visit, no voice to replay, not even a name to put on the wound. Just the hole with its clean edges, aching.

"I gave something," she whispered. "Didn't I."

"You did."

"It was important."

"It was the most important thing you had," he said. "That's why it counted. That's why the well took it and the hill is quiet tonight—can't you feel it? Listen."

She listened. Beneath them, beneath the moss and stone and the founders' buried chapel, something that had been restless for days had gone still. Not gone—she could feel it down there the way you feel a large animal sleeping near you in the dark, the displacement of air, the warmth of it, the terrible awareness. But fed. Appeased. One station closer to whatever the ledger was driving her toward, and five stations still between her and the harvest moon.

"One memory," she said. "One, and it bought us a night."

"One true one, freely given," Étienne corrected. "The founders gave hundreds. Unwillingly, at the end. That's why it went wrong for them." He was looking down into the well now, into the colorless dark, and his face had that old tiredness again. "Willingness is everything here. It's the only currency the thing respects. Which is why—" He stopped himself.

"Which is why what?"

He turned back to her and smiled, and the smile was warm and wrong and she loved it and hated it in the same heartbeat.

"Which is why you should be careful who you let hold your hand," he said, "when your pockets are empty. People notice what's missing. Some of them will offer to fill the space." He squeezed her cold-numbed fingers once and let go, and stood, and looked east, where the sky had gone the deep bruised violet of early October evening. "Come on. I'll walk you back. Madame Perrin will have the stove lit, and you need to eat, and you need to sleep, and tomorrow—"

He stopped.

Lena followed his gaze. Down the hill, in the gathering dusk, the village of Saint-Aubin-du-Mont lay quiet under its first true frost-light—and at the far end of the main street, where the Delacroix house stood, every window was glowing amber with lamplight.

All of them. Every window of a house that had no electricity, no lamps, no oil, and no reason.

And standing in the uppermost window, small and distinct against the light, was a figure. Watching them. Even at this distance, Lena could see that it was raising one hand—not waving.

Beckoning.

"That's my room," she heard herself say. "The counting room. It's—it's facing the wrong direction. It shouldn't be able to see the well."

Étienne said nothing for a long moment. When he finally spoke, his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man reading words he wished meant something else.

"It can see everything now," he said. "You've paid it twice. It's starting to pay attention back."
