## 16. Station of the Bell

The ledger said: *the sixth station is struck, not spoken. Bring iron. Bring your own hand.*

Lena read it three times in the yellow light of her headlamp, kneeling on the cellar floor where she'd found Delacroix's book a week and a lifetime ago. Outside, the sky was doing something wrong—clouds moving against the wind, gathering over the hill like an audience taking their seats.

She packed the surveyor's hammer last.

It had been her father's, actually—a brass-headed thing older than she was, with a worn leather grip that fit her palm the way only used tools do. She'd carried it through every job for eleven years. It had measured houses in Lyon, barns in the Dordogne, a decommissioned lighthouse on the Atlantic coast. It had never once been used as what it was about to be used as.

*Bring your own hand.* Not Étienne's. Not Perrin's. Hers.

Étienne was waiting at the door when she came up from the cellar, and she made herself look at him—really look, past the warmth of his face to the impossible fact underneath it. He'd told her everything two nights ago. The house's memory of a boy dead since 1911, given shape and a smile and a way of saying her name that made her chest ache. Given shape *to keep her willing*. And still her body betrayed her, leaning toward him like a plant toward light.

"You don't have to do this one alone," he said.

"The ledger says my hand."

"The ledger says a lot of things." He held out his coat—her coat, rather; he'd fetched it from the hook because he knew she'd forget it, because he knew everything about her now, which was its own kind of horror. "It's going to be cold up there."

The tower's foundation stood on the hill's shoulder above the village, a ring of pale stones no higher than her knee, grass-grown, unremarkable to anyone who didn't know that a church had stood here until 1911 and that its bell had been melted down for war metal before Lena's grandmother was born. Madame Perrin had shown her the archive photographs: solemn men with mustaches posing beside the cracked bell, the whole village turned out, children in the front row. Everyone smiling. Everyone knowing, maybe, what they were really selling—or not knowing, which might have been worse.

The stones were warm. They were always warm. In October frost they steamed faintly at dawn, and dogs from the next valley over wouldn't come within fifty meters of them.

Lena climbed the hill as the sun went down behind her, so her shadow ran out ahead of her all the way to the foundation ring, long and thin and pointing at the stones like an accusation.

* * *

She'd asked Perrin once why the ritual needed six stations before this one. Why not just ring the bell, if ringing the bell was the point.

"Because it isn't a bell," Perrin said. She'd been shelling beans at her kitchen table, and she hadn't looked up. "It never was. The founders heard a voice under the hill in the winter of 1712, and they were starving, and they would have called anything a bell if it meant the voice would answer. The metal was just—" she gestured vaguely with a bean pod, "—a glove. A shape the voice could wear. When they melted it down in 1911, they thought they were cutting off its hand." She finally looked up, and her eyes were old and tired and kind. "You can't cut off a voice, girl. You can only stop listening. And then, eventually, you have to start again."

"So tonight I listen."

"Tonight you *answer*." Perrin went back to her beans. "Five stations you've spoken, given, named, read. This one you strike. There's a difference. Everything before this was you opening doors. This one is you knocking."

"On what?"

Perrin's hands went still over the bowl. "On the table," she said, "where something has been waiting a very long time to be invited to dinner."

* * *

Dusk came early to Saint-Aubin-du-Mont in October, sliding down the eastern slope of the hill while the west still held a bruised orange light. Lena stood inside the foundation ring with her hammer in her right hand and the ledger open on her left forearm, propped there like a mason's tablet, and she waited for the moment the old diaries all agreed on.

*At the exact meeting of day and night,* Silas Delacroix had written in 1911, in handwriting already beginning its slide from measurement into prayer. *Not before. The stone must be neither warm from sun nor cold from dark. It must be remembering both.*

The light drained. The cold rose. And in the narrow band of time between them—ten seconds, maybe twenty, when the world holds its breath between two kinds of dark—Lena raised the hammer and struck the foundation stone.

The sound was not a sound.

It went through her arm, her ribs, her teeth, her skull, the way the dusk-ringing had gone through her the first night, but this time she was on the *sending* end of it, and the difference was like the difference between hearing music and being the instrument. The note came up out of the hill itself, out of whatever hollow thing lay beneath the warm stones, and it passed through her body on its way into the air, and for one vertiginous instant she understood that she hadn't rung a bell at all. She had *been* the bell. A glove of meat and bone, worn for a single note by something enormous and patient and terribly glad to be touched.

Then the world changed.

* * *

Later—much later, in the small hours, sitting in the Delacroix kitchen with a blanket around her shoulders and tea she couldn't drink—she would try to describe it to Étienne and fail. But standing in it, she didn't try to describe anything. She just saw.

The village below her hands became 1911.

Not faded, not ghostly, not a superimposition like the lanterns she'd seen in ruined windows on the first night. *Whole.* Roofs where there were roofless shells. Smoke rising from forty chimneys. The street lamps lit one by one by a lamplighter walking his rounds with a pole, his breath steaming, a dog trotting at his heel. Wagons in the square. Laundry lines. A wedding party spilling out of a house that had been rubble since the war, the bride's white dress shocking-bright in the lamplight, everyone laughing, glasses raised.

And people. Hundreds of people. More people than the village could possibly have held even then, though Lena understood, in the way you understand things in dreams, that these were not only the living of 1911. These were everyone the village had ever been. The founders were there—she knew them on sight, though no photograph existed, the way you know characters in a story you've lived inside. A woman with a burn-scarred forearm who had signed the covenant first. A man whose journal she'd read, whose handwriting she'd traced with her finger, standing in a doorway looking up at her with an expression she couldn't bear more than a second of.

Because they were all looking up at her.

Every face in the village below, every face in the square and the doorways and the fields beyond, tilted upward toward the place where a tower used to stand, toward the woman standing in its absence with a surveyor's hammer in her hand. Lanterns lifted. A child pointed. Somewhere a fiddle stopped mid-phrase.

And they weren't afraid. That was the worst part. They looked up at her with relief—with *gratitude*, vast and terrible, hundreds of faces wearing the expression of people who have waited a hundred years for someone to come home. An old woman pressed her hands together. A man removed his hat. The bride stopped laughing and looked up with tears running down her face, and Lena realized with a lurch that the tears were joy.

*You rang,* they seemed to say. *Finally, someone rang.*

The note was still sounding. It had been sounding the whole time, she realized—it hadn't decayed, wasn't decaying, hung in the air the way a held breath hangs—and inside it she could feel the village breathing, all those lives layered like sediment, 1911 and 1811 and 1712, the starving founders and their well-fed descendants and the empty decades after, all of it present at once, all of it *looking at her*.

She understood, in that breath, what communion meant. Not worship. Not prayer. Just this: being seen, completely, by everyone who had ever stood where you stood. Being known by the dead. Being *counted*.

Her name went through the crowd like wind through wheat. She couldn't hear it—there was no sound but the note—but she saw it move, face to face to face, lips shaping *Lena, Lena, Vasseur, the surveyor, she came back, she's finishing it*—

The light died.

* * *

Night fell like a dropped curtain, and the village was ruins again. Roofless walls. Grass in the streets. One lit window far below—Perrin's—and nothing else. The cold hit her all at once, as if the vision had been holding it off, and her knees buckled and she sat down hard on the warm stone, the hammer rolling from her fingers.

She was crying without having noticed starting.

Not grief, exactly. Something adjacent to grief, something with grief's weight but a different direction—not loss but *contact*, the aftershock of being held by several hundred invisible arms. Her hands were shaking. Her ears rang with the ghost of the note, and beneath the ringing, fainter than memory, she could hear the crowd's attention withdrawing like a tide, reluctantly, the way a congregation files out of a church still humming the hymn.

"They know you now," Étienne said.

He was standing at the edge of the foundation ring. She hadn't heard him climb the hill. Maybe he hadn't climbed it. Maybe he'd simply been elsewhere and then here, the way the house rearranged its rooms.

"I saw them," she said. Her voice came out scraped. "All of them. They were happy. Why were they *happy*?"

"Because someone answered." He crouched beside her, and his face in the starlight was the boy from the 1911 photograph, exactly, perfectly, the son Silas Delacroix had lost. "A hundred years of silence, Lena. A hundred years of knocking on their side of the door and no one knocking back. You have to understand what the silence was like from in there."

"In there." She looked at him. "You were in the crowd."

He didn't deny it. "I'm always in the crowd. I'm the part of it the house can put a coat on." He picked up the hammer, weighed it, handed it back to her with the handle first, careful, courteous, unbearably gentle. "One station left."

"I know."

"After tomorrow, you won't remember any of this. You won't remember me." He said it flatly, facts only, a man reading measurements. "So I'm going to say one thing now, while you can still hold it, even if only for another day." He took her free hand—the one without the hammer—and pressed it between both of his. Cold as well water. Cold as the well she'd given her mother's voice into. "Whatever you see tomorrow night, whatever the house shows you, however it unfolds—you chose this. Write that somewhere. On your arm, on the wall, anywhere future-you will find it. *I chose this.* Because she won't know, and she'll wake up grieving something she can't name, and I can't—"

His voice failed. For a moment he was just a shape in the dark, breathing.

"She'll be happy," Lena said quietly. "That's what the clause promises. Completed freely, the debt closes, the thing sleeps, the village keeps its dead at peace instead of feeding on the living. She'll be happy and she won't know the price."

"Yes."

"And you'll be gone."

"Yes."

Lena looked down at the village, at Perrin's single lit window, at the dark shapes of ruined houses that had been full of laughing people ninety seconds ago. Then she did the bravest and most ordinary thing available to her: she leaned her head against Étienne's cold shoulder and let him hold her, and they stayed that way while the stars wheeled and the foundation stones slowly gave up the day's heat.

Tomorrow was the harvest moon.

Below them, far down in the dark under the hill, something that had waited three hundred years felt the last station approaching—and began, softly, in the roots of the earth, to hum the seventh note.
