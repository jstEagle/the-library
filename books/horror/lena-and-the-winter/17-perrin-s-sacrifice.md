## 17. Perrin's Sacrifice

*Madame Perrin*

The house was quiet in the way she had always hated—breathing-quiet, listening-quiet—and Odile Perrin climbed the stairs of the Delacroix house with her sister's rosary wound twice around her fist and the covenant page folded against her heart.

She had watched Lena sleep. Not from cruelty. From arithmetic. The girl was twenty-nine years old and had a mother somewhere, a life with furniture in it, a future that still had doors. Perrin had eighty-one years, a bad hip, a grave already paid for beside her sister's, and a hope she had carried since 1953 like a stone in her shoe: that someone would end this rather than feed it.

The loophole clause had come to her family the way everything came to her family—through death. Her grandmother had copied it out in 1911 before the fleeing, sewn it into a hem, and every Perrin since had learned it by heart before they learned to write their own names. *If all seven stations are finished by one who gives memory knowingly and freely, the debt closes forever.* Knowingly. Freely. Two words that had never once applied to anyone in Saint-Aubin-du-Mont, because no one had ever been given the choice until the hill chose for them.

But Lena had chosen. Perrin had seen it in her at the well, at the schoolhouse—the way the girl wept and kept walking. That was the whole of it. That was the entire secret of the covenant, and none of the founders' descendants had ever understood it: the thing beneath did not want memory. It wanted the giving.

And so tonight, while Lena slept in the east room with Étienne's name written over and over in her surveyor's notebook like a girl doodling, Odile Perrin would climb to the counting room alone and finish what six stations had begun.

Not because she wanted to be remembered as brave. She had stopped wanting anything from the village decades ago, after they buried an empty coffin for Marguerite and everyone pretended the hill had taken nothing.

She climbed because the ritual required seven stations by harvest moon, and there were only two nights left, and if Lena did them both—if Lena gave everything, mother and career and fear and finally that impossible boy—the girl would wake on the first frost as smooth and blank as new snow, and Perrin could not bear it. She had watched the hill take her sister's body. She would not watch it take this girl's self too. Not when there was an old woman's past sitting right here, mostly used up, going spare.

The stairs were longer than they should have been. They always were, now. She counted forty-one steps where the house had twelve, and did not let herself wonder about it, because wondering was how the house got its hooks into you.

At the top, the corridor opened onto the counting room without her turning any handle. The door simply stood open, warm light spilling out, patient as a mouth.

"I'm coming," she told it. "Don't rush me. I've been dying slower than you for eighty-one years."

Inside, the shelves waited. Ledgers upon ledgers, spines soft with age, each one a villager's surrendered life. And on the great desk, open, the newest ledger—Lena's—its pages filling in handwriting that was and wasn't hers.

Perrin sat down in the chair. It fit her badly, the way everything in this house fit everyone badly. She took out the covenant page, unfolded it, smoothed it flat with two fingers gone knuckle-swollen with arthritis.

Then she took out the pen.

"Léonie," she said, to the empty room, to the hill, to whatever listened. "Marguerite. I'm coming to finish your sentence."

She wrote her own name at the head of the seventh page.

The room exhaled.

*Lena*

Lena woke with her heart slamming and the taste of iron in her mouth, and knew—knew the way you know a phone ringing at 3 a.m. is bad news—that something had started without her.

The east room's window faced the Delacroix house across the lane, and its upper windows glowed with a light that was not electric, not candle, but the color of old paper held up to sun. Étienne was gone from the chair where he'd been reading; his coat was gone from the hook. On the pillow where his head had been, a single line of frost had formed indoors, spelling nothing, or spelling something she couldn't read yet.

"No," she said aloud, to the room, to him, to whoever had left. "No, no—"

She was down the stairs and across the lane barefoot before she'd decided to move, gravel biting her feet, the October cold climbing her legs like water. The front door of the Delacroix house stood open. Of course it did. It had probably been standing open for a hundred years, waiting for exactly this.

"Odile!" Her voice fell into the hallway and didn't echo. Houses like this swallowed sound the way the well swallowed rope. "ODILE!"

The stairs. Forty-one of them, though she'd measured twelve on her first day, back when she still believed in numbers. At the top, the corridor stretched ahead of her, longer than the house, longer than the village, lamplight burning in sconces that hadn't held candles since the last century. She ran. The corridor ran with her, giving ground grudgingly, and she understood with sick clarity that it was letting her arrive exactly when it wanted her to arrive—not sooner.

The counting room door stood open.

*Madame Perrin*

It had seemed so simple, lying awake in her kitchen at midnight, doing the arithmetic. Six stations done. One remained. The clause said *one who gives knowingly and freely*, and it had never said the same one. She had read those words ten thousand times across eight decades and never noticed the mercy hidden in them—or perhaps there was no mercy in them, perhaps it was only her own desperate grammar, but she was eighty-one and tired and it was her page, her family's page, her loophole, and she meant to spend it.

The trouble began at the third name.

The first two came easily enough. Her mother's hands kneading bread. The smell of the kitchen in 1949. She gave them the way you give coins to a collection plate—willingly, even gratefully, glad to be rid of the weight. The ledger drank them in, ink blooming across the page in her own cramped hand.

The third name was Marguerite.

And here was the flaw in her plan, the crack she should have seen from the beginning, the crack that had been there all along, in every generation, waiting: you cannot give away a memory you refuse to release. You can offer it. You can hold it out in both hands. But the giving is not in the offering. The giving is in the letting go.

And Odile Perrin had spent seventy years holding her sister's memory the way you hold a wound closed—with everything you have, forever.

The ledger waited. The room waited. The hill beneath the house waited, and she felt it waiting, felt its attention turn toward her like the slow rotation of something vast and patient under the floorboards.

"I'm giving it," she whispered. "I am. Freely. Take it."

The ink on the page began to move without her hand.

That was when she understood—not with her mind, which was busy panicking, but with her body, which had grown old in this village and knew its weather—that the ritual was not accepting her gift. It was inspecting it. Turning it over. Finding the clenched fists inside the open hands.

An unwilling gift. A gift given to spare another, which sounds like freedom but is only love wearing freedom's coat. The covenant had been very precise, her grandmother's copied words, and precision was the whole point: *knowingly and freely*. Not bravely. Not generously. Not sacrificially.

Freely.

"Oh," Perrin said softly. "Oh, you clever, cruel thing."

The room began to drink.

*Lena*

She reached the doorway in time to see the last of it, and she would spend the rest of her life—however long that turned out to be, however much of it remained hers—seeing it.

Madame Perrin sat in the great chair with the covenant page spread on her knees, and the pages of the ledger around her were turning themselves, faster and faster, and with every turning the old woman grew lighter. Not thinner. Lighter. As if the room were subtracting her by degrees—as if she were being erased downward through her own history, the war years going, then the marriage that never happened, then her mother's kitchen, then school, then language itself peeling off her tongue word by word—

Her face was the worst part. She wasn't screaming. She was smiling. A small, private, relieved smile, the smile of someone setting down a load carried so long the shoulders had grown around it, and Lena realized the horror was not that Perrin was being taken against her will.

It was that some part of her had wanted this—wanted to be emptied, wanted rest, wanted to stop remembering her sister—and the hill had found that want and driven a wedge into it and split her open along it.

"Fight it," Lena said, useless, stupid, running forward. "Odile—fight it—"

Perrin's eyes found her. For one moment they were clear, and sharp, and entirely herself, and her mouth shaped words with the last of her language:

*"The page. Read the page. It's yours—"*

Then the light went out of her face the way a lamp goes out—suddenly, completely, with no ember left behind—and what remained in the chair was a small old woman with clean, smooth features, smiling faintly at nothing, holding a folded paper in her curled fingers like a child holding a note passed in class.

The ledger snapped shut. The lamps went out. Somewhere far below, deeper than cellars, deeper than foundations, something made a sound that was almost satisfaction and almost disappointment, and Lena understood it had gotten a meal after all—just not the meal it preferred. An unwilling gift, half-given, devoured anyway. Appetizer, not covenant.

The house went still around her, sated and silent, and the silence was worse than the bell.

* * *

Lena knelt beside the body for a long time.

There was no blood. There was nothing wrong with her at all. Madame Perrin looked like a woman who had fallen asleep in a comfortable chair after a good dinner, except that her face had the terrible blankness of the newly born, and when Lena touched her cheek the skin was cool as well water, cool as Étienne's hands, and no breath moved her lips.

"You fool," Lena whispered, and her voice broke on it. "You stubborn, wonderful fool."

She had known the old woman for nine days. Nine days of warnings and bitter tea and lessons in the dark, and somehow that had been enough to make her family. Somehow that was how it worked here—the village made family fast, because it had always been starving for it.

Perrin's right hand was closed around something. Gently, hating herself, Lena pried the fingers loose.

A single sheet of paper, old, soft as cloth, copied out in faded brown ink in a hand from another century. The loophole clause. She read it by the gray light beginning at the window, and read it again, and the words rearranged themselves in her understanding the way the house rearranged its rooms:

*If all seven stations be finished by one who gives memory knowingly and freely, the debt closes entire, and the sleeper beneath shall hunger no more, nor wake, nor feed.*

One who gives. Not seven givers. Not a chain of sacrifices down the generations, each handing the debt to the next. One person, finishing all seven stations, giving everything—knowingly. Freely.

Everything.

Lena sat back on her heels. Outside, the sky was turning the color of ash, and the last night before the harvest moon lay ahead of her, and somewhere in the house—or in the lane, or in the space between walls where he lived—Étienne was waiting, and she thought of his face, his laugh, the way he knew streets that no longer existed, and she understood at last what the final station would cost, and why Perrin had tried to climb these stairs in her place.

"You knew," she said to the empty shell in the chair. "You've known since you gave me the page. You weren't teaching me the ritual. You were trying to find a way to do it for me."

The body smiled its small relieved smile and said nothing, because there was no one left inside it to answer.

Lena folded the clause and put it inside her coat, against her heart, where Perrin had carried it. Then she stood, and wiped her face, and looked around the counting room—at the shelves of surrendered lives, at the ledger with her name on it, at the door standing open onto the corridor that led everywhere the house wanted her to go.

One night left. One station. Everything.

Downstairs, faintly, from the direction of the tower foundation, a bell that had been melted down in 1911 rang once—a single clear note, patient, expectant, like a question asked for the hundredth time, waiting at last for its answer.

Lena went to give it.
