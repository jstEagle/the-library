## 1. The Call Comes Collect

The voicemail arrived on a Tuesday, which Silas Underhill would later consider the last ordinary day of his life, though at the time it was mostly notable for a wasp nest in a church attic and an elderly woman in Kettlebridge who kept calling him "the other one."

That was the trick of him, you see. Silas had spent thirty-four years perfecting the art of being forgettable—not invisible, exactly, because invisibility required effort and drama, but *unremarkable*. He had a face like a closed door in a hallway you'd already walked down twice. His name helped. Nobody remembered an Underhill who wasn't attached to the farm; there were four of them in the phone book under U, and two were dead. When clients filled out his invoices they often wrote "Silas" and then paused, pen hovering, before settling on "Underhill?" with the question mark doing all the work.

He'd built a whole business on it. *Underhill Pest & Vermin Control — Quiet, Thorough, No Fuss.* The slogan was true in ways the advertising standards people would have appreciated. Wasps forgot where they'd built. Rats forgot their routes. Even dogs—the great sentinels of the countryside—would look straight through him as he crossed their yards, which had saved his ankles more times than he could count.

So when his phone buzzed against the dashboard while he was eating lunch in the van outside the church, he answered it with the mild dread of a man expecting another quote request for moles.

He missed the call. That was fine. People always rang back.

Except this one didn't.

* * *

The voicemail sat on his phone for six hours, a little red badge of obligation, while Silas finished the wasp nest, drove home to his flat above the shuttered bakery in Marrow Staithe, and ate beans on toast standing up, which he considered the height of bachelor luxury. It wasn't until nine o'clock, when the flat had gone properly dark and quiet, that he played it.

The voice was old. Female. Cracked at the edges like a plate that had been dropped once and glued back together by someone in a hurry.

"This is a message for—" a pause, long enough that Silas almost skipped forward, "—for the boy who left. The pest man. We have need of inspection at Underhill Farm. The walls are speaking again. You'll know what I mean. Or you won't, and you'll come anyway, because you're family, whatever you tell yourself."

Another pause. Then, softer, almost tender: "Bring your own bedding. We never changed yours."

Silas sat very still with the fork still in his hand.

There was no number left on the message. No name. Just that voice, which he did not recognize and recognized completely, the way you recognize a smell from childhood—a specific lard-and-woodsmoke smell that lived in exactly one kitchen in the world.

Aunt Maren. It had to be. She'd be what now, eighty? Eighty-one? The last time he'd seen her he was nineteen, backing out of the farm gate with a duffel bag over his shoulder while she stood on the step not saying *don't go*, because Maren Halloran-Underhill didn't say things like that. She'd said, "You'll want to eat before the road," and handed him a paper bag of sandwiches he'd eaten at the bus stop without tasting a single bite.

He hadn't been back in fifteen years. He'd told himself it was distance, then work, then principle. The truth was smaller and heavier: he'd left because leaving was the only door in that house that opened outward, and he'd gone through it fast, before anyone could ask him to hold something.

His thumb hovered over delete.

It would be easy. It was practically the family motto. *Underhills don't look back; looking back is how they get you.* Delete the message, block the number, and tomorrow there'd be a mole contract in Drayle Fen or a hornet problem at a garden centre, and the world would continue being a place where nothing knew his name.

He pressed delete.

And then—because the universe is a cruel editor—he heard it.

Beneath the click of deletion, underneath the flat digital silence that should have followed, there came a sound. Three knocks. Slow. Patient. Like knuckles on wood—but not on wood, exactly. On wood from the *inside*, if such a distinction can be said to exist, and Silas's stomach informed him that it could, and that it had known this distinction since he was thirteen years old and lying awake in the guest room listening to the walls count.

He checked the phone. Message deleted. Call log empty. No unknown numbers, no missed calls from the afternoon at all—as if the call had never happened, as if the voicemail had been delivered by some other means entirely.

But his ears had heard what they'd heard. Three knocks, spaced like someone taking their time. Like someone who knew he was listening.

"Well," Silas said aloud, to the dark kitchen, because talking to yourself is free therapy. "No."

* * *

He didn't sleep much, which was normal, but the quality of the not-sleeping was new. Every creak of the old bakery building arranged itself into that same rhythm. Three knocks. Pause. Three knocks. At half past three he got up and made tea he didn't drink and stood at the window watching the harbour lights do their slow blink, and he caught himself doing arithmetic he'd sworn off years ago.

Underhill Farm was forty minutes' drive. Maren was eighty-something and alone except for whichever cousin drifted through that month. If the walls were speaking—and God help him, he knew what that meant, he'd known since he was a boy and had filed it away in the same drawer where he kept the memory of his brother's coat hanging by the door—if the walls were speaking, then somebody had to go and look.

Not because it was family. Because it was vermin. That was the story he'd tell himself, and he was good at telling himself stories; it was practically a professional qualification.

By seven a.m. he'd loaded the van. By eight he'd driven eleven miles toward the coast road before pulling into a lay-by to argue with himself properly.

"Look," he said to the rear-view mirror, where his forgettable face did its best impression of a reasonable man. "You could turn around. Nobody would know. That's rather your entire brand."

The mirror offered no counterargument. Mirrors rarely did, in his experience. Though this morning, he noticed—for the first time, and he would remember this later with a cold sliding feeling—the mirror seemed to take slightly longer than usual to show him himself. A half-second lag. A hesitation, like the glass was checking whether he was worth the trouble of reflecting.

"Nonsense," Silas told it firmly, and drove on.

* * *

The lane to Underhill Farm hadn't improved. Fifteen years had turned its potholes from craters into small private lakes, and the hedges leaned in overhead until the van moved through a green tunnel that smelled of wet elder and old rain. Twice he passed gates he remembered. Once he passed a spot where, at fourteen, he'd found a dead crow arranged neatly on a fencepost, wings spread, facing the house. The fencepost was still there. There was no crow. He tried to feel relieved about this and instead felt like the crow had simply stepped out for a moment and would be back.

The farmhouse appeared the way it always had: all at once, as if it had been waiting behind the hedge the whole time, patient as a spider. Grey stone, slate roof patched with newer slate that had already gone grey itself, chimneys like raised eyebrows. And there, on the gable end, the loft window, and below it—though you couldn't see it from here—the bell. The cracked bell that hadn't rung in living memory, hung in the loft where Grandfather Underhill had put it after the war and where, according to family legend that nobody would explain to children, it had better stay.

Silas parked beside the barn and sat with his hands on the wheel.

The house looked *occupied* in a way houses shouldn't. Not lived-in—occupied. Every window held a darkness that seemed to hold still while he watched it, the way animals hold still when they've noticed you noticing them.

"You're a professional," he reminded himself. "You have a laminated price list."

He got out. The air was cold for September and smelled of lard and woodsmoke, exactly as memory promised, and something in his chest went tight and small, the size of a boy with a duffel bag.

Before he reached the door, it opened.

Aunt Maren stood in the frame, tiny and bent and sharp-eyed as a wren, wearing a cardigan the colour of strong tea. She looked at him for a long moment, and Silas braced for anger, or tears, or the particular weaponized gentleness of elderly relatives.

Instead she smiled and said, "There you are. We've been counting the days."

"We?" said Silas.

Maren stepped back to let him in, and behind her, deep in the dim hallway, something knocked on the wall. Three times. Slow. Patient.

Like a welcome home.
