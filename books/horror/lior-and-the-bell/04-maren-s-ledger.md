## 4. Maren's Ledger

Maren Underhill had kept the ledger in the same biscuit tin for sixty-one years, and she kept it in the tin because nobody in the history of the family had ever once wanted a biscuit badly enough to open it.

That was the joke she'd made to her husband, God rest him, back when he was only mostly restful: *The safest place in this house is behind something everyone else wants.* He'd laughed. Then he'd signed his name in the ledger like everyone before him, and he'd stopped laughing around the same time he'd stopped much of anything.

She took the tin down from the top of the pantry now, both hands, because her wrists had opinions these days and none of them were kind. The flour dust on the shelf had been disturbed again — smudges up where no hand should reach, five-fingered and patient as scripture. She didn't look at them long. Looking was a form of invitation, and Maren Underhill had spent eighty-one years declining invitations with a smile.

Inside the tin: the ledger, bound in green cloth gone soft as ear at the spine. She carried it to the kitchen table, set it beneath the good lamp, and poured herself the tea she would not drink because drinking meant getting up twice in the night and her hips had filed a formal complaint.

Then she opened it, the way she did every Sunday, the way her mother had done, and her grandmother, all the way back to a woman named Odile who had supposedly traded a bad winter for a good harvest and gotten considerably more than she'd bargained for.

Six generations of names. Six generations of dates. And after each name, a single tally mark, pressed deep enough to dent the next three pages.

Maren ran her thumb down the column without touching the ink. You didn't touch the ink. Her mother had been very clear about that, right up until the morning her mother's own entry gained its mark and she'd stopped being clear about anything at all.

*Underhill, Odile. 1849.* Tally.
*Underhill, Ambrose. 1871.* Tally.
On and on, a ladder of names climbing down through time toward her, each rung paid for by the person standing on it.

Her own entry waited near the bottom, written in her mother's careful hand when Maren was nine years old: *Underhill, Maren. 1963.*

No tally yet. But the space beside it had the particular emptiness of a chair pulled out for a guest who is definitely coming.

And below hers, in her own hand, added just last month in ink so fresh it still smelled faintly of iron:

*Underhill, Silas.*

* * *

Silas found the pantry on his second day, which surprised him, because he'd been avoiding it on purpose and the house seemed to know.

He told himself he was there to inspect the water damage along the baseboard. Professional reasons. Invoiceable reasons. The fact that the pantry was where the handprints lived — high on the wall, pressed into flour dust that hadn't been touched by human baking since roughly the Nixon administration — was incidental.

Except they were worse in daylight. At shoulder height of someone very tall, or someone who stood differently than people stood, joints bending in an order joints didn't use. He'd photographed them yesterday with the flat, unbothered competence that had made him mildly famous in three counties' worth of pest control Facebook groups (*this man found a raccoon in my chimney and I genuinely cannot describe what he looked like afterward*). Today he crouched and measured one with a ruler he didn't remember taking out.

The span matched his own hand. Not approximately. Exactly, down to the slightly short ring finger he'd resented his entire adolescence.

"Okay," he said quietly, to no one, which was his specialty. "Okay."

"You'll want tea," said Aunt Maren from the doorway, and he nearly went through the shelving. "You always got jumpy in this room. Even as a boy."

"I don't remember being in this room as a boy."

"No," Maren said, in a voice that agreed with him completely and believed him not at all. "Tea."

* * *

Maren watched him from the kitchen window while he walked the fence line with his little clipboard, cataloguing things that could be explained. He walked the way his brother used to walk — no. That wasn't true, and she was too old to lie to herself about anything except the important stuff. Aldous had walked loud, arms swinging, a boy built to be heard. Silas walked like weather. You noticed him only after he'd already passed, and then you doubted you'd seen him at all.

That was why, she told herself, stirring tea she wouldn't drink. That was the whole reason, and it was a good reason, and love was a good reason.

The thing in the walls needed a signature. It had always needed a signature; that was the arrangement, and arrangements were how families survived. Six generations of Underhills had understood that you didn't fight the count. You fed it, on schedule, in order, and in exchange the farm stood, the wells stayed sweet, and the thing stayed politely inside the plaster where it belonged.

Aldous should have signed years ago. Aldous had been meant to sign. And then Aldous had gone into the loft one October night to look at the bell, and come down different, and left in the spring without packing, and the county said missing and Maren's heart said *taken*, though taken implied unwillingness, and the ledger suggested otherwise.

So the count had waited. It was patient the way winter was patient. And now Maren's lungs were closing like a shop at the end of the day, and the papers were drawn, and somebody had to hold the pen.

Not Della. Della had babies and a mortgage and a laugh that filled rooms; the count would eat her alive and enjoy it. Not Bram, who was ninety percent bourbon and ten percent grudges and would sign out of spite and doom them all. It had to be someone strong enough to carry it, and quiet enough to live alongside it, and — here was the part she turned over at 3 a.m. like a stone with something underneath — someone whose absence the family could survive.

Silas had already practiced disappearing. All she was asking him to do was do it *here*.

"Love means choosing someone strong enough," she said aloud, to the empty kitchen, and the radiator ticked in a way that might have been agreement and might have been laughter, and she chose to hear agreement, because she was eighty-one and entitled.

* * *

Silas found the biscuit tin on day four, and he wanted it noted, for the record he was increasingly unsure anyone was keeping, that he had *not* been snooping. He'd been chasing a mouse smell into the pantry — actual mice, blessedly mundane mice, the first normal thing in this house — and the tin was directly above the fresh handprints, and his arm was long, and curiosity was a pest too.

He expected biscuits gone to fossil. He got a ledger.

He knew better than to open other people's books. He opened it anyway, standing on a step stool in the cold pantry, flour dust drifting down around him like slow snow.

Names. So many names, all Underhill, marching backward past anyone he'd ever heard of. Dates. And after each date, a small deep stroke, like a door being shut.

His aunt's name. No stroke yet.

And beneath it, in handwriting he recognized from birthday cards and the one letter she'd sent him at twenty-two that he'd never answered:

*Underhill, Silas.*

The pantry got very cold, or he got very small, or both. He read his own name four times, hoping it would resolve into something else — a cousin, a typo, a different Silas, any Silas. It didn't. It sat there in wet-looking ink, patient as the handprints above his head, and somewhere in the walls — he would swear this later, under his breath, to a recorder — something shifted its weight, the way a person does when they've been waiting a long time and have finally heard footsteps.

He put the ledger back exactly as he'd found it. He climbed down off the stool. He stood in the dark pantry breathing flour dust and thirty-one years of cowardice.

There was a question he'd been carrying since he was fifteen, since the counting under the floorboards, since Aldous. He'd never asked it because asking made things real, and real things could see you.

He asked it anyway, barely above a whisper, to the walls:

"Why me?"

The house considered. A pipe knocked, twice, somewhere far off — but not like plumbing. Like knuckles. Like patience.

And then, faint and reasonable and horribly gentle, from a direction with no direction, a second voice — dry as paper, warm as a hand on your shoulder — said:

"Because you were always going to say yes, Silas. We've simply been waiting for you to catch up."

Upstairs, in the guest room, his recorder's red light blinked on by itself, three hours ahead of schedule, and began to listen.
