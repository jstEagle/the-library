## 2. The Door Sealed From Inside

The excavator's bucket hit something at 6:47 in the morning, and Emrys knew it before she heard it — felt it as a change in the floor of the hospice, a low tremor that traveled up through her shoes and settled somewhere behind her sternum like a swallowed stone.

She was standing at the nurses' station with a paper cup of vending machine coffee going cold in her hand. Through the east windows she could see the expansion site: the fenced rectangle of churned earth where the new wing would rise, and beyond it the yellow arm of the excavator frozen mid-swing, its bucket resting against something pale that had no business being down there.

Basalt didn't belong in Lior. The town sat on limestone and river clay; everyone knew that. Everyone had known it for two hundred years, which was exactly how long the founding families had been dead.

Emrys set down the coffee and told herself the tremor was settling soil. She told herself the pale shape was a glacial erratic, dragged south by ice ten thousand years ago, nothing but geology doing what geology did. She had gotten very good at telling herself things. Seven years of hospice work would do that — seven years of hearing the dying count backwards from ten in their last hours, always backwards, never forwards, and explaining it away as oxygen-starved brains reaching for order, for ritual, for the comfort of a countdown they'd learned from New Year's broadcasts and rocket launches. Seven years of logging it in her head as *coincidence* until the word wore smooth as a river stone and stopped meaning anything at all.

"Miss Vane." Dov Kessler stood in the doorway of the station, still in his hi-vis vest, his hard hat tucked under one arm like a hat he'd forgotten how to wear indoors. He was a broad man gone gray at the temples, with forearms scarred by thirty years of machinery, and right now his hands were not steady. "You're the administrator on duty?"

"I'm the night nurse who hasn't gone home yet," Emrys said. "What did you find?"

"That's why I came to you." He wiped his mouth with the back of his glove. "We broke ground for your new wing. We found a door."

"A door."

"Underground. No frame, no wall around it — just the door, standing in the dirt like it grew there." His voice had the flatness of a man reciting something he'd rehearsed so it wouldn't come out shaking. "Basalt. Blacker than anything I've ever pulled out of the ground. And there's no handle, Miss Vane. No hinges on the outside, no ring, no bolt. Nothing to open it *with*."

"Then maybe it's not a door."

"It is." He said it with terrible certainty. "Because we looked at the edges, and the edges tell you everything about a door. This one's been shut from the inside. There are gouges in the stone — fingernail gouges, if stone took fingernails — all of them dragging *inward*. Toward the seam. Like somebody on the other side pulled it closed behind them and then scratched at it anyway. Like they changed their mind after."

The coffee cup was cold in Emrys's hand. She became aware that she could hear the hospice around her: the hum of the med room refrigerator, the tick of the wall clock, someone's television murmuring through a door left ajar. Ordinary sounds. She held onto them.

"Has anyone touched it?" she asked.

"We put a tarp over it." Dov almost laughed, and the laugh came out wrong. "I don't know why. It just seemed like the thing to do. Cover it up and call the county and let somebody else decide what it is." He paused. "There's something else. When we uncovered it — before the tarp — every man on my crew heard something. Same thing, all of us at once. Like a voice under the ground counting. Backwards."

The clock ticked. The refrigerator hummed.

"From ten," Emrys said quietly.

Dov stared at her. "How'd you know that?"

*Seven years of dying people*, she thought, and did not say it. "Lucky guess," she said instead, and hated herself for it, because the habit of explaining away had become so deep it now operated without her consent, like a reflex, like breathing.

* * *

By afternoon the county archaeologist had arrived, a brisk woman named Okafor who photographed the door through the gap in the tarp and pronounced it "pre-colonial, possibly pre-Columbian, absolutely inexplicable," and scheduled a proper excavation for Monday. The crew was sent home for the weekend. The site was fenced and locked.

That was when the counting started.

Emrys was doing rounds at nine o'clock when she passed Room 4 and heard old Henrik Lund counting under his breath — *nine, eight, seven* — in a voice too strong for a man three days from death, a voice with an odd doubled quality, like two speakers slightly out of phase. She stepped into the doorway. Henrik lay with his eyes open, fixed on the ceiling, his lips moving.

"Henrik. You're okay. I'm here."

He didn't look at her. "*Six.*"

Down the hall, in Room 7, Ruth Okonkwo's daughter burst out into the corridor, white-faced. "My mother — she's counting, she won't stop, she's been asleep for two days and now she's *counting*—"

And in Room 2, and Room 9, and across town, Emrys would learn later, at the county hospital forty minutes away, in the VA facility upstate — every dying patient in the region had opened their eyes or hadn't, had begun to speak or only mouthed it, but all of them were saying the same thing in the same doubled voice, descending together, in unison, like a congregation.

"*Five. Four.*"

Emrys walked the halls with her heart slamming against her ribs, checking each room, and the rationalizations rose in her like floodwater and she drowned them one by one. *Shared delusion* — no, they weren't in contact, some of them couldn't have been. *Coincidence* — the word had worn through; there was nothing left underneath. *Something under the ground has started counting, and the dying can hear it first.*

"*Three.*"

In Room 4, Henrik turned his head. Not toward her — toward the east wall, toward the excavation site, though he couldn't possibly have seen it through the plaster and insulation and dark. His eyes were clear in a way dying eyes shouldn't be. Grateful, almost.

"*Two,*" said every room at once, and the sound moved through the building like water through a sponge, and Emrys stood in the middle of the corridor with her hands pressed flat against her thighs so no one would see them shake.

They stopped at one. None of them died. That was the worst part, she realized later — none of them died that night. They simply fell silent, all together, and lay looking at the ceiling with those clear, patient eyes, waiting for whatever came next.

* * *

Dov Kessler came back at midnight. She found him sitting on the loading dock steps behind the hospice, hard hat in his lap, and he didn't startle when she pushed the door open. As if he'd been waiting for her specifically.

"You heard it," he said.

"I heard my patients."

"My crew hears it too. All day, even after we left the site. Even at home." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Petra says it's loudest in her kitchen. Tomás says he can't sleep unless the TV's on. It's not sound, exactly. It's more like — you know when a truck passes and the windows buzz? It's like that, but inside your skull. A number, buzzing. Right now it's sitting on *one*. Just holding there. Like it's waiting for permission to start again."

"Dov—"

"I know how this sounds." He looked up at her, and in the sodium light of the loading dock his face was older than it had been that morning, aged by a single day. "I've already called the state line. They'll send someone in a week. My guys can't wait a week. So I'm asking you, because you're the one person in this town who didn't look at me like I was crazy this morning. You knew. Before I said it, you knew it counted backwards from ten. How?"

The wind moved through the chain-link fence around the excavation site, and beneath the wind, faint as a pulse in the ear, Emrys heard it: *one*. Sustained. Patient. A held note at the bottom of the world.

She thought about lying. The reflex fired — *stress response, infrasound, mass suggestion* — and she watched the explanations pass through her mind like cars passing a wreck, none of them slowing down anymore. Seven years of smoothing things over, and the smooth surface had finally cracked clean through, and what looked back at her from underneath was simple and cold: the counting had been here all along. She had spent her career at the edge of it, catching its echoes in the dying, filing them away, keeping the secret without knowing she was keeping it. And now something had been dug up, and the secret wanted out.

"My patients count backwards," she said. "When they're close. For seven years. Always from ten. I told myself it meant nothing." She laughed, and it sounded nothing like a laugh. "I'm very good at telling myself things."

Dov nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something he'd already decided. "There's a reason you know, Miss Vane. There's a reason it stopped at one tonight instead of starting over. And there's a reason—" he hesitated, then committed, "—a reason my whole crew swears the counting gets quieter when you walk past. Petra noticed it first. Said it's like the numbers duck their heads when you come into a room."

The held note beneath the wind seemed, for a moment, to lean toward her. Listening.

"Go home, Dov," Emrys said. "Keep your crew off the site. I need to make some calls tomorrow — to the county archive, to my family, if there's anyone left to call." She wrapped her arms around herself against a chill that wasn't in the air. "I think my family might be the reason you should be asking me."

Behind the fence, under the tarp, in the dark, the number *one* waited — and Emrys understood, with the last of her rationalizations bleeding out, that it was not counting down to something.

It was counting down to *her*.
