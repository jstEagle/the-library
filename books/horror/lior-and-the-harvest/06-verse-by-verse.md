## 6. Verse by Verse

The recordings were Emrys's idea, and she hated herself for how long it took her to have it.

Seven years of hospice shifts meant seven years of charting. Every patient got an intake assessment, a care plan, and — because Emrys had once believed that dying people deserved to be heard, really heard, even when what they said made no sense — an audio log. She'd started it as a kindness. Let the families keep the voices. Let there be something left.

Marisol Vega had been in room 4 eleven days. Emrys had recorded her every evening.

She sat in the break room at 3 a.m. with her laptop and a pair of cheap earbuds, the county's dying quiet around her, and she played the file from the night before Marisol died. The old woman's voice was papery, serene, counting backwards from ten with the unhurried patience of someone reading a grocery list. Ten. Nine. Eight.

Emrys had heard it live. Hearing it again, alone, in the dark, was different. The numbers had a shape she hadn't let herself notice before. A rise here, a fall there. A phrase.

She dragged the file into the audio software she'd once used to edit a podcast for the hospice's fundraiser. She slowed it down. She looked at the waveform, and the waveform looked back — peaks and valleys arranged in a pattern too regular for speech, too melodic for counting.

Then she did the thing her hands did before her mind agreed to it. She hit *reverse*.

Marisol's voice played backwards, and out of the scrambled syllables rose a line of melody, clear as a hymn, in a mode Emrys had no name for but her body recognized the way a body recognizes a stair that isn't there. One note of it and the hair on her arms stood up. Two notes and her throat ached with the urge to follow.

She yanked the earbuds out so hard one went skittering under the vending machine.

The counting wasn't a countdown. It had never been a countdown. It was the song, played in reverse — the door's song, the founders' song, leaking out through the mouths of the dying because the dying stood closest to the seam. Marisol hadn't been counting down to death. She'd been singing the seal backwards, note by note, and the door had heard her, and the door had begun to open.

Emrys sat with her hands flat on the table, breathing, until the urge to hum faded to a dull throb behind her sternum.

Then she called Dov Kessler.

* * *

Dov came to the hospice at dawn with dirt still under his fingernails and a look on his face like a man who'd been awake for a week, which he had.

"You found something," he said. Not a question. His crew had stopped going down to the site. They sat in the trailers and drank coffee and didn't talk, because every time they talked, one of them started humming.

Emrys played him the reversed recording. She watched his face as the melody emerged — watched the recognition hit him like a physical blow, his shoulders dropping, his eyes going wet with something that wasn't quite grief.

"That's it," he whispered. "That's what the door hums. That's what's been — in our heads. All of us. For days." He pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. "Petra's been singing it in her sleep. We had to stop sleeping in the same trailer."

"There's a hymnal," Emrys said. "In the town archives. The founders' song. But the last verse is torn out — torn out on purpose, a long time ago. My grandmother's letters say the family kept the count. Kept the *door*." She heard her own voice go flat and clinical and let it. It was the only way to say it. "The verse exists. It's just been reversed. Marisol — the woman who died — she was singing it backwards without knowing. If we reverse the whole recording, all of it, every patient who ever counted—"

"We get the verse back," Dov said.

"We get the verse back."

He was quiet a long moment. "And then what? We sing it and the door shuts and everything goes back to normal?"

Emrys thought about her grandmother's letters. *The song is a door and a toll both. You cannot pass through it without paying.* She thought about the way the melody had hooked into her throat after two notes.

"I don't know," she said. "But I know we can't keep not knowing."

They started that morning. They had no idea yet what it would cost. They learned.

* * *

The first rehearsal was Emrys and Dov alone in the hospice chapel, which wasn't a real chapel, just a room with folding chairs and a window that faced the excavation site. They played the reconstructed verse through the laptop speakers, note by note, mapping it against the fragments in the hymnal until the torn page and the reversed recordings locked together like a bone resetting.

The completed verse was nine lines long. Nine lines, and the hymnal's instructions — written in a hand so old the ink had gone brown — were specific about the rest. *Many voices. One silence. The leader does not sing. The leader holds the count.*

"The leader," Dov read aloud, and looked at her.

"I know," Emrys said.

"You're the bloodline."

"I know."

They practiced the first line together before they understood that they shouldn't. Just two voices, quiet, in an empty room — a test, nothing more, a single line of melody to see if the reconstruction was right.

It was right. That was the problem.

The line came out of them and the room *warmed*, there was no other word for it. The air went soft and golden the way a kitchen goes soft when bread is baking, and Emrys felt something in her chest unclench that she hadn't known was clenched — seven years of grief and loneliness loosening all at once, a hand letting go of a rope it had been holding since she was a child. It felt like being forgiven. It felt like coming home to a house that had been waiting for her.

Dov made a sound she'd only heard from patients, at the very end.

Then the line ended, and the warmth drained out of the room like water through a cracked basin, and Dov sat down hard in a folding chair and stared at the wall and said, in a small voice, "Danny."

"Who's Danny?"

Dov looked at her. And Emrys watched it happen — watched him search for the answer behind his own eyes and find the shelf empty. His face went through confusion, then panic, then a terrible smooth calm as whatever was left of him papered over the hole.

"I don't know," he said. "I was going to say Danny and I don't know why. I had a brother." His voice cracked down the middle. "I had a brother. I know I had a brother. I can't—" He pressed his fists against his temples. "I can see a bunk bed. I can see a lake. I can't see his *face*. I can't see his *name*—"

"Dov." She took his wrists. "Dov. Look at me. It was the song. One line, and it took—"

"It took my *brother*." He wasn't crying. That was worse. He was just staring at her with the blank, scoured look of a man reading an empty room. "One line. The whole verse is nine. Emrys, what does the whole verse take?"

She didn't answer, because she didn't have to. They both did the math at the same time, sitting in the folding chairs in the fake chapel, and the math was simple, and the math was monstrous.

* * *

They got smarter. That's the horror of it — they got *good* at it.

They learned the song's appetite. It didn't take randomly; it took what the singer held closest, and it took it gently, so gently that sometimes the singer didn't notice until the shape of their life had a door-shaped hole in it. They learned to rotate singers, so no one voice paid twice in a row. They learned to write everything down — names, faces, birthdays, the small load-bearing facts of a self — because the song took memories and left the paperwork, and a person could rebuild a little from the rubble if the rubble was written down.

They recruited carefully. Petra came first, because she was already half-sung and wanted to finish the job before it finished her. Then two of Dov's crew. Then, to Emrys's private shame, she understood that she would need more, that nine lines sung by five voices would hollow five people entirely, and she looked out at the town that had been so warm to her, so hungry, and she began choosing.

They rehearsed in the hospice basement, behind the generator, where the sound couldn't travel. Line by line. Each rehearsal, a payment.

Petra sang the third line and forgot the smell of her mother's kitchen, and wept with frustration because she could remember that there had been a smell, could remember loving it, and couldn't remember what it was — a grief with no object, a phantom limb made of nothing.

Marcus, one of Dov's crew, sang the fifth line and forgot he'd ever been afraid of the dark, and reported it as a *benefit*, and Emrys saw in his face the first signs of what the door's warmth did to people when they let it in too far. He smiled too easily after that. He held eye contact too long. She crossed him off the list for the final verse and told herself it was strategy and not mercy.

And Emrys paid too. She couldn't help it — she was the bloodline, the melody lived in her like a second pulse, and every time they rehearsed she mouthed along before she could stop herself, and the song collected from her in small coins.

She forgot the name of her second-grade teacher. She forgot the taste of the lemonade her mother made in summers, though she remembered that there had been lemonade, that there had been summers, that her mother's hands had been wet to the wrist. She forgot which end of the house her childhood bedroom had been on. She forgot the joke her father told at every Thanksgiving, the one where everyone groaned, the one she'd have given anything to hear again — and then she forgot she'd ever had a father's joke to miss, and the absence closed over like water, and it was the closed-over part that frightened her most. The song didn't leave wounds. It left smoothness. You could only grieve what you could still feel the edges of.

She started writing everything down in a notebook she kept in her bra, against her skin. *My mother's name was Alba. She hummed when she cooked. I was born in the house on Corvane Street. I did not want to forget any of this. If I am warm and smiling and I tell you everything is fine, check the notebook.*

Dov found her one night at 4 a.m., sitting in the basement with the notebook open on her knees, reading her own childhood like a stranger's case file, mouthing the facts to press them deeper.

"How much is left?" he asked quietly.

She turned to a page near the back and showed him. She'd started keeping a tally. Not of what she'd lost — of what she had left. It was a shorter list every week.

"My mother's voice," she said. "I've been saving it. The way she sounded. It's the last big thing." She closed the notebook. "The song knows. I can feel it circling it. It's saving it too — for last, for the final line, like dessert." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, thin and glassy. "It wants me to have to choose, Dov. It wants me standing at that door with one line left and my mother's voice in my mouth, and it wants me to think, *well, I could just sing this one myself.*"

"And if you do?"

"Then the leader sings, and the seal fails, and everything we paid gets collected all at once." She looked at him — at the place where his brother used to be, at the smoothness where Danny had been. "The hymnal says the leader holds the count. It doesn't say what it costs the leader to stay silent while everyone else pays. I don't think the founders wrote it down because they didn't want us to know."

Dov was quiet. Then: "Petra had a dream again. The chamber. The ones inside." He rubbed his arms like he was cold. "She says there's a woman in there, conducting. Old. Hollowed out. Petra says—" He stopped. Started again. "Petra says the woman looked at her and said to tell the Quintana girl: *the last verse must be sung by many voices but owned by none. And the door knows your mother's voice, child. It's been practicing it.*"

Outside, above them, through the floor, through the earth, faint as a pulse, the hospice's newest patient had begun to count.

Emrys opened the notebook to a blank page and wrote, in careful letters she made herself slow down for:

*The last verse is tomorrow. Do not sing. No matter what it offers. No matter whose voice it wears.*

*Check this page first.*
