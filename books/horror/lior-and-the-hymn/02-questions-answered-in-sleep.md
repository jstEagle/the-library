## 2. Questions Answered in Sleep

The recorder was a compromise. That's what I told myself, anyway, standing in the master bedroom at nine p.m. with the little Olympus dictaphone in my palm like something I'd shoplifted from my own life.

I wasn't sleeping here. I was *monitoring*. There's a difference, and it lives in the language of invoices and liability forms. A surveyor monitors a property exhibiting anomalous conditions. A surveyor does not sleep in a house that grows a door every dusk.

But I'd stopped driving back to the motel in Ashford. Forty minutes each way, and the fuel was coming out of my own pocket because Dunmore & Associates is me, and me is a laptop bag and a laser measure and eleven thousand dollars of credit card debt shaped exactly like my mother's last six months of hospice care. So: monitoring. Lights on. Recorder running. Door wedged with a chair.

I set the recorder on the nightstand, said the date aloud for the file — "June ninth, interior acoustic baseline, Hymn House" — and lay down on top of the sleeping bag in my clothes.

The house did its inventory of me while I slept. I know that now. But that first night I just dreamed of water stains spreading across a ceiling in the shape of my mother's handwriting, and woke at 6:40 with a stiff neck and the specific dread of a man who has overslept in a place he shouldn't be.

The recorder had captured seven hours and twelve minutes.

I played it back in the kitchen, standing up, coffee going cold beside the previous surveyor's notes. For the first two hours there was nothing. My own breathing. The tick of the house cooling. At 11:53 p.m., a sound I logged as "settling timber" — a long, low exhalation through the walls, like the house clearing its throat.

At 12:04 a.m., a second voice began to speak.

* * *

**TRANSCRIPT — FILE 0609, HYMN HOUSE ACOUSTIC BASELINE**

**[00:00:00] SUBJECT (DUNMORE, L.):** June ninth, interior acoustic baseline, Hymn House.

**[02:41:17] VOICE 2:** He counts the doors again tomorrow. He will find five.

**[02:41:31] VOICE 2:** He will not write it down.

**[03:12:09] VOICE 2:** Ask him about the will.

**[03:12:14] VOICE 2:** No one asked. It doesn't matter. The answer is: she signed it on a Tuesday, and he was in the parking lot, and he heard the pen through the wall.

**[03:12:40] VOICE 2:** The debt is forty-one thousand, three hundred and six dollars. It was his father's before it was his. He tells people it's from the funeral. The funeral was four thousand. The rest went into a restaurant that failed before it opened.

**[04:58:22] VOICE 2:** Ask him about the fire.

**[04:58:23] VOICE 2:** He was nine. The shed. He knows what knocked over the lantern. He has never said it, not once, in thirty-one years.

**[04:58:30] VOICE 2:** We can hold it for him. That is what we are for.

**[06:59:44] SUBJECT (DUNMORE, L.):** [unintelligible — subject appears to be speaking in sleep]

**[06:59:51] VOICE 2:** Yes. That's right. Bring it down to us.

* * *

I listened to it four times.

The first time I stood very still and let the coffee go cold and thought, in the flat professional register I retreat to when the world misbehaves: *contamination. Infrasound. A radio picking up something.* The voice was neither male nor female, or both, or it sounded like a room full of people agreeing so completely they'd become one throat. It didn't echo. Rooms echo. This voice arrived already inside the microphone.

The second time, I noticed it answered questions. You could hear the shape of them in the pauses — the little intake before each answer, the way you lean in when someone finishes asking. But no question was ever voiced. Something was asking. Something on the other side of the recorder's range, or the other side of the wall, or the other side of me.

The third time, I cried. Briefly, angrily, standing in a dead woman's kitchen with my thumb jammed against the stop button like I could pin the sound down. Because the voice knew about the parking lot. It knew about the Tuesday. Nobody knew about the Tuesday. I had never told anyone that I'd sat outside the lawyer's office while my mother amended her will, that I'd heard the scratch of the pen through the drywall, that I'd gone in afterward and smiled and said *whatever you think best, Mom,* and meant it, and hadn't meant it, both at once, and that the will left the house to my cousin and left me the debts because I was, in her words read aloud by a stranger, "good with money."

The fourth time, I transcribed it. Word for word. Because I am a surveyor, and when a thing cannot be explained you measure it until it can, and if it can't be measured then you document it so precisely that whoever comes after you doesn't have to doubt their own sanity the way the last surveyor clearly had. His notes ended mid-sentence. I understood him better now. Some sentences you can't finish because finishing them means admitting what made you start.

* * *

That afternoon I drove into Ashford and spent three hours in the county historical society's basement, which smelled of paper and mouse, pulling everything they had on Hymn House and the family who built it.

The family name was Vael. They arrived in 1847 with money from somewhere no record would say, and they built the house over four years according to plans drawn not by an architect but by a composer — a man named Aldous Vael, whose surviving papers are mostly tuning charts. Not for instruments. For rooms. Ceiling heights specified to the quarter-inch. Corridor lengths annotated in frequencies. One marginal note, in faded iron-gall ink, that I copied out and have not been able to stop reading since:

*A house is a bell that has forgotten it is a bell. We will remind it.*

They were going to sing something in it. That much the fragments make clear. A hymn — the papers call it the Hymn of Accounts, which is such an awful, honest name that I laughed alone in the basement, once, badly. A rite to be performed upon an heir. A settling of inheritance. The Vael children and grandchildren were raised as verses; the house was raised as the instrument; and the ritual required a solstice, a chorus, and someone to be judged.

The file goes quiet after 1911. No deaths recorded. No sales. The house simply passes hand to hand among descendants who never seem to age in the photographs — the same narrow faces, the same folded hands, decade after decade, like a chorus holding one note.

And at the bottom of the folder, a single typed sheet from the county assessor, 1988, regarding a property tax dispute:

*Owners declined inspection. Stated the house "appraises itself."*

I sat with that for a long time under the basement's buzzing light.

Because here is what I had not written in any report, had not said aloud, had barely admitted in the privacy of my own skull: the numbers at Hymn House refused to reconcile. Every surveyor knows houses drift — humidity, settling, bad original work. This was different. The east wing measured longer after dark. The stairwell gained a riser between morning and noon. And the discrepancies weren't random. On my third day, I'd laid the floor plans over my measurements and found the errors accumulating in a pattern — and the pattern was a waveform. The house wasn't drifting. It was *resonating*, and what it resonated with, apparently, was me.

It had taken my inventory. Grief, debt, the fire — itemized, appraised, filed. Somewhere in those tuned rooms my mother's will was a line item. Somewhere a ledger held the true cost of a burned shed, entered thirty-one years late, accruing interest.

The voice had said *we can hold it for him.* Like a lien. Like a bank offering consolidation. And God help me, lying awake that second night with the lights on and the recorder running, part of me wanted to ask it the questions myself. Wanted to hear the parking lot explained. Wanted the arithmetic of my life done by something that never got tired, never judged — no. That was the trap. It judged constantly. That was the entire architecture. Judgment was the load-bearing wall.

The recorder's red light glowed on the nightstand. Down the hall, faintly, I counted doors. Four. Then, as the light outside went from grey to black, five.

At 3:12 a.m., the voice came back, and this time it did not wait for the unseen question.

"Come down," it said, through the little speaker, in a chorus of patient throats. "The undercroft is open. The verse that is you is nearly complete."

I did not sleep again. In the morning I packed my laser measure, my clipboard, my transcript, and my terror, and I went looking for the cellar door — because the house had finally told me where it kept the books, and some ruined, curious, indebted part of me needed to see the ledger with my own name in it.

The door was where no door had been yesterday. It was already open.
