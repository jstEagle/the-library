## 5. Village of Closed Doors

The morning was the color of old pewter, and August Voss walked into Lior's Hollow with her recorder in her coat pocket like a talisman, telling herself that a village was only ever a machine for keeping secrets, and machines could be opened.

She had dressed carefully. Good boots, the wool scarf her mother had—had someone given it to her? The thought snagged and slipped away, a fish turning under ice. She touched the scarf's fringe and moved on. It was warm. That was enough. Warmth was enough to know about a scarf.

The lane descended toward the square in long, shallow curves, past houses whose windows she had catalogued on her first day with a folklorist's tidy delight: leaded glass here, a carved lintel there, geraniums going rust-red in their boxes. This morning the geraniums were still there. Everything else had changed.

She heard it before she understood it—the particular wooden sound of a shutter closing. Not slamming; these were old shutters, patient shutters, drawn shut the way one draws a blanket over a sleeping child. As she passed the first house, the sound came from behind her left shoulder. At the second house, ahead of her and to the right. By the third she had stopped pretending not to notice, and stood in the middle of the lane listening to the village close itself around her like a hand folding into a fist.

An old man was still at his gate, too slow or too proud to have gone inside. He was mending a fishing net that did not need mending, his fingers moving through the mesh with the blind fluency of decades. August smiled at him.

"Good morning. Herr Brandt, isn't it? You sang at Marta's table on Sunday—I wondered if you might—"

"I don't sing," he said, to the net.

"You were singing. I heard you. It was lovely."

His hands stopped. For a moment he looked at her, and what was in his face was not hostility, which she would have known how to answer, but something worse: a kind of grieving apology, the look of a man watching water rise around someone who will not climb.

"Girl," he said quietly, "go home before noon."

Then he gathered his net and went inside, and his shutter closed too, and August stood alone in the lane with the smell of woodsmoke and cold stone and the growing certainty that she had walked out of the world she understood and into the space between verses of a song no one would finish.

* * *

The church sat at the head of the square, squat and dark-roofed, its bell tower leaning a degree or two east as if craning to listen. She had not been inside yet. Villages like this kept their histories in two places—the parish register and the graveyard—and she intended to search both.

The door was unlocked. Inside, the air was candle-wax and dust and something underneath both, faintly sweet, faintly wrong, like flowers kept too long in a closed room. Her footsteps rang off the flagstones. No one else was there—or so she thought, until a voice said, "You shouldn't have come," and she turned to find the priest standing in the shadow of the confessional, an old book open in his hands though he was not reading it.

"Father Anselm." She kept her voice level, professional. "I've been trying to speak with you since I arrived."

"And I've been praying you'd take the hint." He came forward into the thin light falling through the eastern window. He was younger than his voice suggested, maybe fifty, with a scholar's stoop and a face that had been handsome before worry ate the softness out of it. "You're still here, then. After Marta sang for you."

"She sang one verse of a lullaby. A fragment. Hardly—"

"It doesn't matter what she sang." He set the book down on the front pew with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down something he would prefer to throw. "It matters that she sang it twice in her life, and that you carried a machine in your pocket when she did. Do you understand what you've done? No. Of course not. You came here with your little theories and your little recordings, collecting us like pressed flowers."

"I came here to document a musical tradition that's dying," August said. "That's all. These songs are unique—the modal structures alone—"

"Unique." He laughed, and it was an ugly sound, brief and joyless. "Yes. Unique. Come." He beckoned her toward the small side chapel where the parish records were kept, and against every instinct she followed, because he was finally talking, and a folklorist follows the voice that talks.

He pulled a ledger from the shelf, blew dust from its cover, and laid it open on the reading desk. "You told Marta your name is Voss. August Voss. Your grandmother, you said, came from this region—from somewhere near here."

"From up the valley. The family name appears in records all through this century. That's partly why I chose—"

"Look," he said.

She looked. He turned pages for her, deliberately, letting her see them: baptisms, marriages, deaths, the spidery ink of a hundred years of ordinary lives. Müller. Brandt. Keller. Falk. Marta's own maiden name. Page after page after page.

No Voss. Anywhere.

"That can't be right," she said. "My grandmother—" And here the sentence struck the wall she had been walking toward all morning without seeing it. My grandmother what? Sang. Her grandmother had sung. She could almost hear it—a kitchen, steam on windows, a low voice carrying a tune with a strange flattened seventh, a lullaby, the same lullaby—

"Your grandmother," Father Anselm said gently, "sang songs from this valley. That much may be true. But her name is not in this book, nor in the civil registers, nor on any stone in our yard. Either your family never existed here, Miss Voss—or they existed here in such a way that we made certain no record of them survived." He closed the ledger. "And I was not yet born when that decision was made, and I have kept it faithfully, because the alternative is worse. Do you see? Even the lie is a kindness. Even the silence is a mercy. And you have come here with your machine, asking us to sing the mercy aloud."

August's hands were cold. "What happened to Lior's mother?"

The priest went very still. Outside, a shutter closed somewhere—she heard it distinctly, a small wooden syllable in the silence.

"There was no mother," he said at last. "There is no girl. There is a house at the end of the lane that the parish maintains out of charity, and a child we feed out of charity, and nothing else. If you write anything else in your notes, you are writing fiction." He picked up his book again, opened it, and fixed his eyes on it. "Go home, Miss Voss. Before noon, if you can manage it. Before the thing you woke learns your name properly."

"The thing I—" She stopped. Started again. "I recorded one verse. One."

"One verse," he agreed, without looking up, "is how it always begins. It begins with a verse, and then someone wants the rest of the song, and wanting is all it has ever needed. It cannot cross over by itself. It must be *wanted* across. Someone must stand at the glass and wish, with their whole heart, to hear the ending." His eyes lifted then, and they were the eyes of a drowning man watching another drown. "Has anything in that house asked you for the rest of the song?"

She thought of the hallway mirror. Of her blink arriving late, like a letter postmarked somewhere else. Of Lior's hand pressing hers flat against the cold glass, and beneath it—she had told herself she imagined it—another hand, pressing back.

"No," she lied.

Father Anselm nodded slowly, as if she had confirmed something. "Then go home," he said, "and pray it stays that way until you leave. And when you leave—and you must leave, do you hear me—leave the recordings. Burn them. Every one."

* * *

She did not burn them. She walked home instead, up the lane under a sky that had begun, very softly, to snow—not real snow, she thought, not yet the season for it, just a few flakes descending with the unhurried indifference of ash.

The village watched her go. She felt it the way one feels sun through a window: shutters she could not see, eyes behind them, the whole settlement holding its breath in unison. Once, passing the well, she caught movement in the water below—a pale oval, a face looking up at her from the depths—and leaned over, heart hammering, to find only sky reflected there, grey and empty and a half-second slow, she was almost sure, in rippling when the wind touched the surface.

Her grandmother's kitchen. She reached for it as she walked, deliberately, the way one probes a sore tooth. Steam on the glass. Flour on a wooden board. A voice singing. But the voice had no face attached to it now, and the kitchen had no walls, and when she tried to summon her grandmother herself—her name, her hands, the color of her hair—there was nothing. Only the tune. The tune remained, perfect and complete, every note of it, hanging in her memory like a coat on a hook with no one left to wear it.

That's not how memory works, she told herself. Memory decays at the edges first. Names go, then faces, then—

Then what order was it, exactly? How did she know the order? Had she studied this? She tried to remember studying it. She had a doctorate. Didn't she? There had been a university, an advisor, a dissertation on—on—

On songs, she thought desperately. Songs they won't sing twice.

The house appeared at the end of the lane, and she stopped in the road and looked at it for a long moment, because some part of her, some animal part beneath the unraveling scholar, did not want to go inside. Smoke should have been rising from its chimney. It wasn't. The windows were dark. The door stood slightly ajar, breathing out a thread of warmth that smelled of candle wax and, underneath, faintly sweet, like flowers kept too long in a closed room.

"Lior?" she called, and her voice sounded strange to her, thinner than it should be, as if she were hearing it through glass.

No answer. Of course no answer. The girl didn't speak. The girl had never spoken. Why did she keep calling for her as if—

August stepped inside and closed the door behind her, and the latch clicked home with a sound like the last note of a verse.

In the hallway, the mirror waited. She made herself walk past it without looking, made herself hang up her coat, made herself put the kettle on with hands that wanted to shake and were not permitted to. Rituals of the ordinary. She had read about this—in a book, in a life, somewhere—how people hold themselves together with small repeated acts.

It was while the kettle heated that she took out her field notebook, meaning to write everything down—Anselm, the register, the missing name, the shutters—so that the facts would exist outside her, in ink, where whatever was happening could not reach them.

She opened the notebook to yesterday's entry and found her own handwriting describing a conversation she had no memory of. *Marta agrees to sing tomorrow,* it said. *One verse only. She is frightened. Asked me twice to promise I won't ask for more.*

Marta had already sung. Hadn't she? Or was that tomorrow?

August turned back through the pages, faster and faster. The entries were all there, dated, signed with her initials—but the handwriting drifted as it went backward, loosening, simplifying, until the earliest pages were in a script she did not recognize at all, rounder, slower, a child's careful letters. Yet the initials were hers. A.V. A.V. A.V., all the way down, in hands that had never been hers.

The kettle began to scream.

She did not remember putting it on the stove. She did not remember coming downstairs. She was standing in the parlor, in fact, facing the great mirror above the cold fireplace, and her reflection was already there waiting for her, kettle silent in its hand, though in her own hand the screaming went on and on.

"Who are you?" she whispered.

Her reflection mouthed the words back at her—one second late—and then, before the echo finished, it smiled.

She had not smiled.
