## 1. The Counted Doors

The bus to Hollowmere ran once a day, and it was late.

Noor Fairweather watched the town assemble itself out of the October fog the way a photograph develops—slowly, in grays first, then color. A water tower with rust bleeding down its legs like old tears. A main street of brick storefronts, half of them papered over. A church whose steeple leaned a degree or two off true, as if the whole building had been listening to something and tilted its head.

*Hollowmere: A Good Place to Grow,* said the sign at the town line, though someone had scratched out *Grow* and no one had bothered to repaint it.

Noor had taken substitute assignments in eleven towns in six years. They knew the taxonomy by heart. There were towns that were embarrassed to need you, towns that resented you, towns that forgot you existed before your first coffee break. Noor had learned to be legible and forgettable at once—a trick that came naturally to someone who had spent thirty-one years being read wrong at every glance. Substitute teacher. Ma'am. Sir. The name on the roster that never matched the face. You developed a certain fluency in not-quite-belonging.

Hollowmere felt different from the start, though Noor couldn't have said how yet. It wasn't hostility. It was attention—the wrong kind. As the bus rolled down Main Street, curtains shifted in three windows in sequence, left to right, like a wave at a stadium. By the time Noor turned to look directly at any one window, the curtain behind it was already still.

They collected their duffel bag and walked the last quarter mile to Hollowmere Elementary, because the school secretary had said the rental house wouldn't be ready until evening and Principal Merrick wanted to meet them "before the light goes."

That phrase stuck. *Before the light goes.* Not before dark. Not before five. Before the light goes—as if the light were an animal that might bolt.

---

Principal Aldous Merrick was a tall man built like a folded chair, all angles stored away for later use. His office smelled of lemon polish and, beneath it, something older—dust and paper and the faint sweet rot of a basement. He shook Noor's hand with both of his, which always struck Noor as either excessive warmth or a way of making sure you couldn't pull free quickly.

"Miss Fairweather." He'd read the file. They could tell by the pause, the small recalibration behind his eyes, the way he landed on *Miss* and then, hearing nothing objectionable, simply let the word sit there rather than correct himself. Noor let it sit too. Picking the hill to die on was a survival skill; this was a molehill, and the day was long.

"Noor is fine," they said.

"Mr. Voss resigned suddenly," Merrick said, guiding them down a hallway lined with children's artwork—paper pumpkins, handprint turkeys, a crayon rendering of the school itself with every window colored black. "Family matter. Very sudden. You'll be covering his fourth grade through the end of term."

"Did he leave lesson plans?"

"He left everything," Merrick said, and for just a moment his voice did something strange—went flat and careful, like a man stepping around ice. "Everything exactly where it should be."

The classroom was Room 4, second door on the left. Noor noticed, without deciding to notice, that Merrick touched each doorframe as they passed it. Not knocked. Touched. Fingertips dragging along the wood, a gesture so habitual it had worn smooth, the way a stair rail wears smooth under ten thousand hands. When Noor glanced back, they saw he'd done it to every door since the office. One, two, three, four. His lips moved faintly on the last one.

"Something wrong?" Noor asked.

"Hm? No." He smiled. It was a good smile, well maintained, like the rest of him. "Old habit. Counting."

"Counting what?"

"The doors." He said it as if it were the most ordinary sentence in the world, and perhaps in Hollowmere it was. "We've got nineteen interior doors in this building. Twenty with the boiler room, but nobody counts the boiler room." A small laugh, shared with no one. "You get to know a building by its doors. Like faces."

Room 4 was clean. Too clean, maybe—twenty-eight desks aligned with military precision, the chalkboard washed, Mr. Voss's lesson plans stacked in a neat binder labeled *OCTOBER* in handwriting that pressed hard enough to emboss the page. On the wall beside the door hung the class photo from the previous spring: twenty-seven children arranged on bleachers, squinting into sun.

Twenty-seven children. Twenty-eight desks.

Noor counted twice, standing there while Merrick talked about lunch schedules and fire drills. Twenty-seven faces. Twenty-eight desks. And between the second row and the third, a gap in the arrangement—not a missing desk, but a space, as if one had been removed and the others had been nudged apart to hide the fact, badly, the way people nudge things.

"Was there a transfer?" Noor asked, nodding at the photo. "Looks like there's room for one more."

Merrick followed their gaze. Something crossed his face—quick, practiced, gone. Noor recognized it because Noor had spent a lifetime wearing its cousins: the flicker of a person deciding what you're allowed to know.

"Class sizes fluctuate," he said.

"That's not quite what I asked."

"No," he agreed pleasantly, "it isn't." And then, as if rewarding honesty with honesty of a very small denomination: "Mr. Voss kept a desk for a student who moved away. Sentimental habit. Nothing more."

"Which student?"

"You'll find teachers are a sentimental lot in Hollowmere." He checked his watch—an old wind-up thing, heavy gold, the kind that outlives its owners. "I'll walk you out. Light's going."

---

The rental house was on Corvus Lane, at the edge of town where the streetlights gave up and the woods took over. Edith Fairweather's house—Noor's grandmother's house, left to Noor in a will that named no other heirs, because there were no other heirs. Edith had grown up here. Edith had died in March, ninety-one years old and sharp as broken glass to the end, and in her final phone call she'd said, *Go look at the house, Noorie. Go look at it before they tidy it.*

Before they tidy it. Noor hadn't understood then. Standing in the front hall now, coat still on, they understood only slightly more. The house was tidy. That was the unsettling part. Dust sheets over the furniture, floors swept, a vase of dried flowers on the table that someone had refreshed recently—someone local, some neighbor, keeping the place presentable. Presentable for whom?

Noor unpacked minimally: toothbrush, charger, the framed photo of Edith they'd brought from the apartment. In it, Edith sat on this very porch, maybe sixty years old, unsmiling in the way of women who had decided smiling was a tax they no longer paid.

Outside, the light went. Not gradually—all at once, like a switch, the fog swallowing the last gray and pressing darkness against every window. Noor stood in the kitchen with a sandwich and listened to the new sounds of Hollowmere: wind in the pines, a dog barking twice and then thinking better of it, and underneath everything, so faint it might have been imagination, a rhythmic creak from the direction of the school. Like a door opening. Like a door closing. Opening. Closing.

At seven o'clock exactly, headlights swept across the kitchen curtains.

Noor went to the window. Below, on the sidewalk, stood Principal Merrick beside a car with its engine running. He was looking up at the house—at Noor's silhouette in the window—and he raised one hand, not quite a wave. More of a confirmation. *There you are. Still there.*

Then he turned and walked the length of Corvus Lane, and Noor watched him do it, and the skin on their arms tightened into gooseflesh, because Aldous Merrick was touching the fences as he passed. Every fence post. Every gate. Counting his way home through the dark, lips moving, a man taking attendance on a town.

---

Noor didn't sleep well, which was normal, and dreamed of doors, which was not.

In the dream they stood in a hallway much like the school's, except the hallway was longer than any building could hold, and there were doors on both sides, dozens, hundreds, and behind each door a child's voice saying a name over and over—but when Noor reached for a doorknob, the name vanished from their mind like water from a fist. They woke at 3 a.m. with their own name half-dissolved on their tongue, whispering *Noor, Noor* just to feel it stay put.

Morning came up gray and honest. Noor made coffee in Edith's percolator, found bread in a breadbox that someone had stocked, and walked to school through fog that clung like a held breath.

The fourth graders of Room 4 were, by and large, exactly what fourth graders are everywhere: loud, funny, secretly kind, armed with an unerring radar for substitutes. Except.

Except for the girl in the third row, Wren Okafor, who watched Noor the whole first hour with the flat, evaluating stare of a much older person. At recess duty, she appeared at Noor's elbow near the fence.

"You're the sub," she said.

"I'm the sub."

"For Mr. Voss."

"For Mr. Voss."

Wren looked out at the playground, at the children shrieking over a game of tag, and her voice dropped into a register no nine-year-old should own. "He counted the desks every morning," she said. "Out loud. Before anyone got here. I heard him once. He counted twenty-eight, and then he counted again, slower, and then he stopped coming to school."

Noor looked down at her. "What happened after he counted?"

Wren shrugged in a way that fooled no one. "Nothing happens," she said. "That's the whole point of this town. Nothing ever happens here." She kicked at the dirt. Then, without looking up: "There's a picture inside. By the door. Did you see it?"

"The class photo?"

"Don't count the kids in it," Wren said. "And if you do count them—" she finally raised her eyes, and they were dark and steady and afraid in a controlled, rehearsed way, the fear of a child who has learned that showing fear gets you corrected— "don't tell anyone what number you get."

She ran off to the tag game before Noor could answer.

---

That evening, Noor stayed late in Room 4 grading spelling tests, and the building emptied around them floor by floor, the janitor's radio fading, the buses sighing away. At 5:40 they packed up and stepped into the hallway—and stopped.

Aldous Merrick stood at the far end of the corridor in the failing light, facing the wall, his hand resting flat against a doorframe. He wasn't moving. He was standing the way people stand in churches.

One, Noor thought. Two. Watching his shoulders, they could see the count move through him like a swallowed pulse. Three. Four. He reached Room 4's doorway and paused, fingers on the frame, and for a moment his eyes closed, and his mouth shaped a word too quiet to carry.

Then he opened his eyes, saw Noor, and smiled his maintained smile.

"Long first day," he said. "They're always long. You'll want to be off before full dark, Miss Fairweather. Town ordinance, more or less." He said it lightly. Everything about him was light. That was the horror of him, Noor was beginning to understand—he was a man made entirely of pleasant surfaces, and somewhere underneath all that pleasantry was a counting that never stopped.

"Principal Merrick," Noor said, "the class photo. The gap between the rows. Who sits there?"

The smile held. The eyes above it went somewhere else entirely—somewhere old and cold and patient.

"Nobody," he said. "Nobody sits there. That's rather the point."

He touched the doorframe once more, almost tenderly, and walked away into the dark, counting.

Noor stood alone in the hallway of Hollowmere Elementary, invisible in the way they'd been invisible their whole life, and understood—with the particular clarity of the perpetually overlooked—that they had just been warned. Politely. Gently. The way you warn someone you expect to listen.

They went back into Room 4 and stood in front of the class photo, and despite Wren's instruction, despite every instinct honed by eleven towns and thirty-one years of knowing when not to look—

they counted.

Twenty-seven children squinting into the sun.

And in the gap between the rows, where no desk stood and no student stood, the light fell in a shape. A small shape. Shoulder-height for a ten-year-old. As if the sun itself were remembering someone, and the photograph had simply done what photographs do.

Kept the proof.
