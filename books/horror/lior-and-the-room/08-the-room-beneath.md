## 8. The Room Beneath

The school at night was a different animal.

Noor had taught in it for three weeks and thought they knew its moods—the radiator's asthmatic knocking, the way the hallway lights buzzed like trapped wasps, the smell of floor wax and old paper that never quite left the air. But Hollowmere Elementary after dark was a held breath. Every classroom door stood open, and Noor counted them as they passed, the way Principal Voss did each evening, and understood for the first time that he wasn't checking that they were locked.

He was checking that they were all still there.

The ledger rode against Noor's chest in a canvas satchel, wrapped in Edith's child's coat, and it was warm. It had been warm for two days now, ever since Noor had read the first page. Body-warm. Like something sleeping, or something pretending to.

*The room beneath,* Edith's last letter had said. *We took it out of the room beneath. There's a door in the boiler corridor that isn't a door. You'll know it because you'll want to walk past it.*

Noor wanted to walk past it.

The boiler corridor ran along the school's lowest level, a concrete throat lit by caged bulbs every twenty feet. Noor walked it once, twice, three times, and each pass their eyes slid off a stretch of wall between the furnace room and the janitor's closet—a stretch of nothing, a gap in attention, the way you never see your own nose. On the fourth pass, Noor stopped walking and simply stood there, staring at the place where looking away happened.

It was painted the same gray as every other wall. It had the same scuffed baseboard, the same hairline crack running down like a vein. And it was, unmistakably, a door. Once Noor saw it, it was all they could see—the seam of it, the faint rectangle of it, the handle worn bright by hands that had gripped it for fifty years and then, one day, stopped.

*You'll want to walk past it,* Edith had written. *Don't. That's how it stays shut. It's been shut because everyone in this town agrees not to see it. That's all a door like that needs. Agreement.*

Noor set their hand on the handle.

It turned without resistance, without even a click, as though it had never once been locked—as though locking it had never been the point.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than the building went up.

Noor counted eleven steps and then lost the count, and the caged bulbs gave way to a light with no source, a pale diffuse glow like moonlight through fog, though they were three floors under the earth. The air was cold and mineral and old, and beneath it—faint, unmistakable—chalk dust.

The room at the bottom was larger than the school above it. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the walls.

They were papered with attendance sheets.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Class rosters going back generations, brittle brown paper and yellow legal pads and dot-matrix printouts, every one of them pinned or pasted or nailed to the walls in overlapping shingles, and every one of them bearing the same wound: a name inked out. Struck through. Erased down to the fiber in some cases, the paper thinned to translucence where a child's name used to be. Noor walked the perimeter slowly, reading what could still be read. *Hollis, T.* *Amara, J.* *Prewitt, D.* Some of the sheets were older than the school itself—ledgers, church rolls, a census page with a whole family struck from it in one long line.

In the center of the room stood the chair.

Plain wood. Kitchen-simple. The chair from the photographs, the chair from Edith's attic, the chair that had been assembling itself into Noor's family history for weeks. It sat alone in the middle of the concrete floor, facing the door, the way a chair faces a door when someone has been sitting in it, waiting, for a very long time.

On the seat lay a single sheet of paper. Noor didn't touch it. They could read it from three steps away, the pencil faint and childish:

*we kept our side. did you?*

Noor's throat closed. They thought of the silhouette in the oldest photograph, the shape that might have been a child, might have been several children, folded into one waiting form. They thought of Wren, whose name had begun to fade from the roster two days ago—*W* gone first, then the *r*, the name thinning like breath on glass—and who had come to Noor's classroom that morning and asked, in the flat voice of someone trying not to hope, "Am I still on the list? Can you check? I can't see my own name anymore, it's like it's under water."

Noor had checked. Noor had lied, or half-lied, or done whatever you called it when you said *you're right here, I see you* while knowing that seeing was no longer enough.

They set the satchel on the floor and unwrapped the ledger.

It came out of the child's coat like it had been waiting, and the whispers started immediately—the same whispers from Edith's house, but louder here, at the source, dozens of voices layered under the room's silence like water under ice. Noor knelt, opened the book to its first page, and that was when the stairs creaked.

* * *

They came down in their good coats, the way people come to a funeral or a church supper. Aldous Merrick first, silver-haired and straight-backed, followed by his sister Cordelia and behind them a half-dozen others Noor recognized from the harvest festival, from the diner, from the pews of the white-steepled church—faces Noor had catalogued as *kind*, *friendly*, *the woman who gave me extra pie*. Cordelia held a lantern, though the room made its own light. Aldous held nothing at all. He didn't need to.

"Noor Fairweather." His voice was gentle. It was the gentlest voice Noor had ever been threatened by. "You shouldn't have come down here alone."

"Then you shouldn't have made it so easy to find."

Aldous smiled, and Noor watched the smile the way they'd learned to watch everything in Hollowmere—for the effort in it, the seams. "This room is school property. Private property. You're trespassing."

"Am I?" Noor stood, keeping the ledger open in their hands. "Because from where I'm kneeling, this room is the only honest thing in the whole town. Every other building in Hollowmere is lying about what happened here."

"Nothing *happened* here," Cordelia said, and her voice cracked on the word, and Noor saw—genuinely saw—that she believed it, that the agreement not to see had calcified in her into something like faith. "Children transferred. Families moved on. Towns lose track of people. That's all this is."

"Then read it." Noor turned the ledger toward her. "Read one page. Just one. If nothing happened, one page won't hurt you."

Cordelia's eyes went to the page and slid off it, the way Noor's eyes had slid off the door, and Noor watched a woman in her seventies flinch from a piece of paper the way you flinch from a hot stove. Behind her, one of the pie women began quietly to pray.

Aldous sighed, and the gentleness fell away, and what was underneath was older and very tired. "Your grandmother was one of four children who broke into this room in October of 1974. She and her friends took the register and hid it, and for fifty years this town has been—*compromised*. The work stopped. The work that kept us—" He stopped. Chose again. "The work that kept us *whole*."

"The work that erased children."

"The work that kept the *town* whole," Aldous said, and his voice rose, and the lantern in Cordelia's hand trembled. "You don't understand what it was like in those years. The mill was dying. The winters were killing us. The founders found a way to make the town *want* to survive, and the price was the ones who didn't fit. The troublemakers. The strange ones. The ones nobody would miss." He spread his hands, and Noor saw that they were shaking. "It was arithmetic, Miss Fairweather. It was *mercy*, compared to what those children would have grown into."

"No," Noor said. "It was arithmetic. That part's true. But it wasn't mercy, and it wasn't fifty years ago, and that's what you're not going to say out loud."

Silence. The whispers in the ledger seemed to lean in.

"Wren," Noor said. "Wren Okafor. Thirteen years old. Their name started fading off your roster four days ago. So don't stand there and tell me this is history, Aldous. You're still doing it. You're doing it *right now*, to a child who trusts this town, because a substitute teacher started asking questions and your arithmetic needed balancing."

Cordelia made a small sound. One of the men behind her—Noor thought his name was Prewitt, and thought of the struck name on the wall, and felt the room rearrange itself around that thought—stepped back toward the stairs.

"The moon rises tomorrow night," Aldous said quietly. "Give us the book. Go back to your classroom, finish your three weeks, take whatever job you like in whatever town you like, and you will never think of Hollowmere again. That's not a threat. That's the kindest thing anyone in this room can offer you. We can make you forget, Noor. We've done it to better people than you."

"No, you haven't," Noor said. "You've done it to *gone* people. I'm standing right here. I see the room. I see the chair. I see every one of you, and I remember, and that's the one thing your arithmetic can't carry." They lifted the ledger. "You're asking for my permission. That's what all this is—the coats, the kindness, the trespassing. You need me to agree. That's how it works, isn't it? That's how it's always worked. The town agrees, and the door stays shut, and the names stay gone."

Aldous said nothing. His silence was an admission with a floor plan.

"Well," Noor said, "I don't agree. I don't consent. I don't grant permission—not to you, not to the town, not to whatever's been sitting in that chair waiting for my family to keep a promise. Take it up with the law if you want. Arrest me. But the law was never in this room, Aldous. This room is what the law was built on top of, and tomorrow night, when the moon comes up, I'm going to stand right here and read every name in this book out loud. All of them. Every child you spent."

"You'll destroy the town," Cordelia whispered.

"No," Noor said. "I'm going to *count the doors*. All the way down. And whatever's still standing when I finish—that's Hollowmere. That's the only version of this town that gets to keep its name."

The whispers rose. The chair, in the center of the room, creaked—once, softly, like something shifting its weight after fifty years of sitting still.

And on the wall behind Aldous Merrick, on a roster dated this September, in ink that was still wet, a name that had been fading for four days suddenly darkened by a single letter.

The *W* was coming back.

Aldous turned to look at it, and Noor watched fifty years of agreement break across his face like ice off a roof.

"Tomorrow night," Noor said, and opened the ledger to the first page. "You might want to sit down."
