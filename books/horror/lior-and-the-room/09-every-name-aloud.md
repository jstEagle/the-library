## 9. Every Name Aloud

The harvest moon came up the color of a bruise healing—amber at the rim, deepening to rust at its heart—and Noor stood in the room beneath the school with the ledger open on their knees and their voice already raw from the first hour of reading.

They had not stopped. That was the thing Noor would remember afterward, if they remembered anything: that they had simply not stopped. Not when the walls began to weep chalk dust. Not when the attendance sheets papering the ceiling peeled away one by one and drifted down like enormous grey moths. Not when the Merricks came down the stairs, all four of them, dressed for church.

Noor read.

*"Abigail Crane, entered 1974, withdrawn by order of the Benevolent Committee."* The words tasted like pennies. *"Reason cited: irregular."*

Above them, through the floorboards, they could hear the school settling on its foundations like an old dog circling before lying down. The chair sat where it had always sat, in the center of the room beneath the single bulb, and every name Noor spoke made it rock forward an inch and settle back, forward and back, like something breathing.

"Stop."

Elias Merrick's voice was kind. That was the worst part. It had always been kind—the welcome basket, the pie on the porch, the gentle warnings about the lower floor. Kindness as a load-bearing wall, holding up fifty years of silence.

"Noor," he said now, descending the last step with his hands open, palms out, a man approaching a frightened animal. "You don't understand what you're doing. Those names were removed *for their own protection*. Hollowmere was dying. The mills closed, the money left, and children were going hungry, going unloved, going—worse. The founders found a mercy. We kept it."

"You erased them," Noor said, and did not look up from the page. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. They had spent a lifetime practicing steady. *"Tobias Wren, entered 1974, withdrawn by order of the Benevolent Committee. Reason cited: orphaned, unruly."*

"The town survived," Elias said. "Every family here eats because of what was done in this room. You'd trade all of that—for *what*? For ghosts?"

"For people," Noor said.

Margaret Merrick moved then, quick as a heron striking water, and grabbed the ledger's edge. Her fingers closed on the page and Noor watched, with a strange detached clarity, as the skin of her hand went grey. Then translucent. Then fine as cigarette ash. She snatched her arm back with a sound Noor had never heard a human being make—not a scream, but the noise of someone discovering their own hollowness, knocking on a door and finding no house behind it.

"It's the moon," she whispered. "It's the reading. You're—you're unwriting *us*—"

"No," Noor said. "I'm writing you down."

They turned the page.

* * *

Here is what Noor understood, kneeling in the dust of the founders, and what they would never be able to explain to anyone who hadn't been invisible once themselves:

The ledger had never been magic. Magic was just the word small towns used for systems. A book of names, a committee of neighbors, a ritual of removal—attendance taken, attendance corrected, the record amended until the amendment became the truth. Every town has a version of it. Hollowmere's was only unusual in that it worked, and in that it required someone, every generation, to keep reading the corrections aloud. To keep saying *this child is not here, this child was never here*, until the saying wore grooves into the world.

Fifty years of corrections. And corrections, Noor knew from a lifetime of substitute classrooms and borrowed desks, can be reversed. All you need is the original text and a voice willing to read it.

*"Delphine Okafor, entered 1975—"*

The first of them stepped out of the wall.

Not through it. Out of it, the way a figure steps out of a photograph when you tilt the light right—a girl of maybe eleven, dark-skinned, in a corduroy coat two sizes too big, her outline soft at the edges like a pencil drawing left in the rain. She looked at Noor with an expression Noor recognized from their own childhood mirrors: the careful blankness of a child who has learned that being seen is dangerous, and who cannot quite believe it might be safe.

"I see you," Noor said, and kept reading. "Delphine Okafor. You were here. You are here."

The girl's edges firmed. Color rose in her face like dawn coming up over a field.

They came faster after that. As Noor read, the unwritten gathered in the room beneath the school—boys with cowlicks and scabbed knees, girls with names inked out so thoroughly the paper had thinned, twins entered together and withdrawn together, a boy listed under *reason cited: asks too many questions.* They stood in rows without being asked to, the way children do, facing forward, hands at their sides, waiting for instructions from whatever adult had finally come for them.

Noor's throat was bleeding somewhere. They could taste it. They read anyway.

* * *

The Merricks did not crumble all at once. That was the terrible grace note of it—they came apart slowly, politely, the way they had done everything. Elias sat down on the bottom step and folded his hands and grew lighter by degrees, his face smoothing, his edges blurring, until he was less a man than a memory of a man, and then less than that. Margaret stood at the center of the room arguing with the air about property values and tradition and *we meant well, we MEANT WELL,* her voice thinning to a frequency only dogs and the guilty could hear.

And through it all, the youngest Merrick—there had always been a youngest Merrick in every photograph, unchanged across fifty years, and Noor finally understood why—stood against the wall with her arms crossed, watching the children emerge, and wept without any tears, because whatever she was made of, it had run dry decades ago.

"You could stop," the youngest Merrick said. Her voice was a young girl's voice, worn smooth by repetition. "One name left unread and I stay. One. That's how it works. That's how it's *always* worked. Someone reads the corrections, and I keep the count."

Noor looked up from the page for the first time in hours.

The ledger lay nearly finished. Pages of names behind them, spoken and witnessed. And there—one entry, near the end, the ink still wet after fifty years, glistening like a wound that refused to close:

*Wren Halloway, entered 2024, pending withdrawal.*

Wren. Who had told Noor about the roster that loses people. Who had waited for them outside the archives with two sodas and a conspiracy theory delivered in the fierce whisper of a kid who knew exactly what happened to kids who talked. Whose name had begun fading from the attendance sheet three days ago, letter by letter, while Noor watched and raged and read.

"Pending," Noor said. "She was never fully unwritten. There's time."

"There's *mercy*," the youngest Merrick said, and her voice cracked down the middle, and underneath the crack Noor heard something ancient and exhausted and terribly lonely. "Do you know what I am? I'm the counting. I'm the ritual. Nobody made me—I *accumulated*. Every correction read aloud in this room for fifty years built me, and if you finish the book, if you say the last true name, there's nothing left to hold me together. I'll be—" She gestured at the ash that had been her family. "I'll be that."

Noor closed their eyes. Behind their lids they saw the extra chair in Edith's photographs, patient as a held breath. They saw Edith's letters, the trembling confession of a ninety-year-old woman who had stolen a book at fourteen and hidden it instead of burning it because some part of her had always known she'd have to give it back. *I was afraid,* Edith had written. *Being afraid is how they get you. Fear is the toll. Somebody has to pay it eventually.*

"Somebody does," Noor said aloud.

They lifted the ledger. The room tilted toward them—the children, the drifting attendance sheets, the moonlight pouring through the floor seams above like syrup—and Noor read the final entries standing up, because some things you do on your feet.

*"Wren Halloway, entered 2024, pending withdrawal by order of the Benevolent Committee."* Their voice broke on the next part and they let it break. "Withdrawal denied. Witnessed: Noor Fairweather. Present: everyone."

The name re-inked itself.

Noor saw it happen—saw the faded ghost-letters on the page darken stroke by stroke, the way a bruise blooms backward, healing into presence. Somewhere above them, three floors up, in a classroom with a fifth chair, an attendance sheet rustled though every window was shut.

The youngest Merrick made a sound like a bell falling down a well.

Then she wasn't a girl anymore. She was a count of fifty years of silence, and the silence was over, and she came apart the way fog comes apart when the sun insists—not violently, not even sadly. Just thoroughly. Just completely. The last of her was a sigh that smelled, impossibly, of chalk and cut grass and school lunches, and then the room beneath the school held only the living, the returned, and one plain wooden chair, rocking gently forward and back, forward and back, like something finally setting down its burden.

* * *

Noor climbed the stairs at moonset, holding the hand of Delphine Okafor, who had appointed herself first in line and would not let go. Behind them came forty-one children in coats from six different decades, blinking in the hallway light, hungry, confused, alive enough.

At the top of the stairs, silhouetted against the exit sign, stood Wren Halloway—solid, furious, clutching a flashlight and a tire iron she had clearly brought to do murder with, her eyes going wide as the children filed past her.

"Noor," she said. "What—*who*—"

Noor leaned against the doorframe. Their voice was gone; what came out was a rasp, barely a whisper, and it was the sweetest sound they had ever made.

"Attendance," they rasped. "Take it."

Wren stared. Then, slowly, she pulled a battered notebook from her jacket, clicked her pen, and held it ready as the first child stepped forward and said, in a voice fifty years out of practice, her own name.

Outside, the harvest moon touched the ridge line and began, at last, to set.

And in Noor's jacket pocket, folded around the empty space where the ledger used to live, Edith's final letter had changed. New ink. Fresh handwriting. Four words Noor would carry into the next chapter of everything:

*Thank you. Come home.*
