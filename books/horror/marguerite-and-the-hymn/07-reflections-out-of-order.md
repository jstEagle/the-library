## 7. Reflections Out of Order

I want to tell you that I slept after the humming started. I want to tell you I woke the next morning, made coffee, and filed the whole thing under *folklorist's overactive imagination*, which is a very crowded filing cabinet in my head.

Neither is true.

What actually happened was this: I lay awake until three in the morning with my recorder pressed to my chest like a talisman, listening to the walls of Rose Cottage hum a melody I had only ever heard once, sung wrong, by a dead woman's granddaughter's cassette. The humming was patient. It had no beginning and no end. It was the sound of something that had been waiting so long it had forgotten there was ever any point to hurrying.

At some point I must have drifted off, because I dreamed of water — brown river water, the kind that carries silt and secrets in equal measure — and woke at dawn with my mouth tasting faintly of mud.

The mirrors were still lagging. One second behind, faithful as ever, like a clock nobody had bothered to correct.

I told myself it would pass. I am very good at telling myself things. It is, professionally speaking, most of what I do.

* * *

By noon I had set up an experiment, because experiments are how I keep the screaming on the inside.

Here is the methodology, for the record: I positioned myself before the hall mirror with a metronome app open on my phone. Sixty beats per minute. On each beat, I performed an action — raised my left hand, touched my nose, said a word aloud. Then I watched the reflection and counted the delay against the metronome.

One second. Consistently. Every time. My reflection lived its life exactly one second behind mine, a woman trapped in the worst kind of rerun.

Except.

Except that at 12:47 in the afternoon, while I stood in the hall drinking cold tea and not looking at anything in particular, my reflection stopped lagging.

She caught up.

I know how that sounds. I know precisely how that sounds, because I have spent twenty years interviewing people who claim their grandmother's portrait blinked at them, and I have nodded sympathetically and written *possible pareidolia* in my notes. But there is a difference between hearing about a thing and standing four feet from it with your own tea going cold in your hand.

My reflection looked at me. Not the way reflections look at you — that flat, automatic attention, eyes meeting yours because geometry demands it. She looked at me the way you look at someone across a train platform when you're trying to remember where you know them from.

Then she smiled. And I wasn't smiling.

I did what any rational adult would do. I dropped the tea, swore comprehensively, and backed into the kitchen, where I stood breathing hard among the hanging pans and told myself that adrenaline plus sleep deprivation plus a house with bad wiring equals visual artifacts, Lena, honestly, you've read the literature.

When I crept back to the hall mirror, she was lagging again. One second behind. Innocent as anything. Raising her hand when I raised mine, a beat late, obedient as a shadow.

But her smile had lingered somehow. Reflections aren't supposed to be able to leave residue. That's practically the definition of one.

* * *

That evening I made a decision that I would like noted for posterity: I covered every mirror in the house.

This is easier said than done. Rose Cottage has seven mirrors, because whoever built it in 1840 apparently believed a household could not have too many opportunities to catch sight of itself. There was the hall mirror, tall as a door. Two bedroom mirrors. A vanity glass in the bathroom with a crack running through it like a lightning strike frozen mid-thought. A small round mirror in the parlour that had never once reflected anything useful. And two more I kept forgetting existed, discovering them the way you discover a splinter — always by surprise, always painfully.

I hung bedsheets over them. Towels. In one desperate case, a coat. The cottage took on the look of a house whose occupants had died suddenly and whose relatives couldn't face the rooms, which, thinking about it now, may have been closer to the truth than I intended.

It helped. For about four hours, it helped. The house felt like a house again instead of an audience.

Then, around eleven, standing in the kitchen making toast I had no intention of eating, I caught movement in the window above the sink.

The night outside was absolute — no moon, no streetlight, Hallowmere being the sort of village that considers light pollution a moral failing. The window should have shown me nothing but black glass and my own dim shape.

Instead it showed me the kitchen. Perfectly. Lit, detailed, every pan and counter edge crisp — reflected as though the darkness beyond the glass were a mirror itself, polished and waiting.

And in that impossible reflection, my mirror-self was talking to me.

Her lips moved steadily, deliberately, the way people speak to the deaf or the very slow. Her eyes stayed fixed on mine with an expression I can only describe as urgent patience — the look of someone who has explained something six times and is prepared to explain it a seventh.

I didn't turn around. Some animal part of me understood that turning around would be conceding something, agreeing to rules I hadn't negotiated. I stared at her mouth instead and read.

I'm good at reading lips. It comes with the trade; half of fieldwork is watching people decide whether to tell you the truth, and mouths confess before voices do.

Three words. Over and over, in a loop, her lips shaping them with terrible clarity:

*The last verse.*

*The last verse.*

*The last verse.*

"No," I said out loud, to the window, to her, to the house. "No, thank you."

Her expression flickered — frustration? grief? — and then the reflection simply became a reflection again: dark glass, my own pale smudge, the ordinary world reasserting itself like a tide coming in.

I ate the toast. It tasted of nothing. I don't think I blinked for the entire process.

* * *

Here is the thing I couldn't stop thinking about, lying in bed that night with a chair jammed under the doorknob and a towel nailed — literally nailed, I found a hammer in the shed and committed fully to the bit — over the bedroom mirror:

She hadn't threatened me.

If some malevolent intelligence had taken up residence in my reflection, the efficient move would be menace. Show me my own death. Mouth *get out*. Give me the classic treatment. Instead she'd repeated three words like a teacher drilling vocabulary, with all the urgency of someone passing along information she considered vital.

*The last verse.* The verse Tom Bracken couldn't finish. The verse Wren's grandmother's cassette replaced with humming. The verse the whole village arranged its life around never singing.

She wanted me to learn it.

I turned this over and over in the dark like a stone with something underneath it. Because there are two ways to want someone to sing a binding song's final verse, aren't there? Either you want them to complete the binding —

— or you want them to break it.

I fell asleep somewhere in that thought, which was a mistake, because dreams are where this house does its best work.

* * *

I dreamed of the churchyard. Not the churchyard as it is now — overgrown, neglected, its far corner surrendered to nettles and one moss-swallowed stone — but as it must have been in another century. New graves. Fresh-cut grass. And a crowd, a whole village of them, standing in their winter coats around a patch of disturbed earth, singing.

In the dream I knew the hymn. I sang it with them, my voice clear and young, and the words felt good in my mouth, righteous, the way hymns are designed to feel. We sang the first verse and the second and the third, and the crowd's voices swelled, and beneath us — I understood this with dream-certainty, the way you understand things in dreams without being told — beneath us something was listening. Something was being held down by the weight of our harmony, pressed into the earth note by note, and the singing felt less like worship than like a hand pressing a lid down onto a pot that wanted to boil.

We reached the final verse.

In the dream, I opened my mouth to sing it.

And every single villager turned to look at me at once. Dozens of faces, candlelit, expectant, and every one of them wore the same expression I'd seen in my reflection: urgent patience. *Go on,* they said, without saying it. *Sing it. Sing it so we never have to.*

I woke at 4 a.m. with my heart slamming and the taste of river water so strong in my mouth I got up and rinsed it three times, and even then it wouldn't leave. It sat at the back of my throat like a memory.

Through the wall, soft and unhurried, the humming had resumed.

* * *

I barricaded the bedroom properly the next night. I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the only currency I have left in this story: I dragged the dresser across the door. I wedged a chair. I put the hammer under my pillow next to the recorder, which tells you everything about which weapon I actually trusted.

The dresser bought me silence until roughly one in the morning.

Then the humming came through the wall anyway. Not louder — that's the detail that undid me. If it had grown louder, it would have been a siege, and sieges can be waited out. This was the same volume as before, the same gentle, tuneless-patient hum, entirely unbothered by the dresser, the chair, or my dignity. As if the sound had never been traveling through the walls at all. As if it had been inside them the whole time, the way sap is inside wood, and my barricade was a gesture made to a tree.

I sat up in bed with my knees pulled to my chest and listened.

And then the humming changed. It found words.

Not sung words — I never heard actual syllables, not quite. But the melody bent around gaps, pauses shaped like language, and if you let your ear fall into those gaps the way you fall into sleep, you could almost hear what fit there. Almost. The way you can almost see the bottom of a river before the current clouds it.

I got the recorder out from under the pillow and held it toward the wall with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be, and I recorded forty minutes of a house singing to me through plaster and lathe.

At dawn I played it back.

Static. Forty minutes of clean, unremarkable static, with one exception. At minute thirty-one, buried in the hiss, so faint I had to press the headphones hard against my ears and hold my breath:

A voice. A woman's voice, wet-sounding, waterlogged, each word arriving like something carried a long way upstream.

*"Learn it,"* she said. *"Before they make you carry it."*

Then static again, all the way to the end.

I sat on the floor of that barricaded bedroom with the headphones on and the morning coming grey through the curtains, and I laughed. Actually laughed — one short, cracked bark of it, the kind that escapes rather than expresses. Because here was the joke, and it was a good one, the kind folklore specializes in:

I had come to Hallowmere to record a song. And the song had come to record *me*.

* * *

I spent the day doing what I should have done twelve years ago: thinking about Ada.

Ada Merrick. Last living descendant of Marguerite, though she'd laughed when I used the word descendant — "descended from nothing, love, that family's been falling since 1811" — sitting in her kitchen in the blue dusk of an October evening, teaching me verses one through five while her kettle muttered on the stove. Nineteen years old, notebook balanced on my knee, tape rolling, thinking I'd struck gold.

She'd gotten to the sixth verse and stopped. Put the kettle on. Made a show of fussing with cups. And when I'd asked — casual, professional, idiotic — *what about the last verse, Ada?* she'd gone still in a way I'd recorded but never understood.

"Nobody sings that one," she'd said. "Not twice. Not once, if we can help it."

And that night Ada Merrick died. Drowned. In her own bathtub, the coroner said, an accident, a faint, perhaps. Twelve years ago tonight, near enough.

I fled the next morning with the tape in my bag and never played it. Told myself it was grief. Told myself it was respect. Told myself a great many things, the way I do.

But I've known for days now — since the mirrors, since the humming, since a drowned woman started teaching me through glass — that the tape is in the bottom of my field bag where it has always been. That I have carried the answer with me across twelve years and three hundred miles like a stone in my pocket.

Tonight I'm going to play it.

I've told myself I might not. I've spent the whole day telling myself that. But the walls are humming, and the towel over the mirror has slipped an inch at one corner, and through that gap my reflection is watching me write this with an expression I no longer mistake for menace.

She looks like a woman handing off something heavy.

She looks like Ada must have looked, setting down the kettle, deciding whether to tell me.

The tape is in my hand. The machine is loaded. Outside, the light is going, and the house is holding its breath, and somewhere beneath the churchyard, patient as water, something waits to find out whether I finally will too.

I'm pressing play.
