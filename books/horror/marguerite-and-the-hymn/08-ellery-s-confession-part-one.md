## 8. Ellery's Confession, Part One

I have been the vicar of St. Aldhelm's for thirty-one years, and in that time I have learned that the most dangerous thing a parishioner can do is ask a good question at the wrong moment. Bad questions are easy. Bad questions I can deflect with tea and talk of the roof fund. But a good question—precise, sincere, asked by someone who already half-knows the answer—that is a knife slipped between the ribs of a congregation, and all you can do is smile and bleed politely.

Miss Delacroix had been asking good questions for six days.

I watched her from the vicarage window on Thursday morning as she stood at the churchyard wall, notebook open, looking toward the far corner where the yew grows crooked over ground no one has mowed since before my ordination. She did not go in. That was the thing about her—she was patient in a way that frightened me more than urgency would have. Urgency can be managed. Patience has to be waited out, and I am seventy years old, and my waiting is nearly done whether I like it or not.

So I made the decision I had been avoiding since she arrived: I invited her to tea.

* * *

She came at three o'clock, wearing the same grey coat she'd worn to Tom Bracken's funeral twelve years ago. I remembered the coat. I remember everything about that night; it is the parish's particular curse to remember everything and speak nothing.

"Vicar," she said, shaking my hand. Her grip was cool and dry. "Thank you for the invitation."

"Thank you for accepting. Most people in Hallowmere would rather be flayed."

"I've noticed people here are careful with me."

"People here are careful with everyone. It's not personal." I poured the tea—china my late wife bought in Bath forty years ago, the only extravagance either of us ever permitted ourselves—and set out the shortbread Mrs. Petchey makes for the Mothers' Union. "Though in your case there may be some history involved."

Lena Delacroix sat very still. She had the stillness of someone who has spent her life listening to other people talk, which is a rarer gift than you'd think, and a more dangerous one.

"You know why I'm here," she said.

"I know what you told people you're here for. Folk music. Field recordings. A paper for a journal nobody reads." I smiled to take the sting out of it. "I also know that you went to the county records office on Tuesday, and that Mr. Hobbes the librarian telephoned me within the hour, because Mr. Hobbes telephones me about everything, including his hip."

"And you know what I found there. Or didn't find."

"The register," I said. "1811. Yes."

"You knew the pages were cut out."

"I have known that the pages were cut out since I was eleven years old, Miss Delacroix, when my predecessor showed them to me and told me that some knowledge is like a load-bearing wall—you don't pull it out to see what's behind it unless you're prepared to hold up the house yourself." I looked into my tea. "He said it with such satisfaction, that phrase. Load-bearing. As though ignorance were architecture. As though we'd built something worth holding up."

She leaned forward slightly. "Then tell me what's behind the wall."

And there it was. The good question. Asked gently, at the wrong moment, by the wrong person—or perhaps, God forgive me, by exactly the right person at exactly the right time, which is worse.

I am an old man and a coward in small ways and I will not pretend otherwise. But I have never lied at my own table.

* * *

"Her name was Marguerite Ashcombe," I said. "She came to Hallowmere in 1809, a widow, or calling herself one. She kept bees and sold honey and wax, and she sang. That was the whole of her crime, as far as I can establish. She sang—in the fields, at the well, in the lane outside the church during service, which offended the founders mightily. And she sang songs that weren't ours. Songs from downriver, from the coast, songs with old words in them that the founding families decided were... improper."

"Improper how?"

"Pagan, they'd have said. Papist, possibly. Really, I think, simply *other*. The founders were five families—the Brackens, the Hollises, the Merridews, the Catchesides, and the Vyes—and they had come here to build something pure, and purity, Miss Delacroix, is a very hungry virtue. It must be fed. And in the winter of 1811 the winter fed itself on Marguerite Ashcombe."

"What happened?"

"A child died. The Merridews' youngest. Fever, most likely—we had fever every winter—but the child had been seen at Marguerite's cottage two days before, taking honey for a cough. And the founders, who needed their purity fed and their grief given a shape, decided she had poisoned him. Or cursed him. The accusation changed depending on who told it, which is how you know an accusation is false. True accusations stay consistent. Lies have to keep improvising."

Lena's hand had gone to her throat. "They drowned her."

"In the mill race, on the fourth of February. There was no trial. There was a meeting, in the church, and then there was a procession, and then there was water. The register recorded her death—I cut those pages' ghosts myself, if you follow me; I mean the cutting was done long before my time, but the record exists in the diocesan archive, and I have read it. One line. '*Marguerite Ashcombe, drowned by consent of the parish.*' By consent. As though the whole village had voted on it. Perhaps they had." I set down my cup because my hand was not steady enough to hold it. "The child's father, Josiah Merridew, led her to the water himself. And within the year, the hymn was written."

"The Hymn of Marguerite."

"It wasn't called that at first. It was called the Peace-Song. The founders composed it—Josiah Merridew wrote the words, I believe, though he'd never admit authorship while alive—and they taught it to the village as a comfort, a memorial, a way of making peace with what they'd done. Except it isn't a memorial. Memorials remember. This one forgets. It doesn't honour her; it holds her. Every verse is a knot, Miss Delacroix. The melody winds and the words bind, and the final verse—" I stopped.

"The final verse," she said quietly, "is the lock."

I looked at her for a long moment. "You've been talking to Tom Bracken's granddaughter."

"I've been listening to a cassette tape."

"Ah." I closed my eyes briefly. Old Nan Bracken, singing into a machine in 1974, humming where she should have sung. Even her defiance had been dutiful. "Yes. The final verse is the lock. Sung whole, once, at need, it keeps her where the water put her. Sung twice, it frays. Sung wrongly, it frays faster. Sung completely by someone who does not belong to the parish—" I opened my eyes and found hers. "That, we do not know. We have never been willing to find out."

* * *

She was quiet for a while. Outside the window, the light was going amber and long across the churchyard, and I could see the crooked yew throwing its shadow all the way to the wall, reaching, the way it always seemed to reach, toward that unmown corner.

"You believe it works," she said at last. Not mocking. Genuinely asking. "The binding. You're a clergyman of the Church of England, Vicar. You say the creed every Sunday. And you believe a folk hymn keeps a dead woman asleep under your churchyard."

"I believe in God," I said, "and I believe God is very large and very patient and mostly elsewhere, the way a father is elsewhere when the children are fighting. What happens in Hallowmere happens locally. We made something here, Miss Delacroix. Five families made it, and every generation since has maintained it, and I have maintained it for thirty-one years, and I will tell you honestly: I do not know if it is prayer or machinery or simple guilt given a tune. But I know this. In 1811, after the drowning, things happened in this village. Water where water shouldn't be. Mirrors that lagged. Dreams of a wet woman standing at footboards. Children humming a tune none of them had been taught. And after the hymn was written and sung, it stopped. For two hundred years it stopped. Whatever else you believe, believe the chronology."

"And the cost?"

"The cost is that we sing a song once a year and never finish it in company, and we raise our children to think that's normal, and we bury our doubts under the yew along with everything else." I heard my own voice go thin and bitter. "The cost is that I have preached four hundred sermons about a God of truth while sitting on a lie the size of a woman's body. The cost is my brother."

Her head came up sharply. "Your brother?"

I should not have said it. Thirty-one years of discipline, undone by shortbread and an attentive listener. I poured more tea I didn't want, buying seconds.

"My brother is not part of this conversation," I said. "What matters now is you. Miss Delacroix—Lena, if I may—I am going to beg you, and I want you to understand that I do not beg lightly. Stop digging. Take your recordings of the morris dances and the May carols, write your paper, win whatever small glory academia offers, and leave the hymn alone. Leave her alone. Not because we are innocent—we are not, we have never been—but because the arrangement, unjust as its foundations are, has held. Two hundred years of quiet. Two hundred years in which no child in this parish has dreamed of a wet woman, no mirror has lagged, no grave has wept. If you unpick the lock to satisfy your conscience, what comes up through the keyhole will not be justice. It will be her. All of her. And she has been underwater a very long time, and drowned women do not rise gentle."

"She was innocent," Lena said. Her voice was low and even, but I saw her knuckles white around her cup. "You said so yourself. Accused falsely. Drowned without trial. And your solution—for two hundred years—was to sing her back to sleep. To use her own voice against her, in a song written by the man who walked her into the water."

"Yes."

"That's not peace, Vicar. That's a gag with a melody."

"Do you imagine I don't know that?" It came out louder than I intended, and I steadied myself against the table. My wife's china rattled. "Do you imagine I haven't lain awake for fifty years knowing exactly what we are? I was eleven years old when I learned the truth and I have done nothing, nothing, except keep the schedule and say the words and tell myself it was mercy, it was mercy, she is at rest, the song lets her rest. And maybe some of that is even true. But I know what it really is. It is the founders' sin, renewed annually, with music. It is us choosing our sleep over her justice, every single year, and dressing the choice in harmony." I sat back down because my legs had opinions. "So yes, Miss Delacroix. I know precisely what we are. And I am begging you anyway. Because the alternative to our ugly, guilty, functioning lie is not truth. It is the mill race, opening in the churchyard."

* * *

She was silent for a long time. I gave her silence; it was the least I owed her, and the most I could afford.

When she spoke, her voice had gone soft in the way that frightened me most—not angry, not accusing. Sad. Sadness cannot be argued with.

"There's something you should know," she said. "Twelve years ago, I recorded the full hymn. From Ada Bracken. The last person alive who knew the true final verse."

The room went very cold, or I did.

"She died that same night," Lena continued. "And I ran. I took the tape and I left and I never listened to it. I told myself it was grief. It wasn't. It was cowardice—I understood, even then, what I was carrying, and I couldn't bear to look at it." She met my eyes. "I listened to it three nights ago, Vicar. In my rented house, alone. Ada sings the verse entire. And then the recording continues past where it should end, and something answers her. Something that knows the verse too. Something that has been waiting twelve years for me to come back and press play."

I could not speak. The clock on the mantel ticked like a dripping eave.

"The mirrors in my cottage lag," she said. "First by a second. Now by more. Last night my reflection mouthed words at me while I stood perfectly still. Vicar—I read its lips. It said *the last verse*. Over and over. She isn't haunting me. She's teaching me. And I don't think I get to decide anymore whether I learn."

I rose on unsteady legs and went to the window. Below, in the amber ruin of the afternoon, the yew's shadow lay across the far corner of the churchyard like a finger pressed to lips.

"Then God help us both," I said, "because I have one more confession to make, and it concerns my brother, and it concerns a letter written in 1811 in Josiah Merridew's own hand—a letter that admits everything, names everything, and asks forgiveness of a dead woman. I swore on his grave I'd never show it to anyone." I turned back to her. "Come back tomorrow, Miss Delacroix. Come back tomorrow, and I will break that oath."

* * *

She left as the light failed. I stood in the doorway watching her go down the lane toward the cottage she once fled, a small grey figure walking steadily into the gathering dark, and I thought: thirty-one years I kept the wall standing, and it took this woman six days and a pot of tea to put her shoulder against it.

And beneath my feet, faint as water through stone, I would have sworn I heard humming.
