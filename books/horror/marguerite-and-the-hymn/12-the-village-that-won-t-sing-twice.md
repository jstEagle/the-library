## 12. The Village That Won't Sing Twice

The council met on Thursday evenings in the back room of the pub, which told you everything you needed to know about Hallowmere's priorities: God got Sundays, but governance got the good beer.

I'd called ahead. I'd used the word *interview*, which is the folklorist's skeleton key—it opens doors that *I know what you did in 1811* would slam shut. Mrs. Petch, the council's chairwoman and the pub's landlady, had said "of course, dear" in a voice so warm it could have buttered toast, and I spent the whole drive over wondering how a woman could sound that pleasant while her village hummed at me through its walls.

The back room was small and brown. Brown walls, brown table, brown tea set with a chip in the spout like a missing tooth. Five council members sat around it: Mrs. Petch; Tom Bracken, who looked like he hadn't slept since our conversation at the bar; a florid man named Mr. Cobb whose family name appeared in the parish register more times than any other; a thin woman introduced only as Miss Aldersey; and Vicar Ellery, who would not look at me.

They'd left a chair open at the head of the table. For me. Which was either courtesy or an altar.

"Lena Delacroix," Mrs. Petch said, pouring tea before I'd even sat down. "Back after all these years. We were sorry to see you go last time."

"Were you?" I said. "I always wondered if anyone noticed."

"Oh, we noticed." She smiled. Her smile was a masterpiece of local government—warm, competent, and completely opaque. "Ada's death shook us all."

*Ada.* The name landed in my chest like a stone dropped down a well. I heard the splash a long time later.

"I'm here about the hymn," I said, because circling felt dishonest, and because my hands were shaking and I wanted to get to the part where someone lied to me so I could stop feeling like the madwoman at the table.

"We assumed as much." Mrs. Petch set down the teapot. "Tom says you have questions about the final verse."

"I have questions about why no one will sing it. Why no one will sing it *twice*. Why your archive has pages cut out of it with scissors. Why—" I stopped myself. I'd rehearsed this speech on the drive over and it had been measured, professional, devastating. What came out instead was raw. "Why there is a woman standing at my garden gate every midnight with river water in her hair, and why every mirror in my house is running late."

The silence that followed was not the silence of people who didn't understand. It was the silence of people who understood perfectly and were deciding how much to admit.

It was Ellery who broke it, quietly, without lifting his eyes from his untouched tea. "You've seen her."

"Yes."

"And the mirrors lag?"

"One second. Now two." I swallowed. "Sometimes three, if I sing."

Miss Aldersey made a small sound—not surprise. Something closer to arithmetic being confirmed.

Mrs. Petch folded her hands on the brown tablecloth. "Then we should probably tell you the truth," she said, "since you'll have it out of us anyway. You're a persistent one. Ada said the same thing about you, once. Said you'd come back someday asking questions nobody wants answered."

My mouth went dry. "What did Ada say about me?"

But Mrs. Petch had already moved on, the way water moves around a stone, and I understood that she'd mentioned Ada deliberately—a toll paid before crossing the bridge.

* * *

Here is what they told me, in the flat tones of people reading a maintenance schedule:

In 1811, six founding families drowned a woman named Marguerite in the mill race. Not in passion—in procedure. She had been accused of something—I'll get to what—and the accusation was false, and they knew it was false, and they drowned her anyway, because that winter the cattle sickened and the wells turned bitter and a village needs a reason or it needs a sacrifice, and a sacrifice is cheaper.

Marguerite did not stay drowned. Not in body—the body stayed put, buried in the corner of the churchyard where the ground stays soft—but in *presence*. She walked. She sang. She stood at windows. Within a month, two of the six families had lost children to the river, and the river had been perfectly calm both times.

So the founders did what frightened, guilty men have always done with their guilt. They wrote it down. They composed a hymn—four verses of praise and binding, words that wrapped Marguerite's name in chains of melody—and they sang it over her grave, and she quieted. And then they discovered the terrible mathematics of the thing: the song held only while it was remembered, and it frayed if sung carelessly, and it could never, ever be sung to completion by accident. The final verse was the lock. Sing the first four and stop, and the lock holds. Sing the fifth—

"There is no fifth verse," Mr. Cobb said firmly, as though the absence were a legal fact.

"There is," I said. "I've heard it."

"You've heard *fragments*," he corrected. "Pieces. A humming. That's not the verse. That's just the shape of the hole where the verse should be."

"The distinction seems fine," I said.

"It's the most important distinction in this village," said Miss Aldersey, and for the first time she looked directly at me, and her eyes were the eyes of a woman who had done sums for forty years and never once liked the total. "The verse exists. We keep it. One family keeps it—the Brackens—and one person in that family carries it, and that person sings the hymn once a year at the grave, four verses and a stop, and the rest of us live our lives."

"And the fifth verse itself? Sung in full?"

"Never," said Mrs. Petch. "Not in living memory. Not in dead memory either, if it comes to that."

I set down my cup because my hands had begun to betray me. "And the woman at my gate. The mirrors. Is that the binding failing?"

Another silence. This one had texture to it—guilt, maybe, or its country cousin, embarrassment.

"No," Ellery said at last. He finally raised his head, and I saw that his eyes were red-rimmed, that the man had been crying in private for years and had gotten very good at hiding the receipts. "That's her noticing you."

"Noticing me."

"You came back, Lena." His voice was gentle, which made it worse. "Twelve years ago you recorded the hymn from Ada Bracken. You were the last outsider to hear it entire—or nearly entire—and the night after you left, Ada died. And now you've returned, and you've started asking, and listening, and writing things down, and Marguerite has spent two hundred years waiting for exactly one kind of person: someone who might finish the song."

"She thinks I'm going to free her," I said.

"She *hopes*," said Miss Aldersey. "Hope is worse than anger, in my experience. Anger throws things. Hope stands at your gate every night, patient as Sunday."

* * *

I want to be honest about what I expected from that room, because what I got instead is the part that still keeps me up at night, and I've had considerable competition in that category lately.

I expected denial. I expected the polite village facade, the *we don't know what you mean, miss*, the shuffling of feet and sudden fascination with the tea. I had prepared arguments against disbelief. I had evidence organized into folders.

Instead, five people admitted everything in under twenty minutes, calmly, the way you'd discuss drainage.

"So let me make sure I have this right," I said, when they'd finished. "Your village murdered an innocent woman. Then it bound her ghost with a song. Then it outsourced the singing to one family, generation after generation, so the rest of you could get on with harvest festivals and raffles. And when that family's keeper dies—as Ada did, twelve years ago—you simply... wait. You leave the debt unpaid and hope no one notices."

"The schedule resumed," Mrs. Petch said. "Wren sings this solstice. The gap was regrettable."

"The gap." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "A woman died keeping your schedule. My friend Ada died—"

"Ada knew the cost when she took the keeping," Cobb said. "As did her mother before her. As does Wren. It's an honor."

"An *honor*—"

"It is," Mrs. Petch said, and here her voice changed for the first time, acquired an edge of something real. "You want to hear monstrous, girl? I'll tell you monstrous. Monstrous is what happens if that song stops. Monstrous is the winter of 1811 happening again, except this time nothing dies quickly. You've seen the edges of it—that woman at your gate, the mirrors. That's her *waiting*. Polite. Almost. Now imagine her not waiting. Imagine the whole village hearing those four verses sung wrong, or not at all, and imagine what comes up out of that churchyard looking for two hundred years of back wages." She leaned forward. "One verse, sung once a year, by one willing singer. That is the smallest price any community has ever paid for peace. I thank God for it daily."

And there it was. The thing I'd driven over to find, served with tea and a chipped spout.

Not a conspiracy. A *subscription*. Evil as a utility bill, paid quarterly, auto-debited from the conscience of one family so everyone else's could run clean.

"Do any of you," I asked slowly, "know what she was accused of? In 1811?"

"Stealing," said Cobb.

"Lying," said Miss Aldersey.

"Witchcraft," said Mrs. Petch.

They answered in sequence, unhesitating, and the answers didn't match, and not one of them noticed that they didn't match, because none of them had ever needed the accusation to be true. The accusation was scaffolding. It had come down centuries ago. Only the building remained, and the building was their peace, and they were proud of it.

"She was innocent," I said. "You know that. Ellery knows that—he's told me. Your founders drowned an innocent woman and built two hundred years of comfort on top of her, and your entire contribution has been to keep the lid on and call it stewardship."

"Yes," said Mrs. Petch.

Just that. *Yes.* No flinch, no defense. She picked up the teapot and refilled my cup, and her hand did not shake, and I realized with a lurch that I was the only person in the room whose hands were shaking, and that this was precisely how they'd designed it.

"You're horrified," she observed, settling back. "That's natural. Outsiders are always horrified, right up until they understand the arithmetic. Ask yourself, Lena: what would you pay? Truly. If the choice were her rest against your village—your friends, your pubs, your children asleep at night without the river coming up to visit—what would you pay? Be honest. Everyone at this table has done the sum. It takes about a minute, and then you live with it."

I thought about Wren, sixteen years old, carrying a song like a yoke because her grandmother had died and someone had to. I thought about Tom's face at the bar, pale and shaking, and I understood suddenly that his shame wasn't about the secret. It was about the relief. He was relieved it wasn't him. They all were. That was the engine of the whole arrangement—not loyalty, not tradition, but the oldest and quietest sin: *thank God it's someone else's turn.*

"The arithmetic is wrong," I said. "It's been wrong since 1811. You don't balance a murder by scheduling it."

"The alternative," said Miss Aldersey, "is imbalance of a considerably less abstract variety."

Ellery spoke then, barely above the tick of the mantel clock. "Lena. Please. Whatever you're planning—stop digging. Let Wren sing at the solstice. Let the year turn over clean. You can publish everything else—the history, the injustice, the names. Write the truth. But leave the verse alone. Some doors are locked for the sake of what's behind them, not what's in front."

"That's not a door," I said. "That's a woman. And she's been standing at my gate every midnight, and do you know what she does? She doesn't threaten me. She doesn't scream. She *waits*, exactly like Miss Aldersey said—because she thinks I'm the first person in two hundred years who might say her name right." I stood. My chair scraped, and five faces watched me with that same placid, terrible calm. "You've all made your peace. I can see it. It's the most horrifying thing I've ever witnessed, and I have a haunted house."

Mrs. Petch walked me to the door of the back room. At the threshold she touched my arm—kindly, I'll grant her that—and said, low enough that the others couldn't hear:

"Ada loved you, you know. Talked about you for years. The clever student with the recorder." A pause. "She also said you'd be the death of us all someday. She hoped she was wrong. So do I, dear. So do I."

* * *

The walk home took twenty minutes in daylight. I did it in the blue hour, and it took forever, because I kept stopping—at the churchyard wall, at the bend in the lane where the mill race glints through the trees—to ask myself the question Mrs. Petch had planted, the insidious little seed of it.

*What would you pay?*

Because here was the rot at the center of me, the thing I'd carried twelve years in a shoebox of unplayed tapes: I had done the sum once already. Twelve years ago, a woman taught me a song and died the same night, and I ran. I took the tape and I ran and I told myself I was young, I was scared, I didn't know—and maybe all true—but I had known enough to run *from*, and I'd let Hallowmere keep its peace and its price, and I'd slept soundly in cities far away while Wren grew up under a yoke I'd helped, however innocently, to tighten.

The village wasn't the monster. I'd come here thinking I'd find a monster and instead found a ledger, balanced and dusted and approved by committee. The monster was the ledger. The monster was every hand that signed it and every year it went unchallenged, including mine.

The cottage was dark when I reached it. Of course it was. I stood at the gate—the gate, *her* gate—and the evening was so still I could hear the mill race a quarter mile off, whispering its one long syllable.

Midnight was hours away yet. But I already knew she'd be there. And for the first time, instead of dread, I felt something that frightened me far more, because it was aimed inward:

Resolve.

I went inside, took out Ada's tape, and set it on the table beside the recorder.

Tomorrow, I'd tell myself. Tomorrow I'd play it.

But the tape sat there in the lamplight, and the house hummed its low continuous note through the walls, and somewhere beneath the churchyard something waited with the patience of two hundred drowned winters—and I knew, the way you know the weight of a held breath, that tomorrow had already been used up.

There was only tonight.
