## 15. Ellery's Confession, Part Two

I have given perhaps four thousand sermons in my life, and I remember almost none of them. I remember every word I am about to say to Lena Delacroix, and I will remember them until I die, which — if the song has its way — may be sooner than either of us would like.

She arrives at the vicarage at half past three on a Thursday, because of course she does. Grief and truth keep office hours; they come when you've just made tea for one and are pretending that's a pleasure. I hear her boots on the gravel before I see her, and I stand in my study with my hand flat on the drawer where the letter has slept for thirty-one years, and I think: *not yet, not yet, not yet* — the way a man prays when he has stopped believing anyone is listening but cannot stop the habit.

The doorbell goes. I let it ring twice. A small vanity. It makes it look as though I were elsewhere, occupied, a man with a life rather than a man waiting to be undone.

"Mr. Ellery." She stands on my step with her hair doing what it always does, half-escaped from whatever arrangement she's attempted, and there are shadows under her eyes like thumbprints in wet clay. The house across the lane has been working on her. I can tell by the way she glances at my windows — checking them, all of them, the way you check a wound.

"Miss Delacroix. Tea?"

"I'd rather you didn't pretend this is a social call."

"Good," I say. "Neither would the tea deserve it. It's the decaffeinated sort. My sister sends it out of kindness and spite in equal measure."

That earns me half a smile, which I do not deserve, and we go into the study, and I pour two cups of tea that neither of us drinks, and then there is nothing left between me and the drawer but a brass handle and forty years of cowardice.

*

You should understand something about duty, since you have been so patient with this village and its silences. Duty is a wonderful thing right up until the moment you examine what it's *for*. I was ordained at twenty-six and I believed, sincerely, that I had been handed a rope and told to hold on. It took me another decade to ask what was tied to the other end.

In Hallowmere, the answer was Marguerite.

My predecessor, old Canon Wace, told me on my first week, in the way you tell a man the boiler leaks and the roof slates come loose in wind — practical matters, parish maintenance. *There is a hymn,* he said. *It is sung once a year, at midsummer, over the grave in the north corner. You will not sing it yourself. You will not permit it sung twice. You will not ask what lies beneath the churchyard, because the answer is: everything, and it sleeps.* And then he poured himself a sherry with a hand that shook only slightly, and asked after my mother's health.

I thought he was mad. I was young. Madness and tradition wear the same coat in country parishes, and you learn to compliment both.

So I did what I was told. For years I did what I was told. I stood at the churchyard wall each solstice while the Brackens sang their once-a-year verse, and I felt the ground beneath my shoes go quiet as a held breath, and I called that peace, because everyone around me called it peace, and a man can live his whole life inside other people's vocabulary.

Then my brother came home.

*

Peter was the clever one. Everyone says that about the dead — *he was the clever one* — and in Peter's case it happened also to be true. He read history at Durham, came back with a first and a fiancée and an appetite for archives the way other men have an appetite for drink. He found the gaps in the parish register within a month. 1811, cut clean away, as if paper could forget.

"You know what's missing," he said to me one evening in this very room. He had his feet on my fender, which Mother would never have permitted, and a folder of photocopies on his knees. "You don't get ragged edges from age, Thomas. You get ragged edges from accident. These are knife edges. Someone sat down with a blade and took those pages out carefully, on purpose, and then bound the book again so no one would notice."

"People hide embarrassing things," I said. "Bastards. Debts. A bigamous curate or two."

"Not things. A *person*. There's a grave in your churchyard with no name on it, Tom. I've been asking. Do you know what happens when you ask about that grave? People stop talking. Not awkwardly — *completely*. Like someone's turned a tap off. Mrs. Hedley made me a full pot of tea and then forgot I existed halfway through pouring it. Stood there staring at the window until I left."

I remember the fire ticking. I remember thinking: *he has been to the north corner.* I remember deciding, with the part of me that had already begun to rot, to say nothing.

He didn't stop. Of course he didn't stop; he was Peter. By spring he had the founders' names, the land grants, the pattern of who owned what after 1811 — three farms consolidated into the hands of families that had previously held scraps, all within eighteen months of the winter the register went blank. He had a coroner's fragment from the county file, water damage and all: *found in the mill race, no inquest deemed necessary, the parish having satisfied itself as to circumstances.*

"The parish satisfied itself," he read aloud, and laughed, and the laugh had a crack down the middle of it. "Tom. They drowned her. They drowned a woman and then they wrote themselves a lullaby about it, and the whole village has been singing her to sleep for a hundred and seventy years so nobody has to wake up and be ashamed."

"They drowned her," I said. Not a question. Because Wace had told me enough, in the end, in the last year of his life, when the sherry loosened more than his hand. Enough to know. Not enough to act. Just enough to be guilty comfortably, which is the specific genius of Hallowmere.

"Yes," Peter said. "And I'm going to prove it. Publicly. Properly. Records, inquest, a stone with her name on it. Let the thing under your churchyard hear the truth for once instead of the hymn."

And I said — God forgive me, I have said it to myself every night for thirty-one years and God has apparently declined to forgive me, which I consider fair — I said:

"Pete. Some doors are load-bearing."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he smiled, the way he used to when I'd failed a riddle as boys, and said, "You actually believe it. You actually believe the song keeps something down." And he stood, and gathered his folder, and paused at the door, and said, "If it does — if any of it is true — then it isn't peace you're keeping, Tom. It's a hostage. And hostages don't sleep easier because you've got a nice tune."

He was gone by morning. Not dead — vanished. His car at the mill bridge, keys in the ignition, door open. The river dragged for nine days. They found one shoe. His fiancée got a box of river stones and my mother's arms around her and nothing else.

The village closed over his disappearance like water over the shoe.

And here is the thing I need you to understand, Miss Delacroix, the thing I have never said aloud: the singing that year was *better*. The Brackens sang at midsummer and the ground drank it down and the village exhaled, and I stood at the wall knowing — *knowing*, the way you know your own heartbeat — that my brother was under there. That the hymn had needed a singer, or a keeper, or simply a mouth, and it had taken the one man in Hallowmere who wouldn't sing along.

Duty held. I held. I rang the bells, I visited the sick, I preached four thousand sermons, and every midsummer I stood at the wall and listened to a lie set to music, and I told myself it was peace.

It was a hostage. He was right. It was always a hostage.

*

Lena is watching me across the cold tea, and I realize I have been silent for some time, and that my hands are shaking, and that she — being Lena — has not rushed me. She has done the unforgivable, patient thing: waited.

"Your brother," she says quietly. "Peter Ellery. I found him in the archive footnotes. Disappeared 1993. Nobody ever connected him to—" She stops. Recalibrates. "To any of this."

"No one ever connects anything here," I say. "That's rather the point of us."

I open the drawer.

The letter is where it has always been, wrapped in oilcloth inside a biscuit tin inside a box that once held confirmation cards, because shame keeps its relics the way faith keeps its saints' bones — hidden, handled rarely, believed in absolutely. I lay it on the desk between us and do not touch it again.

"This is the founders' letter," I say. "Written the winter after. All nine families. It was kept by whoever held the living — passed from vicar to vicar, never entered in any record, never copied. Wace showed it to me once and told me that if I ever felt moved to righteousness, I should read it again and remember what righteousness costs."

Lena unwraps the oilcloth the way you'd handle a bird with a broken wing.

The paper is brown as leaf-litter. Nine signatures. I know them all; half the village still carries those names. And above the signatures, in a clerk's careful copperplate, the words that have been sitting in my parish like a stone in a boot for two centuries:

*We, being of sound mind and fearing God, do confess that the woman Marguerite, late of this parish, was innocent of all whereof she was accused, and that her death at our hands upon the night of the 14th of December was murder, done for our gain and our quiet. We further confess that the hymn made for her was not a prayer but a chain, and that we chained her innocent voice to hold down our guilt. We bind our heirs to sing it, and we pray God forgive us, though we do not mean to stop.*

"She was innocent," Lena says. Her voice doesn't waver. Her hands do. "They wrote it down. They *knew*—"

"They knew at once," I say. "That's the horror of it, Miss Delacroix. This isn't a sin that grew a cover-up later. They murdered her on a Tuesday and confessed it on paper by Christmas and then chose the chain anyway. Because the chain worked. Because a sleeping ghost asks nothing of you but one verse a year, and a living conscience asks everything." I take a breath. It costs. "We are not a village that forgot. We are a village that remembers perfectly and pays annually. That's worse. I've thought about which is worse for thirty-one years, and I'm telling you: remembering and paying is worse."

She reads the letter twice. I watch her lips move on the second pass, silently, and I think of mirrors, and of what her reflection has been mouthing at her, and I find I am not surprised. Nothing about this house of horrors surprises me anymore. Only my own endurance of it does.

"Ellery." She looks up. "Your brother tried to end this. To speak the truth publicly. And the village—"

"The village lost a historian," I say. "The river gained a shoe. And the following midsummer the hymn was sung especially sweetly." I fold my hands so they'll stop their disgraceful trembling. "I have spent my whole ministry telling myself I stayed to protect these people. That is true the way a cage protects a bird. What I actually did, Miss Delacroix, was choose the chain. Again. Every year. With my eyes open and a letter in my drawer that proves the chain was forged around an innocent woman's throat."

"And now?"

"Now," I say, "you are going to tell me what you've learned without singing. And then I am going to help you end it. Properly. Not managed, not maintained, not paid annually — *ended*. Marguerite gets her name spoken. The truth gets sung in daylight, however many verses, however many times. If the ground opens and takes me first, then it takes a man who finally deserves taking, and I will die considerably improved."

She stares at me. "You understand what you're saying. Your brother—"

"My brother understood it at twenty-four," I say, "and it killed him, and I have spent thirty-one years being the reason it killed him. Yes, Miss Delacroix. I understand exactly what I'm saying. I've had a very long time to rehearse."

I take the letter back from her — gently; it is the truest relic this parish owns — and wrap it again in its oilcloth, and then, before my courage can remember itself and sit down, I hold it out to her.

"Take it. Copy it, photograph it, nail it to the church door if it comes to that. Luther at least had the decency to make his scandal public." My voice cracks on the last of it, and I let it. "And there's something else. Something you'll want before solstice. Wace kept more than the letter. In the vestry safe there's a book — the founders' own hand, the working papers for the hymn. Which verse binds. Which verse was *hers*. The one they cut out of her mouth before they put her in the water."

Lena goes very still. Outside, the light is going long and gold and wrong, the way it does here now, dusk arriving like something walking up the lane.

"The verse they cut out," she repeats.

"The true final verse," I say. "Marguerite's own words. The founders couldn't destroy it — they tried, and the ground wouldn't let the silence hold — so they buried it with her and sang the chain instead." I stand, and my knees complain, and I find I don't care. "Come, Miss Delacroix. The vestry key is in my pocket, and I have kept it thirty-one years against precisely this evening. Whatever rises when we open that safe — and something will; there is always a toll — we'll face it together. You and I and the truth, for once, in daylight hours."

She rises too. At the study door she pauses, and looks back at me with something that is not quite pity and not quite thanks, and says:

"For what it's worth — I think Peter would have liked this."

I stop with my hand on the latch.

"So would I," I say. "That's rather how I know it's going to cost us."

And we go out together, toward the church, where the vestry safe waits with its two-hundred-year-old secret — and where, as we cross the churchyard, every mirror-glass window in Hallowmere catches the last of the sun and holds it a beat too long, like a breath, like a listener, like something below the north corner turning over in its sleep and beginning, very faintly, to hum.
