## 2. Songs Sung Once

The morning after I arrived, I counted the doors of my rented house again.

Fourteen by daylight, thirteen if you stood in the kitchen and looked back the way you'd come. I wrote both numbers in the margin of my notebook and underlined neither, because underlining would mean one of them mattered more than the other, and I wasn't ready to decide that yet. Counting was just a habit. A way of keeping the world in columns so it couldn't pool around my ankles.

I told myself it was a habit.

The Hollow woke slowly, the way a body wakes when it's pretending to sleep. Smoke from a dozen chimneys stood straight up in windless air. Somewhere a dog barked twice and then thought better of it. I set up my recorder on the inn's porch table — a Marantz field unit, fresh tape, external mic with a foam windscreen — and let it run while I drank tea that tasted faintly of iron, as though the well water had been strained through old nails.

Maren came out at half past nine with a basket of eggs she didn't offer to sell me.

"You're the song man," she said. She was maybe nineteen, broad-shouldered, with her father's heavy jaw and none of his caution. She'd watched me set up the recorder with the frank hunger of someone watching a stranger open a box they'd been told never to touch.

"Folklorist," I said. "I record songs. Ballads, lullabies, work songs. Whatever people still sing."

"We still sing." She said it quickly, defensively, like I'd accused her of something.

"I know. That's why I'm here."

She looked at the recorder the way you look at a wasp on your sleeve — fascinated, unwilling to move, certain it will end badly. "Da says you'll want us to sing things over and over."

"Only if you're willing. Repetition helps me transcribe. And honestly—" I gave her the smile I used on archive donors, the one that made me harmless, "—a good song deserves to be heard twice."

Something crossed her face then. Not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of someone hearing their own name spoken in a language they don't speak.

"Da says once is how songs are meant to be sung here," she said. "Once is *whole*."

* * *

Her father, Bram Hale, kept the only inn in the village, which is to say he kept the only building with more than two chairs in its common room. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with hands like loaves, and he greeted me each morning with the same nod he probably gave the weather. When I asked him directly about songs on my second day, he wiped the same clean spot on the bar for a long time before answering.

"There's a harvest round," he said finally. "Older than the church. My grandfather's grandfather sang it."

"Could you sing it for me?"

"It's been sung."

I waited. In oral tradition work, waiting is most of the job. People fill silence the way water fills a hole.

"That's not an answer, Mr. Hale."

"It's the only kind we have." He set down the cloth. "You'll hear it, or you won't. But it won't be sung again for you. Songs here get spent, like matches. You don't strike a match twice to see if it'll light."

"What happens if you do?"

He looked at me then, really looked, and what was in his face wasn't hostility. It was pity, which frightened me considerably more. "Ask Crane," he said. "He's better at explaining things he doesn't believe."

* * *

It was Maren who broke first, three days in.

She came to the house I was renting — the crooked one at the lane's end, fourteen doors or thirteen — carrying a covered dish of stew and an expression like a girl about to jump off something. She set the dish on my table without being asked, glanced at the recorder sitting there with its reels stilled, and said:

"The cradle song. My mother's. I'll sing it once. Not twice. Don't ask me twice."

"I won't," I said, and reached for the machine.

She sang standing up, facing the window, hands folded like a child at prayer. Her voice was unremarkable in range but flawless in another way I couldn't immediately name — until I could. There were no hesitations. None. Every breath fell where a breath should fall; every ornament sat exactly where it had always sat, worn smooth as a stair stone. It was not a performance. It was a reproduction. Somewhere behind her eyes a needle dropped onto a groove older than her mother, older than the village, and the song played itself through her.

It was beautiful. It was also, in some way I couldn't articulate yet, terrible — the way a perfect waxwork is terrible, the way a room no one has entered for years is terrible.

The words were simple. Sleep, the dark is kind, the dark keeps count, the door is small but it is ours. I wrote them down phonetically as she sang, and my pen slowed at the third line because I felt, absurdly, that I was writing something down that had been written down before, many times, and each time erased.

She finished. The last note decayed into the tick of the recorder's reels.

Then I made my mistake, and I knew it was a mistake even as my mouth opened, because folklore fieldwork runs on repetition and I had spent ten years trusting it the way other men trust rope.

"Beautiful. One more take?"

Maren went white.

Not pale — white. The color drained from her face in a single tide, top to bottom, as though something inside her had opened a valve. She didn't answer. She stared past me at the wall — at the small unmarked door beside my pantry, the one I'd noticed my first night and filed under *quirk of local architecture* — and her lips moved without sound, shaping numbers.

"Maren—"

"Don't count them," she whispered. Then, louder, to herself, a scolding: "*Don't count them.*" She grabbed her shawl off the chair. At the threshold she turned, and her eyes were wet and furious and afraid all at once, and she said, in a voice pitched low and hard: "You asked me twice. Nobody asks twice. Whatever you're doing here, finish it fast and go."

The door shut. Through the window I watched her walk away too quickly, and I saw two neighbors pause in their yards to watch her pass, and I saw them exchange a glance over her head that contained an entire conversation I was not invited to.

I sat down and rewound the tape. Her voice filled the kitchen again, note-perfect, breath-perfect. On playback, I heard something I hadn't caught live: beneath the final line, under the words *the door is small but it is ours*, there was a sound like a long slow exhalation. Not Maren. Not the room.

Like something relieved to be remembered, even once.

* * *

I took the question to Father Aldous Crane that afternoon, partly because Bram had told me to and mostly because I wanted to see the church.

It stood at the center of the village the way a spider stands at the center of a web — not menacingly, exactly, but structurally. Everything angled toward it. The lanes bent where they shouldn't have needed to bend. Even the river, I realized, looking at the survey map I'd bought in the county town, curved around the churchyard as if in deference, or dread.

Crane received me in a vestry that smelled of candle wax and cold stone. He was younger than I expected — forty perhaps, gray coming in early at the temples, with the careful gentleness of a man who has learned that sudden movements frighten his congregation. Or himself.

"Bram sent you," he said, pouring tea I hadn't asked for.

"He said you explain things you don't believe."

A small smile. "Bram flatters me. I believe very little, but I explain it thoroughly." He sat, folding his hands. "You've been asking about our singing."

"I recorded a lullaby yesterday. When I asked for a second take, the young woman reacted as if I'd struck her."

"Yes." He said it without surprise, which told me the whole village already knew, which told me the whole village was already discussing me. "Wren Voss. May I be direct with you?"

"Please."

"Some songs are spent." He spoke the way a doctor delivers a diagnosis he's given a hundred times. "Not finished, not forgotten — spent. Used up. A song in this village is sung once, wholly, and then it belongs to the having-been-sung. Asking for it again isn't repetition. It's... exhumation."

"That's a remarkable folk practice," I said. "Is there a term for it locally? An origin story? I'd love to document the—"

"Wren." My first name. He'd learned it, and he used it deliberately, the way you'd use a hand on someone's shoulder. "Why are you here? Truly. Not the grant proposal version."

I gave him the true version, or most of it: dying regional traditions, songs vanishing faster than they could be recorded, the Hollow's reputation among collectors as a place where the old material ran unusually deep. All true. What I left out was the letter that had drawn me here specifically — the unsigned note, postmarked nowhere, containing a single line of a ballad I'd never encountered in any archive, in handwriting that had seemed oddly familiar, though I couldn't say why. I left that out because I hadn't yet admitted to myself how much it had mattered.

Crane listened with his head slightly tilted, and when I finished he was quiet for a moment, and I had the distinct sense he was deciding something about me.

"Document what you can," he said at last. "People will sing for you. Once. Be grateful for the once. And Wren—" he rose, ending the audience, moving me gently toward the door with nothing but courtesy, "—when you notice the doors, don't count them."

The floor tilted under me, very slightly, the way a boat tilts when everyone aboard shifts to one side.

"I'm sorry?"

"The little doors. In the houses." His kindness was absolute and completely unnerving. "Visitors notice them eventually. They're nothing. Old building custom. But counting them is a habit, and habits here..." He opened the vestry door onto the churchyard light. "...habits here have a way of becoming invitations."

* * *

That evening I walked the village end to end and did not count, and failed utterly.

There were thirty-one houses in Marguerite's Hollow. I know because I counted them, helplessly, the way you read a word spelled wrong on a sign. And every house had one — a small door, waist-high or lower, unmarked, unpainted, fitted into a wall where a door served no purpose. Beside the pantry. Under the stairs. Set into the gable end facing the church, in one case, opening onto nothing but air above a flowerbed. Some had hinges gone green with age. One, on the far lane, had a hinge bright and newly oiled.

None had handles. None had locks. None had any marking at all, except—

Except that as I passed the last house before mine, an old woman was kneeling at her doorstep with a stub of chalk, and on the wood of her little door she drew a single stroke, adding it to a cluster of others, and I understood with a lurch that the marks weren't decoration.

They were tallies.

And they were going *down*. I could see the ghost-marks of erased strokes beneath the fresh ones, dozens of them, scrubbed and redrawn smaller each time, a countdown conducted over years, over generations, patient as erosion.

The old woman felt me watching. She rose slowly, joints cracking, and looked at me with an expression I would spend many nights afterward trying to name. It wasn't hostility. It was the look of a person checking whether the flood has reached their door yet.

"Evening," she said.

"Evening," I said. "Lovely village."

"Aye," she said, closing her hand over the chalk. "We paid plenty for it."

She went inside. The small door stayed shut. All of them stayed shut, thirty-one doors in thirty-one walls, and I walked home through the blue dusk feeling the way you feel when a song ends on a chord that refuses to resolve.

At my own house — fourteen doors, or thirteen — I found my recorder where I'd left it, reels still, tape intact. Maren's lullaby safe on its spool. I set the machine on the table, poured myself the last of the iron-tasting tea, and told myself the unease in my chest was just altitude, just strangeness, just a folklorist's ordinary thrill at genuine anomaly.

But before bed, I did something I'd never done in ten years of fieldwork.

I threaded a fresh tape, set the microphone on the pillow beside mine, pressed record, and said aloud, to the empty room, to the small unmarked door in the pantry wall:

"If anything in this house wants to talk — the tape's running."

I slept. I thought I slept alone.

In the morning, the reel had turned all night, unplugged.
