## 4. The Counting Verse

Bram Halloway had kept the Drowned Lamb for thirty-one years, and in that time he had learned that a barkeep's true trade was not pouring but listening — knowing which silences to fill and which to leave standing like furniture too heavy to move. He had thought himself good at it. He had thought, after three decades of harvest nights and funeral wakes and the particular drunk grief of farmers in February, that nothing said over his taps could surprise him.

Then the Vasquez one came back.

He'd recognized the face before the name. Ten years had sharpened it, put angles where softness used to be, but the eyes were the same — that pale, cataloguing stare, always slightly left of your own, as if reading something written just behind your head. Bram had served Jude Vasquez ginger beer at fourteen and whisky now, and both times the child and the adult had watched him the same way: kindly, patiently, and with the fixed attention of someone taking inventory.

It was the third night back when they asked about the song.

* * *

The Lamb was near empty on a Tuesday — old Tilly Fenwick dozing over her sherry by the fire, two farmhands playing draughts without speaking, rain needling the black windows. Jude sat at the bar with a notebook closed under one hand like a card they weren't ready to show.

"Bram," they said. "The harvest song. The one Mrs. Aldery sang Saturday."

The glass Bram was polishing stopped moving. He hadn't decided to stop it; his hand simply had opinions of its own about certain subjects.

"What about it?"

"I'd like to hear the melody. Just the tune. I got most of the words down but the notation's thin — I want to transcribe it properly for the archive." A small smile, professional, disarming. "You don't have to sing the words. Hum it."

"Mrs. Aldery sang it once," Bram said. "That's how it goes."

"I know. I know the custom. But a melody isn't the words, is it? It's just air shaped a certain way. Nobody ever came to harm from humming."

Bram looked at the fire. Old Tilly snored gently. Outside, the rain went on working at the village like water working at stone, patient, certain of eventual results.

"It's just a tune," Jude said again, softer.

And here is what Bram Halloway would never be able to explain afterward, not to himself, not to anyone: he believed them. That was the terrible thing. He believed the song could be split open like a carcass, the melody lifted clean from the meaning, and nothing would spill. He believed it because he wanted to believe it, because thirty years of keeping the rule — *once sung, never repeated; hummed never; whistled never; taught only whole and only once* — had worn the reason away until only the rule remained, a wall whose foundation no living villager could name. And walls without foundations are easy to talk yourself through.

He set down the glass.

"Not loud," he said.

He hummed it.

It came up out of him easier than anything had come out of him in years — that was the second terrible thing. The tune lived somewhere below thinking, in the marrow, and it rose the way water rises in a straw. Six notes rising, four falling, a little hitch in the middle like a foot catching on a stair. A lullaby's shape wearing a work song's boots. He had heard it every autumn of his life, sung once at the harvest fire by whichever elder drew the lot, and he had heard it exactly enough times to carry it forever.

Jude's pencil moved. Bram watched the notes go down onto the page and felt, absurdly, lighter — unburdened, as though the tune were a sack he'd been carrying and the page could hold it for a while.

Then Jude turned the notebook around and slid it across the bar.

"I filled in the words from Saturday," they said. "Numbered them. Look."

Bram looked.

*One for the sower, two for the seed,*
*Three for the miller the grain won't feed,*
*Four for the door that opens wide—*

and there, beside each line, in Jude's neat archival hand, the names. *Sower — Hedley Marsh. Seed — Alice Marsh. Miller — Tom Cutter.* On down the verses, a name to every number, eleven names in all, and at the bottom, in the space where the eleventh verse ended the song:

*Eleven for the one who waves from the gate.*

Beside it: **Willem Halloway.**

The bar went very quiet. Not silent — the fire still ticked, the rain still worked, Tilly still snored — but quiet the way a room goes quiet when something in it has turned its attention toward you. Bram heard his own breathing. He heard the draughts players' hands stop on their pieces.

"That's not—" His voice came out wrong, gravel in a dry well. "Those aren't part of it. Those are just words. You can't—"

"Hedley Marsh," Jude said gently. "Disappeared October 1961. Alice Marsh, his wife, the following spring. Tom Cutter, 1974. I've been through the parish records, Bram. Every name in this rhyme matches a person who left Hollowmere and was never spoken of again. Not died — *left*. Except nobody remembers them leaving. Their houses were sold as if they'd never stood. Your brother's name is the last one."

"My brother emigrated." The words came out flat and rehearsed, and Bram heard, hearing them, how many thousands of times he must have said them into the dark of himself to wear them so smooth. "Canada. Farming out west. He wrote, at first."

"When did the letters stop?"

"They didn't stop. They—" Bram stopped. Something moved behind his eyes, some door swinging on a hinge rusted shut for decades, and what came through the gap was not a memory but the shape where a memory should be: a smooth white absence, like a tooth socket. Willem at the gate. Willem waving. Willem's laugh, which he could not hear. Willem's face, which he could not—

"He waved from the gate," Bram said. His voice sounded far away to himself, a voice coming up a long stairwell. "The morning he went. I remember the wave. I don't remember—" His hand found the bar's edge and gripped it. "I don't remember his face. I'm fifty-eight years old and I cannot picture my brother's face."

Old Tilly woke, or seemed to, and looked at them across the room with milky eyes, and Bram saw that she understood — that she had understood since Saturday, since before Saturday, since she was a girl — and that her understanding was an old, tired animal that had learned to lie very still.

"You didn't know," Jude said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I knew the rule." Bram's knuckles were white on the wood. "Once sung, never repeated. My father told me. His father told him. Nobody ever said why. Nobody ever said *names*. We thought it was luck. Farmers' superstition, like not saying 'rabbit' in March." He laughed, and the laugh came out broken, half sob. "Eleven for the one who waves from the gate. Thirty years I've poured pints under that song and never once counted past ten."

The farmhands had risen without a word and gone out into the rain. Bram noticed their departure only as a draft, a closing door, the sense of the room contracting around the three who remained — the keeper, the folklorist, the sleeping woman — as though the Lamb itself wished to hear no more.

"Who else knows?" Jude asked. "About the names?"

"Nobody. Everyone." Bram dragged a hand down his face. "It's like — you know how you know not to lean over the well? Nobody teaches it. You just grow up already knowing, the way you grow up knowing fire burns. It's in the milk." He looked at the notebook, at the neat columns, at his brother's name sitting in its row like a body laid out. "You've done a counting, Vasquez. Do you understand what you've done? The song counts. It has always counted. And now you've counted the song."

* * *

Jude walked home through the rain without hurrying. The lanes of Hollowmere at night were channels of deeper dark between the hedges, lit sparsely by lamps that seemed to illuminate only their own immediate halos and give nothing to the distance. Water ran along the gutters with a sound like whispering passed hand to hand.

They did not open the notebook again until the cottage door was locked and the lamp lit and the kettle on. Then they spread the pages across the kitchen table and made themselves look, properly, at what they had assembled.

The transcription was sound. That was the first thing — the professional satisfaction of it, clean and cold. Six notes up, four down, the hitch. Mrs. Aldery's phrasing recovered note for note from memory, cross-checked against Bram's humming tonight. Two independent sources, one melody. Good fieldwork. Textbook.

The words were the problem. The words were eleven verses of a children's counting rhyme — *one for the sower, two for the seed* — the kind of thing sung in schoolyards all across the county, harmless, ancient, worn smooth as river stones. Except that in Hollowmere each number had grown a proper name attached like a burr, and except that Jude, cross-referencing against the parish registers they'd photographed on the second day, had matched every single one.

Not approximately. Exactly. Dates of disappearance or unexplained departure spanning a century and a half, each falling within a year of the verse that carried its name. Hedley and Alice Marsh together, which argued the song sometimes took pairs. Tom Cutter, the miller, whose mill had stood roofless on the stream road for as long as anyone could remember — longer than anyone could say why it was roofless. An infant in the fourth verse, *four for the door that opens wide*, named Elsie Brand, born and gone within eight months of 1929, no death recorded, no grave.

And the erasures. That was the part Jude kept circling back to, the part that made the tea go cold untouched. It wasn't only that people vanished. It was that the vanishing left no wake. Parish entries existed; memories did not. Houses had been sold; mourners had not mourned. Bram Halloway could not picture his own brother's face, and when Jude had pressed him — *what did Willem look like?* — Bram had described, with growing bewilderment, a tall man of indeterminate features, a man built entirely out of the wave from the gate. The gesture survived. The man did not. As though the song took the person and left only the pointing-toward, the way a wound leaves a scar that aches without remembering the blade.

Jude sat back. The lamp hissed faintly. Rain on the roof, steady as a held breath.

There was a rational explanation available and they reached for it the way you reach for a banister in the dark. Coincidence plus confirmation bias: a village loses people over a century — villages always lose people — and a rhyme accumulates names the way lint accumulates in a pocket. The forgetting was ordinary human forgetting, dressed up by a folklorist's pattern-hungry mind into something sinister. They wrote this argument out in the margin of the transcript, in full sentences, like a scholar refuting a crank.

Then they read the rhyme through once more, aloud, silently, lips moving — and stopped at the tenth verse.

*Nine for the neighbor who borrows and pays,*
*Ten for the traveler lost in the ways,*

Ten. The traveler. Lost in the ways. And beneath it, in the register, a name Jude had noted without feeling its weight until now: **Marguerite Nystrom**, age six, 1953, listed among the year's departures in a clerk's cramped hand, no destination given.

Nystrom. Their own surname. A relative — great-aunt, some collateral branch, the family tree thinned and blurred by generations of leaving. A child of six, gone the way the others had gone, erased the way the others had been erased, and Jude had grown up in a house where no one had ever once spoken the name.

Except that someone had. Someone small and yellow-raincoated had stood on the porch steps two nights ago with rain that didn't seem to touch her, and said, *You came back late.*

The kettle clicked off. Jude did not move. In the window above the sink, the black glass held the lamp's reflection and, beyond it, the garden going down to the hedge, and beyond the hedge the green, and beyond the green — invisible at night, visible always — the capped stone well, waiting under its iron grate the way a mouth waits behind closed lips.

"Counting rhymes count," Jude said to the empty kitchen, and their voice sounded young in it, younger than forty, younger than twenty. "That's what they're *for*. One, two, buckle my shoe. You count to find out who's *it*."

The lamp flickered. Outside, the rain eased, and in the new quiet Jude became aware of a sound they had been hearing for some minutes without registering it — faint, rhythmic, coming from nowhere and everywhere, from the pipes or the wind or the ground itself.

Someone humming. Six notes up, four down, the hitch in the middle like a foot catching on a stair.

It was not coming from the lane. There was no one in the lane.

It was coming, Jude realized with a calm that frightened them more than any scream could have, from behind the sealed door of the cottage's back bedroom — the door that had been painted shut, the room their landlady had called "just storage" without meeting their eyes.

The humming stopped on the hitch, on the caught step, and did not go on.

Jude sat in the lamplight with their brotherless, sisterless childhood spread out on the table in neat archival columns, and listened to the house hold its breath around a silence shaped exactly like the next line of a song.
