## 5. Yellow Raincoat

The first time Marguerite came inside, Jude was doing dishes.

That was the thing about her — she never arrived at a door. She was simply *there*, the way a smell is there, the way a headache arrives without any moment of beginning. Jude had been standing at the sink with their hands in grey water, watching the last of the daylight go coppery through the window over the basin, and when they turned to set the mug on the drying rack, Marguerite was sitting at the kitchen table with her hands folded, patient as a photograph.

She did not drip. That was the detail that undid Jude first, before the wrongness of her presence itself: the yellow raincoat hung dry and bright in the lamplight, its hood pooled behind her small shoulders, and there was no dark crescent of water beneath her boots on the flagstones. Outside it had been raining since four o'clock. The walk from the crossroads to the cottage was forty yards of mud and standing water. A child coming in from that rain would leave a trail like a wounded animal.

Jude's hands were still wet. They gripped the edge of the sink and said nothing, because saying nothing was a strategy, because if they didn't acknowledge her then some part of them could keep insisting — *stress, exhaustion, the light, a trick of the eye* — and that part of them had been working very hard for three days now, ever since the porch steps, ever since *You came back late.*

Marguerite watched them with the mild attention of someone waiting for a kettle. Her face was exactly as Jude remembered it, which was the second unbearable thing. Faces change. Even remembered faces blur and drift, softened by years of not-thinking-about-them. But this face was sharp as the day it had been made: round cheeks, a chipped front tooth visible when she smiled, eyes the brown of steeped tea. Twenty years old and unchanged. Children were supposed to be erased by time. She had been spared, or exempted, or *refused* — Jude couldn't decide which word frightened them most.

"You're staring," Marguerite said.

"You're in my kitchen."

"I'm allowed." She said it without arrogance, the way you'd say *the sky is up*. "This house knows me."

Jude dried their hands on a towel that had come with the cottage, stiff and sun-bleached, and made themselves turn around, made themselves look directly at her, because fieldwork had taught them that the things you refused to look at grew teeth in the dark. "You were on the porch."

"I'm often on the porch."

"And now you're inside."

"I'm often inside too." A pause, and then, with the gentle correction of a teacher who has all the time in the world: "You just weren't looking before."

There was no cruelty in it. That was somehow worse. Jude had spent their career among storytellers and knew every register of human menace — the threat wrapped in charm, the threat dressed as grief, the threat that wore kindness like a borrowed coat. Marguerite's voice held none of these. It held only patience, vast and unhurried, the patience of something that measured time differently than people did. The patience of stone. Of water working at a wall.

"I'm going to sit down," Jude said, and was annoyed at how much the sentence sounded like asking permission.

They sat across from her. Up close she smelled faintly of rain after all — petrichor, wet slate — though her coat stayed dry. The lamp between them buzzed. Somewhere out in the village a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"You've been listening to the song," Marguerite said.

It wasn't a question, but Jude answered it anyway, because answering felt safer than silence. "I'm a folklorist. It's my work. The harvest song is—"

"It's a counting rhyme." She tilted her head. "You know that already. You worked it out with Bram, and he went white as flour, and now you've been reading the parish book every night with a lamp like this one." Her eyes flicked to the table where Jude's notebook lay closed, where the transcripts lay under a stone from the garden. "You're very good at your work. You always were good at looking at things."

"*Always?*" Jude caught it before they could stop themselves. "You haven't seen me since I was seven."

Haven't I, said the shape of her silence.

Jude's throat tightened. They looked away, at the window, at their own reflection ghosted against the black glass — a thin adult face, tired, thirty-one years of living written into it — and beside it, in the reflection, the small bright figure at the table, watching them watch themselves.

"The song," Marguerite said, "has a last verse."

The words landed in Jude's chest like a stone dropped down a shaft. They waited for the sound of it hitting bottom. It never came.

"Every song has a last verse," Jude managed.

"Not every song's last verse is *unsung*." She unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the table, palms down, the way children do when they are about to say something important and have rehearsed it. "The one the tavern woman sang — that's the naming part. One for this, two for that, a name for every number until the numbers run out. But the song doesn't end there. It ends where everyone stops singing. There's a verse after the counting. Nobody sings it. Nobody *can* sing it, mostly. It isn't kept in mouths. It's kept in—" she searched, frowning slightly, a child hunting for a word just beyond reach, "—in *keeping*. Like a thing put away in a box. And you knew it once, Jude. You knew it whole."

"That's not possible."

"You knew it before you knew your times tables. You used to hum it in the bath. You got the tune wrong on purpose so it wouldn't count anything." Her voice stayed level, kind, terrible. "You were careful even then. You were always careful."

Jude stood up. The chair legs shrieked against the flagstone and the sound seemed to offend the room, seemed to make the lamplight flinch. "I don't remember any bath. I don't remember—" And there it was again, the hole, the soft place in the floor of their memory where a floorboard should be. Their childhood was a house with rooms they'd never entered: they knew the facts of it, the address, the school, the shape of their mother's handwriting, but the texture of it — the smells, the afternoons, the *feel* of being small here — was gauze. They had assumed for twenty years that this was ordinary. Everyone's childhood thinned. Everyone's early years went soft at the edges.

Standing in this kitchen with a dead child's imaginary friend telling them what they used to hum in the bath, Jude understood, with a lurch like a lift cable snapping, that theirs hadn't thinned. It had been *taken*. Neatly. Lovingly, even. The way you'd take a splinter out of a sleeping child's foot.

"Who are you?" Jude asked. It came out rougher than intended. "What are you? Because you're not — you were never real. I made you up. I was a lonely kid and I made you up out of a raincoat and a storybook and you *stopped* when I stopped needing you, that's how it works, that's how it's supposed to work—"

"Yes," Marguerite agreed placidly. "That's how it's supposed to work."

The agreement was worse than argument. Jude pressed both palms flat against the counter and breathed, and counted — one for the well, two for the green, three for the churchyard yew — and hated that they were counting, hated that the rhyme's skeleton lived in them deep enough to offer its bones as comfort.

When they looked up, Marguerite had moved.

She had not stood, had not walked; she was simply closer, three feet nearer than she had been, still seated, still folded, and the space she had crossed was empty behind her like a skipped stitch. Jude's body reacted before their mind: a full-body cold flush, hair lifting on the forearms, the animal certainty of prey. They stepped back and their spine found the cupboard door.

"I'm not going to touch you," Marguerite said, and there was something almost wounded in it, almost young. "I would never hurt you. You have to believe that. Everything I do is so that you won't be hurt. Or—" the frown again, the word-hunting frown, "—so that less of you will be hurt than otherwise. It's complicated. It was explained to me badly and I only half understand it myself."

"What happens in autumn?" Jude asked. The question came from somewhere below thought, from the place where the dread had been pooling all week. "In the song. What happens when the numbers run out?"

Marguerite looked at them for a long moment. In the lamplight her tea-brown eyes had depth to them now, more depth than a child's eyes should hold, strata of something older showing through like rings in cut wood.

"This year," she said, "the song wants a name it hasn't asked for yet. And the village will give it, because the village always gives it. That's what the singing is *for*. That's the trade the whole place is built on, and has been built on since before the church, since before the well had a grate. You've read the records. You've seen how the names line up with the leaving."

"Bram's brother."

"Bram's brother. And others. And soon—" She stopped herself. Jude saw her stop herself, saw the small jaw set, and understood that she was being careful with them, editing for their protection, and that the thing she wasn't saying was a name.

"My name," Jude said. Not a question either. Their voice sounded far away to them, calm, underwater. "It's going to be my name."

Marguerite didn't confirm it. She didn't need to. The silence did it for her, filled the kitchen floor to ceiling with it.

And here was the thing Jude would turn over for many nights afterward, examining it like a stone found in a pocket: in that moment of confirmation, terror was not the only thing in them. Braided through the fear, wound tight around it, was a horrible, shameful *relief* — because if the song wanted them, then the song was real, which meant Marguerite was real, which meant the soft rooms in their childhood had contents after all, that something had been taken rather than never having existed, that the loneliness they'd carried their whole life like a stooped shoulder was an injury and not a nature. Terror and longing, sharing one pulse. They wanted to run from her and they wanted, desperately, disgracefully, to sit back down at the table and ask her to tell them everything, every lost afternoon, every vanished year, to fill them back up like pouring water into a cracked jug.

"Tell me about the last verse," Jude said.

"No." Marguerite shook her head, and the hood shifted behind her shoulders. "Not tonight. Tonight you'll only argue with me, and arguing burns time we don't have."

"Then tell me one thing. One true thing I can check."

She considered this with grave seriousness, the way she had once — *had she once? had there ever been a once?* — considered the rules of games invented on rainy pavements. Then she recited:

"*One for the secret, two for the sin,*"

— and it was not her voice.

It came out of her small mouth in Jude's own voice. Not an impression, not a mimicry — their voice, exact, the specific grain of it, the slight rasp on the sibilants, the cadence their university students would have recognized across a lecture hall. Hearing it issue from that round child's face was like seeing their own reflection worn by someone else, like a mask of themselves fitted over something smaller. The words hung in the kitchen. The dog outside began barking again and then, mid-bark, stopped as if a hand had closed over its muzzle.

Jude's knees nearly went. They locked them. "Say it again."

"No," Marguerite said, in her own voice now, gentle. "Once is enough. You know why. You felt why." She slid off the chair — she'd been sitting on books, Jude realized numbly, two stacked volumes of their own field notes — and stood there, the top of her head barely reaching Jude's sternum, looking up with an expression that was almost apologetic. "The verse is a door, and voices are keys, and yours is already cut. If you sing along — even a line, even humming, even thinking it too hard on a bad night — the door starts opening from your side. And then the giving starts early, and early giving is *ragged* giving. Trust me. Please trust me. I am the only creature in this village that is entirely on your side, and I know how that sounds."

"How does it end?" Jude whispered. "*One for the secret, two for the sin* — how does it end?"

"When you can hear the rest without me saying it," Marguerite said, "you'll be ready. And when you're ready, you'll wish you weren't." She moved toward the back hall — toward the sealed door of the back bedroom, Jude noticed, toward the door with the painted-shut frame and the furniture-shaped shadows under it — and paused there, one small hand resting on the wall as if she could feel the room beyond it through the plaster. Her face, in profile, was suddenly very old and very sad.

"There's something in that room you need to see," she said. "But not tonight. Tonight you should sleep, and lock nothing, because locks only insult me and I forget them by morning anyway." She glanced back, and the chipped tooth showed in a smile that was trying very hard to be reassuring and nearly managed it. "You came back late, Jude. But you came back. That's more than most get to do."

The lamp flickered — a single stutter, less than a blink —

— and the kitchen was empty. The chair sat pushed back. Two of Jude's own notebooks lay squared neatly on the floor beside it, spines aligned, the way a considerate guest tidies up after themselves.

Jude stood in the ringing quiet for a long time. Then, moving like someone wading upstream, they took the stone off the transcripts, opened the notebook to a fresh page, and wrote, in a hand that only shook a little:

*Last verse exists. First line: "One for the secret, two for the sin." Recited in my voice. Do not sing. Do not hum. Do not think it too hard.*

Below that, after a while, smaller:

*I knew it once. She says I knew it once.*

And below that, because honesty is a habit that survives even horror, in letters pressed so hard the nib nearly tore the page:

*God help me, I want it back.*

Out in the hall, behind the sealed door of the back bedroom, something settled — softly, patiently, the way a house settles, the way a held breath settles — and waited for morning.
