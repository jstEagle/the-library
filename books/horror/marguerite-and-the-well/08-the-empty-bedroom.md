## 8. The Empty Bedroom

Jude woke on the fifth morning in Hollowmere to the sound of rain and the certainty that someone had been in the room while they slept.

Not a sound, exactly. Nothing so useful as a creaking floorboard or the click of a latch. It was more a residue, the way a room holds the shape of a voice after the voice has gone — a warmth at the foot of the bed, a faint sweetness like wet wool and wildflowers, and the impression, fading fast as surf, of someone sitting very still, watching them with the patience of a thing that had waited twenty years already and could wait twenty more.

"Marguerite," Jude said to the empty room, and hated how the name came out — not a question, but a greeting. An old habit rising up through the sediment like a coin from a drained pond.

The rain kept on. The cottage kept its silence. But when Jude went to the window, the porch steps below were dark and empty, and Jude felt the absence there like a pulled tooth — which was its own kind of horror, because you could not miss a thing you had never had, and some traitor part of them had been missing Marguerite for two decades without ever once knowing it.

*You came back late,* she had said, the first night. As if Jude were the one who had kept an appointment.

Jude dressed, made tea they didn't drink, and sat at the kitchen table with their mother's letters spread out in the grey light. The letters had come with the cottage — the landlord, a soft-voiced man named Ellery whose face Jude kept forgetting the moment he left a room, had mentioned that the previous tenant had left a box behind, and that it had Jude's name on it in their mother's handwriting. Eleven letters, all addressed to Jude at the old university address, all returned unopened, all postmarked within the same six-month span the year before their mother died. Jude had been traveling. Jude had been, as their mother's final voicemail put it, *very hard to hold onto, my love, you always were.*

The letters mentioned the cottage. The letters mentioned the weather, the roof, the well cap rusting through, the price of coal. The letters mentioned a great many things.

They mentioned one child.

*You were such a small thing in that back room,* the earliest letter said. *I could hear you talking to yourself through the wall, telling your stories to the air, and I never had the heart to tell you I could hear.*

Jude read the line again. *Talking to yourself.* The phrase sat wrong, the way a familiar word sits wrong when you stare at it long enough — *yourself, yourself, yourself* — until it comes apart into noise.

Through the wall.

Jude looked up. The kitchen's back wall held a door. It had held a door all week, and Jude had registered it the way you register a nail in a beam: without interest. It was painted shut — not locked, not boarded, just painted, layer over layer, white over white, until the door had become part of the wall and the wall had become part of the house and no one, looking at it, would ever think *room* at all.

Except that the doorframe was warm.

Jude crossed the kitchen and laid a palm against the painted panels. Warm — not the warmth of the house, which was coal-heated and uneven, but a steady, low warmth, like a hand held over a candle at a careful distance. And beneath the paint, when Jude pressed their ear to it, there was a sound. Not a voice. A hush. The particular hush of a room that has been kept, that has been *tended*, that has been waiting the way a bed is made each morning for someone expected home.

"You promised you'd remember."

The voice came from directly behind Jude, close enough that the words stirred the hair at their nape. Jude spun. Marguerite stood in the middle of the kitchen, in her yellow raincoat, dry in the rain-damp air, her hands folded in front of her like a child at a school inspection. She was exactly as Jude remembered her — and this was the thing Jude could no longer look at directly, the fact that they *did* remember her, in perfect detail, down to the chip in the left knee of the raincoat and the way one sock had slipped down inside her boot. Twenty years. No child's imaginary friend survived that long in the memory at that resolution. Memory was a lossy thing; it blurred and simplified and let go. But Marguerite had been kept. Preserved. Like fruit in wax.

"I promised," Jude said. Their voice sounded strange in their own ears, thin, younger than it had any right to be. "I don't — Marguerite, I don't know what you're talking about. I don't know what you are."

"You know." No accusation in it. Nothing at all in it, really — that alien calm, that terrible evenness, like the surface of the well. "You just aren't allowed to. There's a difference. There's always been a difference." She turned her head toward the painted door, and something moved behind her face that was almost sorrow, almost hunger, almost both at once. "It's time you saw the room."

"I don't want—"

"You never wanted to," Marguerite said. "That's not the same as not needing to."

She reached out and laid her small hand flat against the door, and the paint — twenty years of it, forty, a lifetime of it — came away like the skin of milk.

* * *

The door opened without a sound, which was somehow worse than a groan would have been. Groans are honest. The door simply swung, and the smell came out to meet them: dust, yes, but under the dust something green and living, the smell of a room that had been breathing.

Jude's hand found the light switch by the doorframe — found it *without looking*, found it at the exact height their hand expected, and that small betrayal of the body nearly undid them before anything else could. Their fingers knew this room. Their fingers had done this a thousand times.

The switch clicked. A bare bulb woke overhead, yellow and stuttering, and the back bedroom of the cottage assembled itself out of the dark.

Two beds.

Not one bed and the ghost of one, not a bed and a storage space, not a room that had once held a bed and been converted. Two beds, identical, iron-framed, each with a stripped mattress, each against its own wall, each with its own window — two small windows set into the back of the cottage like a pair of eyes, and through them the rain ran down in two identical curtains. Between the beds, exactly equidistant, stood a small chest of drawers. On it, a jar. In the jar, wildflowers — fresh, vivid, impossible, their petals still beaded with this morning's rain, though the room had been sealed behind paint since before Jude could say.

"Marguerite." Jude's voice cracked on the second syllable. "Whose room is this?"

Marguerite didn't answer. She crossed to the nearer bed and sat on its edge, and the mattress did not dip under her, and Jude made themselves not think about that, made themselves look at the walls instead, because the walls were easier and the walls were worse.

Drawings. Dozens of them, crayon on paper, taped up in careful rows at child height, and Jude recognized the hand immediately because it was their own — the same lopsided letters, the same way of drawing hands as little starfish, the same purple used for everything that should have been brown. Jude's drawings. Jude's handwriting. Jude's name signed in the corner in that wobbling childhood script: *JUDE, AGE 6.*

And beside each one, a second drawing. A second hand.

The second hand was better. That was the first thing Jude noticed, with the cold professional part of the mind that catalogued even while it drowned — the second child drew with more control, finer lines, a surer sense of composition. The second child drew the well on the green, over and over, in careful cross-hatched stone. The second child drew the harvest fire. The second child drew two figures on a swing, and the figures were always holding hands, and the second figure always had yellow hair, and the signature in the corner was not a name.

It was a small round symbol. A circle with a line through it, like a coin seen edge-on. Like a mouth, closed.

"That's mine," Marguerite said. Not looking at the drawings. Looking at her hands. "The ones with the circle. I signed them so she'd know they were mine and not hers. We argued about it. She said I was copying her." A pause, and then, with the first inflection Jude had ever heard in that flat calm — something small and wounded and very old: "I wasn't copying her."

"Who," Jude said. The word came out with no air behind it. "Who is *she*."

Marguerite turned her head, and for a moment — one long, rain-heavy moment — Jude saw past the yellow raincoat and the child's face and the twenty-year stillness, saw down into whatever was keeping that shape upright, and what was there was not a person. It was a room. It was a room full of kept things. It was a room that had been sealed and painted and tended, and it was looking at Jude with all of its windows at once.

"You know her name," Marguerite said. "You sang it to her every night. You made it up together, out of a song your mother sang, and you argued about the last verse the way we argued about the drawings. She wanted it to end with coming back. You wanted it to end with staying." Marguerite stood. The mattress still did not move. "You promised you'd remember, Jude. You hooked your little finger and you said it, and I was there, and she was there, and your mother was downstairs, and the song was in the walls. You said *I'll remember, I'll remember, I'll remember even if I forget everything else.*"

"Stop."

"You were very serious about it. You were six."

"*Stop.*" Jude's hand came up as if to ward off a blow, and Marguerite — patient, gentle, awful — took it. Her hand was cool and dry and it did not feel like a child's hand at all; it felt like paper, like a page, like something written on.

"You didn't break your promise," she said. "I want you to understand that. You didn't break it. It was *taken* out of you. There's a difference. There's always been a difference." She guided Jude's hand, slowly, toward the wall above the second bed, and pressed their palm flat against the plaster. "Feel."

And Jude felt.

Not with the hand. With something older and lower than the hand, some nerve that ran beneath memory the way roots run beneath a road. The wall was warm, and the warmth had a rhythm, and the rhythm was a heartbeat, and the heartbeat was not the house's. It was small and quick, a child's heartbeat, a seven-year-old's heartbeat, and it was coming from the other side of the plaster, and it had been coming from the other side of the plaster for twenty years, and every letter their mother had ever written had said *one child, my love, you were always one child, you talked to yourself through the wall* —

Jude tore their hand away and turned and was sick into the wastebasket, and Marguerite stood beside them the whole time with her hand hovering an inch from their back, not quite touching, patient as rain.

When Jude could stand upright again, they went to the chest of drawers because they could not bear the walls and could not bear the beds and could not bear the windows with their two identical curtains of rain. The drawers opened easily. Inside: folded clothes, small, two sizes, nearly the same size. A hair ribbon, yellow. A child's tooth in a matchbox. And at the bottom of the bottom drawer, wrapped in a tea towel gone soft as skin, a photograph.

Jude unwrapped it with hands that shook and did not feel like their own hands.

Two children on the porch steps of this cottage. Rain boots. A swing visible at the frame's edge. The elder child — Jude, unmistakably Jude, the same face that looked back now from every mirror, younger, rounder, sunburned — sat with an arm slung around the smaller one's shoulders, grinning with the whole-hearted ferocity of a child who has not yet learned that the world subtracts.

The smaller child was blurred. Not motion-blurred. *Removed*-blurred — the features smeared as if a wet thumb had worked at them for years, patiently, lovingly, the way you rub at a stain until the fabric itself gives way. But the yellow raincoat was crisp. The yellow raincoat was the sharpest thing in the photograph.

Jude sat down on the floor of the sealed room, between the two beds, and held the photograph, and wept — not for anything they could name, because there was nothing they could name, that was the whole unbearable architecture of it, a grief with its name scraped out like the last verse of a hymnal, like a face in a photograph, like a word in a register. They wept for a hole shaped exactly like a person. They wept the way you weep for a word on the tip of the tongue. They wept for the sibling-shaped silence in every letter their mother ever wrote, in every story of their childhood, in the shape of the cottage itself, which had been built — had it not? — with a room for two.

Marguerite sat down beside them. She did not touch them. She looked at the photograph in Jude's hands with an expression that on a living child's face would have been called longing, and on hers could only be called *homesickness*, and she said, very quietly:

"She waited for you to come back. That was her half of the song. She wanted it to end with coming back." A pause. The rain filled it. "You were supposed to be the one who stayed."

Jude looked at her. At the yellow raincoat. At the circle-with-a-line signature, the closed mouth, the coin seen edge-on.

"Marguerite," Jude said slowly, "that's not a name. Six-year-olds don't invent names like that. That's a *flower*. That's something out of a song."

Marguerite smiled, and it was the saddest thing Jude had ever seen, and it was the smile of someone about to hand over the last of something.

"Keep going," she said. "You're remembering how to ask."

And from the other side of the wall — faint, rhythmic, patient as it had been for twenty years — came the sound of a small hand, tapping. Not a heartbeat after all. A pattern. Three taps, pause, three taps, pause. The signal of two children in neighboring beds, through the wall, after lights out.

*Still there? Still there? Still there?*

And Jude's own hand, before they could stop it, rose and answered.
