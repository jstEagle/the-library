## 19. Down the Throat of It

The grate had been folded back like a page someone meant to return to, and now Jude stood at the lip of the well with the verse held behind their teeth like a coal they could neither swallow nor spit out.

Below, the singing rose.

The village stood in a ring around them — Bram with his fists clenched at his sides as though holding his own hands down, old Mrs. Tallow with her mouth moving in perfect unison with voices she did not seem to know she had, children too young to know the words singing them anyway, their small faces slack and lit from beneath by something that was not lantern light. The naming verses came out of them in a single braided voice, number after number, name after name, thirty years of given-away people recited by the throats of those who had forgotten giving them. The sound was not loud. That was the worst of it. It was gentle, almost tender, a lullaby sung downward, and the well drank it the way dry earth drinks rain.

Jude could feel it drinking. Standing at the lip, they felt the pull in their sternum, in the soft place behind the eyes where memory lived. Every verse loosened something. The swing in the yard went first — Jude felt it go, felt the ropes and the wooden seat and the particular creak of the chain slide away like water through fingers — and they understood with terrible clarity that if they waited for the song to finish, there would be nothing left of them to trade. The thing below was not waiting to take. It was already taking. It had always already been taking; the song was only the sound of its appetite made polite.

*Learn it whole. Never sing it.*

Marguerite's voice, thin as paper now, spent nearly to nothing: *You have to go down while you still remember why.*

Jude swung one leg over the iron lip. The stone was warm. That detail would stay with them longer than any other, they suspected — not the dark, not the depth, but the warmth, the awful animal warmth of a throat about to swallow. Their boot found a foothold in the old mortar. Then the other. And then, hand over hand down the wet inner curve of the well, Jude Vasquez left the sky.

The last thing they saw above was Marguerite standing at the edge in her yellow raincoat, looking down at them without expression, and the last thing they heard was not the village's song at all but her voice, very small, saying what she had said on the porch on the first night: *You came back late.* And then: *But you came back.*

Then the dark took them, and the dark was not empty.

---

It is difficult to describe falling when there is no bottom to fall toward. Jude descended through cold that became warmth again, through air that thickened until it was less like air than like water, like amniotic dark, like the inside of an ear pressed against a door. Sounds reached them from every direction and none: fragments of conversations, half-sentences, a woman laughing at a joke no one had told in forty years, a dog barking, a screen door slamming, a hymn cut off mid-phrase. Everything Hollowmere had ever swallowed was still here, still going, suspended in the dark like insects in amber. Nothing was destroyed. Nothing was ever destroyed. It was only kept, and kept, and kept, until the keeping itself became a hunger.

Jude saw faces.

They hung in the blackness at the edge of vision — not ghosts, exactly, not even presences, more like the shapes memories make when you try to look at them directly. A man in a flat cap turning as if called. A girl jumping rope, the rope passing under her feet forever. Bram's brother, unmistakably, with Bram's same heavy brow, sitting on a fence rail and whittling a stick that never got shorter, waiting with the patience of the filed-away. He looked up as Jude passed — or sank, or drifted; the verbs failed — and his eyes were not accusing. They were only surprised, the way a photograph is surprised to be looked at after so long.

*I remember you,* Jude wanted to say. *Bram remembers you. He's been carrying your name like a stone in his shoe for thirty years.* But the verse could not be spent here, scattered among the shelves of the taken. It had to be given whole, at the center, to the mouth itself. So Jude kept sinking, past the rope-jumper, past a wedding party frozen mid-toast, past a hundred small ordinary moments that some family somewhere still set an extra plate for without knowing why, and the weight of all of it pressed against them like deep water, and they understood that this was what forgetting cost. Not absence. Storage. Someone paid for every vanished thing, always, and the currency was this: a well full of everything, and no one up top who knew it was there.

And then, in a pocket of dark that was somehow quieter than the rest — quieter the way a room is quiet when someone in it has just stopped crying — Jude saw the swing.

Not the memory of the swing. The actual swing, hanging from nothing, its chains running up into the black overhead and vanishing. And on the grass beneath it — grass that smelled impossibly of summer, of cut green and hot pavement — sat a child.

Seven years old. Small hands. Hair cut badly, the way hair gets cut when a mother does it herself over the kitchen sink. The child was drawing in the dirt with a stick, and the drawing was a house with two windows, and in each window a stick figure, and the figures were holding hands across the space between the houses.

Jude's heart did not stop. Worse: it recognized.

"Hello," the child said, without looking up. "You're late too."

The voice went through Jude like a hook through soft flesh. They knew that voice. They had known it before they were born, almost; they had known it in the cradle, in the dark of shared rooms, in the fevered nights when two small bodies slept back to back for warmth. It was a voice their body remembered even though their mind had been emptied of it, and the recognition was physical, cellular, a grief located in the blood.

"I don't—" Jude began, and knelt, because kneeling was the only possible response to that face. "I don't know your name."

"I know." The child said it without bitterness, the way a child reports weather. "That's okay. She said you'd come anyway." The stick paused in the dirt. "She's getting really quiet now. Marguerite. Did she tell you? She's mostly raincoat now. There's not much girl left inside it." The child finally looked up, and the eyes were the eyes from the photographs in the sealed bedroom, the drawings in crayon, the second bed. "I've been waiting a long time. But I didn't get scared, because I couldn't remember being anywhere else. You can't be scared of a place if it's the only place. That's the trick of it. That's how they got everyone to stop asking."

"What's your name?" Jude asked again, and hated the crack in their own voice, and asked anyway. "Please. I'm supposed to carry it out of here. I need it."

"You'll get it back," the child said. "When you do the thing. She said you'd do the thing." The child stood, brushed off their knees, and looked at Jude with an expression far too old for the face wearing it — the expression of someone who has been seven years old for thirty years and knows precisely what that costs. "But I want to say it once, before. In case. My name is—"

And the dark took the name. Not muffled it — took it, the way the song took names, reaching in between the child's lips and lifting the word out before it could exist. The child's mouth kept moving. No sound came. And Jude watched their sibling try, with all the force of a small furious body, to say their own name into a darkness designed to eat exactly that, and fail, and fail, and fail, and then simply shrug, the way children shrug, and sit back down in the grass.

"It does that," the child said. "Don't worry. It can't keep it if you give the trade right. She was very clear about the trade." A pause. "Are you scared?"

"Yes," Jude said honestly.

"Good. She said the scared ones do it right. The brave ones try to fight it, and fighting makes it hungry." The child picked up the stick again and added a third figure to the drawing — a smaller one, standing between the houses. "That's you," the child said. "From before. When there were three."

Something broke open in Jude's chest, and what came out was not a sob but a vow, wordless, structural, the kind of promise that is made below language. They reached out and touched the child's hair — real, warm, alive, kept alive all these years in the dark like fruit in a cellar — and the child leaned into the touch with the unselfconscious trust of the very young, and Jude thought: *I gave up ten years of you to save myself a delay. I will not leave here without giving back more than I take.*

"Wait for me," Jude said. "One more minute. Just wait one more minute."

"I'm good at that," the child said, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was patient, and patience in a seven-year-old is a crime scene.

---

Jude sank further.

At the bottom of everything — below the stored summers, below the stolen names, below the shelf after shelf of the village's amputated past — there was a chamber that was not a chamber. It was a attention. Jude felt it before they saw anything: the sensation of being regarded, total and ancient, the same pressure they had felt in the dream at the well's lip, but now from all sides, now from inside their own skull. The dark gathered itself. It had shape the way a whale has shape in deep water — vast, slow, mostly suggested — and its skin was made of the things it kept. Jude saw moons of forgotten birthdays turning on its flank. Saw whole Christmases embedded in it like fossils. Saw, near what might have been its eye, a yellow scrap of fabric, and understood with a lurch that part of Marguerite was down here too, or had been, once, before the well returned her as a mercy.

It looked at Jude. And Jude, hanging in the throat of the world, did the thing the frightened ones do right.

They did not fight. They opened.

They let it see everything — the archive recordings with their gaps, the parish register with the scratched-out line, the two beds, the mother's letters, the porch, the crossroads, the nightly lessons, Marguerite growing fainter verse by verse like a candle measured out in syllables. They let it see the swing, the fever, the small hand in theirs. They let it see the child in the grass below, drawing three figures. They held nothing back, because a trade made with a closed fist is not a trade, and this thing had been cheated before — that was the whole history of Hollowmere, generation after generation of villagers who had tried to pay in forgetting instead of remembering, who had handed over their dead and then handed over the knowledge of the dead, feeding it scraps and calling it survival.

Jude spoke.

The last verse came out of them whole, alone, in a voice that did not shake — not because they were brave but because the verse carried them, the way a river carries a swimmer who has finally stopped thrashing. It was a counting rhyme, but counted backward. It named no one. Instead it *unnamed* — it took each number and gave it back its person, each stolen name and gave it back its street, its bed, its laugh, its place at table. It was a song of return, and it had never been sung aloud in the history of the world, and the dark around Jude shuddered at it the way a body shudders at a truth.

And in the middle of the verse, Jude said the thing's name.

They had gotten it from Marguerite in the final lesson, the last coin she had, spent with her last breath of substance: the well had been a well once, dug by hands, named by hands, blessed by a priest whose name was also eaten later — and the thing below had been born from the well's forgetting, a creature made entirely of what was discarded, including its own original name, which was the first thing the village had ever thrown away. Jude spoke that name now, softly, the way you speak to something wounded, and the vast attention flinched through all its fathoms.

"There," Jude said. "That's yours. Nobody else's. Your own name, kept all this time." Their voice cracked at last, and they let it. "Now hear my terms."

The dark listened. All of Hollowmere's dead listened. Somewhere above, faintly, the village's compelled chorus faltered, one voice at a time, like candles going out in a draft.

"Everything you took. Everyone. Every name, every year, every memory you were fed instead of love. Give it back — all of it, tonight, up through the stone, into the heads that are missing it. Let them wake weeping. Let them set the extra places. Let the song end where it should." Jude's hands were fists now, and shaking, and they did not care. "And in exchange—"

Here it was. The price. The dearest thing.

"In exchange you can have all of it. My childhood. Ten years, whole. The swing, the fever, the hand in mine. Her face —" and here the grief hit like a truck, and Jude rode it, rode it down — "*her face*, the yellow coat, the porch, every lesson, every night. Take the knowing of her. Take the knowing of *them*." Below, distantly, the child in the grass looked up. "I'll climb out of this well tomorrow and not know why the back bedroom has two beds. I'll meet my own blood as a stranger. That's the payment. Everything taken, for everything I remember. Fierce love, priced accordingly. Do we have a trade?"

The silence lasted a year.

Then the thing below moved — not toward Jude, but *around* them, a slow tidal turning, and Jude felt its answer arrive not as sound but as consent, vast and relieved, the consent of a creature that had been starving on scraps for centuries and had just been offered, for the first time, a meal given freely rather than stolen in fear. Free gifts weighed more. That was the oldest law in Hollowmere, older than the song. The village had never once understood it.

The dark began to rise.

It rose through the stone like water finding its level, carrying everything — the rope-jumper, the man in the flat cap, the wedding party, the whittling brother, the laughter, the hymns, the names, the names, the names — up and out and home. And Jude, clinging to the inner wall of the well as the exodus thundered past them upward, felt the payment begin at the same moment, gently, from the edges inward.

The swing went. The fever went. The porch went.

Marguerite went — and she went slowly, mercifully, and in the last instant before the yellow coat dissolved from Jude's memory forever, Jude heard her, everywhere and nowhere, finish the verse with them, the only time she ever sang along:

*and what was lost comes home again,*

*what's given freely, given twice—*

Then even the shape of her absence was gone, and Jude hung alone in an ordinary stone shaft that smelled of rain and old mortar, and far above, impossibly far, there was a gray thread of dawn.

And below — below, in the settling quiet, small feet were climbing the dark toward them, and a voice Jude would not recognize in an hour was saying, breathless, hopeful, strange:

"Hey. Hey — wait for me. I don't know your name, but I think... I think you're supposed to help me up."
