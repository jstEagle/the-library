## 4. The Village That Counts Its Dead

Tam Hobb had buried forty-one people in his thirty years as sexton of Hollowmere, and he had a private theory about grief: it was like weather. You couldn't argue with it, you couldn't hurry it, and the only sensible thing to do was dress for it and wait.

Sable Yarrow, in Tam's professional opinion, was dressed for a blizzard.

The man had turned up at the churchyard gate at half past eight in the morning, unshaven, coat buttoned wrong — one hole off, so the whole garment sat crooked on him like a sentence with a word missing — and asked, in the flat voice of someone who has not slept and does not intend to try, whether Tam could show him "the book."

"The book," Tam said.

"The register. The burial register."

"Ah." Tam leaned on his spade. It was a good spade; he'd had it since he was nineteen and it knew more about Hollowmere's soil than Tam did. "Now, that's an interesting request, Mr. Yarrow, because there are two sorts of people who ask to see the register. The first sort is historians. Nice people. Bring their own gloves. The second sort—"

"Which sort am I?"

"Haven't decided yet." Tam studied him. Up close, Sable Yarrow had the look of a man who'd been arguing with a mirror and losing. There was pondweed — actual pondweed, green and cheerful as a salad garnish — caught in the collar of his coat, and either he hadn't noticed or he'd noticed and decided it wasn't worth the fight. Tam respected that. "You're Marguerite's brother."

"I'm—" A pause. A small, terrible pause, the kind Tam had learned to hear the way other men heard a crack in ice. "Yes."

"Well then." Tam shouldered the spade. "Come on, then. Church is cold this time of day, but the vestry's got a stove, and Mrs. Petch leaves a kettle on it out of what I can only assume is fear of me."

* * *

The vestry smelled of dust, candle wax, and forty years of Mrs. Petch's fruitcake, which she baked for the parish and which everyone praised and nobody finished. Tam got the stove going while Sable stood in the middle of the room turning slowly, taking inventory, the way a man takes inventory when he suspects something has been taken.

"You were sexton when my sister died?" Sable asked.

"No." Tam set the kettle on. "I was sexton when your sister *didn't* die, if you follow me. Which I suspect you don't yet, but give it time. Hollowmere's patient."

"That's the second time someone's said something like that to me. Patient. Like the village is waiting for something."

Tam opened the cupboard where the registers lived. They stood in a row, cloth-bound, spines gone soft as old bread, each one fatter than the last because the parish had never quite trusted paper alone and so recorded everything twice — once in ink, once in the vicar's memory, and once more, unofficially, in the margins, where the women of Hollowmere had been adding notes for two hundred years. *Died of the fever. Died of the childbed. Died of nothing anyone could name.* And beside one entry from 1888, in a hand like bird tracks: *died walking on the water, which is not possible, and yet.*

He lifted down the current volume and laid it on the table between them.

"Now," Tam said, "before you open that, I want you to understand something about me. I'm not a clever man. I'm a digger. I dig holes, I fill holes, and in between I keep accounts. That's all a sexton is, really — a man who keeps accounts for the ground. And the ground, Mr. Yarrow, is very particular about its arithmetic."

Sable opened the register.

Tam watched him rather than the page, because he'd seen this before — or something like it, once, decades ago, when old Mrs. Yarrow herself had come asking after her own mother's entry and gone quiet in exactly that way. Sable's finger moved down the columns. Stopped. Moved back up. Stopped again.

"There are two entries," he said.

"There are."

"For every Yarrow death. Two."

"That's right."

Sable looked up. His eyes had the wet-bright look of a man holding himself together with habit. "That's a clerical error repeated across a century?"

"It's not an error." Tam poured two cups of tea without being asked, because Mrs. Petch's rules were Mrs. Petch's rules, and pushed one across. "Drink that. You've gone the color of the lichen on the north wall."

Sable drank. Tam took the opportunity to turn the register around and lay his own blunt finger on the entries, reading them aloud in the voice he used for funerals, which was low and even and left no room for anything to hide in.

"Here. 1843. *Edmund Yarrow, drowned, aged 31.* And beneath it, same hand, different ink — *Edmund Yarrow, died in his bed, aged 74.* Two entries. One man. Thirty-one years apart."

"That's impossible."

"So's most of this book, if you read it straight through." Tam turned pages. "1861. *Alice Yarrow, fever, aged 9.* And here, 1902 — *Alice Yarrow, drowned, aged 50.* Same girl. Twice. Once young, once old. The village has always written them both down."

"Why?"

"Because we count our dead twice in Hollowmere." Tam said it simply, because it was simply true, and simple truths were the only kind he was any good at. "Once for the body and once for the rest of them. My grandmother explained it to me when I was a boy, and she explained it badly, the way grandmothers do, but the shape of it stuck. She said: everything that goes into the well comes back out again eventually, and the book has to be ready for it. So we write the death, and we leave a space, and when they come back — however they come back, however long it takes — we write the return. Two entries. One for going down. One for coming up."

Sable had gone very still. Outside, a crow said something rude to another crow, and neither of them meant it personally.

"And the well's been dry since 1911," Sable said slowly. "Since the flood. Since the bell was melted down."

"Aye."

"So the returns stopped."

"Not stopped." Tam scratched under his hat, where his hair did what it liked these days. "Slowed. There's been three since the flood. All Yarrows. All twins." He watched Sable absorb that. It went into him like water into chalk — you could see it spreading, darkening, finding every crack. "Your grandmother, 1961. Your father, 1994. And now—"

He turned to the final page. The newest ink was barely a season old, brown-black, written in Tam's own hand, because it had fallen to him, as it fell to every sexton, to make the entry when the winter came round.

There were two lines.

*MARGUERITE YARROW, aged 29, this winter.*

And beneath it, dated the same week:

*SABLE YARROW, aged 29, this winter.*

One line was crossed out. Not struck through in anger, not scribbled over — crossed out neatly, with a single rule, the way a careful accountant cancels a figure he no longer believes in. But the ink beneath the stroke was still legible. It said *SABLE*, and it said *this winter*, and it was dated seven days ago.

Tam had written that second line himself, seven days ago, in the ordinary course of his work, because the ground had told him — the way the ground always told the sexton of Hollowmere, in dreams and drips and the particular silence of the churchyard at dusk — that a Yarrow twin had come back. And then the man had walked into the village alive, breathing, complaining about the bus service, and Tam had done the only thing his office allowed. He had ruled a line through it. *Not yet,* the line said. *Not quite.*

He had not told anyone. He had certainly not expected the entry to walk into his vestry and ask for tea.

"When did you write this?" Sable's voice came out sanded flat.

"Seven days ago."

"Marguerite died six days ago."

"I know."

"You wrote my name in the burial register before my sister died."

"I wrote what the ground gave me." Tam met his eyes and did not look away, because looking away from a Yarrow in this matter would have been a kindness, and kindness, in Tam's experience, was often just cowardice wearing a nicer coat. "Mr. Yarrow — Sable, if I may — I'm going to tell you something, and then you're going to finish your tea, and then you're going to go home and sit down, because you look like a fence post in a high wind."

"Tell me."

"The book isn't finished." Tam laid his palm flat on the open page, over the two names, the living and the cancelled. "Every pair in this register resolves. That's the whole point of keeping it. A death goes in, a return goes in, and the account balances. Except yours." He tapped the crossed-out line gently, the way you'd tap a door you weren't sure you wanted answered. "Yours is still open. The ground put your name in this winter, same week as hers, and then you turned up walking and talking, and I thought — well. Perhaps the ground made a mistake. Ground doesn't make mistakes, though. I've dug enough of it to know."

"Then what did it mean?"

"I don't know." And that was the truth, and it cost Tam something to say it, because a sexton who didn't know was like a doctor with no medicine — useless, and aware of it. "But I'll tell you what my grandmother told me, the one useful thing she ever said. She said: *when the book writes a name twice in the same winter, boy, it means the well can't tell the difference yet. It means it's still deciding which one it got.*"

The stove ticked. The kettle sighed itself quiet. Somewhere above them, in the belfry where no bell had hung since 1911, something shifted its weight — a soft, settling sound, like a large animal turning over in sleep — and Tam saw Sable hear it, saw the man's whole face change as he understood that Tam heard it too, and that neither of them was going to mention it, because some things you mention and some things you let lie until you've got more tea in you.

"Mrs. Petch will want her cups back," Tam said at last, gathering them. "And you'll want to look at that journal of your sister's, I shouldn't wonder. She was in here often enough, last year, asking after the old founders' papers. Borrowed things. Never brought them back, mind, but Marguerite Yarrow could borrow anything she liked as far as I was concerned. She had her mother's way of asking." He paused at the door, hand on the latch, and found he couldn't leave it there — couldn't send the man out into the white morning with nothing but a crossed-out name in his pocket. "For what it's worth. When I ruled the line through your entry. I didn't do it because I think you're dead."

"What did you do it for, then?"

"Because I'm hoping the book's wrong." Tam smiled, and it was an honest smile, tired at the edges. "First time in thirty years I've hoped a ledger was wrong. But there's a first time for everything, and this winter appears determined to provide."

He let Sable out through the churchyard, past the fresh grave with its mound of dark earth and its temporary marker, and stood watching until the man had passed the lychgate. Then Tam went back inside, alone, and did the thing he had been putting off since dawn.

He took up his pen. He turned to the final page. And beneath the crossed-out line, in the space the ground had left — the space that was still, stubbornly, open — he wrote today's date, and left the name blank.

Just in case the book wanted to finish the entry itself.
