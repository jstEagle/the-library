## 6. The Well Refills

Sable woke on his third morning in Hollowmere to the sound of water where no water should be.

Not rain — the sky outside was the color of old pewter and holding its breath. Not pipes, either; he had spent two nights cataloguing the house's plumbing with a torch and a notebook, and had concluded that the Yarrow cottage's pipes were dry as sermon bones and sang only out of habit. This was different. This was a slow, patient sound, like something being poured from a very great height into a very deep place.

He lay still under the quilt and listened the way he used to listen as a boy, when Marguerite would dare him to name every noise in the house before they were allowed to sleep. *Floorboard settling,* she'd say, and he'd say *cat on the roof,* and between them they could explain away anything. It had been their first game and, looking back on it now, possibly their last line of defense against everything Hollowmere actually was.

*Something being poured,* said the house.

*The eaves thawing,* said Sable, and got up.

* * *

The well stood behind the church, past the yew and the leaning slate wall, in a square of ground the villagers mowed but never lingered on. Sable had found it on his first afternoon — a low circle of mossy stone with a wooden cover gone silver-grey, and an iron ring set into the coping like a finger crooked in invitation. A brass plate, green with age, read:

**HOLLOWMERE WELL — SEALED 1811 — DRY SINCE THE FLOOD**

*Dry since the flood.* The flood of 1911, when the river had come up through the lower village and taken four houses and, according to the parish register Tam had shown him, a great many names at once. After that the well had been declared dead. Wells, Sable supposed, were the one thing a village could bury without digging.

He lifted the cover.

It came away easily — too easily for a thing that hadn't been moved in decades, the hinges whispering rather than shrieking, as though someone had oiled them recently, or as though the cover itself preferred to be open.

The well was full of black water.

Ankle-deep, perhaps more — it was hard to judge depth when the surface was that dark, that still, that perfectly level, like a sheet of glass laid over a hole in the world. Sable stood very still and did the arithmetic he always did when reality misbehaved. Rainwater collects in hollows; the well is a hollow; therefore rainwater. Except there had been no rain worth the name in nine days. Except a dry well does not gather water like a bucket; a dry well drinks it, or the ground around it does. Except —

Except the water was *level*. Not pooled unevenly against one side of the stonework the way collected water always is, but flat and centered and calm, as if it had been poured by a careful hand and then told to stay.

"Right," Sable said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "Right."

His reflection looked up at him from the black surface, pale and thin and wearing his mother's old coat, which was true, and looking frightened, which was also true, and —

And half a second behind.

He noticed it the way you notice a clock ticking wrong: not all at once, but as an itch. He tilted his head left. His reflection tilted its head left — a beat late, a small, apologetic lag, like a dancer who has lost count of the music. He raised his right hand. The reflection raised its right hand after, politely, as though checking with someone first.

Sable stepped back from the well so fast he caught his heel on the coping and sat down hard in the frost-stiffened grass, and for one absurd moment he lay there staring up at the yew branches and laughing — a short, cracked laugh with nothing warm in it — because this was the moment he understood that the explanations had run out. He had rationed them carefully since the funeral: the empty coffin was a vicar's error; the wrong name was grief talking; the bell was a recording, a prank, a collective hallucination brought on by woodsmoke and inbreeding. But reflections do not lag. Reflections are physics. Physics had been, until this morning, the one friend he could still rely on.

He got up, brushed off his coat, and looked again.

The reflection waited for him, patient as a portrait. When he held still, it held still. When he breathed, it breathed — a half-second late, every time, so that watching it was like watching himself through a window across a street, or like being remembered by someone rather than seen.

"You're not me," Sable told it. "I'm me. You're — light behaving badly."

The reflection did not argue. That was somehow worse.

* * *

He went to find Tam, because Tam was the only person in Hollowmere who answered questions, even if his answers arrived the way parcels arrive from abroad — battered, delayed, and missing the interesting parts.

He found the sexton in the churchyard, cutting back the ivy from the north wall with a pair of shears older than the church. Tam worked the way old men work at physical tasks: slowly, correctly, with the air of a man who has made peace with the ivy and expects to make peace with the ivy again next year.

"The well," Sable said, "has water in it."

Tam didn't stop cutting. "Aye."

"Aye? That's it? *Aye?*"

"What would you have me say?" Tam snipped a tendril free and let it fall. "That it shouldn't have? I expect you've already worked that out. You've your mother's way of working things out, God rest her. She once proved to the whole village that the ghost of the miller was a draft, and then apologized to the draft."

"My mother," Sable said, "drowned in that flood."

"No," Tam said, and set down the shears, and turned around, and looked at him with an expression Sable couldn't file anywhere. "Your mother drowned in the *flood*, plural, and there's been more than one, and that's a conversation for a day with better weather. What I'll say about the well is this." He wiped his hands on his coat. "It's been dry since before I was born. My father said it was dry, and his father before him, and the plate on the wall says dry, and the church records say dry, and every winter for the last — oh, call it seven years — I've come out here on the first frost and looked down, because my father told me to, and every year it's been dry, and every year I've written *dry* in the maintenance book, and every year I've been glad to write it."

"And this year?"

"This year," Tam said, "it isn't."

"How much water?"

"Yesterday, when I looked — a skin. Enough to wet the bottom stones. Enough to make me think my eyes were going, which at my age is the comfortable explanation, and I clung to it like a man clinging to a door in a gale." He picked the shears back up, then put them down again, as if even ivy had lost its appeal. "You're telling me it's ankle-deep now."

"Maybe deeper. It's hard to tell. It's very — " Sable hunted for the word and hated the word when he found it. "*Level.*"

Tam nodded slowly, the way a man nods at a diagnosis he'd already read in the letter the doctor sent ahead. "It levels," he said. "My grandmother used to say the well doesn't fill. It *rises*. Like something coming upstairs one step at a time and pausing on each landing to see if anyone's noticed."

"Has anyone measured it?"

"Measured it." Tam almost smiled. There was real humor in it, somewhere far down, like a coin at the bottom of a fountain. "Aye, lad. There's a notch cut in the inside of the coping, on the north side, worn smooth. Generations of sextons have stood where you're standing with a plumb line and a notebook, and written down the inches, and carried the book to the vestry, and locked it away. Winter is measured in inches here. Always has been. Most folk just prefer not to know the exchange rate."

"Show me."

* * *

The vestry archive smelled of dust, candle wax, and the particular melancholy of paper that has outlived everyone who cared about it. Tam unlocked the cabinet with a key from a ring of perhaps forty keys, each labeled in a different century's handwriting, and drew out a ledger bound in grey cloth gone soft as felt.

**WELL — LEVELS — WINTER OBSERVATIONS**, said the spine.

Inside, the entries marched back through the years in a dozen hands. Sable turned pages. The early ones were beautiful — copperplate, meticulous, dated to the day:

*1st Dec. 1898. Dry. Praise be.*

*14th Dec. 1903. Dry. Frost thick.*

Then 1911, and the handwriting changed — hurried, the ink blotted:

*Flood. Well sealed. Let it stay empty. Let it stay empty. Let it stay*

And then years of *dry, dry, dry*, in hands that grew steadily less certain, less ornate, until the entries became mere ticks, and then, in recent decades, a single word in Tam's own blunt script: *dry.*

Except.

Seven years ago, the ticks stopped. The entries resumed — short, grudging, increasingly unwilling:

*Trace moisture. Condensation, likely.*

*Damp at lowest stones. Will monitor.*

*One inch.*

*Two inches.*

Last winter: *Five inches. Cold winter coming.*

And beneath that, in fresh ink, dated three days ago, in Tam's hand, pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

*Nine inches. Tell him.*

"Tell him," Sable read aloud. "Tell who?"

Tam took the ledger gently out of his hands and closed it, the way you close a door on a sickroom. "Whoever came home," he said. "We didn't know it'd be you. We hoped it wouldn't be anybody. We hoped — " He stopped. Rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he looked every one of his sixty-odd years and several borrowed ones besides. "Sable. Your sister's funeral was Tuesday. The coffin was empty. You know it was empty. I saw your face at the graveside, and I've seen that face before, on other folk, other winters, and I will tell you what I told myself then and what nobody had the stomach to tell them."

"Go on."

"The well doesn't take water the way wells do. It takes it the way debts do. Slow, and then all at once, and never by accident." He tapped the ledger's grey cover. "Nine inches in three days. Last winter took four months to manage five. Whatever schedule it was keeping, it's torn up. And the solstice — "

"— is eighteen days away," Sable said. He'd counted. He'd been counting since the funeral, the way you count the days since a death, except this counting felt less like mourning and more like a fuse.

"Aye." Tam looked at him for a long moment, and whatever he saw in Sable's face made him reach out and grip his shoulder — a rough, brief, honest weight, the first uncomplicated kindness Sable had felt since coming home. "Eighteen days. And lad — don't go listening at that water. Folk do. They can't help it. It's so quiet, and quiet draws them, and — " He stopped himself with visible effort, jaw tightening around whatever came next.

"And what?"

"And it's rude," Tam said, "to answer a voice before you know whose it is."

* * *

Sable walked back to the cottage the long way, along the frozen lane, because he wanted to feel cold in an ordinary way — wind-chill, numb ears, the honest misery of weather — instead of the other kind.

It didn't work. The village was too quiet in the wrong grammar. Smoke rose straight from every chimney. Curtains twitched in a rhythm that suggested watching rather than washing-up. Mrs. Tregale, sweeping her step, stopped sweeping as he passed and gave him a nod that was half greeting and half condolence, as though he were a man walking to his own trial in good spirits.

At the cottage he built up the fire, made tea he didn't drink, and sat with Marguerite's journal open on his knees, at the page he'd read a dozen times:

*the bell will tell you which one.*

"Well," he said to the empty room, to the fire, to the dry pipes that had begun, faintly, to sing their one thin note, "the bell's melted down. So that's no help. But the well's got nine inches of water in it that reflects me late, and a ledger says it means to be full by solstice, and the sexton says don't listen, which means everyone in this village has listened, and — "

He stopped.

Because he had just done the sum he'd been avoiding. Nine inches in three days. Eighteen days to solstice. Even at the same rate, that was fifty-four inches — four and a half feet. But rates like this didn't hold steady; Tam's ledger showed the curve, gentle for years and steepening now, the way a hill steepens near the top. The well wasn't filling. It was *arriving*.

And a thought followed it, quiet and terrible and patient, walking into his mind on soft feet the way Marguerite used to sneak downstairs past bedtime:

*A well that rises wants to reach the top. And a well that reaches the top isn't a well anymore. It's a doorway lying on its back.*

Outside, the light was failing. The frost was coming up out of the ground in feathers. Somewhere behind the church, in the dark, in the square of mowed grass nobody lingered on, the water lay perfectly level in the stone circle, waiting, rising, keeping its own calendar — and reflecting, half a second behind, anyone foolish enough to look down.

Sable banked the fire, checked the locks he knew wouldn't matter, and went up to bed.

He was nearly asleep when the tapping started at the window.
