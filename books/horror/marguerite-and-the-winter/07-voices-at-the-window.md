## 7. Voices at the Window

Sable had always been good at explaining things away.

It was a family trait, or perhaps a twin thing — when you grow up sharing a face with someone, you learn early that most mysteries have a mundane twin of their own. The creak on the stairs was the house settling. The smell of bread in an empty kitchen was the wind carrying it from the Hendersons' chimney down the lane. The sound of your sister laughing in the next room when she was, in fact, standing right beside you — well, that one he'd never fully solved, but Marguerite had laughed and said *echoes get bored, Sable, they go looking for something to be*, and somehow that had been explanation enough for both of them.

He was reaching for those old explanations now, the way a man reaches for a coat he outgrew years ago, and finding the sleeves too short.

The footsteps upstairs, for instance. Every night since he'd arrived, sometime between eleven and midnight, there came a slow crossing of the landing above his bedroom — four steps, a pause, four steps back. He had gone up with a torch the first evening and found nothing but dust so thick it recorded his own footprints like wet sand at a beach. No second set. No disturbance at all except where he'd walked. And yet the next night: four steps, a pause, four steps back, patient as a clock.

*Old houses talk,* he told himself, lying in Marguerite's childhood bed — he couldn't bring himself to sleep in what had been his own room; it felt like trespassing on a stranger's grief, which was absurd, because it was his room, wasn't it, and the grief was his own. *Timber contracts in the cold. The joists shift. It's physics.*

And the pipes. The pipes were worse. There was no water in them — Tam had confirmed this with the grim satisfaction of a man delivering bad news he'd personally verified twice — the supply had been cut when the cottage was emptied seven years ago. And yet every night, faintly, from inside the walls, came the sound of water moving. Not rushing. Not dripping. *Moving*, the way water moves when something large passes through it slowly, displacing it with care, the way you'd move through a room full of sleeping people.

*Thermal expansion,* Sable told the ceiling. *Condensation. Old pipes hold moisture for decades.*

The ceiling declined to comment.

Tonight he'd given up on sleep around half past eleven and come downstairs to sit in the kitchen with the lamp lit and Marguerite's journal open in front of him, because reading her handwriting felt like holding her hand, and holding her hand was the only thing in Hollowmere that didn't frighten him. Her letters leaned hard to the left, as if even her pen was always hurrying somewhere else. He'd read the same entry four times now:

*It knows our names. That's the whole trick of it, Sable. Everything else is just weather. It knows our names and it waits for us to answer.*

Below that, in smaller writing, pressed into the page as though she'd been afraid of it even in ink:

*Don't answer.*

He looked at the window over the sink. Black glass, frost creeping in ferns along the corners, the garden beyond invisible. A normal window in a normal kitchen in a village that rang bells that didn't exist and buried empty coffins with full honours. He was getting used to it, he realized, and the realization frightened him more than any of it had. Fear was supposed to sharpen you. This felt more like the water rising in the well — slow, patient, and higher each morning than it had any right to be.

The tap came at ten minutes past midnight.

Not a knock. A tap. One fingertip, politely, against the glass of the kitchen window, the way Marguerite used to tap on his bedroom door when they were children and she'd had a nightmare and wouldn't say so — *just checking you're alive, don't make it weird* — and Sable's whole body went cold and hot at once, every year of his life arriving simultaneously.

He did not look up from the journal. This took effort roughly equivalent to lifting a car.

*Wind-blown branch,* said the reasonable voice in his head. *There are no branches near the window. I cut them back myself on Tuesday.* *Then ice. A shard of ice off the gutter.* *The gutters are dry. Everything here is dry. That's rather the point of the place.*

Tap. Tap-tap.

Two more, softer, almost shy. And then — and this was the moment Sable would spend the rest of his life failing to explain to himself — the frost on the glass began to melt. Not all of it. Just a patch, a hand-sized oval in the lower corner, clearing from the inside of the pane outward, as though someone on the other side of the black were breathing warmth against it.

Through the cleared glass he could see the garden. The dead apple tree. The low stone wall. The well cover beyond it, pale under a sky the colour of wet slate. Nothing else. Nobody. The garden stood empty and ordinary and utterly still, and the tap came again, exactly at the height of a person kneeling.

"Sable."

Marguerite's voice. Through the glass, muffled slightly, the way voices carry across water. Not a whisper — her actual speaking voice, warm and a little rough at the edges, the voice that had read him stories through chickenpox and argued with him about everything and once, memorably, talked a policeman out of arresting them both by crying on cue.

Every hair on his arms stood up and turned to face the window.

"That's not funny," he said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "Whoever you are. That's not — Marguerite is dead. I'm here to bury her. So whoever you are, you can stop."

Silence. Then, gently, with terrible patience:

"You cut the branches back on Tuesday. You slept in my bed. You've read my journal four times and you always stop at the same entry." A pause, and something almost like a smile moved through the voice. "You bite the inside of your cheek when you're frightened. You're doing it now. You've done it since we were six."

"Stop it." The lamp flickered. Or he blinked. He genuinely could not have said which. "Stop it, stop it, *stop it*—"

"I missed you," the voice said, and it was so precisely, so perfectly her — the slight catch she got when she meant something, the way she rushed the last word — that Sable felt his eyes fill before he'd decided anything at all. "Seven years, Sable. Do you know how long seven years is? Down here? Come outside. Just for a minute. Just let me see you."

His hand was on the latch.

He didn't remember moving it. That was the part that made his stomach drop afterward, replaying it — not that he'd obeyed, but that there was no decision anywhere in the middle of it, no moment of choice, just his hand on the cold iron latch and the door two inches from opening onto the frozen garden and whatever knelt at the window, and Marguerite's journal lying open behind him saying *don't answer, don't answer, don't answer* in her hurrying left-leaning hand.

He snatched his hand back as if the latch had bitten him. Stepped away. Knocked the chair. Caught it. Stood in the middle of the kitchen breathing like a man who'd surfaced from deep water, staring at the door, at the window, at the small cleared oval in the frost.

The voice sighed. And when it spoke again, it was still Marguerite's voice, but something underneath it had shifted — some note held too long, some grain turned wrong-way-round, the way a familiar word repeated fifty times stops meaning anything and becomes only a sound wearing a word's clothes.

"All right," it said. "All right. Don't come out. We'll do it the other way."

"The other way," Sable repeated. Idiot. Answering. *Don't answer.* But silence felt more dangerous, somehow, like turning your back on it.

"Mm. You always liked riddles better when you thought you'd invented them." The tap again, soft, affectionate. "Tell me something, brother. When you drove up yesterday — the long road past the reservoir — what did you remember?"

"What?"

"Indulge me. Humour a dead girl. What did you remember?"

Sable frowned. "I don't — the water. It was iced over. White all the way to the far shore. I remembered—" and here his mouth kept going while his mind stood aside watching it with mounting horror, "—I remembered falling through. The ice. The sound it makes. Like a door closing very slowly."

"Mm," said the voice. "And whose memory is that? Because I fell through the ice, Sable. Seven years ago. At the reservoir. And you were on the bank. You were *safe*. I remember the hole in the ice closing over me, and I remember looking up, and I remember seeing your face through it — upside down, distorted, the way things look from underwater." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "So tell me why you remember it from *my side* of the ice."

The kitchen was very quiet. The lamp flame stood perfectly straight, as if listening.

"That's not—" Sable started. "That's trauma. That's survivor's — people remember things wrong, it's documented, there's a whole—"

"There's a whole literature, yes. You always hid behind books. It's all right." The voice softened, went tender, went *loving*, and that was the worst thing yet, because it was Marguerite's tenderness, undimmed after seven years, and it was aimed at him like a weapon with his name engraved on the handle. "I'm not angry. How could I be angry? You came back. After everything, you actually came back." The tap again. Tap. Tap. "You came back wrong, too."

"*Too.*"

The word hung in the air between them. Sable heard it land. Heard what it implied. Heard, beneath it, the small patient sound of water moving in dry pipes, upstairs and down, all through the walls of the house now, everywhere at once, gentle as circulation.

"You came back wrong too," the voice repeated, savouring it. "Think about what I'm telling you, brother. Think about the funeral. Think about the vicar calling you by a name you didn't know. Think about the register, when Tam shows it to you — and he will, he's already worked up the courage, give him until Thursday." A laugh, low and fond and drowned. "Everyone in this village has known for seven years which one of us died. Everyone but you. Everyone but *me*, some days. Some nights I lie awake — do I lie awake? do I lie at all? — and I can't remember whether I'm the one in the ground waiting for you or the one in the house pretending to be you, and honestly, Sable, darling, does it matter? Does it matter even a little bit? We shared a face. We shared a crib. We spent nine months in one water learning each other's hearts. What's one more wall between us? What's one pane of glass?"

"Marguerite." His voice cracked clean through the middle. "If it's really you. If any part of it's really you. Please. Help me understand."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for him to count eleven of his own heartbeats, and he hated each one individually.

"Oh, I am helping you," she said at last, and now the voice was receding — not fading, *receding*, the way sound travels downward through water, growing quieter and colder and somehow closer. "This is me helping you. Ask the founders, Sable. Ask what they sealed. Ask why the verse stops where it stops. Ask Elowen — she's kept warm, they keep her warm, ask Tam how." Tap. Tap. Tap, going slower now, further down. "And whatever you do — whatever you decide, whichever one of us you turn out to be —"

"Yes?"

"—don't let them finish it without you."

The frost began to close over the cleared oval, fern by fern, like a small white wound healing shut. The taps stopped. The pipes went quiet, mid-motion, as if something in the walls had paused to listen for his reply.

Sable stood alone in the lamplight with his sister's journal open to *don't answer* and his handprint still fogged faintly on the inside of the glass, though he had never touched it.

He looked at his hand. He looked at the print. Same size. Same spread of fingers.

He was almost sure he hadn't touched it.

Outside, far off across the frozen fields, a bell began to ring — one hour past midnight, in a church tower that had held nothing but an empty yoke for a hundred and thirteen years — and Sable Yarrow, or the man answering to that name, sat down heavily in his sister's chair and began, with hands that would not entirely stop shaking, to write down every word the voice had said before he could lose any of it. Because tomorrow — Thursday, she'd said, give him until Thursday — he was going to find Tam, and they were going to open the vestry archive together, and he was going to ask the founders exactly what they'd sealed, and why the ledger of Hollowmere had a column in it that no one had ever explained to him.

A column for names.

Two per death.

One crossed out.
