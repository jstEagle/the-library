## 10. The Drowned Twin

The water was warm.

That was the first wrong thing, though it took Sable a long time to notice it was wrong at all, because warmth in a dream is like the floor of a house — you only think about it when it gives way. He stood at the bottom of the well and the water held him the way a mother holds a sleeping child, close and complete, and he thought: *of course it's warm. It's been waiting for me.*

Above him, far above, there was light. Not sunlight — nothing so generous — but the pale grey gleam that comes through ice when the world on the other side of it is overcast and patient. The well's stone throat rose around him in courses of old, soft brick, furred green with something that was not quite moss, and the water climbed that throat and thinned as it went until, at the very top, it became a ceiling: a lid of ice, milky and imperfect, through which the shapes of the world moved like fish behind frosted glass.

He could see feet up there. Boots, actually — good boots, the kind with a heel, the kind Marguerite used to say made a person walk like they were apologizing to the ground with every second step. The boots paced back and forth across the ice. Paused. Paced again.

*They're deciding where to dig,* Sable thought, and was not surprised by the thought, which was the second wrong thing.

There were other figures too, dimmer, standing in a loose ring the way villagers stand for anything — a funeral, a fire, a flood. Their voices came down through the ice not as words but as pressure, a soft rhythmic pushing against the water, the way a hymn sounds from the next room over. He knew the tune. That was the third wrong thing. He had never learned the founders' hymn; he'd been seven years gone from Hollowmere before he'd ever heard it named. And yet his chest rose and fell with it, or would have risen and fallen if chests did such things underwater, and when the hymn bent toward its refrain something in him bent with it, obediently, like a reed remembering wind.

The boots stopped directly overhead.

Then — a sound he felt in his teeth before he heard it with his ears — the scrape of a spade. Not digging down. Dragging. Something heavy being hauled across frozen ground, its runners hissing against frost, coming closer, closer, until it stopped above him and the whole grey lid of the world darkened with its shadow.

A coffin.

He watched them lower it. Ropes creaked — he heard that clearly, ropes carry through water better than voices do — and the coffin descended the last few feet in slow, lurching swings, and through the ice he could see its shape pass across the light like a cloud passing across the sun, once, twice, three times, each swing slower than the last.

It was a small coffin.

That was the fourth wrong thing, and this one hurt, because small coffins are the worst kind and everyone knows it, and the grief arrived in him whole and pre-aged, as though he had been carrying it for years and had only now been handed the receipt.

*Whose is it?* he tried to ask, and discovered that underwater, in dreams, questions don't come out as questions. They come out as long silver threads that rise through the dark and dissolve against the ice, unanswered, beautiful, useless.

But the boots answered anyway. The boots walked to the edge of the grave — he could hear the earth giving way under them, crumb by crumb — and a voice came down, muffled and familiar, saying words he couldn't parse except for one, which slipped through the ice and the water and the years between like a coin through a grate:

*—Marguerite—*

And Sable understood, with the terrible calm of dreams, that he was attending his sister's funeral from underneath it. That the mourners stood above him. That the coffin was coming down to *him*, or he was rising to meet it, or — and here the dream tilted, gently, the way a boat tilts when someone shifts their weight — there was no difference between those two things anymore.

The coffin touched the ice.

Or the ice touched the coffin. The verbs had stopped cooperating. He saw it plainly now through the milky ceiling: the pale wood, the brass fittings gone green, and beneath the lid — he didn't open it, he would never open it, and yet he saw it — the shape of someone lying with their hands folded, wearing his face, or hers, the two of them at this distance and this depth being the same face the way two notes an octave apart are the same note.

The hands unfolded.

One palm pressed flat against the underside of the ice, right above him, fingers spread, and Sable raised his own hand — he had a hand, then, he had arms, he was assembled after all — and pressed his palm to the ice from below, and the two hands met, separated by four inches of frozen water, matching finger to finger like a promise.

The hand was his own. He knew every line of it. He also knew every line of hers. There was no way to tell whose hand it was, and the not-knowing did not frighten him, and that frightened him more than anything.

*Which one of us is buried?* he asked, in silver threads, in bubbles, in the language of the drowned.

The hand above pressed harder. The ice flexed. Somewhere far off, in the direction of the church that wasn't visible from down here but was somehow always visible from down here, a bell rang — one low note, round and brown and warm as bread — and the ice sang with it, and the coffin sang with it, and Sable's own bones hummed the same note, and he thought with sudden clarity and no alarm at all:

*I'm the one holding the spade.*

He looked down at himself — at the self that had been standing in the warm dark all this while — and found his hands full of frozen earth. He was kneeling beside an open grave. The grave was the well. The well was the grave. Above him the sky was a lid of ice and below him the sky was a lid of ice and he was the thin warm layer in between where the living things got kept, and somewhere in the last minute — or the last seven years — he had changed places with the person in the box without either of them noticing, the way you change places with your reflection in a shop window when you stop walking and it keeps going.

*Oh,* he said, into the water. *Oh, I see.*

The bell rang again. The ice brightened. And the voice that came through it this time was not muffled at all — it arrived perfectly clear, the way voices arrive in the moment just before waking, bypassing the ears entirely — and it said, in a tone of gentle, sisterly reproach that he had not heard in seven years and would have known blindfolded at the bottom of the sea:

"You're letting it get cold."

* * *

Sable woke with the taste of pondwater in his mouth.

Not metaphorically. Not the stale-morning taste that dreams leave behind as a calling card. Actual pondwater — green and mineral and faintly sweet, the taste of the flooded quarry behind Hobben's field where the Yarrow twins had swum every summer of their childhood until the summer Marguerite dove in where the rocks were and came up laughing with blood in her hair, and he had laughed too, because she was laughing, because that was the arrangement, that was the whole architecture of being a twin: whatever she felt first, he felt second, and neither of them had ever established who was supposed to be feeling things first.

He lay still in the narrow bed in the cottage that smelled of cold ash and older dust, and took inventory, because inventory was what he did. It was the habit of a lifetime — the boy who explained away footsteps as beams settling, water in dry pipes as condensation, a sister's voice in the night as memory doing its clumsy carpentry. Name the thing and it shrinks. Measure the thing and it behaves. His father had taught him that, or his mother had, or possibly both of them together in the tag-team way parents have, and it had served him well for thirty-odd years, right up until a village had insisted he was dead and an empty coffin had proceeded with full honours.

Inventory:

One. His mouth tasted of pondwater. Filed under: possible, if he'd slept with his mouth open and dreamed vividly enough about water, the mind being perfectly capable of manufacturing tastes the way it manufactured falling. Except that manufactured tastes are usually sour, and this was specific. This was *Hollowmere* water. This was the well behind the church, which he had looked into yesterday morning and found ankle-deep in black water that had no business existing, and whose surface had shown him his own reflection half a heartbeat late, like a photograph developing.

Two. His hair was wet. Not damp — wet, at the roots, in the way hair gets when it has been fully submerged and has dried unevenly overnight. He raised a hand to confirm this and the hand paused halfway, because —

Three. His hands.

He held them up to the grey window-light and turned them over slowly, the way you turn over a thing you've found on the beach. The skin was wrinkled. Deeply, thoroughly wrinkled — the pruned, puckered corrugation of skin that has been underwater for hours, the fingertips swollen and pale, the creases of his palms gone soft and white. He knew exactly how long skin takes to reach this state. Everyone does. It's the sort of knowledge childhood installs without asking permission: forty minutes in the bath and you come out looking like an old man's gloves. These hands had been in the water since roughly midnight.

"That's not possible," Sable told the ceiling, in the reasonable tone he used for impossible things, the tone that had carried him through an empty coffin and a vicar calling him by a stranger's name and a bell that had been melted down in 1911 ringing midnight over a village that nodded along as if to say *yes, it does that.* "Hands don't go swimming without the rest of you."

The ceiling declined to comment. The ceiling had been declining to comment all week, but lately Sable had begun to suspect it was only being polite.

He got up. His legs worked. His legs, notably, were fine — dry-skinned, ordinary, faintly cold the way legs get in an unheated cottage in December. Only his hands had been anywhere. Only his hands had gone down to the water and come back, or been somewhere the water was, or — 

He stopped at the washstand and looked in the mirror, because he was not yet so far gone that he skipped mirrors, though he noticed he now approached them the way you approach a dog that has bitten you once: respectfully, sideways, ready to withdraw.

The man in the mirror looked tired and middle-aged and damp about the temples, and Sable had gotten reasonably good at looking at him these past few days, had even developed a working theory — *you are Sable Yarrow, you left for London at nineteen, you came back because Marguerite died, the village is mad, grief is strange, proceed* — a theory that had held together about as well as paper in rain.

Because there, in his hair, above the left ear, glinting dull green against the brown: pondweed.

A single strand of it, fine as thread, beaded with water. He lifted it out with two careful fingers and held it to the light and it was unmistakably, botanically real — the little segmented chain of it, the way it caught and held a droplet at each joint. He knew pondweed. Every child in Hollowmere knew pondweed; it grew in the quarry pool and the flooded lanes and, historically, in the well, before 1911, before the water went wherever the water went.

He stood there a long moment, holding the strand, dripping slightly, while outside the window the winter morning lay over Hollowmere like a held breath.

"Right," he said finally, to the mirror, to the weed, to whoever was listening — and he was no longer entirely sure the set of listeners was limited to himself. "Options."

He laid them out, because laying things out was the family trade, apparently, along with drowning.

Option one: sleepwalking. He had, in his sleep, gone out the door, down the lane, past the churchyard, to the well, and put his hands in the black water for several hours, and come back, and none of it had woken him. Possible. People did stranger things. But the well was behind the church, and the churchyard gate — he had noted this yesterday with the small pedantic satisfaction of a man cataloguing evidence — was latched with a loop of wire that took two hands and a certain bad temper to undo. His hands, wrinkled as they were, showed no wire-marks, no scratches, no torn skin from climbing anything. They showed only water.

Option two: he had not gone anywhere. Something had come to him. Something had held his hands in the water from the other side, gently, patiently, the way you hold the hands of someone learning to float.

Option three — and he set this one down carefully, at arm's length, the way you set down something that might be alive — option three was that the question of where *he* had spent the night was not actually answerable in the terms provided, because the terms assumed there was a settled fact of the matter about which body was his, and the evidence of the morning — the water-taste, the weed, the hands, the dream in which he had stood at the bottom of the well watching himself bury his sister, or his sister bury him, the verbs having resigned mid-sentence — suggested that the fact of the matter was currently *in negotiation*, and that the negotiating parties shared a bed's worth of memories and a face and, increasingly, a set of hands.

He wrapped the pondweed strand in a page torn from Marguerite's journal — blank margin, he checked first, he wasn't a barbarian — and put it in his coat pocket, because evidence was evidence, and because some part of him that was getting louder by the day whispered that she would want him to keep it. That she had left it for him. That leaving things was her love language, always had been — notes in his coat pockets, stones in his boots, frogs in his bed, a whole childhood conducted as a series of carefully planted discoveries.

"The bell will tell you which one," her journal had said, in the last entry, in handwriting that slanted the way hers slanted, or the way his slanted, depending on the day and the pen.

Well. The bell had been melted down in 1911, and it rang anyway, so its testimony was compromised. But the water — the water was new. The water was rising. The water had taken his hands in the night and given them back wrinkled, like a message returned unopened, like a letter that says only: *received, and awaiting your reply.*

Sable dressed. His shirt collar was damp too, he noticed. Just the collar. As if someone had leaned over him in the night, close, breathing pondwater breath, checking that he was still — 

He decided not to finish that thought, and finished it anyway, because that was also the family trade.

As if someone had leaned over him in the night to check whether he was still the one on top of the water.

Downstairs, the pipes began to sing — the dry pipes, the impossible pipes, the ones that had no water in them and had been humming softly all week in a register just below hearing, or just above honesty. But this morning they weren't humming. This morning, for the first time, they resolved into something with shape and intention: a single repeated phrase, three notes descending, over and over, patient as tide.

Sable stood on the stairs with his hand on the banister and listened, and his skin prickled, because he knew the tune. Of course he knew the tune. He had heard it all night through four inches of ice, sung by a ring of patient shadows over a small coffin.

It was the founders' hymn. And the house was humming it to him like a lullaby — or like a reminder.

Outside, the light was changing. The sky over the fells had gone the particular bruised pearl-grey that meant snow within the day, and somewhere beyond the church, faint but unmistakable, carried on air too cold to carry anything kindly, the bell rang once — a single note, round and brown and warm as bread — though it was nowhere near any hour that bells are rung for.

Sable pulled on his coat, pocketed the pondweed, and went out to count the inches of water in the well.

He already knew, in the way he now knew tunes he'd never learned and tastes he'd never drunk, that there would be more of it than yesterday. What he did not know — what he would learn standing at the wellhead an hour later, staring down into black water that reflected a man with Marguerite's tired eyes — was that today, for the first time, the reflection would move *before* he did.
