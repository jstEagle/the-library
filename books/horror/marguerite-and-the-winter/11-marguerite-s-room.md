## 11. Marguerite's Room

The door at the top of the stairs had been painted shut.

Not locked — Sable had checked for a lock the first night, running his thumb along the seam of the frame the way their father had taught them to check for drafts in winter, back when checking for drafts was the most frightening thing a person had to do. No lock. Just paint, seven years of it, layered over whatever latch Marguerite had turned on her last morning and never turned again. The door was white once. Now it was the color of old teeth, and it sat in its frame with the patient silence of something that knew it would be opened eventually and saw no reason to hurry.

He'd been putting it off for three days.

This was not cowardice, he told himself, stirring the fire down to embers and pulling his coat on against the cottage cold. This was *method*. There was an order to things. You catalogued the noises first — the footsteps overhead that were probably the chimney settling, the water moving through pipes that hadn't carried water since 1911, which was almost certainly the wind finding some gap in the eaves and whistling down a flue like a boy blowing across a bottle neck. You found the journal second. You read the journal third, twice, and then you read it a fourth time at two in the morning with every lamp in the house lit because the last entry said *the bell will tell you which one* and the bell — the bell that did not exist, that was melted into ploughshares before either of them was born — had rung thirteen times over the village while everyone stood in the street listening with the mild attention of people waiting for a bus.

And then, fourth, when you had exhausted every rational explanation and watched each one die quietly in your hands like a bird held too tightly — then you opened your dead sister's room.

*Which one*, the bell had asked. He still didn't know what it meant. He suspected the room might.

The screwdriver from the kitchen drawer worked between door and frame where the paint had shrunk back. He leaned his weight into it. The seal gave all at once, with a sound like a long-held breath released, and cold air rolled out over him — air that smelled of dust and lavender and, beneath both, faintly and unmistakably, of pond water.

"Well," Sable said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud was how he kept hold of the shape of himself. "That's not encouraging."

* * *

The room was exactly as he remembered it, which was the first wrong thing.

Marguerite had left Hollowmere at nineteen, same as him, and her bedroom had been a teenager's bedroom: band posters curling off the walls, a chair buried under clothes she'd worn once and declared finished with, a constellation of mugs on the windowsill each containing something biological that their mother had forbidden him to investigate. Seven years should have made it a museum of that girl. Instead —

Instead it was tidy. Not just tidy. *Arranged.* The bed was made with hospital corners. The desk held three books squared to its edge. The wardrobe doors stood closed. The whole room had the quality of a stage set between performances: everything in its place, everything waiting, nothing lived-in and nothing abandoned.

And there was no dust.

Sable stood in the doorway for a long moment, letting this settle. Because that wasn't right either. The rest of the cottage wore its seven years openly — grey felt over every surface, cobwebs strung across the stairwell like bunting for a festival nobody celebrated anymore. He'd spent his first morning back wiping the grime off the kitchen window just to see the well behind the church. But here the floorboards gleamed. The mirror over the dresser was clear as still water. Even the air felt filtered, scrubbed, as though the room had been holding its breath and had only now, at the sound of the paint seal breaking, let it out.

"Right," he said. "Cataloguing. We're cataloguing."

It was a habit from childhood, the explanations. When they were small and the house creaked, their father — a practical man, a man who fixed tractors and trusted nothing he couldn't hit with a hammer — had taught them the game. *Every noise has a boring explanation, and the boring one is always true.* Footsteps upstairs: the chimney settling. Scratching in the walls: mice, or the ivy. A voice calling your name from the garden after dark: the wind through the gap in the shed door, shaped by coincidence into something that sounded terribly like your name, and under no circumstances were you to answer it, because answering was rude and also because their mother had gone very quiet and serious about that particular rule in a way that even at six years old Sable had understood was not about the wind at all.

So: cataloguing. He crossed the threshold and began.

The bed. Hospital corners, sheets pale blue — Marguerite's color, or his; they'd shared a palette their whole lives the way they'd shared a face. Explanation: their mother had made up the room before she died, keeping faith with a daughter who never came home. Boring. True, probably. Except the sheets smelled of line-drying, of sun and frost, and it was December, and the washing line outside was a broken pole leaning in the weeds.

The books on the desk. He picked up the top one and his stomach performed a slow, deliberate somersault, because it was *his* book — his copy of the waterlogged field guide to freshwater life that he'd bought at fourteen with saved-up pocket money, his name written inside the cover in his own careful hand, *S. Yarrow*, the S decorated with the little fish tail he used to add to annoy their teacher. It had been in his flat in the city. He was nearly sure it had been in his flat in the city. Underneath it lay two more: a history of Hollowmere parish, and a slim volume on folk ritual that he did not own but that fell open, when he thumbed it, to a chapter on well-sealings, with a passage underlined in pencil in handwriting identical to his own.

Explanation: he'd packed these himself, in the fog of grief, and forgotten. People forgot things. Grief scrambled the memory like eggs.

Except he hadn't unpacked any boxes yet. They were still stacked in the front hall, taped shut, seven years of city life unopened. And except that the field guide had been lost — genuinely lost, mourned-lost — since the accident. Since the river. Since the night he could only ever remember in fragments: headlights, cold, Marguerite's hand letting go of his or his letting go of hers, and after that a long grey time in a hospital with people telling him gently how lucky he was.

Lucky. Yes. He set the books down precisely where they'd been, squared to the edge, because some instinct told him they'd been placed and ought not be disturbed out of order.

The wardrobe next. He braced himself for clothes — hers, or worse, his — but when he pulled the doors open he found hangers, evenly spaced, all empty except one. On that one hanger hung a coat. His coat. Not the one he was wearing; the twin of it, the one he'd owned before the accident, dark wool, brass buttons, a cigarette burn on the left cuff from the night Tam Wetherall had smuggled them both behind the church hall with a stolen bottle of cider and Sable had learned that he was, emphatically, not a smoking man. He touched the burn. The wool was soft and dry and smelled faintly of woodsmoke, as if it had been hung here yesterday by someone coming in from a cold evening.

Explanation: spare clothing, kept by their parents, obviously. Families did that. Kept rooms ready. His mother had kept his room ready too, all seven years, until —

Until. Well. Until.

His hands were shaking slightly when he closed the wardrobe, so he made himself laugh at the empty hangers, one dry note in the quiet room. "Very minimalist," he told them. "Very Scandinavian." The hangers declined to comment. Small mercies.

* * *

It was the shoes that undid the tidiness theory.

They were lined up under the window seat: four pairs, his sizes, his styles, arranged toe-outward like a display in a shop that sold only Sable. Walking boots. The ridiculous green trainers he'd owned at seventeen. A pair of black work shoes polished to a dull shine. And at the end, half-hidden in shadow, a pair of leather-soled dress shoes that he had absolutely never owned, that he had no memory of, and that were nonetheless unmistakably *his* — the way the face in the mirror was his, the way his name was his. Something in the set of them, the wear at the heel, the particular way the laces had been tied in the double knot he'd used since childhood because Marguerite teased him that it took him longer to untie than to tie.

He crouched there a long while, cataloguing explanations and finding the shelf bare.

Then he saw the footprints.

They led from the door — his door, the one he'd just unsealed — across the floorboards to the window. Bare feet, adult-sized, pressed in a fine grey film of dust so thin it was less a layer than a memory of a layer, visible only where the light from the landing caught it at an angle. One trail. Outward only. No return prints. As though whoever had walked to the window had walked there and then simply stopped existing, or stepped through the glass, or dissolved into the room the way breath dissolves into cold air.

Seven years of dust everywhere else in the cottage. Seven years of *nothing* here — except this one fresh track, hours old at most, laid down sometime between his turning his key in the front door downstairs and his breaking the paint seal on this door.

Someone had been waiting in the room. Listening to him move through the house below. And when he finally came up the stairs, they had gone to the window — unhurried, barefoot, familiar with every board — and looked out at the well.

Sable got up off his knees. His legs felt borrowed. He went to the window himself, following the prints, and put his hands on the sill, and looked out at exactly what they had looked out at.

The well stood behind the church in its collar of dead nettles, dry as a cupped palm, black-mouthed, patient. Above it the sky was doing the thing it did here in winter: going bruise-colored early, stacking itself with cloud. Nothing moved. Nothing rang. The village below showed a scatter of lit windows, ordinary and warm, and he thought — with a tenderness that frightened him more than anything else in the room had — *they're all in their kitchens pretending, the way we always pretended, and the pretending is the religion, and I've just walked into the vestry.*

There was a mark on the glass. Low, at the height of a mouth rather than a hand: a small circle of condensation, already fading, as if someone had breathed against the pane and held it there. Watching. Waiting for him to come and stand beside them.

He raised his own hand and, God help him, before he could think better of it, he breathed against the glass in the same spot — a child's gesture, a twin's gesture, the kind of thing they'd done to each other on cold train windows a lifetime ago, drawing faces, playing the game where you guessed what the other was drawing before it finished.

The condensation bloomed under his breath. And in it, in the fog of it, before it faded, a shape appeared that he had not drawn: a single line, curved, like the beginning of a letter. Like the beginning of a word someone had started writing and stopped.

Like the unfinished verse, he thought. Everything in this village was unfinished. That was the whole architecture of the place — a sealing left open, a bell with no bell, a register with a crossed-out name, a sister's room kept ready for a brother who was maybe not the brother.

The mark faded. The room behind him was silent, but it was the silence of held attention, of an audience leaning forward.

Sable turned around slowly. Empty room. Made bed. Squared books. Four pairs of shoes pointing at the door like compass needles, all of them oriented not toward the exit but toward the window — toward the well — as if they'd been arranged by someone who expected him to put them on and walk out across the nettles and step over the lip and down.

"No," he said to the room, firmly, the way you'd speak to a dog eyeing the dinner table. "Whatever this is. Whatever you're — arranging. No."

The room said nothing, which was somehow worse than an answer.

But as he reached the doorway, his foot came down on the last of the footprint trail — the freshest print, the one nearest the door — and he felt, distinctly, through the sole of his shoe, that the board beneath it was wet. He looked down. A dark bloom was spreading slowly across the grey film of dust, seeping up from between the boards themselves, smelling of deep water and cold stone and, underneath that, of lavender.

From downstairs, faint and impossible, the dry pipes began to sing — one low note, rising, like a voice remembering a lullaby it had promised to finish.

And on the desk, the waterlogged field guide he had not touched again lay open now to a page he was certain had been blank: a page bearing a single line of handwriting, fresh as ink, in a hand that was his and was not his:

*You're standing in my room, Sable. Come and see yours.*
