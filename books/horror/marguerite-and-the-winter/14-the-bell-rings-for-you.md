## 14. The Bell Rings for You

The snow came to Hollowmere the way it always came — sideways first, testing, then all at once, as if the sky had been holding its breath since August and finally let it go.

Maren Trelawney felt it on her face while she was still hanging washing, though the sheets had frozen stiff hours ago and she was only going through the motions of taking them down because her mother had taken them down every first-snow of her life and her grandmother before that, and a woman in Hollowmere did not break a chain like that lightly. She stood in the garden with her bare hands full of frozen linen and watched the flakes come down fat and unhurried, and she thought, *well then*, the way everyone in Hollowmere thought *well then* when the first snow fell, which was not quite a greeting and not quite a farewell but something in between, the way you might say *well then* to an old dog who had come back after you'd half given up on him.

She got the sheets inside. She banked the fire. She put the kettle on, because there is no situation so grave that it cannot be met first with a kettle, and then she went to the window and looked up at the church tower, the way everyone in Hollowmere looked up at the church tower on the night of the first snow, without admitting to themselves or anyone else that they were doing it.

The bell rang at nine minutes past seven.

It rang thirteen times.

* * *

Now, you must understand something about the people of Hollowmere, and it is this: they were not a cruel village. They were not even, strictly speaking, a frightened one. Fear is for things you don't know, and the people of Hollowmere had known the thing beneath the well for two hundred years the way you know a bad hip or a leaky roof — intimately, wearily, with a kind of grudging respect. They had built their whole lives around it the way a tree builds itself around a fence wire. You could not say the tree loved the wire. But take the wire away and the tree would fall over.

So when the bell rang thirteen times, nobody screamed. Nobody ran into the street waving their arms. What happened instead was this: every soul in Hollowmere stopped what they were doing, turned toward the Yarrow cottage at the end of Church Lane, and began, quietly, to get ready.

In the Trelawney kitchen, Maren's husband Jory set down his spoon mid-soup and said, "Thirteen," in the voice of a man confirming an appointment he'd been dreading for years.

"Thirteen," Maren agreed. She took the soup off the heat so it wouldn't catch. "You'll want your good coat."

"It's not a funeral."

"No," she said. "But it's not not one, either." And she went upstairs to fetch his good coat, and while she was up there she took the candle from the windowsill — the tall one, the beeswax one they'd bought in October from the chandler in Ashcombe who thought they were very religious — and she carried it down cradled against her chest like a sleeping child.

Jory looked at the candle and then at his wife, and what passed between them was forty years of marriage compressed into a single glance, which is to say: nothing was said, and everything was understood.

"The Yarrow lad," Jory said. "He seems a decent sort."

"He does."

"Polite. Helped me get the gate unstuck in November."

"He did."

Jory sighed, the sigh of a man who has helped someone unstick a gate and must now help drown him. "Well then," he said.

"Well then," said Maren, and lit the candle.

* * *

Across the lane, in the cottage with the crooked chimney, old Mrs. Pentyne was already dressed. She had been dressed, in truth, since the solstice preparations began, on the theory that a woman of eighty-three should never be caught unready for anything, including the end of the world, which she had personally survived four of (the winters of '51, '63, '79, and the one everyone pretended hadn't happened in '94). She heard the thirteenth stroke fade into the snow-hush and she picked up her candle, and her stick, and her hymnal, and she let herself out into the falling white with the brisk air of a woman heading to a committee meeting.

Halfway down her path she paused, went back inside, and fetched a tin of shortbread.

"You don't bring biscuits to a drowning," said her nephew, who had come to check on her and now stood in her doorway with snow melting on his shoulders.

"You don't bring *nothing* to a drowning," Mrs. Pentyne said severely. "That's how you get drowned yourself. The Yarrow boy hasn't eaten properly since he arrived — I've seen his bin. It's all tins. A body can't walk into cold water on a diet of tins." She tucked the tin under her arm. "Besides. If he says yes — if he goes down willing — then it's not a drowning at all, is it? Then it's a wedding of sorts. And you don't turn up to a wedding empty-handed either."

Her nephew had no answer to this, because there wasn't one. He fetched his own candle and followed her out.

* * *

They came from every door in Hollowmere, and they came gently.

That was the thing that would have struck an outsider, if an outsider had been standing in Church Lane that night watching the village assemble itself out of the snowfall: the gentleness of it. No torches. No pitchforks. No mob-shout. Just some sixty souls in their winter coats, each carrying a single lit candle cupped against the wind, walking at the pace of an evening stroll, singing under their breath in a minor key a hymn so old that most of them only knew it the way you know a lullaby — from the inside, from childhood, from having heard it through walls before you knew what the words meant.

*Four hands laid the stone,
four mouths gave the breath,
one above and one below,
and neither one alone—*

Tam the sexton stood at the churchyard gate and counted them in, because counting was Tam's gift and his burden both. Sixty-one candles. He nodded to each person as they passed, and each person nodded back, and there was something almost cheerful in it, terribly, horribly almost-cheerful, the way there is almost-cheerfulness in a household on the morning of a difficult journey — everyone being extra kind, everyone checking pockets, everyone laughing a beat too readily.

"Evening, Tam," said the Hendersons, both of them, carrying one candle between them because they'd always shared everything.

"Evening, Tam," said young Will Pentyne, who was fourteen and had been told all his life not to answer if called by name after dark, and who had therefore developed the habit of answering to nothing at all, which his mother said would be the death of him and his father said was probably what kept him alive.

"Evening, Tam," said Dr. Lassiter, who had moved to Hollowmere eleven years ago from the city and had learned, slowly, the way an immigrant learns a language, that there were things you asked about and things you brought candles to, and tonight was firmly the latter.

And last, padding along at the rear with her collar up, came Maren Trelawney, and Tam fell into step beside her.

"He knows, then?" Maren asked quietly. "About the well. About what's wanted."

"He knows," Tam said. "I told him myself. All of it."

"And he stayed?"

"He stayed."

Maren was quiet for a few steps. The snow came down between the candle flames and made little hissing deaths, dozens of them, thousands, a soft continuous whispering all down the lane like the sea trying to remember itself.

"My gran walked on the water," Maren said at last. It was the sort of thing you said in Hollowmere the way other people said *my gran liked a sherry*. "She went out onto the mere in January of '38 in her good boots, and the water held her up like glass until she got to the middle, and then it didn't. And do you know what the strange part was? Everyone said she looked *happy*. Walking out. Like she'd been called somewhere by someone she'd missed a long time."

"That's the trick of it," Tam said. "It calls you kind."

They walked on. Ahead of them the Yarrow cottage glowed at the end of the lane, its windows amber against the blue-dark snow, smoke rising straight from the chimney, and it looked — this was the unbearable thing, the thing several of them would privately confess later — it looked *homey*. It looked like Christmas Eve. It looked like everything a house was supposed to be, and they were all walking toward it with candles to ask the man inside to climb down a well forever.

Mrs. Pentyne, at the front with her shortbread, summed up the general feeling when she murmured, to no one in particular:

"Poor lamb. Poor, poor lamb. I do hope he eats the biscuits first."

* * *

Sable Yarrow opened the door before they knocked.

Later, everyone agreed on this detail, the way villages agree on details the way stones agree on moss. He opened the door before they knocked, and he stood in the doorway in his shirtsleeves — no coat, though the cold poured past him into the hall like a visiting relative — and he looked at the sixty-one candles and the sixty-one faces and the snow coming down among them, and he did not look surprised.

Perhaps he had heard the bell too. Of course he had heard the bell; the whole county had heard the bell. But there was more than that in his face. There was the look of a man who has found the letter he'd been dreading on the mat and has read it and has put it in his pocket and has gone on making tea, waiting.

"We've come to ask you something," said Tam, stepping forward, because it was his place; he was the sexton, keeper of the register, counter of the dead. "And we've come to ask it kindly, because it should be asked kindly, and because—" here his voice caught, just slightly, just enough that Maren beside him heard it and reached over and squeezed his arm without looking at him, "—because we're asking a great deal."

Behind him, softly, the hymn rose again, sixty voices low and warm, breath steaming around the candle flames:

*One above and one below,
and neither one alone.*

"We're not going to make you," said Mrs. Pentyne, pushing forward with the terrifying gentleness of the very old. "I want that said plainly, Sable Yarrow. No one in this village will lay a hand on you. We'd sooner cut off our own arms. But the water's three feet from the brim, and it's climbing the church walls inside now — you've seen the damp line, haven't you, in the vestry, above the wainscot? — and on solstice night it'll reach the top, and what's down there will reach up here, and it won't be gentle, dear, the way we're gentle. It doesn't know how. It was never taught."

"We've brought candles," said Jory Trelawney, feeling this was inadequate, and it was, and he said it anyway, because what else was there.

"I've brought shortbread," said Mrs. Pentyne, and held up the tin as evidence.

And Sable Yarrow stood in his doorway in the falling snow and looked at them — at the Hendersons sharing their candle, at young Will Pentyne carefully not answering to any name, at Dr. Lassiter who still crossed himself wrong, at Maren with her frozen-wash-stiff hands and her grandmother's boots — and whatever was in his face, it was not hatred. Several of them swore afterward it was gratitude, which was somehow worse.

"You sang me here," he said quietly. "All these years. Didn't you. Every winter. That hymn — Marguerite used to hum it. She said she didn't know where she'd learned it." His hand went to the doorframe and gripped it, and his knuckles were white, and whether that was grief or cold or something older moving under his skin, not one of the sixty-one could have said. "You sang it to keep it asleep. And now the song isn't enough anymore, and you need—"

He stopped. They waited. The snow filled the pause the way snow fills everything: completely, patiently, without malice.

"We need a Yarrow twin," Tam said. "Willing. That's the covenant. One above and one below, and neither one alone. And there's only you left, Sable. There's only ever been you."

For a long moment the only sound was the hymn, still going, softer now, almost tender, sixty voices breathing the old words into the dark like a lullaby sung over a cradle —

*four mouths gave the breath,
one above and one below—*

— and then Sable Yarrow stepped back from the doorway, opening the hall behind him, warm lamplight spilling out across the snow, and he said the words that every one of the sixty-one would carry home and repeat in whispers for the rest of their lives:

"Then you'd better come in out of the cold. Because I'm not deciding anything standing in a doorway — and someone had better explain to me why my sister's handwriting has started appearing in my journal, in ink that's still wet."

The villagers looked at one another over their candle flames.

Nobody had told him about the journal.

Nobody, in fact, had known about the journal at all.
