## 16. The First Founder's Daughter

The crypt door was not locked, which Sable found more frightening than any lock would have been.

He had come to the church at dusk, when the light went thin and blue and the villagers drew their curtains against the early dark. He had told himself he was looking for the founders' ledger again, for anything Tam might have missed, for some clause in the covenant that would let him out of what was coming. But his feet had carried him past the vestry stairs and down the worn steps into the cold beneath the chancel, and there the door stood open, breathing out air that smelled — impossibly, absurdly — of woodsmoke and baking bread.

Crypts were not supposed to smell like home.

"Sable Yarrow." The voice came from the dark ahead of him, and it was old in a way that had nothing to do with age. It was old the way stone is old, the way water is old. "You took your time. I've been waiting since 1811."

A candle guttered to life on a ledge beside the far wall, and by its light he saw her: a young woman seated on a stone bench that had surely not been there a moment before, dressed in clothes two centuries out of fashion, her hands folded in her lap as if she'd been sitting there all evening waiting for the kettle to boil. Her face was pleasant and plain and patient, the kind of face you'd trust with your keys or your secrets. Only her eyes gave anything away. They were the color of well water, and they did not reflect the candlelight so much as hold it, the way still water holds the sky.

"You're—" Sable began.

"Elowen Penhale. My father built this church, and my uncle dug the well, and my grandmother made the mistake of being born a twin." She said it lightly, the way one recites a family grace before dinner. "Sit down, love. You're letting the cold in."

Sable looked behind him. The crypt door was shut. He had no memory of it closing.

"I don't have long," he said. "Solstice is—"

"Tomorrow night. Yes. I know the calendar better than the calendar does, these days." She patted the bench beside her. "Sit. I promise I won't bite. Biting requires teeth, and I've been somewhat short on those for a while now."

Against every instinct he possessed, Sable sat. The stone was warm. That was the detail that undid him, more than the ghost, more than the voice from two hundred years dead. The stone was warm as a hearthstone, and when he settled onto it the warmth seeped through his coat and into his bones, and he felt his shoulders drop for the first time in weeks.

"There," Elowen said, satisfied. "You've been running on fear since you got here. Fear burns hot and fast, like straw. You need something slower. Peat. Oak. Something that will last the night."

"How do you know about—"

"About the solstice, the well, the thing below, the verse, the twin?" She counted them off on her fingers. "I know because I helped write it. Not the ritual itself — that was the four families, my father among them, and God forgive them all. But I wrote down the words. I was the one with the good hand. Everyone else's letters crawled like beetles."

"The ledger," Sable said slowly. "In the vestry. The handwriting changes halfway through."

"Mine doesn't change. It just stops." For the first time something moved behind her eyes, some grief so old it had worn smooth as river stone. "Now. Ask me what you came to ask. You've been circling it since you walked down those stairs. You circle things the way your sister did. Or the way *you* did. I never could tell them apart, and I watched you both from the moment you were born."

The words landed somewhere beneath his ribs. "You watched us?"

"Someone has to keep an eye on the Yarrow line. You're the last thread left holding the knot." She leaned forward, and the candle flame bent toward her as if listening too. "So ask."

Sable looked at his hands. They were steady. He was almost proud of them.

"Tam says someone has to go into the well. Willingly. A Yarrow twin. And I found the rite, and the prayer ends mid-line, and I think — I think I understand that part. Whoever goes down finishes the verse." He swallowed. "But nobody has told me what actually *happens*. Does it kill you? Does it — feed you to whatever's down there? Is it a drowning? Is it a door?"

"No," Elowen said.

"No?"

"It isn't any of those things, and it's all of them, and the truth is worse than any of them, which is why everyone in this village would rather let you walk in blind than say it out loud. Cowards. Kind cowards, but cowards." She drew a breath she did not need. "Listen to me, Sable Yarrow, and listen properly, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to make you tea, because you look like a boy who hasn't had tea in a fortnight.

"The thing in the well cannot be killed. It cannot be starved, though God knows the founders tried — three years they starved it, and it ate the winter instead, and the frost came indoors and sat at the table with them. It cannot be chained, because it eats chains the way you'd eat bread. What it can be is *occupied*. Given a shape to wear. A self to be. You cannot cage a hunger, but you can give it a person to be hungry *as*."

The candle flickered. Somewhere above them, faint as a memory, the bell rang once — though Sable knew, absolutely knew, that no rope hung in that tower and no hand pulled it.

"A life given," Elowen continued, "is nothing. A death is a door slamming; the thing simply waits for it to rot open. No. The cost is not a life. The cost is a *self* — divided. One half above, one half below. Forever answering each other. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Sable thought of the journal. *The bell will tell you which one.* He thought of the register with its paired entries, its crossed-out names. He thought of footsteps upstairs in an empty cottage, of water moving in dry pipes, of a voice at the window saying *you came back wrong too.*

"One twin goes down," he said. His voice sounded very far away. "And stays alive down there. Alive and aware. Forever."

"And the other lives up here, growing old, hearing their name called on winter nights, and answering, because they must, because the answering is half the seal. Every call answered keeps the thing asleep. Every year the seal thins, and every generation it must be renewed — not with blood, but with a *splitting*. One becomes two. One above, one below. Two halves of one soul, forever calling across the water." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile Sable had ever seen. "My grandmother was the first. She went down in 1811, willingly, holding her sister's hand right up until the water took her. And her sister lived to be ninety-one, and every solstice of those sixty years she walked to the well and spoke her half of the verse into the dark, and every solstice something spoke back in her own voice. She told me, near the end, that it was the happiest arrangement she could imagine and the cruelest, and that she had never once decided which."

The warmth of the bench suddenly felt less like comfort and more like a hand on his shoulder, keeping him seated.

"That's why twins," Sable said. "It has to be twins. Because the split has to be even."

"It has to be *symmetrical*," Elowen corrected gently. "Two halves of equal weight, or the balance fails and the thing wakes lopsided, and a lopsided god is a hungry god. Twins are easiest. But symmetry can be made other ways, in desperate years. In 1911, when the flood came and three of the four lines had already ended, the village tried to renew the seal with strangers — a fisherman and his wife, bound together by force rather than birth. It half-held. It held just badly enough that the well stayed quiet for seven-year stretches instead of sleeping true. And each bad renewal costs more than the last." She looked at him with those water-colored eyes. "You've seen the well rising. You've seen how fast. The seal is failing, Sable. It's been failing since Marguerite died without a twin to answer her."

The word landed like ice water. "Marguerite died alone."

"Marguerite died *unanswered*," Elowen said. "There is a difference, and the difference is three feet of black water climbing toward the brim. When a twin dies with no one below to answer her, her half of the soul has nowhere to go. It pools. It waits. It sinks back down to the well, because the well is where split souls pool, and it waits for the surviving half to come and complete it." She paused. "Or for the surviving half to run. Running works too, for a while. The roads bend, but they bend slowly. You'd get perhaps a season of white fells and wrong turns before the water found you wherever you stopped. It always finds you. Water is patient. Water is the most patient thing there is, except for grandmothers."

Despite everything — despite the crypt and the ghost and the three feet of rising dark — Sable felt his mouth twitch. "You're very calm about all this."

"I've had two hundred and fourteen years to practice," Elowen said. "Calm is mostly just fear that's finished its chores." She rose, and her movement had the strange doubled quality of a reflection deciding to move first. From a niche in the wall she took a clay teapot that had no business existing, and poured two cups of tea that steamed, and handed him one that warmed his hands all the way through. "Drink. Now. Questions while it's hot."

Sable drank. It tasted of honey and smoke and, underneath, faintly of pond water, and he decided not to think about that. "If I go down," he said, "and speak the verse — what happens to me? To the part of me that's still me?"

"You go down whole," Elowen said, "and you come apart gently, the way a knot comes apart when you find the right end of the string. Half of you remains below, wearing the shape the thing needs, keeping it dreaming. And half of you rises back up the well shaft and walks home and lives your life — but you'll never be entirely sure, ever again, which half is which. You'll dream in two voices. You'll sometimes taste pond water in your tea." She sipped her own cup. "You'll sometimes wake with weeds in your hair and not remember swimming."

"That's not a life. That's a haunting with extra steps."

"It's a marriage," Elowen said. "The oldest kind. 'Til death do us part' never applied, because death can't reach either half while the other half answers. You will be bound to Hollowmere and to the water and to your twin, forever, in a conversation that never ends." She set down her cup. "Your sister has been waiting seven years for you to pick up your end of it."

The tea went cold in his mouth.

"She's down there," Sable said. "She's been down there, calling me—"

"She's been down there *waiting*, which is different, and kinder, and infinitely more painful. She won't call you by name anymore — she learned that much from watching what happened to the fisherman's wife. She just waits, and taps the glass, and hopes." Elowen's voice softened. "The thing below wears whoever it holds. Right now it wears her loneliness. If you wait too long, it will start wearing yours, and then the two of you will be indistinguishable, and the seal will fail anyway, because a seal made of one soul pretending to be two is no seal at all."

Sable stared into his cup. The surface of the tea trembled, very slightly, in time with something far below the church, far below the earth, patient as water.

"The verse," he said. "The unfinished line. Why couldn't the founders write the ending?"

"Because the ending is a question," Elowen said. "The sealing prayer asks: *who answers?* And only the one who goes down can answer truly, because only the one who goes down knows what they're giving up. Written words are just echoes of a decision. The well wants the decision itself, spoken fresh, in the voice of the one making it." She smiled again, that terrible gentle smile. "That's why they left it unfinished, love. Not out of carelessness. Out of mercy. They wanted every generation to walk down those steps knowing the choice was theirs. Cruel mercy. Founders' mercy."

* * *

They sat together for a long while after that, the ghost and the almost-drowned man, drinking tea that tasted of honey and ponds, while the candle burned without diminishing. Elowen told him about her grandmother, who had gone down singing; about her sister, who had grown old telling the story to children who grew up and told it to their children; about the fisherman's wife, whose half-soul still wandered the white roads in deep winter, looking for a husband who had washed out to sea. She told it all in the tone of a woman discussing weather, and Sable understood that this was her kindness — that she had folded two centuries of horror into something small enough to carry.

At last he stood. The warmth of the bench clung to his coat like a hand reluctant to let go.

"Elowen," he said. "The half that comes back up. The half that lives. Is it — happy? Ever?"

She considered this with the seriousness the question deserved.

"My great-aunt used to say that living half was like keeping one glove of a pair," she said. "It keeps the hand warm. It remembers the shape of the other. And on cold nights, if you hold it up to the fire just right, you can almost feel the fingers move." She looked up at him, and for a moment her well-water eyes were young. "Go home, Sable Yarrow. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow night the village will come for you with candles and hymns, and they'll be gentle, and it will be the gentlest terrible thing you've ever seen. And then you'll walk to the well, and you'll decide — really decide, for the first time in your life, whichever life it was — whether you are the one who goes down, or the one who answers."

"But the ritual needs someone down there regardless."

"Yes."

"And Marguerite is already down there."

"Yes."

"Then what exactly am I choosing?"

Elowen rose too, and pressed something into his hand — small, cold, smooth. He opened his fingers. It was a pebble, grey and ordinary, the kind a child would keep.

"When you stand at the brim tomorrow night," she said, "you'll understand. The choice was never *who*. It was never even *whether*." She began to fade, gently, the way a candle gutters when a door opens elsewhere, and her last words came from everywhere and nowhere, warm as the bench, patient as water:

"The choice is whether you go down as yourself — or let the well decide which of you it kept."

The candle went out. The crypt was cold and empty and smelled of nothing but stone.

And in Sable's pocket, the pebble was wet.
