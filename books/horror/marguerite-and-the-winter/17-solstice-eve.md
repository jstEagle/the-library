## 17. Solstice Eve

The well stood three feet from brimming.

Sable knew this because he had measured it that morning with a length of twine and a fishing float borrowed from the tackle box in the cottage porch — a box neither he nor Marguerite had ever owned, so far as either of them could remember, but which held hooks and lures and a small brass compass that always pointed toward the water. He had dropped the float on a string down into the dark and counted the seconds until it touched, the way you'd count lightning and thunder, except there was no storm coming. There was only the well, filling itself inch by patient inch, and one day left before it didn't need to fill anymore because it would simply be full.

Three feet. He'd tied a knot in the twine to mark it, and then, feeling foolish, untied it again. The well didn't care about knots. The well had its own arithmetic.

The strange thing — stranger than the rising water, stranger than the lag in the reflections, stranger than the way his own handwriting had begun to slant leftward like Marguerite's — was how calm the village was about all of it. On solstice eve, Hollowmere had put up its holly. Mrs. Tregowan had strung paper chains across the shop window between jars of boiled sweets. Someone had swept the church path and scattered ash against the ice, and the whole village smelled of woodsmoke and mince pies, as though the end of everything were a festival, which Sable supposed, for them, it had been for two hundred years.

He found Tam in the churchyard just past noon, chipping ice off the graves with a hand spade, working his way down the rows with the methodical tenderness of a man tucking in children who wouldn't feel the cold.

"You'll ruin your back," Sable said.

"I've ruined worse." Tam straightened, wincing anyway, and looked past Sable's shoulder at the well behind the church wall. He didn't ask. He'd stopped asking after the water reached the second step of the belfry stairs. "Cup of tea? Vicar's got the good stove lit. First time in years anyone's needed it."

"The walls," Sable said. "Has it—"

"Come inside, yes." Tam said it the way you'd report frost on the windows. "Up to the sill of the east window now. Father Anselm's moved the hymnals to the gallery. He says if it reaches the pulpit we'll hold services from the balcony like theatre-goers." A pause, and then, with the ghost of the humor that had survived everything else: "He's rather looking forward to it, I think. Man's been waiting his whole career for a dramatic entrance."

* * *

Inside the church, the water climbed the north wall in a sheet no thicker than varnish, moving without sound, without ripple, catching the candlelight and holding it the way still ponds hold the moon. It did not pool on the floor. It simply rose, defying every law Sable had ever been taught, and where it touched the old plaster it left faint marks — not stains, he realized, studying them. Fingerprints. Hundreds of them, overlapping, small ones and large ones, pressed flat as if someone on the other side of the wall were leaning their whole palm against it, patiently, the way you knock on a door when you know the person inside will open eventually.

"Stop looking at them," Tam said gently. "They only wave harder."

"They're not waving."

"No," Tam agreed. "But it's politer to pretend."

They sat in the front pew with tea that tasted of iron, and for a while neither spoke. The bell yoke hung empty above them, and Sable caught himself listening to it anyway — listening for the silence inside the silence, the shape of a sound that hadn't happened yet but was already rehearsing.

"Marguerite used to sit exactly there," Tam said at last. "That pew. Third from the aisle. She'd swing her legs during the long prayers and her mother would pinch her and she'd start again within the minute." He smiled at his tea. "Terrible child. Wonderful woman. She came to me the winter before she died, you know. Asked me to show her the founders' ledger. I told her it was dust and dullness. She said, 'Tam, everything in this village is dust and dullness, that's what makes the rest of it so interesting.'"

"She knew," Sable said. "Before any of us. She knew what she was."

"She knew what she *wasn't*, maybe. Which in Hollowmere amounts to the same education." Tam set his cup down. His hands were steady, but it was the steadiness of something deliberately balanced. "Sable. I want to say something, and I'd take it kindly if you let me get to the end of it before you argue."

Sable waited.

"I'm seventy-one years old," Tam said. "I've buried four hundred and six people in this yard, and dug two of those graves twice, and I have outlived my wife, my brother, both my knees, and any reasonable expectation of complaint. The ritual wants a Yarrow twin. That's the covenant. But it has never once specified *which* twin, or indeed that the twin need be young, or whole, or — forgive me — particularly useful." He drew a breath. "I've been sexton here fifty-three years. I've kept the false bell ringing since I was strong enough to pull the rope. I have earned this parish's debts the same as any founder's heir, and I am telling you, plain as I can say it: let me go down instead. Tomorrow night. Let me be the sacrifice, and you walk out of here on the morning road and never look back."

The candles guttered, all together, though there was no draft.

"No," Sable said.

"Sable—"

"No." He said it again, quieter, because the first one had come out too fast and too loud, and the fingerprints on the wall had seemed to press closer at the sound of raised voices. "Tam. I've read the ledger. I've read Elowen's account in the crypt. You know what she told me. It isn't a life the well wants. It's a *self divided*. One above, one below, answering each other forever. If you went down, what would answer me? What would I even be answering *to*?" He shook his head. "And besides — it won't hold. You know it won't hold. Elowen said the covenant strains already, with three bloodlines gone. A seventy-year-old sexton standing in for a Yarrow twin would buy this village a winter, maybe two. And then it would rise again, and there'd be no one left who remembered the verse, and no one left to teach it."

"There'd be you," Tam said. "You'd remember."

"I'd be grieving you," Sable said. "I've done enough grieving in this village to last several lifetimes. Possibly literally."

That startled a laugh out of the old man — short, cracked, real — and for a moment they were just two people in a cold church laughing at the worst joke either of them had ever made, and the water on the wall stilled, almost politely, as if it too were listening.

Then Tam's face settled back into its grave lines, and he leaned forward, elbows on knees, an old man pleading with a young one, which Sable thought might be the most unnatural thing he'd seen all winter, and he had seen the well reflect him half a second late.

"I watched them let her die," Tam said. "Do you understand that? Seven years ago, when the accident happened — when *you* went through the ice — I stood on that bank with a rope in my hands and Father Anselm beside me, and neither of us moved. Because the pattern required a drowning, and the pattern required a survivor, and we told ourselves God would sort out which was which." His voice frayed. "I have carried that rope's weight ever since. Don't make me carry yours too. Let an old man pay a debt he can actually afford."

"You didn't kill her," Sable said. "Or me. Whichever of us it was."

"Didn't I?"

"No." Sable heard his own voice come out in two tones, one low and certain, one higher and less so, and pressed on before the gap could widen. "The thing under the well killed her. Or took her. Or — I don't know. But you holding a rope wouldn't have changed what the water wanted. And me letting you drown tomorrow wouldn't change it either. It would just mean Hollowmere traded one grief for another and bought nothing with it." He looked at the wall, at the pale pressed palms. "The ritual wants what it wants, Tam. It's wanted a Yarrow twin for two hundred years. It's been very patient. I don't think it takes substitutes. I think it takes *exactly what it asked for*, or it takes the village, and I am not going to find out which by spending your life to learn."

Tam was quiet a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough. "Your sister argued better than you."

"She had practice arguing with me."

"Aye. That tracks." Tam rubbed his face with both hands, and when he lowered them he looked older and lighter at once, the way men do when a fight they expected to win turns out to be lost. "Then tomorrow night—"

"Tomorrow night I go down," Sable said. "Willingly. That part matters — Elowen was clear. Willingly, speaking the rite, completing what the founders left unfinished." He hesitated. Something had been sitting in his chest since the crypt, since Elowen's cold kind voice in the dark, and he turned it over now and showed it to Tam like a stone found in a boot. "There's one thing I haven't told you. The sealing prayer. In the founders' ledger. It ends mid-line."

"Mid-line?"

"It stops. 'And let the waters keep their keeper, and the keeper keep the—'" Sable spread his hands. "Nothing. Two hundred years, and nobody wrote the ending. Elowen says the founders never wrote it because—" He stopped. His throat had gone tight around the words, and he had to work them loose. "Because the closing words can't be written. They have to be spoken by the one who goes down. From below. The ritual can't be finished from up here, Tam. Whoever descends has to supply the last line themselves. Their own words. Nobody can give them to you. Not the ledger, not the ghost, not the vicar with his balcony theatrics." He laughed, and it came out unsteady. "Two hundred years of heirs reading the same prayer and stopping at the same cliff-edge, and the founders just... trusted each generation to say something true."

Tam stared at him. Then he began, slowly, to laugh too — not the cracked joke-laugh from before but something deeper, almost wondering. "Of course they did. Of course. Those stubborn, frightened, clever old devils. They couldn't finish it, so they built a door that only opens from the inside." He shook his head. "And you're going to stand at the bottom of a well tomorrow night and *improvise*."

"I'm going to stand at the bottom of a well tomorrow night and tell the truth," Sable said. "It's the only thing I've got left that the water hasn't already used."

Outside, the light was failing early, the way it did now, the sun giving up by three o'clock as if it had read the register too. Somewhere across the frozen fells, weather was coming — Sable could feel it in his teeth, in the old break in his wrist, in the place behind his eyes where Marguerite's memories lived alongside his own like books shelved by a careless librarian. Snow by midnight. Solstice by dusk tomorrow. Three feet of dark water, and closing.

Tam walked him to the churchyard gate. At the lych-gate the old man stopped, and gripped Sable's arm with a strength that belied every one of his seventy-one years, and said, not unkindly:

"When you're down there, and you're hunting for the right words — don't reach for the grand ones. Founders always reach for the grand ones. Say what you'd say to her. That's all a closing line ever is."

"And if I can't find it?"

Tam glanced back at the church, at the thin bright sheet of water climbing its walls, patient as ivy.

"Then," he said, "I expect the well will help you finish your sentence."

Behind them, deep under the frost, under the stones, under two hundred years of careful silence, something in the water turned over — and waited, courteously, for evening.
