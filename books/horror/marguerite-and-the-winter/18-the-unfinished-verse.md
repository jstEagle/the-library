## 18. The Unfinished Verse

The vestry archive smelled of candle wax and old paper and, faintly, of the church itself — that cold stone breath that never quite left your clothes. Sable had been there since before dawn, wrapped in Tam's spare coat, a lamp pulled close, the founders' covenant spread open on the table before him like a patient he was afraid to touch.

Tam had gone to fetch tea. Or coffee. He'd said "I'll put the kettle on" with the particular firmness of a man who needed something to do with his hands, and Sable had let him go, because the alternative was watching Tam watch him, and Sable wasn't sure which of them that would be crueler to.

He read the covenant again.

It was not a long document. That was the thing that kept surprising him — he had expected, after everything, some vast grimoire, pages of Latin and sigils, a book heavy enough to require two hands. Instead it was four sheets of good rag paper, folded once, written in four different hands. Four founders. Four families. The Ashcroft hand was small and precise. The Merrilees hand slanted hard to the right, as if its writer had been in a hurry for her whole life. The Penhale hand was nearly illegible, all loops and temper. And the Yarrow hand — his family's hand — was steady, unhurried, almost gentle.

*We bind what sleeps beneath the water,* the first line began, *as we were bound to one another.*

Below that, the terms. One heir from each house, each generation, gathered at the well on the longest night. A shared sacrifice — not blood, the document was careful about this, *not blood, which is cheap, but selfhood, which is dear* — divided between the one who stayed above and the one who went below. Each twin above would speak the keeping-words into the dark. Each twin below would answer.

*So long as the verse is answered,* the Yarrow founder had written, *the water keeps its count.*

And then the prayer itself. The sealing prayer. Sable had read it a dozen times now, aloud, under his breath, feeling the shape of it in his mouth the way you might test an old key in a lock you weren't sure was yours. It was beautiful, in the way old things are beautiful — plain words worn smooth, a rhythm like rowing, like breathing.

*Cold below and warm above,*
*twin to twin, and love to love,*
*what we give we give entire,*
*half a soul against the fire—*

And then nothing.

Sable turned the page. Nothing. Turned it back. Read it again. The prayer simply stopped. Not faded — stopped, mid-line, the ink as black and certain on the final word as it had been on the first. *Half a soul against the fire,* and then blank paper, and then, lower down, in the Ashcroft hand, a single line of commentary:

*The closing words are not written because they cannot be written. They are spoken by the one who goes down, and they are different every time, and they are true every time, or the verse fails.*

Sable sat back in the chair so hard it creaked.

"They didn't finish it," he said to the empty vestry. "They couldn't finish it."

Which meant — and here his mind did the thing it always did, the rational cataloguing habit of a lifetime, except now the catalogue was turning up horrors instead of explanations for creaking floorboards — which meant that every renewal of the ritual, every generation, every solstice for two hundred years, had ended with someone standing at the lip of the well, speaking half a prayer into the dark, and waiting. Trusting. Trusting that whatever went down would find the rest of the line somewhere inside itself, and speak it, and mean it.

Or trusting that whatever came up would.

Sable thought of Marguerite's journal, hidden under the floorboards, its last legible entry — *the bell will tell you which one.* He thought of the register with its paired entries, one crossed out, one waiting. He thought of the well standing three feet from brimming, the water climbing the inside of the church walls like ivy made of night.

And he thought, with a clarity that felt almost like relief: *they've been doing it wrong.* Or rather — no. They hadn't been doing it wrong. They had been doing it exactly as written. But the village, all these years, had treated the ritual as a thing done *to* someone. A sacrifice. A drowning dressed up in hymns. Tam had confessed it plainly enough: they had let Marguerite die rather than break the pattern, because the pattern required a Yarrow twin, and they had assumed the requirement was simply a body. Water wanted water. Fill the quota, keep the count.

But the covenant said otherwise. The covenant said the closing words could not be written, could not be dictated, could not be forced. They had to come from the one who descended — freely found, freely spoken, *true, or the verse fails.*

You could push someone into a well. You could not push them into a poem.

"Oh," Sable said softly. "Oh, you absolute fools."

He was laughing, a little, and then he wasn't, because the laughter caught on something sharp in his chest. Because if the closing words had to be freely given — then Marguerite. Marguerite, who had died in the flood seven years ago, or however she had died, whichever twin she had been. Had she known? Had she stood at the water and tried to speak and found nothing true in her mouth, because she hadn't chosen it? Because the village had chosen for her, gently, terribly, with candles and hymns and the soft collective pressure of people who loved their children and feared the dark?

*The bell will tell you which one.*

Maybe the bell had told her, too. Maybe she had known she was the wrong twin, the unwilling one, the one whose half-line wouldn't come — and maybe that was why the water had kept rising ever since. Why each winter refilled a little higher. Why the thing below slept less soundly every year.

Not because the ritual was failing.

Because it had never, not once in seven years — perhaps not in a hundred — been finished.

* * *

Tam came back with two mugs of tea and the expression of a man who has spent twenty minutes in his own kitchen rehearsing how to say something kind. He set the mugs down, looked at Sable's face, and abandoned the rehearsal entirely.

"You've found something."

"I've found that everyone in this village has been reading the same book for two centuries and none of you read to the end." Sable turned the covenant toward him, finger under the last line of the prayer. "There. It stops. Mid-verse. The founders never wrote the closing words."

Tam frowned. "I knew it ended short. Vicar says it's damage. Damp got at the paper."

"The ink isn't damaged. Look at it. Same blackness, same pressure, same hand. It stops because it was meant to stop." Sable read the Ashcroft note aloud, slowly, letting each word land. "*The closing words are not written because they cannot be written. They are spoken by the one who goes down.*"

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere above them, in the tower with its empty yoke, a beam settled with a groan — or the bell that wasn't there shifted on its hook that didn't exist. Sable had stopped trying to sort those sounds into possible and impossible. The village counted its dead; apparently it also counted its impossibilities, and kept both ledgers balanced.

"Every time," Tam said at last. His voice had gone careful, the way voices do around a wound. "Every generation, someone goes down, and the ones above sing the keeping-words, and then—" He stopped. Started again. "My grandfather stood at that well when he was nineteen. He watched them take old Mrs. Penhale's boy down. Said the singing stopped, and the lad looked up out of the water, and everyone waited, and nothing happened. And then the water closed over him anyway, and they all went home and told themselves it had worked, because the well stayed quiet through the spring."

"It didn't work," Sable said. "It held. There's a difference. It held the way a dam holds — badly, and needing constant repair, and everyone downstream pretending the cracks aren't growing." He tapped the page. "A sacrifice holds. An answer completes. You've been sacrificing people to a poem for two hundred years and wondering why the poem stays hungry."

Tam sat down across from him, heavily, the way men sit when a chair is also a confession booth. "Say it plainer."

"The ritual can't be completed from above." Sable heard his own voice go quiet, and steady, and was distantly surprised by it. "That's the whole design. That's why the founders wrote it this way. Anyone can be thrown into a well. No one can be thrown into a vow. The closing words have to be spoken by the one in the water — willingly, truly, differently every time, because they have to be *his*, or *hers*. Whatever waits down there doesn't want a body. It wants consent. It wants the half of the line that only the drowned can say."

"And if nobody says it?"

"Then the water rises until it says it for us. Badly. In its own voice." Sable thought of the reflections lagging half a second behind. Of the voice at the window saying *you came back wrong too.* "And I don't think we'd enjoy its poetry."

Tam stared at the covenant for a long time. When he spoke again, his voice was rough. "Marguerite."

"Yes."

"She didn't know. Or—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Or she knew, and she tried, and she couldn't, because we'd already decided for her. We carried her to that water singing, Sable. Singing. Like it was a christening. And she went under, and the verse never closed, and we've been listening to it hang open ever since. Seven years of open verse. Two hundred years of open verse." He lowered his hands. His eyes were wet and furious, mostly at himself. "All those winters. All those inches."

"All those inches," Sable agreed gently.

It would have been easy, in that moment, to hate him. Sable examined himself for the hate and found it oddly absent — found instead something more terrible, which was understanding. Tam had been a sexton his whole life, keeper of graves and registers and the small honest arithmetic of a parish. Of course he had believed the ledger was the law. Of course he had believed that a death entered properly was a debt discharged. It had never occurred to any of them that the book was asking a question, not recording an answer.

"The register," Sable said suddenly. "The paired entries. One crossed out, one waiting."

"Aye?"

"That's not a score. That's not the village counting." He was standing now, pacing the little room, the lamp throwing his shadow huge against the vestry wall. "It's a template. It's the founders showing every generation the *shape* of the thing. Two names, one above and one below, forever answering each other. Elowen told me — the first founder's daughter — she said the cost wasn't a life given, it was a self divided. One twin above, one below. The crossing-out isn't death, Tam. It's *assignment.*"

"Assignment," Tam repeated, as if tasting the word and finding it bitter.

"The crossed-out name goes down. The other stays. And the staying one spends a lifetime answering." Sable stopped pacing. His hands, he noticed, were steady. Wrinkled-knuckled, pondweed-scarred, but steady. "Which means the only question that's ever mattered — the only one — is who speaks the closing words. And that can't be decided by a village. Or a vicar. Or a register."

"No," Tam said slowly. "Only by the one in the water."

"Only by the one who chooses the water."

* * *

He walked home through the grey morning, and Hollowmere was beautiful, and he hated how much he loved it. Frost furred the hedgerows. Smoke rose straight from the chimneys. Someone had swept their step and left the broom leaning against the door like a tired friend. At the corner by the shop, old Mrs. Tregowan nodded to him — nodded, not bowed, not flinched, just the ordinary nod of one neighbor to another — and Sable found his throat tight over it. However badly they had read their own book, they had read it frightened, for two hundred years, in the dark, passing the fear down like an heirloom. You could forgive people a great deal for being afraid. You could even forgive them for being afraid of the wrong things.

In the cottage, he built up the fire and laid the covenant beside Marguerite's journal, open to her final pages. Her handwriting had deteriorated near the end — not into madness, he could see that now, but into hurry. She had been so close. Closer than the vicar, closer than Tam, closer than anyone in seven generations.

*Found the Ashcroft marginalia today,* she had written. *The verse doesn't end. It CAN'T end — not on paper. It ends in the water. It ends wherever the one who goes down finds the truth of it. Which means the founders built a door that only opens from the inside.*

And beneath that, in shakier letters:

*I don't think they're going to ask me. I think they're going to carry me. And I don't know what my half of the line is yet. I don't know if you can know it in advance. I keep listening for it. Some nights I hear my own voice under the floorboards, practicing.*

Sable read that line four times. *Practicing.* His sister — or his brother, or himself, the pronouns had long since dissolved like sugar in water — had lain awake in this cottage listening to a drowned voice rehearse a prayer it hoped to speak. Hoping. Even at the end, hoping to get it right.

He closed the journal and sat with his hands flat on its cover, feeling the fire warm one side of his face and the window-cold the other, and understood, at last, what had been asked of him all along. Not courage, exactly. Not sacrifice, though the village would call it that, and mourn him beautifully, and enter his name in the register with a steady hand. What was asked was simpler and harder: to walk to the well on solstice night knowing that no one could tell him the last line. Not Tam. Not the ghost in the crypt. Not Marguerite. He would stand at the edge of the dark water and the dark water would wait, and the true words would either rise in him or they would not, and if they did not, the verse would stay open, and the winter would win, and it would be no one's fault but his own.

Agency. That was the gift buried at the bottom of the whole dreadful design. The founders, in their wisdom or their cruelty or both, had built a lock that could only be turned from within — which meant that for the first time since the coffin, since the bell, since the register and the candles and the white roads that bent home, Sable Yarrow was not being carried anywhere.

He was going to walk.

Outside, the light was failing early, the way it did now, the days shrunk to a fist. Three days to solstice. The well stood three feet from brimming, and tonight, he suspected, it would stand a little less.

Upstairs, in the dry pipes, water moved — and then, for the first time, it didn't just move. It paused. It waited, the way an audience waits.

And far off across the frozen fields, thin and clear and unmistakably patient, the bell that wasn't there rang twice — not thirteen, not a summons, not a toll for the dead. Just twice. Like someone knocking politely at a door.

Like someone asking: *are you ready?*

Sable looked at the journal, and the covenant, and the fire, and said aloud, to the cottage, to the pipes, to the listening dark:

"Not yet. But soon. And this time, I'll bring my own last line."
