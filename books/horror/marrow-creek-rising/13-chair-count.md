## 13. Chair Count

Theo didn't sleep. She told herself it was the coffee, then the deadline, then the drywall dust still in her lungs from cutting open grandma's wall, but by four in the morning she was sitting at her kitchen table with her laptop open and the archive spread across the screen like a body on a table, and she knew the truth: she wasn't staying up. She was keeping watch.

The archive had rules. That was the point of it. Eleven years of pest control jobs, forty thousand photographs, every one timestamped, geotagged, filed by client and structure. Basements, crawlspaces, attic joists, the greased runways of rodents along baseboards. She had built it the way other people built fences—post by post, image by image—until the whole messy world was contained in a system she could scroll. Nothing in the archive could surprise her. That was the covenant.

The chair had broken the covenant.

She started with grandma's house, because that was where the rot had shown first. She pulled every interior shot she'd ever taken there and sorted by date. The earliest were from 2021—a routine wasp nest removal in the eaves, forty-one photos, mostly of soffits and the nest itself, but three of them framed the back hallway by accident, wide-angle, and there in the corner of the frame, soft-edged, patient: a wooden chair.

She had never seen that chair. She had stood in that hallway. There was a coat rack and a shoe tray and a crooked framed picture of Amado holding a fish. No chair. She would have noticed. She noticed everything; that was the whole architecture of her life.

She zoomed until the pixels went to mush. The chair resolved into suggestion—a seat, four legs, a back that might have been a spindle back or might have been nothing at all, just compression artifacts arranging themselves into a shape her brain insisted on reading as furniture. Lens flare, she wrote in her notes, and then deleted it, because she didn't take notes that lied, and if she started lying in the archive the archive was dead and she was standing in a field with nothing between her and the sky.

* * *

She worked backward through grandma's house first, then forward. 2021: the chair in three photos, always the same corner, always slightly out of focus, as if the camera couldn't quite decide it was there. 2022, the termite inspection: eleven interior frames, the chair in nine of them. In two of those, it had moved. Not much. A foot, maybe less. Closer to the hallway.

By 2023 it was in every interior shot, and it had stopped pretending to be blurry. It sat in the corner of grandma's bedroom like it had been bought for the room, like it had opinions about the light. In the March photos it faced the wall. In the June photos it faced the bed.

Theo sat very still in her kitchen while the cursor blinked, and outside a car passed on wet pavement, and she understood that she was doing the thing she always did—documenting instead of deciding—and that the documentation had become a kind of countdown.

She should have stopped there. She knows that now. She should have closed the laptop and called Ruth Halloway-Price and said the words out loud, the words that would have made it real in the right way, the way that comes with witnesses. Instead she opened the folder for her own apartment.

* * *

Her apartment was the clean room. Three years of photographs—she photographed everything, that was the ritual, the private liturgy of control: the mouse she'd trapped under the sink, the water stain on the ceiling she'd documented for the landlord, the moth on the window in July, the moth on the window in October, the moth on the window that might have been the same moth or might have been the same photograph, she'd never checked, she'd never had to check, because the archive was the thing that checked for you.

The first chair appeared in her apartment in a photo dated eight months ago. Her own kitchen. The corner behind the refrigerator, where the wall met the cabinet run, a dead space she photographed because dead spaces were where things lived.

It was the same chair.

She knew it the way you know your own handwriting. The same seat, the same suggestion of spindle back, the same patient angle—turned slightly, always slightly, toward whatever the photograph was about. Toward the mouse trap. Toward the water stain. Toward her, in the one accidental selfie she'd taken to document a swelling on her jaw, her own face half in frame and the chair behind her, in the corner, closer than it had been the month before.

Her hands were cold. She noticed this the way she noticed everything, clinically, at a remove, and then she noticed that the remove was gone, that there was no remove anymore, that the clinical distance she had spent eleven years building had a chair in the corner of it too.

* * *

She did what she always did. She made a spreadsheet.

It took her until sunrise. Every interior photograph in the archive, forty thousand images, and she wrote a script—clumsy, fast, the first code she'd touched since community college—to flag any frame containing a rectangular mass in the lower third with vertical elements above it. It flagged eleven thousand images. Most were furniture, radiators, door frames. She went through them by hand, folder by folder, client by client, drinking coffee that went cold and then making more and letting that go cold too.

The count, when she finished it, she wrote on the back of a lift card because she couldn't make herself put it in the spreadsheet, because the spreadsheet was the archive and the archive was the covenant and this number was a heresy:

2021: 1 chair. Grandma's house only.

2022: 1 chair. Grandma's house, plus 2 photos from the Delgado job, the farmhouse on the county road. The Marrow Creek farmhouse. Of course.

2023: 1 chair, 4 locations. Grandma's. The farmhouse. The old feed store she'd inspected in the spring. Her apartment, once.

2024: 2 chairs. The second appearing first in grandma's bedroom, positioned at the foot of the bed, facing it. Then in the farmhouse kitchen. Then in the feed store. Then in her apartment, twice, in the same week, in two different corners, as if one chair had been joined by another, as if the audience was growing.

2025, the current year, the year of the fingerprints and the locket and the ledger box: 3 chairs. Grandma's room. Her apartment. And in the last set of photos she'd taken at the farmhouse—the dusting job, the lift cards, the ridge detail that spiraled inward without end—three chairs in the front parlor, arranged in an arc, facing the door.

Facing the door she'd stood in.

She looked at the arc for a long time. It wasn't random. She'd arranged enough rodent-bait stations in enough kitchens to know a pattern when she saw one: the chairs were placed the way you'd place seating for something. A presentation. A tribunal. A small, patient audience, waiting for the main event to walk through the door.

* * *

At nine in the morning she called Ruth Halloway-Price, and got the voicemail, and hung up without leaving a message because she didn't know yet what she'd say. *Your grandmother's ghost has been attending my pest control jobs for four years* was not a sentence you left on a machine.

She called Ms. Okonkwo instead, because Okonkwo was real, Okonkwo was billable hours and letterhead, and Theo needed one thing in her life that was still made of paper instead of presence.

"Ms. Villalobos." Okonkwo's voice had the flat warmth of a woman who billed in fifteen-minute increments and meant every one. "I was going to call you. Your grandmother's capacity assessment is scheduled for Thursday, and if the doctor clears her, we can move the signing to—"

"Has anything strange happened at your office?" Theo said. "Papers. Documents. Anything with—marks on it."

A pause. The pause of a careful woman deciding how much to say to a client who might be a liability. "The copier," Okonkwo said finally. "It's been leaving smudges along the margins. I assumed maintenance."

"Hand-shaped smudges?"

Another pause, longer. "Ms. Villalobos, is there something I should know?"

Theo looked at her laptop. On the screen, the parlor of the Marrow Creek farmhouse, three chairs in their arc, and she thought about what she could say that would not get her assessed for capacity herself. "The estate has some complications," she said. "Historical ones. I'm working on them. Don't finalize anything. Please. Whatever comes in, whatever pressure you get from the buyer's agent—stall."

"The buyer's agent has been *very* motivated lately," Okonkwo said, and there was something under the words, something that sounded almost like the beginning of a question. "Oddly motivated. They called twice yesterday asking if we'd set a date. They said—" she stopped. "They said the family had waited long enough."

Theo's skin did something cold. "Did they say who told them that?"

"No one," Okonkwo said. "That's what I mean. There's no name. The receptionist just said a man called, and when she asked for a callback number, the line was—" She exhaled, a small professional sound, a woman putting a strange thing down on a desk and stepping back from it. "Static, Ms. Villalobos. The line was static. I'm sure it's nothing."

"Stall," Theo said again. "I'll explain everything soon. I promise it will make a horrible kind of sense."

She hung up and sat in her kitchen, in her apartment where three years of photographs now contained a growing audience, and she understood something that rearranged the whole shape of the fear. She had thought the thing was haunting grandma's house. That had been terrible in an containable way—houses could be sold, sealed, burned in the imagination. But the chairs weren't in the house. The chairs were in the *record*. Wherever Theo had looked, whatever Theo had documented, the chairs had come to sit and watch, and they were multiplying the way an audience multiplies before a verdict, and there was exactly one event they had all come to see.

The signing. The inheritance. The state's blessing on stolen ground.

They weren't haunting her life. They were attending it. Every photograph she'd ever taken had been, without her knowing, a seat reserved.

* * *

She spent the afternoon in the archive again, but differently now. Not counting. Looking. She pulled the earliest photo of the farmhouse—the first job, the one that had started all of it, the 'rodent problem' call—and she looked past the fingerprints on the doorframes, past the five-feet-six line she'd measured with her own tape, and she looked at the corners.

Empty. Every corner, empty. The farmhouse had been clean in September. The chairs had arrived with her. With her *documentation* of it. The thing hadn't been waiting in the house for her to find it. It had been waiting in the looking.

She thought about the moth. The moth on the window, July and October, maybe the same moth, maybe the same photograph. She opened those files with hands that were not quite steady and she made herself look at the corners of her own window, the glass she'd photographed a dozen times over three years, the boundary between her apartment and the world.

In the October photo, in the reflection, faint, in the corner of her own kitchen behind her, there it was. And beside it, the suggestion of a second.

She had photographed her own window to feel safe, and the photograph had been a guest list.

* * *

Evening came down over the town like a lid. Theo made herself eat—eggs, mechanically, standing up, because sitting at her kitchen table had become a different activity now—and then she did the last thing on the list, the thing she'd been circling all day, the way you circle a trap in the dark.

She called her grandmother.

Rosa picked up on the fourth ring, and her voice was small and far away and lucid, which was worse than the fevers, because the lucid days were when Rosa could still choose what to say. "Mija. It's late."

"Grandma. I need to ask you about the chairs."

Silence. The long kind. Theo held the phone and watched her own breath fog the cold kitchen window, and on the glass, in the fog, she did not draw anything, and she did not look at the corners of the room, and she was proud of herself for both of those things.

"You've been looking at your pictures," Rosa said at last. Not a question. Rosa had known Amado for fifty years; she knew what it looked like when a Villalobos started counting things that couldn't be counted.

"They're in all of them," Theo said. "Grandma, they're in *my* apartment. In my photos. They've been—" She stopped, because the next word was *following*, and the word after that was *me*, and she wasn't ready to give the thing that much shape. "How many were there? In 1974. When Dad—when Amado came home from the creek. How many chairs?"

Rosa's breath crackled against the receiver. When she spoke, her voice had dropped into the register Theo thought of as the ledger voice, the voice from behind the locked box, fifty years deep.

"He said one," Rosa said. "One chair, in the corner of the kitchen, the night he came home with the creek on his arms. He said it was facing the table. He said it stayed facing the table for the rest of his life, and he never once sat with his back to it." A pause. "He counted them, mija. At the end, in the hospital, he counted them. He got to nine."

Theo's mouth went dry. "Nine chairs."

"He said they were waiting for the reading of the will." Rosa's voice thinned. "He said, *Rosa, they're not haunting us, they're attending us, and attendance ends when the debt does.* And then he asked me to promise—" She stopped. The line hissed. Somewhere on the line, under Rosa's breathing, there was a sound Theo had heard before, on the farmhouse job, through a closed door: a slow, dry scrape, wood on floorboard, patient as a clock.

"Grandma," Theo said. "Is there someone in the room with you?"

Rosa didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice had gone soft and strange, the voice of a woman looking at a corner of a room she had looked at for fifty years and finally, tonight, seeing it look back.

"No, mija," she said. "But it's brought a friend. And they've both turned to face the bed."

The line went to static, and Theo stood in her kitchen with the phone against her ear, listening to nothing, while behind her—she didn't turn, she didn't turn, she kept her eyes on the fogged window and her own pale reflection and the dark glass beyond it—something in the corner of her apartment settled itself, quietly, into a seat that had been waiting for her all along.
