## 16. Rosa Signs Nothing

Rosa woke before the light did, the way she had for fifty years, and for the first time in months the room made sense to her. The walls were hers. The quilt was the one Amado's mother had sewn, the one with the crooked star in the corner because Consuelo's eyes had been going even then. The window showed the pecan tree, and the pecan tree showed the sky, and everything was where she had left it.

Lucid. That was the word the doctors used, delivered like a weather report. *She'll have good mornings. Enjoy them while they last.*

Enjoy them. As if a clear head were a gift instead of an inventory.

Rosa lay still and took stock. Her hands ached but obeyed her. Her mind was a house with all the lamps lit — she could feel it, every room swept, every drawer that needed emptying finally open. She knew what day it was. She knew her granddaughter's name, both names, Theo Villalobos, and she knew what Theo had been finding in the walls of this house, because Rosa had put some of it there herself and had been waiting fifty years for someone stubborn enough to go looking.

The chair was in the corner.

Rosa looked at it without surprise. It had been there since 1974, off and on, more on lately. A plain wooden chair, the kind farmhands sat on at noon, its seat worn pale in the shape of no one. Amado had never seen it. He'd have burned the house down around it if he had. But Rosa had seen it plenty, especially after he died, as if his silence had been the only thing holding it at a distance and once he stopped breathing it began to walk.

This morning it stood by the wall, patient as furniture. Not close. Not yet.

Rosa got up, dressed herself properly — the good blouse, the one with the small embroidered flowers, because today was a day for decisions — and went to the kitchen to make coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in. Then she called her granddaughter.

"Theo," she said, when the girl answered on the second ring, breathless, already awake, already afraid. Rosa could hear fear in a voice the way she could hear rain coming in the pecan leaves. "Come early. Bring nothing. I want to talk before my head goes soft again."

"Grandma—"

"Before it goes soft," Rosa repeated. "You know how it is. Some mornings I'm me. Today I'm me. Come."

* * *

Theo arrived at eight with her camera bag slung across her chest like a bandolier, and Rosa almost laughed. The girl photographed everything — doorframes, receipts, the backs of her own hands. Amado would have hated it. Rosa understood it better than Theo knew. When you grow up around a thing that won't be seen, you learn to keep proof. You learn that a record is a kind of armor.

"You look terrible," Rosa said, pouring coffee.

"You look great," Theo said, and it was true, and they both knew why that frightened them.

They sat at the kitchen table, and Rosa set her hands flat on the wood, palms down, the way she did when she meant business. Outside, Marrow Creek ran high and brown from the week's rain, loud enough to hear from the porch, which it hadn't been in years. The land was talking. Rosa had known it would come to this.

"The lawyer wants you to sign Friday," Theo said. "Ms. Okonkwo called me yesterday. She says if we wait any longer, probate—"

"I know what she said." Rosa sipped her coffee. "I've been saying yes to that woman for three months. Nodding along. Letting her think the papers were as good as done."

Theo went very still. It was a quality she had, the stillness — a pest controller's patience, a photographer's patience. Waiting for the thing in the wall to move.

"Why?" Theo asked.

"Because I wanted to see what you'd do first." Rosa turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "I wanted to know if I'd raised a signer or a payer. There's a difference, mija. A signer puts ink where she's told. A payer settles accounts."

"The accounts are settled. The farm's ours. Fifty years of taxes, fifty years of Amado breaking his back on it—"

"Fifty years," Rosa said quietly, "of interest."

The word sat between them like a third person at the table.

Rosa pushed back her chair — her chair, the kitchen chair, the honest one — and walked down the hall to the bedroom, and Theo followed, and Rosa knelt at the closet with the groaning effort of seventy-nine years and dragged out the box.

It was a steel document box, the old kind, olive-drab, bought at an army surplus store in 1975 with money they couldn't spare. AMADO VILLALOBOS was stenciled on the lid in his own hand, though the contents had never been his. Beneath the stencil, in Rosa's smaller script: **1974**. She'd labeled it the year it all happened so that if she ever died suddenly, whoever found it would know exactly which wound to open.

She set it on the bed between them.

"Do you know what your grandfather did in the spring of '74?" she asked.

"He bought the farm cheap. Everyone said Edith Halloway was desperate after her husband passed."

"Everyone said that because we let them say it." Rosa's hands rested on the cold steel. "Edith Halloway wasn't desperate. Edith Halloway was drowning — not in the creek, not yet. In the paperwork. Men came to her table with pens and smiles, and your grandfather was one of them, and the price they paid her was half what the land was worth, and she signed anyway because they made sure signing was easier than fighting. And three weeks later she walked into Marrow Creek with her coat on and didn't walk out."

Theo's face had gone the color of the morning fog. "The ledger box. The locket in the wall. Grandma, I found—"

"I know what you found. I put most of it where you'd find it." Rosa lifted the lid.

Inside: the deed addendum, sealed in wax that had gone the color of dried blood. A bundle of letters tied with string gone soft as cloth. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth against fifty years of damp, a stack of pages in Amado's careful, school-taught hand — the confession he had written and never signed, because a signature makes a thing real and Amado had spent his whole life refusing to make things real.

"There's a story in this family," Rosa said, "that your grandfather pulled something out of the creek the night Edith drowned. Mud to the elbows, wouldn't wash for two days. People said he stole from the dead. Jewelry, papers, God knows." She shook her head slowly. "He didn't steal. He *retrieved*. He went into the water after her body — him and two other men — and it was Amado who found her, snagged on the foundation stones of the old homestead, and it was Amado who took the locket off her neck and the addendum out of her coat pocket, because he thought—" Rosa's voice caught, and she let it catch; there was no one left to perform strength for. "He thought he was protecting evidence. He thought if he held those things, the truth about what they did to her would stay findable. That someday he'd be brave enough to bring them forward."

"And he never was."

"And he never was." Rosa closed her eyes. "And neither was I. That's my sin, mija, and it's bigger than his. He kept the papers out of fear. I kept them out of greed. Because that farm fed my children. Because every year I told myself next year, next year we'll give it back, and every year the corn came in and the children grew and the year after next was easier than now. Fifty years of next year." She opened her eyes and looked at her granddaughter straight on. "I am telling you this now, this morning, while the lamps are all lit, because tonight they may not be."

"Grandma—"

"Listen." Rosa took Theo's hands. They were strong hands, calloused from ladders and crawlspaces, hands that knew how to hold a tool. Good. She'd need them. "I kept the debt so you'd be the one strong enough to pay it. Do you understand? If I'd paid it in 1980, we'd have lost everything and you'd have grown up angry and poor and never known why. If I pay it now, I sign my name to fifty years of theft and the lawyers carve up what's left and the creek takes its price anyway. No. I carried it. I carried it the way you carry a stone in your mouth — long enough that it wears your teeth flat, long enough that you forget it isn't part of you. And now I'm putting it down, and I'm putting it in your hands, because you're the only one in this family who photographs doorframes. You see things, Theo. You always saw them. Even when you were six and swore there was a man standing in the corner of my bedroom and I told you it was the coat rack." Rosa smiled, and it hurt. "It was never the coat rack."

Theo's eyes were wet. "The chair," she whispered. "You've seen the chair."

"Since '74. It comes and goes. Lately it doesn't go." Rosa glanced toward the corner of the bedroom, where the morning light fell clean and empty — for now. "It's waiting for the signature. That's all it's ever waited for. The moment a Villalobos signs that transfer, the land belongs to us whole and legal and blessed by the county, and whatever Edith Halloway left unfinished becomes ours forever, debt and all. It doesn't want to hurt us, mija. It wants us to *commit*. There's a difference between a haunting and a creditor, and nobody ever teaches you that."

"So we don't sign."

"We don't sign." Rosa lifted the oilcloth bundle and the letters and the addendum out of the box and pressed them into Theo's arms, all of it, the whole weight of 1974. "And you take these, and you do what your grandfather couldn't and I wouldn't. You give them back. To the water or to the Halloways or to both — you'll know which, you're the one who sees. And then you file the correction, the quiet kind, the kind that gives the land back without making my family criminals in the newspaper. Okonkwo will fight you. Let her read the confession. Unsigned paper still cuts."

"What about you? What happens to you?"

Rosa patted her granddaughter's cheek, rough palm against soft skin. "I'm seventy-nine and my head empties out like a bucket with a crack in it. Whatever comes for me has been patient a very long time, and I've been tired nearly as long." She said it lightly, the way she said everything that mattered. "Go on. Take it home. Read every page. And Theo — don't photograph the box. Some things you should carry with your hands only."

* * *

Theo left just after ten, the bundle under her coat, walking fast to the truck like a woman carrying embers. Rosa watched from the porch until the dust settled, and she felt lighter than she had in fifty years, and she knew the feeling was a lie, or at least a loan.

The afternoon went gently. Rosa shelled peas on the porch. Called Ruth Halloway-Price's number from the piece of paper Theo had left on the refrigerator and spoke to the woman for twenty minutes, two old debts recognizing each other down the line, and hung up feeling like a weight had shifted from her back to a wider pair of shoulders. She watered the geraniums. She ate soup. She napped in the good chair with the radio murmuring.

The slip came at dusk, the way it always did — not dramatic, just a slow dimming, lamps going out room by room behind her eyes. She noticed it in the kitchen, holding a cup she didn't remember filling. The date slid away from her. Amado's name surfaced and wouldn't sink again, and she called for him twice before she remembered the sound a coffin lid makes.

By full dark, Rosa Villalobos was mostly gone, a lit window here and there in a dark house. She sat in her bedroom in her nightgown, doing the small rituals that still worked — touching the quilt, counting the buttons on the cardigan — and she was aware, in the way you're aware of weather through a wall, that something else was in the room.

The chair had moved.

It stood beside the bed now. Closer than it had ever stood in fifty years. Close enough that if she reached out from the mattress her fingers would touch its back. It faced the bed. It faced her. Patient. Almost courteous. Like a visitor who has finally been told *yes, you may sit down.*

Rosa looked at it for a long time. Part of her, the part still above water, understood exactly what the closeness meant. The papers had left the house. The debt had changed hands. The chair wasn't waiting anymore.

It was keeping her company while it waited for the rest.

"It's in her hands now," Rosa told it, or told the room, or told Amado, or told no one. Her voice came out thin and strange. "She's stronger than either of us. I made sure of it."

The chair did not move. It didn't need to. Down the hall, in the corner of the kitchen where the shadows pooled thickest, Rosa heard — or imagined, or didn't — the soft scrape of wood on linoleum, drawing nearer, one patient inch at a time, all night long.

At dawn, Theo would call, and Rosa would answer in a voice that belonged to someone else entirely, asking sweetly, over and over, when the lawyer was coming, and whether the papers were ready to sign.
