## 2. Grandma's House Has One More Chair

Orsen came up the gravel drive at eleven in the morning, which for a man who slept between four and noon was roughly the equivalent of a dawn raid on his own life. The Blackwood place sat at the end of Halloway Road like a thing that had grown there rather than been built — two stories of gray clapboard, a porch that sagged with dignity, maples older than the town charter throwing shade over everything like chaperones.

He'd brought coffee and a box of archival sleeves from the station's supply closet, which he had no intention of explaining to accounting.

Mirabel was already on the porch, waiting in her rocking chair, small and straight-backed in a cardigan the color of dried roses. Eighty years old tomorrow and she still sat like a woman expecting a photographer.

"You look terrible," she said, by way of good morning.

"You look great for a centenarian."

"Eighty, Orsen. Don't flatter me into the grave." She took the coffee, wrapped both hands around it, and looked at him a moment too long. "You slept?"

"I slept."

"Liar. You have your radio voice on."

He didn't know what that meant, but it unsettled him, so he kissed her cheek and carried the boxes inside before she could say anything else true.

* * *

The house smelled the way it had smelled his entire life: cedar, old paper, and something faintly mineral underneath, like the air after a struck match. The living room table had been cleared for the project — every photo Mirabel owned, seven decades of Blackwoods, was to be sorted, labeled, and mounted for the birthday display. Cousins were coming from three states. Aunt Carol was bringing her deviled eggs and her opinions. Ruth's kids would run screaming through the halls until someone threatened them with the cellar, the way every generation of Blackwood children had been threatened.

Orsen set the sleeves down and looked at the first stack. Photographs fanned across the table like a hand of cards someone had been holding for a very long time.

"Start with the fifties," Mirabel said, settling across from him. "We'll work backward. I want the wall to tell a story."

"What story?"

She considered. "That we were here."

He picked up the first photo. A picnic, 1954 by the penciled date on the back. His grandfather as a young man, squinting into the sun, a checked blanket, a thermos. And beside the blanket, at the edge of the frame —

A chair.

Plain wooden, straight-backed, unremarkable. Empty. Positioned as though someone had set it there for a guest who hadn't arrived yet, facing the camera dead-on.

"Huh," Orsen said.

"What?"

"Whose chair is that?" He turned the photo toward her.

Mirabel looked. Her face did something very small and very fast — a flicker, like a bird crossing a window — and then smoothed over. "I don't remember a chair," she said. "Picnic must have been near someone's house. Someone brought chairs out."

"Sure." He set it aside. "Next."

The next was a Christmas shot, 1961, the tree tinsel-heavy and lopsided. His grandmother at maybe nineteen, laughing at something out of frame. And there, in the corner of the living room, where no furniture in any Blackwood Christmas photo had ever been, stood the chair. Same chair. Same angle. Facing the camera.

"Grandma."

"I see it."

"It's the same chair."

"It's a common chair, Orsen. They made a million of them." She reached for the photo, and he let her take it, and she held it longer than she needed to, her thumb resting just below the chair as if she could feel warmth coming off it.

* * *

Mirabel had known, of course. That was the thing she could not say, the thing she had been carrying since Tuesday like a stone in her apron pocket.

She had found it in the photograph of her own mother, taken on this porch in 1948. The chair had been there, plain and patient, and Mirabel had known — the way you know a face in a crowd, the way you know a voice on the telephone before the caller speaks — that it had not been there when the picture was taken. Her mother had sat on the porch swing. There had been no chair. There had never been a chair on this porch in her life.

She had gone through the albums alone that night, hands steady out of eighty years of practice, and found it in all of them. Every photograph. Every decade. The picnic, the weddings, the christenings, the stiff funeral portraits of people whose names she still said aloud when she dusted. The chair in every one, always empty, always facing out, facing whoever looked. Facing her.

It had not been in the photographs a month ago. She would have noticed. A woman does not forget a chair that watches her from every picture she owns.

She had not told anyone. She had not told anyone the way she had not told anyone other things, once, a long time ago, in this same house, and the not-telling had a taste she recognized, bitter and familiar, like water from a well you know you shouldn't drink from.

Now Orsen was here, turning the photos over with his long radio announcer's hands, and every time he found it — and he found it every time now, he couldn't not — he looked at her with a question in his face, and she gave him the same answer her mother had taught her to give, the answer that was almost a prayer in this family:

Nothing. There's nothing. Look at something else.

"Here's one of you," Orsen said, and his voice had gone careful. "Easter, what, '65?"

Mirabel took it. Herself at twenty-four, in a pale coat and a hat she'd loved, standing on the church steps. And behind her, on the steps, where the other parishioners stood in clusters — the chair. On the church steps. In the sun. Empty and facing the camera, and every person in the photograph stood slightly to either side of it, though none of them appeared to notice it, none of them looking at it, none of them standing *behind* it, as though some part of them, some part below memory, had declined to.

"Orsen." She set it down. "How many have you counted?"

He was quiet. She watched him decide whether to lie to her and watched him decide against it, which was the one decent inheritance she'd given him.

"Fourteen," he said. "Out of fourteen."

"It's a trick of the light."

"Grandma, it's a trick of the light in a photo taken at night." He held up the Christmas one. "In a photo of the lake. There's no chair at the lake. There has never been a chair at the lake. Ruth would have told us. Ruth notices furniture the way other people notice weather."

"Then it's a printing flaw."

"In photos from four different decades, four different processes? Grandma." He put the photos down and looked at her, and she saw that her grandson — her cynical, sleepless, smart-mouthed grandson, the one who made his living not believing things — was frightened. Not of the chair. Of the fact that she wasn't surprised by it.

"Mirabel," he said. He only used her first name when he was serious. "Have you seen this before today?"

The cellar was under the kitchen. It had been sealed in 1961, the year after her brother — but that was not a door she opened, not even in her own head, not even now, not with the chair sitting in every corner of her past like a reserved seat at a show she didn't want to attend.

"I've seen a lot of things before today," she said. "That's what eighty is. Help me with the sleeves."

* * *

Orsen worked for another hour because his hands needed something to do and because stopping meant talking and talking meant the conversation they'd almost had, the one where his grandmother looked at a chair that appeared in every photograph of her life and said *printing flaw* with a face like a woman reading her own obituary.

He cataloged. He sleeved. And the whole time, the chair accumulated.

It wasn't just that it was in every photo. It was that it was *closer* in the later ones. He checked without meaning to, the way you check a clock. The 1940s: chair at the edge of frame, half-cropped, distant. The fifties: mid-ground, fully visible. The sixties: near. By the photos from the seventies — the reunion in the backyard, Aunt Carol gap-toothed on someone's shoulders — the chair stood in the foreground, dead center, close enough that you could see the grain of the wood, the two nails in the seat, the faint wear on the front edge where a hundred hands might have gripped it to sit down or to stand.

It was a good chair, was the horrible thing. It looked *used*. It looked like a chair someone loved, worn smooth in all the right places, and it sat in the middle of seventy years of Blackwood memories like it had attended all of them, patient, attentive, waiting for its turn to be in the picture properly.

"Grandma," he said, "when was the last time you went through these albums? Before this week."

"Christmas."

"And it wasn't in them?"

She didn't answer. She was holding a photo of her own wedding, 1958, and she was holding it very still, and Orsen came around the table and looked over her shoulder and felt the hair rise on his arms.

The church again. The whole congregation turned toward the camera, beaming. And in the center aisle, in the exact spot where a bride walks, close enough to the front pew that whoever sat in it would have been family — the chair. Facing the camera. Facing him.

"Grandma, who sat in the front pew at your wedding?"

"My parents," she said. "And Aldous and his wife." Her great-grandfather. Dead before Orsen was born, a name that came up at holidays the way weather came up — flatly, briefly, and with a strange carefulness, like stepping over something on the stairs in the dark.

"Then who's the chair for?"

"I don't know." And then, so quietly he almost missed it, almost lost it under the tick of the mantel clock: "It's not for anyone yet. That's the trouble."

He turned. "What does that mean?"

Mirabel set the wedding photo face-down on the table, deliberately, like closing a book. "It means I'm an old woman and my eyes are tired and you're going to make me a cup of tea before we do another box." She stood, and her hands, when she gathered the sleeves, were not entirely steady, and she knew he saw, and he knew she knew, and neither of them said it, because that was the Blackwood way, that had always been the Blackwood way: see everything, say nothing, keep the count in silence.

* * *

In the kitchen, waiting for the kettle, Orsen opened his phone and searched his camera roll. He'd taken a picture of the porch when he arrived — a habit, a reflex, he photographed everything, forty photos of nothing most weeks. The porch, the maples, his grandmother in her rocking chair.

He scrolled to it.

There was a second chair on the porch. Plain wooden, straight-backed, set beside the rocking chair, empty, facing the camera. He had been standing not six feet from it. He had walked past it. He had kissed his grandmother's cheek with it three feet away and never seen it, and it was not there now — he crossed to the window and looked, and the porch held one chair and one old woman, and the space beside her was just boards and morning light.

His hands were shaking. He noticed them the way you notice someone else's hands, remote and ridiculous, and he thought: *prank, photoshop, sleep deprivation, the caller last night was a prank and this is your brain still chewing on it, you make your living talking to strangers in the dark, of course you're rattled —*

"Orsen." Mirabel stood in the doorway with the tea things, and she was looking at the phone in his hand, and her face had gone the color of the flour sack curtains. "How many photographs did you take just now?"

"One. Of the porch."

"Show me."

He did. She looked for a long time. Then she set the teapot down on the counter with a click, very precisely, the way you set something down when you are afraid of what your hands will do otherwise.

"That chair," she said, "is the chair from the pictures."

"I know."

"No, Orsen." She looked at him, and behind the eighty years he saw something young and terrified, a child's face surfacing like a body coming up through dark water. "It's the chair from the *cellar*."
