## 5. Family Reunion, Minus One

The Blackwoods arrived the way weather arrives — in stages, with warnings nobody heeded.

Orsen stood on Mirabel's porch at eleven in the morning, holding a casserole dish his producer's wife had sent as a peace offering for all the nights Orsen kept her husband at the station, and watched the first cars come up the gravel drive. Ruth's minivan led the way, kids' faces pressed to the windows like puppies. Behind it came Aunt Carol's Buick, moving at the speed of a woman who had already rehearsed every conversation she intended to have that day. Then a truck with Uncle Dale and his second wife, whose name Orsen could never retain longer than a handshake.

He hadn't slept. The call logs from the station sat in a folder on his passenger seat — forty pages of nothing, no record of the dead woman or of Danny, no trace of either voice ever touching the station's switchboard. He'd brought them anyway, the way you carry a stone in your pocket to prove to yourself the world still has weight.

"Orsen." His grandmother opened the door before he knocked, which was her way. She looked smaller than she had last week, or maybe he was just seeing her more clearly now. Her eyes went to the folder under his arm, then to his face, and something passed over hers — not surprise. Recognition. "You look terrible."

"It's radio. My face is my least important feature."

She took the casserole instead of the folder. Smart trade, he thought. Then, quietly, so only he could hear: "Whatever you found, we'll talk after. Not today. Today I'm eighty, and I'd like one day where this house behaves itself."

*This house.* Not *I*. He filed it away with everything else.

* * *

By noon the kitchen smelled like ham and burnt rolls and Carol's perfume, which she applied the way other people applied insect repellent. The house filled with noise — children thundering up stairs that had been off-limits when Orsen was young, Dale telling a fishing story that grew a pound per retelling, Ruth chasing her youngest with a wet washcloth. It should have been warm. It was warm, mostly. That was the thing Orsen kept circling: how easy it would be to believe nothing was wrong, if not for the way Mirabel moved through her own rooms like a guest checking whether the host was home.

And if not for the cellar door.

It sat at the end of the hallway, closed now, but Orsen caught himself looking at it every time he passed, the way your tongue finds a chipped tooth. He'd asked Mirabel about it once, years ago, and she'd said it was sealed since before he was born, structural problems, damp. But yesterday he'd noticed the hinges were bright. Recently oiled. Somebody wanted that door to open smoothly.

"You're doing it again," said Ruth, appearing beside him with a plate of deviled eggs and her camera hanging around her neck like a stethoscope.

"Doing what?"

"The brooding thing. You've got the same face Dad used to make." She pushed the plate at him. "Eat something. You're skin and cynicism."

Ruth was the family archivist, self-appointed. Every gathering, she documented it — candids, group shots, the cake, the candles, the moment someone inevitably cried. She said somebody had to, because memory was a liar and photographs were the only honest relatives. Orsen had always liked that about her. Today, watching the camera swing on its strap, he felt something he couldn't name yet. A premonition with no image attached.

"Take one of me," he said. "For posterity."

"Smile like you mean it."

"I don't know how."

"That's what makes it honest." She raised the camera. Through the lens he saw himself framed against the hallway, and behind him, at the far end, the cellar door. He almost told her to move. He didn't know why.

The shutter clicked.

* * *

Lunch was served at the long table in the dining room, the one that had seated four generations of Blackwoods and absorbed their spills into its grain. Mirabel sat where she always sat, at the corner nearest the kitchen, close to the work. Carol presided over the ham. The children got the wobbly end seats and considered it an honor.

Orsen counted heads without meaning to. It was a habit from the show — knowing who was in the room, who was listening, who might call in. Eleven people. Twelve counting himself. He did the math twice because the first time felt wrong, and realized the wrongness wasn't arithmetic. It was that some part of him had been expecting thirteen.

"To Mirabel," Dale said, raising a glass. "Eighty years. The toughest tree in the Blackwood forest."

"Hear, hear," said Carol, and then, because Carol could never let a moment breathe, "You know, Mom, you're older than the town charter. Older than the highway. You're older than—" she ticked items on her fingers, playing to the table, "—the school, the feed store, the water tower—"

"Carol." Mirabel's voice was light. Her knuckles, around her fork, were not. "Let an old woman eat."

Laughter. More toasts. Underneath it, Orsen watched his grandmother's gaze travel, once, quickly, to the head of the table — the seat that had belonged to Aldous Blackwood, then to his son, then to nobody, because nobody had wanted it. It sat empty now, as it always did, a high-backed chair worn smooth by hands seventy years dead.

Nobody sat there. Nobody ever sat there. It was one of those family laws nobody remembered passing.

* * *

After lunch, the afternoon went soft and golden. Kids in the yard. Men on the porch arguing about engines. Carol organizing leftovers with the fervor of a general securing supply lines. Orsen helped Mirabel dry dishes, their shoulders bumping in the small rhythm of a chore they'd done together his whole life.

"The folder," he said finally. "In my car. Call logs from the station. There's no record of those calls, Grandma. None. Either the system ate them, or—"

"Or they never touched the system." She handed him a wet bowl without looking up. "Your great-grandfather used to say the telephone lines out here ran through ground that remembered things. Superstition." A pause. "Most of what Aldous called superstition was warning."

"What does that mean? Grandma. What does that *mean*?"

But Ruth came through with her camera and the moment broke like a soap bubble. "Group photo! Everybody in the dining room, squeeze in around the table, Mom wants the whole clan in one frame."

They assembled the way families do — slowly, complaining, rearranging. Dale put the kids in front. Carol fussed with collars. Mirabel was placed at her corner, and Orsen ended up beside her, his hand on her shoulder. And the empty chair at the head of the table loomed in the frame like a missing tooth, and someone — Orsen never learned who — said, "Should we...?" and someone else said, "Leave it," and everyone understood, and nobody understood, and the shutter clicked three times.

Later there was cake. Later there were candles and Mirabel closing her eyes while the room held its breath, and whatever she wished for, her face while wishing it made Orsen's chest hurt. Later the sun went down and the porch lights came on and the gathering began its slow dissolution into coats and keys and promises to visit more often.

It was nearly ten when Ruth, packing her camera bag in the living room, stopped moving.

Orsen saw it happen from across the room — the sudden stillness of her, the way she lifted the camera and thumbed through the display, and how her thumb slowed, and stopped, and her face performed a small, controlled collapse, like a building deciding to fall politely.

"Ruth?"

She didn't answer. She turned the screen toward him.

The group photo. Eleven smiling faces, the birthday banner, the ham-strewn table. And at the head of the table, in the chair nobody sat in, something else.

Not a person. Not quite. The chair was occupied the way a shadow occupies a doorway — a darkness shaped like sitting, faintly darker than the room behind it, edges soft as breath on glass. Facing the camera. Facing *them*, all of them, with the patience of furniture.

"That's a smudge," Orsen heard himself say. "Lens flare. Dust on the—"

Ruth swiped to the next photo. The cake. The candle smoke curling upward, and beside Mirabel, at the table's edge, the chair again — though it hadn't been near the table's edge during cake, it had been against the wall, he was sure of it, he *remembered* the wall — and the shape in it, fainter here, but present. Waiting.

Next. The porch shot, everyone waving. The chair visible through the window behind them, inside the dark dining room, facing out.

Next. Next. Next.

Every photograph. Forty-one images spanning six hours, and in every single one, the chair — sometimes distant, sometimes close, sometimes half-cropped at the frame's edge as though it had been leaning in to see itself better — and in most of them, the shape. In the earliest photos it was barely a suggestion. In the latest ones it had definition. Shoulders. The suggestion of a head, tilted, attentive.

Like something that had spent the whole day getting comfortable.

"No," Ruth said. It wasn't denial. It was arithmetic failing. "No, no, that's — Mom, MOM!"

Carol came in wiping her hands, followed by Dale, followed by the slow gravitational pull of every remaining Blackwood. Ruth showed them the screen. Carol took the camera in both hands the way you take bad news about a test result.

"It's a trick of the light," Dale said. "Old houses, flash photography—"

"There's no flash, Dad. Look at the timestamps. These are daylight shots. Window light." Ruth's voice had gone thin and fast. "It's in all of them. All forty-one. What are the odds of a lighting trick following a *chair* around the house for six hours?"

"Delete them," Carol said. "Just delete them, honey, cameras malfunction, film used to double-expose all the—"

"It's digital, Aunt Carol."

"*Delete them.*"

Ruth deleted them. All forty-one, right there, thumbs moving quick and shaking. The screen went blank. Everyone exhaled.

Then Carol did the thing Orsen would see in his mind's eye for the rest of his life, the thing that would play behind his eyelids during every quiet break between songs.

She started counting.

It began as muttering — a habit, Orsen knew; Carol counted everything, place settings, pills, steps to the mailbox. Her lips moved. Her finger rose and pointed, ticking through the room, through the memory of the table, through the photographs she could still see behind her eyes.

"One, two, three..." Dale. Ruth. The kids, who were upstairs now, thank God. "...eight, nine, ten..."

Her finger reached the head of the table. The empty chair.

"...eleven."

Eleven. They had been twelve at that table. Orsen counted it again himself, silently, frantically — himself, Mirabel, Carol, Dale, Dale's wife, Ruth, Ruth's husband, five children. Twelve. Twelve plates. Twelve servings of ham. He had counted it at the time; he'd done the math twice.

"Mom?" Dale said. "Mom, what is it?"

Carol's face had gone the color of the tablecloth. Her mouth worked twice before sound came out.

"There were twelve," she whispered. "There were twelve of us at that table. I buttered twelve rolls. I *counted*—" Her hand flew to her mouth. "I counted twelve and I thought I'd miscounted, I always miscount, I thought—"

"Carol." Mirabel's voice cut across the room, quiet and absolute, the voice of a woman who had been waiting all day for exactly this moment and dreaded its arrival. "Stop counting."

"But the chair—"

"*Stop counting.*"

The silence afterward had texture. Orsen could feel it pressing on his eardrums. Around him his family stood in the lamplight of a living room that smelled like birthday cake, and he watched the exact moment the horror stopped being his private madness and became communal property — watched it land on each face in turn, Dale's baffled anger, his wife's dawning dread, Ruth clutching the emptied camera to her chest like a wound.

Because here was the thing, the thing Orsen couldn't say aloud yet, the thing that was already writing itself into him like a needle into wax:

Aunt Carol had counted eleven.

One short.

And the chair at the head of the table had been full all day.

Mirabel crossed the room and took Carol's face in her two old hands, gently, the way you hold something that might shatter. "Go home," she said softly. "All of you. Take the children and go home, and don't talk about this tonight, and don't count anything after dark. Do you hear me? Don't count."

"Aunt Mirabel, what is going on in this house?" Ruth's voice cracked on *house*.

What's going on, Orsen thought, is that something has been keeping attendance. Something has been taking roll. And today it sat among us and let us pass it the bread.

He walked his family out to their cars, one by one, standing on the porch until the last set of taillights dropped down the county road. Carol's Buick left last, moving too fast, and he saw her silhouette hunched over the wheel and heard, faintly, even through glass, her lips still moving. Counting. Unable to stop.

When he turned back, Mirabel was standing in the doorway behind him, and past her shoulder, at the end of the hall, the cellar door stood open.

Just a few inches. Just enough to breathe out cold air that smelled like earth and paper and something older than either.

"Grandma," Orsen said. "The door."

"I know," Mirabel said. She didn't turn around. "It opens when it's pleased."
