## 6. What the Ledger Says

The attic smelled like every attic in every old house in America: dust and cedar and the sweet rot of paper that had been dying slowly for a hundred years. Orsen stood at the top of the pull-down ladder with a flashlight in one hand and his phone's voice recorder running in his shirt pocket — an old habit from the days when he still believed evidence mattered to anyone but him.

He'd told Mirabel he was up here looking for baby pictures. Her eightieth birthday was three weeks out, and she wanted a slideshow, something gentle, something that would make the cousins laugh about haircuts and fashion crimes. That was the story he'd given her, and it was even mostly true.

But the real reason he climbed the ladder was the chair.

He hadn't been able to stop thinking about the photographs since Sunday. He'd gone back through every picture on his phone taken inside this house — forty-one of them, spanning six years of holidays and funerals and one disastrous Thanksgiving — and the chair was in all of them now. Not just the recent ones. All of them. He'd zoomed in until the pixels broke apart into gray mush, and there it was anyway: plain wood, straight-backed, empty, facing the camera like it had been waiting for someone to notice.

Photographs didn't work that way. Photographs were fixed. Whatever had gotten into them had gotten into time itself, or into the house, or into him.

So he'd come to the attic, because attics were where houses kept their confessions.

* * *

The light came through a single round window caked with grime, falling in a dull amber column that made the dust look like something alive, drifting with intention. Boxes everywhere. Trunks. A dress form wearing a wedding gown gone the color of weak tea. A child's rocking horse with one eye scratched blank.

Orsen worked methodically, the way he did everything now — the way a man works when he no longer trusts his own mind and needs procedure like a handrail. Family name on each box. Date range. He opened them with a flathead screwdriver pried under the lids, because the tape had fossilized.

Christmas cards from the seventies. His grandfather's war letters, bundled with twine. A shoebox of teeth — actual human teeth, his own baby teeth probably, though for one bad second he counted them twice.

Then, under the eaves, wedged behind a trunk marked ALDOUS in faded block letters, he found the strongbox.

It was small, iron, rusted at the seams, with a keyhole shaped like a keyhole in a cartoon — exaggerated, almost mocking. He shook it. Something inside shifted with a dry, papery whisper.

He should have brought it down to Mirabel. That was the decent thing, the family thing. But his hands were already working the hasp, and when the lock refused him he set the box on the floor and hit it once, hard, with the heel of his fist, and the rust gave like rotten bread.

Inside: a Bible, swollen with damp. A bundle of letters tied in black ribbon. And a ledger.

Not a financial ledger — or not only. It was a school composition book, the cheap marbled kind, its cover soft as cloth from handling. The date on the first page, in fountain pen gone brown, read *March 1932.*

And above the date, in the same careful hand:

*Accounts.*

* * *

Orsen sat down on the trunk because his legs informed him they were no longer interested in standing.

The handwriting was beautiful. That was the worst part, the part he'd keep coming back to at three in the morning for the rest of his life. It was the handwriting of a man who took pride in himself — tall, slanted, confident, the loops closed like doors. Aldous Blackwood, his great-grandfather, dead thirty years before Orsen was born, a name that lived on in the family only as a portrait in Mirabel's hallway: a lean man with a mustache and eyes that looked slightly past the painter, as if the painter weren't worth his full attention.

Orsen knew the face. He'd walked past it ten thousand times. He'd never once wondered what the man was looking at.

Now he knew. The man was looking at a list.

*March 4th — T. Okafor. Settled.*
*March 9th — H. Pruitt. Settled.*
*March 14th — S. & M. Delacroix. Settled.*

Names. Dozens of names, marching down the page in columns, each followed by the same word in the same serene hand. *Settled.* Some entries had small annotations, tidy as bookkeeping:

*T. Okafor — disputed fence line. Resolved.*
*H. Pruitt — spoke out of turn at the mill meeting. Resolved.*
*S. & M. Delacroix — harbored. Resolved.*

Harbored. Harbored what? Orsen turned pages with fingers that had begun, faintly, to tremble. The dates ran through spring, through summer. The names thinned as the year went on, as if the accounts were being collected faster than new debts could be incurred. In July there was a gap of three weeks, and then a single entry written larger than the others, the pen pressed so hard it had torn through to the next page:

*E. Vane.*

No annotation. No *resolved.* No date of settlement. Just the name, alone on its line, the ink around it bloomed dark with moisture — or with something held too long against the paper — and after it, instead of the word that ended every other entry, a long horizontal stroke. A line left open. An account carried forward.

Carried forward ninety-three years.

Orsen became aware that he was holding his breath and let it out slowly, and the sound of it in the sealed attic was too loud, like someone else exhaling behind him.

"Okay," he said aloud, because saying things aloud was how he proved to himself he was alone. "Okay. It's a grudge list. Old man kept a grudge list. People do worse."

People did do worse. But people didn't write *settled* next to the names of men who died in the same weeks — he was almost sure of it, almost sure he remembered Pruitt as a headstone in the churchyard with an 1932 date worn nearly smooth. And people certainly didn't leave one account open for a century while the interest compounded into a wooden chair that appeared in photographs like a reservation at a restaurant.

He went back to the strongbox. The letters tied in black ribbon were addressed to Aldous, most of them, and he skimmed them fast, hungry and afraid in equal measure. Land disputes. A threatening note from a bank. One letter, unsigned, that said only: *You were seen. Everyone saw. God saw.* And beneath it, in different ink, added later, in a hand Orsen recognized with a lurch — Mirabel's hand, younger, shakier, but hers:

*I know what you did. I heard her.*

The date scrawled under it was 1961. The year the cellar was sealed. The year Mirabel's brother went missing, according to the family story, the story told in low voices at funerals: *ran off, poor boy, never found.*

Orsen put the letter down very carefully, the way you put down something that might be sleeping.

* * *

He didn't remember deciding to search harder. He was simply doing it — pulling trunks away from the eaves, opening boxes he'd have sworn an hour ago he had no right to open. Because the ledger was an account book, and account books had sources, and somewhere up here was the record of what E. Vane had supposedly owed.

He found it in a hatbox, of all things, nested under a fox-fur stole that shed onto his sleeves like the animal was still dying. A newspaper. The *Carrow County Courier*, August 1932, folded to page two, the fold lines gone soft and furry with age.

The headline was small. That was what struck him first — how small it was. Two columns, below the fold, beside an advertisement for motor oil.

**WOMAN MISSING; SEARCH SUSPENDED**

The text was brief, bloodless, and obscene in its politeness. *Etta Vane, 19, of the Cross Creek road, last seen the evening of July 30th... described by neighbors as troubled... Mr. Aldous Blackwood, who organized the initial search party, expressed regret that the young woman could not be located... authorities have concluded she likely left the county of her own accord.*

Organized the search party. Orsen read the sentence four times. The man who wrote *E. Vane* in the ledger, who left her account open, had organized the search for her. Had stood in front of the town with his beautiful handwriting and his confident face and directed everyone to look everywhere except the one place he knew.

There was no photograph of Etta Vane in the article. There was a photograph of Aldous, though — standing on the courthouse steps, hat in hand, expression grave and reasonable, a citizen. The caption called him *prominent local landowner.*

Orsen's phone buzzed in his pocket and he nearly screamed.

It was nothing. A weather alert. He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it, and the laugh echoed off the rafters and came back wrong, delayed, like the attic was considering whether to return it.

He looked at the newspaper again. *Described by neighbors as troubled.* Nineteen years old. Accused of — of what? The article didn't say. Articles like this never said, because the accusation was never the point; the accusation was the permission slip. He thought of the ledger again, the neat word *harbored,* and understood with a cold, sliding certainty that Etta Vane's crime, whatever the town had named it, had been something simpler. She had crossed Aldous Blackwood. She had been entered into the book.

And the book was still open.

* * *

By the time he climbed down the ladder, the sun had moved and the round window glowed orange, and the dust in the light column looked less like something alive and more like ash. He carried the ledger against his chest with both hands, the way you carry a wounded bird or a live grenade.

Mirabel was in the kitchen, snapping beans into a colander, radio murmuring some station that played songs about trains. She looked up when he came in, and whatever she saw in his face stopped her hands mid-snap.

"You found something," she said. Not a question.

"There's a—" He started toward her and stopped. The words felt dangerous in his mouth, like carrying water across ice. "Upstairs. In the strongbox. There's a ledger. Grandmother, it's Aldous's handwriting, and there are names, dozens of names, and every one of them says *settled* except—"

"Except Etta Vane," Mirabel said.

The beans hung forgotten in her fingers. She didn't look surprised. She looked like a woman hearing a train she'd been listening for, far off, for sixty years, finally rounding the bend.

"You knew," Orsen said. "You knew it was up there."

"I knew it existed." She set the beans down with great care, aligning them in the colander as if order in small things could hold against disorder in large ones. "I haven't seen it since I was nine years old. My father burned most of Aldous's papers after my brother—after 1961." Her eyes went to the ledger under Orsen's arm, and something moved through her face, quick and terrible, like a fish passing under ice. "But that one he couldn't burn. He tried. He told me he tried."

"Why couldn't he?"

Mirabel was quiet for a long moment. The radio sang about a train that wasn't coming back.

"Because it wouldn't burn," she said finally. "And because—" She pressed her lips together, and Orsen watched an eighty-year-old woman fight a promise made to a dead man by a nine-year-old girl, and lose, and hate herself for losing. "Because the count isn't finished, Orsen. That's what my father said. You can't close a book while an account is open. He said the ledger isn't a record." Her hands had begun, very slightly, to shake. "He said it's a *bill.*"

Orsen looked down at the composition book in his arms. Under the kitchen light, the long unfinished stroke after *E. Vane* seemed darker than it had upstairs, wetter, as if the ink were still drying after ninety-three years, as if the pen were still moving somewhere, slow and patient, writing the only word left in the book.

Behind him, out in the hall, the framed photograph of Aldous Blackwood fell off the wall and landed face-up on the floorboards, and neither of them moved to pick it up, because both of them had heard it land facing the kitchen — facing them — like a man turning his head to see who had spoken his name.
