## 11. Down the Stairs

Mirabel had locked the cellar door with a padlock older than Orsen's father, and the padlock hung open.

That was the first thing he saw when he came down the back hall at six in the morning, sleepless, still smelling of the studio's burnt coffee and his own cowardice. The hasp swung loose against the wood, ticking softly with the house's breathing, and the door itself — the door he had never once seen open in thirty-one years of birthdays and Christmases and funerals — stood ajar by exactly the width of a hand.

Cold air came through the gap like a draft from somewhere much deeper than a cellar. It smelled of wet limestone and old paper and, faintly, of something floral that had rotted in its own sweetness a long time ago.

"Grandma," he said, and his voice came out wrong, flattened by the hallway the way voices go flat in hospitals.

She was in the kitchen. She had not slept either; he could tell by the light on in the pantry and the teacup gone cold on the counter, the tea bag still steeping in it, the water gone the brown of old varnish. She stood at the window with her back to him, small in her flannel robe, her hands folded at her waist like a woman at a graveside.

"You saw it," she said.

"The lock's open."

"I know it's open." She turned. Her face was the face from the photographs now, the one that kept appearing at the head of every table — patient, waiting, arranged. "I've been standing here since two o'clock deciding whether to wake you."

"Why?"

"Because I promised myself that if it opened, I would tell you not to go down." Her chin trembled once and steadied. "And I don't trust myself to keep a promise anymore, Orsen. I haven't since 1961."

He crossed the kitchen and took her hands. They were ice.

"Then tell me now. Tell me what's down there."

"Etta," she said simply, the way you'd say the name of a weather system, a fact of the county. "Etta is down there. Etta has always been down there. And everything the family lost is down there with her, filed away, and she is very, very good at waiting." Her grip tightened on his fingers with a strength that startled him. "Don't go down the stairs, Orsen. Whatever you think you owe her, you don't owe her your body. She doesn't want your body. She wants something worse."

"What could be worse than that?"

Mirabel looked at the cellar door for a long moment, and whatever she saw in the dark gap made her let go of his hands.

"Worse," she said, "is being right."

* * *

He waited until she dozed in the blue armchair, her rosary slack between her fingers, the television murmuring a mass for the shut-ins. He told himself it was mercy, letting her sleep through it. He told himself a lot of things while he pulled on his boots and found the flashlight in the junk drawer and stood in front of the cellar door with his hand on the knob, listening to his own heart like it was a caller on hold.

The door didn't creak. That was the worst part, at first. A door sealed for sixty years should have screamed on its hinges, should have needed a shoulder, should have put up some fight. This one swung open with the smooth, oiled courtesy of a door that had been opened recently and often, and the darkness beyond it received the flashlight beam the way deep water receives a stone — without comment, without bottom.

The stairs went down farther than the stairs should go.

He knew the geometry of this house. He'd measured it as a boy, played at being an architect with a tape measure and graph paper, and the cellar under Mirabel's kitchen was eight steps deep, twelve feet by fourteen, dirt-floored, shelved with preserves. He had stood at the top of those eight steps a hundred times while Mirabel held the door and told him children didn't go down there, and he had counted the steps with his eyes the way you count the teeth of a dog behind a fence.

The beam of the flashlight found seventeen steps. Then twenty. Then it stopped finding numbers, because the numbers stopped meaning anything. The stairs descended in a long, patient spiral, and the walls changed from fieldstone to poured concrete to something older — hand-hewn blocks, black with moisture, sweating cold. The air grew heavier. His ears popped twice, the way they did on the drive up into the mountains, and he understood that he was descending farther underground than the house had foundations, farther than the well, farther than anything men had any business building under a kitchen in Harlan County.

He thought about turning back. He stood on a landing that had no business existing and thought about it very seriously, and while he thought, from somewhere below, came the sound.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

Slow. Even. Spacing between the blows as regular as a metronome, as a pulse, as someone keeping time with something. Not asking to be let in. Not asking anything. Just counting. He had heard it in his grandmother's stories without knowing it — the knocking she'd described in her sleep when he was a boy, the knocking she'd sealed the door against in 1961 after her brother went down and didn't come up.

It was still keeping time. Sixty years, and it had never once lost the beat.

Orsen went down.

* * *

The cellar was larger than the house.

He knew it before he reached the bottom, knew it in his knees and his inner ear, the way you know a room's size in the dark — and when his boots finally met flat floor and he raised the light, the beam traveled and traveled and found nothing to stop it. The space opened out around him like the inside of a held breath. The ceiling vanished into black above the reach of the flashlight. The floor was smooth concrete, or something like it, unbroken in every direction, and the cold down here was not the cold of underground. It was the cold of a courtroom. Of a church in January. Of somewhere built for people to sit still in.

"Hello," Orsen said, and the space took his voice and gave it back to him from all directions at once, so that it sounded like a congregation answering.

The flashlight found the walls.

The first flyer was at eye level, pinned with a rusted straight pin: a grainy black-and-white photograph of a young man with Orsen's own heavy brow, the jaw he saw in the mirror every morning. *MISSING — WALTER BLACKWOOD, 22, LAST SEEN MARCH 1961.* Below the photo, the family had offered a reward that would have been a fortune in 1961. The paper was yellowed but not faded, not damp, not rotting — preserved the way things are preserved when something wants them legible.

He walked. The flyers continued, edge to edge, floor to the invisible ceiling, overlapping like shingles, decades deep. He lifted a corner of Walter Blackwood's flyer and found another beneath it: *MISSING — HARLAN BLACKWOOD, 34, 1938.* Beneath that, *MISSING — JOSEPHINE BLACKWOOD, 9, 1954,* a gap-toothed girl squinting into a summer sun, and Orsen made a sound he didn't recognize coming out of his own throat, because Josephine was on the family tree, a small crossed-off branch, a name that came up at reunions only as *the one before the war we don't discuss.*

He walked and the flashlight walked with him and the flyers went on. Some were typed. Some were handwritten by hands he knew — Mirabel's young handwriting on one from 1979, offering information, offering anything, *please, please, please.* Some were printed on dot-matrix paper with the sprocket holes still attached. Some were printed last month. He found one with his own station's logo in the corner, a flyer he had never made, with his own face on it, and no date at all, and a blank where the date should be, and he stood in front of that one for a long time, breathing.

Every flyer. Every single one, across ninety years of paper. Blackwood. Blackwood. Blackwood.

Except the first one. The one at the very center of the far wall, oldest of all, pinned alone with space cleared around it the way you'd clear space around a portrait of a saint or a president. The paper was brown as a paper bag, soft as cloth. The photograph was a studio portrait, the kind that cost a week's wages: a young Black woman, maybe nineteen, maybe twenty, hair pinned, eyes level and unafraid and looking directly out of the paper at him with an expression he could only call *expectant.*

*ETTA VANE,* the flyer said. *LAST SEEN AT BLACKWOOD FARM, OCTOBER 1932. ANYONE WITH INFORMATION —*

The rest had been torn away. Not faded. Torn, deliberately, long ago, by a hand that had wanted that part gone.

"Okay," Orsen said to the room, to the walls, to the patient dark above him. "Okay. I see it. I see what you kept."

The knocking had stopped.

He noticed its absence the way you notice a refrigerator going quiet at night — the silence had a shape now, and the shape was listening.

* * *

The chair stood at the center of the floor.

He had seen it in a hundred photographs by now — Ruth's reunion pictures, the birthday snapshots, Aunt Carol's last morning, the one on the fridge with the chair one place closer — and it was exactly the chair from the images, and exactly not. In the photographs it had been a plain wooden chair, kitchen-grade, unremarkable. In the room it was *finished* in a way no photograph had conveyed. The wood was dark, dense, older than the house above, older than the county maybe, and it had been worn smooth in the places hands and bodies wear things — the arms polished pale, the seat hollowed into a shallow gloss. It faced the wall of flyers. It faced Etta Vane's torn flyer the way a witness faces the stand.

And in front of it, in the floor — the depression.

Orsen crouched over it with the flashlight and his stomach turned over slowly, completely, like something rolling in its sleep. It was a shallow trough worn into the concrete — no, not worn. *Pressed.* A negative space in the shape of a human body, arms at the sides, legs straight, head slightly tilted as if resting on a turned cheek. It was body-length, and it was shallow — an inch deep, maybe less — and its edges were soft and smooth, and Orsen understood with a clarity that felt like falling that it had not been carved.

It had been made the way a riverbed is made. By something lying there. For a very long time.

He put his palm into the depression. The floor was cold, but the shape itself was colder — a defined cold, a body-shaped cold, the exact temperature of the cellar air that had been pouring under Mirabel's door, and when he lifted his hand his palm came away damp, and the damp smelled of that same rotted-sweet floral.

The chair was a seat being reserved. He had understood that much for weeks. But the depression told him the rest, told him the thing the photographs had been circling: the reservation had been *occupied* before. Something had lain down in this floor, in this dark, under this house, year after year after year, while the family above it ate birthday cake and took pictures and counted their living. And whatever had lain there had gotten up. Recently. The chair was out. The chair was at the table. The count was running.

"You were down here," he said. His voice cracked on the second word. "Nineteen thirty-two. You've been down here the whole —"

The flyers moved.

All of them, at once, a soft papery shiver running around the entire circumference of the room like wind through wheat — though there was no wind, the air dead still — and in the shiver Orsen heard, very faintly, from everywhere and nowhere, a sound he had been hearing on his phone lines for two weeks. The sound of a woman speaking calmly from inside a closed casket. Not words. Just the *tone* of words. Patient. Reasonable. Almost kind.

The flashlight flickered.

He turned it toward the chair and the light steadied, and he saw that the seat was no longer empty.

Not occupied — he would have screamed, he thought, if it had been occupied. It was *warmed.* The hollow of the seat, that shallow gloss worn by decades of waiting, held a faint bloom of condensation, the way a chair holds the warmth of the person who just rose from it. As if someone had been sitting there, watching him read the walls, watching him touch the floor, and had stepped away only a moment before — into the dark past the reach of his light — and was standing there now, at the edge of the beam, waiting for him to be ready.

The knocking started again.

Knock. Knock. Knock. But it wasn't coming from the walls anymore. It was coming from behind him, from the direction of the stairs, and it was coming *up* the stairs, unhurried, one step at a time, and Orsen understood that it had never been a knock at all. It was footsteps. It had always been footsteps. Something in this cellar had been walking up toward the sealed door for sixty years, and it had never once been in a hurry, because it had never once needed to be.

The flyers shivered again. Etta Vane's torn flyer lifted on its pin, hung a moment as if considering, and lay back flat.

Orsen ran.

He took the spiral stairs three at a time with the flashlight bouncing wild, the footsteps below him keeping their even, patient time — not chasing, never chasing, just *following*, the way a verdict follows a trial — and the stairs went on longer going up than they had going down, and twice he passed a landing he was sure he'd already passed, and the cold air at his back smelled of rot and roses, and somewhere above him, impossibly far, a thin line of kitchen light showed under a door.

He came through the cellar door and slammed it and threw the padlock's hasp shut with shaking hands, and the lock closed on nothing, on air, on a door that led to a stairwell that could not fit inside his grandmother's house, and he stood there gasping in the hallway with his palms pressed flat against the wood.

From the other side, gentle as a hand laid on his shoulder, came one last knock.

Then Mirabel's voice, behind him in the kitchen doorway, small and old and broken:

"Oh, Orsen." She was holding the photograph from the fridge. She turned it so he could see it. "It updated."

In the picture, the chair no longer sat at the head of the table.

It stood at the foot of the cellar stairs. And it was turned to face the camera — facing *him* — and on its seat, faint as breath on glass, sat the outline of a figure, rising.
