## 5. Camille at the Threshold

There are three rules, and I learned them before I learned to ride a bike.

Rule one: salt at the sills. Every exterior door, every window that opens, refreshed on the first of the month and after any storm heavy enough to make the gutters sing. Mama taught me with a coffee can and a slotted spoon when I was six, and I thought it was a game, and then I was seven and I thought it was a chore, and then I was nine and I saw what happened the one week we ran out, and I have never thought of it as anything but survival since.

Rule two: no mirrors facing each other. The hall mirror and the bathroom mirror used to make a corridor between them, and things would walk down that corridor at three in the morning, wearing your face but getting the expression wrong, like someone describing a smile over the phone. Papa covered one with a sheet the year I was born and it stayed covered until he didn't come back, and then I took the sheet off, because I figured the House owed me a look at whatever it was hiding. Bad instinct. Theo covered it again the same night without saying a word, and that's the closest we've ever come to a conversation about it.

Rule three: never answer a voice from upstairs. The House has two floors and a half-attic and no reason for anyone to be up there after dark, and when it calls down—*Camille, come help me find my glasses, Camille, the stove's on, Camille, I'm cold*—in a voice that is almost Mama's and almost Papa's and almost, worst of all, mine, you keep your mouth shut and your feet on the ground floor. Answering isn't an invitation. Answering is a signature.

I've kept the rules for thirty-one years. I keep them the way other people keep kosher or Lent or their side of the road: not because I believe anything, exactly, but because the alternative is data I don't want to collect.

And then Wren Vasquez moved into the blue room, and started asking questions.

* * *

I noticed it the way you notice a draft—not the thing itself, but the small wrongnesses it causes. The salt line outside his door disturbed on a Tuesday and not the first of the month. Him standing in the downstairs hall with his head tipped back, listening to the ceiling. Him asking Theo, in that soft nurse's voice of his, whether the radiator in the east bedroom had always knocked in threes, and Theo going still as a held breath and saying, *it's an old house,* which is the family catechism, which is what we say instead of the truth the way other families say *fine* instead of *divorced.*

An old house. Yes. And the ocean is a large puddle.

Here's what you have to understand about me: I am not brave. I want that on the record, in ink, notarized. Theo is brave in his mute, stubborn way—he feeds the House its little obediences, tightens the hinges it sulks at, paints the porch rail it keeps peeling no matter what we do, and the House purrs and lets us live. Mama is brave the way a gambler is brave, all in on one terrible hand. Me, I'm the one who keeps the rules and keeps her head down and keeps a notebook in the floorboards, and the notebook is not brave, it's the opposite of brave, it's a coward's archive, thirty-one years of *the hallway was longer today* and *the voice used Papa's cough* written in pencil because pencil can be erased and pen is a commitment and I have never in my life committed to anything except survival.

But I was lonely. That's the confession underneath all of it. Thirty-one years of rules makes you lonely, because you can't have people over—guests ask questions, and guests who ask questions in this House tend to stop asking anything ever—and you can't leave, not really, not for long, because the rules need keeping and because—and this is the part I've never written down, not even in pencil—because I'm not sure the House would let me. I went to Tucson for a week when I was twenty-three. I came back to find every door in the house open, every salt line scattered, my bedroom rearranged so the bed faced the corner, and the hall mirror uncovered, reflecting the covered one across the hall, corridor open for business, something standing at the far end of it wearing my face and getting the expression wrong. I re-salted the sills with shaking hands and I have not spent a night away since.

So when a stranger with kind eyes and a nurse's calm moved into the blue room at a rent Mama had no business charging that low, I did what I always do with hope: I checked it for wiring.

* * *

I caught him properly on a Thursday, in the upstairs hall, which was itself a violation worth noting. Nobody goes upstairs after dark. It was after dark. He was standing outside the door to the room that has been locked since before I was born—the one Mama calls *storage* the way the ocean is a large puddle—and he had his ear against the wood, and from behind the door came a sound I have heard maybe four times in my life and never gotten used to: a slow, dry counting.

*Fifty-one. Fifty. Forty-nine.*

Not from upstairs. From *inside the door.* From inside the walls around the door. The House counts sometimes, in the weeks before it doesn't, and we have all learned to pretend it's the pipes.

Wren's lips were moving. He was counting along. He knew the numbers before they came, the way you know the words to a song you hate, and when I saw that, my stomach did something complicated and cold, because either this man was already half-claimed—and I'd be harboring it, feeding it, keeping it salted and warm like an egg—or he'd heard the counting somewhere else, somewhere before, and had come here the way a man who's been struck by lightning hangs around weather.

"Hey." I kept my voice level. Nurse-adjacent. I've had practice sounding unbothered; I grew up in a house that punishes the bothered. "House rules. Ground floor after sundown."

He turned, and I'll give him this—he didn't do the guilty startle. He just looked at me with those tired, careful eyes and said, "Sorry. I heard—" and then stopped, because he was deciding, right there in front of me, how much truth he could afford. I recognized the arithmetic. I do it forty times a day.

"Heard what?" I said. And then, because loneliness makes you reckless: "Actually, don't answer that here. Walls."

He glanced at the door. The counting had gone quiet, the way a held breath goes quiet, and I had the distinct, sick feeling that the House was listening to us the way we were listening to it. Two listeners, one door.

"Kitchen," I said. "Kitchens are neutral. Probably. Come on."

* * *

I made tea because that's what my hands do when my brain is busy, and I made it strong, and I put the good salt cellar on the table between us—not using it, just letting it sit there, which is a message in our family's private language, and I watched him clock it. He looked at the salt cellar the way you look at a word in a foreign language you almost know. Not blank. *Almost.*

"You've heard it before," I said. "The counting. Before this house."

He wrapped both hands around his mug like it was something that might be taken from him. "Three years," he said. "Hospice ward, night shift. Patients counting backwards from a hundred. Dying early, at odd numbers. I told myself it was—" he did a small, exhausted laugh— "tinnitus. Grief. Exhaustion. Pick one. I picked all three, on rotation."

"And now?"

"Now I have a recorder," he said, "and a second voice on it that knows my mother's maiden name," and I swear the kitchen got four degrees colder, and the salt cellar sat there between us like a small stone god, and I thought: *entry. He's an entry. Mama, what did you do.*

I didn't know yet. Let me be clear about that, because for a long time afterward I had to reconstruct exactly what I knew and when, and the honest answer is: that night I knew only that Wren was marked and Mama was hiding something and the two facts were sitting at my kitchen table drinking tea and refusing to look at each other.

So I did the thing I'd been doing my whole life, which is manage the situation with the tools available. I told him the rules.

"Salt at the sills," I said. "Yours gets refreshed on the first. Don't sweep it up, don't step over it if you can step around it, and if you ever find it disturbed, come get me before you come get anyone else. No mirrors facing each other—the one in the upstairs hall stays covered; don't uncover it, don't peek, don't be curious. Curiosity is how this House feeds." I took a breath. "And never, ever answer a voice from upstairs. No matter whose it sounds like. *Especially* if it sounds like someone you love. Especially if it sounds like you."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "Has it ever used your voice?"

"Once." The word came out smaller than I wanted. "When I was nineteen. It called down in my voice and it was crying, and it said, *Camille, I'm stuck, Camille, come up, Camille, it's me, I'm stuck up here,* and I stood at the bottom of the stairs with my hand on the banister for—I don't know. An hour? The sun came up." I turned my mug a quarter turn on the table. "That's the year I started the notebook."

"What notebook?"

And there it was, the threshold, the one the chapter of my life is named after, though I didn't know that at the time, obviously, I'm not a novelist, I'm a woman with a pencil. Because here's the test I was running, and I ran it without fully admitting I was running it: every person who learns about the notebook becomes either a fellow prisoner or a threat. Fellow prisoners help you carry the pencil. Threats tell Mama, or worse, tell the House—because the House hears things, especially things said with love or fear, and a notebook full of its moods and movements is exactly the kind of truth it cannot abide.

"Floorboards," I said. "My room. Thirty-one years of observations. Dates, times, which doors stick before it's hungry, what the counting sounds like at different distances." I looked at him straight. "You're either the first person I've ever told, or you're the biggest mistake I've ever made, and I genuinely don't know which, and I'd like to point out that I noticed you counting along with the wall, which means you already know more than a tenant should."

Wren set down his mug. He had the face of a man setting down something heavier than a mug.

"I've watched people die counting for three years," he said, "and told myself it was nothing, because nothing was survivable and anything else wasn't. And then I moved in here and the House—" he stopped. Started again. "Your mother charged me two hundred a month for a furnished room in a house like this. I've been a hospice nurse long enough to know what a discount means. Somebody wanted me here, Camille. And I've been asking myself all week whether I'm the tenant or the inventory."

The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere above us—upstairs, where nobody was—a floorboard settled with a sound exactly like a sigh, and we both looked at the ceiling, and then at each other, and that was it, that was the moment, two lonely people recognizing the same cage from opposite sides of it.

"Fellow prisoner," I said quietly. "I'm choosing to believe that. If I'm wrong, I've got salt and a pencil and thirty-one years of practice, and you've got—" I gestured at him, the whole tired, careful, haunted length of him— "whatever a man like you has."

"Questions," he said. "I've got questions."

"Questions are dangerous here."

"I know." He almost smiled. It was not a comforting almost. "I've been dying in rooms with people for three years, Camille. I'm not afraid of dangerous. I'm afraid of *quiet.*"

And God help me, I understood that completely. Quiet is the House's favorite food. Quiet is what it raised us in, quiet is what Papa's disappearance was wrapped in, quiet is the sound of Mama's locked door and Theo's whisper and thirty-one years of a girl writing in pencil. I had spent my whole life mistaking quiet for safety, and here was this man telling me quiet was the thing he'd survived and refused.

"Okay," I said. "Fellow prisoner. Then here's your first lesson: the House moves. Hallways lengthen, doors open onto places that aren't behind them, and it does it when it's afraid of us. Remember that word. *Afraid.* It's the only advantage we've ever had, and nobody in this family has ever been brave enough to use it."

"Are you?" he asked. "Brave enough?"

I looked at the salt cellar. I looked at the ceiling, at the locked room above us where the counting lived. I thought about the notebook under the floorboards, thirty-one years of pencil, and how a pencil is a thing that waits.

"No," I said honestly. "But I'm lonely enough, and I'm starting to suspect those are the same thing."

He laughed—a real one, short and surprised—and the kitchen warmed by exactly the four degrees it had lost, and for one whole minute it was just two people drinking tea too strong in a kitchen at night, and I let myself have the minute.

Then Wren went quiet in a different way. The careful way. The way people go quiet when they've decided something and are measuring whether to say it.

"There's a recorder in my room," he said. "It caught the voice. It caught *answers,* Camille. To questions I only thought." He turned the mug a slow quarter turn. "Tomorrow I'm going to play it for someone, because I can't be the only one who hears it, I *can't*—and I wanted you to know that before Theo finds it."

I felt the cold come back into the room like a tenant returning.

"Why Theo?" I said. "Why would Theo find it?"

Wren looked at me with those tired, careful eyes, and said, "Because he already did."
