## 8. The Dying Speak Backwards

The new patient arrived on a Tuesday, which is the day hospice admissions always seem to happen, as if the universe keeps office hours for catastrophe. Her name was Dolores Finch. Seventy-eight years old, pancreatic cancer, no family listed on the intake form except a niece in Tucson who had signed the paperwork by fax and never once called after.

I read her chart at the nurses' station and felt the particular hollow relief I'd learned to feel over three years of this work: a stranger. No connection to me, to Delmore Street, to anything. Just a dying woman who needed morphine titrated and sheets changed and someone to sit with her while her body conducted its slow, private negotiation with the inevitable.

"You're Wren," she said when I introduced myself that first evening. She was propped up against pillows that had been arranged and rearranged so many times they'd achieved a kind of architecture. Her eyes were yellow at the edges, sharp at the center. "Odd name."

"My mother liked birds."

"She liked being different, more like." She smiled with half her mouth. "I can always tell. People who name their children strange things aren't naming the child. They're naming themselves."

I laughed, because it was true, and because laughing at deathbed observations is one of the small mercies of my job—you get to be honest with people who have run out of time to be polite.

She was lucid that night, and the next. We talked about Tucson, which she hated, and the coast, which she missed, and a husband named Gerald who had been dead eleven years and whose photograph she kept facedown in her nightstand drawer because looking at him made her "greedy for something that's finished." I liked her enormously. That was the mistake. I've started to believe it's always the mistake—letting them become people instead of patients, letting them matter. The count has never once reached for someone who didn't matter to somebody.

* * *

On her fourth night, I came on shift at eleven and heard her voice through the door before I opened it.

"—ninety-six, ninety-five, ninety-four—"

I stood in the hallway with my hand flat against the door and did not go in. This is a thing I do now. I collect ten seconds of standing still before these moments, like a man checking whether the floor will hold his weight.

"Ninety-three," said Dolores Finch, in a voice that was not hers.

I want to be precise about what I mean by *not hers*, because precision is the last tool I have left. It wasn't a disguise or an impression. It was the way a radio station sounds when you're driving out of its range—the same voice, technically, but with something else underneath it, some other signal bleeding through, dry as paper dragged across wood. I had heard it before. In Mr. Ellery's room two years ago. In Mrs. Okafor's room last month. And most recently, most unforgivably, on a recorder sitting in a drawer in my bedroom on Delmore Street, answering questions in my own murmured voice with facts about my life that no living person should know.

"Ninety-two. Ninety-one." A pause. "Ninety."

Then, in the paper-dry voice, addressed to no one visible: "*Where does the bird sleep?*"

My skin went cold from the scalp down. Because I knew that cadence. I had transcribed it at three in the morning with shaking hands, played it back eleven times, written the questions in a notebook I now kept locked in my car: *Where does he keep the salt? Who signs the ledger? What does the house eat?*

It asked its questions through the dying. That had been my working theory, ugly as it was—the backwards count was a summons, and the summons carried traffic both ways. But every patient before had answered only what was asked of *them*. Their own names. Their own debts. Mr. Ellery had recited his father's middle name like a man reading off a form.

Dolores Finch had never been to Delmore Street. She had never met me before Tuesday. There was nothing in her to ask about.

"*Where does the bird sleep,*" the voice repeated, patient as water finding a crack.

And then Dolores, in her own voice again, thin and confused, answered: "He sleeps on Delmore. Second floor. The room with the cold corner."

I opened the door.

She was alone in the bed, of course. The lamp was on. Her eyes were open and fixed on the ceiling above her headboard, tracking something across it the way you track a fly, except there was nothing there, and her expression wasn't fear. It was concentration. The look of someone doing arithmetic.

"Dolores." I crossed to the bed and took her wrist. Pulse fast but steady, skin hot. Not delirium from fever, not yet. "Hey. It's Wren. You're at Rosewood Hospice. You're safe."

Her eyes came down from the ceiling and found me, and for a moment she was entirely herself, and she looked almost embarrassed. "Was I talking?"

"A little."

"About you?" Something flickered behind her eyes—not confusion. Recognition. "You're the one it asks about."

The words landed in my chest like a dropped stone into a well. I heard it hit bottom a long time later.

"What asks about me, Dolores?"

She turned her hand over and gripped mine. Her fingers were cool now despite the heat of her skin. "It comes when I'm between," she said. "When the pain medicine floats me up near the ceiling and I'm not quite in my body. There's a counting. It goes backward, and while it counts, it asks. And lately—" she frowned, searching, "—lately all the questions are the same question. Different shapes, but the same question underneath. Like it's trying doors."

"What question?"

"*Is the bird ripe.*"

I did not move. I am a hospice nurse; I have held hands through every kind of exit the body offers; I did not move. But somewhere inside me, three years of practiced numbness tore along a seam.

"That's nonsense," I said, and my voice came out level, which I counted as the professional achievement of my life.

"Course it is." She patted my hand, brisk, almost maternal. "Everything it says is nonsense. That's how you know it isn't from anywhere. Real things come from places." She closed her eyes. "Ninety-eight," she murmured. "Ninety-seven."

"It restarted," I said stupidly.

"It always restarts when it loses interest in me." She opened one eye. "It hasn't lost interest tonight. It's just waiting for you to leave so it can finish asking."

"I'm not leaving."

"No," she agreed softly. "I don't suppose you are. You've got that look. My husband had it. The look of a man who's decided to stand in the doorway of something." She settled deeper into the pillows. "Pull up the chair then, bird. Let's see what it does."

* * *

What it did, over the next six nights, was get closer.

I documented everything. That's the discipline that holds me together—charting, timestamps, clinical language. *Patient alert and oriented x2. Reports auditory phenomena during periods of drowsiness. Denies distress.* I filled a notebook alongside the official chart, the real record, the one in my handwriting that I would take home and lock in my car and compare against the recorder in my drawer.

Night five: she counted from one hundred and stopped at sixty-one, waking suddenly, asking for ginger ale. Night six: she got to thirty-three and the paper voice asked, through her mouth, in her voice, "*Does the house know his mother's name?*" and I said, out loud, "Don't answer that," and Dolores's mouth curved into something that was not her smile, and the temperature in the room dropped four degrees—I checked the wall thermometer afterward—and the count resumed at thirty-two and ran smoothly down to zero, and she woke up refreshed and starving and ate an entire container of applesauce.

That was the pattern I finally understood, lying awake in my cold-cornered room on Delmore Street at four in the morning: it wasn't counting down to her death. It was counting down to *answers*. Every zero was a door it tried. When she refused—or when I refused for her—it lost interest, reset, waited. The harvest moon was coming. Eleven days away, then ten, then nine. And the thing in the count was using a stranger's dying as a telephone because someone in the House had entered my name in a ledger, and it wanted to confirm the entry before collection.

It was verifying me. The way you verify a delivery address.

I told myself I could quit. Transfer her to another facility, request a different assignment, put forty miles between us. I drafted the request in my head twice. Both times I imagined her counting alone at 3 a.m. with no one in the doorway, no one saying *don't answer that*, and I deleted the draft. Whatever this was, it had chosen her room as its instrument, and abandoning the instrument didn't unmake the music. It just meant no one was listening who cared what got said.

Camille noticed the change in me immediately, because Camille notices everything. She caught me in the kitchen on the seventh morning, standing over coffee I hadn't drunk, and she didn't ask what was wrong. She asked, quietly, "Has it started reaching outside the House?"

I told her about Dolores. All of it. She listened with her arms folded tight, and when I finished she stared at the window for a long moment and said, "Grandmother used to say the count travels. That near the moon, it doesn't wait for you to bring things home. It goes shopping." Then she'd looked at me with something terrible in her face—not pity, worse, kinship—and said, "Eleven days, Wren. Whatever it's asking her, don't let it finish the list."

* * *

Eve-minus-eleven fell on a Thursday. I had requested the night shift specifically, though requesting it felt like volunteering for something I couldn't name. Dolores was weaker now, sleeping eighteen hours a day, waking into a soft amber confusion that the doctors called terminal lucidity's opposite number. The end was close in the ordinary medical way, the way deaths are usually close—organ by organ, honestly, without theater.

At 11:40 p.m., she began to count.

"One hundred," she said clearly, from a dead sleep, and I set down the chart I'd been pretending to read and pulled my chair to the bedside and took her hand.

"Ninety-nine. Ninety-eight."

Her eyes stayed closed. Her breathing was labored but regular, the rattle not yet in it. I squeezed her fingers and spoke aloud, calmly, the way you talk to someone surfacing from a nightmare: "Dolores. It's Wren. You're safe. Nobody here is going to answer any questions."

"Ninety-five. Ninety-four."

The paper voice slid under hers like a blade under a rib. "*What does the bird fear?*"

"Don't answer," I said.

"*What does the bird fear?*"

"Nothing," I lied. "It fears nothing."

The room went cold so fast the window fogged from the inside. On the sill, the glass ticked as it contracted. Dolores's hand spasmed in mine.

"*Lie,*" said the voice, in her mouth, tasting the word. And then, horribly, gently, in a tone I recognized from my own recorder playback—the tone it used when it was pleased—"*We know what the bird buried. We know what he let die alone. Shall we say her name?*"

"Stop." I was standing now. I don't remember standing. "Stop. She doesn't know anything. She's never been to the House. Take whatever you think you're going to take and leave her out of it."

"*Ninety,*" said Dolores, serene, eyes still shut. "*Eighty-nine. Eighty-eight.*"

It wasn't listening. It had never been listening. I understood then, standing in the fogged cold of that little room, that I had spent seven nights believing I was guarding a door, when actually I was standing inside the conversation, a participant, a specimen. Every refusal was data. Every lie was a flavor. The count didn't need her permission any more than a scale needs the flour's consent.

So I did the only thing left. I sat back down, and I held her hand, and I talked to *her*—not the thing wearing her voice. I told her about Tucson. I told her Gerald's photograph sounded like it belonged facedown, that greedy-for-the-finished was the truest sentence anyone had ever said to me. I told her she mattered, that the niece in Tucson didn't know what she'd failed to call, that I would call her myself and tell her Dolores Finch was funny and sharp and right about names.

"Seventy," said the paper voice, bored now, circling.

"Sixty-nine," said Dolores, and her breathing changed.

I knew the rhythm. Every nurse knows it. The long apneic pauses arriving on schedule, the pulse going threadlike under my fingers. She was dying in the ordinary way, the honest way, and the count was simply riding it down like a man descending stairs he hadn't built.

"Fifty. Forty-nine."

"Stay with her," I whispered—to whom, I don't know. To the part of her that was still hers. "Stay with me, Dolores. You pick your own number. You hear me? Pick your own."

"Forty. Thirty-nine."

Her lips moved. I leaned close. Her eyes opened halfway, found me, focused with that sudden terrible clarity the dying get, the last clean light through a dirty window.

"Thirty," she said, in her own voice, and then, faintly, amused even now: "Cheeky thing. Thinks it gets to choose."

"Twenty-nine. Twenty-eight."

"Wren," she said.

Not a question. Not a summons. Just my name, spoken by a woman who had known me nine days, spoken like a hand placed on a shoulder. And I understood, in the space of one skipped breath, that it wasn't the count saying it. It was her. Warning me, or claiming me, or simply making sure that somewhere in the wreckage of this arithmetic, one person had died knowing exactly who was holding their hand.

"Twenty," said the paper voice, urgent now, hurrying.

"Thirteen," said Dolores, and smiled at me with her whole mouth, the half-mouth gone, all of her present. "Twelve. Eleven."

"Ten," said the voice, and it was no longer in her mouth. It was in the room. In the radiator. In the fogged glass. In the space behind my ear where tinnitus lives, except it had never been tinnitus, it had never been tinnitus, three years of denial collapsing into a single syllable—

"Nine. Eight. Seven."

"Dolores," I said. "Dolores, stay with me."

"Six," she whispered. "Five. Four."

Her pulse stuttered under my fingers like a bird deciding.

"Three," we said together, her voice and the other braided into one. "Two."

And then, at the threshold, at the last door, with her heart slowing toward its final interval, the count stopped. Hung there. One breath. Two. Three. The room so cold my exhalations showed.

It wanted the zero. It needed the zero. And it couldn't find it, because she wouldn't give it—because somewhere in the between-place where the pain medicine floated her, Dolores Finch was keeping her own count, on her own terms, and she had decided the number where she stopped was hers to choose.

"Zero," she said softly, distinctly, in her own voice alone, and died.

The monitors did their ordinary work. The alarm did its ordinary crying. I did my ordinary job—called it, noted the time, 12:47 a.m., eve-minus-eleven, performed the small dignities. But my hands shook through all of it, because I had gotten what I wanted. She'd beaten it to the door. She'd died at zero on her own schedule, her own word, her own choice.

And her last word had been my name.

* * *

I sat with her until the dawn shift came, because you do that, because someone should. Then I drove home through streets going gray with early light, and the House on Delmore Street stood waiting at the end of its block with its porch light burning, patient as a held breath, and I sat in my car across the street for a long time, engine ticking, watching it watch me.

It had reached through a stranger's death to touch me. It had asked its questions through a woman who owed it nothing, and when she refused to pay, it had taken her anyway—taken her death, ridden it down, tried to spend it. That's what I kept circling as I climbed the cold front steps and let myself into the hall that smelled of dust and old roses: it wasn't enough for the House to wait for its contracted entry. Near the moon, it shopped. It verified. It pressed its face against every window with a dying person behind it, looking for the bird.

In my room, I took the recorder from the drawer and set it on the nightstand and pressed play, because I needed to hear the ordinary static, the sound of a machine recording nothing.

The tape hissed for four seconds.

Then the paper voice, dry and close, said:

"*Entry nine confirmed. Eleven days.*"

And somewhere below me, deep in the bones of the House, something began—very softly, very patiently—to count.
