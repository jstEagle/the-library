## 13. Theo Breaks

The House started calling me by my father's name on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult to Tuesdays specifically. They had always been my quietest day. Laundry day. The day I re-caulked the bathroom sill and oiled the hinges on the pantry door and did all the small maintenance that kept the House fed without ever making it hungry.

"Theo," the radiator said at six in the morning, in a voice like a draft moving through a keyhole. "Theo."

That was fine. That was mine. I could live with being Theo; I'd been doing it for forty-one years with varying degrees of success.

But then, an hour later, from somewhere inside the walls above the kitchen: "Emil."

I dropped the wrench I was using on the sink trap. It rang against the porcelain, and the House drank the sound the way it drank everything—greedily, completely, leaving no echo behind.

"Emil," it said again, patient as arithmetic. "Emil Aldous. Breakfast is getting cold."

My father had been dead nineteen years. Taken, if you wanted to be precise about it, which the House always was. And here was the thing about the House: it did not make mistakes. It was many things—a liar, a landlord, a mouth—but it was never wrong. If it called me Emil, it wasn't confused.

It was impatient.

* * *

You need to understand something about me before you judge what I did next, which is that I have spent nineteen years perfecting the art of not looking directly at things. It's a skill. It's a discipline. Camille looks at everything—she catalogs the House's moods in a notebook like a naturalist studying a predator—and Marisol looks away entirely, into her prayers, into her ledgers, into whatever bargain she thinks she's still negotiating. But I look *near*. I look at the edges of things. I have made a life in the peripheral vision of my own home.

So when the House called me Emil, I did what I always do. I went to the attic.

Not to confront anything. God, no. To tidy. There were boxes up there that needed sorting, and sorting is prayer for people like me, and if I kept my hands busy maybe the House would lose interest, the way it lost interest in anything that didn't answer it.

The attic was cold in the way the unused rooms were cold—not empty-cold but *attended*-cold, the temperature of a room where something is holding its breath. I moved boxes. I found Christmas ornaments, my mother's old sewing machine, a trunk of Camille's college papers she'd never come back for.

And then I found the coat.

It hung on the nail by the attic window, where my father used to keep it, and I want to be very clear: I had looked at that nail every time I came up here for nineteen years, and there had never been a coat on it. There had been a nail, and a rectangle of unfaded wallpaper behind it, and nothing else. Nineteen years of nothing.

The coat was charcoal wool, double-breasted, with a small tear at the left pocket where he'd caught it on the fence gate the week before he went. I knew the tear. I had watched him curse at it over his coffee, turning the fabric in his thick fingers, saying he'd fix it Saturday, and he hadn't fixed it Saturday because there was no Saturday after that one.

I touched it the way you touch a stove you already know is hot—with the backs of two fingers, fast, a test disguised as an accident.

It was warm.

Not sun-warm. Not radiator-warm. Warm the way a coat is warm ten seconds after its owner takes it off. Warm the way a coat is warm when someone has just stepped out of it and might step back in.

"Emil," said the House, from everywhere, gently. "Your coffee's going cold."

I sat down on a box of Camille's college papers and put my head between my knees and breathed until the attic stopped tilting.

Here is what I understood, sitting there in the cold with my dead father's warm coat hanging three feet away: the House was done waiting. Marisol had written Wren's name in the ledger believing a nurse's death would settle the debt, and Wren had started digging, and the House had tasted all that truth coming loose like a tooth, and now it wanted payment *now*, and if the contracted name wouldn't come quietly, it would take the name it had been keeping warm for nineteen years.

Me. It would take me. It had been fattening me on obediences for two decades—every hinge oiled, every sill salted, every whisper I'd given it instead of the world—and now it was ready to collect, and it was calling me by the name of the last man it had taken because in the House's ledger, apparently, sons are just entries waiting to mature.

I stood up. My legs worked, which surprised me.

I took the coat off the nail.

I don't know why. Yes I do. Because if I left it there, it would stay warm forever, and some part of me—the part that had been feeding this thing small obediences since I was twenty-two—would keep coming back to check on it. Taking the coat was the first disobedience of my adult life, and it was so small, so pathetic, a piece of dry cleaning stolen from a ghost, that I almost laughed.

I carried it downstairs instead of putting it back.

* * *

Wren was in the kitchen when I came down, standing at the counter with his recorder in his hands, turning it over like a man deciding whether to keep smoking. He'd been different lately. Sharper. He asked questions the way Camille did now, and the House had noticed, and the temperature in the east rooms had dropped four degrees in a week, and I had been watching all of it happen from inside my silence like a man watching a flood rise through floorboards.

He looked at the coat in my arms and then at my face, and whatever he saw there made him set the recorder down slowly.

"Theo," he said. "Are you okay?"

And here is the thing about nineteen years of whispering: it turns out the voice doesn't atrophy. It waits. It sits in your throat like a bird in winter, feathers puffed, conserving heat, and when the door finally opens it comes out whole.

"No," I said, in a full voice, in daylight, to another person, for the first time since I was nineteen years old. "No, I'm not okay, and I'm done pretending I am, and if you make tea while I talk I'm going to need the whole pot."

He made the tea. He didn't ask questions. He sat across from me with his big careful nurse's hands wrapped around his mug, and I told him everything.

I told him about the year I turned nineteen, how the House had gotten restless the way it does near the harvest moon, how my father had sat us down at this very table and explained the family business the way other fathers explain insurance. How there was a debt on the House older than any of us, and how it came due every nineteen years, and how the House preferred to be paid in names that were freely given, because consent made the payment *keep*, the way salt keeps meat.

I told him how my father had gone upstairs on the night of the moon to negotiate, to offer himself, to buy us another nineteen years, and how we'd heard him talking in the hall in a voice that wasn't quite his, asking questions, and answering them, and how at some point the answers had stopped sounding like him.

I told him how in the morning his coat was gone from the nail and his coffee was still warm on the table and there was no body, no note, no anything, and how the police had found the paperwork impossibly clean, a man who had simply decided to stop existing, and how everyone outside this family had accepted it within a month because the House is very good at tidying.

"And I stopped talking," I said. "Because the first thing the House told me—through the drain, through the wall, the night after—it said, *'We liked his voice better, but yours will do.'*" I wrapped my hands around the cooling mug. "So I figured if I gave it as little as possible, it would stay interested in him. In the memory of him. I whispered for nineteen years, Wren. I oiled its hinges. I salted its sills. I answered its little questions with hums and shrugs. I thought I was starving it."

"You weren't," Wren said quietly.

"I was *feeding* it." The words came out of me like something surgical. "Obedience is food. Silence is food. Every morning I got up and maintained this house like a good tenant and it counted every small surrender, and I told myself I was surviving, and maybe I was, but I was also—" My voice cracked, and I let it, because I was done managing myself. "I was keeping the account open. A debt dies when nobody tends it. I tended it every single day for nineteen years."

Wren was silent for a moment. Then: "The recorder. You destroyed the memory card."

"I did." I met his eyes. "I panicked. I thought if the House heard itself recorded it would take the machine, or worse, take the proof and leave us nothing. I told myself I was protecting everyone. But mostly I was protecting the arrangement, because the arrangement was the only world I knew how to live in." I laughed, and it came out awful. "You want to know what cowardice looks like after nineteen years of practice? It looks exactly like prudence. It looks exactly like a man who's very good at maintaining things."

"Theo—"

"My mother wrote your name in the ledger." I said it flatly, because it needed to be said flatly, because he deserved to hear it without cushioning. "She thinks I don't know. She thinks Camille doesn't know. But the House talks to me more than anyone, because I'm the one who listens least, and it likes to gloat. She offered it a nurse's death to buy my freedom. To finish what Dad started." I pushed the mug away. "And the House took the offer under advisement, and kept the debt rolling, because that's what it does—it never closes an account, it just moves the balance around—and now it's tired of waiting, and it's calling me Emil, and there's a coat upstairs that's still warm."

Wren had gone very pale, but his hands were steady. Nurse's hands. Hands that had held dying people through their worst nights and hadn't shaken yet.

"The coat," he said. "Is it still warm now?"

"Yes."

"Then it's not a memory. It's an invitation." He leaned forward. "Theo, listen to me. I've been reading the archive. This thing honors bargains literally—that's its whole nature, that's the crack in it. It can't take what isn't given. Your father gave himself. Your mother gave my name. Everything it has, it was handed."

"So?"

"So it means the way out is also literal. It means there's a version of this where nobody gets taken, because nobody agrees to be taken." He said it carefully, testing each word. "I don't know the mechanism yet. But I know the principle. Consent is the lock and consent is the key."

I sat with that. Outside, the light was starting to go long and amber, harvest-colored, and somewhere in the walls the House was listening—I could feel it, the particular pressure of attended silence, the way the air gets dense when something wants you to keep talking.

"There's something I have to ask you," I said. "And I need you to understand that I've been rehearsing this question for nineteen years without knowing it."

"Okay."

"Is there any way out of this house"—my voice held, barely—"that isn't a betrayal? Of my mother, who did what she did out of love? Of my father, who gave himself so we'd have nineteen years? Of Camille, who's kept the rules her whole life?" I swallowed. "Because every path I can see ends with somebody paying. Her instead of me. Me instead of you. Dad's sacrifice wasted or Mom's repeated. And I can't—I can't be free if freedom costs one more person in this family. I'd rather it took me than carry that."

Wren looked at me for a long moment. And I want to record, for whoever finds this, whatever this is, that he didn't give me comfort. He gave me something better. He gave me the truth, unpolished, the way you'd hand someone a tool with the sharp edges showing.

"I don't know," he said. "I honestly don't know yet. But I know this: your mother's bargain runs on a lie—she thinks a death buys a life. Your father's ran on one too—he thought he could negotiate with it alone. Both of them tried to pay in secret. And secrets are its currency, Theo. Truth is the one thing it can't digest." He tapped the table between us. "So maybe the way out that isn't a betrayal is the way out where everybody knows. Where the whole bargain gets read out loud, in front of everyone it binds. No more private payments."

"That's not a plan. That's a hope."

"It's both. Most true things are."

In the walls, faintly, almost fondly, the House spoke.

"*Emil,*" it said. "*Emil, dinner's nearly ready. Won't you come tell your mother?*"

And I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that it wasn't calling my father anymore.

It was rehearsing me.

Wren heard it too. His eyes went to the wall, then back to me, and he mouthed one word—*tonight*—and I nodded, and picked up my father's coat off the chair beside me, and held it against my chest like a shield, like evidence, like the first thing I had ever taken from this House instead of given.

Upstairs, faint and patient, the count began.

"Nineteen," said the House, in my father's voice, in my voice, in a voice that was learning to be mine. "Eighteen. Seventeen."

Eleven days to the moon. But the House, it seemed, had stopped waiting for the calendar.

It was starting early.
