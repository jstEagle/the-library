## 12. Developer's Son

Marcus Hale arrived at 9:14 on a Tuesday morning, which told me two things immediately: he was the kind of man who believed problems were best confronted in daylight, and he had no idea what kind of house he was walking toward.

I knew it was him before I saw the car. The dead had been chattering about it all night — a low, anticipatory hum running through the walls like current through old wiring. *He's coming,* they'd said, in that chorus of voices I'd spent three nights pretending was tinnitus. *The son is coming.* The house had been tidying itself. That's the only way I can describe it. Doors I'd left open stood closed. The hallway smelled of lemon polish. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked under footsteps that weren't there, the way a stagehand tests the boards before the show.

I was sitting on the porch steps with a thermos of coffee I hadn't drunk and my grandmother's ledger open on my knees, because apparently this was my life now — a forty-one-year-old radio host doing homework for a haunting.

The car that pulled up behind mine was a silver Audi so clean it looked embarrassed to be on Fenwick Lane. The man who got out of it was maybe thirty, wearing a jacket that cost more than my first car and an expression of brisk, weaponized competence. He had his father's jaw and none of his father's patience. He saw me on the porch and did not slow down.

"You're not supposed to be here," he said, by way of hello.

"Good morning to you too."

"This property is contingent. My father's office sent the notice." He flipped through a leather portfolio without looking at me, the way you'd shoo a cat off a table. "If you're a squatter, I'm obligated to inform you—"

"I'm Casper Winterbourne. My grandmother owned this house."

That stopped him. Not out of kindness — out of recalibration. His eyes went from me to the house and back, running some internal arithmetic about liability. "Winterbourne," he said. "Edda Winterbourne. Yeah. Voss's paperwork references her." He said her name the way you'd read a line item. "You're the heir? You should have been served months ago."

"I've been hard to reach. I do a radio show. Nights."

"The conspiracy guy?" A flicker of recognition, and something worse beneath it — amusement. "My dad listens to you. Thinks you're a fraud."

"Your dad has excellent instincts."

Marcus almost smiled. It died quickly, like he'd caught himself being human on company time. He turned to face the house fully, and I watched something happen in his posture — a small settling, the way a dog settles before weather changes. He didn't notice it. I did.

"So this is what we're working with," he said, consulting his portfolio. "Structurally sound per the '09 inspection, though the appraiser noted—" he squinted, "'anomalous thermal readings in the east rooms.' Probably bad insulation. Dad wants demo by spring. There's a buyer for the lot. Big footprint, big build. Nine units."

"Nine units," I repeated.

"Townhomes. It's Fenwick Lane, not Fenwick Palace. Nobody's crying over this place." He started up the walk, and I stood, and the ledger slid off my knees and hit the porch boards with a slap that sounded, in the morning quiet, like a door closing.

"Mr. Hale," I said. "Marcus. Before you go inside, I need you to hear something, and I need you to not laugh until I'm finished."

He turned around. Up close he had tired eyes, the kind you get from inheriting someone else's ambition. "I'm on a schedule."

"Eleven years ago my grandmother died in that house. Before that, people died in that house going back further than the county has records. Last week, four of them called my radio station. Dead people, Marcus. On air. They all mentioned this address." I held up the ledger. "This is my grandmother's handwriting. She kept accounts for something that lived here. Payments. Teeth, time, names. And the reason I'm telling you this instead of checking myself into somewhere with soft walls is that your father is about to buy this house, and when he signs, whatever holds the deed holds the invitation. Do you understand me? Your father isn't buying a lot. He's buying a lease."

For a long moment Marcus just looked at me. I braced for the laugh, the eye-roll, the word *fraud* delivered with his father's certainty.

What I got instead was: "The appraiser quit."

I blinked. "What?"

"Dale Okafor. He did the walkthrough in September. Two weeks later he sold his business, moved to Duluth, and won't take my calls. Dad says he flaked. Dale doesn't flake. Dale once drove through a blizzard to close a deal on a funeral home." Marcus's jaw worked. "And the thermal readings. East rooms. He logged them twice because he thought his equipment was broken. Both times, the readings were body-temperature."

The wind moved through the yard, and every dead leaf on the lawn lifted at once and settled again, like the ground exhaling.

Marcus watched the leaves come down. When he spoke again, his voice had changed register — lower, careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice and testing it. "My dad listens to your show," he said. "Every night. He falls asleep to it. Lately he's been having dreams about this street. He told me over Sunday dinner that he dreamed a woman was standing on this porch, smiling at him, and she never blinked, and when he woke up his pen was on the nightstand and the contract was signed in his sleep." He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "He thought it was funny. He framed it as a work ethic thing. *Even asleep, closing deals.*"

"That contract needs to be destroyed," I said. "Not signed. Not renegotiated. Burned."

"And you know this how?"

"Because the thing in the walls told me. Through a girl who's been sitting on this porch since before either of us was born."

I expected him to leave then. I'd given him every exit, wrapped it in crazy and handed it over. Instead Marcus Hale looked past me at the front door — at the door that had been closed all morning, standing shut against a windless day — and said, very quietly:

"It opened."

It hadn't. I would have sworn it hadn't. But the door stood ajar now, six inches of darkness showing, and the darkness had depth to it, the way a throat does.

"Stay on the porch," I said.

"Yeah," Marcus said. "Yeah, staying on the porch."

But he didn't. Of course he didn't. He was thirty years old and raised by Alder Voss's best customer, a man who taught him that hesitation was the only unforgivable sin, and when his phone buzzed in his pocket he answered it on speaker out of habit, and the voice that came through wasn't his father's.

*Come inside, sweetheart,* it said, in a woman's voice, warm as bathwater. *We've been waiting so long for family.*

Marcus went white. "Dad?"

*Inside, sweetheart. Everyone's inside. Everyone you've ever lost is inside.*

"That's not—" He took a step. I grabbed his arm and he shook me off with surprising strength, and I understood with terrible clarity that this was how it worked, this was the whole mechanism: it didn't chase you, it *invited* you, it made the door look like homecoming. The same trick it had pulled on desperate families for a century. The same trick, probably, it had pulled on my grandmother.

"Marcus!" I put myself between him and the doorway, which was stupid, physically stupid, I'm five-eleven and soft from twenty years of night shifts and vending machines, but I put my hands flat on his chest anyway. "Look at me. Look at *me*. Your father is alive. He's in a care facility on Delmore Road, and if we don't stop him signing, he dies inside that building and everything he owns — including you — gets fed to this house. That voice is bait. It's been fishing with the dead all week and you are *not* catching it today."

The voice came again, closer now, from just inside the door, and it had stopped being warm. *He signs Friday,* it said, in a voice like paper tearing. *Friday, Friday, Friday,* and other voices took it up from deep in the house, a congregation of the drowned chanting the calendar, and Marcus's legs nearly went out from under him.

I did the only thing I could think of. I talked over it. Twenty years of late-night radio, talking over dead air, talking over silence, talking over the sound of your own doubt at 3 a.m. — I talked like the microphone was hot and the whole county was listening.

"My name is Casper Winterbourne," I said loudly, to the door, to the dark, to whatever kept books in there. "This man is not invited. This man is not resident. This man is leaving, and if you touch him, I will find every name in my grandmother's ledger and say every single one of them out loud, on air, until sunrise, and we'll see whose lease holds."

The chanting stopped.

The silence afterward was worse — total, attentive, the silence of something reconsidering. Then the door swung shut, gently, almost politely, and the temperature came back up ten degrees, and Marcus Hale sat down hard on the porch boards like his strings had been cut.

For a while neither of us said anything. A real breeze came through, ordinary and cold. Somewhere down the street a dog barked at nothing in particular, bless it.

"How long," Marcus finally said, "has this been happening?"

"To me? Four days. To this house?" I picked up the ledger. "A hundred and thirteen years, give or take."

"And the girl on the porch. Mirabel." He said the name carefully, like handling evidence. "Dale Okafor mentioned her. In his last email. He wrote, *'Tell the Hales there's a girl on the porch who watches the windows, and tell them I mean it.'*" Marcus rubbed his face with both hands. "I marked it spam."

"People do."

He looked at me then, really looked, and I watched the last of the developer's son drain out of his expression and something else arrive — scared, furious, and stubborn, which is the exact recipe for an ally. "My father is ninety-four years old and he thinks he's buying a retirement project," Marcus said. "He's signing Friday morning at the facility. His lawyer's coming. If what you're saying is true—"

"It's true."

"—then I have to be there too. And you're coming with me, because you're the only person in this county who knows what we're actually dealing with, and because—" he gestured weakly at the shuttered, listening house, "—apparently you can make it stop by yelling at it."

"Yelling is maybe ten percent of it."

"What's the rest?"

"Honestly? Still figuring that out. There's a girl named Wren involved. And a fire."

Marcus stared at me. "A fire."

"A fire," I confirmed. "But first we need my grandmother's diary, the original deed, and a ninety-four-year-old man to hold still for forty-eight hours. Can you keep your father from signing until Thursday night?"

Marcus Hale, who had come to Fenwick Lane to measure a lot for nine townhomes, looked at the house that wanted to eat his family, and said the words I'd been waiting three days to hear from anyone with a car and a credit card:

"I can stall anything. I grew up with him. Give me until Thursday."

* * *

We sat in his Audi for an hour after that, engine running for the heat, because neither of us was ready to be alone yet and the porch felt like a stage. He told me about his father — the empire built on foreclosures, the second wife, the loneliness dressed up as discipline. He told me Dale Okafor used to bring his kids to the office Christmas party and stopped coming after September. He told me that when he was eight, he'd had an imaginary friend too, a tall man who stood in corners, and that his mother had made him stop mentioning it, and that he'd forgotten until this morning.

"Forgetting is the house's favorite trick," I said. "It's generous that way. It lets you not-know."

"That's not generosity."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

Before he left, Marcus walked to the edge of the porch and looked back at the house — really looked, the way you look at a person. The curtains in the upstairs east window twitched aside, just enough. Something small stood there. Watching. Smiling, I'd have bet money.

"Not Mirabel?" he asked quietly.

"No. Mirabel stays outside. Whatever that is, it only comes out after midnight, and it counts."

"Counts?"

"To sunrise. Children's voices. Behind the pantry there's a door that isn't there during the day." I closed the ledger. "Thursday, Marcus. We get to your father before Friday, or the next chapter of this story gets written in somebody else's blood."

He nodded once, jaw set, and got in his car. As he pulled away, the radio in my own car — parked dead silent in the drive, battery disconnected since Monday — crackled to life all by itself.

One voice. Familiar. Patient. Almost fond.

*Thursday,* it said. *Bring the boy. The Tenant likes an audience.*

Then static, and the smell of lemons, and the long afternoon stretching ahead of me like a held breath.
