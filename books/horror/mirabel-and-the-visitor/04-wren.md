## 4. Wren

The phone in the hall of Blackwood House had not rung since before Orsen was born, or so he assumed; it sat on its walnut shelf like a relic, its dial a dead eye. He used his mobile instead, standing in the kitchen where the light came down green through the ivy on the window, and listened to it ring four times before Wren picked up.

"Orsen." Not *hello*. Just his name, flat as a stone set into a path.

"Wren."

"You're there, then." A pause filled with the sounds of her house behind her—Ida's television, something boiling over faintly, the particular chaos she wore like a coat. "You actually went back."

"I'm here. The house is—" He looked for a word that wouldn't alarm her. "It's all still here."

"That's what worries me." He could hear her moving, the phone pressed between ear and shoulder, hands busy as always. "Orsen, I want to say this kindly, so I'm going to say it quickly: are you sleeping? Are you eating? Because last time you went somewhere alone with a bag full of equipment—"

"I'm fine."

"—you didn't wash for eleven days and you called me at three in the morning to tell me the seagulls were spelling your name."

"They were arranging themselves alphabetically. It's different."

"It is not different." But he heard the small crack in her voice, the one that was always there under the armor, the one that meant she'd been waiting for his call and pretending she hadn't been. "Why did you go back? Really."

He watched the ivy leaves press their palms against the glass. Somewhere upstairs a door eased shut on its own weight, the way old houses do, the way this house had always done, except that now he knew—or half knew—that doors up here closed because someone small passed through them.

"I found something," he said. "In the songs. And I need you to come. You and Ida."

The silence on the line went long enough that he checked whether the call had dropped.

"Come to Hollowmere," Wren said finally. "You want me to bring my six-year-old daughter to the village where Mum grew up, which you once described—and I'm quoting your thesis chapter—as 'a place where memory has gone septic.'"

"I was younger then. I was being dramatic."

"You were being accurate. That's what everyone said about you."

He turned from the window. On the porch, visible through the front room's dusty panes, the swing moved. There was no wind. He made himself look away.

"There are things here I can't explain over the phone," he said. "Things I need to see with my own eyes before I'll believe I'm seeing them. And if they're what I think they are, Ida shouldn't be anywhere near this village without me knowing exactly where she is every second."

"Orsen, that is the single worst sales pitch anyone has ever given me."

"I know."

"You're telling me: come to the haunted place, bring the child, danger unspecified."

"I'm telling you I need you," he said, and heard how the words came out raw, unpolished, the way truth does when it's been sitting too long in the dark. "I've needed you for thirty years and I've been too proud to say it. Please."

Behind him, at the edge of hearing, someone began to hum. Three notes, rising, patient. A harmony looking for its melody.

Wren exhaled. "Saturday," she said. "We'll come Saturday. And Orsen—if this is another episode, if you're not taking your medication, I will drive us straight home and I won't be kind about it twice."

"Saturday," he agreed, and hung up, and stood in the green kitchen light with his heart going like a trapped bird, because he had just done the arithmetic he'd been avoiding: two days. Two days to decide what Mirabel was, and whether bringing Ida inside the count was protection or delivery.

The humming stopped when he opened the door to the porch. She was sitting on the swing, feet not reaching the floor, exactly as always.

"You called your sister," Mirabel said.

"I did."

"She remembers me wrong," Mirabel said, without bitterness, the way you'd report the weather. "She thinks I wasn't real. That's all right. For a long time, so did you."

* * *

Wren Blackwood-Ashe drove north on Saturday with her daughter asleep in the back seat and a knot in her stomach that had been there since Tuesday.

She had spent the intervening days doing what she always did when Orsen surfaced: checking. His prescription refill history—current. His bank activity—sane, boring, groceries and diesel. His last email—a perfectly composed request for parish records from the county archive. By every measurable metric her brother was functioning. That was precisely what frightened her, because she remembered the last time, after Mum died, when he had been *most* lucid in the weeks before the seagulls, most articulate, most himself, as though the illness refined him first and broke him second, the way frost makes a window more beautiful right before it makes it useless.

And there was the other thing. The name.

*Mirabel.*

He'd said it on the phone only once, quickly, the way you'd mention a mutual acquaintance: "Mirabel thinks the song predates the chapel record." As if Wren would know who Mirabel was. As if there were some shared file in her head she'd simply failed to open.

She hadn't. She'd lain awake Wednesday night going through childhood the way you go through a dead relative's drawers—not wanting to find anything, unable to stop. The summer holidays at Hollowmere. The long grass. The well with its mossy lip. Orsen, seven years old, thin and watchful, talking to himself in the orchard—which was normal, children do that—but talking to himself *in dialogue*, pausing for answers, laughing at things said by no one. And once—she was nine, maybe ten—she had come around the corner of the barn and found him holding hands with the empty air, walking slowly, both of them careful of each other, the way you walk with someone much smaller than you.

"What are you doing?" she'd asked.

"Walking with Mirabel," he'd said, matter-of-fact, and then, with the sudden shame of the marked child: "Don't tell Mum."

She never had. She'd also never believed it mattered. Imaginary friends were scaffolding; you took them down when the building stood. Except Orsen's building had never quite stood, had it. There had always been a gap in him shaped like something else, a deference in the way he occupied rooms, as though he were subletting his own life.

"Mummy." Ida's voice from the back seat, sleep-thick. "Are we nearly at the singing house?"

Wren's hands tightened on the wheel. "What singing house?"

"The house that sings." Ida was looking out the window at the fens sliding past, brown water and grey sky, utterly untroubled. "You said we're going to Uncle Orsen's house."

"I said nothing about singing."

"You hummed it," Ida said placidly. "Just now. In your head. I heard it."

Wren realized her own lips were pressed together around a tune she had no memory of starting. Three notes, rising. Patient. She stopped humming so hard her teeth clicked, and told herself it was a song from the radio, some earworm, anything, and drove the last ten miles with the windows down and the fen wind loud enough to drown out whatever else might try to be heard.

* * *

They arrived at dusk, because of course they did; Hollowmere seemed to arrange arrivals at the hour when shadows stop being absence and start being occupancy.

Orsen met them at the gate. Wren took one look at him and felt the knot in her stomach do something complicated—half relief, half grief. He was thinner, but clean, clear-eyed, steady on his feet. He hugged her hard, longer than usual, and she felt in the hug an urgency he wasn't putting into words.

"You look terrible," she said into his shoulder.

"I look better than the house."

That was true. Blackwood House slumped against the darkening sky like a man leaning on a wall to finish a conversation. Ivy had colonized the eastern face entirely. But the windows of Orsen's old room glowed warm yellow, and the porch—

Wren looked at the porch swing and felt, unaccountably, that she was interrupting something. The cushions were dented. Two dents. One large, one small.

Inside smelled of dust and woodsmoke and, beneath both, something older and sweeter, like apples left too long in a closed room. Ida ran through the halls immediately, six years old and fearless, her footsteps drumming overhead while Wren and Orsen drank tea neither of them touched.

"So," Wren said. "Tell me why I'm here."

Orsen set down his cup. She watched him decide, visibly, how much truth the evening could hold, and hated him a little for it, and loved him for the care it took.

"Do you remember," he said, "when we were children. Did I have a friend here. Someone you couldn't see."

Here it was. Wren put her cup down carefully. "Orsen—"

"Her name was Mirabel."

"I don't remember any Mirabel." The words came out harder than intended. "I remember you talking to yourself. I remember Mum crying about it and Dad saying boys do that. I don't remember a *name*, Orsen, and I don't remember you ever mentioning one until this week."

"Thirty years ago I mentioned her constantly."

"Then I'd remember!" She stood up, and heard her own voice climb, and forced it back down because Ida was upstairs and mothers learn to modulate. "Do you understand what you're asking me? You're asking me to believe that my brother—who has a documented history—isn't sick this time but *right*, and that the friend who lived in his head has been living in this house for three decades waiting for him. That's not a story, Orsen. That's a relapse with better production values."

He didn't flinch. That was the worst part. The old Orsen would have crumbled, apologized, retreated. This one just looked at her with a patience that was almost unbearable, and said:

"Ask Ida."

"Ask—I'm not using my daughter as a diagnostic tool—"

"Mum!" Ida's voice from the stairs, bright, breathless. "Mum, come see! There's a girl!"

Every hair on Wren's arms rose. She crossed the hall at a speed she'd deny later and found Ida at the landing window, pointing down at the garden, at the swing—no, not the swing, at the *path beside the swing*, at empty air above flagstones silvered with rain.

"There's a girl," Ida repeated, delighted. "She says her name's Mirabel and she knows Uncle Orsen's middle name and she says I can't play hide-and-seek with her yet but soon."

Wren looked. There was nothing there. Rain-dark garden, one swing cushion dented, the fen beyond going the color of a bruise.

Then, from the corner of the room, Orsen spoke quietly, to the space beside the window, in the tone of a man continuing a conversation already underway:

"Thank you for not letting her see you yet. It would frighten her mum." A pause. "Yes. Yes, I'll tell her." Another pause, longer, and when he turned back to Wren his eyes were wet and resolute at once. "She says to tell you she kept me safe. All the times you wondered why nothing bad ever happened to me, even here—even in this house—she says that was her."

Wren's mouth was dry. "Orsen. There is no one there."

"I know," he said gently. "I know that's what you see. That's the whole horror of it, Wren—it doesn't matter what you see. It matters what's keeping count."

"Count?" The word snagged on something. "Count of what?"

But Ida was already turning cartwheels down the hallway, chanting under her breath in the way of children narrating their own joy, and Wren caught the words as she passed—caught them and felt the floor tilt:

*"One for the door and two for the sill, three for the water standing still—"*

"Where did you hear that?" Wren grabbed her, too hard, and saw confusion bloom on her daughter's face.

"You were humming it," Ida said. "In the car. You hummed it all the way here."

Wren looked at Orsen. Her brother had gone very pale, and very still, and when he spoke his voice was barely above the sound of the rain beginning on the roof.

"She's not supposed to know it yet. Nobody's supposed to teach it to her yet."

"Well, nobody did," Wren snapped, hauling Ida close. "She made it up. Children make things up. That's what they do, isn't it, Orsen? That's what *you* did."

But even as she said it, she was counting on her fingers—she caught herself doing it, the way the villagers did, the way she'd seen old Mrs. Petch do outside the shop that morning before closing her door—and the numbers came out to eleven, and she could not for the life of her remember why eleven mattered, only that her hands had known before she did.

Upstairs, in the room Wren had slept in as a girl, the wardrobe door swung slowly open on its own, and something inside it—patient, small, humming three rising notes—waited for the house to settle before it settled too.
