## 5. What Imaginary Friends Do

The strange thing was how quickly it stopped being strange.

I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only instrument I have left, and I have spent a lifetime calibrating it. I am a man who records things. I timestamp my tea. I have notebooks for conversations, notebooks for dreams, a notebook once devoted entirely to the different sounds the wind made in the chimney of my London flat—four distinct registers, catalogued over one wet February. So believe me when I tell you that I noticed what was happening to me in those first days after Mirabel came back to the porch swing. I noticed, and I documented my own surrender like a field researcher watching a subject walk willingly into the fen.

Day one: I set an extra cup on the table out of some reflex of hospitality and hated myself for it. Day two: the cup was drunk from, or seemed to be, the level lower by two fingers, and I told myself about evaporation. Day three: I stopped telling myself anything at all, and simply poured two cups, because the tea went cold otherwise, and she liked it with too much honey, exactly as she had liked it when we were both small enough to fit under the kitchen table together.

That was the thing about Mirabel. She did not announce herself. She *reentered*, the way water reenters a dry channel—the channel doesn't have to do anything; it only has to remember its shape.

* * *

She had routines, or rather she had mine, worn smooth as river stones from thirty years of absence. In the mornings she was gone—not vanished, just elsewhere—and the house felt like a held breath. By mid-morning she'd appear at my elbow while I worked through Tam Yew's tapes, and she would stand behind my left shoulder, close enough that the fine hairs on my neck stirred, and listen to the hiss and warble of dead men's songs with her head tilted like a wren's.

"You breathe too loud," she told me the first morning, apropos of nothing. "You always did. When you were little you breathed like a kettle."

"I was seven."

"You're still breathing like a kettle." And there it was—that flat, unbothered certainty, the voice of a child stating physics. I laughed before I could stop myself, a real laugh, ugly and surprised, the kind I hadn't produced since before my mother's funeral. The sound bounced off the kitchen tiles and came back wrong, too many echoes for such a small room, but I was laughing and didn't care.

She fetched things. That's the detail I keep circling back to, the one that undoes me. Not levitation, not apparition, none of the vocabulary the horror paperbacks would reach for. She fetched. My reading glasses, left on the porch rail, appeared beside my elbow. The good flashlight I couldn't find for three days turned up in my coat pocket, warm as if just held. Once, deep in transcription, muttering that I needed the second notebook, the blue one, the one I'd left upstairs—I looked up and it was already open on the desk to the correct page, the page with Tam Yew's third verse, as if the book had known where it was wanted.

"Thank you," I said. Aloud. To the empty room, any observer would have said.

"You're welcome," said Mirabel, from somewhere near the window, and the curtain moved though the windows were shut.

Here is what frightens me more than her existence, more than the impossible arithmetic of a child unchanged across three decades: I felt *sane*. For thirty years I had carried a low hum of wrongness, the sense that I was a document slightly misfiled, a photograph hung a half-inch crooked. Therapists had names for it. Wren had theories. I had notebooks full of attempts to transcribe the feeling, and every transcription failed, because the feeling wasn't a feeling at all—it was an absence, a missing counterweight, and you cannot describe a hole except by describing everything around it.

Mirabel was the counterweight. With her humming in the next room—with her *presence* bending the air the way heat bends light off a summer road—the hum stopped. I slept without dreaming of the house. I ate standing at the counter like a person instead of performing eating like a scholar observing rural foodways. On the fourth evening I caught myself narrating my work aloud to her—"and here he flattens the seventh, listen, listen, he drops it like a stone down a well"—and I heard my own voice and thought, not *I am talking to no one*, but *I haven't sounded this alive in years.*

That is the seduction. Not her face, not her small cold hand occasionally resting on my wrist like a bird landing. The seduction is sanity itself, offered back to you by the one thing whose existence should make sanity impossible.

* * *

At night she stood watch.

I don't know how else to put it. The first night after the porch, I woke at some black hour to a sound I hadn't heard since childhood: a slow, rhythmic creak from the direction of the stairs, the third step, the one that sang. I lay in my narrow bed in my narrow room listening to it and felt something in my chest unlock that had been locked so long I'd mistaken the lock for the chest.

In the morning I found her on the landing, sitting cross-legged where the hallway met the stairs, facing the front door below.

"What are you doing?"

"Watching," she said.

"Watching for what?"

Her head turned. Her eyes were gray, the exact gray of fen water under cloud, and they held the particular patience of children who know things adults have paid dearly to forget. "For whatever comes up the path," she said. "Something walks at night. It walks the hedgerows and counts the windows. It's been counting them a long time." A pause, and then, lighter, in the tone of a child reporting a fact about weather: "It never used to count ours. Now it does. That's why I'm here."

I should have asked then. I should have seized that thread and pulled until the whole garment came apart. But she said it the way she said everything—flatly, finally, the way you say *the sky is up*—and some cowardly scholar in me decided to file it under *local folklore, further research needed*, and I let her bring me tea instead, and the tea was warm, and the morning was beautiful, and the dread arrived on schedule anyway, punctual as a train, and I drank it down with the honey.

* * *

She hummed harmonies.

This is the part I will defend to my grave, the part that makes me certain—whatever else I doubt—that she was real, that she is real, wherever she is now. I was working through Tam Yew's second session tape, the one where the old organist sang "The Drowned Girl's Carol" with his hands flat on his knees, and the melody has a gap in it, a deliberate hole in the third phrase where the singer goes silent for two beats. Every folklorist who ever touched the song assumed corruption—a lost line, a damaged memory. I had written as much myself, in ink, in a published paper I now think of as my first sin.

Mirabel filled the gap.

Not singing over Tam—under him, beneath him, a harmony so low and true it seemed to come up through the floorboards, through the foundations, through the peat itself. Two beats of a note I had no name for, a note that made the window glass shiver and the lamp filament sing faintly, and then Tam's voice resumed and the song was whole, and I understood with total, sickening clarity that the gap had never been damage. The gap was a door, and the song was built around it the way a lock is built around a keyhole, and something was supposed to answer there. Something had always been supposed to answer.

"Where did you learn that?" I asked. My hands were shaking. I recorded the moment on the cassette running on the table—God forgive me, I recorded even that, even her, reflexively, the way a man blinks.

"I didn't learn it," she said. She was sitting on the floor with her back against the radiator, which was not warm, though she pressed against it as if it were. "I *am* it. Part of it. The answering part." She picked at a thread on her hem—she wore the same dress as thirty years ago, gray wool, bone buttons, and it was not faded, not worn, not aged a single day either. "Songs need voices on both sides, Orsen. That's what nobody remembers anymore. A song is a conversation. You all stopped answering, so the other side got lonely."

"And the other side is—"

"The Visitor." She said it without fear, the way you'd say *Tuesday*. "Don't write that word down."

I looked at my notebook. I had already written it. She looked at the notebook too, and then at me, and her expression did something I had never seen a child's face do: it went old. Just for a second, like a lantern guttering, and behind the gray of her eyes there was a depth of gray that belonged to no child, to no living thing, and then it closed over and she was seven again and asking if I wanted to play cards, and I said yes, God help me, I said yes, because the alternative was to keep asking questions, and the questions were starting to cost something I couldn't name yet.

* * *

On the sixth day I called Wren. I stood in the hall with the phone pressed to my ear and watched Mirabel upside-down through the banister rails above me, hanging off the landing like a gargoyle in a gray dress, making faces to make me lose my place in the sentence. Wren talked about Ida's school, about the long drive, about whether the house had heating. I said yes to everything. Behind Wren's words I could hear her doing dishes, the domestic percussion of a life being lived forward, and I felt the distance between us open like the gap in the drowned girl's carol.

"Orsen." Her voice changed. "You sound better than you have in years. That worries me more than the other thing."

"Wren—"

"Last time you sounded like this was after Mum died, right before you started sleeping in the garden and calling me by her name. Is someone there with you? Is there a woman?"

I opened my mouth. Above me, Mirabel went still on the railing, watching me with her chin on her fists, and her face was perfectly, terribly neutral, and I understood that whatever I said next would be a kind of testimony.

"No," I said. "No one. Come see for yourself. Bring Ida."

There was a silence on the line with a whole marriage's worth of history in it. Then: "Friday. We'll come Friday. Orsen—if you're unraveling, tell me now, and I'll come tomorrow, and I won't judge you, and we'll get you help."

"I'm not unraveling," I said, and it was the truth, and that was the horror of it. "I'm coming together. For the first time in my life, Wren, I feel like all of me is in the same room."

I hung up and stood in the quiet hall and heard myself say, aloud, to the air, "Well. That's done, then."

"That's done," Mirabel agreed from the top of the stairs, and swung her legs down through the banister gap and landed without sound, and came and took my hand, and her fingers were cool and dry and small and real, real, real, and I walked into the kitchen a happy man.

* * *

That night I dreamed of the count.

Not the song—or not only the song—but numbers themselves, vast and patient, arranged in the dark like sheep in a fold. I dreamed a voice going through them one by one, unhurried, thorough, a shepherd's voice, a schoolmaster's voice, ticking off names against a ledger older than the parish register, and each name it passed I felt as a small cold touch on the back of my neck, the way you feel individual raindrops through a coat. And I understood in the dream that the voice was approaching a number where my name should have been, and I braced for the cold touch—

—and it skipped me. Passed over me like a stone skipping water, like a finger passing a blank line in the ledger, and moved on, and in the dream I turned to look at the blank line and saw that it wasn't blank at all. It was covered. Something small was lying across my name the way a cat lies across a warm keyboard, the way a child lies across a bed to keep a place in it, and the something was humming the answering part of the drowned girl's carol, two beats, low and true.

I woke before dawn with the taste of peat water in my mouth and found the bedroom door open, though I had closed it. Mirabel sat on the floor against my bedframe, knees up, arms wrapped around her shins, facing the doorway the way she faced it every night. Standing watch. Keeping the door.

"You were talking in your sleep," she said, without turning around.

"What did I say?"

"Numbers." Her voice was very quiet. "You counted to ten and then stopped, and waited, and I thought—well. I thought don't. Don't count to eleven, Orsen. Whatever you dream, whatever asks you. There's a verse for every soul in Hollowmere, and the eleventh verse isn't finished yet, and nothing that isn't finished should ever be finished."

The light was coming up gray outside the window. Somewhere out past the hedgerow, in the fens, I heard—or thought I heard, and the distinction no longer comforted me—a sound like a single voice, far off, working its way patiently through a list.

"Go back to sleep," Mirabel said, and patted the floor beside her, and I lay back down on the bed above her like a child above its dog, and I slept, and it was the best sleep of my life, and I have never forgiven myself for it.

Because Friday was coming. Because Wren was bringing Ida. And because Mirabel, watching the door, began softly to hum—and it took my sleeping mind three verses to recognize the tune, and by then, in the dream, I was already counting along.
