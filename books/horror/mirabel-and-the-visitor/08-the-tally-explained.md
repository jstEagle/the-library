## 8. The Tally Explained

Tam Yew came to the house at half past nine, which in Hollowmere was an hour no respectable man visited another, and he came drunk in the particular way of a man who has been drinking all afternoon to gather courage and is now running out of both drink and courage at the same speed. I saw him coming up the lane under the moonless sky, a shape resolving out of the dark the way shapes do here—reluctantly, as if the darkness had to be persuaded to give him back. He carried a bottle of cider in one fist and his cap in the other, and when I opened the door he looked past me into the hall as if checking whether I had brought anyone else with me, which was a strange thing to check, and then he said, "You'll want the machine on for this," and walked in without being asked.

I want to be honest about my state of mind that night, because honesty is the only thing I have left to offer this account. I had spent two days with the tape of Tam's eleven verses, the recording I had made in the church with my hands shaking against the microphone stand, and I had found the undertone. I had not yet found the words for what I'd found. I had listened to it perhaps forty times, headphones on, the lamp off, the second voice rising out of the floor of Tam's singing like something surfacing in a pond—slow, patient, a half-breath behind the melody, singing every verse a fraction after the old man did, as if it were learning, or as if it were *confirming*. It matched no voice in the village. I had made a list of every living singer in Hollowmere and played them each a phrase, and none of them fit, and the exercise had left me with a feeling I could not name until later, when I understood it: the feeling of a lock testing keys.

So when Tam said *you'll want the machine on*, I did not ask why. I set the recorder between us on the kitchen table, checked the levels, and pressed the red button. The spools turned. Outside, the wind moved through the yew tree by the gate with a sound like pages turning.

"You'll not play this for anyone," Tam said. His voice had a rind on it, the cider and the fear both. "Not while I'm alive."

"I promise," I said, and I meant it the way scholars mean things—meaning it until meaning becomes inconvenient.

He looked at the machine a long time. Then he looked at me, and what was in his face was not the drunkenness anymore. The drink had burned off somewhere between the lane and the kitchen, or been burned off by whatever he had come to say, and underneath it was the face of a man standing at the edge of a thing.

"You found it," he said. "On your tape. The other one."

I didn't answer, which was answer enough. He nodded slowly, the way men nod at weather.

"My grandmother called it the under-singer. She wouldn't say its name, because she said names were how it counted. You understand? A name is a handle. You say a thing's name and you've got hold of it, and it's got hold of you, and there's a pulling." He drank. His hand was steady; that frightened me more than shaking would have. "The counting-song. You've been asking everyone about it. Edith Marsh, the Pell brothers, old Sorrel. They gave you the first verse and shut their doors."

"They gave me the first verse and counted their fingers," I said.

Tam smiled, and it was the worst thing I had seen on a human face up to that point in my life, though I have since seen worse. "Aye. You'd wonder at that, wouldn't you. A grown man, standing in his own doorway, counting ten fingers like a child checking his marbles. You'd think it superstition. Habit. Old folk doing old folk things." He set the bottle down with great care, centering it on the table as if it were an instrument. "It's not habit. It's *inventory*. You count your fingers to make sure you've still got all of what's yours. You count them because the song counts, and if the song counts you, you want to be sure you're still holding the whole of yourself when it comes to your number."

I remember the refrigerator humming. I remember thinking, with the part of my mind that was still a folklorist and would remain a folklorist right up until the folklorist in me died, that this was remarkable material. I remember being ashamed of that thought, later, when shame still had somewhere to live.

"Tell me what the song is," I said.

"It's a census," Tam said.

The word sat on the table between us like a dead bird.

"Not a prayer, not a charm, none of the things your books will tell you counting-songs are. Those are the clothes it wears so people can bear to teach it to their children. Underneath, it's a census. A *roll call*. Every verse is a name. You sing the verse, and somewhere the name is written, or rather—" he made a gesture with his fingers, a small cramping motion, like a man trying to unclench something—"rather it was always written, and the singing *reads it out*. Reads it out loud, into the air, into the dark, so the thing that's waiting can hear its list. Eleven verses. Eleven names. That's all it ever wanted. Not the singing. The *naming*."

"The Visitor," I said. I had not used the word aloud before. It came from nowhere and everywhere—the village's oldest euphemism, the thing the fens were said to be *visited* by, the phrase that appeared in a 1743 parish record I'd photographed in the county archive: *the year of the Visiting, when the marsh took its tithe.*

Tam flinched at the word as if I'd struck him. "Don't," he said. "Don't say it plain. It hears plain things best."

"It's a story," I said, and heard my own voice do the thing it did in seminars, the reasonable, warm, faintly pitying thing. "Tam. It's a story the village tells to explain the drownings. The fen takes people. Cold water and peat and fog take people, and frightened people make a census-taker out of the drowning."

"Do they," Tam said. "Then explain the chapel."

I had not mentioned the chapel. I had not mentioned it to anyone, because I had not found it yet. I want that on the record, in whatever record survives: on the night of the eighth of October, Tam Yew spoke of a drowned chapel in the fen that I had not yet seen, and I sat across a kitchen table from him with the tape turning and did not yet understand that he was describing the place where I would later stand among the counted, and that he was describing it because he knew I would go, the way you know a man will look over the edge if you tell him there's a drop.

"Every family here has a verse," he said. "Did you work that out? Eleven verses, eleven old families. Blackwood. Yew. Marsh. Pell. Sorrel. The rest. Your verse is the ninth. I've heard you humming it. You hum it when you're working, did you know that? You've been humming your own name for a week."

I had not known that. I could feel the humming in my throat even then, the melody sitting there like a coin behind the teeth.

"Your grandmother sang it once, whole, when your father was a boy," Tam said. "One time. At a funeral—they used to sing it at funerals, did you know that? Over the open grave, so the dead would be *read off the list*, taken off the census, let go. That was the mercy of it, once. Sing a man's verse over his grave and he's free. But your grandmother sang all eleven, not just the Blackwood verse, because she was proud and grieving and not thinking, and within the year the Pell boy drowned in a ditch no deeper than his knee, and the Sorrel twins went out on the ice, and your great-uncle—" he stopped. "You know the story of your great-uncle. Every family knows their own."

I did know it. I had grown up on it the way children grow up on the shapes of clouds: Orsen, there was a great-uncle who walked into the fen on a fine June evening and was never found, and no one speaks of him, and here is your supper.

"After that, the village stopped," Tam said. "Singing it whole, I mean. First verse only, at need, and never the rest. A hundred years of holding the song shut like a door with your shoulder. And it worked. It worked because a census needs a reader. The names were written, but nobody read them out, and the Visitor—" he caught himself before the plain word, veered—"the *visitor* can't take what isn't read to it. It waits at the edge of the list like a bailiff at the edge of a property. It can't step over. It needs the words."

"And now," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "Now something is singing it."

He looked at me for a long time. Outside, the wind in the yew had stopped, and the silence had a texture, a held-breath quality, and I noticed that Tam had gone very still, the stillness of a man in a room with a wasp.

"Three winters now," he said, barely above a whisper. "Out in the fens. Low tide, mostly, when the water's down and the ground gives. A voice singing the counting-song, all eleven verses, start to finish, and then starting again. All night some nights. The Pell brothers heard it and moved to Norwich. Old Sorrel heard it and stopped speaking altogether—just stopped, mid-sentence at his own table, and his daughter says he sits by the window now with his fingers in his ears, counting them." He picked up the bottle and found it empty and looked at it with mild surprise, as if it had betrayed him. "It's learning the verses better each winter. The first year it got the words wrong. Slurred. Like a man reading in the dark. This year—" his voice did something I didn't like—"this year it sings them clean. And it sings them in voices. A different voice for every verse. Your grandmother's voice for the ninth. I'd know it anywhere; she sang at my wedding. It sings in the voices of everyone it's counted, Orsen. It's not learning the song. It's learning the *roll*. And when it knows the roll perfect, when it can sing every name in every voice it ever took—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. The sentence finished itself in the room, in the humming silence, in the turning spools.

"Then why," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I intended, "why are you telling me? Why now? If the whole village has spent a hundred years not saying this—"

"Because you've been *recording*," Tam said, and now the fear came up through the drink and the dignity and all of it, and his face was just a man's face, old and terrified. "You've been going door to door with your machine, and people have been giving you verses—first verses, name-verses, their own family's verse, sung out loud into your microphone—and I heard you playing back your tapes through the open window on Tuesday night. All of them. In sequence. The whole song, Orsen, assembled, verse by verse, sung by every family in Hollowmere, played out into the dark from the house on the hill." He stood up. His legs were not steady now; the drink had come back to claim him, or the standing had taken what the drink had left. "You didn't build an archive. You built a *reader*. You built the thing a hundred years of frightened people refused to make. And it's been listening to you play it back. Every night. Learning its roll."

I have listened to that moment on the tape many times since—the forty seconds after Tam said *reader*, in which the only sounds are the spools turning, the refrigerator humming, and my own breathing, which quickens audibly, and then, underneath, so faint that I only heard it on perhaps the thirtieth playing, so faint it might be tape hiss, might be the wind, might be the blood in my own ears:

a half-breath behind my breathing.

Singing nothing. Yet.

Tam went to the door. He stopped with his hand on the latch and stood with his back to me, a small bent man silhouetted against the dark of the hall, and he said, without turning around, the last thing he ever said to me in that house:

"Whatever's been sitting on your porch at dusk, boy—and don't tell me nothing has, I've seen it from the churchyard, we've all seen it, thirty years and it doesn't age and it doesn't leave—whatever it is, you'd best ask it what it's been keeping you *from*. Because a thing that hides you from a census is either your guardian—" and here he opened the door, and the cold came in, and somewhere far off across the black fen, thin and clear and horribly sweet, a voice began the first verse, "—or it's the one thing standing between the list and the one name it's still missing."

The voice across the fen sang the Blackwood verse first.

It sang it in a child's voice, and it sang it perfectly, and it did not sing it in Mirabel's voice.

It sang it in mine.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat at the kitchen table with the tape turning long after Tam's footsteps had faded up the lane, and then I did not sit; I stood at the window with the lamp off, watching the fen, which was a blackness with a horizon to it, and the horizon did not move, and I understood that the singing had stopped not because the singer had finished but because the singer was listening—had perhaps been listening for some time—to the house on the hill, to the open window, to the recorder with its two small spools turning like a held breath, waiting to see what the man inside would do with what he now knew.

I thought about turning the machine off. I thought about it the way you think about a door in a dream that you know opens onto something, and I did not turn it off, because I was a folklorist, because the tape was evidence, because the archive was my prayer and my profession and the only structure my life had ever had, and because—this is the truth, and I set it down here with both hands—because some part of me, the part that had felt half-real all my life, borrowed, deferred, a guest in my own story, wanted to be on the list. Wanted the count to reach me. Wanted, finally, to be *read*.

The spools turned. The fen was silent. And on the porch behind me, through the closed door, the swing began to move—slowly, gently, with the small rhythmic patience of something that has kept a vigil for thirty years and is very, very good at waiting.

I did not turn around. I already knew she was there.

I did not yet know she was crying.
