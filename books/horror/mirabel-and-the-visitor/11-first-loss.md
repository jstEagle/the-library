## 11. First Loss

The knock came before dawn—not on the door but against the window of my mother's old room, three soft raps, like a child asking politely to be let in from the rain.

I woke with Mirabel's name half-formed in my mouth and found her already standing at the glass, small hands pressed flat against it, looking out into a darkness that had texture to it, a wet grey weight like wool held over a candle flame.

"He's stopped," she said.

"Who's stopped?"

She didn't turn around. In the window's reflection her face was pale and smooth as an egg, and behind her reflection the garden waited, motionless, listening. "The organ man. Listen."

I listened. I heard nothing, and that was the wrong answer, because for three nights running—since the session in Tam Yew's kitchen, since I'd packed my recorder away with hands that would not stop trembling—I had been able to hear the organ through two fields and a hedgerow, faint as a pulse under a thumb. Tam played at night now. He hadn't told me he would; I don't think he'd decided to. He'd simply said, at the end of our last interview, buttoning his coat with fingers that kept missing the buttons: *It wants keeping company, that song. A thing sung alone too long gets lonely.* And then he had gone home across the fen path, a stooped black shape against the last grey light, and every night after, the music had come thin and wavering over the water, eleven verses, always eleven, never twelve, as if even in the dark he was careful to stop where stopping mattered.

Now there was nothing. The silence had a shape, and the shape was a man-shaped hole in the world.

I dressed without turning on lights. The house watched me do it—I felt its attention the way you feel a draft, along the back of the neck—and when I passed the landing mirror I made myself look, because Mirabel had taught me that years ago, or reminded me, or perhaps I had invented the whole ritual and she only nodded along: *Look at yourself going past, Orsen. Every time. So it knows you know your own face.* My face looked back at me, forty pounds heavier than the boy who'd left Hollowmere, grey at the temples, eyes rimmed red from nights of headphones and worse nights of not wearing them. It knew itself. It went downstairs.

Mirabel walked beside me through the village lanes without touching the ground, which I had stopped noticing, which frightened me more than anything else about her—the ease with which the impossible became furniture. The mist stood in the hedges like unspooled wool. Somewhere a dog began to bark and then thought better of it. St. Brannoc's church rose out of the fog at the end of the lane with its tower leaning a degree or two off true, the way it had leaned all my childhood, so gradually that no one could say when it had started, only that it was still leaning, still patient, still going.

The door was unlocked. That was the first wrong thing. Tam Yew locked that door with two locks and a bar, and once, when I'd asked him why a church needed locking, he'd looked at me with his clouded eyes—blind since the fever of '63, blind longer than I'd been alive—and said, "Not to keep things out, lad."

* * *

The smell reached me before the light did. Beeswax and cold stone, yes, the church's ordinary breath—but under it something sweetish and wrong, like fruit left too long in a closed room, like the inside of a violin case in summer. I stood in the nave with my hand on the door and let my eyes adjust, and the windows gave up their grey ghost-light one by one, and I saw him.

Tam Yew sat at the organ the way a man sits who has been sitting a very long time. Upright. Composed. His head bowed slightly forward as though in prayer or concentration, his white hair fine as frost in the light from the east window. He might have been resting between verses. He might have been listening for something in the pipes.

His hands were on the keys.

I want to write this plainly because I have never managed to write it plainly since, and plainness is all the dignity I have left to give him: his fingers were worn through. Not cut, not broken—*worn*, the way a stair is worn, the way a coin is worn, the way a threshold stone goes concave under a century of feet. The flesh of his fingertips was gone entirely, ground away to expose the bone beneath, and the bone itself was polished, smooth, gleaming faintly in the grey light like river stones, like things tumbled a long while in water. The ivory keys were dark with blood in the creases between them, dried to a brown so deep it was nearly black, and the pattern of it—this is the detail that comes back to me at four in the morning, that has replaced whatever I used to think about at four in the morning—the pattern of it showed he had not struck the keys. He had *rested* on them. Pressed down and held, note after note, chord after chord, the whole weight of a dying man's arms committed to each one, playing the way a drowning man holds a spar.

Eleven verses. He must have gone through them more than once. The pedal board was slick. His shoes had split at the seams.

"Orsen." Mirabel's voice came from very close, very quiet. She was standing beside the organ bench, looking up at Tam's bowed head with an expression I had never seen on her before and have spent a year failing to describe. It was not fear. It was recognition—the look of someone meeting, after long separation, a person they had hoped never to see again. "He sang himself empty," she said. "They use what's nearest. When they can't reach the ones they're counting, they reach the ones who know the names."

"Who?" My voice came out sanded flat. "Who used him?"

She turned her face up to me, and in the church's drowned light her eyes were the colour of the fen water, green-brown and depthless, and she said, "You know what's singing in the fens, Orsen. You've had it in your headphones for a week."

* * *

I did not run. I remember being surprised by that—that some older instinct than panic held my legs, some archivist's discipline drilled so deep it survived even this. I took out my camera. I photographed Tam Yew from the doorway, from the aisle, from beside the organ, and my hands were steady, God forgive me, my hands were steady as surveyor's instruments, because documenting was the only prayer I knew and I did not yet understand what my prayers had been summoning. In the viewfinder his face was peaceful. Blind men die with their eyes open less often than you'd think; his were closed, the lids sunken, the lashes white. He looked like a man asleep at his post, except for the hands, except for the hands.

It was only when I lowered the camera that I saw the tape.

It lay on the music shelf above the keyboard, wedged upright between two hymnals, a standard C-90 cassette in a cracked plastic case, and across the label, in Tam's handwriting—shaky, the letters leaning like the tower, written by feel because he could not see what he wrote—was my own name.

*For O. Blackwood. Last.*

My field case was in the vestry. I had left it there two nights ago, after the session, because Tam had asked me to record one more thing—"a private matter, lad, just for you, not for your book"—and I had set the case down on the vestry table and never picked it up again, because what he'd recorded had emptied me of everything except the need to get home before dark. The case sat where I'd left it, buckles fastened, patient as everything else in Hollowmere. And when I opened it—when I lifted out the tapes one by one, checking them against my logbook the way I checked them every morning, a ritual, a rosary—every cassette was there except the space where the newest should have been. The slot was empty. The foam lining held a Tam-shaped absence, dust-free, waiting.

He had carried it to the organ himself. Blind, in the dark, across the village, carrying his own death warrant back to the instrument it concerned, and he had labeled it, and he had shelved it above the keys where anyone coming to find him would be sure to see it, and then—

Then he had sat down and played until there was nothing left of his hands to play with.

I stood in the vestry holding the tape in both hands. It weighed almost nothing. They all weigh almost nothing; that's the obscenity of them, ninety minutes of a human voice in a shell lighter than an egg. Through the wall I could hear Mirabel moving in the nave, her footsteps making no sound at all, and beyond her, beyond the walls, beyond the mist, the fen lay flat and silent under a sky just beginning to consider the idea of morning.

*Last,* the label said. He had known it was the last. He had recorded it knowing, and given it to me anyway, because I had asked him for truth and he was too honest a man to give me less than all of it.

* * *

I played it in the car, in the churchyard, engine off, doors shut, because I could not carry it into the house and I could not leave it unlistened-to either; an unlabeled tape is a debt, and Tam Yew had paid enough.

Static first. Then his voice, closer to the microphone than he'd ever sat before, so close I could hear the wet click of his throat, the whisper of his coat collar.

"If you're hearing this, I'm done talking sense, so listen instead." A pause. His breathing. "You'll have found me by now, or someone will have, and they'll say heart, they'll say age, and let them, because the truth needs saying once and once is all I've got strength for. The song's a census, boy. I told you it was a counting-song and I told you we don't sing it twice, and you wrote it all down nice and neat, and here's the part I couldn't say with the machine running, because it hears the machine, do you understand? It hears through the machine. It's been hearing through yours since the day you drove into Hollowmere with all those pretty reels."

The tape hissed. Under the hiss—faint, far down, like a voice through a floor—something else breathed.

"I counted my fingers this morning," Tam said, conversationally, terribly. "Eleven. I've been counting them every morning since you came, and every morning there's one more than there should be, and I know what that means because my grandmother told me before she was taken, and her father told her. When your count goes over ten, you've been entered. You're on the list. And the list doesn't forget, and the list doesn't forgive, and the only mercy in it is that it takes you doing what you love." A sound that might have been a laugh. "So I'll play, then. Better the organ than the girl child. Better me than anyone younger. You tell Wren Blackwood I said so. You keep that little one indoors, Orsen. Keep her from the well water. Keep her from counting."

And then, because he was Tam Yew, because he was the most faithful man I ever met and faithfulness was the very hinge of his destruction, he said: "One more thing, and then I'm done. The second voice on your recordings. The one under mine. You've heard it. Don't go looking for whose it is. Some names are better lost than found, and some of us"—his voice dropped, and the microphone caught the tremor in it, the raw animal reluctance—"some of us have been carrying other people's names so long we've near forgotten our own. Ask your friend on the porch. Ask her what year she stopped growing. Ask her who wrote her name down first."

The tape ran on. There was no more speech. But there was music—distant, muffled, as if recorded through a wall or from the bottom of a well: the counting-song, sung whole, all eleven verses, in a voice I did not recognize and could not place and knew, somewhere below knowing, that I had heard before. It was a child's voice. It was patient. It was getting louder on every verse, verse by verse, as though the singer were walking toward the microphone across a long flat country, and on the eleventh verse it was very close indeed, and on the eleventh verse it pronounced a name.

It pronounced *Blackwood.*

The tape ended. In the churchyard the mist was lifting off the graves in slow ropes, and the east window had gone from grey to the colour of old pearl, and somewhere behind me, inside the church, though I had shut the door and the door had latched, an organ key sank slowly down under an invisible weight and held its single low note, patient as the tower, patient as the peat, patient as everything in Hollowmere that had learned to wait.

Mirabel was standing at the passenger window of the car. I hadn't seen her cross the grass.

"You have to burn them," she said. "All of them. Before you understand why."

Through the glass, her breath should have fogged it. It did not.
