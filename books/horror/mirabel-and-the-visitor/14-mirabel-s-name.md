## 14. Mirabel's Name

The peat had taken the chapel the way sleep takes a body—slowly, from the edges inward, until there was no telling where the dreaming ended and the sleeper began.

I stood in the drowned nave with my camera hanging forgotten at my chest and my breath coming out in small white ghosts, though it was June. The water was ankle-deep and black as tea, warm in a way that water should not be warm, warm like something recently alive. Around me they stood in their rows: the counted ones, generations of them, sunk to the waist or the shoulder or only the knees depending on how long ago the Visitor had come for them. Their faces were upturned. Their mouths were open. Not screaming—I understood that now, standing among them—but mid-verse. Every one of them caught at the same moment, the moment when a note leaves the throat and belongs to no one anymore.

I had photographed everything before I could stop myself. That was the sickness of me, the family sickness maybe: the world presented its worst face and I reached for documentation like a drowning man reaches for rope, not because the rope holds but because holding is what hands do. Forty-one exposures. I could hear the film canisters clicking against each other in my coat pocket like small teeth.

But it was the ledger that stopped me.

It lay on the altar—or what had been an altar; the stone was furred green and split down the middle like a healed wound—and it was open, and it was dry. Everything else in this place was wet, was peat, was rot, but the ledger's pages were dry and white and soft-looking, and I knew without touching it that if I touched it I would not be able to stop touching it.

The chapel had been waiting for someone to read it. I think it had been waiting for me specifically.

* * *

I waded up the aisle between the standing dead. They did not move. I want to be honest about this, because honesty is all I have left of her: part of me wanted them to move. Part of me has always wanted the world to be more than it appears, and here was the world, finally, exceeding itself, and my hands shook not entirely with fear. A woman stood near the third pew with a shawl still knotted at her throat, the wool dark with a century of water, and her eyes were closed, and I said *excuse me* to her, actually said it aloud, the way you'd step around a stranger at a bus stop, and then laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, came out like a sob that had gotten lost on the way up.

The ledger's cover was leather gone the color of old blood. No title. No cross. Just a name pressed into the hide in letters so shallow I had to tilt it toward the gray light falling through the broken roof:

**HOLLOWMERE — THE COUNTING — BEGUN IN SORROW, KEPT IN FEAR**

The first page was a hand I didn't recognize, cramped and pious, ink browned to rust:

*In the year of our Lord 1891 the Visitor came first to Hollowmere, and it took Mirabel Blackwood, aged seven years, daughter of Edmund Blackwood of the fen-farm, who sang the counting-song at her skipping and did not know what she sung. Eleven verses she sang, and on the eleventh verse she gave her own name, for the song asks a name and a child will answer honestly. The Visitor took her at dusk from the yard while her mother watched through the window and could not make her legs go to the door.*

*And the village learned what the song was for.*

I read it three times. The water moved around my ankles with a patience that felt deliberate. Somewhere behind me, back in the fens, back toward the house, a bird made a sound like a hinge.

Mirabel Blackwood. 1891. Seven years old.

My family's name. My family's blood. My family's oldest wound, cut into a child and then cut out of memory, because I had grown up in that house and I had never once heard the name Mirabel spoken by anyone living—and yet I had known it, hadn't I? Known it at seven years old myself, when she appeared on the porch swing with her braids and her gap teeth and her habit of humming harmonies to songs nobody had taught either of us. I had told my mother about my new friend Mirabel and my mother had dropped a cup. I remembered that now. I remembered the cup breaking and my mother going white and saying *where did you hear that name* in a voice I had never heard from her before or since, and seven-year-old me saying *she told me, she lives on the porch,* and my mother kneeling down and gripping my shoulders hard enough to bruise and saying, *never say that name in this house again.*

So I hadn't. And she had stayed anyway. Thirty years, staying anyway, unspoken, unasked, keeping her post on the porch swing like a soldier left behind by a war everyone else agreed to forget.

* * *

The ledger went on. Page after page, hand changing every few decades, the ink shifting from rust to blue-black to pencil during some lean year, but the form never changing: a date, a name, an age, a verse number. The count. The actual tally, kept in a drowned church by no one, updated by no one, and yet the entries continued past the last human handwriting, continued in a script I recognized with a lurch that turned my stomach over—the thin, patient, upright letters I had spent weeks pulling out from underneath Tam Yew's voice on my tapes. The undertone. The second voice under the song. It wrote the way it sang: evenly, without hurry, the way water writes on stone.

*1911 — Aldous Crane, 44, v. 9*
*1923 — Bethia Crane, 9, v. 11*
*1937 — Samuel Yew, 61, v. 4*

On and on. Names I half-knew from gravestones in the Hollowmere churchyard, names whose stones I had walked past with my recorder bag and my scholar's detachment, never once wondering why so many of them clustered in certain years, why whole families went quiet in the registers at once. The song wasn't a summons. I had understood that much already, standing in Tam Yew's kitchen while he wept into his beer. The song was a census. But a census implies a counter, and a counter implies—

A ledger. A book that keeps itself. A book that dries itself in a drowned chapel and waits.

I turned pages faster. My fingers were pruned white and I didn't care. I was looking for one thing now, one entry, and some cold arithmetic part of me already knew what it would find, because Mirabel had told me once—had told seven-year-old me, on the porch swing, in the purple light of a summer evening—when I asked her how old she was. She had thought about it seriously, the way she thought about everything, head tipped, braids swinging.

*I'm older than the house,* she'd said. *But I'm seven forever. Those are different things.*

*1968 — Wren Blackwood, listed, struck through*
*1968 — Orsen Blackwood, listed, struck through*

My own name. My sister's name. Written in the Visitor's patient hand in the year of our birth, entered in the count like lambs born into a flock—and then struck through. Struck through in a different stroke, a fiercer one, a line dragged so hard across the page it had torn the paper slightly. Someone had crossed us out. Someone had spent thirty years crossing us out, holding us outside the tally, hiding us inside herself, and I knew—I knew with the certainty of a man reading his own obituary—that the hand that struck those lines was not the Visitor's even, patient script. It was small. It was fierce. It was the hand of a seven-year-old girl who had been collected in 1891 and had decided, alone, in the dark of whatever the Visitor kept its collections in, that she would not participate anymore. Not fully. Not with these two.

She couldn't strike us out entirely. I saw that now too, saw it in the way the strikes were drawn—the names still legible beneath, the way a scar shows where the wound was. She couldn't remove us from the book. She could only stand in front of our names the way she stood on the porch: between us and the thing that reads. For thirty years she had been a door held shut by a child's body, and children tire, and doors are heavy, and I had spent my whole life feeling half-real, borrowed, deferred, and I had thought it was a flaw in me. It was a bill being paid. It was the weight of her, leaning against my name, year after year after year.

* * *

I don't know how long I stood there. The light through the broken roof changed from gray to grayer. The standing dead kept their verses. At some point I sat down in the black water—I remember the shock of warmth through my trousers, that obscene warmth—and I read the ledger from the beginning like a novel, like scripture, like the only true document I had ever touched, and everything else I had ever collected, forty-one photographs and two hundred hours of tape and a lifetime of notebooks, shriveled in my estimation to what it had always secretly been: a man counting raindrops in a flood, proud of his arithmetic.

Because here was the thing the ledger made unavoidable, the thing that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone:

Mirabel Blackwood was not my friend.

Mirabel Blackwood was my family. My blood, four generations back, a girl from the fen-farm with my cheekbones and my stubbornness and my habit of answering honestly when asked for a name. And she had been taken for it—taken by a song, by her own honest child's mouth—and instead of passing out of the world like the others in their rows, she had come back. Come back wrong-shaped, or right-shaped, seven forever, older than the house, and attached herself to the descendants of the family that let her be taken. Attached herself to me.

Loyalty, I had called it, on the porch, in the good years of this terrible summer. Devotion. Love, even—I had let myself think the word love, sitting on that swing beside her while she hummed.

But the ledger suggested another word, and the word was *leash.* Because protection is a strange gift. Protection given long enough becomes keeping. She had hidden me inside herself for thirty years, and a man hidden inside another person for thirty years is not free—he is *held.* Every choice I had ever made, I had made inside the shelter of her, and now I looked at my life—my estrangement from Wren, my retreat into archives, my inability to finish anything, to commit to anything, to be fully present in any room I stood in—and I saw the shape of a man who had never once been wholly in the world. Half-real. Borrowed. Deferred. Not because something was missing from me.

Because most of me was being kept somewhere else. Kept safe. Kept *hers.*

No. I pushed the word away. That was fear talking, and exhaustion, and the chapel's cold working into my bones. She had chosen me twice—once in 1891's aftermath, once on the porch thirty years ago—and you did not call a leash what a starving creature gives away.

But then why, asked the cold voice, the scholar's voice, the voice that had noticed the undertone on the tapes before anyone else—why does the hiding feel exactly like a debt? Why does every kindness of hers arrive pre-paid, non-negotiable, without asking what I would have chosen?

I looked up at the nearest of the standing dead—a young man, maybe twenty, mouth open on a note he'd been singing before my grandfather was born—and I asked him, quietly, *did anyone ask you?*

The water answered with that patient lap-lap against the pews. The ledger lay open on my knees, and the Visitor's handwriting waited on the next page, and I turned it, because I had never in my life not turned a page.

*1993 — the hiding weakens. The keeper tires. The count resumes where it paused.*

And beneath it, fresher, the ink still sharp, dated this summer, dated days ago:

*Tamuel Yew, 71, v. 1–11, complete.*

Tam. Dead at his organ even now, probably, or found by now, fingers worn to bone. Entered in the book while I slept fifty yards from him with my recorder running, my machine drinking the song down and carrying it home to broadcast to whoever—whatever—listens.

And beneath Tam's name, the pen—no hand, just the pressure of intention moving through the page—had begun a new line. The beginning of a letter. A single careful stroke, vertical, patient, the start of a letter that could be an I, could be a W, could be—

The chapel exhaled. All that black water moved at once, gently, in one direction, toward the door, as if the fen had leaned in to see what I would do.

I closed the ledger. I picked up my camera. And I turned for the long wade back through the rows of the counted, back through the fens, back toward the house where a seven-year-old girl was keeping my name safe—back toward the porch where I was going to look my oldest friend in the face and ask her the question I should have asked thirty years ago:

*Mirabel Blackwood—who told you to protect me?*

And what, exactly, happens to a girl who stops?
