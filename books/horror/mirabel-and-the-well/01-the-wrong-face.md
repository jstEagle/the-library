## 1. The Wrong Face

The bus from the city took eleven hours, and I spent nine of them drafting the eulogy in my head and the other two drafting it out loud, to a woman across the aisle who was either deeply moved or pretending to sleep. I am a documentarian by compulsion, not by profession — nobody pays you to record things, they only pay you to make the recording disappear — so I had my recorder running the whole way, capturing the rattle of the windows and the woman's polite breathing and, at some point past midnight, my own voice saying *Mirabel, I'm sorry I didn't come home sooner* into the dark like a man leaving a voicemail for the dead.

The dead, I am told, do not check their messages.

The homestead looked smaller than grief had painted it. The gate still leaned at the same apologetic angle, the mango tree still dropped its fruit with the same insolent thud onto the same patch of red earth. My aunt Ngozi was on the porch, and she stood when she saw me, and she did not hug me, which was correct. We Cranes are not a hugging family. We are a *noticing* family — we notice everything and say nothing, and then we write it down, at least the women did, my mother most of all, in her leather notebooks with their cracked spines and their terrible arithmetic.

"Ada," Ngozi said. "You look like your sister."

"I'm told that's normal for twins."

"It's not what I meant." But she didn't explain, and I didn't ask, because asking questions in this house has always been like knocking on a door that opens onto another door.

The body had arrived the day before me, which felt like a small administrative insult. My sister had died before I could reach her, and then she had traveled without me. The casket sat in the front room where my mother used to take her afternoon naps, on trestles borrowed from the church, with a white cloth over it and a candle at each corner doing their slow, dutiful work.

I want to be honest about what I felt standing in front of that casket, because honesty is the only thing I have ever been any good at, and it is about to become the only thing I have left. I felt nothing. Not the nothing of shock — I know that nothing, I interviewed a man once whose entire village had washed away, and he had that nothing, a held-breath nothing, a nothing with a scream inside it. This was different. This was the nothing of a room you walk into and immediately feel you have walked into before. Grief was supposed to be there. I went looking for it the way you pat your pockets for keys. It wasn't in any of them.

I lifted the lid.

* * *

The undertaker was a small, precise man named Mr. Eze, with the kind of moustache that looks maintained by a committee. He came around the side of the house when he heard me make a sound — I don't know what kind of sound; the recorder caught it and I have not listened to it since, and I will not describe it here.

"You shouldn't open it," he said, not unkindly. "It's bad for the face."

"Whose face?"

"Your sister's." He said it the way a man reads a number off a form. "Mirabel Crane. A fine woman. We did good work on her."

The face in the casket was mine.

I want to be careful with this sentence, because I have read it back a hundred times in my notebook and it has not softened and it has not changed and it has not, in any of those hundred readings, become explicable. The face in the casket was mine. Not similar. Not a sister who happened to share the family nose and the family jaw and the family habit of looking slightly annoyed at the world. It was my face, down to the scar over my left eyebrow that I got at nine years old when Mirabel dared me to climb the mango tree and then, with a twin's perfect instinct for self-preservation, did not climb it herself. It was my face, and it was dead, and it was wearing my expression, which in life is usually mild irritation and in death had settled into something like disappointment, which I felt was fair.

"That's me," I said.

Mr. Eze looked at the face, then at me, then back at the face, with the weary patience of a man who has seen relatives say stupid things at caskets before. "Grief does funny things," he said. "You see yourself in her. Twins. Very common."

"It's not grief. It's my scar. It's my — " I touched my own eyebrow. The scar was still there, on my face, attached to me, doing its usual work of making me look like I'd lost an argument with a door. The face in the casket had the same scar in the same place. Two scars. Two faces. One of them mine, one of them also mine.

"Mr. Eze," I said, "when the body came to you, who delivered it?"

"A constable. From the well road." He said *the well road* the way you'd say *the flooded road* or *the bad road* — a small shadow crossing the word and moving on. "There were papers."

"May I see the papers?"

He produced them from his satchel with the reluctance of a man handing over a knife. The death certificate was folded in thirds, official paper, official stamp. I read it standing in my mother's front room with my own face watching me from the box.

Name of deceased: **Ada Crane**.

"That's my name," I said.

"That's the name on the certificate," Mr. Eze agreed, which is the kind of sentence that sounds like agreement and is actually a border crossing.

"Mr. Eze. I am standing in front of you. I am Ada Crane. I am, as far as I can establish, alive."

"Then it's a clerical error," he said comfortably. "Happens all the time. The family will correct it at the registry. You'll need her birth certificate, your own identification, a small fee — "

"Mr. Eze, the certificate says Ada Crane died. It gives a date. It gives a cause. It says *drowning*."

He looked at me for a long moment then, and something moved behind his face — not fear exactly, but the neighbor of fear, the cousin who shows up at funerals and eats too much. "Yes," he said. "Drowning. At the well." He began packing his instruments with great attention. "I'd leave the lid open a while, if I were you," he added, not looking at me. "Sometimes the face settles. Sometimes what you see at first isn't what's there by morning."

"Is that a professional observation?"

"It's a family one," he said, and left.

* * *

I did not sleep that night, or rather I slept the way you sleep on a train, in installments, each one ending with a small jolt. Somewhere past two in the morning I got up, found a lamp, and sat in the kitchen with my mother's old leather notebook — the one I'd taken from her things years ago, the one I carry the way other people carry rosaries — and I began to do the thing she taught me without ever teaching me, which is to write down what is true before anyone can tell you what happened instead.

I made a list. Lists are prayers for people who can't kneel.

**Discrepancies, Night One:**

1. The face in the casket is my face. Scar, jaw, the left ear that sticks out — all of it. Mirabel's ear did not stick out. I have photographs. I will find the photographs.
2. The death certificate reads Ada Crane. Date of death: the fourteenth. That is three days ago. I was alive on the fourteenth. I was on a train, complaining about the air conditioning, into my recorder, at length. There is a recording.
3. Cause of death: drowning. Location: the well. There is no well on this land. There hasn't been since I was a child. There is a paddock, and a dry depression at the far fence where the ground sinks, and Ngozi told us once never to play there, and when we asked why, she said *because I said so*, which in our family is a complete theological system.
4. Mr. Eze said the body came from the well road with a constable. I will ask which constable. I will ask everyone. I am aware that this is how horror stories start. I am choosing to proceed anyway, because the alternative is to sit in this kitchen until the candle burns down, listening to my own face breathe in the next room.

That last line I wrote and then sat looking at for a while. *Listening to my own face breathe in the next room.* It was meant as a figure of speech. It was two in the morning and the house was making its usual noises — the mango tree, the tin roof ticking as it cooled, the small industrious sounds of geckos — and beneath all of it, or through all of it, or possibly nowhere at all, I thought I could hear something that was not a gecko and not the roof and not the tree.

Water. The sound of water. A long way off, and then not so far off, and then gone, like a door closing on a bathroom in a house where nobody has run a tap in thirty years.

I wrote: *5. The house makes a sound like water where there is no water. I am choosing to note this and not interpret it. Interpretation is for people who have slept.*

* * *

In the morning the face in the casket was still my face. It had not settled into Mirabel. It had, if anything, settled further into me — the disappointment deepening overnight, as though the dead man in the box had spent the night reviewing my life and found it wanting in specific, itemizable ways. I stood over him — over *her*, over *it*, over the problem — and I said, out loud, because the recorder was running and I have never in my life passed up a chance to narrate:

"Whoever you are, you're wearing my face badly. Mirabel smiled more. Mirabel was kind. Mirabel would never have been caught dead in this shirt."

It was a joke. I want that on the record: it was a joke, and jokes are how the Crane men say the unsayable, and I said it and immediately wished I hadn't, because the silence that followed it had a texture. Not empty. *Attentive.* The way a room is attentive when someone in it has just decided something about you.

Ngozi found me there an hour later, standing over the casket with the notebook open on my palm like a small dark bird.

"You should burn that," she said.

"The notebook?"

"The notebook. The questions. All of it." She was looking at the casket and not at me, and her hands were doing something I had never seen them do, which is nothing. Ngozi's hands are always working — shelling, sorting, scolding. Now they hung at her sides like tools put down in a hurry. "Some things are written down so they can be finished," she said. "Not so they can be *read*."

"Aunt Ngozi. The certificate has my name on it. The body has my face on it. Somebody in this family is going to have to explain to me, slowly, with small words, why I appear to be dead."

She turned and looked at me then, and my aunt — who raised me half the time, who taught me to gut a fish and win an argument — my aunt looked at me with such plain, exhausted sorrow that for one moment I forgot the casket entirely.

"Mirabel died years ago, Ada," she said. "At the well. You know this. Everyone knows this. There's a grave."

"Then who," I asked, "is in the box?"

Ngozi looked into the casket. The morning light came through the window and lay across my own dead face, and my aunt looked at it for a long, long time, and then she said the sentence that I have since transcribed into the notebook and underlined twice and read aloud to an empty room at three in the morning, the sentence that started everything:

"Whoever it is," she said quietly, "the well sent him back wearing the wrong name. And it will want to correct that."

Outside, faint and unmistakable in the dry morning air, I heard water.
