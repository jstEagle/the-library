## 4. First Recording

The exhaustion came on like weather.

Not the ordinary tiredness of grief and travel, though there was plenty of that, but something older and more deliberate, as if sleep itself had decided to keep office hours elsewhere. Ada would lie down at eleven, bone-tired, eyes stinging, and by two he'd be staring at the ceiling with the peculiar clarity of a man who has been awake for years rather than hours. His body ached in the way bodies ache before fever. His thoughts moved through him like fish through dark water—visible for a moment, then gone.

He tried everything his mother had taught him about rest, which was not much; she had treated sleep the way she treated most things, as an activity requiring documentation. *Slept badly,* her diaries said, night after night, decade after decade. *Heard water.* Ada had assumed it was insomnia. Now, lying in the narrow bed of his childhood room with the house ticking around him, he was beginning to suspect his mother had never once slept well in this building, and had simply been honest about it in a way the rest of the family was not.

On the third night of it, he gave up on sleeping and did what he always did when the world refused to make sense: he reached for the recorder.

It was a small thing, a battered Olympus he'd owned since university, held together with tape and habit. He had recorded lectures with it, interviews, the last coherent conversation with Mirabel over a bad phone line two months before her death—*you sound far away, Ada, are you eating?* He kept the file. He could not bring himself to listen to it, but he kept it, the way you keep a key to a door you're afraid to open.

He set it on the bedside table, pressed record, and spoke into the dark.

"Recording note. It is—" he checked his watch "—2:47 a.m., Thursday. Subject: myself. Complaint: inability to sleep, third consecutive night. Ambient conditions: quiet. No wind. No water sounds tonight, which I note because I have begun noting things whether I want to or not."

His voice sounded thin in the room. He almost laughed at himself—a grown man, narrating his own insomnia like a nature documentary—but the laugh died somewhere in his chest. The house was very still. Stillness in this house had a quality he was learning to distrust: not empty, but attentive.

"Question for the record," he said, because saying it aloud felt safer than thinking it. "Why does everyone in this village talk about the well like it's a person? And why—" he hesitated, then made himself finish, "—why do I feel like it's waiting for me specifically?"

Nothing answered, obviously. Houses don't answer. He turned off the lamp, lay down facing the wall, and told himself firmly that he would sleep now, thank you, like a normal person.

The last thing he registered was the red light of the recorder, still burning in the corner of his eye like a small patient star.

* * *

He woke at dawn with the specific, filthy exhaustion of having slept deeply and wrongly, the way you sleep on a train or in a hospital chair. His mouth tasted of iron. There was a faint ache in his jaw, the kind you get from clenching.

And the recorder's memory card was nearly full.

He sat up slowly. He had recorded perhaps four minutes of himself muttering. The device said otherwise: one continuous file, six hours and twenty-two minutes long.

Ada looked at it for a long time. Then he did the only sensible thing, which was to make tea first, because whatever lived inside that file deserved a man who had already had his tea. He put the kettle on, watched it like it might also betray him, carried the mug back upstairs, and sat on the edge of the bed with the recorder in both hands.

He pressed play.

For the first several minutes there was nothing but the room breathing—the tick of the house, a dog somewhere down the hill, his own voice at 2:47 delivering its little documentary narration. Then silence, real silence, the silence of a man asleep.

At 3:11 a.m., the breathing changed.

Ada heard himself stop breathing entirely—and then someone else began.

It was his voice. That was the horror of it, the part his mind kept snagging on like a nail: it was unmistakably his voice, his particular flat northern vowels, his habit of swallowing the ends of sentences. But it was coming from a mouth that wasn't moving, because his own mouth, on the recording, was making small involuntary sounds—the wet clicks of a throat being used by someone else, the way a puppet's jaw works from behind.

And the voice was answering questions.

That was the second horror. There were no questions on the recording. Nobody spoke but the voice. But it answered anyway, patiently, thoroughly, in the tone of a man who has been asked the same thing many times and has never once tired of explaining:

"—no, deeper than that. You're thinking of depth wrong. A well isn't deep the way a grave is deep. A grave ends. This one opens."

A pause, long enough that Ada checked the counter. Four minutes of nothing. Then the voice again, warmer now, almost fond:

"The stones go green after the first ten feet. Not moss—something smoother, like the inside of a shell. And below that it's not stone anymore, it's rope. Old rope, all the way down, knotted every arm's length. You can climb it if your hands are steady. Most hands aren't steady."

Ada's tea went cold in his hand. He did not notice.

"The water at the bottom isn't black," the voice continued. "Everyone says black, but they've only seen it from above, and from above everything looks like ending. Up close it's clear. You can see faces in it. Not reflections." A small sound that might have been laughter, or might have been grief wearing laughter's coat. "Faces that were put there. Kept. Like photographs in an album, except the album breathes."

The voice described the walls of the shaft, how they narrowed and then widened, how there was a ledge at forty feet where someone had once carved names—it recited three of them, surnames Ada knew, Crane among them—and how the air below the ledge smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet. It described all of this with the unhurried tenderness of a man giving directions to his childhood home. It knew the number of knots. It knew which knot was loose.

And then, near the end of the file, at 5:58 a.m., the voice said:

"He'll want to know if it hurts. Tell him no. Tell him it's like being remembered. That's the true answer, and he'll believe it, because he's the one who writes things down."

Silence. Then, softer, almost gently, in Ada's own voice:

"Ask me again tomorrow. You always ask again tomorrow."

* * *

Ada played the file four times through. He took notes. His handwriting, he observed distantly, had gone very neat, the way it did when he was frightened and refusing to admit it—his mother's handwriting, the ledger hand, each letter standing at attention.

*3:11 a.m. — onset. Voice = mine (vocal match confirmed). Answers questions not present on recording. Content: interior of well, described in detail beyond any local knowledge I possess.*

*Content includes names carved at 40-ft ledge. Verify against parish records.*

*"You always ask again tomorrow." — implication: this has happened before. To whom? Mother? Check diaries, 1990s entries, insomnia references.*

He wrote these things because writing them made them objects, and objects could be examined, filed, survived. What he did not write down—not yet, not in ink—was the other thing, the thing sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone:

Part of him had not been afraid during the fourth playback. Part of him had leaned toward the speaker, hungry, the way you lean toward a fire in winter. Because whoever the voice was, it *knew*. It knew the well from the inside, which meant it knew what had happened to Mirabel, or to whoever wore Mirabel's face in that casket, or to every Crane who had ever gone down those knotted ropes. It was the closest thing to a witness the family had ever produced, and it was speaking directly to him, in his own voice, patient as a teacher, offering—

Offering what? Answers. Communion. The terrible intimacy of being understood by something that lived where his sister had died.

Ada stopped writing. In the quiet of the morning room he became aware of two facts simultaneously, and they did not fit together, and he let them sit side by side anyway, like twins:

He should burn the recorder.

He needed to record again tonight.

He thought of Ngozi's face when she'd begged him to stop asking questions—fear, yes, but under the fear something else, something that looked horribly like recognition, like a woman watching a younger relative develop the family illness. He thought of his mother's diaries: *slept badly, heard water*, fifty years of nights, and had she talked in her sleep too? Had some version of this voice kept her company in the dark, describing the green stones and the knotted ropes, answering questions she'd never dared ask awake?

Had she *asked again tomorrow*?

Ada looked at the recorder lying on the quilt, small and dumb and faithful, its little screen glowing with the evidence. Six hours and twenty-two minutes. Somewhere in the middle of the night, while his body lay clenched and dreaming, something had borrowed his mouth and given a guided tour of hell with the warmth of a man showing off a garden.

He picked up the notebook and wrote one final line beneath his notes, pressing hard enough to emboss the next three pages:

*It wants to be interviewed. God help me—I think I do too.*

Then he checked the batteries, laid the recorder beside his pillow, and spent the whole bright morning dreading the fall of night.
