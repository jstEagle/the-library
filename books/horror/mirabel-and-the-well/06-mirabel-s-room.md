## 6. Mirabel's Room

The key to Mirabel's room had been lost for twenty years, according to Aunt Ngozi, who said it the way other people said *the moon is made of cheese* — a fact so settled it had become furniture. The door itself sat at the end of the upstairs corridor, painted the same tired green as every other door in the house, except that this one had been painted over. Twice, by the look of it. The frame still showed a ghost of the old color beneath: a child's blue, the blue of a certain kind of sky that only exists in memory and in paint catalogues.

I stood in front of it on my fourth morning home, holding my father's old toolbox, feeling like a burglar in my own inheritance.

"Ada." Ngozi's voice came up the stairs behind me, flat as a laid table. "What are you doing?"

"Fixing the hinge," I said, which was true in the way that a lie is sometimes true — the hinge *was* broken, rusted half-shut, and I did intend to fix it. I just intended to fix it from the inside.

She climbed the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, and looked at me with an expression I was beginning to catalogue the way I catalogued everything else. There were only three expressions left in her repertoire since the funeral: grief, warning, and the terrible third thing which was both at once.

"That room has been shut since before you were born," she said.

"Then nothing in it can hurt me."

"Nothing in it has ever needed to." She said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "Ada, listen to me. Some doors are closed because of what's inside. Some are closed because of what wants to be let out. And some—" she touched the doorframe, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, "—some are closed because of what wants to be let *in*."

"I'm going to open it," I told her. "You can watch or you can go make tea. But either way, I'd like the tea afterward."

Something flickered across her face — almost a smile, quickly drowned. That was the thing about Ngozi; even now, even with everything, the humor got through sometimes like light under a door. It was how I knew there was still someone in there worth saving.

"You sound like your mother," she said, and went downstairs.

That was the kindest and cruelest thing anyone had said to me all week.

* * *

The lock surrendered in eleven minutes. I know because I narrated the whole operation into my recorder — a habit now, God help me, a compulsion — and the timestamp read 11:47 when the mechanism finally gave up its long argument with my screwdriver. Eleven minutes of scraping and swearing and one genuinely impressive moment where the screwdriver slipped and I nearly took out my own eye, all preserved forever on magnetic tape for whatever future historian would eventually have to make sense of me.

The door opened inward with a sigh, the way doors do in houses that have been holding their breath.

Dust, yes. Twenty years of it, grey and soft as felt, laying itself over everything like snowfall in a photograph. A small bed with a quilt folded at its foot. A wooden horse on the windowsill, one ear chewed round by some dog three generations dead. A row of picture books on a shelf, spines faded to pastel ghosts. A child's dress hanging in the wardrobe, yellow, gone cream with age.

Untouched. Perfectly, impossibly untouched. This was the room of a child who had stepped out in 1970-something and simply never come back, and the house had kept her place the way churches keep relics.

Except for two things.

First: flowers. Fresh ones. A jar of marigolds and frangipani on the sill by the wooden horse, petals still firm, stems still green, water in the jar still clear. Someone had been tending this room. Recently. Continually. For years, if the faint ring-mark on the sill was anything to go by — the jar had been lifted and replaced so many times it had worn a little halo into the wood.

Second: the diary.

It lay on the pillow, centered, deliberate, the way you'd leave something for someone to find. Black cover, elastic band, the kind sold in every stationery shop in the country for less than the price of a meal. My stomach did something complicated, because I owned one exactly like it. In fact — I pulled it from my jacket pocket, heart going strange — it was the same brand, same size, same everything.

Mine was maybe a third full. This one was full to bursting.

I sat down on the dusty floor because standing suddenly seemed ambitious, and opened it to the first page.

The handwriting stopped me cold.

It was mine.

Not similar. Not *a hand like mine*. Mine — the slight forward lean, the way my 'g' descenders loop too far, the little tick I put over my i's because a schoolteacher named Mrs. Adeyemi beat the dots out of us in Primary Four and I never unlearned it. Every page, every line, every date.

And the dates. That was the second cold shock, arriving while I was still processing the first. The diary began three days ago — the day I arrived home — and ran forward. Page after page dated into next week. Next week, which had not happened yet, described in my own hand in my own voice with my own irritating habit of starting entries mid-thought, as though the previous sentence were still ringing in the air.

*—couldn't sleep again. The well sounds like it's directly under the house now. Ngozi made jollof rice and pretended everything was normal, which is her love language and her defense mechanism and possibly the same thing.*

I laughed. I actually laughed, alone in a sealed room, reading my own handwriting describe a meal that hadn't been cooked yet. The laugh came out wrong, high and thin, and died in the dust.

I turned pages faster.

*Tuesday. Interviewed Mr. Eze about 1998. He cried. I didn't expect that. I keep expecting the village to be villains and they keep being people, which is worse.*

*Wednesday. Found Mother's ledgers. I don't want to write what's in them but I will, because that's the whole point of me. Names. So many names. All twins.*

My hands were shaking. I checked the dates against my phone twice, three times, as if the phone might confess to lying. No. The entries ran ahead of me like footprints in snow leading into a forest I hadn't entered yet.

And then the last entry.

The last entry was dated the night after tomorrow. The night of the wake.

*The wake goes wrong. I don't know how to say it more kindly than that. The cousins get out — nobody watches them, everyone is crying or arguing or drinking, and children are water, they always find the lowest ground. They reach the well. There is fire — I don't understand the fire yet, I've written this entry before I understand it, which is a sentence I never thought I'd compose — and someone is screaming my name. Screaming it like it's a warning and a prayer at the same time.*

*I have read this entry four times now, sitting on the floor of your room, Mirabel, waiting for you to explain yourself. You never do. You never did, even when you were alive and I could reach you by just shouting through the wall.*

*One more thing, and then I'll stop writing in futures: whoever is keeping this room — changing the flowers, keeping the dust off the horse — I know it's you. I don't know how. I don't know what you are now. But the marigolds are fresh and the water is clean and somebody loves this room enough to tend it, and I choose to believe that somebody is my sister.*

*If I'm wrong, please don't tell me tonight. Let me have one more day of being right.*

The entry ended there. The remaining pages were blank.

I sat for a long time. Somewhere below, Ngozi was making tea whether I wanted it or not, and the smell of it climbed the stairs like a small brave animal checking on me. Outside, absurdly, a bird sang. The world continued to be the world, indifferent and green and ordinary, and I found that obscene.

Because here is what I understood, sitting in the dust of a room that should not exist: the diary wasn't prophecy. Prophecy is a gift. This was something else. This was *documentation*. My own compulsion, my own need to record everything, turned backward — aimed not at the past but ahead, writing down events as they happened somewhere slightly to the left of now. Time in this house didn't move in a line. It moved the way water moves in a well: down, around, returning to itself.

And the well was listening. The well had always been listening. Maybe the well was the one holding the pen.

No. I rejected that immediately, fiercely. The handwriting was mine. Whatever force filled these pages, it used *my* hand, *my* jokes, *my* grief. Which meant the future in this book wasn't fixed. It was drafted. A first draft. And I had spent my whole life learning that drafts could be revised.

I closed the diary and held it against my chest and became aware, gradually, of a fourth presence in the room besides me, the dust, and the dead girl's kept place. It wasn't frightening, exactly. It was attentive. The particular attention of someone listening through a wall.

"The marigolds," I said aloud, to the empty room, feeling like an idiot and doing it anyway. "They're lovely. She always liked yellow."

The silence changed quality. I can't describe it better than that. Silence has textures the way fabric does, and this one went from cotton to silk, from absence to *presence*, warm and close and unbearably sad.

On the windowsill, in a room sealed for twenty years, the surface of the water in the jar trembled — once, twice — rings spreading outward from the center, as if something far below had dropped a stone.

Or as if something far below had answered.

I took the diary downstairs. At the bottom of the stairs, Ngozi stood with two cups of tea, and when she saw my face she set them both down on the hall table very carefully, as if her hands could no longer be trusted with hot things.

"You went in," she said.

"I went in." I held up the diary. "Auntie. Who changed the flowers?"

She looked at the black book for a long moment. Then at me. Then past me, up the stairs, toward the green door standing open at the end of the corridor, and her face did the third thing — the grief-warning thing — and then broke past it entirely into something I had no category for.

"Every week," she whispered, "for thirty years, I have bought marigolds and told myself it was for a grave." Her voice cracked clean through. "Ada, I stopped buying them last month. I couldn't afford the lie anymore. I haven't been near that room since."

The tea went cold on the hall table. Neither of us touched it.

Upstairs, faintly, through the floor, we heard the jar being refilled.
