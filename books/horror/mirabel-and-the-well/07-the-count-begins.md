## 7. The Count Begins

The relatives began arriving on Thursday, in the way that water begins to arrive before a flood—first a trickle, then a rumor, then everything at once.

Ada heard the first car before he saw it, tires grinding the red laterite drive outside the homestead, and by the time he reached the veranda his aunt Ngozi was already halfway down the steps with her arms open wide, crying out greetings to a family that had scattered itself across three countries and was now converging on this house like iron filings toward a magnet nobody could see.

"Ada! Ada, come and greet your people!" she called over her shoulder, and there was something in her voice he hadn't heard before—a brightness stretched too thin, like a drumhead pulled past its proper tension.

The first arrivals were Uncle Emeka and his wife from Enugu, loud and warm and smelling of long travel and fried plantain. Then a busload of cousins from Lagos, then a pair of great-aunts who remembered Ada's grandfather and had apparently never forgiven him for something none of them would name. By nightfall the compound was full of voices, full of cooking smoke, full of children running between legs and around the old rain barrel, and Ada stood in the middle of it all with his recorder in his pocket and felt like a man documenting his own drowning.

He recorded anyway. He always recorded.

*Thursday, 6:40 p.m. Forty-one relatives present and counting. The wake begins tomorrow at sundown. Everyone is laughing. Nobody will look at the paddock.*

* * *

From the veranda, Chidera watched her cousin Ada stand at the edge of the yard doing nothing, which was what he did most of the time, according to her mother, who said it not unkindly but often.

Chidera was ten years old and had arrived that afternoon with her twin brother Chidi, both of them deposited in the village for the funeral like parcels their parents couldn't store in the city. She didn't mind. Village funerals meant staying up late, and staying up late meant hearing the grown-ups say things they'd never say in daylight.

"Your cousin is strange," Chidi whispered to her now, mouth full of puff-puff. "He keeps writing in that book."

"He's a documentarian," Chidera said, because she'd asked her mother the same question in the car and been given the same word. "It means he writes down true things."

"What's strange about that?"

Chidera considered this. "I don't know," she admitted. "Maybe nothing."

But she looked at Ada again—at the way he stood so still, facing the far corner of the compound where the old paddock sat behind its collapsed fence—and she thought: *he's listening.* And then she thought, without knowing why, that she was listening too. That she had been listening since they crossed the village boundary, to something underneath all the other sounds. Something like water being poured very slowly into a very deep cup.

She decided not to mention it. Some true things are better left unwritten.

* * *

That night, the sound came again.

Ada had taken to sleeping with two recorders now—one beside the bed, one at the window facing the back of the property—and when he played back the night's tape in the grey of early morning, he heard what he had half-hoped and half-dreaded to hear.

Water.

Not rain—the sky had been dry as chalk for a week. Not the tap, not the gutters. A low, patient pouring sound, the sound of a great quantity of liquid moving somewhere below the level of the earth, and beneath it, fainter than breath, a rhythm like someone counting.

He took the recorder outside and walked toward the paddock, holding the microphone up like a lantern. The sound grew louder with every step. At the broken fence it was unmistakable: rushing, filling, *rising*. He put his hand flat against the earth.

The ground was bone dry. It had been bone dry for weeks. But under his palm, through the soles of his shoes, through the roots of the guava tree at the fence line, he could feel it—that slow hydraulic pulse, the well drawing up whatever it drew from, preparing itself the way a throat prepares to swallow.

He checked the ledger against his knee in the grey light. His mother's handwriting, steady across forty years:

*One twin per generation. Offered, not taken. The count does not err.*

And beneath it, in fresher ink, his own addition from three nights ago:

*If no adult offers, it will choose. It has never had to choose before.*

He had written that sentence himself and still did not know how he knew it. That was the worst part. The knowledge arrived in him the way the sound arrived in the recordings—from somewhere below, seeping upward through ground he had thought solid.

* * *

Ngozi woke on Friday morning knowing two things: that the wake was tonight, and that she had slept badly because of water.

There was no water near her room. There had been no rain. But she had dreamed all night of a sound she knew intimately, a sound she had last heard standing at the lip of the well in 1972, holding her brother's hand while he told her not to cry, told her it was an honor, told her—this was the part that still made her furious after fifty years—told her to be happy about it.

She found Ada on the veranda with his notebook, looking like a man who had aged ten years in ten days.

"The children," she said, without greeting. "Where are the twins? Chidera and Chidi."

"Asleep. Why?"

"When did you last see them?"

Ada's pen stopped moving. "Last night. At dinner. Chidi ate six pieces of chicken and Chidera told everyone my recorder was haunted." He almost smiled. "Why, Auntie?"

Ngozi sat down heavily beside him. Her hands, usually so busy—shelling, stirring, scolding—lay folded and still.

"Because I sleepwalked once," she said quietly. "In 1972. The month before my brother went down. Every night for nine nights, Mama found me standing at the paddock fence, asleep, facing the well. Nine nights. And on the tenth day, Obi offered himself, and I never walked again." She looked at Ada, and her eyes were terrible with an old arithmetic. "The children have been here four days, Ada. Ask them if they've been sleeping well."

* * *

They asked at breakfast, gently, the way you ask children anything you're afraid to know the answer to.

"I sleep fine," Chidi said, reaching for the bread.

"You talk in your sleep though," Chidera said. "You've been saying numbers."

The table went quiet. Uncle Emeka lowered his newspaper an inch.

"What kind of numbers?" Ada asked.

"Just numbers. Downward." Chidera frowned, trying to remember. "Like when you count backward before a rocket launches. But slower. And last night—" she hesitated, glancing at her brother, who had suddenly become very interested in his bread, "—last night I woke up and the door was open and Chidi wasn't in bed. I found him in the yard. He was just standing there, at the fence, looking at the paddock. I brought him back and he didn't remember any of it. He does it every night, actually. I've been walking him back every night." She said this with the mild indignation of a girl whose duties had gone unappreciated. "His feet are always red in the morning. From the dirt."

Every night. Four nights.

Ada looked at Ngozi. Ngozi looked at her plate. Around the table, the older relatives had developed a sudden collective fascination with their food, their tea, the middle distance—the practiced blindness of people who had grown up in this house and knew exactly what was being discussed and had agreed, decades ago, never to discuss it.

"Nine nights," Ngozi said softly, to no one, to the plate. "We have until tomorrow sundown. Maybe less."

* * *

That afternoon, Ada did something he had not done since he was a boy: he went to church.

Not to pray, precisely—or not only. The village church was the one building in town with a locked archive, and Father Damian, a small round man with the gentle humor of someone who had buried half his congregation twice over, owed Ada's mother a debt of sentiment he was happy to convert into access.

"Baptismal records?" he said, unlocking the cabinet. "For the Cranes? My boy, I baptized most of them. Which year?"

"1890," Ada said. "And every generation after. I want the twin entries. All of them."

Father Damian's hands paused on the ledger spines. For a moment his face did something complicated—grief and recognition wrestling briefly before politeness won.

"You've been reading your mother's books," he said.

"Yes."

"Then you know more than I do, God forgive us all." He pulled a volume wrapped in oilcloth and set it down. "I'll tell you what I can tell you, and then I'll tell you what I'm going to do about it, which is nothing, because I am seventy-three years old and a coward, and God and I have an understanding about that." He opened the book with fingers that trembled only slightly. "Here. 1890. Twins, born to Ezekiel Crane. One baptized. One—" he turned the page, and there it was, a small notation in faded sepia, "*received by the water*"—and Ada felt the floor tilt gently beneath his chair.

Every twenty-five years. Every generation. Baptized twin, and a second entry, always the same phrase, always in a different priest's hand, as though each man had discovered the arrangement fresh and recorded it the way you record weather.

"Received by the water," Ada read aloud. "That's all anyone ever wrote."

"That's all anyone ever dared." Father Damian closed the book. "Your mother used to sit where you're sitting, you know. Thirty years ago. Asking the same questions. I gave her the same book." He smiled, and it was a warm smile, genuinely warm, which made it worse. "She thanked me. Then she went home and paid the debt, and I rang the bells on Sunday and pretended the Crane family was simply unlucky in childbirth. We are all keepers of records here, Ada. The difference between you and me is that you haven't learned yet when to stop keeping them."

"I learned when to stop," Ada said, standing. "I just decided not to."

* * *

Night fell early, the way it does before a storm that hasn't decided to happen yet. The compound filled with kerosene lamps and laughter and the smell of pepper soup, because a wake is a wake even when it's a countdown, and grief and celebration in this family had always shared a pot.

Ada moved through the crowd recording everything—names, faces, the exact pitch of every laugh—because if the well wanted a complete record, then by God it would get one, and he would make sure the record included every single person the well was threatening. Let it listen to what it was asking for. Let it hear Chidi's laugh, high and cracked and ten years old.

At midnight, the house went quiet. At 12:40, Ada heard the screen door sigh.

He was in the hallway before he fully woke, heart slamming, and caught them at the gate—both of them, hand in hand, barefoot, eyes open and utterly empty, walking with the unhurried certainty of sleepers following a sound only they could hear. Chidera too. Both twins. The well had stopped waiting for one.

"No," Ada said aloud, stupidly, uselessly, and grabbed them both by the shoulders, and they turned their heads to look at him with faces that were not asleep and not awake, and Chidi said, in a voice pitched low and wet and wrong, in perfect unison with his sister:

"Tomorrow. When the singing stops. Count with us."

Then their eyes cleared, and Chidera blinked up at him and said, "Uncle Ada? Why are we outside?" and began to cry, and he held them both in the dark while behind the paddock fence, beneath the dry earth, the water rose another inch, patient as a held breath, one night from full.
