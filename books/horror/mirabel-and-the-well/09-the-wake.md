## 9. The Wake

### Ada

The wake began the way all Crane gatherings began: with too much food and not enough honesty.

Aunt Beatrice had driven up from Enugu with a cooler of pepper soup she insisted on reheating in Mama's old kitchen, filling the house with the smell of scotch bonnet and dried fish until, for one merciful hour, it smelled like a home instead of a held breath. Cousins I hadn't seen since childhood filled every chair. Someone had brought palm wine. Someone else had brought a generator, because the light company, as ever, was mourning its own death.

I set my recorder on the sideboard between the jollof rice and the funeral rice, which were technically the same rice but had been prepared by two women who no longer spoke.

"You're recording a funeral?" asked Chuka, a cousin whose face I remembered mostly from Christmas cards.

"I record everything," I said. "Occupational hazard."

"Documentary man," he said knowingly, and helped himself to meat. "My brother-in-law does weddings. Same thing, smaller coffins."

I almost laughed. That was the trick of grief nobody warns you about—it keeps making room for jokes at the worst possible moments, like an uninvited relative who turns out to be good company.

But under the laughter, under the clatter of spoons, I was watching the window. The sun was going down behind the paddock, and the well's sound had been carrying farther each night—water where there is no water, that soft patient lapping, like something turning over in its sleep to check whether we were still here.

Tonight we were still here. Tonight was the count.

* * *

### Ngozi

Ngozi had counted the children twice, and both times she came up two short.

The twins—Ada's little cousins, Emeka and Emenike, ten years old, identical down to the gap in their teeth—had been underfoot all evening, stealing puff-puff and daring each other to touch the casket. Now the sitting room was full of adults and empty of boys.

She found their sandals by the back door, placed neatly side by side, toes pointing toward the paddock.

Her heart did not pound. That was the terrible part. Her heart went quiet and cold, the way it had gone in 1972, when she was nine years old and her brother Obinna had kissed her forehead and said, *You stay, I'll go,* as if he were offering her the larger piece of cake.

She walked out into the blue dark without calling for help. Some things you do not do in front of a crowd.

* * *

### Ada

I noticed Ngozi leave the way you notice weather changing—a pressure, a silence in the conversation where her voice had been. Then Beatrice said, "Where are those boys?" and the room's warmth inverted all at once.

We went out with lanterns and phone torches, a stumbling congregation of relatives, and the sound reached us before the paddock did: water. Lapping. Close enough now that I could hear it through my shoes.

The twins stood at the mouth of the old well, holding hands, barefoot in the wet grass though there had been no rain. They were not sleepwalking exactly. They were *listening*, heads tilted like birds, and when Emeka turned to look at us his eyes were open and calm and absolutely elsewhere.

"It's counting us," he said pleasantly. "It got to ninety-eight."

"Emeka." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Step away from the edge."

"It says we're almost a hundred," said Emenike, and smiled with both gaps in his teeth. "It likes round numbers."

* * *

### Ngozi

Later, people would say Ngozi Crane moved faster than any woman of sixty-three should move, and she would let them say it, because the truth was uglier: she wasn't moving fast. She was moving *late*. She had been late for fifty-two years.

She crossed the paddock and threw herself across the mouth of the well, arms spread, chest down against the cold stones, the way her mother had once thrown herself across a doorway during the war—not to stop the bullet, but to choose where it landed.

The pull came up through the stones like a hand testing fruit at market. Gentle. Appraising. It found the loose grip of her age, the guilt worn smooth as a river stone, the fifty-two years of borrowed time—and it began, patiently, to draw.

"Ngozi!" Ada's voice, close now.

"Don't touch me," she gasped, because she knew—if they pulled her free, the well would simply reach past her for the boys, and the arithmetic would continue. "Listen to me. It can't take what isn't offered. So *nobody offer*—"

The stones hummed beneath her. Cold climbed her arms like water rising in a glass.

*Obinna,* she thought. *I'm coming to argue with you at last.*

* * *

### Ada

I have thought often since about the half-second in which I understood three things at once: that Ngozi meant to be taken; that the well was considering her offer; and that it was going to *refuse*—because I could feel it refusing, the way you feel a handshake end, the interest withdrawing, the attention sliding off her toward the small warm shapes standing behind me.

The well didn't want the spared twin. It wanted the count completed. And Ngozi, God forgive her, was trying to pay a debt with money that had already been spent.

So I did the only thing I knew how to do.

I turned on the recorder, held it high over my head like a lamp, and pressed play.

* * *

### Ngozi

What came out of that little machine, in Ada's flat documentary voice, in the middle of a paddock at night, was the whole rotten inheritance read aloud.

*"Entry, March 1890. Ezekiel Crane records payment to the keeper below: one twin, Tobias, given freely, in exchange for the rains."*

*"Entry, 1914..."*

Name after name. Year after year. Every generation's quiet arithmetic, every mother's ledger, every twin who walked down or was carried or, once—in 1946, a woman named Adaeze who had fought, oh how the entry noted she had fought—given to the water so the rest could eat.

The family stood in the dark listening to themselves. Beatrice sat down in the wet grass. Uncle Somto, who had always said the Cranes were simply *blessed*, made a sound like a hinge.

And Ngozi, pressed against the stones, felt the pull slacken—not release, not yet, but *hesitate*. Because the well, whatever else it was, had never once been told the truth about itself. For a hundred and thirty years it had been fed in whispers. No one had ever read it its own history.

*"Entry, 1972,"* said the recorder, in Ada's voice, and then in a different voice entirely—an old recording, Ngozi's own, from the night before, when she had finally confessed—*"Obinna Crane, aged nine, offered willingly by himself, for his sister Ngozi. He was not afraid. He asked me to write down that he was not afraid."*

The well went silent.

Not quiet—silent. The lapping stopped. The hum stopped. Even the crickets, Ngozi realized, had been gone for some time, and now even the absence of them stopped. The whole night leaned in around the little machine like a congregation around a letter from home.

It was listening.

*Let it listen,* Ngozi thought, tears cutting through the dust on her face. *Let it hear what it is.*

* * *

### Ada

I played everything. The ledgers. Mother's final page with the two names. Mirabel's diary in my handwriting, dated into next week, describing this very night—the fire, someone screaming my name. The 3:11 a.m. sessions, the second voice describing the inside of the well in loving detail, and then, near dawn of the last tape, the voice answering the question I had finally asked it:

*"Who are you?"*

*"You know who I am,"* the recording said, gentle as rain. *"I'm the one Ngozi lost. I keep the count. And I will tell you exactly how an offering works, cousin, because I am so tired, and I want to be replaced—"*

I stopped the tape there. Some doors you show the family; some you hold shut with your body.

The silence afterward was total. Forty relatives standing in a paddock, hearing for the first time the price of every harvest they'd ever eaten. Beatrice was praying aloud, fast and fragmented. Somto was staring at the well as if it might apologize.

And the well—

The well stayed silent. Listening. The way it had listened, I understood suddenly, through every whispered transaction, every hushed name, every generation of us paying it in the dark and pretending by daylight that nothing had been spent. We had never once spoken to it honestly. We had never read it its own account.

Truth, it turned out, was not a weapon. It was a wound—and I was holding it open for everyone to see.

Emeka blinked. The elsewhere went out of his eyes. "Uncle Ada?" he said, small and ordinary and ten years old, and Emenike started to cry, which was the most beautiful sound I heard that year.

* * *

### Ngozi

They got the boys back to the house wrapped in tablecloths, because nobody could find blankets and nobody cared. Ngozi walked last, and at the edge of the paddock she stopped and looked back at the well.

It was silent. Truly silent, for the first time in her life. Not satisfied. Not sated. *Listening*—the way a debtor listens when the ledger is finally laid open on the table between you.

"You've heard everything now," she said to it quietly. "Every name. Every year. What we did, and why, and what it cost. There's nothing left to whisper."

The stones said nothing. But she had the feeling—unreasonable, unshakeable—that it was waiting for the rest of the sentence. For the account to be *closed*, not merely read.

Behind her, Ada stood at the paddock's edge with the recorder still in his hand, looking at the well the way a man looks at a door he has already decided to walk through.

"Go inside, Auntie," he said gently. "Get warm."

"And you?"

He smiled, and it was Mama's smile, and it frightened her more than the well had.

"I have one more entry to make," he said. "Dawn's in four hours. I want to get the light right."

Ngozi stood in the cold a moment longer, watching him walk back toward the house with his tapes and his ledgers and his terrible, beautiful patience—and she understood, with the certainty of a woman who had been spared once already, exactly what he meant to give the well at sunrise.

She ran after him anyway.
