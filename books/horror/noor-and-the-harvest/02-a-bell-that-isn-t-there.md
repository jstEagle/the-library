## 2. A Bell That Isn't There

The bell rang at dusk, and Ivo Reyes was counting doors when it happened.

Fourteen on the east corridor, fourteen on the west — the number never changed, but the counting did something to the hallway that merely looking at it could not. It settled the lights. It told the building, in whatever language buildings understood, that someone was paying attention. Mrs. Adeyemi had gone quiet around six, her backwards count finally resting at eleven, and Ivo had taken the silence as mercy until the sound came rolling down from the hill like a stone dropped into still water.

*One.*

Ivo's hand stopped on the doorframe of room nine. The brass was cool under their palm. They stood in the amber wash of the corridor lights — always on, always warm, the kind of light that made even dying look gentle — and they listened.

*Two.*

The tone was deep and bronze and wrong in a way Ivo couldn't immediately name, the way a familiar face is wrong when you meet it out of context. It took three more rings before the wrongness resolved itself into a fact so old and so obvious that Ivo felt foolish for not seeing it at once.

St. Alder's had no bell.

Hadn't for over a hundred years. The tower stood on the hill above the village green like a broken tooth — roofless, open to the sky, its arches filled with pigeons and ivy and the slow work of weather. Every child in Hollowmere learned the story before they learned their times tables: in 1911 the parish had fallen on hard times, or fallen out with the diocese — accounts differed — and the great bell of St. Alder's had been lowered from its yoke, hauled down the hill on an ox cart, and melted at the foundry in Ashcombe. The metal had gone into church bells elsewhere, people said, into smaller parishes that could pay. Some said it went into munitions, though that was a war too early. The tower had been left as it was because leaving things as they were was what Hollowmere did best.

And yet.

*Five.*

Ivo walked to the window at the end of the corridor, the one that looked north across the kitchen garden toward the hill. The dusk was doing its October work, pulling the color out of the fields in long grey strokes, and against that greying sky the tower stood black and open-arched, empty as a ribcage.

The sound came from inside it. Not near it. *Inside* it — Ivo was certain of that in the way you are certain of certain things, without evidence, the way Mrs. Adeyemi had been certain at two in the morning that someone was standing at the foot of her bed counting her breaths. The bell tolled from the hollow place where a bell should hang, and there was nothing there to toll.

*Seven.*

Down the corridor, a door opened. Ivo turned to see old Mr. Fenwick standing in his doorway in his dressing gown, one hand on the frame, his head tilted like a bird's. He was ninety-one and mostly deaf — he'd needed the television at full volume for years — and he was listening.

"Fenwick," Ivo said gently. "It's cold in the hall. Let me get you back to bed."

He didn't look at them. His eyes were fixed on the window, on the hill, and his mouth moved once, silently, shaping a word Ivo couldn't catch. Then the bell rang again — *eight* — and his lips pressed together, and he let himself be steered back into his room like a man returning to a chair he'd been sitting in for a very long time.

Ivo tucked him in. Checked his pulse. Wrote the time in the log: *8:14 p.m., patient ambulatory, agitated by —*

By what? Ivo sat with the pen hovering. The chart paper waited, white and patient. In the end they wrote *agitated by noise,* which was true, and left out everything else, which was also true, and both truths together made a lie, and Ivo was beginning to understand that Hollowmere ran on exactly that arithmetic.

* * *

The night staff gathered in the kitchen at half past, the way they always did, for tea and the handover. Tam was there already, sleeves rolled, scrubbing the big pot from supper even though it was clean — Tam scrubbed things when his mind was loud, and Ivo had learned to read the state of his inner weather by the squeak of the sponge. Sister Brigid sat by the stove with her knitting, needles clicking, though she hadn't produced a finished anything in the four years Ivo had known her. The knitting was a rosary she could do with her hands.

"Did you hear it?" Ivo asked.

Tam's sponge stopped. Brigid's needles didn't; if anything they clicked faster, small and bright and deliberate, like a woman walking quickly past something she doesn't want to look at.

"Hear what?" Tam said.

"The bell."

"There's no bell." He said it evenly, without looking up, and that was the thing — the evenness. Tam lied badly about everything else. He lied about how much sugar he took and whether he'd slept and whether the foxes had got at the bins, and every lie arrived wrapped in guilt so thick you could smell it. This lie came out smooth as river stone. Practiced.

"I heard it," Ivo said. "From the tower. Eleven rings, maybe twelve. St. Alder's hasn't had a bell since—"

"Since 1911," Brigid said, to her knitting. "Everyone knows."

"Then what did I hear?"

The kettle chose that moment to climb toward its whistle, a thin rising note that filled the pause the way sand fills a footprint. Tam turned off the hob before it whistled. He poured the tea. He handed Ivo a cup with both hands, the way he did, and said, "Dusk is strange here. Sound carries off the hills. Could've been the quarry at Marlowe Bend — they blast sometimes at closing."

"They don't blast at closing," Ivo said. "And it wasn't a blast. It was a bell. It rang twelve times. I counted."

Something moved through the kitchen then — not a draft, though the door was shut. More like a settling, the way a house settles, the way a congregation settles. Brigid put down her knitting. She looked at Ivo for a long moment with those pale eyes that seemed always to be reading a document just over your shoulder.

"You counted," she repeated.

"I always count."

"Yes," she said softly. "You do." And then, louder, brisker, in the voice she used for changing the subject at parish meetings she no longer attended: "Mrs. Adeyemi's count — where did it rest?"

"Eleven. It's been at eleven for two nights."

Brigid nodded slowly, and picked her knitting back up, and said nothing else, and the conversation closed over the bell like water over a dropped coin. But Ivo stood there holding the tea and understood, with a cold spreading certainty, that they had just watched two people agree not to have a conversation. That the agreement was old. That it had a shape worn into it, like a path worn across grass by decades of feet all choosing the same detour.

* * *

Ivo walked home the long way, past the green, because the long way passed under the tower.

This was not courage. Ivo wants to say now that it was diligence — a nurse checks the source of a symptom — but honesty compels the record to show it was closer to compulsion, the same engine that counted doors and kept lights burning and checked Mrs. Adeyemi's locks twice and then a third time knowing the second check had been sufficient. There is a kind of fear that can only be answered by walking toward it. Ivo had lived inside that fear so long it had become furniture.

The village at dusk was performing normalcy with tremendous effort. At the Hartley house, windows glowed yellow and the television murmured; through the lace curtain Ivo could see the whole family arranged in the front room, not watching it. Old Mrs. Calloway stood at her gate, ostensibly taking in the milk bottles, actually facing the hill. Two men Ivo knew by sight — the Postlethwaites, father and son, farmers going back further than anyone could say — stood talking by the war memorial, and their talk had the cadence of men reciting rather than conversing, words exchanged as a cover for the fact that both were listening with their entire bodies.

Nobody spoke about the bell. Nobody was speaking about the bell, anywhere, and the silence had texture. It had the texture of a held breath held so long the holders had forgotten they were holding it.

The path up to St. Alder's was chalky and steep, bordered by yews that the parish had stopped trimming sometime in the previous century. Ivo climbed it with their coat collar up and their breath smoking, and the higher they went, the more the evening smelled of cold iron, which was absurd, because there was no iron up there. There was no anything up there. That was the whole point of the story everyone knew.

The tower rose against the last bruised band of sunset. Up close it was worse than it was from below — the open arches gaped, the ivy hung like torn curtains, and the floor of the belfry was visible through them, bare stone, dust, pigeon filth, and absolutely nothing else. No beam. No yoke. No mounting bolts. Whatever the bell had hung from had been stripped away with the bell itself, and the emptiness up there was total and architectural, an emptiness built on purpose.

Ivo stood in the doorway of the ruined nave and waited.

The sun went down. The colour drained. A rook crossed the sky and said one coarse word and was gone. The cold came up through Ivo's boots from stones laid down eight hundred years ago, and the tower stood over them, hollow and dark and silent as a pulled tooth.

Then, at full dark, it rang.

Not from the belfry. From *below.* Ivo felt it before they heard it — a shudder in the ground, in the bones of the feet, in the teeth — and then the sound arrived, vast and bronze, swelling up out of the earth beneath the tower as though the tower itself were a bell's mouth and something underneath were ringing it from within. Dust sifted from the arches. The rooks exploded off the hill in a black ragged sheet. One ring only, enormous, and it went on longer than any ring has a right to go on, decaying so slowly that Ivo stood through all of it, heart slamming, hands pressed flat against the cold wall as if bracing for a wave.

When it faded, the silence afterward was different from the silence before. Before, it had been empty. After, it was *attentive.*

Ivo became aware, gradually and then all at once, that they were not alone on the hill.

They were spread along the path and at the edges of the churchyard — figures in coats and dressing gowns and wellingtons, a dozen of them, maybe more, villagers who had climbed the hill after dark the way Ivo had, drawn by the same gravity. Mrs. Calloway. Both Postlethwaites. The vicar's widow, Agnes Dunn, who hadn't set foot in the churchyard since her husband died. Young Danny Fenn with his dog pressed trembling against his leg. They stood in a loose ring around the tower, and none of them spoke, and none of them looked at each other, and every one of them was pretending, with all their might, to be somewhere else — out for air, checking a fence, unable to sleep — while standing together in the dark at the site of the impossible thing they had all heard.

A bell that wasn't there had rung, and the whole village had come to stand beneath it and agree, silently, mutually, that nothing was happening.

That was when Ivo understood the shape of the thing. Not the bell. The pretence. It was communal. It was *maintained.* You could hear the maintenance in it, the way you can hear the maintenance in a choir — dozens of separate voices finding one pitch. Everyone in Hollowmere had heard the bell. Everyone in Hollowmere had decided, long ago, in some year Ivo had not been present for, that hearing it would be a private matter. That acknowledging it would be a breach. That the price of living in this valley was carrying this sound alone.

How long? Ivo wondered. How many dusks? Since 1911? Since before?

Danny Fenn's dog began to howl, a thin hopeless sound, and Danny hushed it roughly, and the hush was the first spoken word anyone had offered, and it broke the ring. The figures stirred, remembered themselves, remembered their covers — air, fences, insomnia — and began to drift away down the path, each alone, each careful not to walk beside anyone else. Within minutes Ivo stood in the churchyard entirely alone except for Mrs. Calloway, who was slowest, and who paused at the lychgate and looked back.

For a moment the old woman's face was unguarded in the dark, and what was on it was not fear. It was weariness — the profound, muscular weariness of someone who has carried a heavy thing up and down this hill for seventy years and sees no end to the stairs.

"You'll want to ask questions," she said. Her voice was dry as the chalk path. "Everyone new does. First bell they hear, they want to ask."

"What should I do instead?" Ivo asked.

Mrs. Calloway considered them. Behind her the tower stood open to the stars, empty, and Ivo would have sworn — would swear it now, would write it in any ledger that would take the entry — that the emptiness up there was *leaning* toward them slightly, the way a listener leans.

"Do what we do," she said. "Count your doors. Keep your lights on. Be kind to the dying — they know the schedule better than we do." She gripped the lychgate, knuckles bloodless. "And when it rings again tomorrow, be somewhere with other people, and talk loudly about the weather."

"It rang tonight," Ivo said. "Why tonight? What changed?"

Mrs. Calloway's face did something complicated and closed, a door shutting behind her eyes.

"Nothing changed, dear," she said, which was the smoothest lie Ivo had heard all night, smoother even than Tam's, and therefore the most frightening, because it meant the practice went back generations. She turned and went down the hill, slow step by slow step, and Ivo stood alone in the churchyard of a church with no bell, under a tower with nothing in it, listening to the attentive silence and understanding that they had just been given instructions for living inside a secret.

At home, Ivo locked the door. Counted the locks twice. Counted the windows — five — and the rooms — four — and then stood in the kitchen with the light on and wrote in the notebook they kept for the hospice counts:

*Dusk. Bell. Twelve from above, one from below. Whole village heard it. Whole village agreed not to.*

Below that, after a long time, they added:

*Mrs. Adeyemi stuck at eleven. Ask her what comes after eleven.*

Because that was the thought that had followed them home like a stray, the one that wouldn't be put out: the dying counted *backwards*, down toward zero, down toward something arriving. And a bell that shouldn't exist had begun to ring. And Ivo Reyes, who had spent years learning the language of the dying, could not shake the certainty that these were the same sentence, spoken in two voices —

and that the village had heard the second voice answer the first,

and had decided, together, a very long time ago,

to pretend it hadn't.
