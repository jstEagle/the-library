## 15. Hollowmere Counts Its Dead

The bell did not stop at dawn.

Ivo Reyes stood at the hospice window with a cup of tea gone cold in their hands and watched the light come up over Hollowmere the way it always did — grey, then gold, then the long flat white of an English morning — and through all of it the bell rang. Not the measured thirteen of Mr. Vance's death. Not the dusk tolling they had all pretended for weeks not to hear. This was continuous, a rolling bronze insistence that had no rhythm Ivo could find, as though someone were swinging the rope without ever letting it settle, or as though the sound itself had stopped caring about ropes and towers and the physics of things that had been melted down in 1911.

The tower was still a roofless shell. Ivo could see it from the window, past the yew hedge, past the low stone wall: St. Alder's stump of a steeple standing open to the sky like a broken tooth. Nothing hung in it. Nothing could hang in it. And yet the sound came from there — Ivo was sure of it now, sure in the bones the way Odile had described, sure in the teeth — and it came without pause, and the village below was waking to it.

They came out of their houses slowly, the way people come out after a storm to count what's lost. Ivo watched Mrs. Petch stand in her garden in her dressing gown, looking up at the tower, her mouth moving. Counting, probably. Everyone in Hollowmere counted; it was the village's mother tongue. But Mrs. Petch's face was wrong. Her face was the face of a woman who had counted all her life and had just realized the numbers might run out.

By eight o'clock there were men on the green with shotguns and axes, and by nine there was a fire in a barrel outside the Marchetti farm gate, and by ten the word *sacrifice* was being said out loud, in daylight, by people who had spent a century saying nothing at all.

* * *

Tam heard it first as a change in the dogs.

He had been awake since four, lying in the narrow bed above the potting shed, listening to the bell work its way into his sleep the way water works into a wall. His cousin's name was Emeka. Had been Emeka. The photographs were blank now — he'd checked again at midnight, holding each one to the lamp, seeing only pale rectangles where his cousin's laughing face had been — but Tam kept saying the name to himself anyway, over and over, a private tally of one. *Emeka. Emeka.* If he stopped saying it, he was afraid the subtraction would finish, would reach back into his own skull and take the memory too, and then Emeka would be nobody's, which was worse than dead.

At four the dogs started. Hollowmere kept dogs the way it kept secrets — casually, everywhere — and Tam knew every bark on his street the way he knew birdsong. But this was different. This was the low continuous chorus of animals that can smell something wrong in the air, and under it, faintly, the sound of boots. Many boots. Men walking with purpose before dawn is a sound every undocumented man learns early, and Tam's body knew it before his mind did: he was out of bed and dressed and moving the loose brick behind the stove where his papers weren't — he had no papers — but where he kept the photograph of his mother, and the little folding knife, and the forty pounds he hadn't sent home yet.

By six he was in the hospice kitchen, and by seven the kitchen had become what it had quietly been becoming for months: a household. Sister Brigid at the stove making tea for twelve because tea was the one sacrament she still trusted. Odile at the table with the founders' ledger open in front of her, copying names onto index cards in her small fierce handwriting. Ivo coming down the stairs with that look Tam had learned to read — the look of a person who has counted something twice and gotten two different answers.

"They're gathering," Tam said. "On the green. I heard them organizing. They're saying—" He stopped. Saying it out loud felt like handing them the knife.

"Say it," Brigid said. She set down the teapot with a click. "We've earned the truth. We've paid for it."

"They're saying the outsiders brought it back." Tam looked at his hands. "That the bell was quiet until we came. That if the tally needs feeding—" His voice did something humiliating, cracked in the middle, and he hated it. "Mr. Marchetti stood on his own gate and said the village had a hundred years of peace and one autumn of bells, and asked everyone to do the arithmetic."

"The arithmetic," Odile said flatly. She was sixteen and had the coldest voice in the room when she chose to. "Four hundred villagers. Four of us. Very difficult sums."

"It isn't about the sums," Ivo said quietly. "It's never been about the sums. It's about permission. They've fed this thing other people's deaths for a hundred years and called it hospice care and natural causes. Now they want to feed it ours and call it justice."

* * *

Odile went out at noon because somebody had to, and because she had learned that being sixteen and female and unclaimed made her the least threatening thing in Hollowmere, and therefore the best scout. She wore her hood up and walked the long way round, past the school, past the shop where Mr. Aldous was nailing plywood over his window as though the danger might come through the glass instead of through the people, and she counted what she saw, because counting was how you kept the world from sliding.

Eleven men with guns at the green. Two trucks at the Marchetti gate. A bonfire being built in the pit behind the forge, big enough for — she didn't finish the thought. Chalk on the war memorial: fresh writing, a list, and her own name third from the top. *Reyes* first. Then *Brigid*. Then *Odile*. Then *Tam*, spelled wrong, which somehow frightened her more than anything else, because it meant whoever wrote it didn't even know him well enough to spell him, and they were going to kill him anyway.

She stood in front of the memorial for a long time. The bell rang above her, tireless, and she noticed — with the strange clarity that had been hers since the undercroft — that the ringing had a texture now, a grain, like a voice pushed past hoarseness. It wasn't menacing anymore. It sounded almost tired. Almost like asking.

"You shouldn't be out," said a voice, and it was young Tom Petch, seventeen, holding a cricket bat, wearing the expression of a boy who had been handed a gun-shaped hole in his life and told to fill it. "You should be — look, Odile, my mum says—"

"Your mum says what?"

He couldn't say it. She watched him try and fail, watched the whole inherited architecture of Hollowmere politeness strain against what his mother actually wanted done to the girl who'd once helped him with his maths homework, and she felt something in her chest go hard and clean.

"Tell your mum," Odile said, "that we know what she wants. And tell her the bell doesn't want what she thinks it wants. Tell her I've been underneath the tower. Tell her I've *heard* it."

Tom stared at her. Behind him, on the green, a shotgun went over a shoulder like a harvest tool, and Odile turned and walked home through a village that had decided, in the space of one sleepless night, that she had never really lived in it at all.

* * *

By evening the constable had come and gone, and that was its own kind of answer.

Sergeant Wills was a soft man nearing retirement who had spent thirty years in Hollowmere writing up sheep thefts and parking complaints, and he stood in the hospice kitchen with his hat in his hands and would not sit down, would not take tea, would not look at any of them directly.

"There's a crowd at the Marchetti place," he said. "Forty, fifty. Armed. And I've got two officers and a station that closes at five."

"So do your job," Brigid said. Her voice was gentle, which was worse. "There's a mob planning murder, Sergeant. That's rather the foundational case, isn't it? The first thing they teach you?"

Wills' face worked. "And when I arrest Tom Petch's father, and Billy Marchetti, and half the parish council — who backs me? The chief constable goes to St. Alder's memorial service every year. The magistrate buried his wife in that churchyard. There's no court for this, Sister. There's no—" He gestured helplessly at the bell-sound filling the room like weather. "There's no *law* that covers whatever this is. I can offer you protective custody at the station overnight. That's it. That's everything I have."

"And tomorrow?" Ivo asked.

Wills was quiet for a moment. When he answered, he answered the floor. "Tomorrow I'm off shift. And tomorrow there'll be more of them. Miss—" he meant Odile, couldn't manage her name either, "—the list on the memorial. I'd take it down if I could. I'd arrest whoever chalked it. But I'd be arresting the grandfather of a child in my wife's Sunday school, and by Friday I'd be policing a village that's decided I'm on your side, and I don't—" His voice failed and restarted. "I've got a family too. That's my whole confession. I've got a family, and I'm sorry."

He left his card on the table, which was the smallest possible coin, and they let him keep the fiction that it meant something.

When the door closed, Brigid sat down heavily. "No court," she said. "No church — I rang the diocese this afternoon. Exorcism requests go through a committee. A *committee*, Ivo. Six weeks minimum, and the man I spoke to asked me twice whether I was quite certain I'd been validly defrocked, as though that were the emergency." She laughed, a short bitter sound with no humor in it anywhere. "A hundred years ago the Church ran the tally. You'd think they'd at least send a pamphlet."

"No constable," Tam said. "No priest. No judge."

"No neighbors," Odile said, and then, because honesty was the household's currency now, corrected herself: "Some neighbors. Tom Petch couldn't even say it. That's not nothing. That's a crack."

Ivo stood at the window again. Below, in the blue dark, the fires were lighting — three of them now, spaced around the village like a ring closing — and the bell rang on, and Ivo found themselves doing the terrible arithmetic they had spent a lifetime teaching others not to do. Forty against four. Guns against a kitchen. A century of buried complicity against a handful of people who had never been given a stake in the bargain and so had never agreed to pay it.

"We can't win a siege," Ivo said. "We can't outrun it — they'll watch the roads. We can't wait it out — the bell gets louder every day, and hungry things get bold." They turned around. The lamplight caught all their faces: Brigid's lined and steady, Tam's exhausted and unbroken, Odile's too old for sixteen. "So we stop playing defense. Tomorrow, one of us goes down into that undercroft and finishes finding out what the Tenant actually wants. Because Odile's right — it doesn't want blood. I've listened to the dying for eleven years, and I know the difference between a thing that feeds and a thing that *waits*. This thing has been waiting since 1911. Waiting things want something specific."

"What makes you think it will tell us?" Tam asked.

Ivo looked at the window, at the fires, at the roofless tower black against the last of the light, ringing and ringing like a heart that refused to stop.

"Because it's already talking to one of us," Ivo said. "Odile. It talked to you down there. And tonight, before those fires burn down and those men find their courage — you're going to tell us exactly what it said."

Odile set down her pen. On the index card in front of her, half-finished, her handwriting had gone strange and slanted, and none of them had noticed until now that she had stopped copying the ledger an hour ago and had been writing something else entirely — the same sentence, over and over, filling the card edge to edge:

*It doesn't want a death. It wants a door.*
