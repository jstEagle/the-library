## 1. The Commission

The contract arrived on a Tuesday, which Noor Vane would later think of as the last ordinary day of her life.

She was eating cold noodles over the kitchen sink when the email came through, and she read it twice because the number at the bottom refused to behave like a real number. Forty thousand dollars for a pre-auction appraisal of a single property. Her last three jobs had paid four hundred each — a boundary dispute in Ashford, a flood-elevation certificate, a widow's estate nobody wanted to touch. Forty thousand was not an appraisal fee. Forty thousand was a ransom.

The sender was Wren & Associates, Estate Law. The subject line read: *URGENT — Hollowmere House — Full Structural & Historical Survey Required Prior to Sale.* The body was brief, courteous, and strange in the way that expensive things are strange: no haggling, no scope negotiation, no request for references. Just terms, dates, and a wire transfer authorization already made out.

Noor set down her fork and did what she always did with anything that looked too good. She checked it for rot.

The firm existed. She called the number; a receptionist with a voice like warm honey confirmed everything and added, unasked, "Mr. Wren says you come highly recommended." Noor had never heard of Mr. Wren. She asked who recommended her. "He didn't say," the receptionist said pleasantly, and Noor wrote that down in her notebook anyway, because she wrote everything down — it was the one discipline she'd never broken, the habit that had kept her solvent and sane through nine years of measuring other people's mistakes.

Her bank balance said yes before her judgment could finish saying wait.

* * *

The deed arrived by courier the next morning, hand-delivered in a leather folio that probably cost more than her car payment. Noor sat cross-legged on her apartment floor and read it the way she read all documents: twice, slowly, with a pencil.

Hollowmere House, parcel 7, County Road 9. Constructed 1841. Original owners: Aldous Wren and — here the pencil paused — a name that had been struck through so thoroughly the paper had thinned, leaving only a ghost of ink and a faint depression where a pen had once pressed hard enough to tear. Someone had hated that name out of existence.

She held the page up to the window light. Under the strike-through, if she tilted it just right, she could make out the first letter. An *A*. Maybe an *Ad*.

"Adeliza?" she murmured, guessing at old-fashioned names, and felt slightly foolish for talking to paper.

She read on. Easements, mineral rights, a conservation restriction she'd need to verify. And then, in Section 14, a clause written in language older than the rest of the document, as though copied from something earlier:

*"Should the House stand to be sold, let it be sold entire and undivided. And should any party complete the Work begun by the founders — the appraisal of the House, whole and true, unto its final measure — then to that party, the Completer, shall descend all right, title, and interest herein, notwithstanding any claim of blood or purchase."*

Noor read it three times.

It wasn't unheard-of for old deeds to contain odd conditions. She'd seen clauses about church pews in perpetuity, about a well shared between feuding farms, about one property that legally required its owner to keep bees. But this clause wasn't odd in the way of forgotten customs. It was odd in the way of intent. *Notwithstanding any claim of blood or purchase.* Whoever drafted it had wanted to make sure that neither inheritance nor money could trump the completion of — what? An appraisal?

An appraisal was her job. It was, in fact, precisely her job. The clause read almost like an invitation addressed to someone exactly like her.

She photographed every page, logged the folio's condition, and told herself the small cold feeling at the base of her skull was just the excitement of a big payday. She was very good at telling herself things.

* * *

County Road 9 gave out to gravel two miles before the property line, and the gravel gave out to nothing a mile after that. Noor drove the last stretch with her headlights cutting a tunnel through late-October fog, the trees leaning in overhead like witnesses.

Hollowmere House appeared the way large houses do in fog — not gradually but all at once, as though it had decided to permit itself to be seen. Three stories of grey stone, steep-gabled, with a slate roof gone soft-edged with moss. Chimneys like raised fingers. Windows dark, except —

Except that wasn't right either. She parked, checked her watch: 5:47 PM, sunset at 6:02. The light was failing evenly, the way light does, and yet the house seemed lit from some interior hour of its own, the windows holding a dim amber glow that might have been reflection and might not.

Noor sat in her car for a moment and ran her routine. Site arrival: document time, weather, approach conditions. Photograph exterior from all cardinal points. Establish datum point. Begin exterior measurements before losing daylight.

Routine was the whole religion of her profession. Measure first, interpret never — or at least not until the numbers were safe on paper. People thought surveying was about land. It wasn't. It was about refusing to guess.

She stepped out into air that smelled of wet stone and, underneath it, faintly, of woodsmoke — though there was no smoke visible anywhere, no sign anyone had lived here in years.

* * *

The porch ran the full width of the façade, deep enough for furniture that wasn't there, its boards sound under her boots despite the house's evident age. Noor planted her tripod on the gravel turnabout, leveled it, and began the exterior photo sequence, working left to right as she always did.

That was when she counted the doors.

It wasn't deliberate. Surveyors count openings the way sailors count waves — automatically, constantly, as background process. Ground floor: front door, double-leafed, black oak with iron strap hinges. Then along the east wing: a service door, another service door, a garden door with a fanlight. West wing: a French door onto what had been a terrace, a side entrance beneath a small portico, a cellar bulkhead door at grade level. Upper floors, visible from her position: a balcony door on the second story, a dormer access on the third, and — she zoomed her camera and checked twice — a narrow door set into the gable end of the attic, painted the same grey as the clapboard, nearly invisible.

Eleven. She noted it without thinking: *11 ext. doors visible.*

Then she climbed the porch steps, unlocked the front door with the key the firm had couriered — brass, heavy, older than her grandmother — and stepped into the front hall.

And stopped.

The hall was vast, panelled in dark wood gone honey-brown with age, lit by the last grey light through a transom window above the door. A staircase swept up and divided. Portraits lined the walls, their subjects darkened to near-silhouette. Dust lay over everything in a fine, undisturbed velvet — and yet the air did not smell stale. It smelled, again, faintly of woodsmoke. Of a fire burned recently somewhere below, or above, or in some room she hadn't found yet.

Noor stood on the doormat and did the thing she could not stop herself from doing, the thing her hands did before her mind authorized them.

She counted the doors.

Front door behind her: one. Doors off the hall proper — she turned a slow circle, cataloguing: library, left. Drawing room, right. Dining room, beyond the drawing room arch. A door beneath the stairs, low and narrow — servant's stair, probably. Two doors flanking the fireplace wall, symmetric, leading to who knew what. A corridor at the back of the hall with three more doors visible along it, plus what looked like a conservatory door of glass and white paint at the far end.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve.

Twelve interior doors on the ground floor alone.

Noor lowered her notebook. That wasn't alarming by itself — big houses had many doors; that was what made them big houses. But the number snagged on something, some splinter of pattern-recognition, and she stood in the middle of the hall with her pencil hovering and finally went looking for the splinter.

It took her four minutes to find it, and when she found it she wished she hadn't, because the finding changed the texture of the silence around her.

From the porch, she had counted eleven doors *on the entire visible exterior.* Eleven openings, ground floor to attic ridge. Inside, on the ground floor alone, twelve doors — and every one of them, as far as she could see, opened inward onto rooms. None of them was the outside door she'd entered. So where were the exteriors of these twelve? Where did the library door sit relative to the garden door she'd photographed twenty minutes ago? A building's skin had to match its organs. Walls had thicknesses. Doors had places.

Unless the arithmetic was wrong. Unless eleven outside and twelve inside meant that somewhere in this house there was a door whose other side simply — wasn't accounted for.

"Don't," she said aloud, to herself, in the empty hall. "Don't do that. You haven't measured anything yet."

Because that was the truth, and she clung to it like a rail. She hadn't measured anything. She was standing in a foyer making assumptions off eyeball geometry, and eyeball geometry was how amateurs got sued. The house was old; old houses grew additions, sealed openings, shifted their skins across a century and a half of renovations. There would be a boring answer. There was always a boring answer. Nine years of practice had taught her that the boring answer was the most common substance in the world, more common than water.

She got to work.

Datum point: the iron hinge plate of the front door, northwest leaf. She shot her first reference lines while the light lasted, laser measure chirping softly, numbers falling into her log in neat columns. Front hall: 22 feet 4 inches by 18 feet even. Ceiling height, 12 feet 2. All normal. All beautifully, reassuringly normal.

By full dark she had the ground floor roughly sketched, and she allowed herself one last walk-through before setting up camp in the front hall with her equipment cases, a camp stove, and the sleeping bag she'd brought against the possibility of a multi-day job. The firm had offered her a hotel in town, twenty minutes away. She'd declined. Appraisers who slept on site caught things appraisers who commuted missed — the settling sounds, the drainage smells, the way a house behaved at 3 AM when no one was watching.

She locked the front door. Tested it. Locked the two flanking parlor doors, out of habit more than purpose, and hung her key ring on her belt where it clinked with every step like a small stubborn metronome.

In the last pass down the back corridor, she counted the doors again. Professional compulsion. Confirming the sketch.

Ten.

She stopped mid-stride. Went back to the head of the corridor. Counted again, touching each frame lightly with two fingers, the way she'd been taught to verify a chain measurement.

Ten.

There had been twelve. She had written twelve in her notebook not ninety minutes ago, in her own handwriting, in her own shorthand. She flipped pages back and there it was: *GF int. doors = 12,* beside a sketch showing every opening placed.

Noor stood in the dim corridor with her fingers still resting on a doorframe and felt, for the first time since accepting the contract, the professional detachment develop a hairline crack — thin, high-pitched, running from the base of her skull down into her chest.

Old houses settle. Old houses shift. But houses did not lose doors in ninety minutes, and they especially did not lose doors in a corridor she had walked four times, in a building with dust thick enough to record footprints — and there were no footprints but hers, none, anywhere, as though the house had been waiting empty for exactly one visitor, or as though it cleaned up after itself, or—

"Boring answer," she whispered. "Find the boring answer tomorrow."

But she slept that night in the front hall with all the lights on, and she set her phone to record audio out of pure habit — she always recorded her site notes verbally, lying in her bag, dictating the day's figures — and the little red dot glowed in the dark of the enormous house like a single patient eye.

At some point past midnight, half-dreaming, she thought she heard a door close somewhere far above her. One door. Softly. Like something being shut carefully, so as not to wake her.

Or so as not to be counted.

In the morning, she would re-count the doors three times, and get a different number each time, and the third number would be twelve again — but that was tomorrow, and tonight she only pulled the sleeping bag higher, and told herself, one more time, in the voice she used for clients and for herself:

Everything has a measurement. Everything.

Somewhere in the dark above her, unhurried and almost gentle, something in Hollowmere House seemed to agree.
