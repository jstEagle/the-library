## 9. Counting Wrong

The hallway behind her was longer than the hallway ahead of her.

Noor stood at the midpoint of the second-floor corridor with her laser measure in hand and did not turn around. She had learned that much by now. Turning around was a form of observation, and observation was a negotiation, and she was losing every negotiation she entered.

She had walked this corridor eleven times since dawn. On the first pass it had been forty-two feet. On the fourth, fifty-one. On the ninth, she had stopped counting feet and started counting her own footsteps instead—sixty-four going east, sixty-one coming back. The house was eating distance somewhere behind her heels, quietly, the way a tide takes sand.

"Fine," she said aloud. Her voice sounded small and professional in the long light. "Fine. I'll measure you as you are today."

The laser ticked against the far wall: 58.3 feet. She wrote it down. Underneath, in smaller letters, she wrote: *as of 9:14 a.m.* Because that was the truth of Hollowmere now—it had no dimensions, only appointments. It was a building that kept its own books, and its books disagreed with hers on principle.

She had been here nineteen days. The appraisal deadline Elias Wren had mentioned sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: thirty days from contract, and the county would take the house if no one "completed the work." She still didn't know what the work was. She knew only that the house did, and that it was trying to tell her in the only language it had left—the language of numbers, which happened to be her language too.

That was the thing she hadn't told anyone, because there was no one to tell. The house wasn't haunting her. It was *corresponding* with her.

* * *

By noon the door count had changed four times.

She kept the tally on a legal pad now, one column per hour, because the old system—one count per day, verified three times—had become meaningless. At eight o'clock there had been twelve doors. At nine, eleven; the door to the linen closet off the landing had simply declined to exist, leaving a stretch of unbroken wallpaper that she photographed twice, the flash glinting off a pattern of small grey flowers that she could have sworn were arranged differently in the second image.

At ten, thirteen doors. The new one stood at the end of the west corridor where the wall should have been—a plain six-panel door, painted the same tired white as all the others, with a brass knob worn bright by hands that were not hers. She did not open it. She had rules about new doors, and rule one was that a door that appeared without being measured was a door that wanted something.

At eleven, back to twelve. At noon, twelve again—but they were not the same twelve. She'd begun marking each door with a small pencil dot at knee height, an inventory habit so ingrained it felt like breathing, and when she made her noon circuit, two of her dots were missing and one door bore a dot she didn't remember making, placed slightly higher, as if someone shorter—or someone else—had counted before her.

She stood in the upstairs hall with her pad pressed to her sternum and let herself feel it, just for a moment, without armor: the vertigo of being inside a thing that was also inside itself. Then she breathed out, squared the pad, and did what she always did. She measured.

*Twelve doors,* she wrote. *Two unaccounted dots. One foreign dot. Corridor lengths unstable between 9:00 and 12:00 hrs. Hypothesis: the house is not malfunctioning. It is communicating. A shifting count is a sentence I haven't parsed yet.*

Her hand hovered over the page. Then, because the thought arrived whole and would not be refused:

*Or it's panicking.*

* * *

The lights told her more than the doors did.

Every morning she found them burning—rooms she had locked, switches she had thrown, bulbs she had unscrewed and carried away in her survey bag like confiscated evidence. And every morning the electricity meter in the cellar had run backward another few revolutions, spinning gently counterclockwise in the grey light, patient as a metronome. She had stopped logging it as an anomaly. Anomalies repeat. This was policy.

Tonight, though, the lights did something new. She was in the kitchen at dusk, eating standing up over her field notes, when every bulb in the house came on at once—not the flicker-surge of a failing grid but a clean, total illumination, floor to roof, as if the house had drawn a breath and held it.

Then, room by room, beginning at the top of the house and descending, the lights went out again—all except one.

The lamp in the front parlor. The room where she'd first slept, the first week, before she'd moved her cot to the kitchen for reasons she'd written down as "drafts."

Noor set down her fork. She walked through the lit doorway into the parlor, and the light was warm and low and exactly the color of lamplight in a house where someone is waiting up for you.

"You want me in here," she said. "Okay. I'm in here."

She sat in the wingback chair by the cold fireplace. The lamp hummed. Outside, the first true dark of evening settled over the grounds, and the windows became black mirrors, and Noor looked at her own reflection sitting alone in a pool of light in a dead man's parlor, and then she looked at the reflection beside her.

There wasn't one. There never was, in this room, in this chair. But the cushion on her left held a shallow depression, as if something had been sitting there a long time and risen only recently, and the air above it was warmer than the air around it by a degree or two—she checked with the back of her hand, because she was who she was—and she found, to her own surprise, that she wasn't afraid.

"I know you've been listening to me," she said to the warm air. "I talk to myself when I work. Occupational hazard. Half of it's nonsense." She turned her notebook to a fresh page. "But some of it isn't. So if you have something to say, say it in a way I can write down. Numbers are fine. I'm good with numbers."

The lamp dimmed once, twice. Three pulses. Pause. Three pulses.

She wrote it down before she understood it, her pen moving on instinct: *3 — 1 — 19 — 20.* Then the letters assembled themselves beneath the numbers the way meaning assembles under a surveyor's transit, line by line, until the shape of it stood up off the page.

*C-A-S-T.*

Cast. As in cast out. As in the founders' word, the word from the ledger's final frantic pages—*it must be cast, it must be cast down, we will cast it into the deep foundation and appraise it at nothing.*

"No," Noor said, and heard her own voice come out flat and certain. "I'm not casting anything. If that's what you think I'm here for, we need to clear that up right now."

The lamp burned steady. The warm patch on the cushion beside her seemed, if anything, to lean closer.

"I'm here to appraise," she said. "To find out what things are worth. That's all I've ever done. And whatever they bound down in your foundations—" she paused, choosing, "—whoever they bound—I don't think they ever found out what *you're* worth. I think that was the whole point. They priced you at nothing so they could keep you."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the silence of a thing listening very hard, the way she herself listened to a client who has just said something that changes the whole scope of the job.

Then, faintly, from everywhere and nowhere—from the walls, from the floor joists, from the space behind her own ears—a voice. Low. Patient. Old the way bedrock is old.

*"He is coming,"* it said. *"He is coming to finish it his way."*

Noor's pen stopped moving.

"Elias," she said.

*"His way is the old way,"* the voice said. *"A body for a boundary. A person for a price. His family built me a cage and called it a house, and every generation one of them comes back to check the lock."* A pause, and when it resumed there was something in it she had never heard from the recordings—something that took her a moment to name, because she had never expected to hear it from a haunted house.

Urgency.

*"You count my doors. He counts my debts. Measurer—do not be here when he comes to collect."*

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat in the parlor until the lamp finally went out on its own, sometime after two, and then she worked by headlamp in the kitchen with every recording she had spread across the table like a hand of terrible cards.

Nineteen nights of sleep audio. In the early ones, only her own breathing, her own sleep-murmurs, the questions she asked the dark without knowing she asked them. Then the second voice arriving, night by night, growing clearer, growing closer—answering her sleeping self in that low patient register, correcting her measurements, telling her the true depth of the foundation, the true age of the timber, the names of trees that had been felled to build it.

And in last night's recording—she played it three times to be sure—in last night's recording, the voice had spoken while she was awake. To her waking mind, through the walls of a house she had been living inside for nineteen days, and she had answered it, and it had answered back, and neither of them had needed the dark to do it.

That was the part that made her hands shake. Not the voice. The conversation. Somewhere in nineteen days of measuring and tallying and talking to empty rooms, the house had stopped being a subject and become a correspondent, and she had crossed that line without noticing, the way you cross from acquaintance into friend.

She fast-forwarded through the files, hunting. And near the end of night fourteen—before Elias's second visit, before the dinner in town, before the smooth voice and the thirty-day deadline—she found forty seconds she had missed.

*"He was here today,"* the entity said, in her sleeping voice's pause. *"He walked my halls and touched my walls and he was measuring me for grief. He carries his grandfather's arithmetic in his blood. They taught him that everything can be owned if the price is paid in the right currency."*

In the recording, her sleeping self murmured something unintelligible.

*"Not money,"* the voice said, answering anyway, answering always. *"Never money. Money is only the receipt. The currency is people. It has always been people. And he is nearly out of family, measurer, so he will pay with whoever is holding the tape measure when the thirtieth day comes."*

Noor stopped the playback. The kitchen was very quiet. Her own face looked back at her from the dark window, lit green by the recorder's display, and behind the reflection the house stood around her in the darkness—twelve doors, eleven doors, thirteen, she no longer knew, she no longer entirely cared—holding its breath the way it had held it in the parlor.

"He's coming tomorrow," she said aloud. "Isn't he. He said end of the week, but he's coming tomorrow."

The hallway outside the kitchen creaked, once, in a place where nothing walked. But it wasn't a threat. She understood that now, the way she understood a bearing or a boundary line. It was a nod. It was the sound of a very old, very wronged thing agreeing with her—and asking, in the only grammar it had left, what she intended to do about it.

Noor looked down at her legal pad. Nineteen days of tallies. Door counts that shifted hourly, corridor lengths that breathed, a building that had been screaming in numbers for weeks while everyone assumed it was merely broken.

She turned to a fresh page and wrote, in her cleanest hand, the way she wrote anything that might someday matter in court:

*Hollowmere House is not derelict. Hollowmere House is occupied. The occupant claims the current owner's family owes it a debt of seizure, erasure, and imprisonment, and I am inclined to believe the claim has merit.*

She underlined *merit* twice. Then she added, smaller:

*Elias Wren intends to settle that debt with a human being. Find out what the founders' ritual actually requires. Find out what Adeliza Mott tried to do. You have maybe one day.*

Outside, rain began against the windows, soft and steady, like ink falling from a great height. And in the hall, all twelve doors—she was sure of it suddenly, sure the way you are sure of your own heartbeat—stood open at once, waiting to see which one she would choose.
