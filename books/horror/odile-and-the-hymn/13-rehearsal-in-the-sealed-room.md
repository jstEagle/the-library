## 13. Rehearsal in the Sealed Room

The padlock on the classroom door was older than my mother, and it gave like a rotten tooth.

Dell Hargrove had brought a pry bar and a flask, and he used both before we were through. The lock didn't so much open as surrender, shrieking once against the metal, and then the door to Room 4 swung inward on hinges that hadn't moved since before I was born, exhaling sixty-one years of held breath into our faces.

It smelled the way old paper smells when it's been dreaming. Dust and chalk and something sweeter underneath—pencil shavings, maybe, or the ghost of orange peel some kid had left in a desk in 1961 and never come back to claim. I stood in the doorway with Yara's hand on my shoulder and let that smell move through me, and I understood, in a way I couldn't have explained to anyone who hadn't heard singing at three in the morning at the edge of the woods, that this room had been waiting for us specifically. Not someone. Us.

"Nobody touches anything," Ruth said, though her voice had none of its sheriff's bark left. It came out soft, almost reverent, the way people talk in churches and hospitals. "Not yet. Let it know we're here."

*Let it know.* As if the room were a thing with ears. But I'd already learned that everything in Odile had ears—the walls, the wells, the hymnals with their four verses and their careful silence after. So I stood very still and let Room 4 take my measure while the others filed in behind me: Dell first, because he couldn't help himself, his big organist's hands hovering over everything like a man walking through his own dream; then Ruth, hat in her hands; then Yara, whose grip on my shoulder tightened once and released, which was how they said things they couldn't say out loud.

The desks were still in rows. Twenty-six of them, plus one apart from the rest near the window, and nobody said anything about the twenty-seventh, but everybody looked at it, the way you look at an empty chair in a photograph you've seen too many times.

And the chalkboards—

God, the chalkboards.

There were three of them across the front wall, slate gone gray-green with age, and every inch of them was covered in music. Not clean music, not whole songs, but fragments: staves that began mid-phrase and trailed off into smears where a hand had dragged through them. Clefs drawn and redrawn until the wood beneath had grooves worn into it. Whole measures erased and rewritten, erased and rewritten, so that the boards looked less like teaching tools and more like something trying to remember itself. And beneath the lowest board, in handwriting small enough to be ashamed of itself, words I could read even from the door:

*verse five — not finished — do not finish*

"You said half-erased," Yara murmured. "You didn't say it would be *asking*."

"It's not asking," Dell said. His voice cracked down the middle. "It's warning. My mother wrote that." He crossed the room like a man wading upstream and put two fingers under the words, not touching them, just tracing the air above them. "Eleanor Hargrove. She taught here. She wrote the fifth verse, and then she—" He stopped. We all knew what she'd done, or rather what had been done to her, one name underlined in sixty years of ledgers. "She tried to warn whoever came next."

"And whoever came next locked the door," Ruth said, "and threw away the key, and sang four verses every Wednesday for sixty years like good little sheep." She set her hat on the nearest desk—the twenty-seventh one, and I watched her do it deliberately, claiming it, defusing it—and turned to face us all. "Well. We're the ones who came next. Let's get to work."

* * *

We worked by lamplight, because the windows in Room 4 had been painted over from the outside sometime in the decades since the sealing, and no amount of daylight found its way through. Dell had brought three lanterns and a battery lamp besides, and we hung them from the coat hooks and the pull chain of a ceiling fan that no longer turned, and the room filled up with a warm, trembling light that made the chalk ghosts seem to breathe.

That's what I started calling them that first night. Chalk ghosts. The half-erased phrases that lingered on the boards like breath on glass. You had to look at them sideways to see them properly—if you stared straight on, your eye slid off, the way it slides off the extra child in a classroom full of students—but tilt your head, soften your focus, and suddenly the faint pressure of erased notation rose up out of the slate like a body rising out of dark water. Someone had written the fifth verse here. Someone had written it more than once. And someone else had tried, again and again, to scrub it away, without ever quite succeeding, as if the chalk had sunk too deep into the stone, or as if the verse simply refused to be forgotten.

"Here." Dell stood at the center board with a notebook open, his mother's notebook, its pages soft as cloth from handling. "This is what she kept. Four bars—just four—and I've carried them in my head for sixty years like a stone I swallowed." He hummed them, quiet, and I felt the room change around us. The dust seemed to lean in. The lantern flames all bent the same direction, toward him, like flowers toward sun. It wasn't frightening exactly. It was worse than frightening. It was *beautiful*, and I understood in my bones why the town sang, why the founders had woven the hymn into every gathering and graduation and funeral. Once you've heard those four bars, some part of you is always listening for the rest.

Yara wrote them down in pencil, then stopped, pencil hovering. "If I write it, does it count as finishing it?"

"It counts as starting," Ruth said grimly. "Finishing is what we're deciding whether to do."

"We already decided," I said.

They all looked at me. I hadn't meant to speak. But the fragment was still hanging in the air, and underneath it I could feel the shape of what came next—not the notes themselves, not yet, but the *socket* they fit into, the way you can feel a missing tooth with your tongue. Ever since the tree line, ever since the choir braided my voice into theirs for one terrible minute, I'd been carrying a splinter of the song in my head, and it ached the way a splinter aches, wanting to be dug out or pushed deeper, anything but left where it was.

"I hear it in my sleep," I said. "I've been hearing it since I was little. I thought it was just—dreams. Everybody has song dreams." I wrapped my arms around myself. "It's not dreams. It's rehearsal. They've been rehearsing me my whole life."

Yara crossed the room and sat beside me on the edge of a desk built for a twelve-year-old, knees up around their ears, absurd and dear. "Then we use it," they said. "Everything they put in you, we take back out and turn around. That's the whole plan, Marisol. They made you a soloist. Fine. Soloists get the melody."

* * *

Here is what I dreamed, and what I finally told them on the second night, sitting cross-legged on the floor of Room 4 with chalk dust settling on me like snow:

I dreamed of a line of children walking into the trees, holding hands, in no particular hurry, the way you walk to recess. Their faces were smooth. Not blank—smooth, like clay before the artist decides, like faces waiting to be told what to be. And above them, threaded through the branches, the fifth verse unspooled note by note, and in the dream I knew it perfectly, every interval, every rest, the way you know your own name. There was a place in the middle where the melody climbed higher than any of the other verses dared, hung there a moment like a bird deciding, and came down on a single long note that felt, in the dream, like a door opening.

"There," Yara said, when I'd sung what I could remember, voice shaking, into the hush of the sealed room. "That high part. That's the hinge. Everything turns on it."

"How can you know that?" Ruth asked.

"Because it's the same hinge in the fourth verse," Yara said, and went to the boards. "Look—your mother left the scaffolding up, Mr. Hargrove. She erased the fifth verse but she left the *structure*. See? Here, here, and here—the phrase endings match the ends of verses two, three, and four. She built the fifth verse out of the other four. That's why the town only needs to sing four. The fifth one is already inside them, folded up, waiting for somebody to unfold it."

Dell made a sound low in his chest, half laugh, half sob. "Mama always said a hymn is just a house you build out of verses. She said the Odile Hymn was built with a room nobody was supposed to find." He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, leaving pale streaks in the chalk dust on his face. "She built the room anyway. Couldn't help herself. Composer's disease."

"She built it so there'd be somewhere to *put* them," Ruth said quietly. "When they took the children. A verse to carry them home in."

"And now we're going to sing it backwards and carry them home out," Yara said.

Nobody answered. The lanterns trembled. Somewhere far off—I want to say outside, I want to say beyond the painted-over windows, but I honestly don't know where—a note sounded, long and patient, like something tuning itself.

* * *

Three nights we worked. Maybe four. Time goes strange in Room 4; the painted windows keep out more than light.

On the third night—or the fourth—we had it mostly whole. Yara had transcribed the chalk ghosts onto butcher paper taped across the boards, filling gaps from Dell's four bars and my dreams, checking each recovered phrase against the structure of the older verses the way Ruth checked a lock, turning it, testing it, refusing to trust anything that fit too easily. When a phrase was wrong, the room told us. I don't mean anyone said so. I mean the air got heavy, and the lanterns dimmed, and the twenty-seventh desk creaked though nothing sat in it, and once—only once—all the chalk ghosts on all three boards faded at the same moment, like a held breath, until Yara erased the wrong phrase and the room exhaled.

When a phrase was right, the dust on the floor stirred. Just that. A little shimmer across the boards, a warmth in the cold air, the feeling you get when a stranger smiles at you on a bad day.

"That one," I said, the first time it happened. "That's hers. That's Eleanor's. She's helping."

"Or it's bait," Ruth said.

"Or both," said Dell, who knew his mother best, and wrote the phrase down anyway.

By the end we stood before a complete fifth verse written out in Yara's careful pencil, and I will not write it here, and I never will, and if you ask me I'll tell you I've forgotten it, and most days that's nearly true. What I can tell you is what it did to the room when we read it through silently, lips moving, no sound. The lanterns bowed. The chalk ghosts rose fully visible for the first time, every erased phrase surfacing at once, so that all three boards blazed with overlapping music like a lake showing every drowned thing on its bottom. And the twenty-seventh desk, Ruth's hat still sitting square on its surface, turned—slowly, with a sound like a knuckle cracking—to face the front of the class.

Ruth picked up her hat. Nobody blamed her.

"It knows we have it," Yara whispered.

"It's known since the tree line," I said. "Since it chose me. This isn't discovery. This is *showing our work*." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, too bright, skittering around the sealed room looking for a way out. "We're the dumbest kids in Odile. We did the assignment. We finished the forbidden verse. And now the teacher's going to call on us."

"Marisol." Yara's hand found mine in the lantern light, chalk-dust dry and steady. "Look at me. We're not doing the assignment. We're doing the *opposite* of the assignment. Remember what Dell found."

What Dell found. His mother's marginalia, in the back pages of the notebook, in pencil so faint we'd needed the battery lamp pressed right up against the paper to read it. A note to whoever came next, written in a hand already shaking:

*They will teach you the verse forward. Forward it feeds. Reverse it — end where it begins, last note first — and it unmakes. It cannot abide its own name spoken backwards. But you will need four voices who carry the first four verses in their blood, and one who has heard the fifth. Five, or it fails. Five, or it takes you all.*

Five. The Odile Five, sworn in this very room, no one taken alone, no one fed to the song.

Dell sang bass; sixty years of hymns lived in his chest like furniture. Ruth had a congregation's lifetime of the first four verses, and a lawwoman's stubbornness besides. Yara had the ear, the training, the terrible fluency—they could hear a wrong note the way other people hear their name called. And me. I had the fifth verse growing in me like a second heartbeat, planted by a choir that thought it was preparing a soloist, never guessing it was loading a weapon.

"One run-through," Yara said. "Backwards. Quiet. Tonight, so we know we can."

"No," Ruth said.

"Yes," said Dell. "Better we find out what breaks in here than out there."

They looked at me. Of course they looked at me. I was the one who'd heard the fifth; I was the fuse. And I was scared—scared down to the wet animal core of me, scared in a way the tree line hadn't even managed, because this was chosen, this was deliberate, we were going to pick up the thing that had been hunting my town for sixty years and *play* it.

But underneath the fear, I'm not going to lie to you, there was something else. Something with teeth. Sixty years of underlined names, of empty chairs creeping closer in photographs, of a town singing its children into the woods every Wednesday and calling it tradition. If the verse could be reversed, then the hunted could hunt back. And I had spent seventeen years being nobody in Odile, friendless, humming nothing, keeping my mouth shut exactly like everyone told me to, and I was so tired of shut mouths.

"Count us in, Mr. Hargrove," I said.

Dell raised his hand. Ruth squared her shoulders like a woman facing a storm she intended to arrest. Yara nodded once, and their eyes met mine, and whatever passed between us in that moment, I'll carry it past the end of everything.

We sang the fifth verse backwards, pianissimo, five voices in a sealed room with painted windows, ending where it began.

For one bar, nothing. For two bars, the lanterns steadied, and the dust lay still, and I thought: *it doesn't work, we were fools.*

Then the chalkboards screamed.

Not the room—the boards. Every ghost phrase surfaced at once and *peeled*, curling up off the slate like scorched paper, and the twenty-seven desks shuddered in their rows, and from somewhere beneath the floor—from beneath the school, from beneath the ground the school stood on—came a sound I will hear until I die: a vast indrawn breath, the whole choir, sixty years deep, inhaling all together. Not angry. Worse. *Interested.*

The lanterns went out. All of them, all at once, and in the dark I heard Ruth say, very calmly, the way she must have talked to men with guns: "Don't stop. Whatever happens, don't stop singing."

And then, from the direction of the woods—miles away, impossibly close, inside the walls, inside my own ribs—the choir began to sing the fifth verse forward.

All of it. Perfectly. Coming for us.
