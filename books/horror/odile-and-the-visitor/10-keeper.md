## 10. Keeper

The bread arrived on the first of every month, wrapped in a cloth that smelled of woodsmoke and anise, left on the doorstep of the Marchand house by hands Beatrix never saw. By the third month, the hands had faces attached: Madame Roussel's daughter, shy and freckled, who set the loaf down and said, *Bonjour, Madame Reyes*, with the particular reverence the village now paid her — the reverence of people addressing a well they drink from and do not look into too deeply. By the fifth month they called her Beatrix, and the butcher saved her the end cut of the terrine, and children were allowed, for the first time in living memory, to play at the foot of the hill.

She understood what the warmth was. She had spent six months in this village before they gave it to her, and she had spent those six months reading the shape of its silence the way she'd once read the shape of a melody. The warmth was not love. It was gratitude, which is a colder thing, and older, and it did not require her to be happy. It required only that she stay.

She stayed.

* * *

In October she walked to the crossroads below the hill and stood on the road that ran west, toward the valley, toward the train station at Vireloup, toward everything she had been before. The road was a good road. It had been resurfaced two years ago; she could see the tar seams like stitches. And yet her body refused it the way a hand refuses a hot iron. Not fear, exactly. Fear had a flavor, and this had none. It was more like standing at the edge of a room and knowing, without being told, that the room ended there — that beyond the tar and the gravel and the poplars the world simply declined to continue being real, and that if she walked far enough she would arrive nowhere at all, or worse, arrive somewhere that had been waiting.

She had tested it once, in August, on a bicycle borrowed from the Roussels. She had made it four kilometers. At the fifth, the light had gone strange — not dark, not dim, but *thin*, the way a voice goes thin when it is stretched over too great a distance — and the poplars had begun to repeat themselves, the same three trees, the same three trees, and she had turned around without deciding to, her legs making the choice her mind was still arguing about. She had pedaled home weeping without being able to say why, and the house had been warm when she came in, and the chair had been at the table, and she had sat in it because there was no other place to sit.

The chair was companionable now. That was the word her mind kept reaching for, though she hated it. It had stopped advancing sometime around the dawn in June, when the house had screamed and the windows had bled light and Marcel's missing verse had come out of her throat in a language she did not speak and could not forget. After that morning the chair had simply been there — pulled up to the table's long side, angled slightly toward the head, the way a friend angles a chair when they intend to listen. Its wood had gone from grey to a warm honey color. In the winter she had caught herself draping her coat over it. In the spring she had caught herself talking to it, narrating her days, and had stopped only when she realized she was no longer embarrassed.

She was not, she told herself, its keeper. She was the house's. There was a difference. She held to the difference the way a drowning woman holds to a plank that is, on close inspection, part of the ship.

* * *

The letters came less often now that no one wrote to her, but they came. In November there was one from a doctoral student in Lyon named Camille Fontaine, who had found a citation of Beatrix's early fieldwork in a footnote and written with the breathless, slightly presumptuous eagerness of the young — the same eagerness Beatrix recognized because she had worn it herself, twenty years ago, arriving in villages with a tape recorder and a certainty that the world's old songs were hers to collect.

*Madame Reyes,* the letter began, *I am writing to ask about the lullaby of Sainte-Odile. I have found the 1911 collection — Barroux's *Chansons des Cévennes oubliées* — and the note that the collector never returned to the village, and that no one has recorded the song since. The melody is notated only in fragments. I believe it may be a survival of a pre-Christian cradle rite. I intend to travel there this summer. Any guidance you could offer would be invaluable.*

Beatrix read the letter four times at the kitchen table, with the fire going and the bread cloth folded beside her and the chair at her left hand, patient, companionable. Outside, the first snow was coming down the way it always came down in Sainte-Odile — slowly, heavily, as if the sky were reluctant to let go of it.

She got out paper. Her hand, when it began, was steady. She had practiced this letter in her head for months, in the way one practices a confession, or an argument with the dead.

*Mademoiselle Fontaine —*

*Don't come.*

She stopped. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs, in the room that had been Odile Marchand's and before Odile's had been a folklorist named Ilse Brandt's, a floorboard settled with a sound like a sigh. Beatrix looked at the two words she had written and understood that they were true and useless, that they were the words Odile had surely written to someone once, or meant to, or had burned in the stove along with the rest of the truth. *Don't come* is not a sentence that stops anyone. It had never stopped her. It was, she suspected, the most reliable invitation ever devised — a door marked *do not open* is a door that opens in the mind before the hand ever touches it.

She thought about Camille Fontaine, twenty-three or twenty-four, with her footnotes and her theories about pre-Christian cradle rites, packing a rucksack in a Lyon apartment, printing out train schedules. She thought about the thin light at the fifth kilometer. She thought about the six keepers whose names she had recovered from Odile's hidden letters — Barroux himself, in the end, who had collected the song in 1911 and never left; Ilse Brandt, 1934; the Vasseur brothers, who had lasted eleven years between them; a quiet American named Halloran whose field notes Beatrix now kept in the cedar chest; and Odile, who had lasted fifty-one years and whose last letter had begged whoever found it to run before sunrise, and who had known, even as she wrote it, that no one runs before sunrise.

She thought about the village below the hill, where the children played now, where the priest said mass without the salt shaker in his pocket, where the thing on the threshold was fed and quiet and the something worse beyond it stayed beyond.

She picked up the pen.

*Forgive the bluntness. The song you are chasing is real, and it is not a lullaby. Barroux's notation is accurate as far as it goes, and that is precisely the danger — it goes exactly far enough to be sung, and no farther. If you have already learned the four bars, stop humming it. If you have dreamed it, write to me and I will tell you what the dream means, and you will not believe me, and I will keep writing anyway.*

*There is no melody to collect in Sainte-Odile. There is only a house, and a chair, and a debt that the village pays in the only currency it has. I pay it now. I pay it gladly, most days, and I will not pretend to you that the other days do not exist.*

*Don't come.*

She sat with it. The fire had burned down to a red heart. She should seal the envelope; she knew she should. And then her hand moved again, the way her legs had moved on the bicycle, making a decision her mind was still arguing about, and she wrote:

*But if you come anyway — and I know you will, because I came, because they all came, because this letter is the same letter six people have written and six people have watched be ignored — then come to the village by the morning road, not the evening one. Knock at the Roussels' first and say my name; they will feed you and keep you below the hill until you've had time to think. Do not take lodging at the Marchand house. Do not accept a key to it. If you find yourself at my door after dark, I will let you in, because I am not yet the kind of keeper who can do otherwise, and I am writing these directions so that at least one of us will know where the trap is before we are both standing in it.*

*Come in summer. Bring bread. Bring nothing you would grieve to lose, and no one you could not live without, because the house learns faces quickly and it learns the loved ones fastest.*

*I will leave the lamp in the west window lit. It is what Odile did. I understand now that it was not a welcome. It was a warning that could not be spoken any other way.*

*— B. Reyes, Sainte-Odile*

She read it through. Then she added a postscript in smaller letters, the way one slips the true thing in sideways, past one's own defenses:

*P.S. The chair by my table is not mine. It has never been mine. But it waits for me the way a dog waits, and some mornings, when the snow is falling and the bread is warm and the village children are shouting below the hill, I cannot tell whether I am its keeper or it is mine, or whether those were ever two different things. If you come, you may see it. Do not sit in it. That is the only rule I will insist on, and I insist on it because I once broke it, and the breaking was the gentlest thing that ever happened to me, and that is the whole horror of this place, Camille — it is gentle. It is so gentle.*

She sealed the letter and set it on the table for the morning post. The chair stood at her left, honey-warm in the firelight, angled toward her, listening. She put her hand on its back — she did this now, the way one touches a shoulder — and the wood was warm, and the house around her settled deeper into its winter sleep, and below the hill the village of Sainte-Odile slept too, safe, fed, grateful, and the snow came down on all of it like a lullaby no one would ever need to sing again.

In the west window, she left the lamp lit.
