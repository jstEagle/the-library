## 2. Twenty-Six Desks

The light in Room 3 came through the east windows at a slant, the way morning light does when it has to push through fog first, and it made everything look slightly underwater. Ulyana stood in the doorway with their coffee going cold in one hand and the roster in the other and counted the desks for the third time.

Twenty-six.

They counted again, touching each number to a desk with their eyes, left to right, front row to back, because that was the kind of person they were—the kind who counted twice, who checked the stove, who reread emails before sending. Their mother had been the same way. Their mother had also been afraid of wells, which was a thing Ulyana had never thought about until this week.

Thirty-one names on the roster. Mrs. Harkaway had handed it over yesterday with the keys, a single typed sheet, thirty-one surnames in alphabetical order, and Ulyana had skimmed it in the car—Okafor, Tuller, Voss—and thought: a full room. A good-sized class. The kind of class where you learn names fast or drown.

Twenty-six desks.

Five desks short. It wasn't even a strange number of desks to be short; rooms got reconfigured, furniture broke, districts underfunded. There were a hundred boring explanations. Ulyana stood there drinking cold coffee and generating them like a machine: five desks in storage. Five desks removed for spacing. Five desks never delivered. Five desks—

"Room's set up how Mr. Aldous liked it."

Ulyana turned. The janitor was in the hallway behind them, pushing a mop bucket that didn't slosh so much as shift, heavy and sullen, like something being dragged against its will. He was maybe sixty, built like a fence post that had weathered a lot of winters, with a gray mustache that seemed to be doing most of his talking for him. His name tag said PELL, and beneath the name tag, faintly, as if the shirt had once carried another tag that had been picked off, a paler rectangle of fabric.

"Mr. Pell," Ulyana said. "Good morning. I'm—"

"I know who you are." He looked past them into the room, at the desks, and something moved across his face—not an expression exactly, more like weather. "Coldharbour. Harkaway said."

"That's right. Ulyana Coldharbour." They put out their hand.

Pell looked at the hand the way a man looks at a puddle he's deciding whether to step around. Then he shook it, briefly, his palm dry and cool as chalk. "Aldous taught here thirty-one years," he said. "Set the room up himself every August. Never let anybody touch the arrangement."

"He retired?"

"Mm." Pell said it in a way that contained no information whatsoever, a sound shaped like a word but empty as a bottle. He wheeled the bucket past the doorway and stopped. Looked in at the desks again. Ulyana watched him do it—watched him count, actually, saw his lips move faintly, saw the count land somewhere and stay there, the way a bird lands and then doesn't move.

"Mr. Pell," Ulyana said, "can I ask you something? The roster lists thirty-one students. There are twenty-six desks."

The mop bucket made a sound. Not a slosh. Something lower, wetter, like a swallowed syllable.

Pell didn't turn around. When he spoke, his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man walking on ice he already knew was thin. "You'll want to get your room key working on the supply closet. Sticks sometimes. You jiggle it left."

"That's not—"

"Aldous jiggled it left," Pell said. "Thirty-one years." And he pushed the bucket down the hall, wheels whispering on linoleum, and did not look back.

* * *

By nine-fifteen Ulyana had decided the whole thing was a clerical error and had almost convinced themselves.

It helped to be busy. First days were always chaos—finding the attendance system login (the password was written on a sticky note inside the desk drawer, *grayfen1*, which felt less like security than surrender), discovering the projector remote missing its battery cover, meeting the fifth-grade teacher next door, Ms. Amos, who had a laugh like a door hinge and immediately began telling Ulyana which kids to watch for ("the Voss girl bites, but only when she's scared, so really you're watching for what's scaring her").

At 9:40 the bell rang and the children came in.

They came in the way children come in everywhere, Ulyana supposed—a river of backpacks and noise, breaking around the doorway, spilling into seats. Ulyana stood at the board writing their name—*Ms./Mr. Villalobos is fine, or Ulyana if we're feeling brave*—and listened to the room settle, that particular settling sound, chairs and voices and the small civilization of a classroom assembling itself.

Then they turned around with the roster and started taking attendance, and the arithmetic happened again, except now it had faces attached.

Twenty-six children sat in twenty-six desks. Ulyana counted them twice while they answered to their names, because that was the kind of person they were, and both times the count came back twenty-six, and both times the roster in their hand insisted on thirty-one.

"Bram Tuller?" Ulyana said.

Silence. A girl in the second row—Wren Okafor, according to the seating chart Ulyana had improvised from the roster—looked up at the sound of the name. Just for a second. Just a flicker, like a window catching light from a room where nobody was standing. Then she looked down at her notebook.

"No Bram," said a boy near the window, cheerfully, the way children say things that end conversations. "Nobody's called Bram."

"It's on my list," Ulyana said lightly. "Maybe he's new. Maybe he moved." They smiled the smile teachers keep holstered for moments like this. "If anyone knows a Bram Tuller, send him my way."

Nothing. Twenty-five other names went smoothly after that, and then the roster still had four names left over, and Ulyana read them anyway, into the quiet, feeling increasingly like someone reading a menu in an empty restaurant:

"Dahlia Merrick?"

No one.

"Cole Fenwick?"

No one. Somewhere in the back, a chair creaked.

"Sable Pruitt?"

A boy in the third row raised his hand halfway. "My grandma's name is Sable," he offered. "But she's dead."

"Thank you, Marcus," Ulyana said, and heard Ms. Amos's hinge-laugh in memory, *watch for what's scaring her*, and wrote nothing down.

"Edith Grayfen?"

The silence after that one was different. Ulyana couldn't have said how—it just was, the way a room can go quiet in two different keys. Edith Grayfen. Named for the town, or the town named for her family; either way the name sat in the air like a stone dropped down a shaft, and everyone waited for it to hit bottom, and it didn't.

"Okay," Ulyana said brightly, too brightly, folding the roster in half like that solved anything. "New seating chart tomorrow. For today, sit where you are, and let's talk about what we're reading this year."

Wren Okafor didn't look up for the rest of the period. But when the bell rang and the river flowed out, she was the last one at the door, and she paused there—small, twelve or nearly, with her braids tied with blue thread and an expression much older than the thread—and she said, without preamble, the way children deliver verdicts:

"You counted wrong."

Ulyana blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"The desks." She said it kindly, which was the worst part. Kindness, in a child, aimed at an adult, about a thing the adult hadn't asked about. "You counted them twice. You always will. It won't help." And she was gone, blue threads and all, into the hallway noise.

* * *

Lunch was a cheese sandwich eaten standing up at the window while Ulyana tried the boring explanations on again, one by one, like coats that had all shrunk overnight.

Clerical error: possible. Districts made typos. But five extra names, all plausible, all local-sounding, none duplicated? Five was a lot of typo.

Desks in storage: Ulyana had checked the supply closet at recess, jiggling the key left, right, left, until it surrendered. Inside: paper, paint, a broken easel, a box of trophies with dates going back decades—1968 sat among them, tarnished, engraved *Grayfen Grammar — Regional*. No desks. No folded table legs. Nothing that had ever held a child.

And then there was the ledger.

Mrs. Harkaway had mentioned it yesterday—"you'll take attendance in the book, the district wants the paper trail"—and produced it from her office safe with two hands, like something hot. It was leather, old, cracked at the spine, and heavier than it should have been, the way old books are heavy with more than pages. ATTENDANCE was stamped on the cover in gold that had gone the color of old teeth.

Ulyana opened it at lunch, at the window, and understood within a page why Harkaway had handled it that way.

The early entries were beautiful—fountain pen, copperplate, decades of names marching in neat columns. 1954. 1953. Names Ulyana half-recognized from gravestones they'd passed on the walk up from the bus stop. And then, scattered through the columns like teeth knocked out of a jaw, gaps. Pages razored out—not torn, *razored*, close to the spine, by someone patient. Ulyana turned pages and found the scars where pages had been: a stub here, a sliver there, the ghost-line of glue. Whole months gone. Whole *springs* gone, several springs in a row across different years, as if someone had gone through history with scissors and a policy.

Ulyana counted the stubs. That was the kind of person they were.

Nine. Nine pages removed, in a book that was maybe sixty years old.

"You're doing it already."

Ulyana's sandwich hand jerked. Pell was standing in the classroom doorway with a recycling bin, though there was nothing in the room yet that needed recycling, and he was looking at the ledger, not at Ulyana.

"Doing what?" Ulyana said.

"Counting." He came in, set the bin down, and stood there with his hands hooked in his belt, working up to something. Outside the window the fog had thickened since morning; the playground had gone soft-edged, the swings moving in wind Ulyana couldn't feel through the glass. "Aldous used to stand right there. Same window. Same book."

"Mr. Pell, I have to ask somebody, so it might as well be you. The roster says thirty-one. There are twenty-six desks. This morning I called five names and no one answered, and when I asked about it, a ten-year-old told me I'd counted wrong, and the janitor told me about a supply closet." Ulyana kept their voice level, professional, the voice of someone filing a reasonable request. "I'm not trying to make trouble. I'm trying to teach. Which five kids am I missing?"

Pell was quiet long enough that the fog seemed to come into the room, filling the pause. Then he said, "You want the honest answer or the one that lets you sleep?"

"That's not a real choice."

"No," Pell agreed. "It ain't." He rubbed his mustache with the back of his wrist. "Roster's thirty-one because the district prints it off last spring's numbers. Desks are twenty-six because that's how many Aldous left out. Attendance book's got holes in it because—" He stopped. Started again, slower, choosing each word like a man crossing stones. "Because some years, some names don't need writing down. Everybody in this town knows which years those were. Nobody writes 'em down anymore, and nobody asks, and the school runs fine, and the kids get taught, and everybody gets to keep living here, which is more than most towns can say."

"Keep living here," Ulyana repeated. "That's a strange way to put it."

"It's the accurate way." Pell picked up the recycling bin, empty, having recycled nothing. At the door he stopped, and for a moment he looked older, and tired in a way that had layers to it, geological. "You're a Coldharbour. Half this town will tell you that means something. The other half will tell you it don't. Both halves are lying." He tapped the doorframe twice with one knuckle, an odd little ritual, superstitious or apologetic. "Here's the only advice worth giving, and it's free. Don't ask about the difference. Not the desks, not the names, not the book. Teach what walks in the door. Count what counts back. And when the fog comes up the hill tonight—" he glanced at the window, at the white wall of it pressing against the glass, closer than it had been at breakfast— "keep your lights on."

"Pell—"

"Jiggle the key left," he said, and was gone.

* * *

The afternoon went normally, which by then felt like a lie the building was telling.

Ulyana taught. The children learned, or performed learning. Wren Okafor wrote in her notebook constantly and covered it with her arm whenever Ulyana passed, and once, glancing sideways, Ulyana caught a glimpse of the page—not words but numbers, columns of them, tallied and crossed out and begun again, and beside the columns little marks like doors, drawn open, drawn shut, drawn open.

At three o'clock the bell rang and the river reversed and the room emptied, and Ulyana stood alone among twenty-six desks and did the thing they knew they shouldn't do, the thing Wren had promised wouldn't help.

They counted.

One, two, three... twenty-six. They walked the rows and touched each desktop, cold under their palm, and said the numbers aloud because saying them aloud made them realer, and at twenty-six they stood at the window with the roster unfolded in their hands and read the five names again. Tuller. Merrick. Fenwick. Pruitt. Grayfen.

When they looked up, their own reflection was waiting in the glass, pale in the fog-thick light, and behind the reflection, faintly, the room. And in the reflection—for one blink, one heartbeat, one count of *one*—there were thirty-one desks.

Ulyana spun around.

Twenty-six. Of course twenty-six. Light, shadow, suggestion, stress, the mind playing tricks on the tired—Ulyana fed themselves the whole litany, and it worked, mostly, the way a coat works in rain that hasn't decided to start yet.

Outside, the fog had reached the schoolhouse wall. It pressed against the windows like something listening. Down the hill, barely visible, the shape of the rented cottage waited with its porch light off, because Ulyana had forgotten to leave it on, because Ulyana hadn't known to, because nobody had said *keep your lights on* until today, and the walk home would be dark, and the doors of the cottage—how many doors did the cottage have? Four? Five?

Ulyana realized they'd never counted.

That night, they would.
