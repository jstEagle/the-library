## 12. Counting Doors

The dream, when it came, was not a dream. It was arithmetic.

Ulyana stood in a hallway that went on longer than the cottage had any right to be, counting doors under a light that buzzed like a trapped insect. *One. Two. Three.* Each door was the same door—the front door, with its paint blistered from years of sea fog—and each time they opened one, they stepped through into the same room they'd left. The hallway grew a door for every lap. By the time Ulyana woke, they had lost count somewhere past forty, and the losing of the count felt less like failure than like being *counted*.

They woke at 4:17 a.m. with their heart going hard and the taste of chalk dust in their mouth, though they hadn't touched chalk since Friday.

The bedroom was cold in a way the cottage never got. The oil radiator in the corner ticked and hissed, doing its work, and the window over the sink in the kitchen—Ulyana could hear it—was rattling in its frame the way it always did when the fog came off the fen in earnest. Normal sounds. A normal house. Ulyana lay in the dark and did the thing Vess had told them to do, the thing they'd been doing every night since the groceries and the rules and the long look over the top of her glasses: they counted the doors.

*Front door. Back door. Bedroom door. Bathroom door. The little door under the stairs that Vess said led to a crawlspace and nothing else, don't go poking.*

Five. Five doors, same as always. Same as the night before, when they'd counted twice and felt foolish both times.

Ulyana lay there and listened to the house settle, and told themselves the dream was stress. It was the ledger, probably. The roster with thirty-one names and twenty-six desks, and the way Mrs. Harkaway had smiled when Ulyana asked—too many teeth, too much patience, like a woman explaining long division to a child who kept insisting two plus two was a fish. It was Bram Tuller, whose desk had vanished by afternoon while the other children bent over their worksheets and no one—not one of them, not the aide, not the secretary, not even the girl who'd shared Bram's table—could say the name back to Ulyana without frowning like it was a word in a foreign language.

*Stress,* Ulyana told the ceiling. *You're a new teacher in a strange town. This is what that feels like.*

Then they got up to get water, because their mouth was dry, and on the way to the kitchen they counted the doors again, because the habit had teeth now, and habits with teeth don't let go.

The front door. The back door. The bedroom door behind them. The bathroom door ahead, on the left.

And then the little door under the stairs.

And then one more.

It stood where the hall narrowed before the kitchen, in a stretch of wall that had been, as far as Ulyana knew, nothing but plaster and a framed photograph of the fen at dusk—a photograph that was no longer there. The door was the same blistered paint as the front door. It was slightly open. Not ajar the way a door leans when a latch doesn't catch. Slightly open the way a mouth is slightly open when the thing behind it is listening.

Ulyana stood in the dark hallway in their socks and did not breathe for a long time.

*Count the doors nightly,* Vess had said, and she'd said it lightly, the way you'd say *take your boots off at the mat*, and then she hadn't looked away, and Ulyana had understood that the lightness was load-bearing. That underneath the grocery bags and the tin of shortbread and the lecture about the radiator was a woman handing her last living relative a survival manual and pretending it was a recipe.

Ulyana counted again, out loud, barely a whisper, because saying it made it a thing said and not a thing imagined.

"Six."

The word went into the hallway and did not echo. That was the worst part, later—the not-echoing. Sound in the cottage had always been generous, bouncing off the low ceilings, carrying. But the word *six* fell into the hall the way a stone falls into a well, and the house took it, and gave nothing back.

Ulyana did not go to the kitchen for water. Ulyana went back to bed with the lights on—all of them, every bulb the cottage had—and lay awake watching the door of their bedroom, which stayed firmly, blessedly shut, until the fog turned gray and the gulls started their arguments, and then they slept for ninety minutes of thin, shallow nothing.

* * *

In the morning, the seventh door was in the bedroom.

It had not been there when they fell asleep. Ulyana was sure of this the way you're sure of your own name. The bedroom had four walls: window, radiator, bed, and the door to the hall. Now there was a fifth wall's worth of door, set into the wall beside the window where the dresser stood, and the dresser was gone—not moved, not toppled, just *absent*, as if it had been a piece of furniture in a play that had closed. The door was blistered gray paint. It was slightly open. Through the gap: darkness, and a smell Ulyana knew from somewhere deep and early—wet stone, cold iron, the particular mineral breath of deep water in the dark.

The well. It smelled like the well.

Ulyana sat on the edge of the bed with their feet on cold floorboards and made themselves think like a teacher, because that was the one identity they had left that felt solid. *Observe. Record. Don't panic in front of the class.* They found the notebook they kept for lesson plans, turned to a clean page, and wrote, in the neat hand they used for board notes:

**Monday. 6:40 a.m. Doors: seven. (Should be five.) New door: bedroom, east wall. Dresser missing. Smell: well water.**

The handwriting looked insane on the page. It looked like the first page of a file that ended with a padded room. Ulyana wrote it anyway, because writing it made it evidence, and evidence could be fought.

They dressed in the bathroom with the door locked and the fan running, which was absurd—the fan did nothing against a smell that wasn't in the air so much as *under* it—and they did not look at the seventh door on their way out. They failed. You can't not look at a door. A door is an invitation with hinges. Out of the corner of their eye, all the way down the hall, Ulyana watched the gap of it in their peripheral vision the way you watch a stranger at the edge of a platform.

It had not moved. It did not need to move. It was exactly where it was, being open, and the darkness behind it was exactly as deep as it had been at 4 a.m., though the bedroom behind Ulyana was filling with gray morning light.

On the kitchen table, a note. Ulyana had locked the door—had checked it twice, had stood in the fog with their hand on the knob counting to ten—but the note was there anyway, propped against the sugar bowl in handwriting they knew better than their own.

*You counted. I felt it. Don't count out loud. It counts back faster when it hears you.*

No signature. No signature needed. Twenty years of silence and the handwriting hadn't changed, tall and slanted and strange, the letters like a child's idea of grown-up writing, because that's what it had been—Odile's writing, invented on a summer afternoon when Ulyana was seven, a secret alphabet they'd made up together and used for notes passed between the world and the porch.

Ulyana had not seen Odile write anything since. Had not seen Odile since Saturday night, standing at the edge of the porch light like a figure cut from fog, saying things in Ulyana's own seven-year-old voice.

The note was real. The paper was real. It smelled faintly of the same wet stone.

Ulyana put it in their bag, next to the lesson plans, next to the notebook with the evidence, and drove to school with the radio on loud, and did not think about the fact that the drive had felt shorter than it should have, that the fog had parted for them, that the road had seemed to *want* them at the school, and the school had seemed to want them in the building, and the building had wanted them in Room 3, at the desk, facing the twenty-six students who were, today, all present.

All present. All of them, even the ones whose names Ulyana had never heard before this morning, even the two girls at the back who had not been in the class last week, who smiled at Ulyana with identical, patient smiles, and whose names on the roster—Ulyana checked, hands not quite steady—had not been there yesterday.

The roster had thirty-one names. It had always had thirty-one names. Ulyana counted the heads twice during silent reading and got twenty-eight, and then got twenty-six, and then stopped counting, because Odile's note was in their bag and its advice was the only advice in the world that seemed to apply.

*It counts back faster when it hears you.*

* * *

Vess came at dusk, unannounced, the way she always did, with a covered dish and a face like a closed ledger.

She stood in the kitchen doorway—*the* kitchen doorway, the only one, Ulyana checked—and looked at the cottage the way a doctor looks at a patient over the top of a chart. Her eyes went wall to wall. Her mouth got thinner.

"Show me," she said.

Ulyana showed her. The hallway door, where the photograph of the fen had been. The bedroom door, where the dresser had been. Vess walked the cottage slowly, hands in the pockets of her good coat, and she did not touch anything, and she did not go within arm's reach of any of the doors, and when she came back to the kitchen her breathing was carefully, deliberately even, the breathing of a woman who has practiced being calm in this exact room.

"How many did you count?" she asked.

"Seven this morning."

"Out loud?"

"Once. At four in the morning."

Vess closed her eyes. For a moment—just a moment—she looked old. Not elderly; *old*, the way a house is old, the way a debt is old. Then she opened them and was Aunt Vess again, brisk and flinty, taking the kettle off its hook with hands that did not quite tremble.

"It's the season," she said. "The turn. It happens near the reckoning—the house starts... making room. It's done it before. It did it the year your mother was carrying you."

"Making room for what?"

Vess set the kettle on the burner. The click of the gas was very loud.

"For the count," she said. "The well counts, Ulyana. That's what it does. It counted your family before there was a Grayfen, and it counts us still, and when the count comes due it likes to have the numbers ready. The doors are it practicing." She said it the way you'd say *the tide comes in*. "Five doors in a Coldharbour house. Always five. When there are more, it means the count is close, and the house is learning how many mouths it needs."

Ulyana's skin went cold from the scalp down. "Mouths."

"Doors are mouths." Vess said it flatly, without looking at them. "That's the oldest rule and nobody writes it down because writing it down means thinking about it. A door opens both ways. That's the whole horror of a door, if you think about it, and the well thinks about it all the time." She turned, and her eyes were wet and furious and afraid, all three at once, which was more honesty than Vess had shown in twenty years. "You counted out loud. In the dark. Alone."

"It was a dream. I thought—"

"I know what you thought." The kettle began its low note toward boiling. Vess watched it as if it might tell her something. "Your mother did the same thing. The year before she died. Counted out loud at the window, and the next morning there were six doors, and she stood in the hall and counted them again, louder, like she was scolding it, like it was a child tracking mud." Vess's jaw worked. "It likes that. Being scolded. Being *acknowledged*. Every word you spend on it, it keeps."

Ulyana thought of the word *six* falling into the hallway without an echo. Taken. Kept.

"How many doors are there now?" they asked. "Right now. This minute."

Vess looked at them for a long moment. Then she took the covered dish out of its bag, set it on the table, and said, "Eat the stew. It's lamb. And tonight, when you count, count with your eyes and not your mouth, and whatever number you get—"

"How many, Vess."

Vess picked up her keys. At the kitchen door she paused, and did not turn around, and said, in a voice gone quiet and terrible:

"When I drove up, I counted the windows from the road, the way I always do. Windows and doors, out loud, in the car, where it can't hear proper. There were nine windows on the front of this house." She let that sit. "This house has four."

* * *

Ulyana ate the stew because refusing it would have meant thinking about why Vess had brought it, and they could only afford one impossible thing per evening.

Then they walked the cottage with the notebook, and the lights on, and their mouth shut.

Eight doors. The hallway had grown another—set into the wall opposite the first, so the narrow hall now had a door on each side, facing each other, both slightly open, like a corridor between two parted lips. Ulyana stood between them and felt the draft. There was no window in the hallway. There was no source of the draft. But it moved through the hall from one open door to the other, steady as breathing, and it smelled of wet stone, and if Ulyana stood very still in the middle of it they could feel it on the back of their neck like the attention of something patient.

They wrote it down. **8:50 p.m. Eight doors.** Their handwriting had gone ragged at the edges.

They checked the locks on the real doors—the true five, the honest five—and then they sat in the kitchen under the brightest bulb with the notebook and the cold tea and they did not sleep, because sleeping meant the hallway, and the hallway meant counting, and counting meant feeding it.

At some point past midnight, the gulls went quiet.

Not gradually. All at once, mid-cry, the way a record lifts its needle. The fog pressed against the windows. The radiator ticked into silence, though it was still on. And from the front of the cottage, from the direction of the porch, came a sound Ulyana knew from childhood and had spent twenty years pretending to forget: the soft, deliberate creak of the third stair. The one that announced visitors. The one Odile used to jump on, twice, to announce himself, because knocking was for strangers.

Ulyana did not move. The notebook was open on the table. The pen was in their hand. On the page, without their quite meaning to, their hand had written:

**9:00 p.m. Nine.**

They had not counted. They had not gotten up. But they knew, with the certainty of the arithmetic in the dream, that it was true—that the ninth door had come while they sat there, somewhere in the cottage, in a wall or a floor or a place walls go when they're not being watched, and that it was slightly open, and that through its gap the house was breathing.

The third stair creaked again. Then the porch boards. Then—faint, from somewhere below the floor, below the foundation, below the fen itself—a voice. Not Odile's. Not anyone's. A voice like water deciding to be a voice, and it was counting.

It started high and went down, the way you count with a child. *Five. Six. Seven.* Each number came from a different part of the house. *Five* from the hallway, from the facing doors. *Six* from under the stairs, from the little door to the crawlspace, which stood, Ulyana now understood, open. *Seven* from the bedroom. *Eight* from the kitchen wall, an inch from Ulyana's ear, though the wall was solid plaster and always had been.

The kettle ticked as it cooled. The gulls stayed silent. Ulyana sat in the light of one bulb with their pen moving, writing the numbers as they came, because if the house was going to count, then Ulyana was going to keep the record, and a record is a weapon, and a teacher knows how to keep a ledger.

*Eight,* said the kitchen wall.

And then the ninth door—wherever it was, in whatever wall it had grown—said, in a voice Ulyana knew, in a voice that had invented a secret alphabet on a summer afternoon twenty years ago, in the voice of the only friend Ulyana had ever had before the world got complicated:

*Nine.*

Odile's voice. Counting along. Keeping the well's numbers like a good messenger. Like a good jailer. Like something that had once chosen to stand between Ulyana and the dark and was now, apparently, showing the dark where the doors were.

Ulyana wrote it down with a hand that did not shake, because shaking would have meant feeling it, and they could not afford to feel it yet.

**Midnight. Nine doors. Odile is counting for it.**

Outside, far off across the black fen, something answered the nine with a sound like a great wet mouth closing—and then, faint and clear, carried on fog that had no business carrying anything, a child's voice began to sing the counting song. The one from the schoolyard. The one the children sang at recess without knowing they knew it.

*One for the secret, two for the stone, three for the child that walks alone...*

It was not coming from the fen.

It was coming from under the floorboards. And it was singing Wren Okafor's name.
