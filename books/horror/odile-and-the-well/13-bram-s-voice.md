## 13. Bram's Voice

Recess at Grayfen Elementary was twenty minutes of gray light and thinner sound. The playground sat in a bowl between the school and the tree line, and the fog came down into it like something poured, so that by October the children at the far end of the yard played half-dissolved, their shouts arriving a beat late, as if the air were reluctant to carry them.

Ulyana stood on the steps with a thermos of coffee they weren't drinking and watched the count.

They counted the children now. They couldn't help it. Twenty-two out today, which matched the roster, which was itself a small miracle—since Bram Tuller's desk had vanished three weeks ago along with every memory of him except Ulyana's own. Twenty-two children in the fog, and Ulyana counted them twice, then a third time, because counting had become a prayer they didn't believe in but said anyway.

Twenty-two. All present. The number held.

"Miss Coldharbour." Wren Okafor appeared at their elbow without seeming to cross the intervening space, the way she always did. She was twelve and built out of angles and certainty, and she looked up at Ulyana with the flat, unblinking attention of a child who has decided adults are mostly lying and is waiting for one to stop. "You do that every day."

"Do what?"

"Count us."

Ulyana took a sip of coffee to buy a second. It had gone cold and tasted like the inside of a thermos. "Teachers count their students, Wren. That's the job."

"Not like that." Wren squinted out at the yard. "You count like you're afraid we'll come out wrong. Like the number will be different if you look away." She turned back. "Is that what you're afraid of?"

The fog moved behind her, slow and tidal. Somewhere across the yard a girl shrieked with laughter and the sound bent strangely, arriving before it should have.

"Wren," Ulyana said carefully, "have you been sleeping?"

Wren's face did something complicated—the quick shutter of a child who has been asked the wrong question by the right person. "I dream about the well," she said. "But you knew that. You asked me last week." She rocked on her heels. "My mum says I'm too old for nightmares. She says dreams are just the brain taking out the rubbish." A pause, and then, with terrible casualness: "In my dream there's a man down there counting. He never gets past thirty-one. He starts over and over. He sounds tired."

The coffee went through Ulyana like ice water. Thirty-one. The roster number. The number of desks that should have been in Room 4.

"What does he look like?" Ulyana heard themselves ask, and hated the hunger in it.

"I don't know. He's more of a voice." Wren shrugged. "Anyway. Recess is nearly done. You should stop counting and start herding." And she was gone, threading back into the fog toward the swings, leaving Ulyana alone on the steps with a cold cup and a heartbeat doing something arrhythmic against their ribs.

* * *

It started, when it started, as a wrongness in the sound of the yard.

Ulyana noticed it the way you notice a clock stopping—not the silence itself but the shape of the space where the noise used to be. The shouting didn't stop all at once; it thinned. The fog seemed to drink it. One by one the games wound down, not because any whistle blew but because the children drifted toward stillness, standing in loose clusters, faces tilted, like dogs listening to a frequency humans can't hear.

Except Ulyana could hear it.

At first it was only a rhythm under the fog, faint as a pulse through a wall. Then it resolved.

*One for the water, two for the stone,*

*three for the dark where you walk alone—*

A child's voice. Thin, cracked at the edges, hoarse from disuse or from screaming. It rose out of the ground itself—from the low ground beyond the fence, past the dead garden, from the direction of the old Coldharbour parcel where the well stood in its collar of blackthorn, four hundred yards away through fog and falling leaves.

Four hundred yards. And the voice carried as if its mouth were pressed to Ulyana's ear.

*four for the rope and five for the fall,*

*six for the ones who don't answer at all—*

"Bram," Ulyana whispered.

The name left them before they could stop it, and the singing stopped too. In its place: breathing. Wet, labored, close. The sound of someone drawing air through a throat that had been crying.

*Miss?*

The voice was smaller than Ulyana remembered. Bram Tuller had been a big boy, loud, forever in trouble for talking—but the thing speaking now was small and scraped hollow, a voice worn down to wire.

*Miss Coldharbour. Is that you? Please be you.*

Ulyana gripped the railing. The iron was slick with condensation and cold enough to burn. Around the yard, the children stood motionless in their clusters, heads bowed, and none of them turned toward the fence, none of them flinched, because none of them heard anything at all. Only Ulyana. Only ever Ulyana.

"I'm here," Ulyana said. Their voice came out as a ruin. They glanced wildly at the teachers' bench—Mrs. Anselm sat knitting something gray, needles clicking, eyes fixed on nothing—and lowered their voice anyway, though there was no lowering it enough. "Bram. Bram, are you—are you hurt?"

A long pause. When the voice came again it was closer, or sounded closer, and Ulyana understood with a lurch that distance meant nothing to whatever was carrying it.

*I fell. That's what I tell myself. I fell.* A wet laugh with no humor in it. *There's a ledge. There's water at the bottom but it's not—it doesn't behave right, miss. It's warm. Why would it be warm?*

"How long have you been down there?" Ulyana's mind was sprinting down corridors and finding every door locked. Call the police—with what? *Officer, a missing child is singing to me from a dry well on my family's land*? They'd seen how Grayfen handled trouble. They'd read Harkaway's face when they'd asked about absences, that patient, pitying smile, the word *committed* hovering unspoken behind it like weather.

*I don't know. Days. Weeks. It's hard to—there's no morning down here. Just the counting.* The breath caught. *He counts us. He counts and counts and he never gets it right, and when he gets it wrong he starts over, and we have to start over with him.*

"Us," Ulyana said. "Bram—who else is down there?"

Silence. Then, very quietly, in the tone of a child delivering news he knows will break an adult:

*Lots of us, miss.*

The fog pressed in. Somewhere behind Ulyana, Mrs. Anselm's needles clicked on, steady as a metronome, and Ulyana understood suddenly that the clicking was not idle—that it had a rhythm, that the rhythm matched the counting song, and that Mrs. Anselm's eyes were fixed on nothing because they were fixed on nothing *on purpose*.

*She used to leave the window open,* Bram said, as if following the same thought. *Before. In the classroom. At night, when they brought us. Did you know that? There's a door under the school. Under the floor by the coat racks. It's bricked up but the bricks are old and some of them move.* His voice dropped further, urgent now, scraping. *Miss, listen. Listen to me. He lets us talk sometimes. He likes it when we talk to the ones who can hear. He says it keeps the—*

The voice cut off. Not faded—cut, clean, like a hand over a mouth.

And then a different voice spoke from the well, and it was not a child's voice at all. It was vast and unhurried and it did not so much make sound as displace it, the way deep water displaces light, and it said:

***Not yet.***

The yard snapped back to life around Ulyana like a film resuming mid-scene. Children scattered to their games. The whistle blew—or had blown, or was blowing; time stuttered and settled. Mrs. Anselm set down her knitting and rang the bell, and the bell's sound arrived whole and ordinary and obscene.

Wren Okafor was standing at the bottom of the steps, looking up at Ulyana.

"You went somewhere," she said. Not a question. Her eyes were enormous and dry. "Just now. Your face went somewhere else." She climbed one step, then another, until they were level, until she could say the next part quietly. "Was it the well?"

Ulyana crouched to her height. Up close they could see she hadn't been sleeping—hadn't slept in days, maybe weeks, the skin under her eyes bruised violet. Twelve years old and already fluent in the grammar of things unsaid.

"Wren," Ulyana said, "if I told you something true, and it was frightening, and no grown-up in this town would believe it—what would you do?"

Wren considered this with the seriousness of a judge.

"I'd write it down," she said. "Dates and everything. My gran taught me that. She said paper remembers what people pretend to forget." A beat. "Then I'd find the one person who'd believe me. Even if it was just one."

"And if that person told you to stay away from something? To stay home, lock your doors, let them handle it?"

Wren's chin lifted. "Then I'd know they were lying about handling it," she said, "because nobody handles anything by telling kids to hide."

The bell rang again. The children began streaming up the steps, past them, a river of small bodies and fog-damp coats, and Wren held Ulyana's gaze one second longer before joining the current—her hand brushing the railing exactly where Ulyana's own hand had gripped it, as if checking the temperature of fear.

* * *

Ulyana did not go back inside.

They walked the perimeter instead, slow, casual, a teacher stretching her legs, while their heart hammered out the counting song's rhythm against their ribs. At the back corner of the lot, where the chain-link sagged away from its post and the dead garden began, they stopped.

From here, on a clear day, you could see the blackthorn crowning the hill. Today there was only fog, but Ulyana felt the well the way you feel a tooth that's gone bad—a hot point of attention, a place in the landscape that was aware of being looked at.

*Bram Tuller is alive,* they thought, and the thought had weight, mass, gravity. *Bram Tuller is alive and he is down there and everyone in this town has agreed, silently, mutually, without ever saying a word, to let him be.*

They thought of the ledger with its torn pages. Of Mr. Pell's warning—*don't ask about the difference*—delivered in a voice like a man setting down something heavy. Of Harkaway's praise: *You don't make trouble, Miss Coldharbour. That's rare in a new teacher.* Of Aunt Vess's rules, recited over groceries like a recipe: lights on, doors counted, never answer the well.

Never answer the well.

Ulyana's hands were shaking. They looked down at them and saw, absurdly, chalk dust still on the fingers from the morning's arithmetic, and something in them went quiet and hard and final, the way a decision feels before the mind has finished making it.

Because here was the thing Ulyana had been circling for weeks without landing: the missing children were not missing. They were taken. They were taken and kept, alive, in the dark, beneath a town that paid for the privilege in silence and small envelopes at harvest meetings. And every adult in Grayfen—the knitter, the principal, the janitor, the aunt who brought eggs and warnings in equal measure—every single one of them knew, and every single one of them had chosen, day after day, year after year, to let the counting continue.

*He lets us talk sometimes. He likes it when we talk to the ones who can hear.*

Which meant the well had wanted Ulyana to hear. Which meant this—this exact moment, this cold railing, this sick certainty—was part of its design, or its appetite, or its game. Ulyana knew that. Knowing it changed nothing. There are traps you walk into anyway because the alternative is becoming the kind of person who walks around them.

"Miss?" A small tug at their sleeve. One of the second-graders, a girl with braids and a runny nose, looking up with mild concern. "Recess is over. You missed the line."

"So I did," Ulyana said, and found a smile from somewhere, and walked her back toward the building, and at the door they stopped and looked back once at the fog swallowing the hill.

That night, after the lights were on and the doors were counted—five, five, still five, though each one stood open a finger's width, though the house breathed through them—Ulyana sat at the kitchen table with a sheet of paper and wrote, in the steadiest hand they could manage:

*Bram Tuller. Taken approximately [date]. Alive as of today. Location: the well, Coldharbour land. There is a door beneath the school, near the coat racks. Bricked. Some bricks are loose.*

Paper remembers what people pretend to forget.

They folded it twice and hid it inside the cover of the leather attendance ledger, among the torn stubs of pages, where no one in Grayfen would ever willingly look.

On the porch, visible through the window, Odile stood at the rail facing the hill, tall and gray and motionless, and did not turn around, and did not speak, and somehow that silence was the loudest warning yet:

*You heard him. Now he knows you can.*
