## 17. Odile Interferes

The constable's office in Grayfen was a converted feed store on Corrick Street, wedged between a shuttered pharmacy and a laundromat that had been "closed for repairs" so long the sign had bleached to the color of bone. Ulyana had walked past it four times that week without going in. On Friday morning, after Wren Okafor looked up from her desk and said, *I dreamed about the well again, Miss Coldharbour. It said my name like it knew me*, Ulyana stopped walking past.

They stood on the sidewalk with their hand on the door, and the fog pressed close around them, and somewhere down the street a dog began to bark—not at anything, just at the air—and then stopped all at once, mid-sound, as if a hand had closed over its muzzle.

"You shouldn't do this."

Ulyana turned. Odile was standing at the edge of the sidewalk where the pavement met the gravel shoulder, gray and tall and still, the way a heron is still before it strikes. There were no footprints in the frost behind them. There never were.

"This is none of your business," Ulyana said.

"Everything here is my business." Odile's voice hadn't changed in twenty years either. It was the voice of someone reading aloud in an empty room—flat, careful, each word placed like a stone on a wall. "You're going to tell a stranger what happens in Grayfen."

"I'm going to report a missing child."

"Wren isn't missing yet."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"It's supposed to make you *stop*." Odile took one step closer, and the fog seemed to lean with them, dragging a gray curtain across the street so that the constable's office blurred and wavered like something seen through water. "If you go through that door, you break the terms."

Ulyana laughed, and hated the sound of it—too high, too thin, the laugh of someone standing too near a drop. "Terms. You sound like Aunt Vess. What terms? I never signed anything. My great-grandmother signed something, maybe, if half of what you've told me is true. The debt isn't mine."

"The debt is the blood's." Odile tilted their head, and in the flat white light their face did something wrong—not moving so much as *rearranging*, features settling into new positions like furniture shifted in the dark. "You are the blood. You have always been the blood. That's why they gave you the cottage. That's why they gave you the ledger. That's why I'm here."

"Why *are* you here, Odile?" Ulyana stepped back from the door, not because they wanted to but because some animal part of them understood that you don't argue with a thing like this while standing inside its reach. "Twenty years. I drew you in crayon. I told you secrets. And now you show up on my porch and quote my own childhood back at me and stand between me and a police station. What are you?"

For a moment—one long, cold moment—Odile said nothing at all. The fog moved around them without touching them. Down the block, the laundromat's dead neon buzzed once and went dark.

"I'm what stands between you and the well," Odile said finally. "As long as you stay inside the terms."

* * *

They walked home the long way, because Ulyana refused to be escorted and Odile refused to leave, and the compromise was a silence that stretched the whole two miles up from Corrick Street through the birch woods to the Coldharbour land. Ulyana kept waiting for the feeling of being followed to fade. It didn't. It wasn't menacing, exactly—that was the worst part. It felt like being walked home by a parent after a playground fight, except Ulyana was forty-one years old and the fight was with an entire town and the parent had been invented by a seven-year-old with too many nightmares.

At the gate, Ulyana rounded on them. "Say it plainly. If I talk to the authorities—if I file a report, call county, whatever—what happens?"

"Interference voids the protection." Odile stood with their hands folded, patient as weather. "The bargain has conditions. The Coldharbours keep the payments current. They keep quiet. They keep the well fed and the town fed and no one speaks of what lies under the school or who lies under the hill. In exchange, the well takes only what it is given. One child per season. Never a Coldharbour. Never more than the count allows."

"And if I speak?"

"Then the count doesn't matter anymore. The count is a courtesy, Ulyana. A schedule. A way of keeping the hunger orderly." Odile's eyes—gray, lidless in a way Ulyana had never let themselves notice as a child—did not blink. "Break the order and there is no schedule. There is no *one*. There is only the taking, until it is full, and it is never full."

"You're threatening me."

"I'm describing the world to you. There's a difference, though I understand it's hard to see from where you're standing." Something moved across Odile's face that might, in a human face, have been pain. "I watched what the well does when it isn't paid. Before your time. Before the schedule held. Ask your aunt about the winter of '68. Ask Mr. Pell, if he'll speak at all. Three children in one week, and the third was found—" Odile stopped. Recalibrated. "Found is not the right word. *Returned* is not the right word either. There is no word for what came back up out of that water wearing her coat."

Ulyana's stomach turned over. "Wren is twelve years old."

"Yes."

"She dreams about the well. She told me it knows her name. That means it's already chosen her, doesn't it? The season's tithe. Your neat little schedule already has her name written in it."

Odile was quiet for long enough that the wind filled the space, moving through the bare birches with a sound like paper being counted.

"Yes," Odile said at last. "It has her name. And if you do nothing, she will walk down to the water on the appointed night, because the well makes sure the ones it wants stop being afraid, and she will go into it gently, and the town will grieve politely, and the ledger will balance, and next spring everyone will plant their gardens. That is the protection. That is what your family bought with other people's children for a hundred years. I am telling you the price of ending it, and the price is not zero. It has never been zero. Nothing in Grayfen costs nothing."

* * *

Inside the cottage, Ulyana put the kettle on because their hands needed something stupid to do. The house was warm. Aunt Vess had stocked the woodbox. On the kitchen table sat the leather attendance ledger Ulyana had brought home from the school—the one with pages torn out, the one whose remaining entries sometimes rearranged themselves when Ulyana wasn't looking directly at them. They had taken it home intending to study it. They had mostly been avoiding it.

Five doors in the cottage. Ulyana counted them now, out of habit, out of Vess's rule: front, back, bedroom, pantry, the little door under the stairs that opened onto nothing but cobwebs and a smell of cold stone. Five. All shut. They made themselves look at each one twice.

When they turned around, Odile was sitting at the kitchen table.

Not entering. Not opening any door. Just *there*, seated in the chair across from the ledger, hands flat on the wood, as though they'd always been sitting there and Ulyana had simply failed to notice. The chair didn't creak. The floorboards didn't shift. Odile weighed nothing; Odile displaced nothing; Odile was a hole in the shape of a person, and the house poured itself around the hole.

"You can't stay in here," Ulyana said.

"I've stayed in worse places."

"That's not—I mean *you can't*. This is my house."

"Is it?" Odile looked around the kitchen with something almost like curiosity. "Your aunt grew up in this room. Her mother before her. The table is older than the town charter. Every Coldharbour child has counted these doors, and every Coldharbour child has been taught to count them by someone who loved them enough to teach counting as a survival skill." Their gaze came back to Ulyana. "You used to count doors with me. When you were small. Do you remember? You'd get scared at night and you'd say, *count the doors, Odile, count the doors with me,* and we'd count them together, and when we reached the last one you could sleep."

Ulyana remembered. God help them, they remembered—the small dark bedroom in the house in Marrow County, the summer the bad year happened, the summer their parents' marriage went to glass. Counting doors with a friend nobody else could see. Counting until the numbers became a rope and the rope held.

"You were real," Ulyana said quietly. "Before. Weren't you. You weren't imaginary at all. You were a child, and they—"

"Don't." For the first time since the porch, Odile's composure cracked. It was a small crack—a tightening around the mouth, a stillness that became rigid instead of calm—but Ulyana had spent twenty years teaching children and knew every species of flinch. "That is not tonight's conversation."

"When is it? Because you keep showing up, Odile. You keep standing in doorways and quoting rules, and every time I ask what you actually *are*, you change the subject like a politician. So here's what I think. I think you know exactly what it's like down there. I think you know what Wren will feel on the appointed night. And I think that's why you can't stand here and watch me pick up the phone."

The kettle began to scream. Neither of them moved toward it. It screamed and screamed and then, absurdly, wound down on its own, subsiding to a mutter, to a whisper, to nothing—as if the house itself had decided the noise was unwelcome.

"If you contact the authorities," Odile said, very evenly, "the first thing that happens is nothing. That's important for you to understand. No lightning. No flood. The well is patient the way geology is patient. The second thing that happens is that Mrs. Harkaway hears of it by evening—she hears everything in this town by evening—and by morning there will be a letter recommending a medical leave. By the following week, if you persist, there will be doctors. There have been teachers in Grayfen who asked questions, Ulyana. There is a filing cabinet in the school basement with their names on folders, and the folders are thick, and none of those teachers ever taught anywhere again. The town protects the bargain the way your body protects a wound—with inflammation, with scar tissue, with whatever it must do to keep the poison sealed inside."

"So I should let Wren die to protect my career."

"You should let Wren die to protect thirty other children, and the town, and yourself, and the last shreds of a family that traded everything it had for the privilege of continuing." Odile leaned forward, and the light in the kitchen bent subtly toward them, the way water bends toward a drain. "But that isn't why you won't make the call. Let's be honest with each other, since honesty is apparently the currency of this household now. You won't make the call because some part of you already believes me. Some part of you heard Bram Tuller's voice coming up out of the ground and felt it *recognize* you. Some part of you knows that the world has a floor, and you've seen through a crack in it, and you understand that screaming about the crack won't mend it. It will only widen it."

Ulyana gripped the counter. The enamel was cold enough to hurt. "Then tell me what to do instead. If the system is rotten and the authorities are complicit and the town is scar tissue, tell me the alternative, Odile. Because right now you're not a guardian. You're a jailer. You're the lock on the door of a burning building telling me fire safety regulations prohibit exits."

Something flickered in Odile's face at the word *jailer*—something old and wounded and briefly, terribly young.

"There is an alternative," Odile said. "There is always an alternative. But it isn't the constable, and it isn't the county, and it isn't the newspaper in Harlow Bend that prints the fishing reports. The well cannot be policed. It can only be *renegotiated*. And renegotiation requires standing at its edge with something it wants more than a child." They rose from the chair without the chair making a sound. "Go to the harvest meeting tomorrow night. Watch what the town does. Watch how they pay. Then decide whether these people deserve to be saved by exposure—or whether they'll only drown you along with the truth."

"And if I decide to expose them anyway?"

Odile paused at the threshold of the kitchen—standing, Ulyana realized with a chill, precisely where no doorway existed, framed by empty air as if the house had grown a sixth door just to hold them.

"Then I will have a choice to make too," Odile said. "And I am asking you—*begging* you, in the only language I have left—not to make me choose."

Then they were gone, and the kitchen was just a kitchen, and outside the window the fog lay against the glass like something listening.

* * *

Ulyana didn't sleep. Around two in the morning they gave up trying and sat at the kitchen table with the ledger open under the lamp, and read the surviving pages again, slowly, the way you'd read a ransom note. Names. Dates. Amounts. And beside certain dates, in a cramped antique hand, single words: *paid in full. paid in full. substituted—see private book.* And once, in ink so fresh it might have been written yesterday: *one owed.*

They were still staring at that phrase when headlights swept the curtains. A car coming up the hill road at three in the morning, slow, stopping somewhere near the gate. An engine ticking. Then silence, then the crunch of footsteps on gravel, deliberate, unhurried, and a knock at the front door—three knocks, spaced evenly, polite as a summons.

Through the window, in the wash of the porch light, Ulyana saw Aunt Vess standing on the step, coatless in the cold, holding a shoebox in both arms the way you hold something that might still be breathing.

"Open the door, Ulyana," Vess called softly. "We need to talk about Wren. And about what your friend Odile hasn't told you."
