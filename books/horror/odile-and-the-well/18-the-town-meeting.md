## 18. The Town Meeting

The notice went up on the schoolhouse door on a Tuesday: HARVEST MEETING — GRAYFEN GRANGE HALL — ALL FAMILIES ATTENDING. No time was listed. No agenda. Just the words, printed in the same careful hand that had filled out thirty years of attendance ledgers, and beneath them a small line Ulyana had to read three times before it made sense: *Please come settled.*

Settled. As in debts. As in accounts.

Mrs. Harkaway mentioned it at Friday staff meeting without looking up from her tea. "You'll want to be there," she said. "New teachers always attend their first one. It's considered—" she turned the word over like a stone she was checking for worms— "*courteous*."

"Is it a fundraiser?" Ulyana asked.

Harkaway's cup paused halfway to her mouth. For a moment her eyes did something complicated, the way a person's eyes do when they're deciding how much of a lie a listener can carry. "It's for the school," she said finally. Which was true, Ulyana would learn, in the way that a noose is for the neck.

* * *

Ulyana walked to the Grange Hall alone. Odile had been strange all day—not speaking, not even repeating, just standing at the edge of the yard where the fog pooled thickest, gray against gray, like a figure someone had started drawing and abandoned. When Ulyana had asked if they were coming, Odile had shaken their head once, slow, and said in Ulyana's own seven-year-old voice: *"You said you'd go even if I told you not to."*

"I don't remember saying that."

*"You will."*

The hall sat on the flat stretch of Main Street between the feed store and the shuttered dentist's office, a broad white building with a pitched roof and windows that glowed amber in the early dark. October had come down hard this week; the fog didn't lift anymore so much as change texture, thickening at dusk into something almost granular, like breath on cold glass. Ulyana could hear the meeting before they reached the door—not talking, exactly. A murmur with no words in it. The sound of a room full of people being quiet together, which is its own language.

Inside, folding chairs in rows, maybe two hundred of them, and nearly every one full. Ulyana recognized faces from the classroom doorway: Wren Okafor's mother, tall and straight-backed in the third row, Wren herself beside her with a library book open on her knees, reading by the light of a lantern on the stage. Bram Tuller's father—or the man who would have been Bram Tuller's father, though when Ulyana had tried to speak to him about his son he'd looked at them the way you look at someone who has addressed you in a language you've decided not to know. He sat near the back. His hands were folded. His knuckles were white.

Mr. Pell was there too, wedged into an aisle seat, his janitor's coveralls exchanged for a suit that fit him like a memory of a thinner man. He caught Ulyana's eye and gave a small shake of his head. Ulyana couldn't tell what it meant. *Don't sit here. Don't ask questions. Don't be here at all.* All of it, probably.

There were no refreshments. That was the first wrong thing. Harvest meetings, in every town Ulyana had ever lived in, meant cider and paper plates and children running underfoot. Here there was only the lantern light, the murmur, and at the front of the room a long table draped in black cloth, empty except for a single object: a wooden box, old and dark, about the size of a bread loaf, with a slot cut into its lid.

A collection box. But there was nothing written on it. No cross, no star, no name of any god or fund or cause. Just the slot, and around the slot a ring of small carved marks that Ulyana's eyes slid off of, again and again, like a thumb sliding off wet glass.

Ulyana took a seat at the end of the last row, near the door. Professional optimism, they told themselves. Community engagement. You wanted to know this town; here is the town knowing itself.

At seven o'clock by the church clock across the street—which struck seven with a sound like a coin dropped in a dry well—the murmur stopped.

All at once. Two hundred people going silent in the same breath, as if someone had cut a wire.

A woman rose from the front row. Ulyana recognized her distantly: Mrs. Aldery, whose daughter Maren was in the fourth grade, a round cheerful woman who waved at pickup every day. She walked to the table without hurry. She opened her purse. She took out an envelope—plain, unmarked—and stood before the box for a moment with her head bowed, not praying, Ulyana thought. Listening. The way you hold still to hear whether a sound in the house is the furnace or something else.

Then she fed the envelope into the slot and returned to her seat, and she never once looked up. Not at the box. Not at the room. Not at anyone. Her eyes stayed fixed on the floorboards in front of her chair the entire way, as if the floor were the only honest thing in the building.

And then the next person rose. And the next.

It went on for over an hour.

Envelope after envelope. Farmer, shopkeeper, teacher, the young doctor who'd come from the city two years ago and was rumored to be leaving. Each one walked the same walk, bowed the same bow, paid the same blind payment, and returned to the same seat with the same terrible economy of motion, like figures in a music box winding through their single permitted gesture. Nobody spoke. Nobody sang. A baby started to fuss somewhere in the middle rows and was hushed so quickly, so completely, that Ulyana felt the hush like a hand on their own throat.

Ulyana watched Wren's mother rise and pay. Watched her sit back down and put her arm around Wren, who had closed her book and was watching the box with an expression no twelve-year-old should have: not fear. Recognition. The look of a child doing arithmetic she has done before and gotten the same answer every time.

Wren looked up then, across the room, and found Ulyana's eyes. Held them. And very slightly—so slightly that only someone watching for it would see—shook her head.

*Don't,* the head-shake said. Or possibly: *Not yet.* With Wren it was hard to tell the difference between a warning and a plan.

* * *

Near the end, the envelopes changed.

Ulyana noticed it gradually, the way you notice water rising around your ankles. The first fifty envelopes had been thick. You could tell by the sound they made going into the slot—a soft papery slap, the sound of folded bills. But somewhere past the halfway mark the sound thinned. A drier slap. Then, from some people, almost no sound at all: an envelope so light it entered the box like a leaf entering water.

And the people paying the thin ones—Ulyana began to map them, because mapping was the only thing keeping the panic at a manageable temperature—they were different afterward. The thick-payers returned to their seats and folded their hands and stared at the floor like everyone else, but there was something loose in their shoulders, something spent but relieved, the posture of a bill paid in full. The thin-payers came back differently. Tighter. Their eyes stayed on the floor too, but their jaws were set, and several of them, Ulyana noticed, kept one hand flat against their own chest or stomach, pressing down, as if holding something in place that wanted to rise.

Bram Tuller's father paid last of all the parents. His envelope made no sound at all. When he sat back down, he pressed both hands flat against his thighs and stared straight ahead—at the wall, past the box, past everything—and his mouth moved once, silently, shaping a word. Ulyana read it off his lips from twenty feet away.

*Bram.*

Then he looked down at the floor like everyone else, and the word was gone, and it was as if it had never been in the room.

When the last envelope was in, Mrs. Aldery rose again, carried the box off the stage through a door at the back, and returned without it. The meeting simply ended. No announcements, no votes, no closing words. People stood, gathered coats, and filed out into the fog in a silence so complete that the only sounds were chair-scrape and breath. They passed Ulyana at the end of the row without looking at them, without looking at each other, two hundred people leaving a building in which they had all just done the same thing and none of whom would ever say so.

Ulyana stood in the emptying hall a long time.

The lantern on the stage had burned low. In its shrunken light the black cloth on the table looked less like cloth than like a hole cut in the shape of a table. Ulyana walked up and put their hand where the box had been. The wood underneath was warm. Not lamp-warm. Body-warm. The specific warmth of something that had recently been held, or that was holding itself.

There was a scratch on the tabletop, near the edge, half-hidden by the drape. Old, but not ancient—decades, not centuries. Letters, gouged deep, by someone working fast and in the dark:

**COUNT US**

Ulyana took their hand back. Across the street, the church clock began striking eight, and on the eighth stroke, somewhere below the floor of the Grange Hall—below the foundation, below whatever the foundation was founded on—came a sound that Ulyana felt in their teeth rather than heard.

A count. Answering.

* * *

Outside, the fog had thickened to wool. Figures moved in it, homeward, dim as fish under ice. Ulyana fell into step behind Mr. Pell, whose suit shoulders rode unevenly, whose gait had the careful stiffness of a man conserving what little strength the evening had left him.

"Pell." Ulyana kept their voice low. "What's in the envelopes?"

He didn't stop walking. "Money, mostly."

"*Mostly.*"

"Some years money's enough." He said it to the fog ahead of him, not to Ulyana. "Some years it isn't. Some families got more owed than cash covers. So they put in what they got." A pause. His breath came out white and hung there. "You saw the thin ones."

"I saw them."

"Don't ever let yours get thin." He stopped at the corner where his road bent downhill toward the school. He still wouldn't look at Ulyana directly; he spoke to a point slightly left of their shoulder, the way people talk to ghosts, or to the versions of themselves they're ashamed of. "First year I worked the school, there was a teacher—Miz Ansel. Bright girl. Asked questions at her first harvest meeting, loud ones, right there in the hall. Wanted to know where the money went." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "She taught out the year. Come spring she packed her car and drove off and nobody's seen hide nor hair. And the next fall there was a new teacher, and the new teacher's first meeting, she sat in the back row and paid attention and asked nothing, and she lived a long life and died in her bed at eighty-one, and they named the reading room after her."

"What happened to Miz Ansel?"

Pell finally looked at Ulyana. His eyes in the fog-light were the color of dishwater, and very tired, and underneath the tiredness something moved that might have been pity or might have been fear wearing pity's coat.

"Nothin' happened to her," he said. "That's the thing you keep missin', teacher. Nothin' *has* to happen. The town does it. Town makes you so lonely, so crazy-seeming, so *unbelonged*, that leavin' feels like your own idea." He pulled his collar up. "Well sends no monsters after the troublesome. It just lets the town do what towns do."

He turned down his road. Three steps into the fog he stopped, without turning around.

"You counted the envelopes tonight. I watched you countin'. You count everything—it's why you're still sane, probably, and it's why you won't stay safe." The fog took his voice and gave it back thinner. "Go home. Count your doors. And teacher—" the pause stretched long enough that Ulyana thought he was done— "count 'em twice. Tonight of all nights. There's a feelin' in the air like the year's runnin' short."

Then he was gone, and Ulyana walked home through a town of drawn curtains and banked lights, past houses where behind every window a family was sitting very still around a kitchen table, having paid, having not looked up, having agreed together without a single spoken word to keep agreeing.

* * *

The cottage was dark when Ulyana got there, except for the porch light, burning.

Odile was not on the porch. Odile was inside, standing in the front room, facing the interior wall—the blank wall between the kitchen and the stairs, a wall with no door in it, had never had a door in it—and as Ulyana came in, shaking fog off their coat, Odile raised one long gray hand and laid a finger against the wallpaper.

"Count," Odile said. It wasn't Ulyana's child-voice tonight. It was something older, flatter, worn smooth like a river stone. "Now."

Ulyana counted. Front door. Kitchen door. Back door. Bedroom door. Cellar hatch. Five. Five doors, five doors, five—

On the third counting, between the bedroom and the cellar, there was another.

A sixth door. Narrow, unpainted, set into the hallway wall where the hallway had always been solid plaster. It was closed. Around its frame, faint as breath on glass, the wood was warm.

"Five," Ulyana said aloud, because saying it made it a professional observation instead of a madness. "There are five doors."

Odile's finger was still on the blank wall. Slowly, without turning, they shook their head.

"There are six," Odile said. "And tomorrow there will be seven. It's starting to count *you*, Ulyana. The town paid tonight, and the well heard every envelope, and now it knows the season's turning, and it's making room." At last they turned, and their face in the porch light's spill was the face from the garden, from the summers, from before—but older in some way that had nothing to do with years. "Harvest meetings end the same way every year. The town pays its money. And the house that owes the most—"

They stopped. Ulyana realized their own hand had risen to the sixth door's handle without their permission, and that the handle was turning, gently, from the other side.

"—the house that owes the most opens its doors," Odile finished. "One per night. Until the count comes due."

The handle slipped free of Ulyana's fingers. Behind the door, in the space where the wall's insulation should have been, something exhaled—a long, patient, satisfied breath, like a counter setting down a slate—and began, softly, to number the rooms of the house.

"One," it said.
