## 29. Vess Stands Guard

Ulyana had the key in their fist before they were halfway down the school steps, and the cold of it had already begun to feel like a promise.

Pell had pressed it into their palm with both hands, the way you'd hand someone a live coal. "Bricked door, under the east wing," he'd rasped. "Been there longer than the school. Longer than the charter, I'd swear it. I helped lay brick over it in '68 and I've regretted it every day since." His breath had smelled of menthol and something older, like cellar air. "You want to reach them, you go through there. Not down the hill. Down the hill, you're just another voice it counts."

Ulyana had asked him why now. Why, after forty years of silence, he was handing a stranger—no, not a stranger, a Coldharbour—the key to the one door in Grayfen that mattered.

Pell had looked at their hands, then at his own, and said, "Because I counted the bricks wrong, miss. I left one loose. I've been waiting my whole life for somebody to find it." Then he'd coughed, wet and long, and waved them off before they could thank him.

Now the key rode in their pocket like a second heartbeat. The school was emptying—last bell, buses groaning at the curb, children streaming past Ulyana in the corridor without looking at them, the way they'd stopped looking at them weeks ago. Wren's desk was gone. Bram's was gone. Two others Ulyana couldn't even name anymore, because the names had begun to slide out of their memory like water off wax, and that frightened Ulyana more than the well did: the forgetting was the town's true architecture, and they were living inside it.

The east wing was the oldest part of the building, closed off for "foundation work" for as long as anyone could remember. The door to the sub-basement was behind a janitorial rack, behind a false wall of pegboard, and Pell had drawn the route on the back of a hall pass in pencil so faint Ulyana had to hold it under the corridor lights to read it.

They went down.

The stairs smelled of chalk dust and standing water. Their flashlight found brick, brick, brick—and then, low in the wall, a seam that wasn't a seam. One brick, third from the floor, sat proud of its neighbors by the width of a fingernail. Pell's loose brick.

Ulyana knelt. The key was old iron, long-shanked, wrong for any modern lock, and when they slid it into the hairline gap beside the brick it caught, turned, and something behind the wall—a mechanism older than the wall, older than the school—clunked like a coffin lid.

The brick came free. Behind it: darkness, and a draft that smelled of wet stone and, beneath that, faintly, impossibly, of pencil shavings and orange peel. A classroom smell. A childhood smell.

From somewhere below, faint as a radio through a wall, a child's voice was counting.

"—nine, ten. Nine, ten. Nine, ten."

Wren. It was Wren. Ulyana pressed their forehead against the cold bricks and their whole body shook with something that was not quite relief and not quite grief. She was alive. They were all alive. Down there, in the dark under the school, the taken children were still counting, still waiting for someone to come and say their names.

Ulyana pulled two more bricks free. Enough to make a gap. Enough to—

"Ulyana."

The voice came from the top of the stairs, and it was the voice Ulyana had known since before they could read. Aunt Vess stood in the doorway with the light behind her, and she was holding a shovel.

* * *

Vess had known the moment the fever broke.

She'd been sitting in the cottage kitchen with a cold cloth and a thermometer, watching her great-niece sleep off three days of the sickness that came to Grayfen every generation and took whoever the well didn't want, when she'd noticed the hall pass on the table. Pencil marks on the back. A route drawn in a hand she recognized—not Ulyana's. Pell's. She'd know that cramped, guilty script anywhere; she'd seen it on forty years of maintenance requisitions, all of them stamped APPROVED in her own signature.

Vess had sat very still for a long time, listening to Ulyana breathe, and had understood that everything she'd spent her life holding shut was coming open, and that there was maybe an hour left to stop it.

She'd driven home first. Not to think—there was no time to think—but to get the shovel, because a shovel was what Coldharbours carried when they went down the hill, and had been since before anyone alive could say why. Her father's shovel. His father's. The handle worn to the shape of a Coldharbour grip, smooth as bone.

She'd also taken the ledger. The real one, the family one, the one with the payments written in a column going back past the Civil War, past the charter, past everything. She'd put it in her coat pocket without knowing why. Maybe so she could show Ulyana. Maybe so she could burn it. Maybe just because she couldn't bear to leave it in the house alone.

Now she stood at the top of the sub-basement stairs, and the light from the corridor fell down the steps and found her great-niece kneeling at a hole in the wall, bricks scattered on the floor like pulled teeth, and Vess felt something in her chest crack along a line that had been forming for sixty years.

"Step away from there," she said.

Ulyana turned. In the flashlight's spill their face was fever-pale and furious and so much like Ulyana's mother that Vess's hand tightened on the shovel handle. "Aunt Vess." Ulyana's voice came out flat. "You followed me."

"You left a map on the kitchen table." Vess started down the stairs, slow, each step deliberate. "You might as well have left a confession."

"Then you know what's down there." Ulyana stood, and the flashlight beam swung and caught Vess full in the face, and Vess did not flinch. "Wren is down there. Bram Tuller. Children I taught. Children this town gave away like—like change from a purchase. And there's a door, and I have a key, and I am going through it."

"No." Vess reached the bottom of the stairs and planted herself in front of the wall, between Ulyana and the gap, the shovel held across her body like a bar. "You're not."

"Aunt Vess—"

"You don't understand what you're doing." Vess's voice stayed level, and she was proud of that, proud the way you're proud of not screaming while a bone sets. "The bargain holds as long as nobody interferes. That's the whole of it. That's the entire contract, written nowhere, signed by everyone. We pay, we keep quiet, we count our doors, and the well takes what it's owed and leaves the rest of us alone. Six generations, Ulyana. Six generations of Coldharbours who did exactly that, and the line survived. Every family in this town has a version of that contract. You break ours, and it doesn't just break for us. It breaks for everyone."

"It's already broken." Ulyana's voice cracked, and Vess heard the fever still in it, the weakness, and hated herself for calculating it, for measuring her own kin like a debt. "Wren is gone. The schedule moved up—*you* told me it moved up. Your missed payment, the shoe on the porch. The well isn't waiting for the season anymore. It's counting down. What exactly is it you think you're protecting?"

"The ones who are still up here." And there it was. Vess hadn't meant to say it. She'd rehearsed a dozen speeches on the drive over—about duty, about family, about the dead who couldn't be helped and the living who could—and none of them had contained the truth, which was small and ugly and true: "The ones who are still up here. You. Me. Every child in that school who still has a desk. You go through that wall, you don't save the ones below. You just add the ones above to the count."

"Then let it count me." Ulyana took a step forward, and Vess raised the shovel—not swinging it, just holding it, the head angled down, the way you hold a thing you're ashamed of. "I was offered to it once already, Aunt Vess. Did you know I remembered? Not the night. Not the well. But I remembered Odile. I remembered that someone was taken in my place, my whole childhood, like a splinter under the skin I couldn't find. And now I know what the splinter was. A child went into that hole so I could grow up and inherit a cottage and a column of payments and a shovel. And you want me to walk past that door. To teach on Monday morning. To take attendance and skip the empty desks."

"Yes." The word came out of Vess like a stone from a sling. "Yes. That is exactly what I want. Because that is what it costs to be a Coldharbour, and somebody in this family has to pay it, and it has been me for thirty years, Ulyana. Me. I made every payment. I walked every envelope down the hill. I sat on that porch every equinox with the lights on and my heart going like a rabbit's, and I did it so that you would never have to know, never have to choose, never have to stand where you're standing right now. And I will not let you unmake that. Not tonight. Not for children who are already—"

"Already what?" Ulyana's flashlight didn't waver. "Say it. Say they're already dead. Say it to my face, and then listen to the wall."

And Ulyana turned the light to the gap in the bricks, and Vess heard it.

Counting. Faint, from below, threaded through the draft that came up out of the old well-house like breath: a child's voice, steady and unbroken, counting nine, ten, nine, ten, and beneath it, other voices, smaller, keeping the count like a rope in the dark.

Wren Okafor's voice.

Vess's grip shifted on the shovel. She felt her own hands do it without her permission, felt the old training of six generations move through her arms like water through a channel cut long before she was born. *Block the way. Hold the line. The bargain over everything.* Her father's voice in her blood: *A Coldharbour does not open doors, Vessie. A Coldharbour is the door.*

"Move," Ulyana said.

"No."

"I'll go around you."

"No, you won't." And Vess hated how steady it sounded, how much like her father. "Because I will put this shovel in your way, and then in your path, and if I have to, I will put it in your leg, and you will hate me, and I will deserve it, and you will be alive to do it. That's the arithmetic, child. That's the only arithmetic this town has ever known. Somebody goes down the well, or everybody keeps their children. I have kept the books my whole life. I know how it balances."

"It doesn't balance," Ulyana said quietly. "It never balanced. You just never had to look at the other side of the ledger."

Vess struck out then—no, not struck. She swung the shovel low and hard into the wall beside the gap, and bricks burst inward, and for one horrible instant she thought she had opened it herself, widened the door she'd come to close. Dust rained down. The counting from below stopped.

Then, in the silence, a small voice came up through the hole—clear, unafraid, furious, twelve years old and done with being patient:

"Miss Coldharbour? Is that you? *I can hear you out there.*"

And Vess Coldharbour, who had kept the payments current for thirty years, who had walked the hill in all weathers, who had given up a niece's name to the dark and called it love, felt the shovel handle begin to slide through her sweating fingers—and heard, behind her, at the top of the stairs, the corridor door swing slowly open, and footsteps, many small footsteps, beginning to come down.
