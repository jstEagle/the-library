## 30. The Count Reaches Zero

The first door slammed at 11:47 p.m.

Ulyana knew the time because they were awake anyway—had been awake for three nights now, sleeping in snatches with the lamp burning and a kitchen knife on the nightstand that they had no real plan to use. The sound came from down the hill, from the direction of town: a single percussive boom, like a car backfiring, like a coffin lid dropped.

Then a second door. Closer. The Henderson place, maybe, or the old feed store.

Then a third.

They got up and went to the window, and what they saw made their mouth go dry and stay dry. Fog lay over Grayfen in a low gray tide, and through it, street by street, porch lights were going out—not flickering, not failing, but *going out*, in sequence, as if something were walking the town and pinching each flame between its fingers. And in the dark behind each dead light, a door stood open. Ulyana could see them from the bedroom window: black rectangles punched into the fronts of houses, every one of them open exactly the width of a hand, every one of them breathing out a faint warmth of yellow lamplight from inside, because no one in Grayfen ever turned off the lights anymore. Everyone kept them burning. Everyone counted their doors.

And now every door in town was open.

*Count the doors,* Odile used to say, in Ulyana's own six-year-old voice, standing at the foot of the bed with its too-long arms folded. *Count them before you sleep.*

Ulyana counted now. Five doors in the cottage. Five, where there should have been five—no, wait. They crossed to the hallway and stood in the cold center of it, turning slowly, counting aloud because counting aloud was the rule and rules were all that was left.

"One," they said. Front door.

"Two." Back door, off the kitchen.

"Three." Bedroom.

"Four." The closet that had never been a bedroom but had become one, somehow, sometime in the last week, without anyone building it.

"Five."

Their voice caught. Because there was a sixth door now, at the end of the hall, where the hall ended in a wall—a wall Ulyana had leaned against, had hung a coat on, had *painted*, for God's sake, two summers ago when they'd first rented the cottage and still believed this was an ordinary house in an ordinary dying town. The paint was still there. The coat hook was still there, holding Vess's wool cardigan.

But the door was there too, set into the painted wall like a door set into a painting, and it was beginning, very slowly, to swing inward.

"No," Ulyana said. It came out small. They planted their feet. "No. You don't get to do this. You don't get to come into my *house*—"

From below the floor—from beneath the cottage, from beneath the hill, from everywhere at once—came the voice of the well.

It was not Bram's voice. It was not Wren's. It was not any child's voice, though it wore the shape of one the way a glove wears the shape of a hand. It was vast and patient and it was *counting*, and it had been counting since sundown, and Ulyana realized with a lurch that they had heard it all evening without hearing it—the way you can hear rain for hours before you notice you're wet.

"Four hundred and eleven," said the well. "Four hundred and twelve. Four hundred and thirteen."

It was counting doors. Not the five or six or nine in the cottage. All of them. Every door Grayfen had ever built, every door the town had ever opened onto the dark—the well-house under the school, the bricked mouths, the cellar hatches, the root doors, the doors of the school itself, twenty-six desks behind a door Ulyana had locked with Pell's key and which was, they understood suddenly, already open.

"Four hundred and nineteen. Four hundred and twenty."

The sixth door swung wide.

Beyond it was not a room. Beyond it was the smell of the well—that wet-stone, iron-and-egg smell—and a darkness so complete it seemed to have texture, to have grain, to be looking back. Cold air moved against Ulyana's face. Somewhere in that dark, water dripped upward.

Ulyana did the only thing left that felt like agency. They slammed it.

They put both hands flat against the door and threw their weight into it, and the door gave with a groan like a jaw being forced shut, and they held it there, shoulder against the wood, heart slamming, while the well's voice continued underneath them, unbothered, unhurried, counting.

"Four hundred and thirty-one. Four hundred and thirty-two."

"It's early," Ulyana whispered. "Vess said the reckoning wasn't until—" They stopped. They knew why. They had known since the moment they'd run to the well after Wren vanished, since the moment they'd broken into the well-house, since the moment they'd started pulling children's names off bricks. Every rule broken, every protocol violated, every secret dragged toward the light had been a payment withheld. The interference didn't just risk voiding the bargain.

It *was* voiding it. Door by door. Count by count.

"You're doing this because of me," they said to the dark beyond the door. Their breath fogged even though the wood was between them and it. "Because I wouldn't sit down and be quiet."

The well answered with the next number.

* * *

Odile arrived at midnight, and for the first time since the porch, Odile looked afraid.

That was the thing that undid Ulyana more than the doors, more than the counting. Twenty years unchanged, twenty years of that gray, patient, faintly smiling presence—and now Odile stood in the middle of the multiplying hallway with its long fingers pressed to its long face, and its eyes—those pale, pupil-less eyes Ulyana had loved and feared across a childhood—were fixed on the sixth door with something like horror.

"It shouldn't be tonight," Odile said. "It isn't supposed to be tonight."

"Well, happy birthday to it." Ulyana's voice shook. They were still holding the door closed with their body, absurdly, as if their hundred and forty pounds meant anything to whatever was on the other side. "It moved up the schedule. I wonder whose fault that is."

"Don't." Odile's voice cracked in the middle, split into two voices, Ulyana's childhood voice and something older underneath. "Don't say it like that. I tried to stop you. I *begged* you—"

"You begged me to let Wren Okafor die." Ulyana pushed off the door and turned, and the fury finally came, hot and clean, the first clean thing they'd felt in days. "You begged me to keep paying. You begged me to be my great-grandmother. So no, Odile, I'm not going to apologize for the schedule slipping."

"The schedule slipping means everyone dies." Odile took a step forward, and the hallway lamps dimmed as it passed them, the way candles gutter near a cold draft. "Do you understand what the final count is? When it reaches zero, it doesn't take one child. It doesn't take a tithe. It takes the *debt*. All of it. Every name owed, every substitution, every year of interest—it collects everything it is owed at once, from everyone who owes it. That's four hundred families. That's the whole town. That's—"

"That's the point." Ulyana's voice dropped. "That's exactly the point, Odile. Let it collect from the people who signed the contract."

Odile stared at them. And then, horribly, gently, it said: "There are children on that ledger who never signed anything. There are children born into debt the way you were born into your name. Wren is eleven. Do you want her collected?"

Ulyana had no answer. They had thought they had an answer—they'd been carrying it around for days like a stone in the chest—but standing in a hallway with six doors and a counting dark beneath the floorboards, the answer crumbled. Wren was still down there. Wren and Bram and the others they hadn't gotten out yet. If the well collected everything tonight, it would collect *them* first, closest to the mouth, deepest in the throat.

"How long?" Ulyana asked. "How long until zero?"

Odile was quiet for a moment. Outside, another door boomed—two streets over, maybe three—and beneath the floor the counting continued, steady as a metronome, patient as geology.

"When it finishes the doors," Odile said, "it starts the names."

* * *

They ran for Vess's house because Vess was the last Coldharbour and Coldharbours, whatever else could be said of them, knew the mechanics of the thing. Ulyana took the hill road at a dead sprint, fog swallowing them at every stride, past houses with their doors yawning and their windows lit and their families standing in the yards—standing, all of them, in nightclothes, in coats thrown over nightclothes, staring at their own open front doors the way people stare at a snake in the kitchen. No one screamed. That was the worst part. Grayfen had known this was possible for a hundred and fifty years, and so no one screamed; they just stood in their yards with their hands hanging empty at their sides, waiting to find out how much of the bill was theirs.

Mrs. Aldous from the bakery stood on her porch holding a cast-iron skillet like a shield. She saw Ulyana running and called out, "Is it the school? Is it coming from the school?"

"It's coming from under everything!" Ulyana shouted back, and didn't stop.

Vess's farmhouse sat at the top of Coldharbour land, closest to the well, the way a sentry sits closest to the border. Every light in it burned. Every door stood open.

Ulyana found Vess in the kitchen, kneeling on the floor with the family ledger open in front of her and a box of matches beside her, and she was not burning it. She was reading it. Her lips moved with the names. Her hands, those strong farm hands that had kneaded bread and wrung chickens' necks and gripped Ulyana's shoulder at their parents' funeral, trembled against the paper.

"Aunt Vess."

She didn't look up. "I'm finding us," she said. Her voice was terrible—flat, scraped, a voice that had cried itself out years ago and was now operating on residue. "I'm finding our line in the count. Your great-grandmother signed for three generations. Three generations, Ulyana. I always thought—I told myself we had until spring. The schedule said spring. The schedule has *always* said spring."

"The schedule's void. I voided it." Ulyana crouched, took the ledger's edge. Vess's grip tightened, then released. "Listen to me. Odile says when the count reaches zero, it collects everything. All debts at once. We have maybe an hour. Less."

"I know." Vess finally looked up, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely calm, and that calm frightened Ulyana more than the doors. "I've known since the third door slammed. I've been sitting here deciding."

"Deciding what?"

"What a Coldharbour does." Vess closed the ledger. She rose, joints cracking, and went to the pantry, and from the back of the lowest shelf she took a bundle wrapped in oilcloth that Ulyana had seen once before, as a child, and been told never to ask about. She unwrapped it on the table: a hammer, a cold chisel, and a brick. An ordinary red brick, except that scratched into its face, from the inside, were the letters *M.C.*—Margaret Coldharbour. Ulyana's great-grandmother's name. A brick from the well-house wall, pried loose and kept, all these years, as a receipt.

"There's a way to slow it," Vess said. "Not stop it. Slow it. The count stumbles when it reaches a name that's been paid. It has to check the entry. It has to *read the ledger*, same as me." She wrapped the brick back in the oilcloth with hands that had stopped trembling, which was worse. "Your great-grandmother paid herself in the end, you know. That's the part nobody remembers. The stolen child bought her line forty years. But when the count came due again and there was no one left to substitute, Margaret walked down to the well and put her own name in. That's why we lasted so long. That's why the tithes worked at all—one willing payment buys more than ten taken ones. It's the only currency it respects."

Ulyana's stomach dropped through the floor, through the hill, through the counting dark. "Vess. No."

"Somebody holds the door," Vess said, "while you get the children out. That's arithmetic, love. Not heroics. Arithmetic." She picked up the hammer and chisel and the wrapped brick and tucked them under her arm like a woman heading out to mend a fence. "Pell's key opens the well-house. Go get Wren. Go get all of them. I'll meet you there."

"Vess—"

"*Go.*" And then, softer, with the ghost of the woman who'd brought groceries and rules and a kind of fierce crooked love: "Count the doors on your way down. And Ulyana—if you see a seventh one, don't you dare open it. Whatever it promises. Whatever it sounds like. Some doors are just its mouth."

* * *

The town was screaming now.

Not the people—the people still stood silent in their yards, transfixed, complicit to the last. It was the doors themselves. As Ulyana ran the hill road toward the school, they passed house after house where the open doors had begun to make noise: a low, wet, resonant moan, each one pitched differently, each one unmistakably a voice trying to remember how to speak. The well was wearing the town's doors like a choir wears robes. From the church came a hymn hummed backward. From the school, when Ulyana reached it, its windows blazing, its front doors flung wide despite Pell's key still being in Ulyana's pocket—from the school came a counting song, a skipping-rope chant, dozens of voices braided together:

*One for the secret, two for the shame,*
*three for the mother who traded a name—*

Ulyana took the steps two at a time, key already out, and stopped dead in the corridor.

Every classroom door stood open. Every one. And down the length of the hallway, at the far end, the bricked door beneath the stairwell—the original well-house door, older than the charter, older than the town—stood open too, and had not been forced. The bricks around its frame were intact. The lock hung shattered, but from the *inside*.

Something had unlocked it from below.

From the dark of the stairwell, up out of the earth, drifting through the open doors of every classroom like smoke finding windows, came the count. Not numbers anymore.

Names.

"Harkaway. Edith Marlow Harkaway. Owed: one."

"Pell. Thomas Avery Pell. Owed: one."

"Coldharbour. Owed: three generations, less one paid, less one substituted, balance outstanding—"

And then, clear and young and terribly alive, cutting through the litany like a bell through fog:

"—Miss! Miss Villalobos! Down here! It's starting to call roll!"

Wren Okafor's voice, from the bottom of the well-house stairs, from the mouth of the well itself, where a girl who should have been a tithe was standing in the dark with two smaller shapes behind her, holding a piece of broken brick like a weapon, buying seconds with her sharpness, her fearlessness, her refusal—

Ulyana took the stairs.

Behind them, above them, throughout the town of Grayfen, the count rolled on toward zero, and every door stood open, and the fog leaned in to listen.
