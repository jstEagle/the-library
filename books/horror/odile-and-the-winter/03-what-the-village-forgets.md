## 3. What the Village Forgets

Felix Montrose had taught in six schools before Blackfen, and in five of them she had learned the town's history the same way: from the parents, whether she wanted it or not. Parents were the unofficial curriculum. They told you which families had feuded since the war, which farm had burned twice, which vicar had run off with the organist's wife. It arrived unbidden, wrapped in casseroles and invitations to Sunday suppers.

Blackfen's parents offered her casseroles. Mrs. Hedley brought a shepherd's pie so heavy Felix suspected the dish itself was load-bearing. The Tarrow family sent a jar of honey with a note in beautiful copperplate that said only *Welcome to Blackfen*. But of the town's history—of anything that had happened here before the current harvest—not one word.

It was as if the village had been founded on a Tuesday.

She decided to go door to door. Officially, it was good practice: a new teacher meeting the families of her students. Unofficially, she wanted to ask about last year's class—the one that had been seven children strong in October and four by February, if the trophy plaques and faded photographs in the corridor could be believed. The register Aldous had given her listed no such absence. The register, in fact, seemed to suggest last year's class had never existed at all.

She started with the Hedleys, because they had fed her.

* * *

Mrs. Hedley received her in a kitchen that smelled of bread and lavender polish, sat her down, poured tea before Felix had finished saying hello, and beamed at her with the uncomplicated joy of a woman who loved feeding people.

"You'll want to know about the village," Mrs. Hedley said, folding her hands. "Everyone always does."

"I'd love to," Felix said. "Though actually I wanted to ask about last year's class. I noticed some names missing from—"

"Wonderful school, Blackfen," Mrs. Hedley said. "My mother went there, and her mother before her. Miss Prewitt taught the infants when I was small, lovely woman, hands like a bird. And before her there was—" She paused. Her smile stayed exactly where it was, but something behind it went still, like a pond freezing from the edges inward. "There was a teacher. Before."

"What was their name?"

"Such a nice young man you've got teaching the older ones now," Mrs. Hedley continued, in the same warm voice, as though Felix had asked nothing at all. "Mr. Aldous. He was a Blackfen boy himself, you know. Born and bred. His mother grew the best runner beans in the county." She refilled Felix's cup without being asked. "More tea?"

"Thank you. About last year's class—"

"The winter's hard on the roads out here," Mrs. Hedley said. "You'll want to lay in coal early. Don't leave it till the frost, everyone makes that mistake their first year."

Felix tried three more angles over the next forty minutes. Each one was met with the same gentle deflection: the quality of the school roof, the reliability of the baker, the fact that the church bells had been recast in 1911 and sounded ever so much better for it. Not once did Mrs. Hedley raise her voice or change her expression. It was like trying to interview a painting of a hospitable woman.

Walking back down the lane, Felix realized her notebook held an entire page of notes on coal merchants and not one word about the missing class.

She tried the Tarrows next. Mr. Tarrow answered the door with the honey jar's twin in his hands, apparently intending to give her another one.

"Last year's class," Felix said, before he could get her inside and seated. "I understand several students left mid-year. I'd like to know where they went, so I can write to them, perhaps. Children do better when they know someone remembers them."

Mr. Tarrow smiled. It was a kind smile. It reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse.

"Families move," he said. "Work is work. The young ones go where the going takes them."

"All seven? In the same year? Mid-winter?"

"Now, Miss Montrose." He handed her the honey. "You'll catch your death standing on the step. Take this for your trouble. Our own bees. Good for what ails you."

He closed the door with the softness of a man shutting a book he'd already read.

At the Coopers', the mother said, "Children grow up so fast," in a tone usually reserved for weather. At the Fenwicks', the father said, "Was there a class last year?" and then laughed, and then stopped laughing very suddenly, and then made tea. At the Wicke farm, old Tam Wicke looked at her for a long moment with eyes like unwashed windows and said, "You're new," and then, "You won't stay," and then went back to mending a fence as though she had already gone.

By the fourth house, Felix had begun keeping a tally of her own—a private joke against the silence. Seven interviews. Seven cups of tea. Seven variations on *families move, work is work, the young ones go where the going takes them*, delivered in seven different kitchens with seven identical smiles.

That was the thing that kept snagging in her mind, walking home in the grey afternoon light. Not the refusal. Refusal she knew how to handle; she'd once taught in a mill town where the whole street closed ranks around a foreman who'd broken a boy's arm. Refusal had texture. It bristled. It gave you something to push against.

This wasn't refusal. This was *replacement*. Every answer was warm, vague, and identical, as though the parents weren't deciding what to say but reciting it—as though somewhere in Blackfen there was a single script, and everyone had been issued the same copy, and the copy said: *be kind to the new teacher, offer tea, say nothing.*

People didn't forget naturally. Natural forgetting was ragged. It contradicted itself, left gaps of different sizes, misremembered dates. What she was looking at was too smooth. Too maintained. Forgetting, here, was a chore somebody did—and did well.

* * *

The pub was called the Drowned Bell, which Felix found either charming or ominous depending on how long she let herself think about it. She went on Thursday evening, partly because teachers were allowed a drink like anyone else, and mostly because pubs were where villages kept the truths they didn't keep at home.

The landlord was a broad, comfortable man named Gorse with forearms like hams and the air of someone who had heard every possible human statement and found most of them adequate. He greeted her with genuine pleasure, pulled her a cider without asking, and refused payment with a wave that ended the discussion.

"First week," he said. "How's the brood treating you?"

"They're wonderful," Felix said, and meant it. "Bright. Strange, a little. They flinch if anyone says the word winter."

Gorse's cloth, which had been wiping the bar in slow circles, completed its circle and stopped.

"Do they," he said.

"Is that common? In country schools?"

"Country children are odd creatures." He resumed wiping. "Full of notions."

Felix drank her cider and considered her approach. Directness had failed at seven kitchen tables. Perhaps indirection would fail more interestingly here.

"I've been calling on the parents," she said. "Getting to know the village. Everyone's been terribly kind."

"We're a kind village," Gorse said, with the flat certainty of a man reading from a sign.

"Mrs. Hedley told me all about the coal situation. Mr. Tarrow gave me two jars of honey. Nobody would tell me a single thing about last year's class."

The cloth stopped again.

Felix set down her glass. "Seven children, Mr. Gorse. Gone between October and February, if the photographs in the corridor are right. Their desks are still up in my classroom. Twenty-six desks, nineteen children. I counted."

For the first time since she'd entered, Gorse looked directly at her, and she saw that behind the landlord's amiable bulk was a man doing arithmetic of his own—weighing something, measuring her against it.

"You counted the desks," he said.

"It's what I do. I count things. It's a teacher failing."

He came around the bar, which she took as a good sign, and jerked his head toward the corner table by the window, the one farthest from the taproom door. "Sit. I'll make tea."

"In a pub?"

"Best tea in the parish," he said, and she later confirmed this was true.

He brought the pot himself, along with two cups, and sat across from her with the weariness of a man picking up a weight he'd put down many times and always regretted.

"Here's what I can tell you," he said. "Nothing."

"That's very generous of you."

"I mean it as a kindness, so take it as one." He poured. "You're a counting woman. So count this. Seven houses you visited today. Seven teas. Seven smiles. Did any of those people seem like liars to you?"

Felix thought of Mrs. Hedley's hands folded on her apron, of Mr. Tarrow's kind, terrible smile. "No. That's what frightens me. Liars look like they're carrying something. Those people looked *empty*. Where the answer should have been, there was just—polish."

Gorse nodded slowly, as if she'd passed a test. "Now count this. A village forgets things natural, same as people. Names slip. Dates blur. But forgetting's like a garden, miss. Left alone, it goes to weeds—all tangled, full of contradictions, everybody remembering it different. What you saw today wasn't weeds." He leaned forward. "It was *pruned*."

The word settled over the table like a dropped stone.

"Somebody tends it," Felix said quietly.

"Somebody tends it," Gorse agreed. He turned his cup in his hands. "And I'm going to tell you the last thing I'm going to tell you, and then we're going to drink our tea and talk about the football results like a pair of sensible people. Are you listening?"

"I'm listening."

"There's been talk of a drainage trench behind your school. County men coming with machines to dig." His eyes held hers, and there was no comfort in them now, none at all. "When they come—and they will come, because the county doesn't listen to us any better than you do—you keep yourself indoors that day. You keep your children at their books. And whatever they turn up in that ground—" his voice dropped, not to a whisper but to something quieter than a whisper, the volume of a truth spoken carefully—"you don't touch it. You don't look at it longer than you have to. And above all, Miss Montrose, whatever else happens in this village, however curious you find yourself—"

He stood, collected the teapot, and looked down at her with something that in a harder face might have been pity.

"—don't dig."

* * *

She walked home through lanes gone blue with dusk, past hedgerows stiffening with the first suggestion of frost, and let herself think the thought she'd been refusing all day.

Enforced forgetting required an enforcer. And enforcers required a reason. Villages kept secrets about shame—bastardy, bankruptcy, the vicar and the organist's wife. Shame was heavy but survivable; it got carried for generations like furniture nobody liked. But nobody pruned a garden of memories out of shame alone. Shame made people evasive. This was different. This was *maintenance*, regular and communal and calm, the way you'd maintain a wall that was holding something back.

Or holding something down.

At her lodgings, she lit the lamp, set Tam Wicke's words beside Gorse's—*you won't stay*; *don't dig*—and laid her notebook open on the table. Under the day's useless notes she wrote a single line:

*Nineteen children. Twenty-six desks. Find the other seven.*

Then, because the house was cold and the thought colder, she went to the window to close the curtains—and stopped.

Out past the school, past the dark hump of the playing field, a light was moving. Not a lantern's swing. Not a car. A pale, steady glow, low to the ground, drifting along the tree line behind the school grounds where the county surveyors had staked out their trench—moving slowly, patiently, the way a person walks when they are checking on something that belongs to them.

It sank, gently, and went out.

Felix stood a long while with her hand on the curtain. Then she drew it shut, wrote one more line in her notebook—*something walks the field at night*—and got out her lesson plans for Monday, because tomorrow was Saturday, and on Monday twenty-six desks would wait, and she intended to learn the name of every child who filled them.

Starting, she decided, with Ivetta Tarrow—who had corrected her lesson on local history with the serenity of a girl reciting scripture, and who had mentioned, in passing, a family under the hill.

The family nobody in Blackfen would say a word about.

The family, she was beginning to suspect, that did the pruning.
