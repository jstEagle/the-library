## 10. The Door Opens

Felix had taken to sleeping with her boots by the bed, which was not a habit she'd ever intended to acquire and which she blamed entirely on Blackfen. In London she'd slept like a stone with a salary. Here she woke at small sounds — the settling of the cottage beams, the wind worrying the loose slate on the Hendersons' roof next door, once, memorably, a fox screaming in a way that sounded disconcertingly like a child calling her name.

So when she woke at half past four on the morning after first frost, it was not the cold that brought her upright. It was the smell.

Bread. Fresh bread, warm and yeasty, the kind her grandmother used to prove overnight in a cupboard lined with flannel. And beneath it, fainter, something waxy and sweet — beeswax, furniture polish, the smell of a room someone loved enough to tend.

Her lodgings contained half a loaf of stale shop bread and no candles whatsoever.

She sat up. The window was silvered with frost, and outside, the world was silent in that particular way silence gets when it is being performed. She dressed quickly, pulled on her coat, and stood a moment with her hand on the latch, listening to herself think.

*Go back to sleep,* said the sensible part of her, the part that had survived eleven years of teaching by knowing exactly which battles were worth arriving early for. *It's four-thirty. Whatever it is will still be there at a civilized hour.*

But the sensible part of her had been losing arguments steadily since September, and it lost this one too.

* * *

The excavation site behind the school was fenced with orange mesh and a sign reading DANGER — COUNTY COUNCIL — KEEP OUT, which in Felix's experience was less an instruction than an invitation with paperwork. The trench ran forty yards along the school's eastern foundations, and at its far end, where the ground dipped toward the old well-line, the crew had uncovered the door.

She'd seen it twice now. Once from a distance, the morning Dree's men had brushed the earth from its face — a slab of grey stone, taller than a man, fitted into a frame of the same material as though the wall around it had grown rather than been built. The second time up close, on the Saturday, when she'd stood at the trench edge while Dree counted the carved tally marks under his breath and couldn't seem to stop, his lips moving through numbers past two hundred, past three hundred, until one of his men put a hand on his shoulder and he came to like a man surfacing from deep water.

The door had been bolted shut from the inside. Everyone agreed on this. The bolts — three of them, iron gone to rust-lace — were thrown from within. There was no handle on the outside. There was nothing on the outside but the tallies, and the worn hollow where, centuries ago, someone had rested their palm.

This morning, the bolts were open.

Felix stood at the trench edge in the blue-grey light and looked down into the doorway, because that was what it was now — not a door but a doorway, standing open onto a stair that went down into the earth, and out of that stair came air. Cold air, moving steadily upward like water finding its level, carrying the smell of bread and beeswax and, beneath those, something older and mineral, like the inside of a cave that had been swept.

The footprints started at the bottom of the stairs.

They came up — she could see that even from above, the way the frost on the threshold stones was disturbed, scuffed in the pattern of ascent — and then they crossed the trench floor and stopped at the base of the ladder the crew had left leaning against the trench wall. Small footprints. Bare feet, or stockinged; the prints were soft-edged, without tread. Child-sized. Several sets, overlapping, going up.

None coming back down.

Felix crouched at the lip of the trench for a long time, doing arithmetic she did not want to do. Three students absent since first frost. Beds made, shoes by doors, windows latched from inside. The constable, Mr. Petch, had declined to open a case with the words "children run off to relatives, happens every winter, you'll see them at Christmas," delivered with the serene confidence of a man reciting lines he'd been given. Aldous had filed the absences without comment, his pen hovering over the register a half-second too long each time, like a man stepping over something in the road.

And now the door was open, and small feet had walked up out of it in the night, and the whole village slept on around her as though the air didn't smell of bread.

"Right," Felix said aloud, to the trench, to the door, to whatever might be listening. "No."

She climbed down.

* * *

She did not go through the door. Let the record show — let some future inquest show, if it came to that — that Felix Montrose stood at the threshold, breathed the bread-smell, felt the warmth of it against her cold face like an offered hand, and stepped *back*. She looked down the stair instead: wide steps, shallow-risen, made for children's legs, descending into a light that was not firelight and not lamplight but something steadier, honey-colored, coming from somewhere below the reach of her eye. The walls were plastered. Someone had hung things on them — she could make out the shapes of framed pictures, a row of pegs with small coats.

A home. That was the horror of it, she thought, climbing back up the ladder with her heart going like a drum in a parade. Not a lair, not a tomb. A home, kept and warmed, waiting with the patience of a thing that had waited a very long time already.

She walked the perimeter of the site twice, found the gap in the mesh where the footprints exited — heading, when she followed them across the frosted grass, toward the village. Toward the houses. They faded on the hard ground by the lane, but their bearing was clear, and Felix stood in the middle of the empty road at five in the morning with her breath smoking and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, whose beds she needed to check.

* * *

By seven she had walked the length of Fen Row twice and knocked on four doors. At the Wicke house, Tam Wicke answered in his waistcoat, perfectly pleasant, and told her his grandchildren were fine, all present, thank you, and closed the door with the gentle firmness of a man shutting a book he'd finished. At the Prosser cottage, Mrs. Prosser fed her tea and shortbread and agreed warmly that yes, it was a cold morning, and wasn't it lovely, the frost, how it made everything clean. When Felix asked about the Prosser boy — Danny, nine years old, last seen Tuesday — Mrs. Prosser smiled and said he was staying with his aunt.

"And where does his aunt live?"

"Oh," said Mrs. Prosser, still smiling, still pouring, "near."

The tea was good. That was the worst part. Everything here was good — the tea, the shortbread, the smiles, the swept doorsteps — a whole village performing comfort so flawlessly that Felix began to feel the mad one, the rude one, the woman who wouldn't let a nice place be nice. She caught herself apologizing on the doorstep. She caught herself meaning it.

Then she got to the Hendersons', and everything changed.

Young Alice Henderson opened the door before Felix finished knocking. She was twelve, in her coat, already, at seven in the morning, with her satchel on her shoulder and her boots unlaced, and she looked at Felix with an expression Felix had never seen on a child's face before and hoped never to see again: relief and terror in equal measure, fighting for the same ground.

"You felt it too," Alice whispered.

"Felt what?"

"The door." Alice glanced over her shoulder into the dim hall. "Everyone's pretending they didn't. Mum's made a full breakfast and Dad's reading the paper upside down. But I heard it. In the night. Like a lock the size of a church turning over." Her hands were shaking on the satchel strap. "Miss Montrose, my brother's been standing at his window since four o'clock. He won't come away from it. He says he can smell supper."

* * *

The classroom, when Felix reached it at eight, was wrong in a way she couldn't immediately name, and then could, and wished she hadn't.

Nineteen children sat at nineteen desks. Nineteen faces turned toward her as she entered, and nineteen pairs of eyes were already looking at the window.

Not out of it. *At* it. The glass had fogged with their breathing, and in the fog, dozens of small palms had been pressed flat and wiped away and pressed again, so that the lower panes were printed with ghost-hands, layer upon layer, like leaves pressed in a book. Beyond the glass, the orange mesh of the excavation fence fluttered. Beyond that, the trench. Beyond that, the open door, steaming gently in the morning cold like a mouth about to speak.

"Good morning, class," Felix said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect.

"Good morning, Miss Montrose." Nineteen voices, in unison, without being asked. They never did that. In eleven years of teaching she had never once achieved unison greetings; there was always a straggler, a joker, a child finishing a conversation. This morning they spoke as one body, and the sound of it raised the hair on her arms.

She took the register. Her pen hovered over the first absent name — Danny Prosser — and she made herself say it aloud, made herself mark it, made the class watch her do it.

"Danny Prosser is absent," she said. "Does anyone know where Danny is?"

Silence. But it was a different silence than the village's silences. The village's silences were smooth, lacquered, rehearsed. This silence was full — nineteen children holding their breath, nineteen children glancing at the window and away, at the window and away, like people trying not to look at a sunbeam.

Alice Henderson's hand went up. Slowly. As if it weighed a great deal.

"Yes, Alice?"

"He's happy," Alice said. Her voice cracked in the middle of the word. "That's what everyone keeps saying. That they're happy down there. My brother keeps saying it. He stands at the window and says it like it's a prayer." She swallowed. "Miss, I don't want to be happy like that."

Something in Felix's chest went tight and hot. She set down the register.

"Well," she said, "then we shan't be. Class, books out. We're doing fractions, and we are going to do them so thoroughly that no one in this room has a single thought to spare for anything else. Alice, come sit at the front. Thomas, close the curtains."

"Nineteen heads came up as one.

"Close the curtains?" That was Pell Wicke — small, serious Pell, who had once corrected her on the date of the Blackfen flood with the gentle pity of an adult humoring a child. He was looking at her now with an expression she would turn over in her mind for many nights afterward: not defiance, not fear. Longing. Pure, undiluted longing, aimed past her shoulder at the covered window. "But Miss, it's such a lovely day."

"It's freezing," Felix said.

"It's warm," said Pell, "down there."

The room went very quiet. Outside, muffled by curtain and glass, a machine coughed awake at the dig site — the compressor, starting its day. And Felix watched her students listen to it, watched nineteen small bodies lean almost imperceptibly *away* from the sound, toward the curtained wall, toward the stair beneath their feet, and she understood that the battle was no longer about finding out what had happened to the missing children.

The missing children were below, in the warm, being taught by gentle things with wrong faces, eating bread and learning their letters, and they were, by every account she'd heard, content.

The battle was keeping the ones she still had.

* * *

She taught fractions. She taught them like a woman bailing out a boat, briskly, loudly, with jokes — and the strange thing, the thing she would remember, was that the jokes worked. For stretches of ten minutes, fifteen, the classroom was a classroom: hands up, pencils scratching, Alice Henderson laughing for real at Felix's deliberately terrible pie-chart pun. The children were bright. They had always been bright. And they were fighting, she realized — fighting alongside her, in their way, dragging their attention back from the window by main force, gripping their pencils like railings in a gale.

At half past ten, Pell Wicke asked to be excused.

"No," Felix said, before he'd finished the sentence.

Pell blinked. "I only want some water, Miss."

"Then take your bottle. You may fill it at the sink. In the room. Where I can see you." She smiled, and made it a real smile, warm as she could forge it. "In fact — new rule. Nobody leaves this room alone today. If you need the lavatory, we go in pairs, like a school trip. Consider it a game."

"A game," repeated Thomas Marsh, flatly, at the window.

"A game with rules I invented and always win," Felix said. "Now. Who can tell me what happens when you divide by zero?"

"Nothing," said Pell. "You can't. It doesn't work."

"Exactly right. Some divisions simply cannot be made, however much you want them." She met his eyes and held them, teacher to student, across the crowded geography of the classroom. "Remember that, Pell. Write it in your book. Underline it twice."

He held her gaze for a long moment. Then he wrote it. Underlined it twice. And Felix exhaled, and thought: *I have just fought a duel with an eight-year-old over arithmetic, and I am not certain I won.*

* * *

At lunch she sent Alice Henderson — trustworthy, terrified, loyal Alice — with a note to Dree at the dig site: *Do not leave the trench unwatched. Do not let any child approach the door. Send word if anything moves.* Then she went to find Aldous, and found him standing in the headmaster's office with his back to the door, looking at the wall where the old school photographs hung, rows of solemn faces going back a century, and she saw him counting them, his lips moving, and she knew he was counting the gaps.

"The door is open," Felix said.

"I know." He didn't turn around. "I felt it turn at four this morning. We all did. Every soul in Blackfen lay awake and listened to the lock, and at dawn we all got up and made breakfast and pretended." His shoulders moved — a laugh, or its corpse. "You get good at it, after a while. Pretending. It's the chief subject on the curriculum here, Miss Montrose. I've taught it for thirty years."

"Three more children are gone, Mr. Aldous. And the rest of mine are standing at windows smelling supper. I am going to the constable, and if Petch won't act, I'm going to the county police, and if they won't act, I'll go to London, to newspapers, to anyone—"

"To whom?" Aldous turned at last, and his face was terrible in its gentleness. "To whom, Miss Montrose? To the parents who made the beds? To the vicar who reads the same lesson every Advent? You'll write to the *Times* and tell them a door under a school opened itself, and children walked down it smiling, and the whole village said thank you?" He shook his head slowly. "There is no law for this. There has never been law for this. There is only the count, and the count is nearly—"

He stopped himself. But Felix had spent a term learning to read the silences of Blackfen, and this one rang like a struck bell.

"Nearly what, Mr. Aldous?"

He looked at her for a long time. And she saw the war in him — thirty years of pretending against one autumn of her stubbornness — and she saw which side was winning, and it frightened her more than the door had.

"Keep your classroom curtains closed," he said quietly. "Count your children at the end of every hour. And when the restlessness comes — and it will come, tonight, with the dark — don't stand between them and the stair, Miss Montrose. Nothing that stands in that doorway stays whole."

* * *

She counted them at the end of every hour. Nineteen. Nineteen. Eighteen.

Eighteen, at two o'clock, and her blood went to ice water, and then she found him — Thomas Marsh, hiding in the cloakroom, wedged behind the coats, crying without any sound at all, the way children cry when they've learned crying isn't allowed. He'd gotten as far as the corridor. He didn't know why. He said, "I was just walking, Miss, I wasn't going anywhere, my feet were just—" and stopped, because there was no honest end to that sentence.

Felix knelt down among the coats and took his hands. They were warm. That was the detail she would carry into the dark ahead: his hands were warm as toast, warm as bread, warm as something freshly baked for him somewhere below, and he was nine years old and sobbing because his own feet were committing treason.

"Listen to me, Thomas." She said it low and fierce, forehead nearly touching his. "Your feet are yours. Your name is yours. Thomas Marsh, of Fen Row, of my classroom, third desk by the radiator. Nobody counts you but me. Do you hear me? Nobody counts you but me."

He nodded against her shoulder, and she held him, and through the cloakroom's little window she could see the trench, and the door standing open at its end, patient as a held breath.

That night she did not go home. She set her desk in the corridor outside her classroom, boiled water on the classroom heater for tea, and told nineteen children — eighteen, she corrected herself, nineteen minus the three below plus Thomas returned, the arithmetic of grief already becoming habitual — that tomorrow they would learn long division, and she expected every single one of them present, and she would be checking.

They laughed. Thinly, but they laughed.

And at midnight, sitting sentinel in the lamplit corridor with the school breathing around her, Felix heard it begin: a low, sweet, layered sound rising through the floorboards, through the foundations, through the soles of her boots. Singing. Many voices, small and clear, in a round she almost recognized — an old counting song, the kind children chant to choose who's *it*, the kind that decides who goes and who stays.

Below her feet, the counted were singing the others down.

Felix set down her tea, took the chalk from her pocket, and went to fetch her coat.
