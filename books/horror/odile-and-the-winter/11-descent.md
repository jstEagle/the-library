## 11. Descent

The footprints began at the threshold of the opened door and went down.

Felix stood at the top of the stairwell for a long moment, the cold air rising past her like water running backward, carrying that impossible smell—bread, beeswax, something floral she couldn't name. Below her, stone steps descended into a warm amber glow. Above her, behind her, Blackfen slept under its first hard frost, every window dark, every door locked against a danger no one would name.

She had told herself she would wait until morning. She had told herself she would bring a lantern, a rope, perhaps the constable, though the constable was useless and they both knew it. She had told herself many sensible things while packing her satchel with chalk, a stub of candle, and the school register—the one with names crossed out in pencil—and then she had put on her coat and walked across the frozen grass to the excavation site like a woman keeping an appointment she'd been dreading all her life.

The small footprints led down. They were neat about it. Heel to toe, unhurried, spaced like a child walking somewhere familiar rather than somewhere frightening.

"That's the worst part," Felix said aloud, to no one. Her breath fogged and drifted down the stairs ahead of her, as if going on without her. "That's the *worst part*."

She took out her chalk—a fat yellow stick, the kind she used for the good days, the days when the lesson landed—and drew an arrow on the wall beside the first step. It pointed up, toward the night air and the frost and the village that had forgotten twenty-six children over forty years. Then she stepped over it and began to descend.

* * *

The stairs were older than anything in Blackfen. That was her first coherent thought, some minutes in, when the last grey light of the surface had given way entirely to the warm glow from below. The steps were worn in the centers, hollowed by centuries of feet, and the walls were fitted stone without mortar, tight as a drystone waller's pride. The school above had been built in 1847. These stairs made the school look like a garden shed.

She counted steps to keep her nerve. At two hundred she stopped counting, because the number had stopped meaning anything, and because she had begun to feel the strange sensation—not unpleasant, which was itself unpleasant—that she was being expected.

The chalk arrows helped. Every fifty steps or so she stopped and marked the wall: a bold yellow arrow pointing back the way she'd come. Up. Out. Toward tea and toast and the ordinary cruelty of daylight. She said a word to each one, quietly, like leaving notes for a future self who might be someone else by then.

"Up," she told the third arrow. "You go *up*, Felix Montrose."

The air grew warmer. Not the damp chill of cellars—she'd taught in three schools with cellars and knew their smell of rot and coal—but a dry, hearth-warmed warmth, the warmth of a house where someone has been baking since before you were born. The beeswax smell thickened. Somewhere below, faintly, she heard a bell.

A school bell.

Felix stood very still on the worn step, one hand flat against the stone. It wasn't the cracked brass clatter of Blackfen's own bell, the one Aldous rang with a rope gone green at the grip. This was softer. A small handbell, rung by someone patient, someone who had rung it ten thousand times and loved the sound.

It was ringing for lessons.

Her legs nearly turned around then. She gave them permission to, honestly, standing there in the warm dark with her heart going like a trapped bird. But the register in her satchel pressed against her hip—twenty-six names, eleven crossed out in pencil, three more crossed out just this week—and she thought of Ivetta's face at her window, half-frozen, whispering *they asked me to bring my brother next*, and she thought of Tam Wicke weeping in the pub over a mother's face he could not remember, and she gripped the chalk harder and kept going down.

"Up," she reminded the fourth arrow. "Remember."

* * *

The stairs ended at a landing so unexpected she laughed—a short, startled bark of laughter that echoed strangely and did not echo enough, as if the corridor beyond swallowed sound politely.

The landing opened into a hall.

Not a cave. Not a tunnel. A *hall*, flagged with stone polished by generations of slippers and boots, lit by lamps set in niches that burned without oil or wick or any mechanism she could see, giving off light the color of late afternoon through honey. Doors lined both sides—wooden doors, painted in faded colors, each with a small brass plate. She read them as she passed, her teacher's habit reading names before she could stop herself.

*Infants.* *Numbers and Letters.* *Songs.* *Needlework.* *The Long Ago.*

She stopped at that last one. *The Long Ago.* Under it, smaller, almost apologetic: *Local History.*

"They have a curriculum," Felix whispered. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "They have a bloody *curriculum*."

From behind the nearest door came the murmur of a lesson in progress. A voice—low, warm, unhurried—was saying something about rivers, and beneath the voice, children's voices answered in chorus, bright and willing and *unafraid*. Felix knew what unafraid children sounded like; she had spent nine years learning the difference between recitation and belief. These children believed.

Her hand was on the door handle before she decided to touch it.

Through a gap between frame and door, no wider than a coin, she looked into a classroom.

It was better than hers.

That was the thought that arrived, absurd and shameful and true. The room was round, not square, so no child sat at the back. The desks were old but lovingly kept, each one different, each one clearly *someone's*—carved with initials, worn smooth at the elbows. Light fell from lamps like the ones in the corridor, warm and even, with none of the flicker and gloom that made her own students squint by November. Maps covered the walls—not maps of counties she knew, but of the halls themselves, corridors branching like roots, and one map, large and central, of the land *above*: Blackfen and its fields, drawn with such care and such antiquity that the village looked like a scab on the face of the earth.

At the front stood the teacher.

He was tall and thin and his face was wrong—not grotesque, nothing so simple. Wrong the way a portrait is wrong when the eyes follow you. His features were fine and kindly arranged, his smile was everything a smile should be, and yet some deep animal part of Felix's brain screamed that this face had been *arranged*, that kindness had been applied to it like paint. His hands, though. His hands were beautiful—long, deft, chalk-dusted, moving as he wrote upon a slate board in a flowing antique script:

*Rivers remember where they have been. So do we.*

"And when the river forgets," he was saying, in a voice like a lullaby read aloud, "what happens to the fish?"

"Forgets nothing," chorused the children.

And there, third row, second desk, sat Marta Ellery, who had vanished in March two years ago, aged nine, whose bed had been found made and whose shoes had been left neatly by the door. She was eleven now. She was raising her hand. She was wearing a pinafore of good warm wool, and her hair was braided, and she was *smiling*.

Felix's whole body shook. Grief and fury arrived together, arm in arm, indistinguishable. *She's alive. She's alive and she's happy and she's been here two years and nobody—nobody—*

Marta's eyes found the gap in the door.

Felix would swear afterward that it happened in exactly that order: the child raised her hand, the teacher nodded, and the child turned her head with the smooth confidence of someone who already knew what she would see, and looked directly at Felix through the coin-wide gap, and smiled wider, and put one finger to her lips.

Then she pointed—not at the door, not at Felix, but down the corridor, deeper in.

*This way,* the gesture said. *You're wanted.*

* * *

Felix walked. There seemed little else to do. Running felt obscene; knocking felt worse. She moved down the corridor with her satchel clutched to her chest like a schoolmarm's shield, past more doors—*Arithmetic of the Count*, said one plate, and Felix's stomach dropped through the floor—past a windowless alcove where a shelf held rows of small clay lamps, each one labeled with a name in careful script, still burning.

She recognized four of the names. They were on her register.

She did not stop to think about what the lamps meant. Some knowledge arrives too heavy to pick up, and you must simply walk past it and let it sit where it falls.

More chalk arrows. She was drawing them compulsively now, every few yards, yellow marks on ancient stone, a trail of *up, up, this way out* threaded through the warm heart of the enemy's home. A passing elder—small, round, robed in grey, with that same arranged-and-applied gentleness in her face—paused to watch Felix work, and smiled, and said nothing, and continued on her way carrying a tray of bread rolls as if visitors who marked the walls were a common occurrence and no trouble at all.

That was the horror of it, Felix thought, drawing arrow number eleven. Not cruelty. *Hospitality.* She had prepared herself for chains, for weeping, for darkness. She had not prepared herself for bread.

The corridor opened into a larger space, and Felix stopped dead, because it was a schoolroom the size of a church, and it was full.

Dozens of children. More than the count should allow—children in clothes of decades past alongside children she'd taught herself, sitting in mixed-age rows at long tables, all of them bent over workbooks or listening to a lesson or, in one corner, rehearsing a song with a teacher who conducted with fingers too long and too many-jointed. The light was golden. The air smelled of bread and beeswax and, faintly, of the clay lamps. And every child, without exception, looked *well*. Rosy, rested, well-fed, well-loved. Felix had seen neglected children; she knew the geography of neglect in a face. There was none here. There was only ease, and attention, and the terrible absence of anyone counting the days.

At the head of the great room, on a raised chair like a modest throne, sat a woman so old she might have been carved rather than born, and so composed that Felix understood instantly, with a sinking heart, that this was Mother Sallow, and that she had been expected since the moment the door unbolted itself.

"Miss Montrose." The voice was dry and warm, like paper near a fire. "You've come a long way down. Will you take tea? You look half-frozen, and I can't think why. It isn't cold here. It has never once been cold here."

Behind her, thirty pairs of young eyes turned toward Felix with open, friendly curiosity—the look of children watching a new supply teacher, wondering if she'd be kind, wondering if she'd be fun.

One of them, in the front row, slid a second teacup along the table toward the empty chair beside him.

Felix's hand found her last stick of chalk in her pocket. Slowly, without taking her eyes off Mother Sallow, she drew one final arrow on the flagstone at her feet—pointing up, toward the stairs, toward the frost, toward everything she had come to take back—and stepped over it into the golden light.

"You're right," she said. "I'd love some tea. But first—" she lifted the register from her satchel and held it up, pencil-crossed names and all, "—I'd like to talk about attendance."
