## 12. The Family's Argument

The hall Felix was led into had been a chapel once, or something older than chapels. The ceiling arched in ribbed stone, and along its spine ran a seam of pale light—not windows, nothing so honest as windows, but veins of luminance set into the rock like marrow in bone. It was warmer here than in any room she had ever stood in. Warm as a kitchen. Warm as a hand.

She had left chalk arrows down three corridors and two staircases. She had counted her steps—four hundred and eleven since the door. She had told herself she was a teacher walking into a parent conference, and the lie had held for exactly as long as it took to see the children.

They were everywhere in the hall's soft light. Reading. Drawing. One boy was teaching three smaller ones a clapping game, and the rhythm of it echoed off the stone like rain on a roof. They wore clean clothes. Their faces were pink and their eyes were bright and not one of them looked imprisoned. Not one of them looked up.

Except one. A girl at the far end, curled in a windowless alcove with a book, glanced over—and Felix's chest seized, because it was Marlowe Hetch. Marlowe, missing since Felix's second week, whose desk still held a pencil stub sharpened down to nothing. Marlowe looked at her, and smiled—warm, easy, untroubled—and went back to her book.

That smile was the worst thing Felix had seen since she came to Blackfen.

"You'll want to sit," said the old woman. "Conferences go better sitting."

Mother Sallow was small. That was the first surprise. Felix had imagined—she admitted it to herself now—something vast, something chitinous and tall, a horror out of the deep places. But Sallow was a tiny woman in a grey cardigan, silver hair pinned with what appeared to be a carpenter's nail, her face lined like a well-read page. Her hands were folded in her lap. Her hands were the only wrong thing about her: too many fine lines across the knuckles, as if they had been folded and unfolded for a thousand years, and her fingernails were the pale yellow of candle wax.

She gestured to a chair. It was a school chair. Felix recognized the model—she had thirty of them upstairs.

"You've been in my classrooms," Felix said.

"I've been in *classrooms*," Sallow said, "since before your language had a word for chalk. Sit, Miss Montrose. You came a long way down to be rude standing up."

Felix sat. The chair was warm.

* * *

"Let's not pretend we don't know why you're here," Sallow began. "You want your children back. You've come down my stairs with chalk in your pocket and righteousness in your chest, and you think you're going to march them out into the cold and the grey and the forgetting, and everything will be mended." She smiled. It was a kind smile. That was somehow worse. "So let me tell you what you've actually walked into. You've walked into a rent dispute."

"A what?"

"A rent dispute. The oldest one in England, though we were here before England had a name for itself, and we'll be here after it's forgotten its own." Sallow's voice was unhurried, the voice of someone who had never once needed to raise it. "Your village sits on land that was never theirs. The founders of Blackfen knew that. They came up out of the fen with their pigs and their prayers and they built on ground that belonged to my family, and when my family came up to ask them about it—politely, I assure you, the first time was very polite—the founders did what founders do. They bargained."

"They bargained with children."

"They bargained with *teaching*," Sallow said, and something flickered across her face, quick as a fish under ice. "Do you know what we were, before? Do you know what we lost?" She unfolded her hands, and Felix saw that the fingertips were worn smooth, glossy, like river stones. "We had schools down here when your people were scratching pictures on antlers. We taught. It's all we ever loved. And when the ground was taken from us, when there was no more sky to be had, the founders offered a trade. Keep our halls. Keep our books. And every winter, send down a count of children, so that our school would never go empty. So that we would never again be a family with nothing left to teach."

"So they paid you in *pupils*."

"They paid their rent," Sallow said simply. "Every family pays rent, Miss Montrose. Yours pays it in coin and taxes and years of labor. Ours is paid in the only currency that matters to us. And I'll tell you something your village has spent two hundred years pretending not to know." She leaned forward, and the candle-wax nails caught the light. "Every founder signed. Every generation since has renewed it. Your headmaster's grandfather signed. Your headmaster signed, when he was young, in a hand you'd recognize. The village has never once, in all its history, failed to pay."

"Because it forgets," Felix said. "That's the trick, isn't it? They sign because they can't remember what they're signing away. That's not consent, that's—"

"Is it?" Sallow tilted her head. "Or is forgetting the only mercy a small village could afford? You've met them. The parents. Did they seem like monsters to you? Did they seem like people who could carry the knowledge of what they'd given, year after year, and still plant their gardens, and still say good morning?" She shook her head slowly. "The forgetting was in the original terms. *We* insisted on it. A mother who remembers is a mother who drowns herself in the fen. We are many things, Miss Montrose, but we are not cruel for the pleasure of it."

"You take children."

"We *teach* children," Sallow said, and for the first time her voice had an edge, thin and bright as a wire. "Come. You're a teacher. You'll want to see the classrooms."

* * *

Felix had expected to refuse. She was surprised to find herself walking.

The classrooms were down a corridor that curved gently, always gently, never turning sharply enough to disorient—she noticed that, and hated that she noticed it, because it meant someone had designed these halls to be *kind*. The first room held children of perhaps seven, arranged in a half-circle around an old man whose face was long and gentle and slightly too smooth, like a face remembered rather than seen. He was teaching them the fen. The old fen, the deep fen, the birds that had come and gone before the drainage. The children were rapt.

The second room was older children. Marlowe Hetch was there, and beside her, to Felix's shock, a boy who had been counted before Felix was born—she knew his face from the archive photographs, the 1961 class, the one with three names in pencil. He would be nearly seventy. He looked thirty. He looked *happy*. He was explaining long division to a cluster of younger ones, and he was doing it well, patiently, with a piece of chalk worn to a nub, and Felix felt something in her chest turn over, because that was her chalk. That was her nub. That was the stub from her own classroom, and the boy was using her method, her little rhyme for remainders, *the leftover goes to the poor*, which she had made up herself in her first term of training.

"Where did he learn that?" she whispered.

"From you," said a girl beside her, one of Felix's own, Tansy Reeve, missing six weeks now. "We all learn from you, Miss. He listens at the door sometimes. We all do. We like hearing how you do it." Tansy smiled, and it was a real smile, and Felix's righteous fury stumbled on it like a toe on a stair.

The third classroom was the one that undid her.

It was small, and lit warm as butter, and in it sat the three who had gone first—first frost, first absence, the neat shoes by the neat doors. They were reading aloud. Not drill, not recitation—reading, for pleasure, taking turns, laughing at the funny bits. The book was one Felix knew. It had been in her own classroom cupboard, and she had read it aloud to them herself, in October, in the grey light, and she had thought at the time that none of them were listening.

They had remembered every word. They had found the book. They had come down here and kept reading it.

"They're happier," said Mother Sallow, beside her now, though Felix hadn't heard her approach. "You can see it. You're an honest woman, Miss Montrose—your face has never once learned to lie, which is why you'll never be a politician and always be a teacher. So look at them, and tell me what you see."

Felix looked. She saw children who slept well. Children whose eyes didn't flick to the window when anyone said the word *winter*. Children who were warm, and fed, and taught by people who had nothing else in the world to do but teach them, and who had been doing it for a very long time, and were very, very good at it.

"I see happy children," she said, and her voice cracked on it. "And I've seen unhappy ones upstairs. In the cold. With the forgetting pressing on them like weather." She turned to face the old woman. "But happiness isn't the question, is it? That's the whole point. A gilded cage with excellent long division is still a cage. They didn't *choose* this. They were counted. They were taken while they slept. Their shoes were arranged by their beds by someone who isn't them."

"Was it?" Sallow said softly.

And then Felix looked again, and saw it—the thing she had been too furious to see. By the third classroom door, on a little shelf, were shoes. Dozens of pairs. Small shoes, worn shoes, and every pair was *worn*—scuffed, broken in, shaped by feet. Not arranged by a bed. Arrived on their own feet.

"The first ones were taken," Sallow admitted. "Long ago. I won't lie to you; there's no profit in it. The founders' bargain was ugly and we were uglier in those years, and I have ledgers I'm ashamed of. But the terms changed. Generations ago. Ask your Aldous—no, don't, he doesn't remember the terms, only the fear." She folded her hands again. "For a long time now, the count has worked differently than your village believes. The children are *asked*. Not in their beds. Down here, or at the door, or in dreams that are only dreams. They are shown the halls, and the school, and the warmth, and they are told the truth: that they may go back up whenever they wish, and nothing will stop them, and nothing above will remember to miss them."

"That's not a fair choice," Felix said. "That's a choice between here and nowhere. You've made sure the *nowhere* is unbearable. You made the village forget them. You rigged it."

"The forgetting was your village's price, not mine," Sallow said, "but you're right." She said it simply, without flinching, and Felix realized with a lurch that this was the most honest conversation she'd had since arriving in Blackfen. "You're right. It is not a fair choice. Nothing offered to a child by something old and warm and patient is a fair choice. I know that." Her wax-yellow fingers tightened on each other. "I have known it for a long time. It is the one debt *I* owe that no one has ever come to collect."

"Then change it," Felix said. "If you know it's wrong—"

"Change it how? Unmake the forgetting? Your village would tear itself apart. Every parent remembering at once, every grave that isn't a grave—" Sallow shook her head. "You think I haven't lain awake—do you imagine I sleep, Miss Montrose?—turning it over? But you didn't come down here to audit my conscience. You came down here because you think the count is theft." She looked up, and her eyes were pale and old and terribly tired. "So tell me. If the rent stops, what happens to my family? To the halls? To *him*?" She nodded toward the classroom where the boy from 1961 was teaching, patiently, with love, with Felix's own chalk rhyme. "He has nothing above. No one above. His mother's face is gone from him—your village's mercy did that, not mine. This is the only home that remembers him. If the count ends, does he end with it?"

And Felix, who had marched down four hundred and eleven steps with righteousness in her chest, found she had no answer.

* * *

They sat afterward in the chapel-hall, and Sallow had tea brought—actual tea, in actual cups, and Felix drank it because her hands were shaking and the warmth helped. Around them the children played and read and laughed, and the light came up through the stone like something alive.

"Here is what I know," Felix said at last. "You were wronged first. The land was taken. The bargain was made by frightened people and sealed in forgetting. Every injustice in this story has a parent, and the parent is older than everyone alive." She set down her cup. "But two wrongs and a warm classroom don't add up to a right. Those children upstairs—the ones still there, the ones like Ivetta who ran because you asked her to bring her *brother*—they never signed anything. They were born into a debt their great-great-grandparents agreed to, and they're paying it in the only coin they have. Themselves. That's not rent. That's inheritance of a chain."

Sallow was quiet a long moment. The light in the ceiling-veins seemed to dim slightly, as if the hall itself were listening.

"And what would you do?" the old woman asked. "Burn the ledgers? Free the children? And then what, Miss Montrose? What does justice look like from underneath? You've seen my halls. Tell me what your justice does to the boy from 1961."

"I don't know yet," Felix said honestly. "But I know it doesn't look like a count. I know it doesn't look like a village that forgets. And I know—" she stood, because her legs wouldn't stay folded any longer, because the warmth had begun to feel like a hand on her shoulder, gentle and patient and never letting go— "I know that whatever the answer is, it has to be chosen. By them. Freely. With a real door and a real way back and a world above that remembers their names. That's the only justice I've got. It's small. But it's mine."

Sallow looked up at her, and for a moment—just a moment—the old woman's face was not a matriarch's face at all, but a teacher's, tired and hopeful and hungry for an answer she'd been waiting centuries to hear.

"Small justices," she murmured. "We used to believe in those."

Felix turned to go. She had chalk arrows to check—three corridors, two staircases—and the thought of them steadied her like a handrail. But at the edge of the hall, Mother Sallow's voice followed her, soft as settling snow:

"You should know, before you climb. Your chalk marks are very good. Neat little arrows. A careful woman." A pause, and the warmth in the hall deepened, thickened, like honey poured over everything. "My children found them hours ago. They thought you'd want them kept safe."

Felix turned. Sallow smiled the kind smile.

"We washed them away, dear. Every one. You'll have to find your own way out." She folded her hands. "Or stay for supper. The children would love it. And you've been down here long enough, now, that I suspect—" the wax-yellow fingers tapped once, gently, on the arm of her chair— "the way up has started to look a great deal like the way down."

Above her, faint through four hundred and eleven steps of stone, Felix thought she heard a hatch being opened, and a girl's voice calling down into the dark, over and over, all night long: *Miss? Miss? Miss?*
