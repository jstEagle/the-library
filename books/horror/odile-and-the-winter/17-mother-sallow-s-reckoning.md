## 17. Mother Sallow's Reckoning

The smoke was still rising from the school stove when Felix heard the bells.

Not the church bells—those had been silent since the night of the burning, since the village woke weeping and calling names through the streets like a town struck by fever. These were lower, older, coming up through the floorboards rather than down from the tower. Three notes, repeated, patient as dripping water.

She stood at her classroom window with the ledger's ashes still on her fingers. Below, in the yard, the children were filing into assembly because Aldous had rung the handbell out of habit, out of some desperate instinct that routine might hold the morning together. They moved strangely—looking over their shoulders, holding each other's sleeves. The whole village had spent two days remembering, and remembering, Felix was learning, was a kind of wound.

The three notes came again. Closer.

* * *

Mother Sallow had not walked to Blackfen School. She had simply arrived, the way frost arrives—not traveling so much as manifesting where the conditions were right. She stood at the gate in a coat the color of wet slate, and behind her, filling the lane from the schoolyard fence to the old well, stood the Family: dozens of them, tall and unhurried, their faces gentle in the way that made Felix's teeth ache. Their wrongness was quieter in daylight. That somehow made it worse.

Sallow did not knock. She did not need to; the doors of Blackfen School had never been locked against her, and everyone present understood this without being told.

Felix met her in the corridor, chalk dust on her skirt and ash under her nails.

"You burned it," Sallow said. Not an accusation. A statement, delivered with the mild disappointment of a landlady surveying water damage.

"I burned it."

"Four hundred years of record." Sallow folded her hands. Her voice was warm as a hearth, and it carried down the corridor without effort, reaching every ear in the building whether it wished to or not. "Every name. Every count. Every winter accounted for and balanced. Do you know what you have done, Miss Montrose?"

"I've done arithmetic," Felix said. "You should try it sometime. It doesn't work when one side keeps all the numbers."

Something flickered across Sallow's face—the first expression Felix had seen there that wasn't serene. It might have been amusement. It might have been hunger.

"The assembly," Sallow said. "I believe you were about to begin. I'll join you."

It was not a request, and no one in the history of Blackfen had ever mistaken it for one.

* * *

The hall smelled of floor polish and fear. The children sat in rows, and behind them, pressed against the back wall and spilling in through windows they'd climbed through without shame, stood the village—farmers with hay still on their boots, the publican Tam Wicke with his eyes red-rimmed, Mrs. Halloway from the post office, the constable who had once refused to open a case and now could not meet anyone's gaze. They had come because the bells had called them, or because guilt had, which amounted to the same thing.

Aldous stood at the front, gray as porridge, holding his handbell like a shield.

Sallow walked down the center aisle slowly, and the crowd parted before her the way water parts around a stone—not willingly, but inevitably. She took the stage as though she had been born on it, which, Felix reflected, she possibly had.

"Good morning," Sallow said, and her warmth filled the room like poured honey. "What a fine gathering. I have attended many assemblies in this hall, over many years. You don't remember them. That was the arrangement."

Nobody spoke. A child near the front began quietly crying, and her mother pushed through the crowd and gathered her up and did not sit back down.

"I have come," Sallow continued, "to discuss restitution."

"There's nothing to discuss," Felix said, from the side of the stage. "The record's gone. The debt died with it."

"Ah." Sallow turned to her with genuine, terrible kindness. "Miss Montrose. Sit down. This is a matter between the Family and the village—a matter older than your arrival, older than your nation, older than the language you're misusing. But since you're standing there, let me educate you."

She faced the hall.

"For four hundred years, this village has lived warm. Bad harvests passed you by. Fever skipped your doorsteps. Your wells never ran dry, your children rarely sickened, your winters, though long, were never cruel. You told yourselves this was luck. It was rent. And the price—" her voice softened, became almost tender, "—the price was small. A few children each winter. Children who went below into light and learning and full bellies, while above ground their parents smiled and forgot them by spring. You gave us your surplus. We gave you everything else."

"We didn't know," someone said from the back—it was Tam Wicke, his voice cracking. "We didn't *know*, that's the evil of it, we weren't asked—"

"You were asked," Sallow said gently. "Your great-great-grandmothers signed. In wax. On vellum. The first winter, when your founders knelt in the snow and begged the hill for shelter, the hill named its terms, and they agreed, and their agreement has fed my family ever since. Ignorance was part of the payment. You purchased your forgetting with your children. Do not stand in my hall and claim innocence now that the bill has come due in memory instead."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Then Mrs. Halloway stepped forward. She was seventy if she was a day, small and round and armed with nothing but a knitting bag, and she planted herself directly in Sallow's path.

"My sister," she said. Her voice shook, then steadied. "Winter of 1931. I was six. I woke one morning and there was a gap in our house shaped like Bess, and Mother set four places at table for years after, and none of us knew why. You took my sister and you made my mother forget her own child, and you call that *rent*?"

"I call it the oldest contract in this valley," Sallow said.

"Well." Mrs. Halloway lifted her chin. "Contracts need two parties, and I'm telling you the party above ground is finished. Take it up with a solicitor."

A ripple of something—laughter, ragged and frightened and real—went through the crowd. Felix felt it pass through her too, loosening something in her chest. Fear shared aloud is already half-broken.

* * *

From the stage, Mother Sallow watched the woman with the knitting bag and felt the first unfamiliar sensation of her long tenure: the ground shifting.

She had expected grief. Grief was familiar currency; she had collected it for centuries, had learned its denominations and exchange rates. She had expected rage from individuals—rage was weather, it blew through and passed. What she had not expected was *this*: the whole room turning toward her at once, shoulder to shoulder, like a single animal deciding not to flee.

"Restitution," Felix Montrose said again, stepping forward, and Sallow marked how the villagers unconsciously aligned themselves behind the woman, as though she were a plow and they the field. "Name your terms. Let's hear them in the open, where everyone can refuse them together."

"You cannot refuse me," Sallow said, and heard, even as she said it, how the sentence sounded in the new air of this remembered village. Like an antique. Like a threat from a dead tongue.

"Can't we?" Felix glanced at the crowd. "Mrs. Halloway? Can we?"

"No," said Mrs. Halloway.

"Tam?"

"No, miss."

The constable cleared his throat. "No," he managed, and then, finding his feet beneath him, louder: "No."

And then the whole hall, forty voices, sixty, the farmers and the shopkeepers and the mothers with children on their hips, in a rough unlovely chorus: "*No.*"

Sallow stood in the sound of it and understood, with the patience of her kind, that something fundamental had broken overnight—and that it had not been broken by fire, but by memory. A debt requires forgetting on one side. The forgetting was gone. The contract's engine had seized.

"The record was the authority," she said slowly, working it through aloud, because she had always believed in precision. "Without the ledger, the count is only... numbers. Numbers without a keeper."

"Numbers without a keeper," Felix agreed. "Welcome to arithmetic."

For a moment—one moment Sallow would examine for a hundred years afterward—she considered the old ways. The halls below were deep, and her family was patient, and patience had always won. But she looked at the faces in the hall and saw no prey there. Only debtors who had decided, collectively, to default.

"You will regret this warmth," she said at last. "Winters are long, Miss Montrose. Winters are very long."

"So I'm told," Felix said. "We'll hold the line anyway. All of us."

* * *

But Sallow was not finished, and everyone in the hall knew it. She descended from the stage with her hands still folded, and the crowd parted again—but differently now, uneasily, because a cornered ancient thing is more dangerous than a confident one.

"Very well," she said. "The ledger is ash. But consider, before you celebrate: my family kept faith for four hundred years. We took only what was written. We fed your fields and warmed your beds and never once broke terms. And in return, this morning, you burned our house's memory and called it justice." She paused at the end of the aisle, close enough that Felix could see that her eyes, up close, were the brown of rich soil, and just as patient. "I will not take children. Not today. But I will say this, and you may weigh it however you like: a bargain ended badly leaves a hole shaped like a bargain. Something will flow into that hole. Pray it is not worse than us."

She turned toward the doors.

"Wait," Felix said.

Sallow stopped. Did not turn.

"You built your claim on consent," Felix said. "You said it yourself, just now—the founders *agreed*. Your whole law, your whole logic, rests on freely given word. So here's mine, freely given, in front of witnesses: any child who chooses your halls may go. Chosen, not counted. Asked, not taken. Remembered, always—by name, above ground, in daylight. That's the new bargain. Take it or leave it, but you can't call yourself honest and refuse it."

The hall held its breath.

Slowly, Mother Sallow turned. And Felix saw, with a lurch, that the matriarch was smiling—not the hearth-warm smile from before, but something smaller and stranger, almost wondering.

"Consent," Sallow repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. That has always been our law. Older than ledgers. Older than me." She looked around the hall—at the mothers, at the children, at the woman with the knitting bag who had not budged an inch. "Freely chosen. Never counted. Never forgotten."

"That's the offer."

"Then I accept it," Sallow said, "on one condition of my own." Her soil-brown eyes found Felix's and held them. "The final entry in your burned ledger, Miss Montrose. The entry written decades before you ever came to Blackfen. You read it. You know whose name closed the count. That account was never settled—and accounts, in my experience, do not burn."

The doors opened onto the cold morning, and Mother Sallow departed the way she had come, and behind her the tall gentle-faced ones filed out into the light, and the village of Blackfen stood in the wreckage of its four-hundred-year sleep, victorious and terrified in equal measure, while Felix Montrose felt those words settle over her like snow:

*That account was never settled.*
