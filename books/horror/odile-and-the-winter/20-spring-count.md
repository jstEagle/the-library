## 20. Spring Count

The first morning of the spring term, Felix Montrose arrived at Blackfen School an hour early to do something she had never done in eleven years of teaching: count her students before they arrived.

Twenty-two names on the roster. She had written them herself, in ink — real ink, the good iron-gall kind that stained your fingers and refused to be quietly erased. She had bought a special pen for the purpose, and Mr. Aldous had watched her purchase it at the village shop with the expression of a man watching someone buy a coffin for himself.

"You could just use pencil," he'd said.

"No," Felix had said, "I really couldn't."

The school smelled different in spring. All winter it had smelled of coal smoke and chalk and, faintly, from below the floorboards, of bread and beeswax — a smell that no amount of scrubbing would lift and that Felix had eventually stopped trying to lift, because some part of her had decided that if the children below wanted their halls to smell like home, that was between them and their ovens. Now the windows were open, and the smell was mud and new grass and someone's distant laundry, and Felix found she could breathe without doing arithmetic first.

She pinned the roster to the board where everyone could see it. Twenty-two names, each with a small box beside it, and above the boxes, in letters tall enough to read from the back row: **EVERY NAME, EVERY DAY.**

It was not a sophisticated system. It was, in fact, the least sophisticated thing Felix had ever instituted, which was precisely why she trusted it. The trouble with Blackfen had never been arithmetic. The trouble was that everyone had agreed, politely and all together, to stop counting. So now they counted. Out loud. In ink. With witnesses.

The children began arriving at half past eight, and Felix stood at the door and greeted each one by name, and each one answered "present" before she even asked, because they had practiced it, the way you practice a fire drill or a prayer.

"Pip Carrow."

"Present, miss."

"Maren Ostler."

"Present, miss."

"Dree."

Dree Halloway — the foreman's daughter, nine years old, who had spent the winter refusing to go anywhere near the back field where the door was — came up the path last, as usual, dragging her boots through the last of the mud. She stopped at the threshold and looked up at Felix with the flat, serious gaze of a child who has seen adults fail at something important and is keeping score.

"Present, miss," Dree said. "And I checked the hatch this morning. Still unlocked. Still nothing coming up."

"Thank you, Dree."

"My da says I shouldn't check it."

"Do you want to stop checking it?"

Dree considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "No," she said. "Somebody ought to."

Felix wrote her name in the box. Ink. Witnessed.

* * *

The new rule had three parts, and Felix made the children recite them every morning, partly because ritual steadied people and partly because she had learned — the hard way, down in the warm lit halls beneath the hill — that the Family itself ran on rules, and she intended to beat them at their own game.

"One," the class chanted, "every absence gets investigated. Same day. No exceptions."

"Two: every name gets remembered. Spoken aloud, written in ink, read at solstice."

"Three—" and here Pip Carrow always grinned, because the third rule was his favorite— "nobody goes below alone, and nobody stays below by accident."

That last clause had been fought over. Pell — sweet, stubborn Pell, who had kissed his mother's hand and walked back down the stairs with his chin up — had insisted on it, actually, in the letter he'd sent up through the hatch in February, written in careful copperplate on paper that smelled of beeswax. *Tell them,* he'd written, *that we are not prisoners and will not be treated as lost. Tell them the difference.* Felix had read the letter aloud to the whole school, twice, and then added rule three to the board, and the room had gone very quiet, and Maren Ostler had raised her hand and asked, in a small voice, whether letters from below could be answered.

They could. There was a system now. Letters went into a tin box by the hatch on the first of each month; answers came up on the full moon, folded into squares that smelled of bread. The postal service of Blackfen consisted of Dree Halloway, who had appointed herself postmistress the way other children appointed themselves king of the sandpit, and nobody, including her father, had been able to talk her out of it.

"It's not so bad down there," Dree said sometimes, handing over the mail with the offhand air of someone reporting the weather. "Pell says the lessons are better. He says they've got a globe with countries on it that don't exist anymore."

"That's history for you," Felix said. "Half of teaching is telling children about things that used to be true."

"But he says the teachers never get tired. And they remember everything. Every student, forever."

Felix kept her hands steady on the stack of exercise books. "We're working on that up here too," she said.

* * *

The memorial was Aldous's idea, which surprised everyone, most of all Aldous.

He had come to her lodgings in late March carrying a bottle of elderflower wine and wearing the expression of a man about to apologize to a horse. He was thinner than he'd been in autumn; the confession had taken something out of him that he was only slowly growing back, like grass over a scar. But his eyes were clearer than Felix had ever seen them, and when he spoke, he looked at her directly, which was new.

"I keep a list," he said. "Of the counted. I started it the night after the burning, when everyone woke up remembering. I couldn't sleep, so I wrote down every name as it came back to me. My sister's name came first." He turned the wine bottle in his hands. "Edith. She took my place in the winter of '61. I was seven. I don't remember her face." His voice did something complicated and then steadied. "Nobody remembers her face. That's what they took, Montrose. Not the children. The *remembering*. And I think — I think we owe them a day. A proper day. With their names said out loud, in daylight, by everyone."

So it was settled: the first Sunday of April, on the green behind the church, under the bare-then-budding trees.

Felix spent the week before helping prepare, and discovered that grief, given somewhere official to go, turned out to be surprisingly practical. Mrs. Ostler organized flowers. The pub landlord — who had once offered Felix tea instead of answers and warned her not to dig — spent two days building a long wooden board on posts, sanded smooth, and would accept no thanks for it. "For the names," he said gruffly. "All of them. Painted proper." When Felix asked how many names there were, he looked at her with the old wariness flickering behind his eyes, and then, deliberately, let it go.

"Hundred and six," he said. "Aldous's list. Hundred and six children, going back to before the church was built. My gran's brother is on it. I never knew I had a great-uncle until February." He picked up his brush. "Funny thing. You spend your whole life not knowing a hole is there, and then somebody shows you the hole, and you can't stop looking at it."

"Somebody should have shown you sooner," Felix said.

"Aye." He dipped the brush. "Well. You did. So."

* * *

The morning of the memorial, Felix woke before dawn and lay in bed listening to the birds, and thought about the ledger.

She still dreamed about it sometimes — the ledgers, rather, the whole low room of them, spines cracked, pages soft as cloth, every counted child recorded in a hand so patient and beautiful it made you want to weep. Her own name had been in there, entered decades before she was born, in ink that had dried before her grandparents met. *Felix Montrose. Final entry.* She had burned the master ledger in the school stove and watched the count collapse, but the memory of seeing her own life pre-written — her belonging arranged like furniture in a room she'd never visited — still woke her some nights with her heart going like a hammer.

What saved her, on those nights, was the roster. Twenty-two names. She would light the lamp and read them, in ink, and remind herself: this is the difference. A name written by someone who loves you, today, versus a name written for you, before you existed, by something that needed you to balance its books.

She got up, dressed, and walked to the green while the mist was still on it.

By nine the whole village had gathered. Felix had wondered, privately, whether anyone would come — whether the old enforced forgetting would reassert itself like a bent sapling straightening — but the green filled and kept filling. Farmers in their good coats. The constable, who had once refused to open a case, standing at the back with his hat literally in his hands. Mothers whose children had come up the stairs in March, blinking in the daylight, running into arms. And the children themselves, the returned ones, sitting in a front row that someone had thoughtfully provided, holding candles that wouldn't be lit until dusk but that they gripped anyway, as if the wax itself were company.

Mr. Aldous opened the service. He stood at the painted board — hundred and six names in white on dark wood, ranked by year like a class photo — and for a long moment he could not speak. The village waited. Nobody hurried him. That was the other thing Felix had noticed since the burning: Blackfen had gotten much better at waiting.

"My sister's name is Edith Aldous," he said finally. "Winter of 1861. I was supposed to be the one who went. I don't remember her face. I have tried for fifty years, and I don't remember it, and I believe — I have come to believe — that this was done to me, and that saying her name out loud is the first stone of building it back." His voice cracked clean through. "Edith Aldous," he said. "Present."

And the whole village, hundred strong or more, answered as one, in the words the schoolchildren had taught them:

"Remembered."

* * *

They went down the list all morning.

It took hours. Farmers took turns reading, and shopkeepers, and children too — the returned ones read the names of the years they'd lived through below, their voices steady, because they of all people knew that the ones on the board were not ghosts but classmates. When a name was read, a family member or neighbor stepped forward and said one true thing about the child, if anyone could remember one, and if no one could, they said so, honestly, and the honest saying seemed to matter almost as much.

*"Tamsin Wicke. Winter of 1843."*

"She liked eels," said old Tam Wicke, who had come back from below hollowed and faceless-memory'd thirty years ago, and who had wept for weeks without knowing why, and who now stood straight as a fencepost. "My aunt told me once, before the forgetting got her. Tamsin liked eels and hated church. Present."

"Remembered."

*"Ossian Prew. Winter of 1899."*

Nobody stepped forward. A long silence. Then Dree Halloway, nine years old, postmistress, hatch-checker, raised her hand.

"He's mentioned in the letters," she said. "Pell says Ossian teaches the little ones their letters, down below. Says he's the best teacher they've got. Says he's been teaching for a hundred and twenty years and never once got tired." She paused. "Present."

"Remembered," said the village, and Felix saw several people write that down — actually write it, in notebooks they'd brought for the purpose — because the new institution of Blackfen was not grief. It was record-keeping.

By midday the list was done, and Mrs. Ostler's flowers covered the base of the board, and the pub landlord brought out cider and lemonade, and the memorial became, without anyone deciding it, a fair. Children ran races. Somebody produced a fiddle. Tam Wicke danced with three grandmothers and cried laughing.

Felix stood apart with her cider, watching, and felt a hand tug her sleeve. Pell's mother, a small round woman with her son's same stubborn chin.

"He sent something," she said. "For you. Came up with the full-moon post." She pressed a folded square into Felix's hand, beeswax-scented, and moved off before Felix could respond, which Felix suspected was deliberate kindness.

Felix waited until she was alone by the board, under the hundred and six names, and unfolded it.

*Miss Montrose,* the copperplate read. *Mother Sallow asks me to tell you the following, and I have checked it twice because I did not believe it either. The ledger you burned was the master copy. But Mother Sallow keeps no other. She says a count that must be hidden is not a count but a theft, and she will not be a thief, whatever else she is. She says the old bargain is dead and the new one holds, and she says — these are her exact words, she made me memorize them — "Tell the schoolmistress that the Family also remembers, now. Every name. Both directions. Tell her we are learning her rule, and that it is a better rule than ours ever was.*

*Also, enclosed, please find my spring report. I received top marks in Natural History. We are studying moles. They are more interesting than you would think.*

*Your student (still counting myself yours),
Pell.*

*P.S. Edith Aldous says hello. She wants Mr. Aldous to know her favorite color was blue. She says he'll know what to do with that."*

Felix stood a long time under the board, in the sun, with the fiddle going and the children shouting, and she did not cry, quite, but she had to look at the flowers for a while.

Then she folded the letter, put it in her pocket next to the roster, and went to find Mr. Aldous, because there was work to do. There was always work to do. That was the whole point.

"Mr. Aldous," she said, when she found him. "I have a message from your sister."

He went white, then pink, then held onto the fence.

"She remembered something," Felix said. "Her favorite color. It was blue."

And Mr. Aldous, headmaster of Blackfen School, survivor of the winter count, sat down right there on the grass in his good coat and laughed and wept in equal measure, while around him the village that had forgotten for two hundred years went on loudly, stubbornly, permanently remembering — and behind the school, in the back field, the door stood open in the spring light, and from far down the stairs, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a bell ringing for afternoon lessons.
