## 22. Register of Names

The register took Felix eleven years to finish.

She had not meant it to take so long. When she began—two winters after the burning, when the village had stopped flinching at the word *count* and could say *ledger* without someone leaving the room—she imagined a summer's work. A list of names, dates, perhaps a line or two about each child. She had been a teacher for a long time by then; she knew how to make a tidy column.

But names, she learned, are like roots. You pull one and the soil comes with it.

The first difficulty was that no one had written the children down. The Family kept its ledger, and Felix had burned it, and what remained were fragments: a christening record here, a school fee receipt there, a grandmother in Wicke Hollow who remembered that her sister "went away in the cold year" but could not say which cold year, because there had been so many. The parish archive held baptisms but not departures. The constabulary held nothing at all—the constable of 1911 had filed an entire missing class under *seasonal adjustment*, and his successors had followed suit with the diligence of men filing weather.

So Felix went door to door. Then farm to farm. Then, when the farms ran out, to the returned ones—to Tam Wicke, who sat in her kitchen every Thursday and wept gently while she tried to remember, and to Aldous, who by then had retired from the headmastership and spent his days copying out everything he could recall of his sister Marren: the gap in her teeth, the way she whistled through it, the song she used to sing about a fox who wanted to be a bishop.

"She'd be fifty-eight now," he said once, pencil hovering. "I keep doing the arithmetic. I can't stop doing the arithmetic."

"I know," Felix said. "I do it too."

That was the other thing the register taught her: she was not the only one keeping count anymore. The whole village had become, in its slow and stubborn way, a counting house of grief. Mothers who had forgotten now remembered, and remembered loudly, and demanded that their remembering be written down where no pencil could reach it.

Ink only, they told her. Ink only, forever.

* * *

The reading happened every winter solstice, on the shortest day, in the school hall that Felix had once watched through a window full of children pressing their palms to glass. The hall had been repaired since—new windows, a proper stove, the old chalkboards replaced though Felix had argued to keep one, the one bearing the ghost-faint outline of her descent arrows, scrubbed but never quite gone. She liked it as a reminder that some marks resist erasure if you refuse to stop drawing them.

The first reading had eleven attendees and lasted four minutes. The last one before this year had standing room only and lasted two hours, because the register had grown to three hundred and forty-one names, and because the villagers had developed opinions about how each name should be said.

"You have to give Ivetta's brother his middle name," Mrs. Dree insisted—Dree the foreman's widow, who had taken over the excavation company and turned it into something gentler, a firm that restored old walls instead of cutting through them. "He was Tobias Under-Hill Bramm. His mother was very proud of the Under-Hill. Said it was honest."

"It was honest," Felix agreed, and wrote it in ink.

The children of the current school read too. That had been Ivetta's suggestion, back when Ivetta was seventeen and fierce and training to be a teacher herself. *You shouldn't read them all yourself,* she'd said. *It's not a penance, Miss Montrose. It's a memory. Memories need more than one mouth.*

So the children read, in turns, the youngest stumbling sweetly over names like Ondrey Sixpence and the older ones reading steady and clear. And every year, at the end, came the silence—not an enforced forgetting this time, but a chosen one, sixty seconds in which the stove ticked and the wind worried the eaves and everyone in the hall thought their own private thoughts about their own particular name on the list.

Felix had come to love that silence more than any speech. It was the sound of a village holding a debt it had finally agreed to pay: attention.

* * *

This year, the winter before the register would finally be declared complete, Felix stood at the lectern with her spectacles low on her nose and watched the hall fill.

Aldous came early, as always, and sat in the front row with Marren's copybook on his knee. He was eighty-one now and had stopped doing the arithmetic aloud, though Felix suspected he still did it silently, a rosary of subtraction. Beside him sat Tam Wicke, ancient and serene, who still could not remember her mother's face but had lately begun to remember her mother's *hands*—flour-dusted, quick, one thumbnail bitten short—and considered this a fair trade. "A face is a lot to carry," Tam had said. "Hands are enough to be going on with."

Ivetta came with her own class now, bussed in from the coastal school where she taught, and her students gawked at Blackfen's hall the way country children gawk at anything old. Ivetta herself had grown into the exact shape of certainty Felix remembered from that first week—the girl who corrected the local history lesson, who recited the Family under the hill like scripture. Only now the scripture had been rewritten, and Ivetta had written much of it herself. Her pupils learned the true history of Blackfen: the founding on owed land, the bargain, the burnings and the bargains rewritten. They learned it the way you learn fire safety—thoroughly, and with respect, and without terror.

Mrs. Dree brought the tally stone.

This was new. The original door had been sealed again—by mutual agreement, with hinges this time, oiled ones—but a section of carved lintel had come loose during the restoration work, and Dree's crew had mounted it in the school hall behind glass, next to a brass plate: *THE COUNT ENDED HERE.* Children touched the glass on their way past. It had become, improbably, a luck thing. Felix had objected at first—a tally of stolen children as a good-luck charm!—but Ivetta had talked her round. "They're not lucky because of what was counted, Miss. They're lucky because we stopped."

The reading went long. Three hundred and forty-one names, plus four discovered just that autumn in a farmer's diary, plus one added by request at the last minute: Pell's mother, who asked each year to have her son's name read even though Pell was not dead, was not lost, was in fact sitting right there—

Except this year, for the first time, he wasn't.

* * *

Pell had stayed below at nineteen, then twenty, then thirty, and somewhere in those years the visits had reversed themselves. He came up now the way other men went down into cellars: regularly, comfortably, carrying things. He brought mushrooms that glowed faintly and tasted of honey. He brought his mother's kitchen a warmth that never quite left the walls. And for the last six years he had brought students.

That was the strangest harvest of the whole strange story. Mother Sallow, bound by the logic of consent she herself had invoked, had discovered that teaching freely-given children was sweeter than taking counted ones—and that freely-given children sometimes grew up and wanted to teach in turn. Below, in the halls that smelled of bread and beeswax, a small school had kindled. Not a replacement for the count. An alternative to it. Any child of Blackfen could apply, Felix had stipulated; any child could leave whenever they wished, no ledger, no debt, no forgetting. Sallow had accepted, and to everyone's continued astonishment, had kept the terms with the punctiliousness of a creature who had kept books for a thousand years.

Some children went down for a season and came back. Some stayed years. One girl, Hester Ninefields, had come up at sixteen speaking three languages Felix couldn't identify and gone off to university on a scholarship, writing home letters that began *Dear Everyone* and ended *P.S. Tell the Family the etymology was wrong, I checked.*

And Pell taught. Pell, who had kissed his mother's hand and said he learned everything he loved below, had become the below's ambassador to the above, a broad cheerful man with candlelight in his eyes and a habit of arriving at solstice readings slightly damp, as though the stairwell between worlds were a river crossing.

This year he was late.

Felix felt it the way you feel a missing desk. She read the last names—Tobias Under-Hill Bramm, given his full honors—and called the silence, and the hall hushed, and in the hush she heard, faintly, from the direction of the old excavation site, singing.

Not mournful singing. The opposite. A crowd-song, the kind children make walking home from something wonderful.

* * *

They came up through the hatch at the field's edge in a procession of lantern-light: Pell first, taller than Felix remembered possible, then Mother Sallow—ancient, upright, wrapped in grey wool, nodding to villagers as though the intervening centuries of grievance were a coat check she'd mislaid—and behind them, two dozen children of the below, carrying candles, blinking at the snow like creatures seeing weather for the first time.

"We finished term," Pell announced to the hall at large, breathless, delighted. "Early. Sallow said if the above was going to honor the winter, the below ought to attend." He grinned at Felix, and the grin was the same grin from a stairwell twenty years ago, a boy choosing his own door. "Also we got lost twice. Your surface has too many fences, Miss Montrose. Who approved these fences?"

"A committee," Felix said. "Most sins trace back to one."

Sallow approached the lectern. The hall did not breathe. Felix stepped aside—an act that would have been unthinkable fifteen years ago, and now felt as natural as yielding a doorway—and the matriarch of the Family looked out at the assembled village, at the mothers and the returned ones and the children with candles, and spoke in a voice like a drawer of old paper opening.

"I have kept accounts for longer than your language has had a word for *debt*," she said. "I have taken children because the ground was owed, and I have told myself the taking was rent, and I have told myself the children were happier, and both things were true, and neither thing made it right. This I have learned slowly, the way stone learns rain." She set something on the lectern: a book. Old, leather-bound, new-ruled inside. "Your teacher burned my ledger, and the burning was justice. This is not a replacement. It is a contribution. Every child who ever chose us freely, from the first night of the new terms to this one—written in ink, as your custom requires. So that when your register is read, ours may be read beside it. Belonging should be recorded wherever it is real."

She stepped back. The silence that followed was not the sixty-second silence. It was something rawer, newer—the sound of a village deciding, all at once, in real time, what to feel.

It was Tam Wicke who broke it, rising from the front row with tears running freely down her old face, and saying, in the voice of someone answering a question asked eighty years ago:

"Well. About time somebody wrote the nice ones down too."

And the hall laughed, wetly, hugely, and the winter broke open like a loaf.

* * *

Afterward, when the candles were guttering and the children of two worlds were trading sweets and insults with the easy brutality of all children everywhere, Pell found Felix by the stove.

"You'll retire soon," he said. It wasn't a question. "Ivetta's ready. The register's done. Even Sallow's done—she's talking about *sabbatical*, which I think is a word she invented tonight to avoid saying *holiday*."

"The register's nearly done," Felix corrected. "There's always another name. That's rather the point. A register that ends is a register that's stopped paying attention."

Pell nodded, and then went quiet in a way he rarely did, looking into the stove's orange heart. "Can I ask you something? The night I turned back down the stairs. My mother's hand. Do you ever—" He stopped. Started again. "Do you regret letting me choose?"

Felix considered it honestly, because he deserved honesty and had earned it twice over.

"I regret the cost," she said. "Every solstice, when your mother stands for the silence, I regret the cost. But regret the choice?" She shook her head. "No. A freedom that only counts when it goes your way isn't freedom. It's just a longer leash. You taught me that, standing on those stairs. Best lesson I never taught."

Pell smiled, kissed her hand—the family gesture, passed upward now—and turned to his students, who were attempting to teach the Blackfen children a clapping game that appeared to involve minor sorcery.

Outside, snow fell on the field, on the hatch with its oiled hinges, on the hall roof where the old chalkboard slept behind fresh paint. Inside, Felix opened the register to a fresh page and wrote, in ink, the date, and beneath it a single line that would be read aloud at every solstice hereafter, after the three hundred and forty-five names, after the silence:

*Let every name be spoken, and let every choice be honored, and let no door close on anyone without their own hand upon it.*

Then she blew out the lamp, and listened to the children laughing—one world's laughter, indistinguishable now from the other's—and went home through the falling snow, counting, as she always would, but counting blessings now, and finding the number good.
