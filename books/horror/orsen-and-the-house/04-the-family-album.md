## 4. The Family Album

The records room was on the second floor, at the end of a corridor that smelled of lemon polish and old paper, and Odile found it by accident, which was how he would later insist it happened.

He had been looking for the medication logs. That was the honest version, and he was a man who prized honesty the way other men prized their teeth — something you maintained daily and defended fiercely, because once you started losing them, everything else went soft. The charts for the east wing patients were supposed to be in the nurses' station. They were not. Mrs. Aldous had told him, in her flat, unhurried voice, that historical records were kept upstairs, "in the family's keeping," and that if he needed anything older than the current intake he should ask her first.

He hadn't asked her first. It was 2:40 in the afternoon, the house was quiet in the way large houses are quiet — not silent but holding its breath between sounds — and Eleanor Verrin was sleeping, her chart clipped to her door showing nothing new since morning. He had an hour. He took the stairs.

* * *

The records room had once been a library. You could tell by the ladder bolted to the shelving, by the brass rails where reading lamps had hung, by the ghost-rectangles of faded wallpaper where bookcases had stood for decades before being replaced with filing cabinets and document boxes. Someone had tried to make it administrative and failed; the room kept reverting to what it wanted to be. Dust hung in the light from two tall windows, turning slowly, like something deciding whether to settle.

He found the current patient files within ten minutes — thin folders, recent, unremarkable. Eleanor Verrin's contained her intake assessment, her medication schedule, her advance directive signed in a hand so steady it looked typeset. No history. No family medical background beyond a single typed line: *Cause of parental death: not applicable.*

Not applicable. Odile had read ten thousand of these forms. There was always something. Cardiac, cancer, accident, war, even — once, memorably — *killed by a horse*. Not applicable was not an answer. Not applicable was a door held shut.

Behind the filing cabinets, wedged against the wall where the last bookcase used to stand, there was a stack of photograph albums bound in green leather, their spines cracked to white. No labels. No dates. Just four of them, heavy as paving stones, arranged in a neat row as though someone had recently straightened them.

He should have asked Mrs. Aldous first.

He opened the first album anyway.

* * *

The earliest photographs were sepia, silvered at the edges, the kind that flake if you breathe on them wrong. A man and a woman standing before a tall mirror — he recognized it immediately, and his stomach registered the recognition before his mind did, a cold drop falling through him. The mirror in the east wing corridor. The one with the black frame carved with vines, the one he'd covered without being told to, the one whose reflection had raised its hand after his.

In the photograph the mirror was uncovered, and the family posed before it the way families posed before fireplaces or automobiles: as proof of possession. The man stood with his hand on the woman's shoulder. She sat. Behind them, half-cropped, a younger woman — a daughter, maybe — stood slightly apart, her face turned toward the glass instead of the camera.

Odile tilted the album toward the window light. The daughter's face had been scratched out. Not defaced casually — scratched out with intent, with something sharp dragged across the emulsion in tight, deliberate strokes until the features were gone and only a pale wound remained where a face had been.

He turned the page. Another portrait, a decade later, judging by the clothes. Same mirror. Same arrangement — the standing figure, the seated figure, someone at the edge turned toward the glass. A different generation; the man from the first photograph now sat, older, heavier, while a younger man stood behind him with a hand on his shoulder. And at the edge, cropped at the shoulder, a face scratched out.

Different face. Same scratch.

He kept turning. The albums ran forward through the century like tree rings. The clothing changed — sepia gave way to black-and-white, black-and-white to faded Kodachrome, the poses loosening slightly as the decades made formality unfashionable. But three things never changed. The mirror. The composition: one seated, one standing with a hand on the shoulder, one at the edge facing the glass. And the scratched-out face.

Always one per photograph. Never more. Never fewer.

Odile set the album down and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. There were explanations. Of course there were. Families damaged photographs all the time — divorces, feuds, disgraces, dead relatives too painful to look at. One scratched face meant nothing. Two meant a grudge. Four albums' worth meant a tradition, and traditions in old families were usually just cruelty that had been given enough time to feel like heritage.

But it wasn't the scratching that bothered him. It was the position. In every single image, across ninety years, the excised person stood at the edge of the frame, turned toward the mirror rather than the lens. Whoever they were, whatever they'd done to earn their erasure, they had been erased *in relation to the glass*. The camera wasn't the point. The camera was incidental. The photograph was documentation of something else entirely, and the scratched-out figure was the part of the record that had to go.

He thought about the counting. Three voices, descending in unison, precise as machinery. He thought about the mirror raising its hand a full second after his own. He thought: *these are not related things.* He thought it firmly, the way you press down on a lid.

Then he opened the second album.

* * *

Halfway through it, he found the 1968 photograph, though he didn't know the year yet. He knew it later, when he found the newspaper clipping tucked into the back cover, but in the moment it was just another portrait: a broad-shouldered man seated, a thin nervous woman standing beside him with her hand on his shoulder, and at the edge of the frame, half-turned toward the mirror, a girl of perhaps sixteen whose face had been scratched away with the same tight, furious strokes.

Except this time the scratches were shallower. Recent-feeling. As if the hand that made them had hesitated.

And this time, Odile noticed something he'd missed in the others. In the mirror's reflection — small, silvered, easy to overlook — the figures did not quite match the figures before it. The seated man's reflected head was bowed slightly lower than his actual head. The standing woman's reflected hand gripped the shoulder more tightly than the real one did. And the girl at the edge: in the room, she faced the mirror. In the glass, she faced the camera.

He sat very still for a long moment, listening to the house breathe around him.

Photographs didn't do that. Photographs captured one instant, one truth, however ugly. Unless the photograph had been altered — unless someone had gone into the negative, into the print, and *changed what the mirror saw*, which would require a skill, a patience, and a purpose so strange that Odile's professional vocabulary had no drawer to file it in.

Or unless the mirror had already been lagging in 1968. Or leading. Or simply seeing something the room didn't contain.

He looked at the girl's scratched-out face for a long time. Sixteen, maybe seventeen. Thin wrists. A cardigan buttoned wrong — one button off, the kind of mistake a grieving or frightened person makes. He caught himself constructing her: name, illness, the argument that got her scraped out of the family record. He caught himself caring, and the caring felt like stepping off a marked path into soft ground.

*Stop,* he told himself. *You're here for vitals and med schedules.*

He turned to the clipping folded into the back cover. It was yellowed, brittle, from a regional paper. The headline read: **VERRIN HEIRESS RECOVERING AFTER LONG ILLNESS; FAMILY "GRATEFUL BEYOND WORDS."** Below it, a photograph of the same broad-shouldered man and thin woman, smiling on the steps of Verrin House, and between them, radiant and convalescent, the girl — unscratched, alive, her face unmistakable even at newsprint resolution.

Eleanor Verrin. Nineteen years old, according to the caption. Recovered from an illness the article described, in the evasive language of 1968, as "a prolonged decline that physicians had pronounced irreversible."

Pronounced irreversible. Then recovered. Then photographed, every decade afterward, before the same mirror, in the same pose — Odile flipped forward through the third and fourth albums and found her each time, aging in ten-year increments, always seated now, always with a younger relative standing at her shoulder, always with someone else at the edge of the frame, turned toward the glass, face scratched away.

One per photograph. One per generation. One life, traded in, erased from the record, positioned forever facing the thing that had taken them.

His hands had gone cold. He noticed this clinically, the way he noted a patient's mottled skin, and the clinicality itself frightened him — how easily the habit of detachment reasserted itself, how comfortable it was, how much it resembled the house.

*You're building a story out of nothing,* he said inwardly. *Old families are cruel. Old photographs are damaged. Mirrors in old houses are warped.*

Warped mirrors didn't lag. But he wasn't ready to think about that yet, not in daylight, not alone with four albums and a hundred years of evidence arranged as neatly as a treatment plan.

* * *

It was the fourth album's final pages that undid him.

The most recent photographs were color, glossy, casual — the family had stopped posing formally sometime in the nineties, and the compositions relaxed. But not entirely. Even in a snapshot from what looked like a birthday dinner, the seating arranged itself: one at the head, one standing behind, and someone at the edge, angled toward the hallway where the east wing mirror hung. In that snapshot the edge-figure's face was intact — a young man, laughing — but someone had drawn a single line through his throat in ballpoint pen. Just one line. Like a signature. Like a tally mark.

And beneath the snapshot, glued to the page, a strip of paper with handwriting on it. Small, cramped, urgent:

*We keep the count so it doesn't have to. One per turn. Never volunteer. Never refuse. — E.V., 1998*

E.V. Eleanor Verrin. Who lay downstairs right now, ninety-four years old, counting backwards with two strangers in perfect unison, intending — she'd said it herself, that first night, in a voice like dry paper — *to die owing nothing.*

Owing. The word snagged in him like a fishhook. Owing implied a lender. A lender implied terms. Terms implied a contract, and contracts required signatures, and somewhere in this house — in this room, probably, in the boxes he hadn't opened yet — there might be a document a century old with a clause in it that explained why dying patients counted backwards toward zero like debt clocks winding down.

Odile closed the album. His fingers left prints in the dust of its cover, and he found himself, absurdly, wiping them away — as though the house would notice, as though tidiness mattered, as though he were already negotiating with something for the right to have touched its records.

That was the feeling he couldn't shake as he reshelved the albums exactly as he'd found them, squared their spines, stepped back from the shelf. Not fear, exactly. Fear would come later, at 3:07 in the morning, wearing his own voice. This was worse and quieter: the sense that by opening the albums he had joined a procession. That every person who ever learned the shape of this house's secret became, in that instant, part of its architecture — a load-bearing wall of silence. That the scratched-out faces weren't warnings to stay away.

They were instructions for what happened to those who looked too closely.

He paused at the door, hand on the frame, and looked back at the row of green albums. In the dusty light they seemed less like books than like beds — four of them, occupied, waiting.

Downstairs, faintly, through floors and walls, he heard it begin again: three voices, descending together, steady as a metronome.

Ten. Nine. Eight.

He stood in the doorway and counted along without meaning to, and hated himself for knowing the numbers, and knew — with the certainty of a man reading his own chart — that tomorrow night he would come back up these stairs, and open the boxes he hadn't opened yet, and look for the contract.

Because curiosity, he understood now, was not the opposite of complicity.

Curiosity was how complicity introduced itself.
