## 22. Invitation Open

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to Marisol Vega, and would mean everything to her by Thursday.

It came the way all good news comes to people who have stopped expecting it: thin, official, slightly too late. The envelope was heavy cream stock, the kind of paper that costs more per sheet than Marisol made in ten minutes at the care facility where she'd worked for six years, and the letterhead read *Harrowgate Estate, Office of the Estate Manager*. Inside was a contract of employment so generous she read it three times, certain she'd misread a decimal. Private hospice care. Live-in. Six-month term, renewable. A salary that would clear her mother's medical debt in four months and leave enough over to finally fix the roof.

There was no interview. There was a phone call, brief and pleasant, from a woman with a calm, unhurried voice who introduced herself only as the estate manager and asked questions that were not quite the questions Marisol expected. Not *Are you experienced with end-of-life care* but *Are you comfortable working nights alone.* Not *Can you lift a patient* but *Do you consider yourself a observant person, Miss Vega? Do you notice small things?*

Marisol said yes. She was proud of noticing small things. It was, she would later think, the truest thing she said in the entire conversation, and the woman on the other end of the line seemed pleased by it in a way that sat wrong in her chest for hours afterward, like a stone swallowed whole.

The contract was signed on Wednesday. She was to report Thursday evening.

* * *

Harrowgate Estate stood two hours north of the city, at the end of a road that gave up on asphalt a mile before it gave up on her. The house rose out of late-afternoon fog the way large old houses do in stories Marisol had read as a girl: gradually, then all at once, three stories of gray stone and dark windows, its wings reaching out like something settling in for a long sleep. Ivy climbed the eastern face. The gardens had been beautiful once, and had been permitted, with great dignity, to stop trying.

There was one patient. That was the first odd thing, though Marisol filed it under *eccentric wealth* rather than *odd*. Mr. Aldous Wren, aged ninety-one, the last of his line, dying by slow degrees of something the admitting paperwork described with a vagueness she'd never encountered in fourteen years of nursing paperwork. *Decline, general.* As if the man were not dying of anything in particular but simply of the house, or of time, or of some arrangement.

He was asleep when she arrived, and the night nurse — a tired-eyed man named Okafor who was leaving, who seemed almost to be fleeing — walked her through the handoff in the kitchen, because the house's formal rooms, he said, made him uneasy to speak in.

"Vitals are stable. He sleeps most of the time. When he wakes, he'll ask you what time it is. Tell him the truth. He doesn't like being lied to about the time." Okafor rubbed his face. "He counts, sometimes. Downward. Don't join in. Don't stop him, either. Just — let him count."

"Count down to what?" Marisol asked, and heard herself, and almost laughed, because the question sounded like the first line of a ghost story, and she had read enough of those as a girl to know better than to ask it sincerely.

Okafor did not laugh. That was the second odd thing.

"Miss Vega." He paused at the kitchen door, bag over his shoulder, already half-turned toward the fog and the long road out. "There's a list. Laminated. It's on the inside of the pantry door. The previous staff made it, or — someone made it. Read it. It's not a joke. Nobody wrote it as a joke."

"Who wrote it?"

"That's the thing," Okafor said, and his voice dropped as though the walls had ears with opinions, "nobody remembers. It's been on that door longer than anyone's been employed here. Longer than the current estate manager, she says, and she's been here thirty years." He opened the door, and the fog swallowed him, and Marisol stood alone in the enormous kitchen of Harrowgate Estate with her suitcase and her generous contract and the sound of her own breathing.

* * *

The list was exactly where he said it would be.

It was laminated in heavy plastic, yellowed at the edges, and the handwriting — because it was handwritten, carefully, in a slanting cursive that belonged to no decade in particular — had been photocopied and re-laminated so many times that the letters had gone slightly soft, like something seen through water. Marisol read it standing in the cold light of the open pantry, and the kitchen seemed to get quieter as she read, the way a room does when it is listening.

*RULES OF THE HOUSE.*

*1. Cover the mirrors before midnight. Uncover them after sunrise. Do not look at a covered mirror as though you expect it to answer.*

*2. If the intercom rings, do not answer. The intercom has been disconnected since 1987. Consider what that means.*

*3. Do not enter the east wing.*

*4. If the patient counts, do not count with him. Do not count against him. His count is his own.*

*5. You will notice the mirrors are slow. This is not a defect. Do not attempt to repair it. Do not attempt to time it.*

*6. If you see a face in a mirror that you do not recognize, do not assume it does not recognize you.*

*7. Nothing in this house can take what is not offered. Remember this. Remember it especially when you are afraid, because fear makes people generous.*

*8. Leave before you stop believing the list.*

Marisol read it twice. Then she laughed — one short, sharp, involuntary syllable — because the alternative to laughing was putting the list down and walking out the door and two hours down a road that gave up on asphalt, and she had a mother with medical debt and a roof that leaked and a contract with a salary that felt, in retrospect, less like generosity than like bait.

She made tea. She checked on Mr. Wren, who slept with his hands folded on his chest like a man posing for his own portrait. She unpacked her things in the staff room, which was clean and comfortable and had a window that looked out on the fog. And at 11:40 p.m., twenty minutes before the deadline on a laminated list she had already decided was the eccentric artifact of some long-gone housekeeper with a sense of humor, she found herself standing in the upstairs hallway with a bedsheet over her arm, telling herself she was doing it as a joke, as an anthropology exercise, as a lark.

There were eleven mirrors in the parts of the house she'd seen. She covered them one by one. The last one was in the main hallway, a tall gilt-framed thing that had clearly hung there for a century or more, and as she reached up with the sheet, she caught her own reflection in it and stopped.

Not because the reflection was wrong. Because it was *late*.

She raised her hand. The reflection raised its hand — after. Not much after. A fraction. The space of a blink, the space between a lightning strike and its thunder, except she had seen the lightning and the thunder both, and they had not been the same event.

Marisol Vega stood in the hallway of Harrowgate Estate at 11:52 p.m. and held very still, and her reflection held very still, and the gap between them did not close. She counted — one-Mississippi, two-Mississippi — and the reflection counted with her, one second behind, faithful as a shadow, patient as a debt.

*This is not a defect,* the list had said. *Do not attempt to time it.*

She covered the mirror. She stood for a moment with her palm flat against the sheet, feeling the cold glass beneath it, and she thought about the phone call, and the questions that were not questions, *do you notice small things, Miss Vega*, and she understood, in the wordless way one understands a fall already in progress, that she had been noticed noticing.

She did not leave. That was the third odd thing, and the one she would think about most in the days to come: that she stood in that hallway with her car keys in her pocket and a road outside the window and she did not leave. The salary was part of it. The mother was part of it. But underneath both, honest as a heartbeat, was something she would only later have the language for, something she had read once, months before, in an anonymous account published on a nursing forum and dismissed by everyone in her cohort as an elaborate piece of creepypasta — a long, precise, clinical document about a place called Verrin House, written by a man who signed himself only *O.*, and whose final lines she had remembered without trying to:

*The house does not hunt. It waits to be invited. We are all, every one of us, holding the door open with our curiosity.*

She had read it at 2 a.m. on a night shift, alone, and had felt the prickle of it, and had told herself it was fiction, because it was better written than fiction needed to be and worse documented than fact should be, and because believing it would have meant something she wasn't ready to believe.

Now she stood in front of a covered mirror in a different old house, in a different fog, and the prickle came back, and with it came the memory of the article's last paragraph, the one everyone had mocked: *My reflection is one second late now. Everywhere. It has been eleven weeks. I have stopped checking whether it is getting worse. Some fees you pay by installments.*

"Mr. O," she said aloud, to the sheet, to the glass beneath it, to whoever or whatever might be keeping accounts in the walls. "If that was real — I'm sorry. I should have believed you."

The house said nothing. The house was very good at saying nothing. But somewhere below her, down through the floors, in the east wing whose door she had not yet found and already did not want to, something adjusted itself the way a sleeper adjusts, turning toward warmth.

* * *

Mr. Wren woke at 3:07 a.m.

Marisol knew the time precisely because she was looking at her watch when the sound started — a thin, dry crackle from the intercom unit in the patient's room, a unit she had personally confirmed was disconnected, its wires cut cleanly and long ago, its speaker dark with age. The crackle resolved into a voice. It was her voice. It said, politely, the way one speaks through a door to a stranger: *"Hello? I'm sorry to trouble you. Could you let me in?"*

She did not answer.

The voice waited. The voice was patient. Then it said, in her voice, with her exact intonation, the words she had spoken that afternoon in the kitchen to a fleeing man: *"Count down to what?"*

Marisol stood very still beside the bed. Old Mr. Wren slept on, hands folded, face peaceful. And she understood rule two — *consider what that means* — and she considered it, and what it meant was that whatever used the intercom did not need the intercom, had never needed the intercom, that the machine was a courtesy, an offered door, and that the thing in this house was scrupulous, punctilious, almost courtly about doors. It would not come in uninvited. It only ever asked. The asking was the whole of its power and the whole of its hunger, and every person who had ever answered — with a word, with a look, with a hand raised to a lagging mirror to see how late it was — had signed something.

The intercom went silent.

The doorknob of Mr. Wren's room turned, slowly, all the way, and stopped, and did not open, and Marisol stood in the dark with her heart going like a trapped bird and did not scream, because screaming felt, suddenly and terribly, like answering.

At 3:15, the old man opened his eyes.

They were clear eyes, unclouded by the general decline that was killing him, and they found her in the dark as though the dark were a thing he could see through. He looked at her for a long moment with an expression she would spend the rest of the night trying to name and would name, at dawn, as *pity*.

"You're the new one," he said. His voice was dry as paper. "They always send them young now."

"Mr. Wren, you should rest—"

"I will rest," he said, with the terrible mildness of the dying. "That's the one thing I'm certain of. I've made certain of it." He turned his head toward the window, toward the fog. "Did you read the list?"

"Yes."

"Did you believe it?"

Marisol thought of the mirror, one second late. Thought of her own voice on a dead machine. Thought of a stranger's article about a house called Verrin, and a man named O., and a debt paid by installments.

"No," she said. "But I'm going to act like I do."

Something moved in the old man's face — not a smile, but the place where a smile had been excavated long ago. "That's what she said," he murmured. "The Verrin woman. Eleanor. My grandmother's cousin, or some such. The one who died owing nothing." His eyes closed. "They wrote about her. In the account. She held out. Held the line. And the line held for a while, you know. For a while, after her, nothing was invited anywhere." His breath rattled like a key in an old lock. "But houses are patient, nurse. Houses outlast resolutions. And there is always another letter. There is always another Tuesday."

"Mr. Wren," Marisol said carefully, "what did your family invite in?"

The old man was quiet for so long she thought he'd gone back to sleep. Then, without opening his eyes, he began to count.

"Ten," he said. "Nine."

His voice was soft, and steady, and perfectly alone, and Marisol stood beside him in the dark and did not join in and did not stop him, and outside the window the fog pressed against the glass like a face that had all the time in the world.

"Eight," said Mr. Wren. "Seven."

And in the covered mirror in the hallway, beneath the sheet, one second behind the world and patient as a debt, something waited to be noticed.

"Six," said the old man. "Five."

The house listened. The house had always listened. That was the whole of it, the whole terrible engine of it: a thing made of silence, fed by silence, sustained by every person who saw and said nothing — and somewhere in the fog, in another county, in another decade, another envelope of heavy cream paper was already sealed, already addressed, already traveling toward someone with debts of their own and a gift for noticing small things.

"Four," said Mr. Wren.

The cycle asked again.

"Three."

And somewhere, a mirror ran one second slow.
