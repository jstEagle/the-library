## 2. Eleven Desks, Ten Students

*September 9th*

The trouble with writing things down, I've always believed, is that it works. Ink is a kind of promise. You put a fact on paper and the paper holds it the way a jar holds a moth—visible, contained, unable to pretend it was never there. I have kept journals since I was nine years old, since the year my father left and my mother started sleeping with the radio on, because somewhere in that gray season I understood that memory was a leaky vessel and I needed something with a tighter seam. Forty-one notebooks since then. They sit in boxes in my sister's garage in Sacramento like the sediment of a life, and if you drilled through them you would find every day of me, layered and pressurized, real.

So when I took the attendance ledger out of the bottom drawer of my desk in Room 4 of Orsen Consolidated School, I did what I always do. I opened it. I wrote the date at the top of the page in my careful hand. And I began to count.

Ten students. Ten names.

Eleven desks.

I had noticed them on my first morning, of course—the way you notice a missing tooth in a smile—but I had assumed the discrepancy was bureaucratic, some clerical ghost from the district office. A desk ordered by mistake. A class list that never got updated. Towns like Orsen are full of such small administrative fossils; I'd taught in three dying districts before this one and each had its own sediment. But on Monday I asked Vice Principal Hargrove about it, casually, the way you ask about anything in this town—lightly, so lightly, as if the question were made of soap bubbles—and his face did something I have been turning over ever since. It didn't close. Closing would have been human. It *stilled*, the way water stills when something passes beneath it, and he said, "Old furniture. We'll get it out of there," and changed the subject to the boiler, which apparently has opinions.

The desk was not removed. It sits in the back row, third from the window, and it is not empty the way the others are empty. The other desks hold the residue of their students: pencil shavings, a fossilized eraser, initials gouged into soft wood by some bored predecessor. The eleventh desk holds nothing. Its surface is pale and unwritten, as if it has been waiting to be used rather than abandoned, and twice now I have found it turned slightly toward the front of the room, though no one sits in it and no one admits to moving it.

Today Marisol told me whose it is.

She stayed after the bell, the way she does—not lingering exactly, but not leaving, folding and refolding the strap of her backpack while the other children clattered out into a hallway that smells of floor wax and old rain. Marisol Reyes is ten years old and has the particular gravity of children who have learned early that adults will tell you things if you stand still long enough. She watched me erase the board for a long time before she spoke.

"You shouldn't count eleven," she said.

I set down the eraser. "What do you mean?"

"The desks." Her eyes went to the back row, third from the window, and came back to me with an effort, the way a swimmer's eyes come back from deep water. "You counted them out loud yesterday. When you were doing attendance. You said, 'and one more.'" She hugged the backpack strap against her chest. "You're not supposed to count her."

"Her," I said.

Marisol's mouth worked. I could see her deciding, weighing whatever she'd been told against whatever she'd seen, and I want to record here that I did not press her. I have learned that pressing a child is like pressing a bruise—you get a color, but you lose the shape underneath. So I waited, and finally she said, very quietly, in the tone of someone reciting a rule learned from a grandmother:

"She belongs to the girl who waits."

The radiator ticked. Outside the window, the playground stood in the flat gold light of late afternoon, swings hanging dead straight, and beyond it the fence, and beyond the fence the field where the county road crew has been digging all week, their machines silent now, parked like sleeping animals.

"Who waits?" I asked. "Marisol, who waits where?"

But she was already backing toward the door, shaking her head, and what she said last I have written down exactly, because exactness is the only mercy I know how to offer:

"Not where. *For.*"

Then she was gone, and I stood alone in Room 4 with twenty-two eyes' worth of chalk dust settling around me and one desk turned toward the front of the room like a held breath.

I am not a fanciful woman. I want that on the record too. I believe in thermometers and timetables and the periodic table. I believe that most hauntings are just history that nobody bothered to file. But tonight I keep thinking about the way Marisol said *her*—not afraid, exactly. Practiced. The way you say a thing you have been told not to say so many times that the not-saying has worn a groove, and the words slide along the groove anyway.

*September 10th*

Tonight I am afraid of my own handwriting.

I need to start further back or I'll lose the thread. This morning went normally, or as normally as anything goes in Orsen, where normal is a coat everyone wears buttoned wrong. Ten students present, ten names marked. I called the roll the way I always do, surname first, and I did not say "and one more," and I did not look at the eleventh desk, and I felt the whole room feel me not looking at it, which is a sentence I would once have crossed out and now cannot.

After school I brought the ledger home. This is standard practice—I reconcile my marks against the master roster, check for discrepancies, keep my records clean. My apartment above the hardware store on Vane Street is small and cold and smells of the feed store next door, and I sat at the kitchen table under the lamp with the ledger open and a cup of tea going bitter beside me, and I turned to today's page.

And there were eleven marks.

I counted them four times. Eleven checkmarks in the column beside eleven names, and the tenth name down the page—one I did not write, in handwriting that is mine, that is unmistakably, humiliatingly mine, every loop and ligature of it—reads:

**Kettle, Ada — present**

I did not write it. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left tonight. I called ten names. I marked ten names. I remember the specific texture of the morning, the squeak of Dale Ostrander's crew outside the window, Tommy Aldous asking to sharpen his pencil twice, the light coming through the third window from the left. At no point did any child answer to the name Kettle. At no point did my pen touch the page except ten times.

And yet there it is. Present. Marked in ink the same blue-black as my own, dried to the same age, sitting on the page as if it had been there since morning, since always, since the book was bound.

Here is the part I almost didn't write down, and I am writing it anyway, because if I stop trusting the record then I stop existing, and I refuse.

When I saw the name, I did what anyone would do. I checked the master roster in the front of the book, expecting a clerical error, a leftover from some previous teacher's term. The roster lists ten students. There is no Ada Kettle. There is no Kettle at all among the current families, though the name is everywhere else in this town—Kettle Road, the Kettle Building, RUTH KETTLE, CHAIR, on the school board letterhead. The name is a watermark on the whole town. But no Ada.

So I did the second thing anyone would do. I erased nothing—ink doesn't erase, that's the covenant of ink—and instead I drew a single line through the entry, the standard correction, and initialed it, and wrote in the margin: *entered in error, JV.*

The line went through the name.

The name did not go away.

I don't mean that mystically. I mean that when I looked again—seconds later, the pen still uncapped—the entry read *present* in unmarked ink, the line gone from it, my marginal note gone, the page smooth and white as a lie. Only my ten true marks remained untouched, flanked by an eleventh that had digested my correction like a body digesting a splinter.

I closed the book. I am not ashamed to record that I poured two fingers of the bourbon I bought for emergencies and drank it standing up, looking out the window at Vane Street, where the streetlights came on one by one down the block like a row of struck matches.

Then—because I am who I am, because the notebook is the only prayer I know—I opened the book again.

The entry is still there. **Kettle, Ada — present.** Patient as a stone in a streambed. And beneath it, in the margin, where my own correction note used to be, new writing in my own hand, in ink I did not spill:

*She waits to be counted.*

I have not slept. I have the lamp on and the ledger open on the table across the room, where I can see it, the way you watch a dog that has done something it should not know how to do. Every rational explanation arrives on schedule and dies on schedule: stress, fatigue, some trick of the light, a student's prank with a fountain pen and a steady hand. I catalogued them all, gave each its hearing. None of them survive cross-examination. The handwriting is mine down to the way I cross my sevens.

Which leaves the unbearable alternative: that the record is not failing me. That the record is *ahead* of me. That ink, which I trusted to hold things the way a jar holds a moth, has instead become a door, and something on the other side of it has learned my hand.

Tomorrow I will ask Ruth Kettle, chair of the school board, whether there was ever a student named Ada Kettle in this district. I will ask lightly. I have learned how to ask lightly in this town.

But tonight, one more thing, and then I will close this notebook and sleep with the light on like a child, and be no more ashamed of it than of the bourbon.

At half past midnight, from the schoolyard across the field, I heard singing. Faint, thin, carried on the wind off the hills—a child's voice, or several, in a melody I almost recognized, the way you almost recognize a face across a crowded station. It rose and stopped. Rose and stopped. Like a verse being practiced. Like a roll call being answered.

Like something counting itself, patiently, waiting for someone to finish the tally.

In the morning, they break ground on the new road behind the school. I intend to be there with my notebook.

Someone has to keep the record honest.
