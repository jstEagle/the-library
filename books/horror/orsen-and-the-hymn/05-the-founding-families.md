## 5. The Founding Families

The house on Kettle Hill had been built to be looked at, and it had not been looked at in a long time. Its paint had gone the gray of old bone, its porch boards sighed under Juniper's feet, and the windows—tall, once-proud, curtained in yellowed lace—gave back her reflection like water giving back a stone. She had come without an appointment. She had told herself it was because appointments could be refused, but the truth was simpler and less flattering: she did not trust this town to answer questions honestly if given time to compose itself.

Emmett Kettle was ninety-one years old and answered the door himself, which surprised her. He was a tall ruin of a man, stooped at the shoulders like something that had been leaning into wind for decades, with a face so seamed it looked plowed. His eyes were pale, almost colorless, and they went to the folder under Juniper's arm before they went to her face.

"You're the new teacher," he said. Not a question.

"Juniper Villalobos."

"I know who you are." He stood there a moment, one hand on the doorframe, and Juniper watched him decide something. It happened in his jaw first—a small tightening, like a man biting down on a word—and then he stepped back from the threshold. "Come in before the neighbors see you standing there. They'll talk. Talking's all we've got left."

* * *

The parlor smelled of dust and camphor and, beneath both, faintly of smoke—the way a room can hold a smell for fifty years if no one opens the windows. There was a fireplace that hadn't been lit since, Juniper guessed, another lifetime. On the mantel stood photographs in silver frames, all of them turned face-down except one: a young man in a millworker's cap, squinting into sun.

Emmett lowered himself into an armchair with the care of a man handling something breakable, which perhaps he was. "You've been asking about 1974," he said. "At the diner. At the county office. At the school." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "Ruth called me last night. Told me to expect you."

"Then you know what I want."

"I know what you *think* you want." He folded his hands over the head of his cane. "Everybody wants the story. Nobody wants what comes with it."

"Try me."

He looked at her for a long time. Outside, a crow worked at something in the yard with a patience that felt rehearsed. When he finally spoke, his voice had changed—flatter, older, as though he'd reached down inside himself for a voice he'd buried with everything else.

"The mill burned on the fourteenth of October, 1974. A Tuesday. Cold enough that morning that the pond had skin of ice on it, and my father—God rest him—had the furnaces stoked high because we were rushing a lumber order for the county. Eleven children used to play in the drying sheds after school. Their mothers worked the sorting floor; there wasn't anywhere else for them to go. Everyone knew it. No one stopped it." His thumb moved along the cane's head, back and forth, a small compulsive motion. "That day, someone locked the shed doors from the outside. Chained them. The fire started in the kiln wing, and by the time anyone thought of the children—" He stopped. Started again. "By then it didn't matter what anyone thought."

"Who chained the doors?"

The crow outside screamed once and went silent, as if scolded.

"That," Emmett said, "is the question this town has spent fifty years not asking."

* * *

He told it in pieces, the way a man gives up a confession when he can only bear it in pieces, and Juniper wrote it down afterward in the car with shaking hands, transcribing from memory while the light died:

*The fire was not an accident. My father knew it within the hour. The chains on the shed doors were ours—mill chains, stamped KETTLE LUMBER CO. Someone had taken them from the toolhouse and someone had locked them, and my father knew whose hands fit those padlocks because he'd hired those hands.*

*Eleven children. Ada Kettle—my brother's girl, eight years old. The Ostrander boy. The Pruitt twins. Seven others whose names I will not say aloud in this house, because the walls here are thin and some things listen.*

*My father went to the other founding families that same night. The Harmons. The Vances. The Creels. And Ruth's people, the Kettles of the east branch—we are all one tree, rotten at the root. They met in the grange hall with the lamps out, and what they decided there has never been written down until now.*

*There was a man who came to Orsen that winter. No one knew where from. He wore a good coat and spoke softly and he told them there was a way to keep the town quiet—to keep the grief from spreading like the fire should have spread, to keep the names off the papers and the questions off the law's tongue. He asked for one thing in payment. He would not say what. He only said: someday, someone will knock, and you will answer.*

*They signed. All of them signed. My father signed first, God forgive him, and the others followed because following is what cowards do best. And in the morning the fire marshal's report said accidental, electrical, eleven souls unaccounted for, presumed departed prior to ignition. Presumed departed. As if children could walk out of a locked shed and vanish into the cold like breath.*

*We buried the cellar door beneath the schoolyard because the man in the good coat told us to. He said the children needed a classroom. Those were his exact words. I was nineteen years old and I helped carry the shovels, and I have not slept a whole night through since.*

*We traded justice for quiet. That was the bargain. That was always the bargain. And every family that signed put their name in a book, and the book went down below with the children, and I have sometimes wondered—in the bad hours, the three-o'clock hours—whether the book signs us.*

* * *

When Juniper finished writing, she sat in the idling car in front of the Kettle house and read it back to herself twice. The streetlights had come on. Somewhere down the block a dog was barking—not angrily, but in the rhythmic, reporting way of a dog announcing that something was passing through town that shouldn't be.

Her phone buzzed. Marisol's mother.

*Marisol won't say why. She packed a bag Monday night. She keeps asking me if the hymn has an ending. I don't know what hymn. Mrs. Villalobos, do you know what hymn?*

Juniper stared at the words until they blurred. Then she typed: *I'm going to find out. Keep her home. Lock your doors tonight.* It sounded insane even as she sent it. Everything true sounded insane now.

She looked up at the Kettle house one more time. In an upstairs window, behind the yellowed lace, a light was burning that had not been burning when she went in—or perhaps it had, and she simply hadn't been looking. That was the trouble with Orsen. You couldn't tell anymore what you'd failed to see and what had only just arrived.

Emmett had said one more thing at the door, when she was leaving, so quietly she almost missed it. He had gripped her wrist with a strength that belied his years and leaned close, and his breath had smelled of camphor and old smoke, and he had said:

"The man in the good coat told my father the debt would come due when someone finally kept an honest record. Fifty years we kept false books, teacher. False books, false roll call, false graves. And now here you come with your little ledger full of truth." His pale eyes had been terrible then, wet and bright. "You haven't brought questions to this town. You've brought the due date."

Juniper drove home past the school, because she could not help herself, because documentation was the only prayer she knew. The building stood dark against the stars. And in the lowest window of the east wing—the window that looked down toward the buried cellar door—there was a light.

It was warm. It was patient. It was the color of a lamp left on for someone expected home.

And beneath it, faintly, carried on the cold air through her cracked car window, she heard singing. Children's voices. Eleven of them, she was somehow certain, though she could not have counted. Singing the hymn she'd found folded in the 1974 class photo—singing it straight through the place where the page ended, into verses that had never been written down.

Until, she understood now, someone wrote them.
