## 1. Dead Air

The thing nobody tells you about the graveyard shift is that it isn't a job so much as a vigil. You sit in a cinderblock room on the edge of town with a microphone and a coffee machine that sounds like a dying animal, and you keep the lights on for people who can't sleep. That's the whole gig. KRVL 104.7, "The Voice of the Valley," broadcasting to maybe four hundred insomniacs, two truckers, and whatever else is out there listening in the dark.

I've done this show for six years. Midnight to five, Sunday through Thursday. I call it *The Long Quiet*, which my program director thinks is pretentious and my listeners think is accurate. The format is simple: I play music that doesn't demand anything of you, I read the weather, and between songs I take calls from anyone who needs to hear a human voice say something back to them.

The rule—the only real rule—is that I log every call. Name, time, topic, one line of summary. It started as station policy, some liability nonsense from before my time, but somewhere along the way it became something else for me. A ritual. You write things down, they stay where you put them. They don't follow you home. My grandmother Odile kept ledgers her whole life—household accounts, garden yields, birthdays—and she used to say that a thing written down is a thing you've made peace with. I inherited the habit along with her handwriting, more or less. Slanted, impatient, pressing too hard.

So: the notebook. A black Moleskine, because I'm exactly the kind of man who owns a black Moleskine, filled now with six years of strangers' insomnia. Divorces announced at 2 a.m. Confessions about dead dogs and dead marriages. One memorable gentleman who called every Tuesday for a year to describe, in forensic detail, his hatred of his neighbor's wind chimes. I logged them all. Callers came and went; the notebook stayed. On bad nights—and there had been bad nights, though nothing like what was coming—I would flip back through those pages and remind myself that the world was mostly just people, awake too late, wanting to be heard.

That was the arrangement, right up until the third of October, when a woman named Colette Marchand called in to thank me for playing her favorite song.

* * *

Let me set the scene properly, because scene-setting is half my trade and because I want to be exact. Exactness matters when your sanity is on the line. 1:47 a.m. Rain coming down in that sideways October way that makes the studio windows sound like they're being sanded. I'd just finished a block of slow stuff—some Chet Baker, because I was feeling sorry for myself over a rejected manuscript and Chet Baker is the patron saint of feeling sorry for yourself—and the board lit up.

"KRVL, you're live with Xanthe. Who's this?"

"Colette," she said. "Colette Marchand. Out past Harrow Creek."

Her voice was warm. That's the word I keep reaching for, warm, like a kitchen in winter, like tea someone else made for you. Older woman, seventies maybe, the kind of voice that has spent decades being pleasant on purpose and has worn smooth all its rough places.

"Well, Colette, glad you called. What's keeping you up?"

"Oh, nothing keeps me up anymore," she said, and laughed—a small, delighted laugh, genuinely delighted, the laugh of someone sharing a private joke with herself. "I sleep wonderfully these days. I just wanted to tell you that song you played, the trumpet one? That was Henri's favorite. He used to hum it off-key in the truck. Forty years of marriage and he never once got the melody right."

"That's love," I said, doing the thing I do, the warm-voiced thing, the radio-host thing. "Getting the song wrong together."

"You always know what to say." And here she did something callers don't usually do: she said my name. Not the name I go by on air—I'm just "Xanthe from KRVL," no surname, no biography—but my actual name, the full one, the one on my birth certificate and my grandmother's ledger. "Xanthe Joubert. Your grandmother must be very proud of you."

The rain filled the pause. I want to be clear that I didn't freeze because of anything supernatural. I froze because it was 1:47 in the morning and a stranger had just said my name, my whole name, on an open line, and there is a perfectly ordinary kind of fear that lives in that.

"How do you—" I started.

"I knew your grandmother," Colette said. "Everyone knows Odile. She's a fixture. Like the church steeple, or the flood marker." Another little laugh. "You have her hands, I bet. The Joubert hands. Long fingers. Good for writing things down."

I looked at my hands on the mixing board. Long fingers. Pressing too hard.

"Colette, I have to ask you to—"

"Play another one for Henri," she said. "Something slow. He liked slow things. He was never in a hurry, even at the end." A pause, and when she spoke again her warmth had not changed by a single degree, which was somehow the worst part. "He wasn't in a hurry even when the truck went off the bridge at Miller's Bend. The coroner said he probably didn't feel a thing. Isn't that nice? Isn't that a mercy?"

I remember looking down at the board, at the caller line blinking its steady green, and thinking, with the strange administrative calm that descends on me in crises, *she's telling me how her husband died.* Grief does that. People call grief hotlines at 3 a.m. and narrate their worst moments in voices of terrible serenity. This was a radio show, not a hotline, but the principle held. People need to say the unsayable to someone whose job is to receive it.

"Henri passed?" I said gently. "I'm so sorry, Colette. How long ago?"

"Three days," she said. "You should read the obituary. They wrote a lovely one. My daughter wrote it. She cried the whole time, poor thing, but she got all the dates right." Then, brightly, the way you'd ask about the weather: "Did you get all my dates right?"

"I'm sorry?"

"In your book. The black one. Do you have my dates right?"

I am going to be honest here, because honesty is the only currency this account has: I looked at the notebook. Reflex. Six years of ritual doesn't ask permission. I flipped to tonight's page, pen already moving, and wrote *Colette M., Harrow Creek, 1:47 a.m., widowed, requests slow song* — and then my hand stopped, because the pen had written something else first, something I hadn't decided to write, in my own slanted handwriting:

*Colette Marchand, d. Sept 30.*

September thirtieth. Three days ago.

* * *

There's a phenomenon in broadcasting called dead air. It's the cardinal sin. Silence on the frequency, the signal going out empty, and listeners experience it as something close to panic—they can't see you, they can't see the studio, all they know is that the voice that was keeping them company has stopped, and the dark rushes into the gap. Program directors train you against it. Fill the silence. Always fill the silence.

I gave Colette Marchand eleven seconds of dead air.

In those eleven seconds several things happened, none of them visible from outside. I noticed that the green light on her caller line had not flickered once during the entire conversation—not with her breath, not with her laughter, the way lines do when someone is actually speaking through an actual telephone. I noticed that the rain against the window seemed to have arranged itself into a rhythm suspiciously close to Henri's off-key humming. And I noticed that my hand, resting on the open notebook, was completely steady, which frightened me more than shaking would have. Some part of me had already accepted what was happening and was simply waiting for the rest of me to catch up.

"Colette," I said finally, and I'll give myself credit: my voice came out level. Radio training. You learn to broadcast through anything—fires, funerals, the morning after your fiancée leaves. "Your obituary ran three days ago. In the *Valley Herald*. Page four. I read it during my lunch break, because I read the paper like a man twice my age." A breath. "It says you died on September thirtieth. It says the service was Tuesday."

"Yes," she agreed pleasantly.

"It says you were survived by your daughter and two grandchildren."

"That's my girl. She always was thorough."

"Colette. Who is this?"

And here is the moment I keep returning to, the moment I've written down in four different notebooks since, trying to pin it like a moth, and it comes out different every time, so let me try again: she laughed. Warmly. Gratefully. As if I had asked her the most charming question in the world.

"Why, it's Colette," she said. "You logged me yourself. First of the month, last year—you wrote *Colette M., Harrow Creek, 2:12 a.m., lonely since Henri's stroke, played her Patsy Cline.* Don't you remember? You have such lovely handwriting, Xanthe. Your grandmother taught you well."

I pulled the old notebook off the shelf. Volume four, October of last year. There it was, in my slanted, pressing-too-hard hand: *Colette M., Harrow Creek, 2:12 a.m., lonely since Henri's stroke, played her Patsy Cline.* Word for word. Except—

Except I hadn't written the date of death. Last year's entry didn't have it. Tonight's entry did, and I hadn't chosen to write it, and both facts sat side by side in my skull like two photographs of the same room with one extra chair in the second frame, though I wouldn't understand that comparison for another week, God help me.

"Play something for Henri," Colette said again, softer now. Almost tender. "And then write me down properly. We do so appreciate being counted."

The line went dead. Not static—dead. The particular clean silence of a connection severed, followed by the dial tone of a number no longer in service, except there had been no number. The caller ID display, which had shown HARROW CREEK EXCH for the duration of the call, now showed nothing at all. Blank. As if the call had been placed from nowhere, or from everywhere, or from a place the phone company doesn't run lines to.

* * *

Here's what I did next, and I stand by it: I played Patsy Cline. "She's Got You." Because a woman had asked, and because the alternative was sitting in the silence with what I knew, and because filling the air is the whole job, and because—if I'm being fully honest, which I have promised myself I will be—some part of me believed that if I behaved normally, if I performed the ritual correctly, the night would resolve itself back into the ordinary world where the dead stay dead and the phones behave.

I talked over the intro. Something easy, something warm: "That one goes out to Henri, wherever he's driving tonight. Slow down at Miller's Bend, friend." And then I sat with my notebook and I did the thing that keeps me sane, the thing my grandmother taught me without ever teaching me: I wrote it all down. Every detail. The unflickering light. The blank caller ID. The entry I hadn't meant to write. The phrase *we appreciate being counted*, underlined twice, because underlining is how you tell the page you're scared.

Then I did something I'd never done in six years of the show. I turned to the front matter of the notebook—the blank pages at the start, the ones I'd always left empty—and I began a second list, beneath a heading I printed in careful capitals so it couldn't be mistaken for anything else:

**CALLERS WHO ARE DEAD.**

Underneath: *Colette Marchand, d. Sept 30. Called Oct 3, 1:47 a.m. Sounded happy. Knew my name. Knew my grandmother. Knew my handwriting.*

I stared at that heading for a long time. It looked insane on the page. It looked like the opening page of a case file on myself. But it also looked true, and truth was the only standard I had left at two in the morning, and besides—writing things down is the only ritual that keeps me sane, and sanity, I was beginning to suspect, was about to become a group project.

Because here's the thing I haven't said yet. Here's the thing I told myself was coincidence, the way you tell yourself the noise in the walls is just pipes.

This wasn't the first strange call. It was only the first one I checked.

Two weeks earlier, a man named Aldous Petrie had called to complain about the wind chimes—no, wrong neighbor, wrong grievance—to request a hymn for his mother. I'd logged him and forgotten him, the way you forget most kindnesses that pass through your hands. But after Colette hung up, I went back through volume six, and then volume five, checking names against the obituary pages I'd started hoarding in the studio drawer like a magpie building a nest out of other people's endings. Aldous Petrie, d. August 14. Called August 19. A woman named Ruthie Vance, d. July 2, called July 6, wanted to apologize to her sister. A teenager, Danny Fournier, d. June 11, called June 15, just wanted to hear his favorite song one more time, and I'd played it, and he'd said *thanks, man,* and hung up, and I had thought nothing of it, nothing at all, because the dead are supposed to be quiet and I had no reason to audit my callers for signs of life.

Seven of them. Seven calls in four months. All within days of death. All warm, all grateful, all *wrong* in a way I could now see had been there the whole time, the way you can see a watermark once someone shows you where to hold the page up to the light.

And one more thing, the last thing, the thing that made me put the cap on the pen and press both palms flat against the desk until the tremor stopped.

They had all, every one of them, at some point in their calls, mentioned my grandmother. Ruthie Vance remembered buying eggs from her. Danny Fournier said she'd waved at him from her garden the week before he crashed his bike. Aldous Petrie had gone to school with her. Small mentions, throwaway mentions, the kind of thing small towns run on. But seven for seven is not a pattern you argue with. Seven for seven is a thread, and threads lead somewhere, and I had a sick, rising certainty—as precise and unwelcome as the date appearing in my own handwriting—that the somewhere they led was the Joubert property, and the something waiting there was older than any of us, and patient, and counting.

At 4:58 a.m. I read the weather—rain ending by noon, frost advisory overnight—and signed off the way I always do: "This has been the Long Quiet. Keep the lights on. I'll see you tomorrow night." Then I killed the mic, packed the notebook, and stood in the parking lot under a sky just beginning to consider gray, breathing the cold October air and rehearsing the sentence I'd been composing all night.

*I need to go out to Grandma's house.*

Not because I wanted to. Because the alternative—staying away, pretending the thread pointed nowhere—was no longer available to me. Somewhere out past Harrow Creek, past Miller's Bend, behind my grandmother's collapsing greenhouse, something had started saying my family's name into telephones, and it had gotten my handwriting exactly right, and I needed to see, with my own eyes, what else around that house had been keeping count.

I got in the car. The engine turned over on the first try, which felt, under the circumstances, like a courtesy extended by something that wanted me to arrive.

I drove toward Odile's house as the sun came up, and in the glovebox, riding along with the registration papers and a tire gauge, my notebook lay open to a fresh page, waiting to be written in.

I should have burned it. I know that now.

But documentation is a kind of prayer, and I hadn't yet learned what happens when the thing you're praying to starts answering.
