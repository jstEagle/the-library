## 2. The Estate Inventory

My grandmother's house smelled the way it always had—cedar and old paper and something faintly mineral underneath, like the air after a hard rain on limestone. I'd been smelling that mineral note my whole life and never once thought to ask what it was. That's the thing about family houses. They teach you not to ask.

I'd taken the day off from KRVL, which felt like taking a day off from breathing. The overnight shift gets into you. You start organizing your life around 3 a.m. the way other people organize theirs around lunch. But Marguerite had called twice, and when Marguerite calls twice, she means it.

"The lawyer needs an inventory," she said. "Not a cleaning out. An inventory. Everything cataloged, everything photographed, before we touch anything."

"Since when do estate lawyers care about the coffee mugs?"

"Since Grandma Odile's lawyer is also the town's lawyer and has nothing else to do but be thorough." A pause. "Also since I can't get down there until Friday, and someone has to start."

So there I was, on a gray Tuesday in October, standing in the front hall of the house where I'd spent every summer of my childhood, holding a legal pad and a pen and feeling like a burglar with excellent manners.

The house sat at the end of a gravel drive off Route 9, past the orchard that hadn't produced anything worth picking in thirty years but which Odile Joubert refused to cut down. "Trees remember being useful," she told me once, when I was maybe ten and asked why we kept hauling buckets around for apples full of worms. At the time I thought it was just one of her sayings, the kind of thing old people keep in a jar and hand out like butterscotch. Now, standing in her hall with a legal pad, I found myself writing *orchard — 40 trees, non-producing* and then stopping, because it occurred to me that I had no idea whether trees remembered things or not, and I didn't love finding out that the question scared me.

* * *

I started upstairs because starting downstairs felt too much like starting at the beginning, and I wasn't ready for the beginning. The beginning was the parlor, where her chair still sat by the window, and where she herself had sat until three weeks ago, when the hospital took her and the house went quiet in a way I could feel through my shoes.

Odile wasn't dead. Let me be clear about that, because the inventory forms kept wanting me to write *estate of*, and I kept not writing it. She was at Meadowbrook Care Facility, forty minutes east, fading by degrees they measured in words per sentence. Last visit, she'd managed eleven words total, and six of them were my name. But she was alive, and the house knew it. Houses like this one know the difference. There's a way a place holds itself when its person is coming back, even if the person doesn't know it yet.

Upstairs: three bedrooms, one bath, one linen closet that could have housed a small family. I worked room by room, the way the lawyer's letter instructed. *Bedroom, northeast: bedframe (iron), mattress, dresser (walnut), mirror attached, clothing, assorted.* My handwriting got smaller as I went. There's something about listing a life that makes you want to use less of the page, as if economy were respect.

In the northeast bedroom—the one Odile had slept in for sixty years—I found the photographs.

They were in the bottom dresser drawer, under a layer of tissue paper, in a shoebox that had once held boots and now held the entire visual record of our family. I sat down on the floor, which is what that drawer demands of you, and went through them.

Here is what I noticed, and I want to be precise about this because precision is the only tool I've ever trusted: I noticed nothing. Not then. I cataloged the box—*photographs, assorted, approx. 200, various decades*—and put it in the keep pile, and moved on to the bathroom, where I inventoried forty-one years of accumulated pill bottles and a bar of soap so old it had achieved a kind of geological permanence.

It was only later, downstairs, with the light going amber and the mineral smell getting stronger, that the photographs started doing their work in the back of my mind. Like a song you can't place. Like a caller whose voice you almost recognize.

But I'm ahead of myself. First, the ledger.

* * *

I found it in the study, which is what we called the small room off the kitchen even though nobody in the family had ever studied anything in it. It was more of a keeping-room. A place where papers went to be official. The desk in there was Odile's mother's, and before that, presumably, somebody else's, because in our family furniture doesn't get bought so much as inherited, like debt.

The ledger was in the second drawer, under a folder of utility bills going back to 1987. It was bound in green cloth that had gone gray at the corners, and it was heavy—not dictionary-heavy, but heavier than a notebook has any right to be, as if the pages were dense with something besides paper.

I opened it expecting recipes. Grandmothers keep ledgers of recipes. Canning yields. Egg counts. The domestic arithmetic of surviving.

What I got instead was names.

The first page was dated 1891. The handwriting was copperplate, formal, the kind of script they taught you in school when school believed your descendants might read your homework. One name per line. A date beside each name. And beside each date—

I turned back to the first page and made myself look properly.

*Henrietta Joubert. March 3, 1891.* Crossed out. The line through the name was single, clean, ruled as if drawn against an invisible straightedge.

*Émile Joubert. June 19, 1894.* Crossed out.

*Marguerite Joubert—no, wait. Marguerite Joubert the elder, 1897.* Crossed out.

Page after page. Names in, dates beside them, and every single name struck through. Some entries were years apart. Some clustered close together, two or three in a season, the ink changing hands across generations—you could see the copperplate give way to a looser hand, then a ballpoint, then Odile's own narrow, upright print, which I recognized from forty birthday cards.

Six generations. I counted. Six generations of Jouberts, entered into this book like assets, and every last one of them crossed out.

And here is the detail that took me from unease into something colder, something with structure to it, something that would keep me up past my shift if I let it: the crossing-out dates matched the entry dates' format exactly. Same ink style. Same pressure. As if each name had been written once, at the moment of some registration, and crossed out later—but by the same hand, or the same *thing*, using the same pen. Not scratched out in anger. Not blotted in grief. *Closed out*. The way you close an account.

I sat in my grandmother's keeping-room with the window going dark behind me and did the math I didn't want to do. Every crossed-out name belonged to a dead Joubert. I knew some of the names from headstones in the churchyard up the road. Great-aunts. The uncle nobody talked about—Armand, 1953, crossed out the same year he supposedly ran off to California, except his mother had reportedly never accepted so much as a postcard.

I flipped toward the back, where the handwriting became Odile's entirely, and found the living.

*Marguerite Joubert. April 12, 1988.* Uncrossed. My sister, entered at birth like everyone else, her line waiting there clean and open as a held breath.

*Xanthe Joubert. September 30, 1985.* Uncrossed.

*Sylvie Joubert-Mercier. January 2, 2017.* Uncrossed. Sylvie was seven years old and her name was already in the book, written in my grandmother's careful print, dated the week she was born.

And at the bottom of the last used page, in fresher ink, slightly larger, as though the writer had wanted to make sure:

*Odile Joubert. May 14, 1940.*

Uncrossed. The only uncrossed name among the elders. The last resident, still open, still counting.

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough that the study went fully dark and I was reading by the hallway light. Long enough that the mineral smell seemed to come up through the floorboards specifically to keep me company.

There was one more thing. Pressed between the last page and the back cover, a loose sheet, older than the rest, soft as cloth at the folds. On it, in faded ink, was a drawing—a rough plan of the property, the house, the drive, the orchard. And behind the orchard, where I knew there stood nothing but the collapsed greenhouse and a stretch of bramble nobody cleared because nobody liked the way it grew back, a small circle. A well, marked with a symbol I didn't recognize: a vertical line through a circle, like a coin skewered on a nail.

Beneath it, in that same copperplate from 1891, four words:

*It counts what stays.*

I put the sheet back exactly where I'd found it. Then I picked up my legal pad, walked into the kitchen, and turned on every light in the house, all of them, including the one over the porch that Odile only ever switched on for company.

Then I did what I always do. What keeps me sane. What I tell my listeners to do when the night gets long and the thoughts get loud.

I wrote it down.

*Ledger, green cloth binding, six generations of Joubert names. All deceased entries crossed out. Living entries: M., X., S., O. Loose map sheet re: well behind greenhouse. Note: investigate greenhouse property line Saturday, daylight hours, with phone charged and car facing the road.*

Reading it back helped, the way it always helps. Ink makes things finite. A fact on paper stays on paper.

Except that as I capped my pen, sitting alone in a lit kitchen at the end of a gravel drive, I heard—in the walls, or under them, or in that mineral smell that had been in this house my whole life without my ever asking what it was—a sound I can only describe as patient. Not a knock. Not a voice. Just the sense of something below the floor noting, with satisfaction, that I had stayed long enough to find the book.

That I was, in fact, staying the night.

I checked the guest room for chairs before I slept. There were two. I counted them twice.

Both times, two.
