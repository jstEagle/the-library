## 10. Voices in Rotation

There's a rule in radio that nobody teaches you because nobody has to: the audience can hear your fear through the fader. Not the words you say — those are easy to fake — but the space between them. The breath you take half a beat too early. The way your voice sits a little higher in its register, like a man standing on his toes at the edge of something.

By 2:40 a.m., I was standing on my toes.

The first three hours of the show had gone the way they always went, which is to say the way they had gone for eleven years and then, abruptly, for the last two weeks, stopped going. Callers with warm voices and wrong details. A man who'd drowned in the reservoir calling to say the water was lovely this time of year. A woman who'd died of something the coroner's report called "natural causes, age 84" telling me her hydrangeas needed cutting back before frost, would I remind her son, he never remembers. I logged them all. Names, times, obituary cross-references where I could find them. My notebook had become a kind of cemetery with better organization than most.

I told myself it was still fine. That was the trick of routine — it doesn't just comfort you, it anesthetizes you. You can stand in a burning house as long as you keep doing the dishes.

Then the board lit up, line four, and the caller ID read nothing at all. Not blocked. Not unknown. Just a blank field, like the phone company itself had looked away.

"This is KRVL, you're on with Xanthe. Who am I talking to?"

"Xanthe Joubert." The voice was old and dry and patient, the way a rope is patient. "You know who this is."

I did not know who this was, and I knew exactly who this was, and both of those things were true at once, which is a sensation I'm still working on describing without drinking about it.

"Caller, I'll need a name for the log."

"You've been keeping a log." It wasn't a question. It was approval, and approval from that voice felt like a hand on the back of my neck. "Good boy. Your great-grandfather kept one too. Armand. He wrote smaller than you. Tighter hand. But you have his habit of underlining the dates."

My pen was in my hand. I looked down and saw that I had, without deciding to, underlined today's date twice.

"Let's talk about the family," the voice said.

"Let's not," I said, and reached for the dump button, and found that my finger wouldn't move. Not frozen — worse. Uninterested. My hand lay on the console like it belonged to someone whose job it was to sit there, and I was only visiting.

"Armand Joubert," the voice said, "born 1871, died 1911, crossed out. Henri Joubert, born 1894, died 1938, crossed out. Céleste Joubert, born 1920—"

"Stop."

"—died 1969, crossed out. You see how neat it is? One name a generation, sometimes two if the Well was hungry. Your grandmother keeps the ledger now. Odile, born 1947, *not yet crossed out.*"

The word *yet* sat in the studio with me like a third person. I could hear it breathing.

"Caller, we're going to go to a break—"

"There are no breaks," the voice said, almost kindly. "You know that. You've known that since the woman with the hydrangeas. Dead people don't call radio stations, Xanthe. They don't need to. The station is ours. It has been ours since your family paid for it. What do you think 'KRVL' stands for?"

I had asked that question my first week, eleven years ago, and been told it was the founder's initials. K. R. V. L. I had never learned what the letters were of. I had laughed about it on air, made it a bit. *Mystery letters, folks, like the well on my grandmother's property — deep, dark, and nobody wants to be the one to check.*

I wasn't laughing now.

"Kerveil," the voice said, pronouncing it the old way, the way Odile said it in her sleep sometimes when I'd stayed over as a boy, the word rising out of her like a bubble from deep water. "It means the counting place. You work in the counting place, Xanthe. You always have. We simply hadn't introduced ourselves."

* * *

Here's what I should have done: pulled the master fader, killed the transmitter, walked out into the parking lot, and kept walking until the town stopped being shaped like a question I didn't want answered.

Here's what I actually did: I picked up my notebook, turned to a fresh page, and wrote *Line 4, 2:47 a.m., no ID, male?, elderly?, claims knowledge of ledger.* Because documentation is the only ritual I have, and rituals don't care whether they're serving you or feeding you. That distinction, I was beginning to understand, is a luxury of people who aren't being counted.

"Marguerite Joubert," the voice continued, serene as a hymn. "Born 1986. Sister. Mother of Sylvie Joubert, born 2018."

"Don't," I said, and this time my voice came out with the fear in it, raw and unproduced, and some distant professional part of me noted that the listeners would hear it, all of them, the living and otherwise.

"She draws pictures," the voice said. "Little houses. Little trees. And lately, a chair. She's very gifted with the chair. Do you know why she's gifted with the chair, Xanthe?"

"I'm hanging up now." I said it to myself as much as to him. My hand still wouldn't move toward the button. It rested on the notebook instead, holding my place, like a good little archivist.

"Because children see the count before adults do. Children haven't learned to look away. Sylvie drew you sitting in it last Tuesday. Did she show Marguerite? No. She hid it in her backpack, because even at seven she understood it was a secret, and secrets in this family are inherited property."

The on-air light burned red. Somewhere out in the dark, insomniacs and truckers and night nurses were listening to a dead man recite my family tree, and I was letting him, because the alternative was admitting that the off switch had never really been mine.

"Odile signed in 1969," the voice said. "Her mother signed in 1938. Before that, 1911. Each signature buys twenty, thirty years of safety. Safe and solvent. That was the phrase. Your family loves that phrase. Say it with me sometime. But every contract ends, Xanthe, and every contract names its next party. The papers are coming. The lawyer is already printing them. And when Odile signs the transfer, the count moves to the new resident of record."

"And if she doesn't sign?" I asked. I hadn't meant to ask. The question came up out of me like the bubble out of Odile's sleeping mouth.

A pause. The first pause the voice had taken. In it I heard something underneath the dryness — a sound like wind moving across the mouth of a very deep hole, or like a crowd inhaling in a room with no room in it.

"Then the Well collects early," it said. "It has been patient for fifty-six years. Patience is not the same as mercy. Ask the uncle."

*I don't have an uncle,* I started to say, and then remembered: there was a gap in the ledger, a name inked but never crossed out, a date left blank. Odile had covered it with her thumb whenever I'd looked at the page as a child. Théodore. Théodore Joubert, who according to the family story "went to California" in 1971 and was never mentioned again, whose absence everyone maintained so smoothly and for so long that eventually it stopped being a lie and became furniture.

"He didn't go to California," the voice said, reading my silence like a caller ID. "He asked questions. He went down to the greenhouse with a crowbar. We keep his chair warm."

* * *

At 3:15, the voice ended the call the way callers do — except it didn't hang up so much as stop being anywhere, the line going flat in a way that dial tone doesn't achieve, a deadness deeper than electronic. I sat in the quiet for a full minute, staring at the ON AIR light, doing the math on my own career.

Then line two lit up. Then five. Then one, three, six — the whole board, every line at once, which in eleven years had happened exactly never, because KRVL's overnight audience peaked at maybe four hundred souls and most of them were asleep by three.

I took a breath and opened line one, because if the house is burning you might as well answer the door.

"KRVL, Xanthe speaking."

"Xanthe." A woman's voice, warm, grateful, wrong — the hydrangea woman again. "We wanted to do something nice for you. You've been such a good host. Such a careful boy with your little book."

"We," I said.

"All of us," she said, and behind her, faintly, other voices agreed — a murmur like a congregation settling into pews, dozens of them, the whole rotation of the dead who had called my show these past weeks, and maybe more than the weeks, maybe decades of them, everyone who had ever died inside the radius of that ledger, queued up in the dark with their voices polished and ready.

"It's a recital," she said. "For the family. Families love recitals."

And they began.

Not shouting. Nothing so crude. They spoke in turn, unhurried, each name handed to the next like a candle passed down a line: Armand. Henri. Céleste. Théodore — and at Théodore the murmur dipped, respectful, the way you lower your voice at a fresh grave. Then forward again, generation by generation, cousins and spouses and infants who'd lived nine days in 1954, a litany so complete that I understood, with a lurch, that they knew the tree better than I did, better than Odile did, that somewhere below the greenhouse the entire genealogy was filed and current.

I wrote the names as they came. Of course I wrote them. My hand moved on its own, steady, neat, underlining the dates I knew and leaving blanks where I didn't, and some part of me stood outside myself watching this and thought: *you are taking dictation for the thing that intends to eat your family, and your handwriting has never been better.*

They finished the ancestors and paused, and the pause had a shape to it, a held breath, an orchestra waiting on the conductor.

Then, in unison — forty voices, a hundred, soft as snowfall:

"Sylvie. Joubert."

The studio hummed. The light stayed red. Somewhere in the building a fluorescent tube buzzed through its death rattle.

"She'll be seven in March," the hydrangea woman added, gentle as a nurse. "She likes drawing. She likes chairs. She's going to fit *beautifully.*"

"We're so pleased," someone else said.

"So pleased," said another.

"So pleased," said the congregation, and the sound of their pleasure was the worst thing I had ever heard, because it was sincere. Whatever lived beneath my grandmother's property had waited three generations, and it was happy, and its happiness had my niece's name on it.

The lines went dead all at once. Real deadness this time — dial tone, ordinary, indifferent. The red light clicked off. The automation cued the next track, some soft-rock relic about the morning sun, and the station carried on being a radio station as though nothing had happened, which is the most horrifying feature of any machine: it does not know what it has been used for.

* * *

I drove home at 5 a.m. with the windows down even though it was October, because I wanted the cold to prove something to me — that the world still had weather in it, indifference, anything other than intention. The frost was out on the fields. It proved nothing. Frost is just weather doing its job; it doesn't care who's counting.

Marguerite's car was in my driveway. At five in the morning. Her lights were on.

I sat in the truck for a moment and thought about what I was about to walk into: my sister, awake at dawn, unannounced, with a seven-year-old who liked to draw chairs. I thought about the phrase *safe and solvent*, and about Théodore's blank date, and about a voice saying *we keep his chair warm.*

I thought about lying to her. I want to be honest in this account, wherever it ends up, whoever finds it — I genuinely considered it. Telling her nothing. Letting her believe the world was large and mostly empty, the way I'd believed right up until a dead woman complimented my logging habits.

But she was standing on my porch when I got out of the truck, arms crossed against the cold, and before I could manufacture a single plausible sentence she said:

"Xanthe, Sylvie woke up screaming at three o'clock. She said the radio was saying her name." Her breath fogged. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and terrified, and underneath all of it, the thing that undid me: she was looking at me the way you look at the one person who might already know. "And I need you to tell me — right now, before the sun comes up — why my seven-year-old daughter knows what our grandmother's well sounds like."

I stood on my porch steps with the frost coming down and the east going gray, and I understood that the rotation had ended, and the count had begun, and the only thing left between my family and that warm empty chair was whatever I could manage to say in the next ten seconds.

So I said the true thing. God help me, I said the true thing.

"Come inside," I said. "And bring me the drawing she hid in her backpack."
