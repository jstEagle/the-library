## 17. The Count Advances

I want it on the record that I did not believe my brother. Not at first, not at second, and honestly not even at third, when he stood in Odile's kitchen at two in the morning with a photograph album under his arm like a man presenting evidence at a trial nobody had scheduled.

"Count the chairs," he said.

"Xanthe."

"Count them, Marguerite."

There were four chairs in the 1987 Christmas photo. There were five in the one I'd taken of Sylvie on the porch in September. There were six in the picture I'd snapped of Mom's old bedroom just last week, for no reason I could articulate except that the light was nice. In every single frame, plain wooden chair, facing the camera, patient as a tax notice.

"That's a trick of the film," I said, which is what people say when they would rather be wrong than afraid.

"Digital," Xanthe said gently. "You took them on your phone."

So there went that theory, along with my dignity, somewhere around the time the hallway started being longer than the house.

* * *

Here is what I knew going into that week: my brother hosted a radio show for insomniacs and cranks; our grandmother was dying by inches in the back room; and my daughter had recently developed an artistic interest in furniture. That last one should have been the tell. Seven-year-olds draw suns with faces and dogs with six legs. They do not draw a wooden chair, rendered with unsettling architectural accuracy, occupied by a figure whose face is left blank — and then label it, in careful schoolroom letters, with their uncle's name.

When Sylvie brought that drawing home, I did what any reasonable mother does. I put it on the refrigerator. I told myself the teacher must have said the name out loud. I told myself a lot of things that week, the way you tell yourself the noise upstairs is just the house settling, right up until the house settles into a shape no architect signed off on.

The rearranging started small. The hallway to the bathroom, which I had walked ten thousand times, began requiring eleven steps instead of nine. Then fourteen. Then one night it required me to pass a door that had never been there before — a low door, stone-framed, set into the plaster like it had grown there, breathing a smell of wet rock and cold iron.

I did not open it. I want credit for that. I stood in front of it with my hand on the knob for what felt like a full minute of my finite life, and then I turned around and walked back to Sylvie's room and sat on the floor beside her bed until sunrise, because some doors you can feel through the wood, the way you can feel a storm coming through a window.

In the morning the door was gone. The wall was smooth. But the wallpaper smelled like well water, and so did I.

* * *

The second door opened onto the shaft itself.

I need to be precise about this, because precision is the only weapon I had left. It was Tuesday. Odile had slept through lunch, which frightened me more than anything supernatural, because her sleeping had lately taken on a quality I can only describe as *withdrawal* — as if she were not resting but paying something. I carried a cup of tea past the linen closet, and the linen closet was not a linen closet anymore. It was a doorway, and beyond the doorway was a circle of ancient stone, and beyond the stone was dark, and the dark went down the way grief goes down: further than you think, and then further than that.

And at the bottom — or not at the bottom, but *up*, rising toward me like a bubble through black water — came a sound. Not water. Not wind. A sound like a room full of people sitting very still, all of them waiting for their name to be called.

I dropped the tea. The mug shattered on stone floorboards that should have been pine. And the sound from below changed — it didn't rise or fall, it *attended*. The way a crowd attends when the bride appears. The way an audience attends when the lights go down.

I got Sylvie and we sat in the kitchen with every light on, and I called my brother, and for the first time since we were children I said the words *I believe you* without irony, without armor, without the little sisterly habit of making everything a joke so nothing can hurt me properly.

"Marguerite," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle like dry wood. "Pack one bag. Not two. One bag each. We're leaving tonight."

* * *

Packing one bag when your whole life is in a house is its own small horror. You learn what you actually value by what your hands refuse to leave behind. I took photographs — the real ones, the ones with four chairs — and Odile's ring off the bathroom shelf, and Sylvie's drawing, because it seemed suddenly important not to leave it in the house, as if the house could read.

Odile woke while I packed her bag. Her eyes were clearer than they'd been in months, and she looked at the suitcase, and then at me, and she smiled the way you smile at a child who has finally understood arithmetic.

"He told you," she said.

"Mom— Grandmother. We're leaving. All of us. Tonight."

"No," she said, mild as milk. "Not all of us." And she patted my hand and closed her eyes again, and I stood there holding a cardigan I'd folded and refolded four times, understanding that she had already decided something, and hating her a little for deciding it alone.

But that was a problem for after dawn. First: the car.

* * *

The car would not start.

I want to describe this correctly, because "the car wouldn't start" sounds mundane, and this was not mundane. I turned the key and the engine turned over — healthy, eager, the battery fine, the fuel gauge honest — and then it caught, ran for exactly three seconds, and died. Every time. Three seconds. Like something counting. Like something letting us hear how running sounded before it took the sound away.

Xanthe popped the hood and stood over the engine with a flashlight, and I watched my brother — a man who once drove across two states on a failing transmission rather than admit defeat — go very quiet.

"There's nothing wrong with it," he said.

"Then why won't it—"

"Because it's not about the car." He closed the hood softly, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "It's about the count. The count doesn't reset until dawn. Nothing leaves before dawn. The roads—" He stopped. He looked down the driveway, where the county road should have been visible between the fence posts, and wasn't. Where there should have been asphalt and a distant farmhouse light, there was hedge. Dense, green, October-wrong hedge, growing where a road had been since 1954.

We tried anyway. Of course we tried. Xanthe drove and I navigated and Sylvie sat between us with her stuffed rabbit, asking questions in that terrible guileless way children have — *why are the trees moving, Daddy, why do they keep being in front of us* — and the answer was that the trees kept being in front of us, that every turn away from the house curved politely back toward it, that the hedge parted once to let us see the porch light like a host showing guests to their seats.

We came back up the driveway at 11:40 p.m., and the car died its three-second death in the exact spot it had died before, and Sylvie said, "The chair knows we tried," and neither of us asked her what she meant, because we both already knew.

* * *

Inside, we fortified. I know how that sounds — two adults barricading against a house — but you do what the situation offers. We moved mattresses into the kitchen, the room farthest from the well-door, and we salted the thresholds because Odile, half-lucid, had murmured that salt was "for the bookkeeping," whatever that meant, and at this point I was taking spiritual advice from a ninety-year-old woman who talked to the walls, because the walls were talking back.

Sylvie slept between us. Around two, she sat straight up in the dark and said, "It's closer," and lay back down, and slept, and I lay awake listening to the house work.

That's the word. *Work.* The house worked all night. Floorboards shifting their opinions. Doors trying new hinges. Somewhere above us, the extra chair — Xanthe had found it in the parlor, plain wood, facing the camera, always facing whoever entered — scraped slowly across the floor, an inch at a time, all night long, like something dragging itself toward a destination it had all the patience in the world to reach.

At 3 a.m. it reached the top of the stairs.

I heard it stop there. I heard it *wait*. And I understood, lying in the dark with my daughter's breath against my neck, that the chair was not hunting. Chairs don't hunt. The chair was simply traveling to where it was needed next, and the horror was not its motion but its certainty — the absolute bureaucratic confidence of a thing that has never once been refused.

"It's at the stairs," I whispered to Xanthe.

"I know."

"What do we do?"

"We wait for dawn," he said. "And then we run. And if the roads open—" His voice did something complicated. "Marguerite, if the roads open, you take Sylvie and Grandma and you don't look back. Whatever happens with me. Whatever you hear. You drive."

"Don't say it like that."

"Somebody has to say it like that," he said, "or nobody says it at all, and then we all stand in the kitchen being brave at each other until the chair gets hungry."

I laughed. God help me, I laughed, because my brother has always been able to make me laugh at funerals, and because the alternative to laughing was screaming, and Sylvie was asleep.

* * *

Dawn came gray and reluctant, the way it does in late October, and with it — I swear this on everything — the hedge was gone. The road lay where roads lie, cracked and ordinary and beautiful. The car started on the first try, engine purring like nothing had ever been withheld from it.

The count resets at dawn. Xanthe had been right about that too, and I was learning that being right was costing him something I couldn't see yet.

We loaded Odile wrapped in blankets, Sylvie clutching the rabbit, one bag each, just as ordered. Xanthe stood on the porch with his hands in his pockets, and he did not get in the car.

"The lawyer comes today," he said. "The papers. If Grandma signs, the debt transfers to me, and the Well stops needing anyone else. Stops needing *her*. You take her to Aunt Renée's in Marrow Creek. You don't tell anyone where you've been. You don't come back."

"You're coming with us."

"Not yet." He smiled, and it was the smile he'd worn at our father's funeral, the one that means *I have already decided and I am sparing you the argument*. "Somebody has to intercept the paperwork."

"Xanthe—"

"Margo." He touched Sylvie's hair through the window. She looked up at him with those enormous unafraid eyes and said, "Bye, Uncle Xanthe. The chair says bye too."

He flinched. Only I saw it. Only I ever saw the flinches; that was the whole tragedy of my brother, that he did his bleeding privately, on schedule, like a man changing dressings.

"Drive," he said.

I drove. I watched him shrink in the mirror, standing on the porch of that house with its polite horrible walls, getting smaller and smaller until the hedge — no, until the *trees*, ordinary trees, ordinary morning — took him.

We made it eleven miles before Sylvie, from the back seat, said the thing that turned my blood to well water:

"Mommy? The chair isn't in the house anymore."

"Sylvie—"

"I can feel it walking." She hugged her rabbit and looked out the window at the passing fields, calm as Sunday. "It's going to find Uncle Xanthe first. But after that—" she frowned, working it out, the way she'd worked out the drawing, the name, all of it, "—after that it's going to need somebody else to sit in it. And I'm the smallest."

I pulled the car over. I held her until the shaking stopped, hers or mine, I genuinely couldn't tell you which. And then I made a decision that would have appalled every sensible person I know, and dialed my brother from the shoulder of Route 9 with my daughter watching the mirrors for a chair that walked.

He answered on the first ring, breathless, and before I could speak he said:

"Margo. The lawyer's here early. And he didn't come alone."

Behind his voice, faint and clear, through the phone, through everything, I heard it: the sound of many people sitting very still, waiting for a name to be called.

One of them cleared its throat.
