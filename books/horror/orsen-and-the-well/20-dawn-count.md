## 20. Dawn Count

I have never trusted my brother's voice on the radio.

That sounds crueler than I mean it. What I mean is: Xanthe has two voices, and I've known this since we were children sharing a bedroom wall. There was the voice he used for us—flat, dry, allergic to sincerity, the voice that called our grandmother's ancient dog "the upholstery" and got away with it because Odile laughed until she wheezed. And then there was the radio voice, warm as bathwater, the one that poured out of the kitchen transistor every night at midnight when he thought nobody was listening, telling strangers their griefs mattered. I used to stand in the hallway and feel like a burglar in my own house, eavesdropping on a brother who didn't exist.

For three weeks I lived inside his second voice. It told me to pack. It told me not to look at the chair. It told me, in the flat hours before dawn while Sylvie slept with her back against my spine like a comma against a sentence, that everything he'd said about the Well was true, and that the only way through was forward, and that if I ever doubted him I should count the chairs again.

I counted them eleven times. Eleven times there was one too many.

So no—I never trusted his voice. But at 5:47 in the morning, standing in Odile's kitchen with a suitcase in each hand and my daughter's shoes on the wrong feet, I finally understood what the second voice was for. It wasn't for strangers. It was a rehearsal. All those years of talking gently into the dark were practice for this: for saying goodbye to someone who is already halfway gone.

* * *

The house had stopped pretending by then.

I want to write that down plainly because later—when I am old, when Sylvie asks me what it was like and I am tempted to soften it into something survivable—I will need the plain version. The house had stopped pretending. Doors opened onto the well shaft. Not onto darkness, not symbolically, but onto an actual stone throat descending beneath the floorboards, breathing cold air that smelled of wet iron and old prayers. The hallway ran three feet longer than it should have. The extra chair sat in every room now, including the bathroom, which felt less like haunting and more like a landlord's pettiness.

And Odile was fading in a way that had nothing to do with her heart, though her heart was failing too, politely, in the background, like a musician waiting for the end of the song.

She'd refused to sign. That was Xanthe's doing, or his asking, anyway—he'd knelt beside her wheelchair on the worst night, when the walls were sweating cold, and asked her to refuse, and she'd looked at him with those milky eyes going clear for the first time in a year and said, "You always did ask for the expensive things, petit."

Refusal cost her something every night. I watched it happen. Her memories went first—the neighbors' names, then the neighbors themselves, then whole decades peeling off her like wallpaper in a flooded room. She forgot my grandfather. She forgot the name of the dog she'd loved more than the grandfather. She forgot Paris, which she'd talked about my entire childhood with the specificity of a woman who'd left her heart in the 6th arrondissement. Each morning she woke a little lighter, a little emptier, the Well collecting its interest on her defiance.

But she refused. Every time the lawyer came—and he came twice more, that last week, smiling his filing-cabinet smile, flanked by men whose faces I recognized from the obituaries Xanthe read aloud on air—she pressed her lips together and turned her head toward the window, toward the greenhouse ruins, toward the well she would not acknowledge even to curse.

"You can't take what isn't given," she told him once, in French, in a voice I hadn't heard since I was small. "And you can't wait forever. Even wells silt up."

The lawyer's smile didn't move. "We are very patient, madame."

"We," I repeated, after the door closed. "He said *we*."

"Count the chairs," Xanthe said, from the doorway, and I did, and there were four where three belonged, and none of us spoke for a long time.

* * *

The last night, none of us slept, so I can't tell you when the house changed. I can only tell you how we found it at dawn.

Xanthe had spent the night doing what he always did: writing. He sat at the kitchen table with Odile's ledger open beside his own notebooks, transcribing, cross-referencing, building some index of the whole rotten arrangement as though documentation could save anyone. Maybe it could. Maybe it already had—maybe the reason we were still alive at all was that someone, somewhere below us, was keeping records too, and found ours interesting. He wrote until his hand cramped and Odile dozed in her wheelchair with her mouth open, breathing in the shallow, whistling way that made me check on her every twenty minutes, and Sylvie slept on the couch with her drawing clutched to her chest—the chair, the figure in it, the name *Xanthe* printed in a seven-year-old's careful capitals.

At some point near five, Xanthe put down his pen and said, quietly, to no one: "It's quiet."

It was. That was the horror of it. For days the house had creaked and shifted and breathed around us, doors testing themselves, floorboards rearranging like a slow argument. Now nothing. The silence had texture, like held breath.

"The count resets at dawn," he said. "Odile told me. Whatever the Well takes overnight, it... reconciles. Balances its books." He almost smiled. My brother, who had made a career of talking to the dead, smiled the way people smile at funerals. "If the roads are going to open, they open now."

"And you?" I said, because I already knew, because Sylvie had drawn it, because the ledger had shown me his name uncrossed and I had pretended not to understand what that meant. "What about you?"

"I'm staying."

"Don't." The word came out of me before I could dress it up. "Don't do the noble thing, Xanthe, don't do the *radio* thing, don't narrate your own sacrifice in that warm voice, I swear to God—"

"Margo." He said it softly. He hadn't called me Margo since we were kids. "There's no trade left to make. I made it. Last night, at three a.m., live on air, in front of forty listeners and however many dead ones. I offered myself. My voice, my show, my name in the book. And they took it." He held up his hands, palms out, like a man showing he's unarmed. "Say something."

"Something," I whispered.

"I can't." His voice cracked on the second word—not the radio voice, not the flat voice, some third voice I'd never heard, young and terrified and seven years old again behind the bedroom wall. "Margo, I can't make sound come out right anymore. When I talk, it comes out *wrong*, it comes out in all their voices at once. I tried to wake Sylvie this morning and what came out of me was our great-grandmother. She's been dead sixty years and she used my mouth to say *good girl*. So yes. They took the trade. And the family walks free at dawn, and I stay, and that's the whole contract, and hating it doesn't void it. Believe me. I've read the terms."

Odile stirred in her wheelchair. Her eyes opened, and they were clear—terribly, wastefully clear, lucid at the exact hour when it hurt most to be.

"It's time," she said.

* * *

We loaded the car in silence. Not the house's held-breath silence—a human one, the kind that falls over a family at a hospital bedside, everyone performing calm so the dying person doesn't have to spend strength comforting them.

I packed Odile first. She weighed almost nothing. Sixty years ago she'd been the strongest woman in the parish; now I lifted her from the wheelchair like lifting a bundle of letters. She wrapped her arms around my neck and whispered into my hair, in French, the blessing she used to say over bread, except she'd changed one word. Instead of *this bread and this house*, she said *this child and this road*. I didn't cry. I want credit for that. I did not cry until later, and when I did, it was alone, in a car, at a legal speed, like a functioning adult.

Sylvie wouldn't leave the couch. She stood next to it in her wrong-footed shoes, holding the drawing, looking at her uncle with the grave, unblinking attention children reserve for animals and funerals.

"Are you going in the chair?" she asked.

"Sylvie—" Marguerite, that's me, reaching for her.

"It's okay." Xanthe crouched down to her level. Up close, in the gray pre-dawn light, I could see what the Well had already taken: the color of his eyes had gone strange, a deep water brown that hadn't been there in September. "Yes. I'm going to sit in the chair for a little while."

"Is it comfortable?"

He laughed—one real syllable of laughter, his own, snatched back from wherever they kept it. "You know what? Nobody's asked me that. Three weeks of ghosts and lawyers and haunted furniture, and you're the first person to ask if the chair is comfortable."

"I sit in it in my dreams," Sylvie said matter-of-factly. "It's hard. But it fits."

"That's because it was measured for family," Xanthe said, and kissed the top of her head, and stood up fast, the way you do when staying crouched means falling apart.

Then he hugged me. We are not a hugging family—we are a Joubert family, we communicate through sarcasm and casseroles—but he hugged me the way you hug someone when the hug has to last across a distance neither of you can name. He smelled like ink and cold stone. His heartbeat against my ear was steady, and I hated it, I wanted it to be racing, I wanted proof he was afraid, and instead it was calm, and I realized the calm was worse. Calm meant he'd finished grieving himself already. Calm meant the paperwork was done.

"Listen to the radio," he said into my hair. "Not tonight. In a few weeks. When the static clears."

"What are you going to do down there?"

"Same thing I always did." He pulled back and smiled, and for one second it was entirely his face, the face from the bedroom wall, the face from before. "Document. Somebody has to keep the log. Documentation is a kind of prayer, Margo. I just found out who I've been praying to."

* * *

The roads opened at 6:12 a.m.

I know the minute because I watched the dashboard clock the entire drive out, waiting for the loop to snap shut around us again, for the bend past the collapsed greenhouse to deliver us back to the front gate for the fourth time. It didn't. The gravel gave way to asphalt, the asphalt to the county road, the county road to the highway, and the sunrise came up over the fields the color of egg yolk and forgiveness, and no door in any house anywhere opened onto a well shaft.

In the rearview mirror, the property shrank. The house looked smaller than it had any right to—just a farmhouse, just a roof, just a place where a family had once been unlucky for six generations. Behind it, past the ruined greenhouse, I couldn't see the well cap. But I heard it.

That's the part I'll never manage to explain to anyone, the part that makes therapists go quiet and priests change the subject. From half a mile away, with the windows up and Sylvie humming in the back seat and Odile sleeping under a blanket in the passenger seat, I heard the cap close. A grinding of stone on stone, long and deliberate, like a vault door, like a period at the end of a very long sentence. And underneath it, fainter than the engine, fainter than memory:

Breathing.

His breathing. Steady, unhurried, the breath of a man settling into a chair that fits. It lasted exactly as long as it took the cap to seat itself, and then it didn't stop so much as become part of the ground, and the ground became part of the horizon, and I drove, because driving was the only prayer I knew how to say without crying.

Sylvie stopped humming.

"Mommy," she said. "Uncle Xanthe's not scared anymore."

"No, baby."

"He says the chair fits."

I gripped the wheel. The sun cleared the tree line and filled the car with ordinary gold light, the kind that falls on gas stations and school zones and every road in the world that goes somewhere.

"I know," I said. "He told me too."

* * *

We drove for six hours before Odile woke, and when she woke, she remembered everything—which meant the Well had released its claim, all of it, every memory it had been draining nightly poured back into her like water returning to a dried creek bed. She cried for her mother, dead fifty years. She cried for the uncle who vanished in '68. She cried for Paris. Then she ate an entire omelet at a diner outside Beaumont and complained about the coffee, and I sat across from her thinking: *this is what safe costs. This is what solvent buys.* A woman restored at the price of a man erased, and both transactions signed in the same family blood.

Sylvie drew pictures in the booth. Houses, trees, a sun with eyelashes. No chairs. I checked every page. No chairs.

That night, in a motel with a ice machine humming like a normal machine hums, I turned on the radio. KRVL wasn't reachable from there, obviously—it was a hundred-watt station broadcasting to a county we'd fled—but I tuned the dial anyway, scanning static, and somewhere around 1 a.m., between a country station and a Spanish-language gospel hour, I caught it.

Static. Then, beneath the static, a voice reading names.

Not the dead. Living names. Neighbors, schoolteachers, the pharmacist, the woman who ran the nursery—everyone the Well had ever counted, everyone it had released when my brother paid the balance. A roll call, gentle and unhurried, each name spoken like a debt forgiven. And under the names, so faint I might have invented it, something keeping time. Breathing. Or a pen moving across paper. Or both.

I listen every night now. Marguerite Joubert, widow of nothing, keeper of a vigil nobody assigned her. I keep the volume low so Odile won't hear, though I suspect she hears anyway, and prays along in French.

Because here is what I know, what I knew even before the static resolved into that familiar warmth, that bathwater voice, saying *good evening, listeners, let's begin the count*:

My brother kept meticulous logs because writing things down was the only ritual that kept him sane.

And now he keeps them for something else.

And every night, at the end of the roll call of the living, just before the static swallows the signal whole, the voice pauses—half a second, no more—and says, in a tone no one else would catch, in a flatness only family would recognize:

*"Goodnight, Margo."*
