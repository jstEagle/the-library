## 4. Dev Descends

Here is what you need to know about me: I have never once in my life been the responsible one.

Cyrus is the responsible one. Cyrus has laminated maps of places that don't officially exist. Cyrus carries three flashlights and a first aid kit and a laminated card — an actual laminated card — titled *Rules of Descent*, which she will hand to you like a priest handing you a hymnal if you so much as look at an abandoned building with interest. Rule one is *never go in alone*. Rule two is *never go in without telling someone where you're going*. Rule three, and I quote, because I have it memorized against my will: *the building decides when you leave, not you.*

I want to state for the record, in whatever court eventually hears this, that I broke all three rules in under four minutes, and that I did it because I thought I was helping her.

That's the thing about helping. It's the best excuse there is.

* * *

Cyrus had been at her grandmother's house for six days, and every time I called her she sounded like someone reading a letter she didn't like. Distant. Careful. Once, mid-sentence about probate paperwork, she stopped and said, "Dev, did you ever notice the chair?" and then said "never mind" before I could answer, which, if you know Cyrus, is like a lighthouse saying never mind.

She'd found the maps by then. She told me that much over the phone, in the flat tone she uses for bad news about buildings: hand-drawn tunnels under the house, on no survey, no utility record, no sewer plan, nothing. And names. Dates. A list of names going back decades, and I heard her not-say the word *missing* the way you hear a person not-say a word, which is louder than saying it.

"Send me the coordinates of the lot," I said. "I'll cross-reference."

"You will not," she said. "Dev. I know that tone. That's your *I'm going to do something stupid for science* tone."

"It's my *I'm going to help* tone."

"They're the same tone. That's the problem with you."

She was right. They are the same tone. I've never successfully told them apart myself.

So I did what any loyal friend does when a grieving woman asks him not to do something: I waited until she had a meeting with the estate lawyer, and I drove out to Maple Hollow with a headlamp, a GPS logger, a chalk marker, and the smug, humming confidence of a man who is about to be extremely useful.

The house sat back from the road the way old houses do, like it was leaning away from something. Even from the curb it had a look I can only describe as *attended*. Not empty. Attended. Curtains drawn with intent. Porch swept. A house with a job it was still going to, even with Edith gone.

The root cellar doors were around back, half-sunk into a grassy slope, two wooden doors painted a green so old it had gone gray, with a stone lintel over them. Above the lintel, someone had carved a small arch with a line through it. A door with a bar across it. I took a picture of it because I take pictures of everything, and because some part of me — the part I should listen to more often and have never once listened to — thought *that's a warning, that's the oldest kind of warning, that's a warning in the grammar people used before writing.*

I opened the doors anyway.

* * *

The first thing you need to understand about the descent is that it was *nice*.

That's the part I keep coming back to. It wasn't a horror-movie entrance. It was stone steps, dry and even, worn smooth in the middle by generations of feet, and it smelled like cold earth and apples, the ghost of a hundred years of storage. A root cellar should smell like that. A root cellar has earned that smell.

At the bottom, the cellar itself: empty shelves, a rusted canning press, a window at ground level letting in a gray coin of afternoon. And in the far wall, where there should have been nothing but dirt, an arched opening. Brick-lined. Neat. Deliberate.

Here is the thing that should have stopped me, and I want to be honest about this, because honesty is apparently the whole point of everything that happened after: the arch was *finished*. Nobody lines a tunnel mouth in brick and points the arch unless they mean to keep it open a long time. Tunnels like this — smugglers' passages, bootlegger runs, the fun stuff I usually find — are rough. Hurried. This was craftsmanship. Somebody had built this the way you build a church door.

I logged it. That's the ritual, right? GPS logger in my jacket pocket, running the whole time, dropping a breadcrumb every ten seconds. Timestamped. Unimpeachable. My little black box of *this happened, I was here, I left when I said I left.* I've used those logs to settle arguments, to prove to a property manager that I never entered a restricted area, to prove to *myself*, one bad night in a flooded storm drain, that I'd only been under for forty minutes and not the six hours my panic insisted on.

The log is the truth. That's what I told myself, standing at that brick arch with my headlamp on. Whatever's down here, the log will say.

I stepped through.

* * *

The passage went back farther than the lot.

I want to be precise about this, because it's the first wrong thing and the wrong things matter more than the bells, more than anything else. Cyrus's grandmother's lot is — I measured it later, from the county GIS, because I am exactly the kind of man who does that — sixty-two meters deep. The house sits at the front. The root cellar is at the back. From the arch, the maximum straight-line distance to the rear property line is maybe thirty meters.

I walked four hundred meters. I know because the logger said so. I counted my steps too, out of old habit — a hundred and sixty-two paces at my stride, which is 380-some meters, and then I stopped counting because the counting had stopped helping.

The tunnel didn't turn. That was the other thing. It ran straight, dead straight, longer than the lot, longer than the block, and the whole time I kept doing the mental gymnastics you do — *must curve, must be a seam, GPS drift under stone is notorious* — while some quieter voice in me said: *Dev. The lot ends. You have walked past the lot. You have walked past three lots. There is a hardware store above you now, and a road, and you are under them, and the tunnel does not care.*

The walls were stone, dry, fitted without mortar. And hung with bells.

Small ones. Hand-sized, mostly. Brass, iron, some clay that had gone the color of bone. They hung from iron pegs driven into the walls at intervals — every meter, maybe less — hundreds of them, thousands, receding down that impossible straight corridor into the dark like the world's saddest, most patient Christmas. Some were green with age. Some were bright. Some were bright and had no dust on them, which is a sentence I did not let myself finish thinking at the time.

I touched one. I want that in the record too, because Cyrus will ask, because she asks everything, and I will not lie to her again. I touched one. It didn't ring. It made a sound like a held breath — a *thud*, a felt sound, more in my teeth than my ears — and every other bell on the wall, as far as my light reached, leaned. Not swung. *Leaned.* Toward me. A fraction, like grass under wind, and then back.

I said, out loud, in my big brave voice, "Okay. Cool. Very cool."

The tunnel did not answer, but I had the distinct impression — and I know how this sounds, I've had years now to get used to how this sounds — that it had *heard* me, and had found me adequate, and had made a note.

* * *

Here's where I should tell you what I remember, and then here's where I have to tell you what the log says, and the two of them don't fit together, and that gap is where I live now.

I remember walking. I remember the bells getting older — the iron ones rusted to lace, the clay ones crumbling, and then, weirdly, past the oldest ones, newer bells again, and then very new, and I remember thinking *she's still hanging them, Edith was still hanging them, how far does this go* — and I remember reaching a place where the tunnel opened.

I remember a table.

Long. Dark wood. Chairs on both sides of it, dozens of them, all facing the table, all pushed in neatly except one at the far end, and I remember my light finding that one empty chair and I remember thinking, with a clarity that had nothing to do with my eyes: *someone just got up.*

And I remember — this is the last thing I remember, and it is the thing I would give a finger to unremember — I remember the chair being warm. I put my hand on it. I don't remember deciding to. I remember the wood being warm the way a chair is warm when someone has been sitting in it, and I remember a smell, apples and cold earth and under it something like a struck match, and I remember thinking, in a voice that was not quite mine and not unkind, *you're early.*

Then: nothing.

* * *

The log says I entered the arch at 2:14 p.m.

The log says I exited at 2:47 p.m.

Thirty-three minutes. I can account for twenty-two of them. I've reconstructed it a hundred times — the walk in, the bell wall, the long straight impossible distance, the table. Twenty-two minutes, generously, with pauses. Which leaves eleven minutes. Eleven minutes in which the logger recorded me moving — because it recorded me *moving*, a smooth continuous breadcrumb trail, no gap, no stop, the logger never stopped, that's the horror of it, the logger worked *perfectly* — eleven minutes in which the log shows me walking deeper, past the table, past the far end of the table, down a stretch of tunnel my memory does not contain at all, and then turning, and walking back, and passing the table again, and passing the bells, and coming out.

Eleven minutes of me, doing something, that I was not present for.

I've read the log so many times I could recite the breadcrumbs. There's a stretch — 2:26 to 2:37 — where my pace changes. Smooths out. The GPS drift, which had been jittering all afternoon the way it does under stone, goes dead calm, dead straight, like the signal had finally gotten a clean read on where I was. Like something down there had finally gotten a clean read on where I was.

At 2:31, the log shows me stopping for forty seconds beside a point in the tunnel wall. When I went back with Cyrus — later, with Cyrus, with the laminated card, with all the rules — that point in the wall held a bell I had no memory of hanging.

It was brass. It was new. It had no dust on it.

And scratched into its surface, in a line of letters too neat to be mine and in handwriting that I have spent a long time not comparing to my own, was a single word:

*SOON.*

* * *

I came up the cellar steps into gray afternoon light and the first thing I felt was relief, and the second thing I felt, arriving about a half-second behind the first like a delayed echo, was the certainty that I had left something down there.

Not an object. I inventoried — headlamp, chalk, phone, keys, the logger warm in my pocket. All present. What I'd left was some other thing. Some part of the inventory you can't pat your pockets for.

I closed the root cellar doors and I did the thing I have never once done in fifteen years of urbex, the thing I have openly mocked at parties: I checked that they were locked. They didn't have a lock. I held the handle anyway, for a second, like I was shaking its hand.

Then I sat on the grassy slope behind the house and I called Cyrus and I lied to her.

That's the part I keep circling. Not the tunnel, not the table, not the eleven minutes. The lie. She answered on the second ring, and her voice did that careful flat thing it does now, and she said, "Dev? You're at the house, aren't you. I can hear it in your voice. What did you do?"

And I said, "Nothing. I'm at the shop. I pulled the GIS maps like you asked. There's nothing under your lot, Cy. Nothing. Old cistern, maybe."

Because — and here is my defense, and I know it's a bad one, I've had time to know that — because I looked up at those gray green doors and I thought: *if I tell her, she'll go down.* She'd go down with all three flashlights and the laminated card and her grandmother's hand-drawn maps, and she'd walk that straight impossible corridor, and she'd put her hand on that chair, because Cyrus touches everything, that's who she is, she touches everything and reads it like braille.

And some part of me — the part that had been walking for eleven minutes without me — knew that the tunnel wanted her down there, wanted her specifically, the way a table wants a place setting, and that my lie wasn't cowardice.

My lie was a *guarding*.

I told myself it was a guarding, anyway. It's easier than the other word.

* * *

Cyrus came back to the shop that night. She stood in the doorway under the bell we keep over the door — an actual bell, an old shop bell, the kind that rings when you enter — and she looked at me for a long moment the way she looks at buildings, like she's checking the load-bearing walls.

"You went down there," she said.

"Cy—"

"Don't." She came in and sat down across from me and put her hands flat on the table, and I saw that they were shaking, and I saw that she was making them not shake through pure administrative will. "I'm going to ask you four questions and I need you to answer all four, and Dev — *Dev* — I need you to not lie, because I found the ledger, and I know what my grandmother did, and I know what's under the house, and I have exactly one friend and I need him to be *here*."

I said okay.

She asked the questions. How far did it go. What did you see. Did you touch anything. And then the fourth one, the one she leaned in for, the one I could see she'd been carrying since the phone call:

"Dev. What time did you go in, and what time did you come out?"

And I told her the truth. 2:14. 2:47. I watched her do the math, watched her lips move slightly, watched her arrive at thirty-three minutes, and watched her wait, politely, terribly, for me to account for them.

I opened my mouth to give her the twenty-two minutes, the walk, the table, the chair.

What came out instead was: "Twenty minutes. Give or take."

She sat back. And I watched something close in her face — not anger, worse than anger, *bookkeeping* — and she said, quietly, "Your logger. Can I see the log?"

And I said — God help me, with the logger sitting in my pocket warm as a held hand — "I didn't run it."

The bell over the shop door rang.

Neither of us had touched it. There was no wind. And Cyrus and I both looked at it, and then at each other, and I watched her decide — right there, in that second, with the bell still shivering — that she was going to believe me about the eleven minutes I'd hidden, and that she was going to find out what I'd done with them anyway, because the house had just told her that I'd brought something back with me.

It was standing behind me the whole time. I didn't know that yet.

I know it now.
