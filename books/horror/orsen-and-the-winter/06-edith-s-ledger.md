## 6. Edith's Ledger

I told myself I was organizing. That was the word I used, anyway—*organizing*—as if grief were a filing problem and I were simply the kind of person who filed. I had been in the house four days. I had sorted the kitchen drawers into three categories (keep, donate, and *why does my grandmother own nine corkscrews*), and I had made a spreadsheet for the attic, because spreadsheets are how cowards pray.

Dev came by every afternoon. He'd sit at the kitchen table with his laptop and his energy drinks and his cheerful, unbothered way of occupying space, and he'd say things like "You know you're allowed to just cry, right?" and I'd say "I'm organizing," and he'd let it go, because he was a good friend, and good friends know when the filing *is* the crying.

It was on the fourth afternoon, while Dev was in the bathroom loudly narrating his opinions about Edith's 1970s wallpaper to no one, that I found the loose floorboard.

I wasn't even looking for it. I was chasing a draft. The kitchen had always had one—a cold ribbon of air that Edith claimed came from "the house breathing"—and with the radiator off I'd finally located its source: a gap near the corner cabinet, where the boards didn't quite meet. I knelt down to stuff a rag in it, the way I'd seen her do a hundred times, and my knuckles found the edge of a board that lifted.

Not a gap. A hatch.

Underneath: a shallow space, lined with cedar that still gave off its ghost of a smell, and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string, a book.

"Dev," I called. My voice came out strange. "You're going to want to see this."

He appeared in the doorway wiping his hands on his jeans, and I held the bundle up like evidence at a trial. He looked at it, then at the open floor, then at me.

"Okay," he said. "Before we open that—statistically, in old houses, floor-hatches contain either money, letters, or a reason to move. I want you to be emotionally prepared for all three."

I untied the string.

It was a ledger. An actual ledger, the old-fashioned kind, cloth-bound and swollen with use, its spine cracked into a fan of soft ridges. The cover had no title. Edith's handwriting did not need one.

The first page was dated 1963.

* * *

Here is what I knew about my grandmother, before that afternoon: she raised me after my parents' accident. She made the best apple butter in three counties. She watched the news with the sound off and her lips moving, like she was correcting it. She counted doors before bed—front, back, cellar, pantry, the little one under the stairs that went nowhere—and she turned on every light in the house at dusk, every one, even the rooms nobody used, and if you asked her why she'd say, "Because a lit room is a kept room," and change the subject.

Here is what the ledger said she was.

The entries were short. That was the first thing that undid me—not gore, not confession, just bookkeeping. A name. A date. A line or two of plain, unhurried description. And then, in a hand that never wavered across forty years:

*Taken below.*

A tally mark.

I'll give you the first one entire, because I have it memorized now, the way you memorize a wound:

*March 1963. Raymond Pell. Took the Kessler girl out past the quarry, held her there two hours. She was fourteen. Judge Aldous called it "youthful high spirits" and fined him five dollars.*

*Taken below.*

One mark.

The next entry was eleven months later. *Walter Grimes.* What he'd done was worse, and the county had done less. *Taken below.* One mark.

Page after page. Year after year. The handwriting changed as Edith aged—the confident loops of her thirties tightening, thinning, until in the last decade the letters stood up straight and careful, like a woman writing very slowly so her hands wouldn't shake. But the format never changed. Name, date, deed, verdict of the world, verdict of Edith.

*Taken below.*

I counted the tally marks before I could stop myself. Dev counted them too, separately, on his phone, and we arrived at the same number at the same moment and neither of us said it out loud for a long time.

Thirty-one.

* * *

"Okay," Dev said finally, in the gentle voice he used for me and for injured animals. "So. Hypothetically. Your grandmother was—"

"Don't say it."

"—a very thorough—"

"Dev."

"—amateur historian of local crime," he finished, and then, because he was incapable of not being honest with me: "Cyrus. These are real names. Raymond Pell is in the county archives, I've seen the case files. Walter Grimes—there's a whole thing about Grimes, he vanished in '64, they dragged the river for a week." He scrolled. His face did something complicated. "Hollis Brandt. My dad used to tell a story about Hollis Brandt. Kids in his neighborhood wouldn't go near his house. He vanished too. They all just—*vanished*."

"Or," I said, and my voice sounded like someone else's, "they were never here at all, and this is a very old woman's fantasy journal. Old people write things down. It's a hobby."

"Cyrus."

"It's a *hobby*, Dev."

"Cyrus, your grandmother drew maps of tunnels under this house with *dates on them*. We've been over this. And now you find a ledger where every name in it has a date, and the dates line up with—" He stopped. He looked at the kitchen doorway, at the corner where the plain wooden chair stood, the one that was in every photograph and that no relative, no neighbor, no estate sale receipt could account for.

The chair was three feet closer to the table than it had been that morning.

Neither of us mentioned it. We had an unspoken treaty about the chair, Dev and I, signed in silence, ratified by mutual terror.

"Read me the last one," he said instead.

I turned to the final page. The most recent entry was dated fourteen months ago. The handwriting was Edith's at its most careful, each letter a small act of will.

*October. Dale Merrick. You know what he did. The courts knew. His lawyer knew. Everyone at the county fair knew, and they still bought his honey and laughed at his jokes, and that girl's family moved away because staying was unbearable and he stayed and was *beloved*. I am done waiting for the world to grow a spine.*

*Taken below.*

I sat with that one a long time. Dale Merrick. I knew the name. Everyone in town knew the name. There was a memorial bench.

"He hurt someone," I said. "Didn't he. Someone I—"

"Your friend," Dev said quietly. "From school. The one who moved away junior year. That was her family."

The kitchen was very warm suddenly, or very cold, I couldn't tell which. The radiator wasn't on. The house was breathing.

* * *

Here is the thing I have not said out loud, and I'm saying it now only because a ledger under the floorboards has revoked my right to cowardice:

It felt good.

Reading it felt good. That's the horror, that's the part I keep circling like a sore tooth—thirty-one names, thirty-one men the world had failed, and my grandmother, five feet one inch of apple butter and counted doors, had *done something*. She hadn't written letters. She hadn't marched. She had walked them down into the dark under her kitchen and closed the hatch and gone back to her jam.

I sat there on my grandmother's floor and felt a terrible, blazing pride, and underneath the pride, like a stone under moss, the questions:

*Taken below.* Taken below *by what*? Taken below to *where*? The tunnels on her maps went further than the lot allowed. The chair in the photographs moved. Dev had found bells on the walls of a passage that shouldn't exist.

And the biggest one, the one that made my hands go cold on the oilcloth:

*Why was she keeping books?*

You don't keep a ledger for yourself. Ledgers are for settling accounts. Ledgers are for *showing your work*. She had written every name, every deed, every verdict in a hand that got shakier and more careful with the years, and she had hidden it under her own kitchen floor, in the house she kept lit room by room, door by door, and I understood—with the certainty you understand things at 2 a.m. or at the bottom of a hole—that the ledger wasn't a record.

It was a *debt*. Written down so it could be *owed*. And owed to something that liked its accounts kept in a grandmother's careful hand.

"Dev," I said. "The maps. The ones with the names on them."

"Yeah."

"The names on the maps. Are they the same names?"

He was already pulling up the photos on his phone. I watched him scroll, and I watched his face do the complicated thing again, and I knew the answer before he said it.

"Same names," he said. "Same order. Same dates." He looked up at me. "Cyrus, the maps aren't maps of tunnels. They're a *route*. She was walking them down. Each one. She marked where they—"

He stopped, because the kitchen light flickered. Once. Twice. The radiator ticked on by itself, though the thermostat read sixty-two and I had not touched it.

And from below the floor—from under the cedar-lined hatch, from under the boards, from down the throat of the house—came a sound I will hear until I die. Not a voice. A *satisfaction*. The sound a house makes when a bill has finally been found.

The chair by the doorway turned, very slightly, to face us.

Dev stood up so fast his laptop hit the floor. "We're leaving," he said. "We're leaving right now, and we're calling someone, a priest, an archaeologist, a *lawyer*, I don't care—"

"Wait." I was looking at the last page of the ledger. At the bottom, beneath Dale Merrick's entry, beneath the final tally mark, there was one more line of Edith's writing. I hadn't seen it. It was smaller than the rest, pressed hard, as if she'd written it against the resistance of the page itself.

It said:

*If you're reading this, child, the count is unfinished. Thirty-one is not the number. Thirty-one is the* payment. *The number is what's owed for keeping them. Ask Mrs. Okonkwo before the solstice. Do not let the lights go out.*

*—E.*

The radiator ticked off. Every light in the kitchen held—steady, warm, obedient—and I understood that they weren't holding for me.

They were holding *position*.

Dev was staring at me. "What does it say? What does it say, Cyrus?"

Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of a bruise, though it was barely three o'clock, and down the street I could see Mrs. Okonkwo standing at the edge of Edith's yard, motionless, watching the house with an expression I couldn't read at this distance but which I already, horribly, recognized.

It was the expression of a woman who had been waiting four days for me to find the ledger.

She raised one hand—not a wave. A beckoning.

"Cyrus," Dev said again, "what does it say?"

I closed the ledger and held it against my chest like the world's heaviest book, which it was.

"We're going next door," I said. "And Dev—whatever she tells us, don't interrupt her. I don't think we have a lot of solstices left."
