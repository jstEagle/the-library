## 7. The Terms of the Invitation

Mrs. Okonkwo came the morning after I found the ledger, and she came carrying a covered dish, which is how grief announces itself in small towns. Nobody brings bad news in their bare hands. They bring it wrapped in foil, still warm, because the warmth gives everyone something to look at instead of each other.

I heard her boots on the porch before I heard her knock—two slow knocks, then a pause, then one more, like a question mark standing on its head. When I opened the door, she looked at me for a long moment with eyes that had done arithmetic they didn't enjoy.

"Cyrus," she said. "You've been in the floorboards."

Not *I'm so sorry about Edith*. Not *how are you holding up*. She said it the way you'd say *you've been swimming*—noting the wet hair, drawing the obvious conclusion.

I didn't bother denying it. My hands were still gray with the dust of sixty years, and I'd left the ledger open on the kitchen table behind me, which was either carelessness or a part of me that wanted witnesses.

"Come in," I said. "I made coffee. It's terrible."

"Then it's your grandmother's recipe." She stepped past me, and here's the thing I noticed and tried not to notice: she did not glance at the cellar door. Everyone else who'd entered this house since Edith died had glanced at it—Dev twice, the lawyer once, even the young man from the utility company, some instinct pulling their eyes toward that closed door the way tongues find a sore tooth. Mrs. Okonkwo looked at it the way you look at a staircase you've climbed ten thousand times. Without seeing it at all.

She set her dish on the counter—jollof rice, still steaming, which meant she'd made it fresh at five in the morning rather than bringing yesterday's comfort—and uncovered it, and for a moment we were just two women in a kitchen pretending that food solves things.

"Eat something," she said. "Then we'll talk."

"I'm not hungry."

"You will be. Grief is a furnace, child. It burns whatever you feed it, but if you don't feed it, it starts burning furniture." She served herself a small portion, sat down at the table—and I watched her do it deliberately, watched her choose the chair across from the wooden one, so that neither of them would be beside it. "Now. Tell me what you read."

So I told her. The names. The dates. *Taken below*, over and over, forty-one times in Edith's careful schoolteacher hand, each entry followed by a tally mark and, in later years, by nothing at all—as though even the justification had worn smooth from use. I told her about the tunnel maps under the house that matched no survey. About Dev's eleven missing minutes. About the chair in the photographs, patient as furniture, closer every decade.

Mrs. Okonkwo listened the way my grandmother used to listen to weather reports: nodding occasionally, unsurprised by any of it, waiting for the part that mattered to her.

When I finished, she folded her hands and said, "Your grandmother was the best woman I ever knew, and she did monstrous things, and both of those sentences are true, and you are going to have to carry them together or they will crush you separately."

"That's not an explanation."

"No. It's a warning label." She took a breath, and I realized she was afraid—not of me, exactly, but of the conversation itself, the way you're afraid of opening a door you know has been shut a long time. "How much do you know about why your grandmother bought this house? Sixty years ago, cash, sight unseen, from a bank that was glad to be rid of it?"

"I figured she liked the light."

"She liked the light," Mrs. Okonkwo agreed, "because she needed somewhere the dark had already agreed to live. Sit down, Cyrus. This is a long story and you're going to want to be sitting when it stops making sense."

I sat. Not in the chair. Never in the chair.

* * *

"The town used to be called Harrow's Bend," she began. "Before the waterworks, before the highway rerouted everything. And in Harrow's Bend there was a family named Voss—no relation to your Voss, don't make that face—who owned the land this house sits on, back when it was all orchard. And the Voss family had a problem, because under the orchard there was a hollow, and in the hollow there was something old, and old things under the ground have appetites the way rivers have currents. You don't argue with them. You channel them."

"Channel it into what?"

"Into justice." She said the word carefully, tasting it, finding it wanting. "That's what the Vosses discovered, or decided, or talked themselves into—I've never been sure which. The thing below doesn't distinguish good from evil, Cyrus. It's older than those ideas. But it does distinguish *invited* from *taken*. If you simply feed it, it grows greedy, and greed in that thing looks like the whole town going quietly wrong—men drowning themselves in the cistern, children sleepwalking toward the root cellar, dogs refusing to bark. But if you *invite*. If you bring it the guilty, freely given, freely confessed—" she paused—"it stays satisfied. Contained. Almost gentle. And while it's fed, it does something else. It holds down the worst impulses of everyone around it. Like a weight on a lid."

"Holds them down how?"

"How do you think a town of eleven hundred people went eighty years without a single murder? Not goodness, child. Arithmetic. There was always a man in Harrow's Bend who beat his wife, and everyone knew it, and no one could prove it, and he never escalated. Because something below him was hungry, and some animal part of him knew it, and kept his hands in his pockets." Her voice was steady but her thumb was moving in a slow circle on the tabletop, tracing the grain, around and around. "Edith didn't invent the arrangement. She inherited it, same as you're inheriting it now. The last Voss died without heirs in 1963, and the thing below got restless, and your grandmother—God rest her, God forgive her—came out from the city with a suitcase and a social work degree and a temper, and she knocked on that door, and she said yes."

"Said yes to what, exactly?"

"To the terms." Mrs. Okonkwo finally looked at the cellar door, briefly, the way you check a mirror. "The invitation has to be renewed. Spoken consent, aloud, in this kitchen, every winter solstice. Not written—the thing doesn't read. Not implied—it doesn't guess. Spoken. A living voice, freely offered, saying *I renew the invitation; the table is set; take only what is brought to you.* Every year. No exceptions. Miss one solstice and the terms lapse, and what's below stops being a guest and becomes a landlord."

The coffee had gone cold in my cup. I drank it anyway, because my hands wanted something to do.

"And the feeding," I said. "The men in the ledger. That was the *table being set*."

"Your grandmother spent two years figuring out what the thing would accept. It wouldn't take animals. It wouldn't take the innocent—we learned that the hard way, early, and I won't tell you that story today. What it takes, what it *wants*, is guilt. Real guilt. Men who had done harm and knew it and had never paid for it. Your grandmother found them. That was her genius and her sin, both at once—she had a nose for it, from all those years of social work, all those files, all those women crying in her office about men the courts couldn't touch. She'd invite them here. Tea, first. She was very polite about it. And she'd get them to confess—not to her, to themselves, out loud, in this room—and then she'd show them the cellar door, and explain the choice, and nine times out of ten they walked down on their own feet."

"And the tenth time?"

Mrs. Okonkwo's thumb stopped moving. "The tenth time, your grandmother walked them down. And she never slept right again, and she never spoke of it, and she added a line to the ledger in red ink: *never again unless they choose.*" She met my eyes. "She held herself to it, Cyrus. Forty years, she held herself to it. Whatever else you decide about her, decide it knowing that."

I thought about the photographs. The chair, creeping closer across four decades. "And what did it cost *her*? The renewal—what was she giving it?"

Mrs. Okonkwo was quiet long enough that the refrigerator hum filled the kitchen, and beneath the hum, faint as a pulse through a wall, I could almost believe I heard something else. Something patient.

"Company," she said at last. "It's lonely down there, child. Terribly, endlessly lonely. The renewal isn't just permission. It's presence. One night a year, the one who renews the invitation sits in that chair, at that table, and speaks the terms, and the thing below comes up—just to the threshold, just to listen—and for one night it isn't alone, and neither is the one sitting there. Edith called it the worst evening of her year. She also never missed one. Not for pneumonia, not for the blizzard of '78, not for my Adaeze's funeral. She'd sit in that chair until dawn, talking to it, telling it about the town, the gossip, the weather. Keeping it civilized." Her voice caught, just slightly. "Keeping it *company*. You can hate what someone does and still understand why they kept doing it. Loneliness is a debt too."

* * *

I got up and stood at the window. The yard was doing its ordinary December thing—bare maples, frost on the grass, Dev's tire tracks from two days ago icing over. Ordinary. Except that I found myself counting the windows without meaning to, the way I count doors, and except that the glass seemed slightly warmer than the air should allow, as though the house itself were running a fever.

"Three days," I said.

"Winter solstice. Thursday." Mrs. Okonkwo rose and began covering her dish, movements precise, a woman putting things away. "If nobody speaks the renewal, the terms lapse at sundown. The house will try to protect whoever's inside—it's bound to the bloodline now, to you—but a house is only walls, and walls are only arithmetic, and the thing below has had sixty years to learn our counting." She paused. "There's another option. There's always another option, and it's worse. You can end it. Renounce the invitation entirely, seal the tunnels, salt the thresholds, refuse it forever. Some have tried. The house fights them, and the thing below gets... creative. And the lid comes off the whole town's worst impulses, all at once, everywhere, permanently. Eleven hundred people, Cyrus. Their anger, their cruelty, their three-in-the-morning thoughts—all of it uncaged, because one woman didn't want to sit in a chair."

"That's blackmail."

"Yes," she said simply. "It absolutely is. It's the oldest kind. Now you understand why your grandmother never told you. Why none of us did. You don't hand a young person a life sentence; you wait until it's theirs whether they signed for it or not." She picked up her dish, then set it back down, and turned to face me fully, and what was in her face wasn't pity. It was apology. Old, worn-smooth apology, the kind that's been rehearsed for years against a day like this one. "There's one more thing. About the names in the ledger. Some of them weren't only Edith's findings."

My stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the tunnels, all the way down. "What do you mean, *some of them*?"

"I mean that private justice is never as clean as the person administering it believes. I mean that in 1994, a man named Gerald Pruitt appeared in your grandmother's kitchen, and I am the reason he was in this town at all, and I have carried that for thirty years, and Thursday I may not get the chance to say it again." She lifted her chin, and I saw that underneath the fear she was, in her own way, brave—braver than Edith, maybe, who'd fed her guilt to something else instead of saying it aloud. "But not today. Today you eat, and you rest, and you think about Thursday. And Cyrus—" she stopped at the door, hand on the frame, not looking back—"whatever you decide, decide it *before* sundown Wednesday. The thing below starts listening early. It likes to hear what you're planning."

The door closed behind her with a sound that was far too much like a latch.

I stood in the kitchen alone. The rice steamed on the counter. The chair sat where it always sat, facing the table, patient as furniture, and I could have sworn—could have sworn—that it had turned, very slightly, toward me.

Three days. Somewhere below my feet, something old was already setting places.

I counted the doors. Nine.

For now.
