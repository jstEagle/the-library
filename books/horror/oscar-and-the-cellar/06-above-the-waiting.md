## 6. Above, the Waiting

Maren had grown up with the sound of digging in her ears. Her father's spade against clay was the first music she ever knew — that patient, rhythmic *shk, shk, shk* of earth giving way — and she had learned to read it the way other children learned to read weather. A grave dug well sounded like a promise kept. A grave dug badly sounded like an argument.

The hole beneath the chapel floor sounded like neither.

It sounded like a wound being reopened.

She drove her own spade into the packed soil and heaved, and the lantern light swung and shivered across the walls of the shaft they'd cut over three days and two nights. Ellery worked beside her, sleeves rolled to the elbow, his defrocked hands blistered and split in ways his consecrated ones had never been. He had stopped praying somewhere around the second hour of the second night. Not because he'd lost faith, she thought. Because he'd realized someone down there might be listening, and he didn't want to give it his voice until he understood what voices were worth.

Above them, through the chapel's broken roof, the village hummed.

It had started that morning, Maren realized. Not suddenly — nothing in Hollowmere was sudden — but gradually, the way frost creeps up a window. First old Widow Cottle, humming on her step. Then the Hollis boys, humming as they mended fence. Then everyone, everywhere, all at once, the same four notes rising and falling, rising and falling: the lullaby, or the part of it Hollowmere still permitted itself. The verse before the missing one. The song that ended mid-breath, like a door closing on a word.

*Hush now, hush now, under stone,*

*the dark is patient, we are not alone—*

And then nothing. Every time. The whole village stopping at the same place, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to be.

"They're not doing it on purpose," Ellery said. He didn't stop digging. His voice came out low and rough, half-swallowed by the shaft. "That's what frightens me. Watch Cottle's face when she does it. She doesn't know."

"I know." Maren wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist. The air below the chapel was thick, mineral, wrong — like breathing inside a sealed jar. "My father used to say the village has a mind the way a flock has a mind. No single bird decides to turn. They all just turn."

"And which bird is turning them now?"

"Whichever one it bought." She set her feet and lifted another load of earth. "Three hundred years ago it wasn't birds. It was children."

Ellery went quiet. In three days of shared labor he had told her about the ledger — the founders' book he'd found in the seminary archive, the one that had cost him his collar. *Paid unto the Keeper, twelve voices, in full and good faith.* Twelve entries. Twelve names written in a hand so careful it looked like love. And then, every hundred years after, another entry, another handful of names, the ink changing, the handwriting changing, the arithmetic never changing.

The last entry was dated ninety-nine years ago. Which meant the account had come due.

Which meant Xanthe Blackwood, who had walked into Hollowmere with his recording boxes and his gentle questions and his blood that fit the founders' line like a key fits a lock, had not come here by accident at all. He had been *summoned*. Called the way you call a debt.

"He's been gone two days," Maren said. "Since the door opened for him."

"We don't know it opened *for* him."

"Bram saw it." She kept her eyes on the work. "Bram saw the iron peel back like a flower opening, and he saw the roots come out, and he ran. Bram doesn't run from anything. He once pulled a live adder out of his boot with his teeth." She heard her own voice go flat and hard and didn't try to soften it. "The door opened for him. The village watched it happen and then the village went home and started humming."

* * *

They rested at dawn, if you could call it resting — sitting side by side in the chapel's cold nave, backs against a pillar, sharing bread that tasted of ash because everything in Hollowmere had begun to taste of ash. Through the broken roof Maren could see the sky going pale, and hear the humming change with the light. Louder in daylight. As if the sun fed it too.

Her great-aunt Sef had been taken the year Maren was six.

Nobody had said *taken*. They had said *gone to keep the house*, which was what Hollowmere always said, and little Maren had imagined a house underground with candles and beds, a kind of cellar-home where Aunt Sef got to sleep forever. It was only years later, going through her grandmother's things, that she'd found the tally — her family's private ledger, kept by gravediggers because gravediggers counted what the village refused to count. Names of the missing. Dates. And beside each name, in her grandfather's cramped hand, a small notation: *sang*, or *did not sing*, or, in Sef's case, *sang beautifully*.

Sef had sung beautifully. That was why they chose her. The bargain wanted voices, and it wanted them unspoiled.

Maren had been six years old and the whole village had smiled at her at the funeral-that-wasn't-a-funeral, and brought pies, and said *she's in a better place now, keeping the house,* and not one of them — not one — had asked whether Sef had wanted to go. The choice had been made the way all Hollowmere's choices were made: quietly, collectively, with the arithmetic done before anyone thought to check it. One voice against the whole village's survival. Anyone honest would make the same trade. Wouldn't they?

Wouldn't they?

Maren had spent twenty years not answering that question. Now the question had a face — a tired, kind, stubborn face with ink-stained fingers and a recorder full of songs that all broke in the same place — and the village was answering it again, humming its consent into the morning air, and Maren found that she could not, would not, stand in the nave and let it happen twice.

"I'm going back down," she said.

"Maren." Ellery caught her arm. His grip was stronger than his hands looked. "Listen to me. If we break through to that door while it's open — while *he's* down there — we don't know what comes through. We don't know what we let out. There are things worse than losing him."

"No," Maren said. "There aren't. I've made a list."

That startled a breath of almost-laughter out of him, and she was glad, because she needed him laughing, needed him human, needed him not-humming. But his face went serious again fast, and he looked up through the broken roof at the pale sky, at the village beyond it, and his jaw tightened.

"They'll come," he said. "When we get close. When we're near enough to matter. You understand that, don't you? They won't send men with torches. They'll come smiling. They'll bring bread. They'll tell us the chapel isn't safe, the floor is unstable, there's sickness in the ground. And some of them will believe every word, because the humming makes believing easy." He turned to her. "I was thrown out of the Church for asking questions, Maren. I know how a community handles a question it has already answered. It doesn't argue. It *absorbs*."

"Then we dig faster than it absorbs us."

* * *

They dug through the morning, and the humming grew, and by noon it had words.

Maren felt it before she understood it — a change in the vibration coming down through the earth, through the spade handle, through the soles of her boots. The four notes were no longer enough. Somewhere above, the whole village had begun, softly, in unison, without any single throat deciding to lead, to sing the lullaby forward. Past the gap. Into the missing verse.

*Come down, come down, the last verse sings,*

*one voice to pay for all our things—*

Maren dropped her spade.

The words were old. Older than the hymnal versions, older than the founders' English even — they had the bone-structure of something translated from something else, something that had lived in this valley before there was a valley with a name. And every voice above sang it perfectly, in tune, in time, villagers who couldn't carry a tune at a wedding singing it flawlessly, because it wasn't their throats doing the singing anymore. It was the bargain, using the village like an instrument. Like a bell it had paid for.

"*One voice to pay for all our things,*" Ellery whispered. He had gone grey. "That's the renewal. That's the century turning. They're — God forgive me — they're *ratifying* it. The whole village, together. Consent given in advance."

"Consent for what?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. She did the arithmetic herself, the way her grandfather had taught her to do it, the way the gravediggers' tallies had taught her: the account due, the line of the founders, the stranger whose blood completed it. The village wasn't waiting for Xanthe to be chosen.

The village had already chosen him. It was singing its yes into the ground right now, hundreds of voices strong, feeding the decision downward through the soil like water to a root.

"No," Maren said aloud, to the ceiling, to the village, to the thing beneath everything. "No. You don't get to do it again. You didn't ask Sef and you're not asking him. You don't get to be gentle about it. You don't get to bring pies."

She picked up her spade.

Ellery stared at her a moment — this small fierce woman caked in grave-earth in a defiled chapel, shaking with fury rather than fear — and then he picked up his, and they dug, and the humming above swelled to meet them, and the earth between them and the sealed door grew thinner with every stroke. Three feet. Then two. Then, near dusk, Maren's spade struck something that rang.

Iron.

Not the door itself — a bracket, a hinge-plate, the edge of the frame, buried in centuries of fill. But it rang clean and bright, a true note, and the moment it rang the humming above *stopped*.

All of it. Every voice. At once.

The silence that followed was worse than the song. It was the silence of a held breath, of a flock turning, of a village becoming aware — all together, all at once — of exactly what two people were doing beneath its chapel floor.

Maren knelt and brushed dirt from the iron with her bare hands, and far below, through the stone, faint as a heartbeat, she heard something answer the ringing metal.

A voice. Thin, cracked, human.

Xanthe. Singing.

And underneath his voice, dozens of others — small voices, children's voices, rising to meet him — and the sealed door, warm under her palms, had begun, very slowly, to glow.
