## 7. The Price of Survival

The dark did not end. Xanthe had stopped expecting it to.

He sat — or believed he sat, for in this place sitting was a courtesy the body paid to itself — on a floor of packed earth that was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm. Above him, where the cellar door had been, there was only a suggestion of stone, a grey memory of grey. Time moved here the way honey moves off a cold spoon, and he had learned not to count his heartbeats because they came too slowly, as if his blood had also been asked to wait its turn.

The Thing Below did not loom. That was the first cruelty of it. He had expected a shape, a hunger with teeth, something his novelist's mind could render and thereby survive. Instead there was a presence like a well-stocked pantry, like the smell of bread through a wall, like the particular comfort of a house that has never known disaster. It surrounded him the way prosperity surrounds a town that has forgotten what it paid for it.

"You are cold," it said. Its voice was not a voice so much as a warmth behind the ear, a voice heard from inside. "That is because you are thinking of the choir. The children make the dark feel colder to the living. They cannot help it. They are only echoes, you understand. Echoes of what they might have sung."

"They have names," Xanthe said. His own voice sounded thin, borrowed, a candle in a cathedral.

"They had names. Yes." The Thing said it gently, the way one corrects a child who has confused a dream with a morning. "And the village had winter, and fever, and the wolves at the treeline, and the blight in the barley, and no children at all, if the founders had refused. You have seen the ledger, Mr. Blackwood. You are a man who studies what people write down. Tell me — what did the founders write in the margin of the first bargain?"

He had not seen the ledger. But he had seen, in the choir's shared remembering, the scratch of a quill, the founders' hands shaking around the candle, and old Aldous Mere — Maren's blood, though she did not yet know the whole of it — writing in the margin in a hand gone suddenly small: *We did what the winter asked. God forgive the arithmetic.*

"*We did what the winter asked*," Xanthe said.

"And it asked only once a century," the Thing said. "One voice. One small song, given down into the dark, and in exchange: no famine. No fever crossing the bridge. The wolves turning back at the stones. A hundred years of ordinary Tuesdays, of bread rising, of children — *other* children, children whose turn it was not — laughing in the lane. Tell me that is not a fair price. You have written tragedies, I am told. You know better than most how much suffering a single life can hold, and how much a village is worth."

Xanthe pressed his palms against the warm earth. "You're rehearsing me," he said. "You're giving me the speech you gave them."

"I am giving you the truth," said the Thing, with something that was almost hurt. "The truth is the only coin I accept. Lies sour in the dark. Ask the founders — no, don't. They are past asking. But you, Xanthe Blackwood — you are not past anything. You are *beginning*."

And the dark changed.

* * *

It did not seize him. It *invited* him, which was worse. The warmth behind his ear became a warmth along his spine, and then the cellar-dark thinned like fog off a pond, and he was standing — standing! — in the lane outside the Hollowmere inn, in sunlight, in a morning so golden it felt like a bribe.

He knew it was false the way you know a dream's geography. But oh, the detail of it. The smell of woodsmoke and cut grass. The inn door open, and through it the sound of the whole village at breakfast, laughing, and — this was the detail that nearly undid him — *singing*. A woman at the hearth was singing the lullaby straight through, every verse, including the one nobody above would touch, and her voice did not falter and no one flinched and a child on her knee clapped along.

"You could have this," the Thing said, from everywhere, from nowhere, from the warmth of the bread and the light on the thatch. "Sing the verse. Take the old choir's place as the giver rather than the given, and the century turns to you like a lock to its key. The village will flourish. You will flourish. You will want for nothing, because wanting is what the dark eats, and you will have fed it so well it will have no appetite left for you."

The scene folded like paper and opened again, and he was older — grey at the temples, a scar on his knuckles he did not recognize — sitting in a study lined with his own books, more books than he had ever sold, more than he had ever *written*. On the desk, a manuscript in his own hand: *Oscar and the Cellar*, finished, and beside it six others, and a letter from a publisher with a sum on it that had more zeroes than his father had earned in a lifetime. Through the window, Hollowmere in high summer, fat with harvest, its children running through the barley with ribbons in their hair, and every one of them singing, loud and clear and *free*.

"Look how happy they are," the Thing murmured. "You did that. One verse, one night, one small surrender, and a hundred years of this. And when your century ends, you will be old, Xanthe, or gone, and some other voice will stand where you stood, and they will be told exactly what you were told, and they will make exactly the choice that keeps their neighbors fed. That is not cruelty. That is *stewardship*. That is what every generation owes the next: the world, kept warm."

He was in the inn now, at the long table, and the whole village raised their cups to him. *To the Blackwood!* someone called, and the name went around the room like a toast and like a prayer. Maren was there — older, laughing, her father beside her with color in his cheeks instead of grave-dust — and she raised her cup to him too, and her eyes were bright with a gratitude he had never earned and never would.

And beneath the floorboards, so faint that only he could hear it, because only he knew to listen: a small voice, singing the first line of a lullaby, over and over, unable to reach the second.

* * *

The visions released him gently, the way a hand opens to let a bird go — and he understood that the gentleness was the trap, that the Thing could have shown him all of this with teeth and fire and he might have resisted it, but no one resists being *kindly* offered the world.

"That child under the floor," Xanthe said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "In the next century. The one who pays for all this. Tell me its name."

"There is no name yet," the Thing said. "The ledger is not written that far."

"Then tell me this." He turned — turned toward the warmth, toward the pantry-smell, toward the thing older than hunger that had learned to speak in the grammar of comfort. "In your visions, in your hundred golden years — did you show me a single morning where I woke and *remembered*? Where I sat at that long table with their cups raised to me and did not hear, under every song, under every laughing child, the small voice that bought it? Because I have heard that voice for three days now. I have learned its names. Tabitha Mere. Osric Vale. Little Wren Halloway, who was six. You cannot give me a century in which I do not hear them, because I would carry them into your golden morning the way a man carries a stone in his boot, and I would feel it at every step, and I would know — I would *know* — that the bread was warm and the wolves were far and the children sang, because one child under the floor could not."

The warmth behind his ear went very still.

"The founders made your arithmetic," Xanthe said. "I've read their margin. *We did what the winter asked.* But they were starving, and freezing, and they had watched their neighbors die. They were cornered. You are showing me a sunny lane and a full table and calling it the same thing, and it is not the same thing, because I know the price *before* I eat. That's the whole difference between a bargain and a sin — whether you knew. And you have made very sure I know."

"You would refuse comfort," the Thing said, and for the first time its voice had an edge under the warmth, the faintest grind, like a cellar door being tested. "For *names*. For echoes. For children who have been dark for four hundred years and will never sit at any table."

"They're not echoes," Xanthe said. "They're a choir. And a choir is not an echo. A choir is a *decision*, made together, in harmony, that no voice sings alone."

The warmth flared — not in anger, he thought, but in something like appetite sharpening, the pantry smelling a new kind of hunger it had not been offered before. The golden lane folded away. The study, the toast, Maren's bright unearned eyes — gone, gone, gone, and the packed warm earth rose up around him again, and the dark was dark, honest dark, and he was grateful for it.

"Then let us speak plainly, last verse," the Thing said, and now the politeness had thinned to a membrane over something vast. "You will not take the century freely. Very well. But understand what refusal costs, and who has always paid it. The bargain does not lapse because a folklorist grows sentimental. If no voice is given, the dark takes. It takes the way winter takes — broadly, and without ledger, and beginning with the smallest. You may save four hundred dead children and bury a living village. That is also arithmetic, Mr. Blackwood. I learned it from your founders. Will you do the sums with me?"

And before Xanthe could answer — before he could find the flaw in it, because there was a flaw, there had to be, Maren was digging and Ellery was praying and the choir was *waiting* — he heard it.

Not from the Thing. From behind him. From the deep of the dark where the children kept themselves.

Someone had begun to sing. A small voice, alone, wavering — and then a second voice found the line above it, and a third, and a fourth, and Xanthe turned in the dark toward the sound with his heart going like a fist on a door, because he knew the song. It was his. The one he had hummed to himself, without thinking, on his first night in the Hollowmere inn — the half-finished melody he'd been carrying in his head for years, the one he'd told himself he'd find an ending for someday.

They were singing his song back to him. And they were waiting, all of them, four hundred voices holding one long breath, to hear what he would do with it.

The warmth behind his ear went cold as a coin.

"No," the Thing said — softly, and for the first time, *afraid* — "that is not how it is done."

"It's exactly how it's done," said Xanthe Blackwood, and opened his mouth.
