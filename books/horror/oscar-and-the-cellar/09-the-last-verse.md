## 9. The Last Verse

The dark had learned Xanthe's name by now, and it said it the way a creditor does — patiently, with interest accruing.

*Sing it,* the Thing Below murmured, and its voice was everywhere at once, in the standing water that pooled around his ankles, in the roots that ribbed the ceiling like the vaulting of some buried cathedral, in the hundred small throats of the choir who hovered at the edge of his sight, pale as paper held up to a window. *Sing the verse that is owed. One voice for one century. You have heard what I offer. Warm bread. A name that opens doors. Children who sleep soundly because someone else's child went quiet first.*

Xanthe stood among the dead children and did not answer.

He had spent three days down here — or three years; time pooled in this place like water finding its level, and he had stopped trusting his own counting. Three days was enough to learn their names. That had been Ellery's idea, shouted down through six feet of earth before the village sealed the shaft: *They took your voice to make you a payment. So give them back theirs.* Maren's idea too, though she'd only pressed her palm flat against the stone door and said, over and over, *We're coming. We're coming. Don't you dare finish anything without us.*

So he had begun at the beginning, with the smallest ghost, a girl who had died — no, not died, *been spent*, the way a coin is spent — in the winter the founders first bargained, four hundred years ago. Her name was Odile. She had forgotten it herself until he sang it back to her, set to the melody of the lullaby's missing verse, the one every soul in Hollowmere stopped short of singing.

When she remembered, she made a sound like a bell uncracking.

One by one since then: Tomas, who liked frogs. Brigid, who counted everything twice. The twins, Sorrel and Sef, who had been taken together so the bargain would hold symmetrical. Ninety-one children across four centuries, ninety-one verses of one song, and Xanthe Blackwood — folklorist, collector, thief of other people's music, though he had never thought of himself that way until now — had given each one back.

The choir did not look hungry anymore. They looked *awake*.

And the Thing Below knew it.

*You are spending yourself thin, last verse,* it said, and beneath the politeness something vast shifted, the way a whale shifts under ice. *Every name you return is a coin out of my purse. Do you imagine I will let my account empty?*

"I don't imagine," Xanthe said. His own voice sounded strange to him here, flattened by the dark, but it held. "I know arithmetic when I see it. My whole life I've collected songs, and every one of them was somebody's. You taught this village to think of voices as currency. But a song isn't money." He turned to face the choir, all ninety-one of them, and his chest ached with something that was not fear. "A song is a *hand*. It only works when it's given."

Above him — above all of them — came a sound that did not belong to the cellar.

Iron shrieking. Stone groaning. And threaded through both, faint but unmistakable, human voices raised not in the lullaby but in something older and uglier: the founders' descendants, come to collect what they believed was theirs.

* * *

Maren Voss had broken three shovels and one fingernail and none of her promises.

The door had been sealed from the inside — iron driven through stone by hands that should have been impossible — but iron rusts when a thing below grows careless, and the Thing Below had grown very careless indeed, distracted by ninety-one small resurrections. When Bram's crew had fled the excavation weeks ago, they'd left their tools. Maren had inherited them, along with Father Ellery, along with a fury she carried the way her father carried his spade: always, everywhere, without apology.

Now the shaft was open, and the village had come.

They arrived at moonrise, the whole of Hollowmere, or near enough — the Aldous family at their head, old Hesper Aldous walking with a cane she did not need, her mouth already moving around the shape of the lullaby's missing verse though no sound came out. Behind her, the others hummed. It was the humming that frightened Maren most: forty villagers in perfect unison, eyes half-closed, swaying like wheat, like the congregation of a church whose god lived downward.

"They'll take him," Ellery breathed beside her. He was gray-faced, collarless, holding the crowbar like a man who had never been trained for anything but questions. "If they reach the door before—"

"Then we don't let them reach it." Maren spat on her palms. "My great-aunt Ilsabet went down there in 1923. Nobody volunteered her. Nobody even said her name after. I found her tally marks in the gravedigger's ledger — *one voice, paid in full* — and nobody ever once wrote down whether she screamed." She looked at the crowd swaying down the hill, and her hands did not shake, which surprised her. "I'm done letting this village be polite about its arithmetic."

They wedged themselves into the shaft mouth, two bodies against a tide. Above, the humming swelled into words at last, the forbidden verse rising from forty throats like floodwater:

*Hush now, hush, the cellar knows,*
*the cellar keeps what we forgo—*

"Don't sing it!" Ellery shouted, and it was half plea, half exorcism. "That verse isn't yours! It never belonged to you — it belongs to the *children*!"

Hesper Aldous reached them first. Up close her eyes were terrible, clear, and empty as washed glass. "Step aside, girl. The bargain is four hundred years old. Your bones were bought with it, same as mine."

"My bones are my own," Maren said, and put her shoulder against the doorframe. "And so is his voice."

The crowd pressed forward. Ellery braced beside her, and the two of them held — barely, absurdly, two lonely people against a village — while below, in the pooling dark, Xanthe Blackwood opened his mouth.

* * *

He did not begin with his own voice.

He began with Odile's.

Her verse rose through the cellar first — thin, silver, unmistakably a child's — and the Thing Below flinched the way a ledger flinches at an erasure. Then Tomas. Then Brigid, counting her notes twice. Then the twins, weaving their lines together the way they must have woven them alive, in some farmhouse kitchen four centuries gone. The choir sang the lullaby whole, verse by stolen verse, and each name restored was a nail pulled from the bargain's coffin.

*Stop,* the Thing Below said, and its politeness cracked like river ice. *STOP.*

"No," Xanthe said.

And then he gave it the last verse. His verse. The one he had been summoned to pay.

But he did not sing it alone, and he did not sing it as an offering. He sang it as a *teaching* — the way he'd taught every ballad he'd ever collected, line by line, patient, generous — and the choir caught it and carried it, ninety-one voices braided with his one, until the completed lullaby filled the cellar like light fills water. It was not a payment. It was not a purchase. It was a song sung by everyone it belonged to, and that, he understood in a flash of terrible clarity, was the one thing the bargain could not survive: a song with no debt in it.

The Thing Below began to starve in real time. Its darkness thinned. Its polite voice unraveled into something older than hunger and more pitiful — a great emptiness that had once, perhaps, been only cold, only underground, only alone, until the founders taught it to eat.

Through the floor, muffled but distinct, Maren heard the change. The humming above faltered. Hesper Aldous staggered as if struck, the verse falling out of her mouth mid-word, and forty villagers woke from their swaying like sleepers from a shared fever.

"The door!" Maren gasped. "Ellery — the door —"

It was opening from below. Not breaking — *yielding*, the iron drawing back like a hand unclenching, and light poured up out of the cellar-dark, warm and wrong-colored, gold like lamplight in a place that had never known lamps. Xanthe climbed toward it, the choir streaming past him and around him, not fleeing upward but circling him, bearing him, ninety-one ghosts lifting the man who had given them back their names.

Hesper Aldous recovered. Hesper Aldous saw what was rising out of her family's oldest investment, and she screamed — not words, just refusal — and threw herself at the shaft with the whole awakened village surging behind her.

Maren hit her with the crowbar's flat. Ellery seized the door's edge and pulled. Maren pulled. Bram, shame-faced, weeping, arrived out of the crowd and pulled, and the villagers' hands tore at their shoulders, their hair, their clothes, and still they pulled, and the door came wide—

—and Xanthe Blackwood rose out of the earth of Hollowmere singing.

He was still singing when the bargain shattered. There was no explosion, no thunder. There was a sound like a great book closing gently, like a debt forgiven so thoroughly it had never existed, and every soul on that hilltop felt four centuries of arithmetic simply *stop*. The lullaby hung complete in the night air, whole for the first time since the founders first signed it away, and the children of Hollowmere — the living ones, the ones who had never once been allowed to finish the song — opened their mouths and joined in without knowing why, the way children join any song that finally sounds like theirs.

The choir rose with the last note. Ninety-one lights going up through the cold air like sparks off a bonfire, and if they had faces, and they did, every face was turned toward Xanthe in something that was not gratitude exactly — gratitude is what the living owe — but recognition. *You knew our names. You will carry them.*

Then they were gone, and the hilltop was only a hilltop, and the Thing Below was nothing at all, and the silence afterward was enormous.

It was also, Xanthe realized slowly, with a calm that felt like standing at the edge of deep water, total.

"Maren." His voice worked. He could feel it working, feel the song still shaped in his throat where he would keep it forever, finished, whole, unsingable-to-himself. "Maren, I can't hear it."

She was kneeling in front of him, mud to her elbows, blood on her brow, saying something. Her mouth moved. The village's mouths moved behind her. Somewhere a child was laughing, high and bright, and he saw the laugh and felt nothing of it arrive.

He touched his own ears and brought his fingers away clean, and understood that the price had been taken after all — quietly, precisely, in the moment the bargain broke. Not his voice. Never his voice. Only his hearing, so that the man who saved the song would spend the rest of his life unable to hear it.

Maren caught his hands. Her eyes were wet and furious and shining, and she was speaking, urgent, and he watched her lips shape the words until he understood them:

*What do we do now?*

Xanthe Blackwood smiled, deaf in the ruined moonlight, the completed lullaby locked silent and perfect inside him like a letter he could read but never again hear read aloud.

He mouthed back: *Teach it to me anyway. I want to watch.*
