## 1. The Porch Light

The bus from Harrisburg dropped Jonah at the corner of Delmore and Vine at 4:17 in the afternoon, and the first thing they noticed was that the town smelled the same. Not looked the same, not sounded the same—Hollow Creek had gone visibly to seed in the thirty years since Jonah had left, its storefronts papered over with FOR LEASE signs gone yellow as old teeth, its sidewalks buckled into small mountain ranges by frost heave—but it smelled the same. Wet limestone and cut grass and, underneath it all, something mineral and cold, like the air that came up out of a cellar in July. Jonah stood at the corner with their duffel bag and their grandmother's ashes in a cardboard box the funeral home had provided, and breathed it in, and felt something in their chest turn over like a key in a lock they'd hoped was rusted shut.

They had not wanted to come back. They had said as much, twice, to the lawyer on the phone, and the lawyer—a soft-voiced man named Pell who had known Jonah's grandmother for forty years—had said, in the tone of a man reciting a fact about weather, "She left the house to you specifically, Mr. Okonkwo. In the will. In her own hand." As if that settled anything. As if a dead woman's handwriting were a summons that could not be refused.

Jonah walked. The funeral home was only six blocks from the house on Delmore, and they had never learned to drive, a fact their urban exploration followers found charming and their mother had found, in the last years of their speaking to each other, symptomatic. They walked past the shuttered Rexall and the church with its shingle roof gone moss-green and the water tower, still standing, still bearing the town's name in letters that had faded to the color of weak tea. HOLLOW CREEK. Beneath it, in older paint that had never quite been covered, a ghost of an older sign: a slogan from some chamber-of-commerce optimism of the 1950s. Jonah had spent a whole childhood trying to read it and had never managed. It was just a smear of white now, a scar of enthusiasm.

The light was going long and gold, the way October light does, stretching every shadow out like taffy. Jonah's shadow ran ahead of them down Delmore Street, long and thin and slightly wrong in the way shadows always are when you finally look at them, and Jonah made a point of not looking at it. They were thirty-seven years old. They had crawled through storm drains under three cities and slept in the shell of a condemned sanatorium and once, in Detroit, spent a night in a tunnel that the maps insisted did not exist, and they did not scare easily, and they were not scared now. They were grieving. Grief, they had learned, felt exactly like fear from the inside: the same cold hands, the same shortness of breath. You just had to know which one you were carrying.

The house on Delmore was the fourth from the corner, and Jonah saw it before they were ready to, the way you see a person you've been avoiding across a crowded room.

It was worse. That was the first thing. The paint—the particular gray-blue that Jonah's grandmother had mixed herself, that Jonah had helped stir as a child with a paint stick in a five-gallon bucket while she told them to mind the drips—had peeled back to bare wood in long vertical stripes, so the house looked flayed. The gutters sagged. A shutter hung by one hinge. But the porch light was on.

That was the second thing, and it was worse than the first.

The porch light was on, and it was not the flickering sodium-orange of a dying bulb in an empty house. It burned steady and white and warm, the exact warm white of the light that had burned on that porch for all of Jonah's childhood, the light Edith Okonkwo left on every night of her life because—she said—her mother had left it on, and her mother's mother before her, and a light on the porch meant somebody was expected home. Jonah stood at the edge of the cracked driveway with the box of ashes in their arms and stared at that light and felt thirty years collapse into the width of a heartbeat. They were seven years old. They were running up this driveway in the dark with their shoes in their hand because they'd been told not to get them wet, and the porch light was on, and somebody was expected home, and they were expected.

"Stop it," Jonah said out loud, to themselves, and their voice sounded strange in the empty street.

They made themselves walk up the drive. The gravel had gone to weeds. The box of ashes was heavier than it should have been, or their arms were weaker, or grief was a physical weight after all, which every poet says and no one believes until it happens. They climbed the porch steps. The boards groaned under their weight in the same three places they had always groaned. Jonah's hand went to the doorknob out of pure animal habit and found it locked, and they laughed—a single dry note with nothing in it—because of course it was locked, because she was dead, because the light was on and nobody was home.

That was when they saw the figure on the porch steps.

It was sitting on the third step from the bottom, on the left, in the exact spot where the wood had worn smooth from decades of small bodies sitting down. It was a child. A boy, by the cut of him—corduroy pants, a striped t-shirt, sneakers with the laces double-knotted the way mothers double-knot them so they'll hold. He was small. Six, maybe seven. His hair was a brown that hadn't decided whether to be dark or light, cut in a bowl that no barber had given him since 1975. He sat with his knees together and his hands flat on the step beside him and he was looking at Jonah with an expression of perfect, patient attention, the way a dog looks at a door it knows will open.

The porch light fell across him evenly. It did not flicker. Nothing about him flickered.

Jonah did not scream. Later, they would be proud of that, in the way you are proud of a small decency performed in a burning building. What they did instead was take one step backward, put the box of ashes down on the porch boards very carefully, as if it were the child who might be startled by noise, and say, in a voice they barely recognized as their own:

"No."

The boy tilted his head. He did not blink. Jonah had time, in the long second that followed, to take in the details they had spent three decades trying to forget and had instead memorized with the terrible fidelity of childhood: the gap in his teeth on the left side, the small scar through one eyebrow, the way his corduroys ended an inch above his ankles as if he'd been growing and someone had forgotten to update him. The freckles. The particular brown of his eyes, warm as the porch light itself.

"Hi, Jonah," Oscar said.

His voice was exactly the same. That was the thing that broke something loose in Jonah's chest. Not a child's voice remembered imperfectly, not an adult's approximation of a child. The same voice. The same slight lisp on the *s*, the same habit of letting the last word of a sentence drop away as if he'd lost interest in it. Jonah had heard that voice every day of the summer they were seven. They had heard it the last morning of that summer, too, standing in the yard at dawn while their mother burned drawings in a coffee can and told them, with a face like a locked door, that Oscar was not real, that Oscar had never been real, that Jonah was a big kid now and big kids put away the things that weren't real.

They had put him away. They had buried him so deep and so thoroughly that by fifteen they could not remember his face, by twenty-five they could not remember his name without looking at the single surviving photograph—a birthday party, a blur in the corner that their mother swore was a smudge on the film—and by thirty they had built a whole life, a whole self, on the foundation of that forgetting. Urban explorer. Documentarian of abandoned places. A person who went into empty houses for a living because, though it had taken them years to admit it, every empty house was a house where nobody was expected home, and therefore no porch light was burning for anyone, and therefore no one was waiting.

"You're not real," Jonah said. It came out steady. They were almost impressed with themselves.

"I know," Oscar said, agreeably, and did not move.

"You died. Or you left. Or—" Jonah's hands had gone cold. They shoved them in their jacket pockets like a teenager. "You were never there to begin with. My mother said—"

"I know what she said." For the first time something moved behind the boy's face—not an expression, exactly, but a depth, like a light passing behind a curtain. "She was scared. People say a lot of things when they're scared. Your grandmother said things too."

The name landed like a stone in still water. "You knew her."

"I lived here," Oscar said, simply, as if that answered it. As if thirty years were a thing a person could live through on a porch step. He looked at the box of ashes beside Jonah's feet. "You brought her home."

"They're her ashes. It's not—" Jonah stopped. Corrected. There was no point correcting a hallucination, and there was even less point correcting whatever this was. "The funeral's Saturday. In Harrisburg. I'm just here to clear out the house."

"I know."

"Of course you know." Jonah picked the ashes back up. The cardboard was warm from the porch light, which was impossible, which was the kind of detail their brain was supplying now, apparently, wholesale, without permission. "You're in my head. Grief does this. I read about it. Grief hallucinations are extremely well documented."

"Sure," Oscar said, in the tone of a child humoring an adult, which was somehow the most frightening thing he had said yet.

Jonah turned to the door. The key was in their pocket—Pell had mailed it, a small brass thing on a paper tag with DELMORE in their grandmother's handwriting, and Jonah had carried it for a week like a stone in their coat. They fit it to the lock. Their hands were shaking now; there was no pretending otherwise. Behind them the boy sat on the step and watched, and did not blink, and the porch light burned down on both of them with the even, unjudging warmth of a light that had been left on for someone expected.

"Jonah," Oscar said, just as the lock gave.

Jonah did not turn around. "What."

"You came back." The voice was soft. Happy, even. That was the worst of it—that underneath the dread, underneath thirty years of careful forgetting, some sealed room in Jonah's chest had opened its door at the sound of that voice and was *glad*, was standing in the light with its hand up, was seven years old and expected. "I knew you'd come back. I waited."

The door swung inward onto darkness and the smell of the house—dust and old paper and, beneath it, that mineral cold, the cellar smell, the town's smell. Jonah stood in the doorway with the ashes in their arms and did not go in and did not turn around.

"How long did you wait, Oscar?"

The pause was one beat too long.

"Since Tuesday," he said.

Jonah went inside and closed the door, and stood with their back against it in the dark, heart slamming, and told themselves the thing they would keep telling themselves for the next three days, right up until the floorboards and the locked tin and the child's drawing of a door took the story out of their hands entirely:

*Grief does this. Grief does this. Grief does this.*

Outside, through the window, the porch light burned on. And on the third step from the bottom, in the spot worn smooth by decades of small bodies, the boy sat in the warm white circle of it and waited, patient as furniture, for someone to be expected home.

* * *

Jonah did not sleep so much as surface, all night, like a swimmer who keeps coming up short of air. The house made its sounds around them—the tick of the water heater, the settle of old wood, the small animal scrabbles in the walls that every old house has and that Jonah's body catalogued and dismissed, catalogued and dismissed, until around three in the morning it occurred to them, lying rigid on the couch their grandmother had reupholstered in 1988, that the sounds in the walls of this particular house had always had a rhythm to them. A hum. Almost a tune.

They had thought about that hum a great deal, as a child. They had never once thought about it as an adult, and the fact that they were thinking about it now, in the dark, in the house, was not an accident, and they knew it, and they got up anyway.

The kitchen was where they'd left it, more or less. The wallpaper with the little yellow birds, faded now to the color of weak butter. The clock on the wall, stopped—Jonah checked—at 4:44, on a day neither the clock nor anyone else would identify. The table with its oilcloth cover, the pattern of strawberries worn to ghosts in the places where elbows had rested for sixty years. Jonah stood in the doorway and let the room do what rooms do to the grieving, which is everything at once. She had made eggs here. She had rolled out pie dough here on flour-dusted newspaper, humming, and the humming had gone *through* the table and *through* the floor and *into* the walls, and little Jonah, sitting at this table with legs that didn't reach the floor, had believed—had known, the way children know things, whole and unarguable—that the house was humming back.

The hum was there now. Faint. Under the refrigerator's drone. Under the wind.

Jonah turned on every light in the kitchen, which helped not at all, and made coffee with the ancient percolator in the cabinet, because ritual is a kind of armor, and sat at the table with the box of ashes in the chair beside them like a guest, and drank coffee at four in the morning, and did not look at the window over the sink.

The window over the sink looked out on the porch.

They looked. Of course they looked. The light was still on. The step was still occupied—the small shape of him, knees together, hands flat, head bowed now as if asleep, or as if listening to something under the porch. Jonah watched for a long time. The shape did not move. The light did not flicker. And slowly, horribly, the cold in Jonah's chest began to change temperature, to warm, to soften into something that felt disgracefully like *relief*—because he was still there, because he had waited, because some seven-year-old archivist deep in them had checked the file and found it intact: *Oscar, on the step, where he always was.*

Jonah put their head down on the oilcloth, on the worn strawberries, and breathed the smell of a dead woman's kitchen, and let themselves, for exactly one minute, be glad.

Then the hum in the walls changed key, and every light in the kitchen went out at once, all of them, the way lights go out in dreams—and in the dark, from the porch, through the glass, clear as a bell, a child's voice said, gently:

"You can come out when you're ready. I'm not going anywhere."

* * *

Morning came gray and honest and put everything back into scale, the way morning does. The porch step was empty. The porch light was off—Jonah checked the fixture and found the bulb burned out, black at the neck, dead for months by the look of it, and stood in the doorway holding a dead lightbulb and feeling the floor of the world tilt a degree or two and settle. The lawn was wet. No small footprints. Of course no footprints. There had never been footprints, their mother had said, there had never been anything, and Jonah had spent thirty years agreeing with her.

They spent the day doing what the executor in them knew how to do: opening rooms, opening drawers, making lists. The house was a museum of a life pared down by decades of a woman quietly outliving everyone—her husband, her son (Jonah's father, vanished 1974, presumed drowned, case closed, *case closed*, said the file Jonah had read at nineteen with their heart going like a drum), her friends. There was very little. Clothes in careful rows. A television with a dial. Photographs in shoeboxes, and Jonah went through them with a kind of professional numbness, setting aside the ones with faces for the funeral, and in none of them—this they noticed only afterward, this they would notice at two in the morning a week from now—was there a single picture of a small brown-haired boy, though there were gaps in the albums, whole pages with the cellophane lifted and the corner-mounts empty, as if something had been removed with great care.

It was under the floorboards in the back bedroom—her room, the room that still smelled faintly of the lavender she'd been dying her whole life to smell like—that Jonah found the tin.

They hadn't been looking for it. They'd been prying up a board to check for wiring, because the outlet in that room had never worked, not in thirty years, not when Jonah was small and had asked why and been told *it just doesn't, leave it be* in a voice that ended conversations. The board came up easier than it should have. Beneath it, in a cavity lined with what looked like wax paper, sat a tin—biscuit tin, blue, the kind that outlives its biscuits by half a century—with a padlock on it. Small. Brass. The kind sold at hardware stores for school lockers.

The padlock was locked. The tin was light. Whatever was inside was paper.

Jonah sat on the floor of their dead grandmother's bedroom with the tin in their lap for a long time. The house ticked and settled. Somewhere below, in the walls, in the floor, in the town's whole limestone under-story, something hummed—or didn't; there was no proving it; there would never be any proving anything in this house again.

They did not open it. Not yet. They turned it over instead, and on the bottom, in their grandmother's hand—in the same firm cursive that had written DELMORE on a paper tag and *Jonah* on a will—was a line, pressed hard enough into the tin's blue paint to emboss it, written by someone who had needed the writing to hold:

*It's not a debt if you never open it.*

Jonah read it three times. Outside the window, the gray afternoon was darkening toward evening, and down the street, in the yard of the Kessler house and the yard of the Pruitt house and the yard of the house that had been the Aldermans', neighbors stood watching the lit windows of the Okonkwo house, motionless, saying nothing, the way they had stood and watched and said nothing for fifty years.

And on the porch step below, in the last of the light, the small shape was back—knees together, hands flat, face turned up toward the window of the back bedroom, patient as furniture, waiting for Jonah to come down.

Jonah put the tin in their bag, zipped it shut, and did not turn on the back bedroom light.

They knew, with the whole unarguable knowing of a seven-year-old, that it was going to be a long night—and that the boy on the steps had not waited since Tuesday at all.
