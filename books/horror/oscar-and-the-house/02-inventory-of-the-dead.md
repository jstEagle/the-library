## 2. Inventory of the Dead

The house smelled the way it always had—cedar and camphor and something underneath both, something older, like the smell of a room that has been closed for a long time even when it hasn't. Jonah stood in the doorway of their grandmother's bedroom with a roll of contractor bags in one hand and a legal pad in the other, and for a full minute they could not make themselves cross the threshold.

Grief, they had learned in the years since their father vanished, was mostly logistics. Someone had to call the county about the death certificate. Someone had to cancel the newspaper subscription that had somehow continued into its fourth decade. Someone had to go through the house of a woman who had lived eighty-one years in the same rooms and decide what of her could be thrown away. The grief itself was a small animal that lived behind the sternum and only came out at night, or at odd moments—when Jonah found a hairpin on the bathroom sill, when they opened the freezer and saw the last of her blackberry jam, labeled in her spidery hand: *JULY. FOR JONAH WHEN THEY COME HOME.*

*When*, not *if*. She had known they would come back eventually. She had probably known it would be for this.

Jonah crossed the threshold.

The bedroom was the hardest room, so they did it first, the way you pull a splinter. Her clothes went to the church donation pile. Her medicines went into a paper sack for disposal. Her jewelry—a modest collection, most of it costume—went into a shoebox marked with a question mark, because Jonah couldn't yet think about who would want what. On the nightstand, a Bible with a ribbon marker at Psalms, and beneath the Bible, a photograph Jonah had never seen before.

It showed the house from the front yard, taken in hard winter light. Every window was dark except the porch light. And standing on the porch steps, slightly blurred as if the camera had caught them mid-motion, was a small figure.

Jonah's hands went cold around the frame.

They had told themselves, all through the drive back and the first sleepless night in the guest room, that they had imagined it. That the figure on the porch had been a trick of dusk and exhaustion, a child-shaped shadow cast by the rail, a memory wearing the present like a mask. Thirty years is long enough to bury anything, and Jonah had buried Oscar deep—under therapy and art school and twelve years of urban exploration that everyone politely declined to connect to the childhood they'd left behind. You don't crawl through abandoned tunnels for a living if your childhood was quiet.

But here was the figure again, in a photograph that predated last night by decades—the film stock yellowed, the porch rail still painted white where now it hung gray. A child in a striped shirt, standing exactly where Jonah had seen him stand, exactly that stillness, that patience, as if he had been waiting on those steps his whole life and the photograph had merely been polite enough to wait too.

Jonah turned the photo over. In pencil, in their grandmother's handwriting: *He waits well.*

They put the photograph face-down in the shoebox with the question mark, and their hands were shaking, and they told themselves it was the cold in the house. The heat had been off for two weeks. That was all. Old houses held their cold like a grudge.

* * *

By noon Jonah had worked through the bedroom, the hall closet, and the bathroom, and had filled nine contractor bags with the ordinary sediment of a life. The neighbors had begun to gather.

That was the thing about Hollow Creek that thirty years hadn't changed and Jonah had forgotten until now: the town watched. Not with hostility, exactly, and not with warmth either. It watched the way the house watched—with a patience that felt structural rather than personal. When Jonah carried the first bags out to the rental car's trunk, Mr. Aldous was standing in his yard across the street, ostensibly examining a fence post. Mrs. Pell from next door sat on her porch with knitting she wasn't knitting, needles stilled, eyes moving. Two kids on bikes drifted past and slowed, and an adult called them home before Jonah could see whether they stared.

Nobody waved. Nobody said *sorry for your loss*, though every single one of them had been at the funeral yesterday, sitting in the back pews, singing the hymns half a beat late, leaving before the benediction finished. Ruth Ellery had been the only one who spoke—Ruth, who had brought a covered dish of chicken and dumplings to the reception and pressed a card into Jonah's hand with her phone number on it and said, quietly, so no one else could hear: "If you find anything in that house you don't understand, you call me before you do anything else. Anything at all."

At the time Jonah had assumed she meant paperwork. Wills, deeds, the tangle of an estate. Now, carrying bag after bag past the silent yards, they began to wonder.

"Morning, Mr. Aldous," Jonah said, on the fourth trip.

Aldous looked at them. He was a big man gone soft, in a canvas jacket, and his face did something complicated—grief, maybe, or guilt, or the particular expression of a man watching someone walk toward a hole he knows is there. "Miss Okonkwo." He didn't say sorry. He looked at the house over Jonah's shoulder, at the dark windows, and then quickly away, the way you look away from an accident. "Your grandmother was a fine woman."

"She was."

"You'll be selling, I expect."

"I don't know yet."

He nodded slowly, twice, as if Jonah had said something else entirely, something in a language he'd been warned about. "Best not to be here after dark," he said, and walked back inside before Jonah could ask what he meant by that, or whether he'd meant anything at all, or whether the whole town spoke in this code and had simply agreed, decades ago, to stop translating for outsiders.

Jonah stood in the yard with a bag of linens in each hand and counted the houses that faced the street. Eleven occupied. Seven with someone visible in a window or doorway, all of them looking, none of them waving. It occurred to Jonah, with a clarity that arrived like cold water, that the neighbors weren't watching the funeral work.

They were watching the house. Watching to see what it did.

* * *

The kitchen took the afternoon. Grandmother's kitchen was the heart of the house and the hardest inventory of all: the cast iron that needed reseasoning, the jars of preserves, the recipe box with its dividers in her handwriting—*CAKES, BREADS, THINGS THAT KEEP*. Jonah found themselves reading every card, unable to throw any away, tasting each instruction in her voice. *Bake till it smells done, not till the timer says so.* At the bottom of the box, behind a divider labeled MISCELLANEOUS, a card with nothing but an address on it—an address in Hollow Creek that didn't exist, a house number higher than the street ran—and below the address, three words: *Ask about 1974.*

Jonah set the card aside with hands that wanted very much to be steady and were not.

1974. The year the father disappeared. The year mother burned the drawings. The year, according to everything Jonah had ever been able to piece together, when the family's history bent like a nail. They had spent their whole adult life treating that year as a wound, and here it was again, surfacing in a recipe box like a body in thawing ground.

They were still holding the card when they noticed the floorboard.

It was the kind of thing you only notice because you've spent twelve years reading buildings—because urban exploration teaches you that structures lie constantly, and the pleasure of the work is catching them at it. The board beside the pantry door sat wrong. Not loose; wrong. Its grain ran the same direction as its neighbors, but its color was a shade deeper, and the gap at its edge was a hair wider, and there—when Jonah crouched and put an eye to it—a scuff pattern in the dust along one side, faint arcs, the marks of something dragged across it many times over many years.

Someone had opened this board often enough to wear a path.

Jonah fetched the flat bar from the trunk of the car. The board came up easier than it should have, nails pulled and reset so many times they'd lost their grip, and underneath was a cavity lined with cedar shavings, and in the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth tied with butcher's twine, a tin.

Not just any tin. Jonah knew it instantly, the way you know a face. A blue biscuit tin, gold lettering worn to ghosts, the same tin that had lived on the top shelf of the pantry for Jonah's entire childhood—the tin mother had once stood on a chair to reach, the tin whose contents Jonah had never once been allowed to see. Mother had searched for it after grandmother moved to the care facility. Jonah remembered the phone call: *If you're ever in that house, look for a blue tin. Don't open it. Just tell me where it is.* And Jonah, twenty-three and tired of the family's locked rooms, had said, *Mom, it's a cookie tin,* and never looked.

Now it sat in their palms, heavier than cookies had any right to be, and the lock on its front was small and brass and real.

The key was taped underneath the lid of the cavity, in an envelope marked, in grandmother's hand, with a single word: *JONAH.*

Their name. Not mother's. Theirs.

Jonah sat down on the kitchen floor, in the cold, in the smell of cedar and camphor, and opened the envelope with fingers that refused to hurry, and inside the envelope was a brass key and a folded note, and the note read:

*It's not a debt if you never open it.*

*I'm sorry, baby. I kept my half. Keep yours.*

*Don't let it talk you into arithmetic.*

Below that, in smaller writing, pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn the paper:

*If Oscar comes back, it's already too late for arithmetic.*

The kitchen was very quiet. The refrigerator, unplugged, ticked as it cooled—or as it warmed, Jonah couldn't have said which direction a dead appliance ticks—and outside, a car passed without slowing, and somewhere down the street a dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

Jonah read the note four times. Then they looked at the tin.

Here is what they should have done: called Ruth Ellery. Called their mother. Put the tin in the trunk and driven to the nearest city and handed it to a lawyer, or a priest, or anyone whose job was to stand between people and the things they bury under their floors. Here is what they actually did, because they were their grandmother's grandchild and their father's child and a person who had spent twelve years opening doors other people sealed: they fitted the key to the lock, and turned it.

The lock gave with a sound like a swallowed word.

Inside the tin, on top, lay a child's drawing. Crayon on construction paper, the waxy strokes of a seven-year-old's sure hand. A house—but not this house, not any house; a house drawn in elevation like a face, with windows like closed eyes and a door that was wrong, drawn too tall and too narrow, black instead of brown, black filled in so hard and so many times the crayon had torn the paper. Above the house, instead of a sun, a circle with lines radiating from it that might have been rays or might have been ribs. And standing in front of the door, holding hands with a stick figure whose hair was drawn in careful black curls, a second stick figure. Small. Striped shirt.

Underneath, in the enormous uneven letters of a child learning to write: *OSCAR SAYS THE DOOR IS HUNGRY.*

Jonah's mouth went dry. They knew the handwriting. Of course they knew it. It was their own.

Beneath the drawing, a ledger page—not the whole book, just a single sheet, old, ruled in faded columns, dense with entries in a hand that was not grandmother's, formal and slanted and patient, the handwriting of something that had all the time in the world. Most of the columns were names. Some of the names Jonah recognized from the cemetery up the hill. Beside each name, numbers. Years, Jonah thought at first. Then, reading closer, with a sensation like the floor tilting: not years. Ages. Ages of entry. And beside some of the ages, a second number, larger, struck through.

And there, near the bottom of the page, one entry circled in grandmother's shaking pen, the ink dug deep:

*OKONKWO, J.—7—*

The rest of the line was torn away. Torn cleanly, deliberately, the way you tear a coupon or a ticket stub. Someone had removed a piece of this page, and the removal had been careful, and the carefulness frightened Jonah more than anything else in the tin, because careless things are accidents and careful things are decisions.

The last item in the tin was a photograph, and Jonah recognized it because they had put it in a shoebox six hours ago: the house in winter, the porch light burning, the small figure on the steps. But this copy was older, less faded, and in this one the figure's face was visible.

It was Oscar. Unchanged. Unaged. Smiling the way he had smiled in 1994 when he'd asked Jonah to come see the door, and Jonah had said no, and mother had burned the drawings that same week, and Oscar had never come again—until last night, on these same steps, waiting exactly as patiently, thirty years later, for an answer to a question a seven-year-old had been wise enough to refuse.

*He waits well,* grandmother had written.

Jonah sat on the cold kitchen floor with the wreckage of their family spread around them—a drawing, a page, a photograph, a note that read like a warning written backward—and understood, finally, in the place where understanding becomes physical, that they had not come home to bury their grandmother.

They had come home to inherit her debt.

Outside, the light was going amber and long. Through the window over the sink, Jonah could see the street, and the street was full. Mr. Aldous. Mrs. Pell. The man from the corner house whose name Jonah had never learned. A dozen neighbors, standing in their yards in the failing light, all facing the house, all perfectly silent, none of them pretending anymore to garden or knit or wait for anything at all.

And on the porch steps, in the last of the sun, sat a small figure in a striped shirt, head tilted, waiting to be noticed, patient as furniture, patient as winter, patient as a debt.

He raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. The way you signal to someone across a crowded room that you can see them seeing you.

Jonah did not wave back. Jonah reached out and, with hands that shook only a little now, picked up the torn ledger page and read the circled entry one more time, and then looked at the space where the missing piece had been, and heard their own voice in memory, high and seven years old, saying the thing mother had burned the drawings to make them forget:

*"Oscar says everybody in the house owes him something."*

Down the street, every porch light in Hollow Creek came on at once.
