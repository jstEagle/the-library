## 9. Wren's First Lie

The rain came at dusk on the fourth day, a thin gray drizzle that turned the lane outside the cottage into a ribbon of pewter. Ines had spent the afternoon with the ledgers spread across her bed like a patient awaiting surgery — four founding surnames cross-referenced against nineteen-year intervals, her own name circled twice in red pencil until the paper had gone soft as cloth. She needed to stop looking at it. She needed to not look at it, and so she looked at it, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

Wren sat on the windowsill with his knees drawn up, watching the rain.

He did that a lot now. Watched things. Rain, birds, the slow rot of an apple in the bowl on the dresser. He watched with the patience of something that had all the time in the world and knew precisely how much of it was left, though he'd never said so. When she'd asked him that morning how long he intended to stay, he'd smiled his crooked smile and said, *As long as you need me,* and the answer had been perfect, and perfect answers were beginning to frighten her more than imperfect ones.

"Wren," she said. "Tell me about the night we met."

He didn't turn from the window. "You know about the night we met."

"I want to hear you say it."

"Again?" A small laugh. "You've heard it a hundred times. You made me tell it when you were eight, when you were nine, when you were twelve and embarrassed of me and wanted proof I was real—"

"You said it rained," she interrupted. "You always said it rained."

"It did."

"It didn't." She stood. The ledger slid off the bed and hit the floor with a slap she didn't flinch from. "I checked. I've been checking everything since I got here, Wren, it's what I *do*, I check things, I put them in order — and I called the meteorological office yesterday because I couldn't sleep, and they read me the records for October 1989. The seventeenth. The night I turned seven. Clear skies. Frost by midnight. Not one drop of rain for two hundred miles."

Now he turned.

His face did something complicated and quick, like weather moving across a field — surprise, then calculation, then a softness laid over both like gauze over a wound. It happened fast. She might have missed it if she hadn't spent three days learning to watch him the way he watched everything else.

"That's strange," he said mildly. "Memory does that. Fills in weather."

"Memory fills in weather," she agreed. "But here's the other thing." She picked up the ledger off the floor, held it against her chest like a shield, which was absurd, because it was paper and he was — whatever he was. "You told me once — only once, I was eleven, we were sitting under the yew tree behind the church, do you remember? — you said you found me at the edge of the stubble field. That I'd wandered out there chasing a hare and you found me crying because it got away."

"Yes."

"The hare." Her voice came out flat and careful, each word set down like a foot on ice. "I don't remember a hare. I've tried. There's no hare anywhere in my memories of that night. But there's this—" She pulled the folded page from between the ledger's leaves. The photocopied parish record, grainy, the ink bleeding. "A harvest account from 1970. Listen to this. '*The child went willingly to the field, as is proper, and the field was glad, and the singing was good.*' Nineteen seventy, Wren. Nineteen years before me. And every account after that, every nineteen years, mentions the same thing — the child goes to the field, and then—" she swallowed, "—and then someone new appears in the village. Someone who wasn't there before. A cousin visiting. A lodger. A friend of a friend who stays."

The rain ticked against the glass. Wren had gone very still, and stillness, she realized, was not natural to him. He fidgeted. He swung his legs. He was made of small motions, always had been, a boy stitched together from restlessness. This stillness was the stillness of a thing holding its shape by force.

"Ines," he said.

"Say I'm wrong," she whispered. "Say it, and mean it, and I'll burn these pages and we'll never speak of it again. Say the hare was real. Say you just found me. Say you're mine."

The silence stretched so long she could hear the house settling around them, the old beams ticking like cooling iron.

"I can't," he said.

* * *

She made him sit down. It felt important, suddenly, that they be sitting down, that this happen across a table like a deposition, like testimony, because she was a folklorist and folklore was just testimony that had survived long enough to be believed, and she needed him to survive this too. She needed it so badly her hands shook as she poured tea neither of them would drink.

"Start at the beginning," she said. "Not my beginning. Yours."

He wrapped his hands around the teacup without lifting it. His knuckles were pale. She noticed, with a distant academic part of her mind that never stopped taking notes even while the rest of her was drowning, that his hands were exactly as they'd been when she was seven — small, chapped, a scab on the left thumb that had never healed or changed. Twenty years and the same scab. Things that were imaginary didn't have scabs. Things that were imaginary didn't need them.

"I was sent," he said.

The word landed in the room like a stone into still water. *Sent.* Not born. Not imagined. Sent.

"By whom?"

"You know who. You've known since you read the first ledger. By the field. By what lives in it — or what lived in it before it learned to live in other things. It doesn't have a name. Names are for things that end." He looked at the tea. "In 1970, the village gave it a child. That was the bargain — one child every nineteen years, and Hallowmere prospers. Good harvests. No sickness. No fires. The founding families signed it in blood and ink and hymn, and the field kept its side, and the village kept its side, and everyone pretended the missing children had gone to kin."

"And you were—"

"I was the child," he said simply. "Thomas Wren. Eight years old. My mother sang the second verse of the Harvesting without knowing what she was singing — Crane's predecessor told her it was a lullaby. They took me out at moonrise. I walked into the field because I was told God wanted it, and the field closed behind me, and I should have ended there." His crooked smile appeared, but wrong this time, worn thin as the gauze. "Should have. But the field doesn't destroy what it takes. It keeps. And keeping isn't the same as using. For nineteen years I was — held. Like a coat in a closet. Like a song nobody sings. And then the debt came due again, and the field reached for the next child, and it reached through me to do it, because I was its hand, its glove, its—"

"Me," Ines said. "It reached for me."

"It sent me to find you. To learn your shape. To make you love the field before you knew what you loved, so that when the time came, you'd walk out willing, the way I did. Willingness matters to it. It can take anyone, but it can only *keep* the willing ones. That's why the bargains. That's why the hymns. Fear runs off a person like rain off oilcloth. Love soaks in." He finally lifted his eyes to hers. "That was my task. That was what I was sent to do."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the drizzle thickened into true rain, drumming on the porch where he'd sat waiting for her twenty years after she'd stopped needing him.

"And instead?" she asked. Her voice was very quiet. "Instead you—"

"I refused."

*Refused.* She held the word up to the light and turned it, looking for cracks, the way she'd been taught to examine any artifact. Refusal implied choice. Choice implied freedom. And nothing about the ledgers, the red lines, the children gone to kin, suggested that anything in Hallowmere's arrangement allowed for freedom.

"How?"

"I don't fully know." He laughed, short and helpless. "You think I haven't turned it over? Nineteen years in the dark of that field, and then I'm poured out onto a lane at dusk wearing a body I barely remembered, handed a task like a knife, and I walk to the edge of the stubble field and there's this little girl crying — not about a hare, that was a lie I gave you later because the truth was worse — crying because her mother had told her that evening that they'd be leaving Hallowmere soon, going away, and the little girl didn't want to go, didn't understand why, could feel that everyone was being kind to her the way you're kind to something you're saying goodbye to. And I was supposed to be kind to her too. That was the whole instruction. Be kind. Be needed. Be loved." His voice cracked on the last word. "And I looked at her, and she looked at me, and she said, 'Will you be my friend?' — and I said yes. And the moment I said yes, I felt the field let go of me. Or lose its grip. Or maybe I tore free, I don't know, I only know that something in me chose her over it, and choosing was the one thing it had never taught me to do, because things that are kept don't choose."

Ines sat with her hands flat on the table, pressing down as if the room might tilt otherwise.

"You lied to me," she said. "For thirteen years. About the rain. About the hare. About—"

"About everything except loving you," he said. "That part was never a lie. It was the only part that was ever true from the first minute."

"And I'm supposed to believe that." She was standing now; she didn't remember standing. "I'm supposed to believe that the thing the ledgers say was given to the field — that the entity's own collateral, its glove, its hand — just *declined*, out of the goodness of its heart, to deliver me? Wren, Marta told me—"

She stopped. Too late. His face changed.

"Marta told you what?" Very softly.

"That it wears the shape you love best," Ines said. "That's how it collects. So tell me why I shouldn't believe that's exactly what you are. A shape. A coat it put on. Something wearing my loneliness like a costume, walking me gently toward the field for nineteen years, humming me lullabies, telling me bedtime stories about refusing — making me love it so thoroughly that when the moon comes full, I'll walk out willing, and it won't even have to sing."

The words hung in the air between them, terrible and precise, and she hated herself for every one of them even as she said them, because some cold scholarly engine in her chest insisted they be said, insisted that doubt was due diligence, that love unexamined was just another kind of ledger entry.

Wren didn't defend himself. That was the worst of it. He sat there with his hands around the cold teacup and his scabbed thumb and his unchanged face, and he absorbed it, and when he finally spoke his voice was steady and low and more adult than she had ever heard it.

"If I'm a costume," he said, "then the thing wearing me has spent twenty years learning to lose gracefully. Because I've watched you grow away from me twice now — once when you were twelve and decided friends weren't real, and once when you left for university and didn't write. If I were it, Ines, I'd have kept you close. I'd have made sure you never left. Instead I let you go, both times, and waited on a porch for twenty years knowing you might never come back, because the alternative was becoming the kind of thing that doesn't wait." He set the cup down. It didn't clink; his hands made no sound at all when they touched things, and she had never noticed that before, and noticing it now nearly broke her. "You can believe whatever the evidence tells you. That's your gift. But ask yourself one question before the moon comes."

"What question?"

"When you were seven, and you said 'will you be my friend' — who do you think taught you to hope for an answer?"

She opened her mouth. Closed it. Because the memory rose unbidden, whole and warm: the lane at dusk, frost coming, a small figure on a fence post swinging his legs, and the feeling — the impossible, foundational feeling — of being *expected*. Of arriving somewhere she'd always been awaited.

"I don't know," she whispered.

"No," he agreed. "Neither do I. That's the honest answer. Maybe I invented myself the moment you believed in me. Maybe the field made me and I betrayed it. Maybe I'm its glove and I've been lying to us both since the beginning. I can't prove any of it, and I won't insult you by trying." He stood, and for a moment the lamplight went through him strangely, like light through smoke, and she couldn't tell if it was the bulb flickering or him. "But there's a procession tonight. I can feel it starting — the way you feel a storm in your teeth. They'll walk to the field and stand facing the treeline, and something will breathe back at them, and they'll call it prayer. If you want to know what I am, don't ask me. Ask it. Go and see what looks back."

He moved toward the window, then paused, his back to her, his patched coat dark against the gray glass.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the rain lie wasn't mine. You told *me* it rained, the next morning, and I let you keep it, because you were seven and you needed the night to have been softer than it was. Every lie I ever told you started as a kindness you asked for first." He glanced back, the crooked smile returning, fragile as frost. "Check that one too, if you like. Check everything. Just — check it before the moon fills, Ines. After that, there won't be time to be sure of anything."

Then he was gone — not dramatically, not in smoke, just absent, the way a note is absent after it ends, the room somehow shaped around the space where he'd been.

Ines stood alone in the lamplight with her scattered papers and her cold tea and her hands that wouldn't stop shaking. Outside, far down the valley, she saw them: figures emerging from doorways in the failing light, moving without lanterns toward the stubble field, silent as a congregation, the founding families going out to meet the dark.

She grabbed her recorder, her coat, and her courage — none of them enough, all of them necessary — and followed.
