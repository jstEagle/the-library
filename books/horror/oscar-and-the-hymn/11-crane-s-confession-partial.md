## 11. Crane's Confession, Partial

I found him because I stopped looking for him.

Three days of doors closing in my face had taught me that much: in Hallowmere, the truth moved like a shy animal, and if you chased it, it went to ground. So on Thursday evening I did nothing. I sat in my room at the Lamb and Wool with my recorder off and my notes face-down, and I watched the light go out of the sky over the churchyard wall, and at half past nine a shape detached itself from the lych-gate and crossed the lane toward the vicarage with the careful, deliberate gait of a man who has been waiting for someone to stop asking.

Reverend Aldous Crane was seventy-eight years old and walked like a man carrying something breakable in each hand. He didn't knock. He stood at the bottom of the stairs and said, "Miss Iversen. You'll want your coat. And you'll want to leave that machine here."

"Then how will I remember what you tell me?"

"You won't need a machine." His eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "What I have to say, you'll remember the way you remember a scar."

* * *

We walked. That was his condition — not the vicarage, not the church, but the lane behind the churchyard that ran along the top of the stubble field, where the wind came off the treeline and there was no one to see us and nowhere for anyone to stand and listen. He carried a flask in his coat pocket and drank from it at intervals with the grim discipline of a man taking medicine he despised. The smell that reached me between gusts was communion wine cut with something cheaper.

"You've been to see Marta," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Marta Kessler sang for me. Once."

"And then she took hold of your wrist like a woman pulling a child back from a well."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed a diagnosis. "Marta has the strongest voice left in this parish. Strongest hands, too, when they're needed." He drank. "You'll have asked her why. She'll not have told you. None of them will tell you. They'll circle it, the way you'd circle a hole in the ice. So it falls to me, because I'm the one who keeps the book, and because—" he laughed, a dry terrible sound, "—because I've been drinking toward this conversation for fifty years."

The field lay below us, silvered by the rising moon, stubble standing in rows like a congregation on its knees. Somewhere out in the middle of it, a single crow shifted on a fencepost and settled again.

"The hymn," Crane said. "The one Marta sang you. What did you make of it?"

"It's beautiful," I said. "Modal, probably pre-Reformation in structure, though the text is odd. It doesn't address God. It addresses — I thought at first it was the land itself. A thanksgiving. 'Come and see what we have kept, come and take your portion—'"

"Stop." His voice cracked like a stick. He steadied himself against the churchyard wall. "Say the next line."

"I don't remember the next line. She only sang it once, and I—"

"Good," he whispered. "God help you, girl, that's good. Don't try to remember it. Whatever you do, whatever it costs you, do not go hunting that tune in your memory. It hunts back."

We stood in silence while he composed himself, or failed to. When he spoke again his voice had dropped into a register I hadn't heard from him before — flat, exhausted, the voice of a man reading out a sentence he'd passed on himself.

"You came here thinking it's a prayer. Everyone thinks it's a prayer. It's got the shape of a prayer — call and response, the verses, the amen. Your whole discipline is built on songs like it, harvest hymns, wassails, the old begging-songs where the singers ask blessing of the field. And so when an outsider hears 'The Harvesting,' she files it where her training tells her to file it." He turned and looked at me, and his eyes in the moonlight were wet and furious. "It is not a prayer, Miss Iversen. A prayer goes up. This one calls across. It is a summons. It has always been a summons. We sing it once, at the turning of the year, to call a thing up out of the earth, and we give it its portion, and it goes back down, and Hallowmere prospers. That is the whole of our harvest festival. That is what the sheaf-figure is for. That is why the founding families stand at the front of the church and never sing."

My mouth had gone dry. "Call what?"

"You know what." He said it gently, horribly. "You've seen the field at night. You've felt it breathe. Don't make me name it. Naming is half of calling, and I've spent my life keeping my mouth shut around that hole in the world."

* * *

He led me further along the wall, to a spot where a stone stile crossed into the churchyard, and there he sat down heavily on the steps as though his legs had finally filed their resignation. The flask came out again. This time he offered it to me. I refused. He looked almost pleased.

"In 1932," he said, "my predecessor's predecessor was a man called Ezra Fenn. By all accounts a fine vicar — devout, beloved, a little too fond of order. And in the autumn of 1932, the singing went wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"There are two verses, Miss Iversen. Have you understood that yet? Two verses, sung in sequence. The first verse calls. The second verse binds — sends the thing back down, seals the bargain, closes the door. Call and dismissal. Open the gate, close the gate. For two hundred years the village sang both verses and the thing came and went like weather." He stared out at the field. "In 1932, they sang the first verse twice."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the night.

"How?" I managed. "How does a village sing the wrong verse twice?"

"Because Ezra Fenn tore the second verse out of the hymnal first." Crane's laugh again, worse than before. "That's the part no one believes. It wasn't accident. It wasn't confusion in the dark. Fenn had decided — from scripture, from principle, from pride, who knows — that the second verse was unchristian. Binding-summons, bargaining with a thing in the ground, giving it a portion — he called it witchcraft wearing a psalm's clothing. So he removed the page. Burned it, most likely. And then Harvest Eve came, and four hundred people opened their mouths and sang what was in the book, and what was in the book was the summons, and when they reached the end and the thing rose up expecting to be sent home—" he spread his hands, empty, "—there was nothing to send it home with. No dismissal. No seal. Just four hundred voices trailing off into silence, and the thing standing in the field, unbound, and every soul in Hallowmere understanding at once, in one long communal heartbeat, exactly what they had done."

"What happened?"

"It stayed." He said it simply. "It couldn't be made to leave, because leaving requires the second verse, and the second verse was ash. But it could be fed. It could be *kept*. Kept things grow quiet. So the founding families made the arrangement — the child, every nineteen years, given to the field — not as the old bargain, mind you. The old bargain was honest commerce: a portion for prosperity, paid and done. What Fenn created was interest. Debt upon debt. Every child since 1932 hasn't been payment. They've been *maintenance*. Keeping the uninvited guest calm in the house it can't be put out of."

I sat down on the stile beside him without deciding to. My legs had opinions of their own.

"The page," I said. "The missing verse. You told me some pages of the parish hymnal were 'on loan.'"

"I lied," Crane said. "Badly, I'm afraid; I'm out of practice. There is no loan. There is a hymnal with a wound in it, and the wound has been bleeding children for ninety years." He drank, long and desperate. "And before you ask me for the verse — because I can see the question assembling itself behind your eyes, folklorist, I can hear you cataloguing — I don't have it. No one living has it. Fenn burned it, and whatever copies existed died with the people who'd have hidden them. The second verse of 'The Harvesting' is gone, and the thing in that field knows it is gone, and it grows less patient every nineteen years."

"Then how—"

"*How do we send it back*" he finished for me, bitterly. "I don't know. I have prayed on it for fifty years and received no answer, which I confess has damaged my faith rather more than the drinking has. Perhaps it cannot be sent back. Perhaps the debt simply deepens until the village drowns in it. Perhaps that is justice." He looked at me sideways, and something in his expression shifted — guilt curdling into fear, or fear into guilt; with him they'd long since become the same color. "But you should know, Miss Iversen, that I did not come to you tonight out of courage. I came because I am a coward who has run out of road. The ledger falls due this autumn. Nineteen years since 1989. And the founding families have already chosen whose name is written in it."

The crow lifted off the fencepost, all at once, without sound, and the field below us seemed to exhale.

"They've chosen," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "Chosen who?"

Crane closed the flask with a small, final click, like a lock going home.

"You know," he said. "You've known since you read the ledgers. You've known since you did the arithmetic on the porch with that — that friend of yours watching you count." He stood, slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and would not look at me. "Your mother tried to stand in your place in 1989. Did you know that? She went down to the field and offered herself, and the field refused her, because the debt wants what it was promised, and what it was promised was an Iversen child born under the harvest moon. She fought it for years after. Prayed, bargained, wrote letters to bishops who wrote back kind useless letters. In the end she did the only thing left to her." 

"What was that?" I whispered.

"She hid you," Crane said. "She took you away and hid you past the reach of the parish, and for twenty years it worked, because a debt deferred is not a debt denied. And now you've come back, Miss Iversen. Of your own free will. Walking in through the front door with your recorder and your questions, right on schedule, nineteen years to the season." He finally turned to face me, and I saw that he was crying without any noise at all, the way old men cry, as if the machinery had worn smooth. "They didn't choose you. You chose yourself. And God forgive me, I let you walk into it because I wanted — I *needed* — someone to know. Someone outside the walls. If it must happen, let it at least be witnessed. Let there be a record."

I stood. The field breathed below us. Somewhere in the dark of it, faint as a memory of a memory, something hummed three bars of a modal melody and stopped.

"Reverend," I said. "If the second verse is gone — truly gone, burned, unrecoverable — then tell me this. Is there anyone else who might have kept it? Anyone who'd have had reason? Fenn burned the hymnal's copy. But a village that sings a song every nineteen years for two hundred years doesn't keep only one copy. Not if it's careful. Not if it's *frightened*."

Crane went very still. The drunkenness seemed to drain out of him all at once, leaving something older and more frightened underneath.

"Why do you ask that?"

"Because Marta Kessler held my wrist hard enough to bruise," I said, "and her hands were strong, and her voice was strong, and she is the last person alive who sang the hymn whole — before the tearing, before the forgetting. And because today I noticed her sewing box has a false bottom, and I noticed that Marta Kessler embroiders everything, Reverend. Everything. Cushions, samplers, altar cloths. A woman like that doesn't trust paper. Paper burns." 

For a long moment Crane said nothing. Then he began, very quietly, to laugh — and it was the worst sound of the whole terrible night, because underneath the laughter was hope, and hope in Hallowmere sounded exactly like grief.

"Fifty years I've kept that book," he said. "Fifty years I've guarded a wound. And the answer may have been sitting in a sewing box in a cottage on Chapel Lane the entire time." He gripped my arm, sudden and fierce, his strength astonishing. "Listen to me. If there is a verse hidden anywhere in this parish, the founding families cannot learn of it. Do you understand? They will burn the cottage, the box, the woman, and you, and they will sleep soundly after. You have perhaps days. Maybe hours. The moon is coming, girl, and it is coming for you."

He released me and walked away up the lane, small and crooked against the moonlight, and just before the dark took him he paused and said, without turning around:

"When you find it — and may God grant you find it — remember what I told you. The first verse calls. The second verse sends home. But a summons answered wrongly is worse than a summons ignored. Whatever the verse says, whoever it names — read it twice before you speak it once."

Then he was gone, and I stood alone on the stile above the breathing field, and in my pocket, unnoticed until that moment, my recorder's little red light was burning — and had been burning, I realized with a chill that started in my scalp and ended in my heels, since before we left the inn.

Every word. I had every word.

And somewhere out in the stubble, very softly, something had begun to hum along.
