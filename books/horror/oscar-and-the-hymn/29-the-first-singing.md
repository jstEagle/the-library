## 29. The First Singing

The moon came up the color of a wound.

Ines watched it from her window at the inn, watching it swell through the bare branches of the elm like something rising through water — orange at first, then paling to bone as it cleared the treeline, until it hung over Hallowmere fat and full and terrible, throwing shadows that were too sharp for midnight. She had not slept. She had not tried. Sleep was a country she'd been exiled from three days ago, when Marta's body was found in the stubble field with frost on her eyelashes and a smile stitched across her face that no dead woman should have been able to make.

On the desk beside her, the tapes stood in their neat rows. Forty-one hours of testimony. The ledgers photographed page by page. Marta's voice, singing *The Harvesting* once, beautifully, and then Marta's voice again, ragged and fierce: *Never twice. Never for anyone.*

And in her fist, wrapped so tightly around the embroidered scrap that the thread had printed itself into her palm, the second verse.

She unfolded it again. She had unfolded it perhaps two hundred times since she'd found it folded inside Marta's sewing box, hidden beneath spools of gray thread and a child's christening gown that had never been worn. The stitches were tiny and sure, worked by hands that must have trembled only at the beginning and end:

*What was called may be un-called.*
*What was welcomed may be turned away.*
*But only by the voice that held the door.*
*Only given. Never taken.*

Below it, in thread the color of dried blood: *the debt-holder sings it twice, and the invited one consents to go.*

Wren sat on the edge of the bed, his patched coat dark in the moonlight, his crooked smile gone somewhere and not coming back. He was fading. She could see the headboard through his shoulder now, a soft blur where there should have been wood grain, and every hour it got worse, the entity drawing him back into itself like breath drawn before a word.

"You should run," he said. Not for the first time.

"No."

"Ines—"

"I said no." Her voice came out flat and hard, a stranger's voice. "You keep saying it like running is a thing I could do. Like it's on the table. They slashed my tires, Wren. They took the distributor cap. Crane told me this morning that if I'm not standing in the field at midnight, they'll come get me, and they'll be gentle about it, because they're always gentle, that's the whole horror of them." She looked at him. His edges shimmered faintly, like heat off summer road. "There's no version of tonight where I'm somewhere else. There's only versions where I'm standing there doing nothing, and versions where I'm not."

He was quiet a while. The house creaked around them the way old houses do, settling, or pretending to settle.

"When you sing it," he said finally, "it will know everything you feel. It wears what you love best. So whatever you do—" his voice caught, an actual catch, a human imperfection she had never heard from him before, "—don't look at me while you sing. Look at the moon. Look at your recorder. Look anywhere but me."

"Why?"

"Because I'm going to be wearing its face," Wren said, "and it's going to be wearing mine, and one of us is going to be lying to you."

* * *

They came for her at half past eleven.

Not violently. That was the thing she would tell the recorder later, her hands shaking too hard to hold it steady, her voice cracking on the words: *they came gently.* A knock at the inn's front door, soft as snowfall. Through the window she saw them gathered in the street under the swollen moon — maybe forty of them, the founding families and those who served them, dressed in their ordinary clothes, their ordinary coats, holding lanterns that threw warm circles of light on faces that were kind and empty as plates.

Edith Marsh stood at the front. The innkeeper's wife was beside her — Ruth, who had sung one song for Ines and gone white at the request for another, who had stepped forward to stand with her in the square, who had wept over Marta's body and then, this morning, had stopped meeting Ines's eyes. Ruth wouldn't look up now either. Her hands were folded in front of her like a schoolgirl's.

"We've come to walk with you," Edith Marsh said. Her voice was mild, reasonable, the voice of a woman discussing a church fete. "It's easier with company."

"Is that what you told the children?" Ines asked. "In 1951? 1970? 1989?" She let the years fall into the street like stones. "Did you walk with them too?"

Something flickered across Edith's face — not guilt. Guilt would have been a mercy. It was impatience, the flicker of a woman delayed over something important. "We walked with them," she said. "We held their hands. We told them stories until the end. You think that counts for nothing, but it counted for something. Ask anyone who remembers." She extended her hand. "Come, Miss Iversen. Your mother walked too, once. Did you know that? She volunteered. It didn't take — the field knows its own debts — but she walked. You come from walking stock."

Ines did not take her hand. But she went.

The procession moved through the village streets without a word, forty pairs of boots on frozen mud, lanterns swaying, breath rising in silver plumes. No one spoke. No one sang. The silence had weight and texture; it pressed against Ines's eardrums like deep water. She thought of the night she'd followed them here weeks ago, when they had stood facing the treeline and something vast had breathed back at them from the dark. She knew now what the breathing was. She knew its name, or near enough — the name the hymn circled and never said, the name the ledger called only *the Tenant*, in a clerk's careful hand, as though it paid rent.

It did pay rent. Nineteen years of harvests at a time. Paid in children.

The field opened ahead of them, black and silver under the moon. The stubble stood in rows like a congregation, brittle and pale, and beyond it the treeline waited, and between the two the air itself seemed thicker, denser, as though the whole valley were a lung holding its breath.

They arranged her at the center. There was no discussion of it; hands simply appeared at her elbows, guiding, and she let them guide because fighting would spend strength she needed for later. The villagers formed a ring around her, wide as a wagon's turning circle, and the founding families — she could see them now, Edith Marsh, the Hollis brothers, old Tam Vane with his ruined hands, Ruth still refusing to look at her — took their places facing inward.

Crane was there. At the ring's far edge, apart from the others, his collar askew, his face gray in the lantern light. He caught her eye across the distance and gave her nothing — no nod, no signal, nothing that any of them could read. But his right hand was closed in a fist at his side, and he held it closed the way a man holds a coal he means to carry somewhere.

*Fifty years,* he'd told her, drunk and weeping in the vestry. *I've kept the last line in my skull for fifty years, waiting. Praying someone would come asking the right questions. Don't waste it. God forgive us all, don't waste it.*

The moon touched its zenith.

Edith Marsh raised her chin, and began to sing.

* * *

The hymn was worse than Ines remembered.

Marta had sung it alone, in a small kitchen, and even alone, even once, it had made the walls seem thinner. Now it rose from forty throats in the open field under the full moon, and it was not a song anymore. It was a mechanism engaging. The melody was simple — four falling phrases, round as a lullaby, the kind of tune a child could hold after one hearing, which was of course the point, which had always been the point — but underneath the simplicity the harmonies ground against each other like millstones, intervals that shouldn't exist sliding against each other, producing a sound that was less heard than felt, a vibration in the teeth, in the sternum, in the wet dark of the inner ear.

*The Harvesting.* The summons. Sung once to call.

Ines stood in the center of the ring with the embroidered verse crushed in her fist and made herself listen, made herself learn it, phrase by phrase, because the second singing required the first, required it complete, required every note of the invitation before the un-inviting could begin. Crane had explained it in the vestry, his breath sour with communion wine: *You can't turn away a guest who hasn't arrived. You have to let them finish knocking. Then you answer the door yourself, and you say what the verse says, and you mean it, and God help you, girl, you have to mean giving him up.*

The first verse ended. The ring breathed. Somewhere behind her, a woman was crying silently, tears running down a face otherwise perfectly composed.

Then the second verse — the summoning verse, the one that remained, the one that had not been torn out because tearing out the binding verse had left the calling verse naked and hungry — and the ground answered.

Ines felt it before she saw it. A shudder through the soles of her boots, deep and slow, rhythmic, and she understood with a clarity that felt like falling: it wasn't trembling. It was swallowing. The earth beneath Hallowmere was opening its throat.

The stubble rows began to move. Not breaking — bowing. Every stalk in the field bent outward from the center, away from her, in a perfect expanding circle, as though a great pressure were rising beneath the soil and pressing the field flat like a hand smoothing a bedsheet. The smell hit her next: loam and iron and something sweeter underneath, something like overripe apples, like the inside of a church, like the particular smell of a room where someone has died and been lovingly tended.

The moonlight bent.

That was the moment her body understood what her mind had refused: the light falling into the circle at the field's center curved, poured inward, draining toward a point in the darkness below her feet, and the darkness there was not shadow but depth, a blackness with texture and appetite, widening like a pupil dilating, like a mouth remembering how to open.

The singing stopped.

The silence afterward rang louder than the hymn. Forty people stood in a ring around a hole in the world, and out of the hole, gentle as fog off a pond, something began to rise.

It didn't rise fast. It rose the way warmth leaves a body — gradually, inevitably, taking the shape of whatever holds it. And the shape it took first was her mother's.

Ines's knees nearly went. Her mother's face, her mother's soft jaw and wind-chapped cheeks, her mother's smile — the real one, the private one, the one from before the tiredness, from before 1989, from before the diary entries that ended with *It keeps him. It calls him Wren too.* The face hovered above the opening, made of dark and moonlight and memory, and it looked at Ines with her mother's eyes and said, in her mother's voice, in a voice that carried to every corner of the field without effort:

"There you are, love. You're late. Supper's gone cold."

*Don't look at me while you sing,* Wren had said. *Look anywhere but me.*

Her mother's face smiled wider. Too wide. The smile kept going past the borders of the face, past anything a face could hold, and the face came apart like smoke and re-formed —

— as Wren.

Seven years old, patched coat, crooked smile, standing at the lip of the opened earth with his hand extended, exactly as he had stood on her porch steps twenty years ago. Exactly. The same. Unchanged. And that was how she knew — the thing wore him unchanged because it had never bothered to look closely, because to it he was collateral, a marker in a drawer, a shape kept in storage. Only Wren himself had ever grown, changed, learned to be almost real.

"It's time to come home," the thing said in Wren's voice, and the voice was perfect, and that was the worst thing yet, that it was perfect, that somewhere in the dark below the field it had kept seventy years of recordings of a stolen boy and could play them back note-perfect.

Ines ran.

She didn't decide to. Her body decided, some animal core of her that had done the math days ago and known there was only one direction left. She broke the ring — hands reached for her, caught at her coat, and she twisted free, and someone gasped, and Ruth's voice cracked across the field, high and broken: *"Let her go — Edith, let her go, for God's sake—"* — and then Ines was through, stumbling over bowed stubble, running for the center, for the mouth, for the place where the moonlight drained into the dark.

The embroidered verse burned in her fist. Behind her the founding families were shouting — Edith's voice rising in outrage, *stop her, stop her, the debt isn't discharged, the debt isn't—* — and ahead of her the earth gaped wide, wide enough now to swallow a cart and horses, wide enough to swallow a childhood, and the cold sweet smell rolled up out of it like breath.

At the rim she stopped. Because down in the dark, at the very edge of what the moonlight could reach, something else was climbing.

Not rising. Climbing — laboriously, hand over hand, the way a boy climbs out of a swimming hole. Patched coat. Crooked smile. Fading, translucent, the moonlight showing clean through his shoulder, but climbing, and looking up at her with eyes that were only his.

"Ines," Wren said, from inside the throat of the thing that had kept him for fifty-four years. "Whatever happens — don't sing to me. Sing to *it*. Promise me you'll know the difference."

Behind her, the founding families were coming. Ahead of her, the dark was waiting. Above them both, the moon stood at its height, full and white and pitiless, counting down the hours until sunrise.

Ines gripped the embroidered verse, planted her feet at the edge of the opened earth, and drew the first breath of the hymn.
