## 31. The Second Singing Begins

The earth had opened like a throat, and Ines ran into it.

She felt the ground give beneath her boots with every stride—not collapsing, not swallowing, but *yielding*, the way a mouth yields around a word it has waited a long time to say. The stubble field that had been flat and silvered under the harvest moon now sloped inward from all sides, a shallow bowl of dark soil breathing warmth against her ankles. Behind her, the founding families' voices rose in the first singing's final cadence, and the hymn came off them like something physical, like rope thrown over a pulley, hauling something up from underneath the world.

Ines did not look back. Looking back was how they got you. Marta had told her that once, weeks ago, in a lucid hour between two cups of tea: *Don't look at it when it comes up, girl. It wears what you love best, and love is heavier than fear. Fear makes you run. Love makes you stay.*

So she kept her eyes on the embroidered scrap of cloth balled in her fist, though she could not read it in the dark—she didn't need to. She had spent the whole of yesterday with it spread across her knees while Wren faded beside her like breath on glass, tracing the stitched letters until the words lived behind her eyelids.

*What was called may be un-called. What was invited may be un-invited. But only by the voice that holds the debt, and only freely given, twice sung, before the sun.*

Twice sung. Once by the village to call it. Once by her—to send it home.

Her lungs burned. The cold air of the small hours tasted of turned earth and old rain. Ahead of her, at the center of the bowl, the soil was moving—not shifting, *moving*, rising in slow ridges like the skin of some enormous animal waking beneath a blanket. And out of that movement came light, a sickly amber glow bleeding up through the cracks between clods, as if the field were lit from below by a hearth no one had tended for ninety years.

*It keeps him,* her mother had written. *It calls him Wren too.*

Ines planted her feet at the center of everything, where the soil was warmest, where the light pooled thickest, and she unwound the embroidery from her fist and held it against her chest like a talisman, which was exactly what it was. Thread instead of ink. A woman's needlework instead of a priest's page. Ninety years hidden in a sewing box because a man named Crane had torn the verse out and an old woman had been brave enough—or frightened enough—to keep it the only way a woman of Hallowmere could keep anything: quietly, in plain sight, stitched into a christening gown no one would ever ask to see.

Behind her, the hymn ended.

The silence that followed was worse than the singing. It was the silence of a held breath, of a trap about to spring, of four hundred people standing at the edge of a field waiting for their prosperity to be delivered. Ines heard the founding families begin to move down into the bowl—their footsteps soft on the yielding earth, unhurried, terrible in their gentleness. They had escorted her here like mourners once. Now they came like ushers showing a guest to her seat.

"Ines." Crane's voice, hoarse and close. He had run after her; she could hear it in his breathing. "Girl, get out of the center. You don't know what you're—"

"I know exactly what I'm doing." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You memorized the last line fifty years ago. You've been waiting for someone brave enough. Well. Here I am."

"That was before—" He stopped. In the amber light she saw his face, drunk-ruddied and wrecked, and behind it something older than drunkenness: the particular exhaustion of a man who has spent his whole life keeping a door shut with his body. "They'll stop you."

"Then they'd better be quick."

Aldrich Iversen reached them first—her cousin, her blood, the man who signed the ledgers now. His face in the unearthly glow was composed, almost kind, which was the most frightening thing about it. "Miss Iversen. You're distressed. Let us take you home."

"I'm not going anywhere."

"You are," he said gently, "going into the ground. That was always the arrangement. Your mother tried to change the terms and it cost her everything. Don't make her mistake twice."

Something in Ines went very still and very cold at those words—*her mistake twice*—and she understood, in a single white flash, that this was the moment. Not when the singing started. Not when the thing rose. This moment, standing in front of the man who thought he owned her death the way his grandfather had owned her grandmother's signature.

"No," she said. "I'm not making her mistake. I'm making mine."

And she began to sing.

* *

The hymn was called 'The Harvesting,' and Ines had heard it exactly once, from Marta Kessler's cracked and beautiful throat, and once was all anyone was ever supposed to hear it. But the melody had lodged itself in her the way a splinter lodges—uncomfortable, impossible to ignore, working its way deeper whenever she pressed at it—and now, opening her mouth in the opened earth, she found the notes rising in her like water finding its level.

Her voice cracked on the second note. Cracked again on the fourth. She was not a singer; she was a folklorist, a collector of other people's songs, a woman who had spent her career holding the recorder toward other mouths. But the verse she sang now had never been recorded, had never been collected, had lived for ninety years in thread and memory, and it needed a voice—even a broken one, even a terrified one—more than it needed a good one.

*What was called may be un-called.*

The effect was immediate and catastrophic.

The amber light stuttered. The breathing earth went rigid beneath her feet, the whole field clenching like a fist. Down at the rim of the bowl, the founding families stopped moving, and then Aldrich Iversen made a sound Ines had never heard a grown man make—a high, thin sound of pure animal alarm—and screamed:

"*Stop her.*"

They came fast then. Hands on her arms, her shoulders, trying to turn her, to shake the song out of her body the way you'd shake water out of your ears. Ines locked her knees and kept singing, let her voice crack and splinter and continue, and discovered something in the cracking: the hymn didn't want polish. The hymn wanted truth. Every fracture in her voice seemed to drive the words deeper into the ground, and the ground shuddered harder with each one.

"She's got the verse!" someone shrieked—a woman's voice, Edith Marsh perhaps, the butcher's wife who had refused to sell Ines bread. "She's got the torn page! Stop her mouth!"

Fingers clawed at her lips. Ines bit them—tasted salt and dirt—and spun away, and Crane was suddenly there, impossibly, his black coat flaring, putting his body between her and the founding families like a door swinging shut.

"Fifty years," he roared at them, and his voice carried over the whole bowl, over the breathing earth, over the gathering dark. "Fifty years I have kept that last line in my skull like a stone, waiting, *waiting*, praying God would send me someone with more spine than the lot of you put together. You will not touch her. You will not touch her, or so help me, I will sing it myself, and I will sing it *wrong*, and none of us will like what comes of that."

The hands fell away. In the amber half-light, Ines saw the founding families' faces—Aldrich, Edith, old Tam Foley, the innkeeper's husband—and saw, beneath their fury, the thing she had been too afraid to hope for: doubt. Ninety years of inherited certainty, cracking along the same fault lines as her voice.

She kept singing.

*What was invited may be un-invited.*

The earth answered her now—not with warmth but with resistance, a deep subsonic groan rolling up through her boot soles and into her teeth, the sound of something vast being asked to do the one thing it had never been asked to do. To leave. To be released. To be *given back*. The light guttered. Somewhere below, in the dark under the dark, Ines felt attention turn toward her—an attention so old and so vast that her mind flinched from it like a hand from a stove.

*Only by the voice that holds the debt.*

That was the part that had nearly broken her, alone in Marta's kitchen with the embroidery spread out. Because she hadn't invited it. She hadn't been born in 1932. The debt wasn't hers—it belonged to the founding families, to the signers of the ledgers, to the throats that had sung the first singing tonight and every nineteen years before.

But then she had understood, in the way you understand things at three in the morning with a fading boy beside you: the debt wasn't the invitation. The debt was what the invitation had *bought*. And the collateral—the child taken in 1970, kept young and shapeless in the field's keeping, given her name to wear and her childhood to remember—was hers. Had always been hers. Wren was the debt, and the debt was Wren, and the voice that held him had been hers since she was seven years old and invented a friend out of loneliness and called him into being without knowing what she was calling.

She held the debt. She had held it for twenty years.

*And only freely given.*

Her voice broke completely on that line, and she let it break, and stood in the ruined silence of her own singing with tears freezing on her cheeks, and drew breath, and began again.

* *

"Stop. *Stop.*"

Aldrich again, but the composure was gone now, stripped away, and what was underneath was not anger. It was terror—the specific terror of a man watching ninety years of careful bookkeeping catch fire. "Do you know what happens if you finish? Do you know what happens to Hallowmere? The blight comes back. The wells go sour. The children starve like they starved in '31. We did what we had to do—"

"You fed children to a hole in the ground," Ines said, and her voice, cracked as it was, cut through the bowl like wire. "For wheat prices. For good harvests. For *comfort*. Don't you dare dress it as necessity. I've read the ledgers. I've recorded the testimonies. There are tapes, Aldrich. Dozens of them. Hidden where you'll never find them. Whatever happens tonight, the world is going to know what Hallowmere is."

"It won't matter if we're all dead."

"Then we'll be dead *free*."

She lifted the embroidery above her head so they could all see it—the little stitched verse glowing faintly amber in the field's light, a dead woman's needlework raised like a standard—and she sang the next line louder than she had ever sung anything in her life.

The ground convulsed.

Not the slow breathing rise of before—this was violence, the whole bowl of the field bucking like a struck animal, and the villagers at the rim scattered screaming, and Crane staggered into Ines and caught her arm as she nearly went down. Through the splitting soil the amber light poured upward in columns now, and the heat of it hit her face like an open oven, and out of the heat came a sound that was almost a voice: low, vast, wounded. The sound of something that had been kept for ninety years hearing, for the first time, the word *home*.

"It's working," Crane breathed. His grip on her arm was trembling. "God help us, girl, it's actually—"

Then the light shaped itself.

Ines watched it happen and could not look away, Marta's warning be damned. The amber columns wove together, thickened, took on weight and outline—and became a figure standing ten feet away in the ruined center of the field. Small. Thin. Wearing a patched coat with the elbow worn through, and a crooked smile that did not reach its eyes.

Wren's face looked at her out of the thing that had kept him for seventy years.

"Ines," it said, and it was his voice, perfectly, exactly, down to the slight lilt he put on her name. "You came back. You always come back."

Her heart tore straight down the middle. The hymn died in her throat.

Behind her, at the rim of the bowl, the founding families had gone silent, watching. Above her, the sky was beginning—if she was reading the stars right, and she was—its long, slow turn toward gray. Hours before dawn. Maybe less.

The thing wearing Wren's face tilted its head, and its smile widened just slightly, and it said, gently, the way you talk someone down off a ledge:

"Don't finish the song."
