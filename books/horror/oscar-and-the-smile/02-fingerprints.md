## 2. Fingerprints

The pantry smelled of flour and old wood and something underneath both—something mineral, like the air over a deep hole in summer. Edmund Joubert stood in the doorway with her flashlight off for a full ten seconds before she turned it on, because she had learned that the first light you cast into a room was a kind of question, and it was worth deciding what you were going to ask.

The Holloway pantry was a narrow room off the kitchen, shelved floor to ceiling on three walls, jars of preserves ranked in amber rows, sacks of potatoes breathing softly in the corner. The fourth wall held a window the size of a shoebox, its glass fogged with condensation on the inside, though it was October and cold enough outside to make your knuckles ache. On the counter beneath the window sat a wooden trough of flour, half full, dusted smooth as fresh snow.

Someone had pressed their hand into it.

Edmund crouched. She did not touch anything yet. First came the looking; the touching could wait until the looking had finished telling her everything it intended to tell. This was the discipline her father had taught her before he'd taught her anything else about pests: *the animal is always more honest than the people who called you.* A rat leaves a story in grease and droppings and gnawed wood, and the story never lies, even when the farmer does.

She took out her phone and photographed the print from four angles, then set a coin beside it—a quarter, standard scale—and photographed again. The flash made the flour glow like bone.

Then she let herself look properly, without the phone, and felt the first hairline fracture run through the morning.

The print was human-shaped. That was the thing she would return to later, turning it over in her mind like a stone with an odd weight: not the size, but the shape. Five fingers. An opposable thumb, splayed at the angle a human thumb splays when a palm presses flat and deliberate. The whorls of the fingerprint itself had even transferred, faint ridges visible where the flour had settled into them, fine as the grain on a moth's wing.

But the hand was eight inches long. She measured it twice with her tape, once from wrist-crease to middle fingertip and once along the outer edge, because her hands were shaking slightly and she wanted to be sure the number wasn't a product of her own pulse. Eight inches. Her own hand, spread flat against the counter beside it for comparison, looked like a child's mitten next to its mother's glove.

And there were no calluses. Every working hand she had ever photographed—the farmers, the slaughterhouse men, the one woman who ran the orchard outside Harrowgate—carried its history in callus: thickened pads at the base of the fingers, horned ridges along the palm. This hand was smooth. It had never gripped a tool or a rope or another person's collar. It had never done work. It had only ever touched.

*Too large to be human,* she wrote in her notebook, standing there in the cold pantry with her breath ghosting. *Too gentle to be hostile.*

She underlined it, which she almost never did, and hated herself a little for underlining it, because underlining was what amateurs did when they wanted a sentence to be true.

* * *

She found the others over the next hour, and each one rearranged the picture slightly, the way individual tiles begin to suggest a mosaic.

There was a second print in the flour, overlapping the first, as if the hand had pressed down, lifted, and pressed again a few inches away—testing, or playing, or simply taking its time. There was a smear along the doorframe of the pantry at a height of six feet nine inches, a greasy gray streak exactly like the ones rats leave along their runs, except that rats do not travel at six feet nine, and the smear was not random. It was a single clean stroke, the width of a forearm, laid down with something that looked almost like care.

In the cellar, on the underside of the stairs, she found prints in the fine coal dust that had sifted down through the floorboards of some earlier decade. Five of them, descending. Each one complete. Each one placed with the spacing of a man walking slowly downstairs in no particular hurry, except that the stride between prints was fifty-two inches, and the last print was at the bottom of the stairs, facing the dark of the cellar, and there were no prints coming back up.

Edmund stood at the top of those stairs for a long moment, listening to the house breathe around her. Old houses breathed; she knew this; it was settling timber and shifting joists and the wind finding gaps. But this house breathed differently. It breathed the way a sleeping animal breathes when it is aware of you and has decided, provisionally, not to mind.

She photographed the descending prints. She logged the time: 11:47 a.m. She wrote *no return prints* and then sat down on the third step from the bottom, which was unlike her, and put her face in her hands for perhaps forty seconds, which was very unlike her.

Then she got up and went back to work, because work was the shape her fear took, and she had learned long ago that fear needed a shape or it would fill whatever container you gave it.

* * *

Aldous Holloway was in the farmyard when she came out, splitting kindling with an economy of motion that suggested sixty years of practice. He was a tall man gone stooped, built like a fence post left too long in weather, with a face that had been handsome once and had since been repurposed entirely for endurance. He didn't look up when she crossed the yard. He split another length of oak. The axe went through the wood with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"Mr. Holloway," Edmund said. "I need to ask you about the pantry."

"Flour," he said. "Mara bakes. You'll have seen the flour."

"I saw the flour." She kept her voice level, professional, the voice she used for men who wanted to tell her about their wives' opinions before they'd tell her about their rats. "Someone's been putting their hands in it."

The axe paused—not stopped, paused, the way a clock pauses between ticks—and then came down again. The split halves jumped apart on the block.

"Mice," Aldous said.

"No, sir."

"Cats, then."

"Mr. Holloway." She took out her phone and opened the photograph and held it toward him, screen out, the way you'd hold a lantern toward a thing in the dark to see whether it flinched. "That's a human hand. It's eight inches long. It has fingerprints. Mice don't have fingerprints."

He looked. She watched him look, and what she saw was not surprise. That was the detail she would write down that night, sitting in her car with the engine running and the heater ticking: *he was not surprised.* He looked at the photograph the way a man looks at a bill he has been expecting—wearily, resignedly, with his jaw set against the amount.

"That camera of yours," Aldous said. "It sees a lot."

"It does its job."

"So does mine." He set the axe against the block and finally turned to face her fully, and up close his eyes were pale gray, the color of the sky over water, and they were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "You'll want to finish your survey, I expect."

"I'd like you to tell me what's making these prints."

"I expect you would." He picked up the split kindling, gathered it against his chest with both arms, and walked past her toward the house. At the door he stopped, without turning around, and said: "You'll find them in the cellar too, I shouldn't wonder. And the barn loft. And once, years back, in the flour bin my mother kept, same as now. Same as always." He shifted the wood in his arms. "It keeps its hands clean. That's more than most can say."

And he went inside, and the screen door clapped shut behind him, and Edmund stood alone in the yard with her phone still lit in her hand, showing a photograph of a hand that could not exist, pressed into flour by something that had apparently been doing so since before Aldous Holloway's mother kept a flour bin.

* * *

She worked the rest of the property because the work was there and because her hands needed tasks. She checked bait stations along the foundation—untouched, every one, which was itself wrong, because a farm like this should have been running with mice by October. She inspected the barn loft and found hay arranged in a spiral, a slow careful spiral six feet across, as if something had been walking in circles in the dark for its own reasons. She found grease smears at shoulder height on the outside wall of the house, below the eaves, where nothing should have been traveling at all, and the smears did not lead anywhere. They began nowhere and ended nowhere. They were just strokes, laid on the clapboard like brushwork, like someone practicing a signature.

At two o'clock she ate the sandwich she'd brought and sat on her car's hood in the yard and let herself think the thoughts she'd been holding at arm's reach all morning.

The first thought was: *this is not a pest problem.*

The second thought was: *this is not my problem.*

The third thought was the dangerous one, and it arrived quietly, the way the truest ones do: *but nobody else is going to look.*

Her sister had been dead eleven years. People had looked away from that too—doctors who had signed forms, neighbors who had heard things through walls and decided the walls were none of their business, a whole town performing the small daily miracle of not seeing. Edmund had been twenty-three, and she had responded the only way her particular mind knew how: she had documented. She had written down everything—the dates, the words said at the hospital, the exact quality of the silence in her sister's apartment afterward, the smell of the coat she couldn't bring herself to give away. Four notebooks' worth. Because if it was written down, it lived on the page and not inside her. Because a fact that is recorded can be set down, and a thing that is set down can be walked away from.

She had told herself for eleven years that this was grief management. Sitting on the hood of her car in the Holloway yard, with a photograph of an impossible hand glowing on her phone, she allowed herself to consider, for the first time, that it might also be a hunger. That she went toward the unexplained the way other people went toward churches. That the notebooks were not armor but prayer—that writing *it happened, it happened, it happened* was her version of kneeling.

"Get a grip, Joubert," she said aloud, to the yard, to the crows strung along the fence wire like notes on a staff.

The crows did not answer. But somewhere inside the house—or inside the walls of the house, which was not quite the same thing—something shifted its weight, slow and vast and unhurried, and a board sighed, and Edmund felt the sound pass through the seat of the car and up through her spine like a note played on a string she hadn't known she was.

* * *

She went back into the pantry one last time before leaving, because she wanted a final set of photographs in the afternoon light, and because—she admitted this to herself with clinical honesty—she wanted to see the print again.

The flour had changed.

Not much. Not dramatically. The two great prints were still there, side by side, unmistakable. But between them, in the space a smaller hand might occupy, a new mark had appeared: a single fingertip, drawn through the flour in a short arc. One stroke. Deliberate. The way you might tap the page of a book to show someone where to read.

Edmund stood very still. The pantry was cold. Through the little fogged window, the October light came down thin and colorless, and the dust motes hung in it without moving, as if the air itself were holding its breath.

She raised her phone and photographed the arc. Then, because her hands were steadier than she expected them to be, she took out her notebook, dated the page—October 14th—and wrote:

*Second visit to pantry, 2:40 p.m. New mark in flour: single finger-draw, approx. 4 inches, curved. Appears to have been made between 11:50 and 14:40 while premises occupied by E.J. (myself) and A. Holloway. No one entered pantry in that period. I did not make this mark. Conclusion: subject active during daylight hours, aware of observation, responsive to it.*

She stared at the word *responsive*. Then she added one more line, in smaller handwriting, the handwriting she used for things she wasn't sure she wanted future-Edmund to read:

*It waited until I was watching.*

Outside, a car door closed. Footsteps on gravel—light, quick, a young person's walk. Through the pantry's little window she saw a boy crossing the yard toward the house: fifteen or sixteen, all elbows and dark hair, a school bag slung over one shoulder. Wren, presumably. The son. He reached the steps, and then he stopped, and turned, and looked directly at the pantry window—at her, though the glass was fogged and the light was wrong and there was no way on earth he could have known she was standing there.

He looked at her for three seconds. His expression was not fear. It was recognition, and pity, and something older than either—the look you'd give someone standing on a railway line who hadn't yet heard the train.

Then he went inside, and Edmund Joubert closed her notebook, and understood that she had just logged the date on something that had already begun logging her.
