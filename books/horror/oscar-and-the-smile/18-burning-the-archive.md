## 18. Burning the Archive

The handwriting was mine.

That was the thing I kept returning to as I sat on the edge of my bed in the Holloway guest room with the ledger open across my knees, the dawn not yet arrived, the house holding its breath around me in the particular way it had learned to hold its breath. The ink was mine. The slight forward slant, the way I crossed my t's with a single impatient stroke, the little hook I put on the descender of a g because a schoolteacher in the fourth grade had told me my handwriting looked like it was trying to escape the page. All of it, reproduced perfectly. Better than perfectly. The way a mirror reproduces you, without mercy.

*Edmund Joubert,* it said. *Tomorrow.*

And beneath my name, in the column I had come to think of as the column of surrender, the column labeled with no label at all but understood by every Holloway for a hundred and thirteen years: a number. Nine. The same number of days that had stood in the ledger's refill count when I'd first read it, as if the two counts had been running in parallel all along, as if my days and the well's days had been converging toward a single date the way two lines converge toward a vanishing point.

I had documented everything. That had been the whole architecture of my survival, laid down brick by brick in the weeks after my sister died, when I discovered that if I wrote a thing down—*the hospital smelled of oranges and bleach; her hands were cold by four o'clock*—it moved out of me and onto the page and stopped circling my skull at three in the morning like a moth against a bulb. Documentation as exorcism. Documentation as prayer. I had believed, with the simple fervor of a convert, that the notebook was a container and I was safe outside it.

But a ledger is also a signature. A prayer is also an address. And I had spent three weeks writing my way deeper into this house, page by page, date by date, in handwriting so distinctive that something in the walls had learned it the way you learn a song you can't stop hearing.

It hadn't broken in. I had held the door.

I closed the ledger. The sound of it was very small and very final, like a coffin lid in miniature, and somewhere inside the wall above the headboard—so close I felt it through the plaster, through the slats, through the old horsehair insulation that had been there since before the war—something exhaled.

Not a breath. A satisfaction.

I dressed in the dark. I took the ledger and my notebooks—eleven of them now, the first begun the night I'd arrived and found the droppings that weren't droppings, the grease smear at the shoulder height of something very tall—and I carried them downstairs past the kitchen where the clock said 4:17, past the pantry where the flour still held the prints, eight inches long, five fingers, no calluses, pressed into the soft white surface like a hand learning the shape of a hand. I did not look at the flour. I looked at the door.

The yard was the color of iron. Frost had come in the night, the first of the season, and it had done something merciful to the farm, silvering the dead garden, the sagging fence, the well house with its padlocked door I had broken open nine days ago and never repaired. The grass crunched under my boots with a sound like small bones. My breath hung in front of me and did not disperse, and for a moment I watched it—my own breath, my own proof of warmth, of aliveness, of a body that was still only mine—and then I walked to the center of the yard, where the frost was thinnest, where the ground rose slightly as if the earth itself had been trying over the decades to keep its distance from the well house, and I set the ledger and the notebooks down in a pile.

I had brought the can of kerosene from the barn. I had brought matches. I had not decided, until my knees hit the frozen ground and my hands began to move, that I was actually going to do it.

Here is what no one tells you about burning your own archive: it is not like burning a letter from an enemy, or a photograph of someone who hurt you. Those burnings are easy, theatrical, the stuff of movies. This is different. This is burning the externalized self. Every page in those eleven notebooks was a piece of me that I had amputated on purpose so that the rest of me could keep walking. The entry from the second night—*droppings too large, too regular, almost placed*—that was a piece of me. The transcript of the recording, transcribed by hand at two in the morning with my hands shaking so badly I'd had to press the paper flat with my elbow—the moment the second voice said *Cordelia*, my sister's name, in a voice low and patient and impossibly gentle—that was a piece of me. Nine questions and nine answers, my own voice on the tape saying *yes, I think being remembered is a kind of love, but it's a poor substitute, it's what you settle for when the person is gone*—that was the truest thing I had ever said aloud in my life, and I had only been able to say it to a darkness that asked, and now I was going to burn it.

My hand hovered over the kerosene.

The house made a sound I had not heard before. Not the settling groan of old timber. Not the wet shifting in the wall cavities I'd been mapping for weeks. This was higher, thinner, a keening that seemed to come from everywhere at once—from the eaves, from the chimney, from the ground beneath the frost—and it took me a long moment, kneeling there with the can in my hands, to understand what I was hearing.

It was the sound of something reading over my shoulder and realizing the book was about to close.

* * *

I poured the kerosene slowly, the way you pour a libation, the way you pour out something that has been carried too long. The liquid darkened the frost in a widening bloom. The pages of the ledger, at the bottom of the pile, drank it in and went translucent, and through the translucence I could see the columns—names, dates, offerings, the long unbroken descent of *DAYS TO REFILL*—and I made myself look at each one. I owed them that. A hundred and thirteen years of Holloways who had done the arithmetic of survival, generation after generation, each of them writing down the count the way I wrote down everything, because writing it down was the only way to keep it outside yourself, and the count had never once gone to zero. Not in 1911, when they had given the farm its covenant in a famine winter. Not in the dust years. Not in '87, when the cousin ran and learned what following meant. Every entry in that ledger was a person who had chosen, in the only way available to them, to keep the thing fed and the family alive, and I was not going to pretend, kneeling there in the iron-colored dawn, that my act was braver than theirs. It was only different. They had burned themselves slowly, a page a year. I was going to do it all at once.

I struck the match.

The flame stood in the still air, small and absolute, and for one heartbeat I hesitated—not from fear, but from something I can only call homesickness, a sudden grief for the eleven notebooks as if they were eleven rooms in a house I had built and lived in alone, as if I were about to watch my own history go up in a light that would leave me standing in an empty field with nothing between me and the dark.

Then I thought of the entry in my handwriting. *Edmund Joubert. Tomorrow.* And I dropped the match.

The fire took the kerosene first, a soft whump of blue and orange, and then it took the notebooks, and then the ledger, and I stayed on my knees and I watched. I watched the cover of Notebook One curl and blacken, the cover I had labeled in block letters, *HOLLOWAY FARM — PEST ASSESSMENT*, the professional fiction I had maintained for exactly four days. I watched the pages of the ninth notebook, the one with the nine questions, flare and lift and go up as sparks, whole sentences rising into the dark sky still legible for a fraction of a second—*do you think it hurts, to be remembered by someone who's afraid of you*—before the heat ate the words and the wind took the ash. I watched the ledger burn last, because it was thickest, because a hundred and thirteen years of ink does not surrender quickly, and as it burned the names came apart one by one—Aldous, Aldous, Aldous, a name handed down like a duty, Mara, Wren, and all the others, the ones from 1911, the ones from before my grandmother was born—and I found that I was saying them out loud as they went. Not reading. Witnessing. There is a difference, and the difference is that a reading is for yourself and a witnessing is for them.

The house was screaming.

I don't use that word lightly. I have spent three weeks in this house learning its vocabulary, the groans and ticks and wet movements, and I know its range the way a farmer knows the range of a particular animal. This was outside the range. It came through the walls the way sound comes through the walls of an instrument—amplified, resonant, the whole structure of the farmhouse acting as a body for a voice that had no throat of its own—and it was not the low patient voice from the recordings. It was high and ragged and it rose and fell and rose again, and the frost on the windows of the kitchen shivered loose and slid down the glass in sheets, and the horses—I had forgotten the Holloways kept horses, two of them, out in the far paddock—began to scream back, and for nearly a minute the whole farm was one animal in pain.

And I sat in the middle of it, on my knees in the frost, and I did not stop burning the pages. I fed them in, notebook by notebook, because I understood now—understood it in my sternum, in the place where the enormous gentle fingers had rested two nights ago while the recorder ran and the voice said *you document because no one documented you*—I understood that the archive was not my armor. It was my tether. Every page I had written was a thread, and the thing in the walls had been gathering the threads, winding them, and the ledger entry in my handwriting was simply the knot it had been tying at the end. Burn the archive, break the threads. Simple. The way amputation is simple.

The fire burned down to a gray bloom of ash, and the ash was warm through my gloves when I finally reached out and touched it, and the house went quiet—not calm, I want to be clear about that, not calm, but quiet the way a person goes quiet after screaming, emptied, spent, listening. The frost was melting around me in a dark circle. The horses had stopped. Somewhere in the walls, something was moving very fast, very agitated, from the kitchen wall to the pantry wall to the stairwell wall, a circuit, over and over, like a creature looking for a door that used to be there.

I had kept one page.

I had torn it out the night before, after finding the entry, after sitting with it until the light came gray through the curtains. It was the page from Notebook Seven, the one where I had written, in the plainest language I could manage, the plan: *All four of them leave together. Split the memory. No single bearer.* I had hidden it against my skin, folded small inside the waistband at the small of my back, and it was warm now from my body, warm the way the hidden page in the epilogue of every horror story is warm, and I was not so foolish as to think that keeping it was anything other than a contradiction. You cannot burn the archive and keep a page. You cannot break a covenant and keep a clause. But the plan was the only thing I had that was not a prayer and not a signature—it was strategy, it was arithmetic, it was the cold professional competence I had worn like armor since before this farm, since before Cordelia, since I was a girl who learned that if you write it down it stays outside you—and I was not ready to be a person with no armor and no plan and nothing between my skin and the dark. Not yet. Maybe not ever. Maybe that was the real lesson of the ledger: that everyone keeps one page.

The kitchen door opened behind me.

I did not turn around. I knew the weight of the footsteps. Aldous Holloway walked the way his father had walked and his grandfather before him, a slow deliberate tread that seemed to apologize to the ground for disturbing it, and I heard him come down the three steps into the yard and stop at the edge of the ash circle, and I heard the long silence in which he took it in: the burned notebooks, the ledger's spine still smoldering like a black rib, the melted frost, his farm's hundred-and-thirteen-year covenant curling upward as smoke.

"The horses woke us," he said.

"I know."

"Mara's at the window. She won't come out. She's been at the window since the screaming started." A pause. "Wren's with her. He's holding her hand. He's never done that. He's sixteen and he's never once held her hand."

I turned around then. Aldous was standing in his coat with no hat, his gray hair stiff with frost, and his face in the pre-dawn light was the face of a man watching a structure come down that he had spent his whole life shoring up—not with grief, exactly, and not with relief, but with the terrible vertigo of a load suddenly removed, the way a wall feels when you take out the beam it's been leaning on. He looked at the ash. He looked at me kneeling in it.

"You understand what you've done," he said. It was not a question and not an accusation. It was the voice of a man confirming the depth of a hole before deciding whether to climb down into it.

"I understand that it wrote my name in your ledger," I said. "In my handwriting. Dated tomorrow. I understand that every page I wrote was a page it read, and that I didn't document my way to safety, I documented my way into its hands. I understand that your family has spent a century feeding this thing its own history one entry at a time, and I did it in three weeks because I write faster." My voice cracked on the last word and I let it. "I understand that the archive was never a prayer. It was a signature. And I've burned my signature, Aldous. All of it. Except one page, and the one page is the plan, and the plan is that all of you leave this farm with me, today, before the count runs out, and we split what we know between us so that no one person carries the whole of it—because the thing follows the memory, and the memory is only dangerous when it's whole."

He was quiet for a long time. The sky behind him had begun, very faintly, to lighten—not dawn yet, but the promise of it, a gray seam along the tree line. In the walls of the house the frantic circuit continued, kitchen to pantry to stairwell, kitchen to pantry to stairwell, faster now, and I thought: it is looking for the notebooks. It is looking for its library. For a hundred and thirteen years it has had a family that fed it, and for three weeks it has had something better—a reader, a writer, someone who answered its questions honestly on tape—and I have taken the books away and it does not know yet what it has left.

"I can't leave the land," Aldous said, and it was the same sentence he had given me three days ago, the same flat finality, the land is his duty, the land is the covenant, a Holloway does not leave a Holloway farm. But then he looked at me again—at the ash on my knees, at my hands, which I saw now were burned, two small blisters rising on the fingers that had fed the fire, blisters I had not felt until this moment—and something moved through his face that I had not seen in him before. Not agreement. Not surrender. Recognition.

"Three weeks," he said quietly. "You've been here three weeks, and it wrote your name in the ledger, and it touched you in the night, and it asked you nine questions, and you burned eleven books this morning while the house screamed, and you're still kneeling in the frost with burned hands and a plan." He shook his head, slowly, the way a man shakes his head at a price he cannot pay. "My father went down to that well every year of his life and it never once wrote his name. It kept him. The way you keep a tool. But you—" He stopped. Started again. "It wants you. Not fed. *Kept.* And I have spent sixty-one years telling myself that keeping it fed was the same as keeping my family safe, and watching you kneel in that ash, I finally see the difference."

"Aldous—"

"It only counts what you give it," he said. "My father's words. And you gave it everything, and now it's counted you, and there's no ledger left to uncount you in." He looked at the house—at the windows where his wife stood behind the frost, at the boy's silhouette beside her—and when he spoke again his voice was so low I had to lean forward through the ash-smell to hear it. "I'll leave the land. Not for me. A Holloway doesn't leave for himself. But for you—" and here his voice did something I will remember for the rest of my life, which is break, "—for you, because I have watched that archive eat you the way it ate my father, one page a year, and I have never once in my life seen anyone burn it back."

Behind him, inside the house, the frantic circuit through the walls stopped.

All at once. The way a clock stops. And in the silence that followed, in the gray seam of almost-dawn, every window on the ground floor of the Holloway farmhouse fogged from the inside, and into the fog of each one, pressed from within, gentle and enormous, five-fingered, unhurried, came a single handprint—eight inches long, no calluses—arranged on the glass like a wave goodbye.

Or like a promise to follow.

I stood up. My knees were soaked. The page against the small of my back was warm, and getting warmer, and I put my burned hand into Aldous's unburned one and said the only thing left to say.

"Go wake your family. We leave at dawn."

"It is dawn," Aldous said.

"Then we leave now."
