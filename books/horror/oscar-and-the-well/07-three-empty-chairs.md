## 7. Three Empty Chairs

The morning the pattern completed, Mirabel woke before her alarm to a sound she had stopped pretending was pipes.

It came up through the floor of her room above the school — a low, wet hum, like a finger circling the rim of a glass that had been filled with something thicker than water. She lay in the dark and listened, and for the first time since arriving in Hollowmere, she did not reach for an explanation. No pipes ran under this building. Idris had shown her the plans. The sound rose anyway, patient as tide, and beneath it — fainter, woven into it — a melody she knew.

She sat up. The humming stopped, as if it had only needed her attention.

Her room was cold in a way October didn't explain. On the windowpane, condensation had gathered on the *inside* of the glass, and someone — something — had drawn a single line through it with a fingertip, top to bottom, the way a child tests whether a surface is real.

Mirabel dressed quickly. She told herself it was the hour, the season, the strangeness of a new town settling over her like ill-fitting clothes. But her hands were steady in the way hands are steady when they've already accepted something the mind hasn't caught up to.

She was at her desk by six, taking attendance for a class that wouldn't arrive for two hours, because the ledger was the one thing in Hollowmere that behaved.

*Ashford, Wren. Present.*

*Bexley, Tom. Present.*

*Calloway, Oscar. Present.*

She wrote the names down the column, and her pen slowed as she reached the middle of the list, where three names now sat in a row like missing teeth.

*Dunmore—* no. That wasn't right. She crossed out nothing; there was nothing to cross out. The names were: *Harrow, Silas. Kell, Maren. Voss, Edmund.*

Three children absent since September. No transfer papers. No withdrawal notices. No forwarding addresses. When she'd asked the office, the secretary had looked at her the way people look at a stain they'd hoped no one noticed, and said the families were "between addresses." Between addresses, for six weeks. Three children between addresses, and no one in town seemed to find it strange except her.

And now, as she copied the roll into her grade book, she counted the empty desks in her classroom and found there were four.

Four chairs pushed in neatly, dust on three of them from weeks of disuse — and on the fourth, nothing. Clean wood. As if someone had been sitting there yesterday.

She checked the name card taped to the desk. *Pell, Jonah.* He'd been in her class Monday. He'd raised his hand during arithmetic. He'd asked, in his serious, gap-toothed way, why the water in the fountain sang sometimes when nobody was drinking from it.

Jonah Pell had not come to school today.

Mirabel stood very still in the empty room. Outside, the light was coming up gray over the well field, and somewhere below the floorboards, faintly, almost kindly, something hummed the first bar of a song she had never learned.

* * *

They found the mother first, or rather the mother found them — standing in the schoolyard at eight sharp, still in her nightdress under a borrowed coat, her hair unbrushed, her voice carrying across the yard with the terrible clarity of a woman who has passed through screaming and come out the other side into something worse.

"His bed was made," Ruth Pell said. "Do you understand? His bed was *made*. Jonah has never made his bed in his life. I make it. Every morning, nine years, I make that bed, and this morning it was made, corners tucked, blanket straight, like a hotel, like a coffin, and he was gone and his shoes were dry."

She gripped Mirabel's arm. Her fingers were freezing.

"Dry," she said again. "But there's water on the floor. A line of it. Little wet footprints going out his door and down the hall, getting smaller, like — like he was walking on his toes, or like he was getting *lighter* —"

"Hush now, Ruth." Idris Vane materialized the way he always did, coat buttoned wrong, eyes already moving across the gathering crowd. "We'll organize a search."

"A search." Ruth laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle. "You said that about Silas Harrow. You said that about Maren Kell. You stood in my kitchen last spring and drank my tea and told me the well field was *safe*, Idris, you told me —"

"The field is dry," Idris said. "It's been dry for forty years."

"Then explain the footprints."

No one could explain the footprints. By noon there were thirty searchers walking the well field in a loose grid, boots crunching on cracked earth that hadn't held water in two generations, and every one of them could see what the earth could not possibly hold: a trail of small wet prints, leading from the edge of the schoolyard straight toward the old stone lip of the well, each print distinct, each print dark, each print already drying at the edges as though the ground itself were drinking them back.

Ada Kell crouched over the prints near the wellhead and did not touch them. Her crew had broken through to the sealed door behind the school eleven days ago, and she had not slept properly since. Now she watched the prints approach the dry well and stop — not fade, not scatter, but *stop*, in a neat pair, toes pointing down toward stone that had been capped since before her grandmother was born.

"They're walking *in*," said one of her men, and no one told him to shut up, because everyone had been thinking it.

Mirabel stood at the field's edge with the other teachers. She had insisted on coming; Idris had insisted she stay back; she had won, and now regretted it, because being here felt less like helping and more like attending something. The searchers moved around the wellhead the way swimmers move around a rip current — carefully, unconsciously, never quite naming the shape of their avoidance.

Ruth Pell did not avoid it. Ruth Pell walked straight to the capstone and put both palms flat against it and said her son's name in a voice that cracked the morning open.

"Jonah. Jonah, love, it's Mam. Come up now."

The field went silent. Thirty adults stood in the dry grass and listened to a woman talk to a stone, and not one of them laughed, and not one of them left.

Nothing answered. But Mirabel, watching, saw the wet prints around the wellhead darken — just slightly, just for a moment — as if something far below had turned its face toward the sound of a mother's voice, and wept upward through forty feet of dry earth.

* * *

By evening, the tenderness had curdled.

Mirabel felt it change in the taproom of the Lamb and Flag, where she'd gone because her rooms above the school felt suddenly like a held breath. Conversation didn't stop when she entered — it *dipped*, the way a scale dips when weight is added, and then resumed a half-tone lower, and she understood that she had been added to the weight.

"She's new," someone said, not quietly enough.

"New to us," someone else said. "Maybe not new to it."

Mirabel kept her eyes on her tea. Around her, the town performed grief the way towns do — loudly, communally, with casseroles and candle plans and a vigil announced for Sunday — but underneath the performance ran a colder current, and she could feel it finding her. Three children lost in one term. It had happened before — she knew that now, whatever Idris pretended — but it had been years, decades, and now it was happening *again*, immediately, conspicuously, in the term the new teacher arrived.

A woman at the next table leaned toward her companion and spoke with the careful loudness of someone who wants to be overheard.

"My gran used to say the well keeps count. Fifty years, she said. And it always starts with a stranger's voice in the walls."

Mirabel set down her cup. Her hand was not entirely steady.

"You could ask her," the woman's companion said. "Ask her about the humming. My boy says the new teacher hums. Walks the corridors humming, he says. Same tune every time."

"I don't—" Mirabel began, and then closed her mouth, because the truth was she *did* hum. She'd caught herself at it twice this week, a melody she couldn't place, and each time she'd stopped with the peculiar shame of a sleepwalker waking mid-stride.

The women looked at her. She looked back. In the window behind them, the dark of the well field pressed against the glass like a face.

"He was in my class," Mirabel said quietly. "Jonah. He was a good student. He asked good questions. I want him found as much as anyone."

"Do you," said the first woman. It wasn't quite a question.

Idris appeared at Mirabel's elbow, paid for her tea without asking, and walked her out into the cold. They didn't speak until the pub's light was a smudge behind them.

"It's starting," he said.

"What's starting?"

He looked at her, and in the lamplight his face was older than it had any right to be, worn like a step worn by feet. "The counting," he said. "Three every time. Always three. Silas, Maren, and now Jonah — the third is always the one that finishes it. And after the third, the town starts looking for someone to blame, because blaming is easier than remembering." He shoved his hands in his pockets. "And this time there's a newcomer with a song in her mouth and no family tree anyone can find."

"My family tree isn't your business."

"No," Idris agreed. "But it might be the well's."

He left her at her door. Inside, Mirabel locked it, and stood listening to the silence of the school, and hated herself for what she did next, which was hum — one note, barely voiced, a test, the way you test ice.

Below the floorboards, something hummed it back.

* * *

Ruth Pell did not sleep. She sat in her son's room with the made bed beside her like an accusation, and she catalogued everything, because cataloguing was the only prayer she knew.

His shoes by the door. His coat on its hook. His drawing pinned above the desk — he drew constantly, always the same things: houses, dogs, and lately, over and over, a door. A round-topped door in a stone wall, drawn in brown crayon, and in the newest one, finished the day before he vanished, he had drawn it *open*, just a crack, and in the crack he'd colored a thin line of gold.

She had thought it was a church door. She had told him so. He'd said, "No, Mam. It's under the school. It's warm."

Under the school. She'd laughed and ruffled his hair and thought nothing of it, because mothers cannot afford to think of everything.

Now she sat in his chair and held the drawing and let herself remember the week before, the small strangenesses she'd filed away as childhood: Jonah talking in his sleep in a language she didn't know, a sweet low language like water over stones. Jonah standing at the open window at midnight, face lifted, listening. Jonah saying at breakfast, "Mam, do you ever hear it under the floor? It knows my name. It says it *kind*."

She had told him not to be silly. She had told him it was the pipes.

God forgive her. She had told him it was the pipes.

Toward dawn, Ruth heard it herself — rising through the floorboards of her son's room, through the foundations, through whatever old channels connected her house to the deep places of Hollowmere. A hum. Low and patient and horribly gentle, the hum of something comforting a child in the dark.

And threaded inside it, unmistakable, a small boy's voice, singing words she didn't know in a language she didn't know, and the voice was not afraid.

Ruth Pell got up off the chair. She took the crayon drawing off the wall. She folded it into her pocket, put on her coat, and walked out into the blue-black morning, past the dry well field, past the school with its dark windows, to the one house in Hollowmere where the lights burned all night — the chapel, where Mother Agnes kept a lamp in the window for exactly this kind of hour.

Agnes opened the door before she knocked.

"I know," the old woman said. "I heard him too. Come in, Ruth. There's something you need to see, and something you need to ask the new teacher — before the town decides what she is."

Behind them, far under the fields, the hum continued, unhurried, contented, keeping count.
