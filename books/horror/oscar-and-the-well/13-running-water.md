## 13. Running Water

She left at 3:40 in the morning, before the birds, before the light, before the town could arrange its face into something innocent.

Mirabel had packed the way she'd learned to pack as a child in three foster homes — everything that mattered in one bag, nothing that couldn't be abandoned. The true verse was not in the bag. It could not be in the bag. She had copied it out once, in Agnes's kitchen, and then burned the paper over the sink while the old woman watched with her ruined hands folded and her eyes closed, as if listening to it die.

"You can't burn what's written in you," Agnes had said.

"I can try."

"Child." Agnes had opened her eyes then, and they were the color of well water. "It isn't trying to catch you. It's trying to come home. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and I'm too old to explain it better than that."

So Mirabel drove. Her car — a gray Corolla she'd bought used in another life, in a city where wells were things in fairy tales — sat under a skin of frost. She'd left the note for Idris on his doorstep weighted with a stone, four lines only, because anything longer would have become a confession and confessions were how the song got in. *Don't follow me. Don't sing. Teach the children something loud.*

The roads out of Hollowmere were empty in the way roads are empty at that hour, which is to say not empty at all but patient. Frost silvered the fields. The well field itself she avoided by taking the back lane past the chapel, and even so she felt it — a pressure against the left side of her chest, like a hand pressed flat, like someone standing close enough that their breath moves your hair. She did not look. Looking was a door too.

At the county road she turned east, toward Marlow Junction, toward the interstate, toward anywhere with more than four hundred souls and none of them humming.

The radio came on by itself.

Not the whole radio. Just a thread of static, low, beneath the heater's drone, and she told herself — she was still telling herself things, that was the last of her rationalism, a small stubborn clerk filing absurd reports — she told herself it was interference from the hills, from power lines, from the cold. But the static had a grain to it, a pulse, and the pulse had a shape, and the shape was the first two verses rising and falling like breath in a sleeping room.

She snapped the radio off. The static continued.

She pulled the fuse. The static continued, thinner now, coming up through the floor of the car, through the soles of her shoes, sung in no throat she owned.

"Stop," she said aloud, and heard how much it sounded like pleading.

* * *

Marlow Junction arrived when it should have — forty minutes, give or take, according to the odometer and the clock and every law of distance she had ever trusted. It was a gas station, a diner, a water tower with MARLOW painted on it in flaking white. She stopped for coffee because stopping was a normal thing a normal person did, and normalcy was armor, and she needed all the armor she could carry.

The girl at the register was maybe nineteen, chewing gum, phone propped against the cigarette display.

"Fill it up?" the girl asked.

"Just coffee."

"Long drive?"

"Getting longer."

The girl smiled the way young people smile at adults who say strange things — politely, already elsewhere. Then, as Mirabel waited for the machine to drip, the girl began to hum.

Two bars. Four notes. The interval that fell like a step down into dark water.

The cup trembled in Mirabel's hands. Coffee slopped over her knuckles and she didn't feel it burn.

"Where did you hear that," she said. Not a question. A demand, ragged at the edges.

The girl looked up, gum stilling. "Hear what?"

"That — you were just humming—"

"I wasn't humming." The girl said it easily, without offense, the way you correct someone about the weather. But her eyes went, for half a second, to the floor. To the drain in the tile by the ice machine, rust-ringed, dry.

Mirabel set down the coffee. Walked out. Drove.

Behind her, faint through the glass doors, she thought she heard the hum pick up again — and this time it carried the third line, the one that didn't exist yet, the one that lived only in her, the one she had never taught anyone.

It knew the shape of what she carried. Of course it did. It had written her.

* * *

The interstate took her south. She drove ninety minutes past feed stores and shuttered motels and a billboard for a lawyer whose face had been sun-bleached to a skull's grin. At the second exit she meant to cut west, loop wide around Hollowmere entirely, come at the county line from below where the hills flattened out and the land forgot how to hold water.

Instead she found herself on Route 9.

Route 9 ran straight into Hollowmere from the south. She knew this the way you know a scar. She had not turned onto Route 9. She had been watching the signs, she had been vigilant, she had checked every green rectangle of highway instruction like a woman counting stairs in the dark — and still the car was on Route 9, and the frost on the fields was the same frost, and ahead the water tower rose out of the mist with HOLLOWMERE painted on it in letters that seemed, in the early light, freshly done.

She pulled over. Put her forehead on the wheel. Made herself breathe.

Okay. Okay. She'd missed the turn. People missed turns. Exhaustion made people miss turns; she hadn't slept properly since the night the door spoke her name in her own handwriting; sleep deprivation caused exactly this kind of—

The odometer. She made herself look at the odometer, because numbers were the last honest things left. Since Marlow Junction: 94 miles. The trip should have been 60. The extra 34 miles had gone somewhere, had been spent somewhere, had been *drunk* somewhere, and the land had handed her back the change in the form of this road, this tower, this town sitting in its bowl of hills like water sitting in a cup.

Every road loops back. Not because the roads were enchanted. Because the roads were *hers* — grown through her the way roots grow through a pipe, and wherever she drove, she was driving through herself, and herself ended here.

"No," she said to the windshield, to the frost, to the pale sky doing its indifferent dawn. "I am a person. I have a name that has nothing to do with this place. I was raised in Ohio. I like cheap red wine and bad television and I am afraid of moths. I am *nobody's*—"

The static in the dead radio swelled, gentle as a tide, and resolved — fully, sweetly, unmistakably — into the first verse. Sung in her mother's voice. Sung in the voice of the woman from the oldest ledger, the voice she had never heard in her life and recognized instantly, completely, the way you recognize your own hands.

And underneath it, harmonizing, patient as geology: the voices of the children.

* * *

She tried twice more. She wants that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps accounts now that Idris's ledgers are ash in her memory — she tried twice more, and both times the world simply declined.

The second time she drove north, hard, windows down despite the cold, as if speed itself could scourge the melody out of her blood. Forty minutes in, the pines along the shoulder gave way to elms, and the elms gave way to the crooked fence posts of the Hollowmere boundary, and the sign read WELCOME TO HOLLOWMERE — POP. 412, and beneath the official count someone had long ago scratched a smaller number into the paint, and she had never noticed before what the scratched number was.

Three. Always in threes. Someone had been keeping score on the town's own face for a hundred years.

The third time she didn't drive at all. She walked. Out past the school, past the chapel, past the well field with its grass lying flat in a perfect circle like something enormous had pressed its palm there and lifted it away. She walked the county road on foot, in plain daylight, daring the world to do its trick to a woman on her own two feet.

At mile three the road bent left around a hill. She remembered the hill. She rounded it, and the road bent right around the same hill, approached now from the other side, and she stood in the middle of the asphalt in the wind and understood that she could walk this bend until her shoes wore through and her bones came up through her feet, and the hill would keep offering itself to her, courteous, inexhaustible, a host who will not let a guest leave hungry.

She sat down in the road. She laughed until it turned into something else, and then she let that be what it was, alone, in the middle of Route 9, while somewhere above her a bird sang four descending notes and she covered her ears and the notes came through her palms anyway, through bone, which is where the song had always lived.

* * *

Dusk found her at the edge of the well field, though she had no memory of walking back. That was the worst part — not the loops, not the static, but the missing time, the smooth unbroken film of it, hours spliced out of her like frames cut from a reel. She kept finding herself places. She suspected that if she examined her own thoughts closely she would find gaps there too, soft edits, kindnesses performed on her memory by something that wanted her calm.

The field was wet.

That was wrong. The well field had been dry for a generation — Ada Kell had said so, spitting, the day the crew broke through to the door: *driest ground in the county, always has been, that's the joke of it.* But tonight the grass lay dark with moisture, and the soil gave under Mirabel's shoes with a sound like lips parting, and the air smelled of stone and deep cold water and, faintly, of iron.

Of blood, said some older brainstem part of her. Of the price.

The excavation site glowed in the distance with work lights nobody had ordered. The door stood at the center of its trench like a headstone laid flat, and even from here she could see that it was no longer flush with the earth. It had risen. An inch, perhaps two. Enough for darkness to seep out around its edges like breath fogging a seal.

And on the ground between her and the trench, printed in the wet grass, were footprints. Small ones. Children's feet, bare, dozens of sets, all pointing toward the door — and one set, larger, pointing away from it, toward the road, toward her.

They had come out to meet her. They had walked to the edge of the field and stood there, in the dark, waiting, and then gone home below again when she didn't arrive.

Because they knew she would. That was the thing she finally let herself understand, standing at the field's edge with the wet soaking through her shoes and the static singing lullabies through her molars: the well had never chased her. Chasing was for things that feared losing you. The well had simply *permitted* her flight, the way you permit a child to run the length of the yard, because the yard is fenced, because the running is good for them, because they always come back to the lit window eventually.

Escape is a fiction the well permits. It lets you leave so that leaving can teach you what staying means.

"It's not calling me," she said aloud, and her voice cracked the stillness of the field like ice giving way. "It was never calling me."

The static rose in her, tender, triumphant, and she let it finish the sentence because the sentence was true and truth was the only weapon she had left:

"It's calling *itself*. Through me. Home."

The song didn't want a singer. Songs don't want anything. The song wanted to be *whole* — and it had spent a hundred years scattering its pieces: verses hidden in chapels, names carved behind doors, a bloodline smuggled out of town in the body of an infant with a changed name. It had let Agnes hide her. It had let twenty-five years pass. Because a song that is given freely comes back louder than a song that is taken, and the well had learned patience the way water learns stone.

Every road loops back to Hollowmere because every road runs through her, and she ends here, and the ending is the verse.

Mirabel Dunmore turned from the field. She did not run. Running was permitted, and she was done accepting permissions.

She walked back toward the town's lights — toward the chapel where a dying woman held the last of her secrets, toward the school where Idris Vane was surely awake, surely watching the road, surely not following her the way she'd asked — and as she walked she began, silently, moving only her lips, rehearsing the shape of the final verse.

Behind her, in the trench, the door rose another inch.

And in the grass, the small wet footprints turned, all together, and began to follow her home.
