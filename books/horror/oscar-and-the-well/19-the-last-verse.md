## 19. The Last Verse

The drug they'd given her was supposed to keep her quiet.

It didn't work the way they wanted. It worked the way the well wanted. Mirabel felt it in her limbs first — a warm slackness, a loosening of the strings — and then in her throat, where her voice sat waiting like a bird that had been told, gently, over many years, that the cage was a kindness.

They carried her through the well field on a stretcher made of two ladders and a church pew board. She could see the sky above her, gray and low, and the faces leaning over the edges of her vision: Councilman Roth with his jaw set like a man doing arithmetic; the Hollis brothers, who had once sold her eggs at market and now wouldn't meet her eyes; old Mrs. Tarn, weeping without sound, her hands folded as if in prayer or apology. Behind them, further back, a thinner crowd — Idris, Ada, a scatter of townsfolk Mother Agnes's dying words had pulled out of their houses. They were shouting. She could see the shouting. The sound came to her as if through water.

The drug made everything water.

*That's how it gets you ready,* she thought, with a strange clarity. *Not with fear. With softness. You can't sing a song you're fighting.*

The children were already there.

They stood in a ring around the door — the door that had stood sealed from the inside for two hundred years, now exposed and ajar by a hand's width of darkness. The excavation collapse had torn the earth open like a scab picked too soon, and the stone door rose out of the wound, scratched and dented and carved over and over with one name in a hand that matched her own: *MIRABEL, MIRABEL, MIRABEL.*

The missing children stood closest. Tam Weller, who had been gone since September. The Pruitt sisters, side by side, holding hands as if they'd never learned to let go. And little Danny Fenn, taken just three nights ago, still in his pajamas, his bare feet blue-white against the mud. They were pale as paper but they were *there* — visible now in a way they hadn't been since the door cracked open, and their eyes were the worst part. Not empty. *Patient.* Eyes that had learned to wait the way stone learns to wait.

Behind them, the sleepwalking children of Hollowmere — a dozen, two dozen, drifting out of the dark in their nightclothes, drawn down the lanes and across the fields by a melody that seeped up through the soles of their feet.

The ring began to sing.

Verses one and two, in perfect harmony, the song Mirabel had hummed her whole life without knowing why. It rose out of them the way water rises in a well — not performed but *produced*, drawn up from somewhere below their voices. The sound went through the drug and through the fog and found the place in her chest where the melody had always lived, and her lips parted before she could stop them.

*No.*

She bit down on the note. It hurt — a physical hurt, a cramp in the throat, the song pushing up against the back of her teeth like a tide against a door. The children's harmony filled the space she refused to fill. It was beautiful. That was the trap's cruelest engineering: it was genuinely, devastatingly beautiful, and every instinct in her body wanted to join, wanted it the way the drowning want air, the way the homesick want home.

The stretcher stopped. Hands lifted her. The gray sky swung away and was replaced by stone — the door's face, close enough to touch, close enough to see the scratches from the inside that had never been scratches from the outside at all.

They stood her up. Someone — Roth, his voice shaking with the effort of sounding official — read words from a paper. *Covenant. Consent of the bloodline. Gratitude of the town.* The old bargain dressed in new clothes: anesthesia instead of chains, gratitude instead of guilt. Every fifty years, a descendant of the first singer. Every fifty years, a voice.

Mirabel's knees were water. Idris was fighting through the crowd behind her — she could see him, could see Ada holding back two of the Hollis brothers, could see the fault line of the town splitting open in the torchlight. But it was too far, and too late, and the door was too near.

So she did the only thing left.

She listened.

*The last verse isn't sung,* Mother Agnes had told her. *It's surrendered.* And Agnes, dying, had pressed the true verse into her hands — written in a language older than the town, older than the chapel, older than the names on the founding covenant. Mirabel had spent two weeks learning its shape without voicing it, silent rehearsals in a classroom that sweated and a floor that tilted toward the well field. She knew every contour of it now. She knew what it wanted: her voice, given whole. Her selfhood, poured out like water into dry stone. That was the price the founders had paid, generation after generation — a soul spent to keep the door shut, a child fed to the hunger to starve the hunger a little longer.

And standing at the threshold, drugged and held upright by men who believed they were saving their town, Mirabel finally understood the trap in its entirety.

It wasn't that she might be forced to sing.

It was that *any* voice completing the song fed the well. Willing or unwilling. Surrendered or stolen. A child dragged screaming to the stone, a teacher anesthetized and propped up like a doll — it made no difference. The song only required completion. It did not care about consent, because consent was never the mechanism. *Completion* was the mechanism. The founders had never defeated the well. They had fed it, on schedule, with ceremony, and called the feeding a covenant, and called the covenant a duty, and raised her — the last of the bloodline — like a calf in a field, twenty-five years of grass and shelter before the slaughter.

The harmony swelled. Verses one and two, sung by stolen children in a ring around a door, waiting for the lead voice that would make the song whole.

The door was warm against her back. She could feel it breathing.

*Then don't complete it,* she thought. *Change it.*

Her mother — her true mother, the woman from the oldest ledger, the woman who had sung to quiet the well and given her away to save her — had taught her the only thing that mattered, though she'd never met her, though she'd only learned her name a week ago. She had taught her that a song is not a chain. A song is a *shape*, and shapes can be remade. Agnes had said the last verse was surrender. But surrender was just one way to end a song.

There were others. Lullabies ended too. And no one was ever devoured by a lullaby.

Mirabel opened her mouth.

The crowd went quiet — all of it, the council and the rescuers, the weeping and the shouting, as if the whole town inhaled at once. The children's harmony faltered, waiting. The door pressed warm against her spine. Somewhere below, something vast turned its attention upward, patient and kind and hungry, the way a well is all three.

She did not sing the last verse as it was written.

She sang it as a lullaby.

She took the shape Agnes had given her — the old language, the true words, the verse that meant *I give you my voice, I give you my self, I give you everything I am* — and she bent it. She slowed it. She softened the consonants until they rounded, lifted the melody at the ends of the lines the way you lift a child, and where the verse demanded *surrender* she sang *release*, and where it demanded *forever* she sang *sleep now*, and where it demanded *take me* she sang *go home*.

It should not have worked. Every law of the covenant said it could not work. The song was the song was the song, two hundred years of it, carved in stone and ledger and bone.

But a lullaby is the oldest magic there is, older than covenants, older than doors, and it has only ever had one purpose: to make the frightened thing feel safe enough to close its eyes.

The well did not know what to do.

She felt it falter — felt the hunger below her stumble like a great animal meeting a kindness it had no architecture for. The song wanted completion, and she was completing it, but she was completing it *wrong*, or rather *right* in a way no founder had ever dared, because every one of them had believed the price was fixed, had believed the hunger was the whole of what waited below. None of them had ever asked what the well might be besides hungry. None of them had ever sung to it the way you sing to a child who cannot sleep because it is afraid of the dark.

*You are the dark,* Mirabel sang, in the old language, in a mother's cadence. *And I am not afraid of you. Sleep now. The children are going home. Sleep now.*

The ring of children stopped singing.

Not silenced — *released*. Their harmony came apart like a knot gently untied, and Tam Weller's mouth closed, and the Pruitt sisters' hands dropped to their sides, and Danny Fenn looked down at his own bare feet as if seeing them for the first time in three nights, or three decades. Their faces changed. The patience drained out of their eyes and something younger flooded in — confusion, cold, the ordinary animal shock of children standing barefoot in a muddy field at night.

The door grew hot.

Then — warm. Then — not warm at all. The heat reversed, the way a held breath reverses, and the stone began to glow from within, a low gold light rising through two centuries of scratches and dents and one name carved a hundred times by a hand that had been hers in a life she couldn't remember.

The door opened inward.

Not onto darkness. Not onto the wet throat of the well, the hunger, the stairs that descended beyond all dreaming. Onto *light* — a soft, diffuse gold, like late afternoon through a schoolroom window, like the inside of a held note. And in that light, shapes. Figures. Generations of them, the fifty-year payments stretching back and back, the founders' children and the founders' children's children, all the surrendered voices standing in the gold like people at the end of a long corridor, blinking.

And they were not being devoured. They were being *let go.*

Mirabel sang the last line. Her voice cracked on it — the drug, the cold, the sheer human weight of the moment — and the crack didn't matter, because a lullaby sung imperfectly by someone who means it is worth more than a hymn sung perfectly by someone who doesn't.

*I give you back your names. Sleep now. Go home.*

The light took the figures one by one, gently, the way evening takes the day. And Mirabel felt it take her too — felt the song draw out of her chest the melody that had lived there her whole life, the hum she'd never learned, the homesickness for a town she'd never visited. It left her the way a fever breaks. It left her *lighter*.

And it left her less.

She would understand that later — standing at the sink, or catching her reflection in a window, and feeling the small wrongness, the half-beat delay, the part of herself the song had kept as its final payment. But at the threshold, in the gold light, with the door opening inward onto mercy instead of hunger, she felt only this: that she had been asked all her life to be a voice, and she had chosen, at the last and only moment, to be a *mother* instead.

The door closed. Not sealed — *closed*, the way a bedroom door closes when the child inside is finally asleep. The glow faded through the stone like a lamp turned low. The well field was silent.

Utterly silent. For the first time in two hundred years, Hollowmere could not hear itself humming.

Mirabel's legs gave out. She didn't fall — arms caught her, Idris's arms, shaking, his face wet, saying her name over and over as if testing whether it still worked. Behind him the town stood scattered across the mud, council and congregation, the guilty and the grieving, all of them looking at the closed door with the expressions of people waking from a very long, very bad dream.

Danny Fenn was crying. Small, ordinary, hiccuping sobs, the crying of a cold six-year-old who wanted his mother. His mother reached him first, and the sound she made wasn't words.

The Pruitt sisters sneezed. Tam Weller asked, in a voice rusty as an unused hinge, whether it was almost morning.

It was. In the east, past the dry well, the sky was going pale at the edges, the gray giving way to a thin, astonished rose.

Mirabel stood in Idris's arms and watched the children of Hollowmere turn toward home, and she felt the silence settle over the town like snow — total, soft, and strangely bereaved.

Because in the silence, if she listened very carefully, she could hear the shape of the part of her that was gone. A held note, fading. Patient.

Almost kind.

* * *

They didn't let her walk back alone. Idris on one side, Ada on the other, the three of them crossing the churned mud of the well field while behind them the mothers and fathers gathered up their sleepwalking children and carried them home through the rose-gray dawn. No one spoke. There was nothing yet to say. The covenant was ash in Councilman Roth's hands — Idris had burned it an hour before the council could use it, and the burning had settled nothing and everything, because the well was silent now and silence made all bargains obsolete.

At the edge of the field Mirabel stopped and looked back once.

The door stood closed in the earth, already darkening, already beginning its long work of becoming just a stone. The name carved across it was still there. *MIRABEL.* But looking at it now, in the growing light, she saw what she hadn't seen before: the carving was old. Decades old. A child's hand had cut it, a child who had known, before she was born, before she was given away, before the name was changed to hide her — a child who had known who would come, and had scratched her name into the inside of the door not as a claim.

As a *message*. *We know you're coming. We held on. Finish it differently.*

She had.

Somewhere below the stone, in the gold light that was now only light, something that had been hungry for two hundred years was learning to sleep.

Mirabel turned away from the door and walked toward the town, toward the school, toward the empty desks that would not be empty much longer — and did not see, far below, in the dark the lullaby had not quite reached, a smooth stone turning slowly over in the silence, beginning, very softly, to hum.
