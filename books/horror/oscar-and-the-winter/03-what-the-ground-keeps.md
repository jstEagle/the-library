## 3. What the Ground Keeps

The excavation crew arrived on a Tuesday, which felt right. Bad news in Hollow Creek always seemed to come on the most administratively convenient day possible, as if the town scheduled its misfortunes around the school calendar.

I heard them before I saw them — a diesel cough and a beeping that sounded like a truck reversing into eternity — and when I stepped out during my planning period to investigate, I found three men and a small yellow excavator gathered behind the gymnasium like mourners at a very industrial funeral.

"You the new teacher?" asked the one holding a clipboard he clearly didn't trust.

"Coral Sandoval. Room Four."

"Delgado." He shook my hand with the distracted grip of a man whose mind was already underground. "We're putting in the footings for the annex. Shouldn't take more than a week." He said it the way people say *shouldn't* when they mean *won't*.

"Ground trouble?" I asked, because in my three weeks at Hollow Creek Elementary I had learned that everyone here answered questions you hadn't quite asked yet. It saved time. It also meant nobody ever told you anything you actually wanted to know.

"Ground's fine," Delgado said. "Ground's always fine."

He turned back to his crew before I could ask what that was supposed to mean, and honestly, by then I'd stopped expecting sentences from this town to resolve like normal ones. Hollow Creek conversation was all subordinate clauses. Everything depended on something else that nobody would name.

I should have gone back inside. I had twenty-six essays on *Charlotte's Web* to grade, twenty-three of which would ever be handed in, but that's getting ahead of myself. Instead I leaned against the gym's brick flank and watched the excavator bite into the earth behind the building, and for a while it was just dirt — brown, ordinary, unremarkable dirt, the kind of dirt that has never done anything interesting in its life.

Then it wasn't.

The bucket hit something about four feet down and the whole machine shuddered like a dog shaking off rain. The operator killed the engine. The silence afterward had texture to it, the way a room does after someone says something unforgivable at dinner.

"Huh," said Delgado.

"What is it?" I called, already walking over, because I have never once in my life possessed the instinct for self-preservation that other people seem to come standard with.

"Rock, probably." He dropped off the machine and knelt at the edge of the hole, brushing soil away with his palm like an archaeologist who hoped he wasn't one. "Limestone shelf, maybe. This whole valley sits on—"

He stopped talking. That was the thing I noticed most, later: not what he found, but how completely he stopped finding words for it. His hand kept moving, clearing dirt in small careful strokes, and his mouth just closed like a drawer being shut.

I looked past his shoulder.

It wasn't rock. Rock doesn't have corners. What showed through the soil was pale gray and flat and met another pale gray flat surface at a right angle so precise it looked machined, except nothing machines that smooth anymore, and certainly nothing did it four feet under a elementary school playground where children had been playing foursquare since before I was born.

"That's stone," I said, brilliantly.

"That's stone," Delgado agreed. He stood up, wiped his hands on his thighs, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. Not surprise. Not fear, exactly. Something more like recognition — the face of a man meeting an old acquaintance he'd been hoping had moved away.

"We'll need to see how far it goes," he said.

"It?"

"The stone."

He hadn't said *it* about the stone. He'd said *it* the way you say *it* about something alive, or at least something with intentions. But I let it go, because the bell was ringing and twenty-three children were about to pour out of recess and I had developed, in three short weeks, a fierce and irrational protectiveness regarding their proximity to holes.

That night I graded essays I barely read. Wilbur is some pig, wrote Dev Okafor, in handwriting like a seismograph reading, and underneath, unprompted: *why does charlotte have to die tho*. Same, Dev. Same.

I want to be clear about something, because it matters to how the rest of this went: I wasn't scared yet. Uneasy, yes. Hollow Creek had been marinating me in unease since the day I arrived — the lesson plans that stopped mid-sentence, the desk in the back row that no child would touch, the roster with its three ghosts, the way conversations here ended like doors closing gently rather than finishing. But unease is a background hum. You can teach through it. I had been teaching through it for three weeks and telling myself stories: budget cuts, small-town politics, a predecessor who'd quit for personal reasons and left her materials in disarray. People leave jobs mid-sentence all the time. Look at half the novels in the world.

The notebook changed the genre of everything.

* * *

I found it on Thursday night, and I found it stupidly, the way you find anything important — by accident, while looking for something else entirely.

My classroom, Room Four, has a floor that predates every reasonable expectation. Wide pine boards, honey-dark with age, that creak in a pattern I'd started to memorize the way you memorize a creaky staircase in a new apartment. Third board from the radiator, second row of desks: a creak with a little *skip* in it, a hitch, like the floor clearing its throat.

Thursday evening I stayed late to rearrange desks — a futile ritual I perform whenever the week has beaten me, moving furniture instead of my life — and I dragged the back-row desk across that board and heard the hitch go wrong. A clunk. A hollow sound, wood on emptiness instead of wood on wood.

I told myself it was a cable channel. Old buildings have them. I told myself this while kneeling on the floor of an empty classroom at seven p.m., prying up a board with a letter opener, which is either the beginning of a horror story or a home improvement blog, depending entirely on what's under there.

Under there was a oilcloth bundle, tied with string, tucked into a cavity between the joists with the care of someone hiding something they intended to come back for.

She never came back for it. That was the thought that arrived cold and complete, before I even untied the string. Ms. Ansel — Ruth Ansel, according to the personnel file Aldous had grudgingly shown me, eleven years in Room Four, gone since September with nothing but a resignation letter so bland it read like it had been written by committee — had hidden this under the floor of her own classroom, and whatever was in it was why she'd hidden it, and she was gone.

Inside the oilcloth was a notebook. Composition book, marbled cover, the kind sold at every drugstore in America. Her name was on the front in ballpoint: R. ANSEL. And below it, smaller, almost apologetic: *do not read.*

So of course I read it.

The early pages were normal, almost painfully so. Seating charts. Notes on students — kind notes, observant notes, the notes of a teacher who actually saw her kids. *Marcus reads two grades ahead but won't raise his hand unless I make eye contact first.* *Priya's grandmother died; give her jobs.* It made me like a woman I'd never met, which made what came after worse, the way you grieve harder for someone once you've seen photographs.

Because somewhere around page forty, the notebook changed.

The dates started appearing without context. October 12. October 3. October 19. Just dates, alone on lines, some circled, some crossed out, one underlined twice with such force the pen had torn through to the next page. Then names began accumulating beneath the dates, in columns, in handwriting that got smaller and tighter as the pages turned, as if she were trying to fit more truth onto less paper:

*1961 — E. Marsh*
*1970 — T. & S. Whitlow (both)*
*1979 — D. Ives*

D. Ives. I sat back on my heels. The janitor. Gerald Ives, sixty-ish, keys on a carabiner, nods hello every morning with the weary courtesy of a man who has swept the same floors for decades. D. Ives, 1979. His brother? His father? I filed it away and kept turning pages, and the floorboards creaked above me in the empty building, and I want you to know that I noticed the creaking and chose, consciously, to keep reading. That's the kind of person I am. It's going to matter later.

The word appeared first as a margin note, small, almost casual: *harvest again?* Then it came back. *Harvest moon Oct 4.* Then it came back bigger, boxed, and then it came back circled — circled obsessively, dozens of times, the ink going over and over itself until the paper shone through worn thin, until *harvest* stopped looking like a word and became a sigil, a stain, a wound in the page.

In the margins around it, in handwriting that deteriorated from teacher-neat to something jagged and slantwise, fragments:

*They call it transfer. Ask what transfers.*
*Quiet ones. Always the quiet ones. Why do we keep choosing the quiet ones. Who is choosing.*
*Aldous says founding families' arrangement. Says ask no questions. Says it protects everyone. Protects WHO.*
*I counted. Eleven. Eleven in sixty years and every single one in autumn and every single one the child who would be least missed and I am supposed to stand in this room and take attendance and NOT COUNT.*

The last dated entry was September 30th of this year. Three weeks ago. Two days before she vanished.

*Tomorrow I'm going to ask him directly. If he lies to me one more time I'm going to the county. Let them dig. Let the ground say what it keeps.*

Below that, nothing. The rest of the notebook was blank, except — and this is the part I keep returning to, the part that has taken up residence behind my sternum like a swallowed stone — except for one final line, written on the last written page, in different ink. Bluer. Fresher. As if it had been added after everything else, maybe much after, maybe recently, maybe —

I'm not going to finish that sentence. I've learned that about myself this week: I am capable of not finishing sentences now. Hollow Creek is teaching me things.

The line said:

*You found it. Good. Now count with me.*

* * *

I did not sleep well, in the way that people in novels don't sleep well, which is to say I slept exactly enough to dream badly and woke at 3 a.m. with the notebook open on my chest like something that had climbed there.

Friday morning I watched the excavation from my classroom window instead of the playground, which I told myself was about supervision logistics and knew was about cowardice. The crew had spent Thursday mapping the stone — Delgado's phrase, delivered flatly, *mapping the stone*, as though it were a coastline — and Friday they brought in a longer probe and a man named Okonkwo who supposedly knew old foundations.

By lunchtime, the news had traveled through the school the way news travels through schools: instantly, silently, and wrong. By the time I walked out at recess duty, the fourth-graders had it that the crew had found "a castle," "a grave," and — from Wren Hollis, who said it quietly while the others laughed — "the bottom of the school."

"The school has a bottom?" I asked her.

Wren looked at me with those serious gray eyes kids deploy when they're deciding whether adults can handle the truth, and decided, apparently, that I was close but not ready. "Everything has a bottom, Ms. Sandoval," she said, and went back to her jump rope.

Behind the gym, the probe rig stood over the hole like a gallows. Delgado caught my eye across the fence and shook his head once, a small precise motion, and mouthed something I couldn't read. Or maybe he didn't mouth anything at all. Maybe that was just his face now. Faces change fast around here when the ground gets involved.

Okonkwo's verdict, relayed to me secondhand by the secretary, Mrs. Pell, who relayed everything secondhand because I think firsthand information physically pained her: the stone extended at least twelve feet in every direction they'd probed, was fitted rather than poured, and was older than the school. Considerably older than the school. Older, Okonkwo reportedly said, than it had any business being.

"And the school was built on top of it," I said.

"Well," said Mrs. Pell, in the tone of a woman declining a canapé, "they wouldn't build it under it, dear."

That afternoon I stayed late again. I told myself it was to grade, and I did grade, technically, three essays, but really I sat in Room Four watching the light go amber and then bruise-colored, listening to the building settle around me, thinking about Ruth Ansel standing in this same room writing *let the ground say what it keeps* and then walking out of her life mid-sentence.

At six, Ives came through with his vacuum, the way he does every evening, pushing that ancient Hoover down the hallway in slow passes like a man mowing a very small lawn. He paused at my door. He looked at me. He looked at the floor — specifically, I realized with a lurch, at the third board from the radiator, second row, where a faint scar in the varnish marked where the board had been lifted and reseated.

"Found something," he said. Not a question.

"A notebook," I said. Because lying to Ives felt like lying to a church.

He nodded slowly, unsurprised, unsurpriseable, a man whose entire emotional range had been mortgaged decades ago. "Ruth was a counter," he said. "Counters don't last here."

"Ives—" I started, and he raised one hand, just slightly, and the gesture was so tired and so final that I stopped.

"Ms. Sandoval." He said my name carefully, like a word in a language he used to speak. "Whatever she wrote in there. Whatever you're thinking about doing with it." He looked at the window, where the dark was coming down over the playground, over the hole behind the gym, over the stone that was older than it had any business being. "Monday," he said. "Wait till Monday. Some things you shouldn't learn on a weekend, with nobody around."

And he pushed his vacuum down the hall, and the sound faded, and I was alone in Room Four with a dead woman's arithmetic and a floorboard that creaked like a throat clearing.

I put the notebook in my bag. I locked my classroom door. And as I walked out through the empty corridor, past the trophy case and the fire drill map and the crayon mural of the four seasons — winter painted largest, winter painted best, by some child who understood something — I caught myself doing it.

Counting.

Eleven names in sixty years. Twenty-six on my roster, twenty-three in seats. One desk nobody will sit near. One teacher gone mid-sentence. One stone door under the playground, fitted and old and waiting.

One weekend, with nobody around.

Ives had meant it as a warning. Standing in the parking lot with my breath fogging in air too warm for fog, I understood it as something else entirely: a schedule. Whatever this town did, whatever bargain it had struck with whatever kept the ground, it ran on a calendar, and the calendar was turning, and Monday was coming whether I counted or not.

Behind the gym, in the dark, the excavator sat silent over its hole.

I could swear — I will swear, later, to anyone who asks, and people will ask — that as I pulled out of the lot, the floodlights the crew had rigged over the excavation flickered once, all together, like something below had tested the wiring.

Like something below, checking whether anyone was still watching.
