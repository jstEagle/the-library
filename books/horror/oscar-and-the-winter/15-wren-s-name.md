## 15. Wren's Name

The vent in Room 4 starts talking in October. Everyone knows this the way everyone knows the Pledge of Allegiance — by rote, without thinking, without asking what it means.

I knew before most kids because I sit in the second-to-last row and the vent is right under my chair, and on cold mornings it breathes up through the floor grates like something underneath us is sighing. Not wind. Wind doesn't have a rhythm. This has a rhythm, slow and patient, like a sleeper's breathing, like my grandmother's old clock that ticked wrong on purpose so you'd have to listen harder.

The other kids put their backpacks over the grate. I used to think that was to block the cold. Now I know it was to muffle the sound, because sometimes — only some Octobers, only when the moon gets fat — the breathing says things.

This year it said my name.

* * *

Let me back up, because Ms. Sandoval would want me to back up. She's always saying *context, Wren, context*, like she's grading my life the way she grades my essays. She's been here three weeks and already she asks questions nobody else asks, which is exactly how you get noticed by the wrong audience, if you ask me. Which nobody did. That's sort of my whole problem.

Here's what I know about being chosen, assembled from eleven years of paying attention while pretending not to:

One: it happens in autumn. Always autumn. The town pretends this is because of harvest festival and flu season and whatever else they can hang a calendar on.

Two: it happens to the quiet ones. Not the shy ones — there's a difference. Shy kids want to talk and can't. Quiet kids *could* talk and have decided the conversation isn't worth having. The Winter doesn't take shy kids. The Winter takes the ones who've stopped asking.

Except me. I never stopped asking. I asked about the empty desk in the back row until Ms. Sandoval moved it into the hall. I asked why our roster said twenty-six names when twenty-three chairs were ever full. I asked Mr. Aldous why the annex excavation kept stopping at sundown, and he smiled at me with his whole face except his eyes, which stayed flat as taped-over windows, and he said, "Curiosity is a wonderful quality, Wren. In moderation."

Three — and this is the part nobody says out loud, the part you have to figure out from the way mothers go hollow-eyed and fathers suddenly need to check on something in the garage — three: everybody knows. Not the details. But the shape of it. You can know the shape of a thing your whole life without ever looking at it straight on, the way you know the shape of the dark under your bed without ever putting your face down there to check.

I checked. That's why it's me.

* * *

On Thursday night I packed a bag.

Not a runaway bag. A going bag. There's a difference, and the difference matters, though I couldn't have told you why yet. A runaway bag says *I'm leaving because I'm scared.* A going bag says *I know where I'm supposed to be and I'm choosing to show up.*

Into the bag went: two granola bars (the kind with chocolate chips, because if you're going somewhere terrible you should at least like the snacks), my flashlight, the good one Dad bought for camping and never took camping, a photograph of my family from the lake two summers ago where Mom is laughing so hard she's blurry, and my notebook. The notebook matters. It has everything in it. Dates. Names. The pattern, written out in my smallest handwriting, because small handwriting feels more like evidence and less like a crazy person's diary.

Eleven names in sixty years. I had nine of them for sure. Dev Hollis made ten — he went home "sick" two weeks ago and his mom told Ms. Sandoval he'd "been invited," and I heard her say it through the open door of the supply closet where I was hiding, because hiding in supply closets is another thing I do instead of being quiet. And the eleventh slot, the last line of the page, I left blank.

I didn't write my own name. Writing it felt like signing something.

But I knew. God, I knew. The vent had started saying it three nights ago, soft as frost forming: *Wren. Wren. Wren.* Not menacing. Almost gentle. Like a teacher taking attendance. Like the ground had learned my name from all those years of listening through the floor and finally decided to use it.

And here's the thing that made me pack the bag instead of telling my parents, which I tried first, I want that on the record: I told my mom. Tuesday night. I sat at the kitchen table and I said, "Mom, the vent is saying my name, and I think I know what happened to Dev, and I think I know what happened to all of them," and I slid the notebook across the table with both hands like I was surrendering a weapon.

She looked at the notebook for a long time. Long enough for the kettle to start whining. Then she closed it, slid it back, and said, "Wren, honey, October is hard on this town. It's always been hard. Don't borrow trouble."

Borrow trouble. As if the trouble were a library book I'd checked out myself. As if I hadn't just handed her sixty years of due dates.

That's when I understood the shape of the thing completely. My mom wasn't lying to protect herself. She was lying to protect *me*, the same way she lied about Santa Claus and about Grandpa going to live at the facility, the same loving lie, the same *it's easier if you don't know*. The whole town was my mother. The whole town had decided long ago that children could survive anything except the truth.

Maybe they were even right. I've had three days to consider that they might be right.

But then I thought about Dev, who once gave me half his sandwich when I forgot my lunch, who is quiet the real way, who never asked a single question his whole life and got taken anyway because somebody upstairs decided quiet equals willing. And I thought: if the truth is the price of admission either way, I'd rather pay it awake.

So Thursday night, after my parents fell asleep in front of the TV, I wrote them a note that said *Gone to school, don't worry, I'll explain everything* — which is a ridiculous note, because nothing about it explains anything, but I couldn't fit sixty years on an index card — and I walked to Hollow Creek Elementary in the dark.

* * *

The school looks different at night. In daylight it's just a building, brick and linoleum and the smell of pencil shavings and floor wax. At night it stands there like something waiting to be remembered. The windows are black rectangles. The flagpole makes its thin creaking sound against the sky. Behind the gym, where the excavation crew dug their pit and then quit — quit all at once, every one of them, mid-week, no notice — the floodlights are dead and the hole breathes out cold air you can see from thirty feet away, a silver fog rolling up out of the earth like the ground is exhaling winter early.

I didn't go near the pit. I'm brave, not stupid. Those are different things; Ms. Sandoval taught me that too, or maybe I taught myself and just let her take the credit.

I went to Room 4. My room. Our room. The door was unlocked, which should have frightened me and instead felt like an invitation, and I stood in the doorway a long time deciding whether invitations from buildings count.

Inside, the frost was already on the windows.

It was fifty degrees that day. Everybody said so. Fifty degrees and sunny, and yet the glass of Room 4 wore a skin of ice ferns, spreading from the corners inward, and in the middle of the biggest pane, where the frost thinned, something had been traced — not written, *traced*, from the inside of the glass, which is impossible, I know it's impossible, I've decided to believe it anyway — a single letter.

W.

My initial. Or the beginning of my name. Or the beginning of *why*, if the frost had a sense of humor, which I doubted.

I sat down at my desk and waited. Because that was the plan, the whole plan, the entire strategy I'd built over three sleepless nights: get there first. If I had to be given to the Winter, I was going to walk in the front door of it instead of being dragged. I was going to sit in my own chair, under my own name in the attendance book, and when the cold came up through the vent I would look it in the — well, vents don't have faces, but I'd look at it anyway, straight on, the way nobody in this town ever had.

That was the plan. It lasted forty minutes, until the door opened and Ms. Sandoval came in holding a flashlight and wearing an expression I recognized from my own mirror.

"You're supposed to be home," she said.

"So are you."

"Wren." She crossed the room and crouched by my desk, and up close her eyes were red-rimmed, like she'd been crying or hadn't slept or both. "Your parents—"

"Know I'm gone. They'll find the note." I pulled my bag onto my lap and hugged it. "You can't send me back. You know you can't. It doesn't work like that."

Something moved behind her face — a flinch, a confirmation. "What do you know?" she asked carefully. "Tell me exactly what you know."

"I know about the ledger. I followed you to the church on Monday. I'm small and I'm quiet when I want to be, which is ironic, apparently, given my current situation." I said it fast, before I lost my nerve. "I know there's a bargain. I know it's sixty years old. I know the vent picks someone every fall and everyone pretends the kid transferred. I know it's me this year. The frost spelled my letter. The vent's been saying my name since Sunday."

Ms. Sandoval sat down hard in the empty desk beside mine — the desk from the back row, the one nobody would sit in, the one she'd moved into the hall and then, I noticed last week, quietly moved back. Oscar's desk, probably. The first one.

"Okay," she said. And then, again, like she was testing whether the word would hold weight: "Okay. Here's what I'm not going to do, Wren. I'm not going to tell you it'll be fine. I'm not going to tell you it's not real. I'm not going to tell you any of the things every adult in this town has been telling children for sixty years, because I've read the ledger, and I know now that every single one of those sentences was a lie told on purpose, by people who loved their kids, which somehow makes it worse."

I blinked. This was not the speech. The speech was supposed to be *everything's fine, honey, let's get you home.*

"The founders invited it," she went on, quieter. "During the famine. They offered it people — children — the ones who'd be least missed, that's the actual phrase in the ledger, *the least missed* — and in exchange the crops came back and the town survived. And then they built the school on top of the door to keep the arrangement polite. Sealed from the inside. Do you understand what that means? It means the thing below never took anyone. It means we handed them over. Every year. With signatures."

"I know," I whispered. "I read your face through the church window. I couldn't hear, but I saw you reading, and I saw what reading it did to you."

For a moment neither of us spoke. The vent breathed. Up close, in the empty room, I could hear what it was really saying under my name — not words, exactly, but the memory of words, like a recording of every roll call ever taken in this room, layered and looping, dozens of teachers' voices saying dozens of names, and under all of it, patient as sediment, one voice that sounded like a boy.

"Oscar," Ms. Sandoval said, following my eyes to the vent. "He was first. 1963. His own teacher volunteered him. She loved him — that's in the record too, she wrote that she loved him enough to save everyone else. He's been down there ever since, keeping the others company, doing the town's bookkeeping for it." Her jaw tightened. "He asked me to confirm this year's name. Politely. Like borrowing a cup of sugar."

"And you wouldn't."

"No. I won't. Not ever." She turned in the little desk to face me fully, and her hands were shaking, and she didn't hide it, and somehow that was the most honest thing anyone had done in my presence in eleven years. "Which means the bargain breaks, and when it breaks, the terms say the Winter gets one last night to collect everything it's owed. Tomorrow night. The full moon. So I need you to listen to me very carefully, Wren, because I am about to tell you the truth, all of it, with nothing softened, and then you're going to hate me a little, and then you're going to help me anyway. Can you do that?"

I should have been terrified. I was, a little. But mostly — and I've thought about this moment a lot since, turning it over like a stone — mostly I felt something in my chest unlock that had been locked so long I'd forgotten it was a lock.

"Yes," I said. "But first answer me one question. Honest answer. No adult voice."

"Ask."

"Why me? The rule is the quiet ones. I'm not quiet. I'm the opposite of quiet. I've been loud about this since September. By the rules, I should be safe."

Ms. Sandoval looked at me for a long moment, and then she reached out and touched my notebook, still hugged inside my arms, like she could feel the evidence through the canvas.

"Because you asked," she said. "Don't you see? The quiet ones were easy — they never questioned the story, so nobody had to work to keep the lie intact around them. But you asked questions in front of Aldous. In front of the class. You made the lie expensive. The Winter doesn't take the least missed anymore, Wren. It takes the ones who threaten the arrangement. It's not hungry for silence. It's hungry for *witnesses*."

The frost on the window crackled. The letter W spread a new branch, reaching toward the center of the glass like a hand uncurling.

"So running wouldn't have saved me," I said.

"Running might have. Hiding wouldn't. And—" she hesitated, and I watched her decide, deliberately, to finish the sentence honestly, "—and I don't actually know. Nobody knows. Sixty years of complicity means nobody ever tested the edges of the thing. We're going to test them tomorrow night, you and I, and Ives, and whoever else in this town has a spine. We're going to burn the ledger and say every name out loud, all eleven, all correct, no euphemisms, and we're going to hold out until sunrise, because the original terms promise that dawn ends any night the bargain fails. That's the loophole. Sixty years, and the loophole was in paragraph one."

I looked down at my bag. The granola bars. The flashlight. The photograph of my blurry laughing mother. All the equipment of a girl planning to surrender with dignity.

Then I opened the notebook to the last page, found a pen, and wrote my own name in the eleventh slot. Not because it was giving in. Because it was mine to write, and I wanted it in my handwriting, in ink, claimed — so that tomorrow night, when Ms. Sandoval read the roll call of the given and the taken, my name would already be spoken, already true, already burned.

"There," I said. "Now it can't be used. Only remembered."

Ms. Sandoval stared at the page, and then at me, and something in her expression shifted — respect, maybe, or recognition, one survivor of loving liars finding another.

"God," she said softly. "Remind me never to assign you an essay."

Outside, behind the gym, the dead floodlights flickered once — twice — and held. And from the bottom of the pit, faint but unmistakable, rising through the frozen ground like heat through a stove top, came a sound none of the missing children had ever been allowed to make in sixty years.

Laughter. A boy's laughter, rusty with disuse, astonished at itself.

Ms. Sandoval stood up so fast the little desk skidded. "That's not possible," she breathed. "He's not supposed to be able to—"

The vent under our feet exhaled one word, clear as a bell, in a voice that had waited since 1963 to use it:

"*Ready.*"
