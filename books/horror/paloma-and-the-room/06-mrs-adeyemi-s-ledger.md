## 6. Mrs. Adeyemi's Ledger

The follow-up call came on a Tuesday, which felt right. Nothing about Verrin Street had ever happened on an interesting day of the week.

"Routine recheck," my dispatcher said, and her voice did the thing again—the small stutter, like a record skipping over the house number. "Four-oh—four-oh—" A pause long enough for me to hear her swallow. "You know the one."

I knew the one. I'd spent four days trying not to know it.

Here's what nobody tells you about pest control: it's ninety percent paperwork and ten percent crawling into spaces where things have died. I'd built a whole career on that ratio. Paperwork is wonderful. Paperwork has your name on it in your own handwriting, dated, filed, unremarkable. Nobody remembers the man who files Form 27-B. That's the whole point of me.

Except the Verrin Street house didn't file itself into memory the way other jobs did. It sat there instead, in the back of my skull, like a stone in a shoe.

I told myself I was going back because the contract required a fourteen-day follow-up inspection. I told myself that in the truck, out loud, twice, the way you repeat a password to yourself so you don't forget it. The truck radio played something soft and stupid and I let it, because silence on the drive to Verrin Street had turned out to be a bad idea. Silence was where Paloma lived now, in the space between songs, wearing her yellow raincoat, saying *you kept the lights on, good* in a voice that hadn't aged a day since 1993.

Fourteen days. I'd left every light in my apartment burning the whole time. My electric bill looked like a hostage situation.

* * *

Mrs. Adeyemi opened the door before I knocked.

I want to be clear about how unsettling that was. Her porch had three steps and a rail, and I made noise on all of them—I'm a big man in work boots carrying a metal case, I do not move quietly—and still the door swung inward as I raised my hand, as if she'd been standing there with her fingers on the latch, waiting for the exact moment.

"Mr. Nakamura." She said it like she was checking a box. Maybe she was.

"Follow-up inspection," I said, and held up my clipboard like a talisman. Clipboards are underrated in the horror genre. "Should take forty minutes. You don't need to accompany me."

"I know," she said, and stepped aside anyway.

The house smelled different. That was the first wrong thing. Two weeks ago it had smelled like every old house smells—dust and decades and the ghost of a hundred casseroles. Now it smelled like paper. Old paper, the kind that goes sweet and brown at the edges, the smell of libraries and attics and, if I'm honest, the inside of my grandmother's Bible, which I was not allowed to touch as a child because God apparently lived in the margins.

"You've been reading," I said, to say something.

"I keep the books," Mrs. Adeyemi said.

I wrote that down. I don't know why. My hand just did it: *keeps the books.* Underlined. I underlined things when they scared me, which was a habit I'd picked up from no one, because no one had taught me anything, which was rather the point of my childhood.

The attic was where I'd found the handprints, so the attic was where I went first. Procedure. You always recheck your worst finding first, because either it's worse or it's better, and either way you want to know before lunch.

I clicked on my flashlight at the top of the pull-down ladder and stopped.

The dust had been disturbed. Not by animals—there were no tracks, no drag marks, none of the little highways mice carve through attic grime. The dust had been *tidied*. Swept into neat windrows along the joists, like someone had come up here with a very small broom and a great deal of patience. And in the cleared center of the floor, pressed into the bare boards, was a fresh handprint.

Adult-sized. Childlike spacing. Fingers splayed wide, the way children press their hands to wet cement, the way they press their hands to windows, the way they press their hands against the glass of doors they're not allowed to open.

It was facing the ladder. Facing me.

"Okay," I said aloud, because saying *okay* out loud is another ritual, one of mine, right up there with leaving lights on and counting doors. "Okay. Sure."

I knelt beside it without touching it and did the professional thing, which was photograph it, measure it, and narrate my findings into my phone recorder in a voice so calm it could have sold insurance. Seven inches across the palm. No smudging. Dust undisturbed around the edges, meaning the hand hadn't pressed down from above—it had pressed up from below, or else the dust simply *agreed* to move aside, and I filed that thought in the drawer where I kept all the thoughts I wasn't having.

That's when I heard the pen.

* * *

It came from below—a small, dry scratching, rhythmic as rain. Writing. Someone in this house was writing, and the sound carried up through the floorboards like it wanted to be heard.

I should have finished the attic. Procedure says finish the attic. But procedure also says this is a pest control job, and we had all silently agreed weeks ago that it wasn't, so I went down the ladder instead, following the sound through the hallway—the hallway that hummed, the hallway whose eleven doors I counted again out of spite (eleven, eleven, eleven; the number only changed when I blinked too long)—down to the parlor at the front of the house, where the afternoon light came in amber and thick as syrup.

Mrs. Adeyemi sat at a writing desk I would have sworn on a stack of invoices hadn't been there two weeks ago. It was oak, old, scarred with ink rings, and it fit the bay window the way a key fits a lock it was made for. She was bent over a ledger the size of a tombstone.

She didn't look up. "You heard the pen."

"I heard a pen."

"Mm." The pen scratched on. "Everyone hears the pen eventually. The house likes an audience."

I stood in the doorway like a man at the edge of a pool he's been assured is heated. "Mrs. Adeyemi, I need to ask you some questions about—"

"Sit down, Mr. Nakamura."

There are voices you obey because they're loud and voices you obey because they're true. Hers was the second kind. There was a chair near the desk—there was always furniture in this house exactly where it needed to be, which is its own kind of horror—and I sat in it, and my case sat by my feet, and outside the window the light went on being amber and useless.

She turned the ledger toward me.

"It's accounts," she said. "The house keeps them. I write them down. Somebody has to. Houses can't hold pens."

The ledger was open to a page dense with names in faded brown ink—not black, brown, the color blood goes when it dries into paper—and each name had a date beside it, and each date was a year, and the years went back and back and back, the handwriting changing century by century, quill to fountain pen to ballpoint to the cheap gel pens of my own decade, like the ledger had been written continuously since before electricity and simply updated its equipment.

They were children's names.

I knew that before I read a single one. You can tell children's names in an old book by the company they keep—little Ediths and Thomases and Winifreds stacked up like headstones, generations of them, and then the handwriting modernizes and suddenly it's Kyles and Ashleys and Jaydens, and the horror doesn't change, only the spelling.

"Whose names are these?" I asked, though my mouth had gone dry enough that it came out as more of a weather event than a question.

"Kept ones," Mrs. Adeyemi said. "Mostly. Some are just... called for. Reserved." She licked her thumb and turned back several pages, and I watched the years scroll backward—1987, 1974, 1961—and then she stopped, and tapped the page once with a fingernail yellowed like old piano keys.

And there, between a *Marcus T., 1981* and a *Ruthie O., 1958*, in handwriting I recognized because I saw it every day on Form 27-B:

*Nakamura, O. — 1993.*

My name. My name, in a ledger older than my parents' marriage, dated the year I turned seven.

The room did a slow, gentle tilt, the way rooms do when your body decides the floor is optional. I gripped the chair arms. Somewhere far away, a version of me who had never driven to Verrin Street was living a small, safe, forgettable life, and I hated him with a purity I usually reserved for myself.

"That's not possible," I said. "I'd never been here in 1993. I'd never even heard of this street. I grew up in—" I stopped. Because that was the thing, wasn't it. I grew up nowhere. We moved the way other families vacationed, constantly and to escape something, and the towns blurred together into one long beige corridor of rented houses and new schools where I learned to be furniture before I learned cursive.

"The house doesn't wait for you to visit," Mrs. Adeyemi said, in the tone of a woman explaining rain. "It writes you when it wants you. The date isn't when you arrived. It's when you were *entered*." She drew the ledger back toward herself, protective, the way people hold books they love. "1993. You were entered in 1993. I remember the entry. The pen moved strangely that night. Eager."

"Eager," I repeated.

"These pages get moods." She said it the way you'd say *the car gets good mileage.* "Some names go down smooth. Some fight. Yours went down like it had been waiting at the tip of the pen for years, just looking for an excuse."

I laughed. It came out wrong, a single barked syllable with nothing behind it. "This is a joke. This is—you found my name somewhere, off a work order, and you're writing it in an antique to mess with—"

"Nakamura, O.," she read aloud, softly, tracing the letters. "Seven years old. Entered under *invisible*, subcategory *practiced*." She looked up at me, and for the first time since I'd met her, Mrs. Adeyemi blinked. It was somehow worse than the not-blinking. "That's what it says. *Practiced.* The house admires practice. Most children are invisible by accident. Yours was a discipline."

My ears were ringing. Not metaphorically—there was an actual high thin tone, the kind you get after fireworks, and underneath it, faint as a radio through a wall, I could almost swear I heard humming. Three notes, looping. Sweet. Patient.

I stood up so fast the chair skidded.

"I'm going to need you to stop reading my file," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle like I was thirteen, and I hated that, I hated everything, I hated the amber light and the ink rings and the fact that my own name looked *at home* in that book, settled in between Marcus T. and Ruthie O. like it had been holding their places.

Mrs. Adeyemi closed the ledger with both hands, gently, the way you close a casket.

"Where are you going?"

"Finishing my inspection." I scooped up my case. "Attic's clean. Well. Attic's *attic*. I'll file my report."

"There's no report that covers this," she said, not unkindly.

"No," I agreed, already moving. "But there's a form for everything else, and I am going to fill out every single one."

At the door she spoke again, and I stopped, because it wasn't a command this time. It was quiet, almost tired, and it landed on the back of my neck like a cold fingertip.

"You were seven," she said. "When you were entered. What happened when you were seven, Mr. Nakamura? Think carefully. The house never writes a name without a reason. Something made you invisible enough to matter."

And I opened my mouth to say *nothing, nothing happened, nothing ever happened to me, that was the whole problem*—

—and instead, unbidden, unwelcome, rising up out of whatever locked drawer I'd put it in thirty years ago, came a yellow raincoat, and a porch, and a counting song with a tune I could still hum and words I suddenly, terribly, could not quite remember all of.

Three verses. I'd known three verses.

I had never, in thirty years, wondered why there wasn't a fourth.

Behind me, in the parlor, the pen began to scratch again—fast now, eager, a new line starting—and Mrs. Adeyemi said, in a voice gone strange and formal, like she was reading from dictation:

"Oh. Oh, dear. It's writing *tonight's* date."
