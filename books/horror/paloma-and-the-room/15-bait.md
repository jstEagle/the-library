## 15. Bait

The plan, such as it was, fit on the back of a pest-control invoice.

Orsen wrote it out at their kitchen table at two in the morning, because that was when plans like this got made — the hour when every bad idea looks like a strategy and every strategy looks survivable. The pen moved in small, stubborn strokes. *Stop hiding. Be seen. Be loud. Be useless to them.*

Underneath, they added, in smaller letters, like a footnote to their own courage: *A house that collects invisible children has no use for a person everyone can see.*

It sounded right. It sounded almost scientific. Orsen had spent thirty years perfecting the art of not being perceived — the soft voice, the downcast eyes, the birthday nobody knew, the sister who'd stopped calling because Orsen never called first and never called second either. If invisibility was what made them prey, then visibility had to be the antidote. You couldn't be swallowed by a room that was waiting for someone no one would miss.

Orsen read the note three times, then folded it into their shirt pocket, over the heart, like a talisman or a confession.

"Okay," they said aloud, to the empty kitchen, to the porch light burning yellow through the window. "Let's get noticed."

* * *

The trouble with spending a decade teaching yourself to disappear is that unlearning it feels exactly like falling downstairs.

Orsen's first act of visibility was bureaucratic, which felt safe, which was the point. They filed a formal report with the county about the Verrin Street property — structural anomalies, unpermitted additions, eleven doors in an eight-door house. They attached photographs. They wrote *please advise* in the subject line of an email to the city assessor, cc'ing two council members whose names they'd looked up specifically so they could spell them wrong with confidence.

Then they called Mrs. Adeyemi's number from the work order and left a message on her answering machine, speaking clearly, using their full name — Orsen Nakamura, licensed pest control operator, license number recited twice — and said, "I know what your ledger says. I know my name is in it. And I'm telling you now, loudly, so there are witnesses: I am not coming quietly."

There were no witnesses. It was an answering machine. But it counted. Orsen decided it counted.

By Wednesday they'd escalated to the human version. They showed up at the precinct downtown and asked to speak to someone about a missing child, Dev Okafor, last seen entering 1140 Verrin Street, and they did not mumble, did not study their shoes, did not do any of the small apologetic choreography that usually made clerks forget them before they finished a sentence. They stood at the desk like a person with a body and a grievance.

The officer who took the report was named Reyes, and she had the patient face of someone who processed a lot of haunted-house reports in October and a fair number the rest of the year too.

"You say the boy went inside a vacant property."

"It's not vacant," Orsen said. "There's a woman there. Adeyemi. She keeps a ledger of children's names. Some of them date back decades. One of them is mine."

Reyes wrote that down without changing expression. "And how do you know your name is in this ledger?"

"Because I read it."

"With your own eyes."

"With my own eyes."

She looked up then, and something in Orsen's face must have landed, because Reyes's pen paused. "You want to tell me why you're shaking?"

"Because I'm doing something brave," Orsen said, "and it turns out bravery is mostly fear with paperwork."

That got half a smile. The report got filed. Orsen got a case number, which they wrote on their hand like a grade-schooler with a crush, and walked out of the precinct feeling — there was no other word for it — *visible*. Seen by a stranger. Recorded in a database. A person who existed in triplicate.

For one whole afternoon, it worked.

Orsen could feel the difference the way you feel a fever break. The hallway of their apartment didn't hum. The corners of rooms stayed honestly dark. When they passed a mirror, the person in it looked tired but *present*, anchored, a creature with edges. Even the porch light seemed less like a vigil and more like a habit.

They let themselves believe it for exactly four days.

* * *

Marcus noticed the change before anyone else, because Marcus noticed everything about Orsen except the things he said out loud.

"You're different," he said Friday morning, leaning against the van while Orsen loaded sprayers. "You made eye contact with Doris from accounting. She asked me if you were dying."

"I'm not dying."

"You told her your *birthday*. Unprompted. She had to sit down." He squinted at them. "Who are you and what have you done with Nakamura?"

Orsen tightened a strap and considered how much truth a coworking relationship could hold. Marcus had been the closest thing to a friend they'd had in nine years, which meant Marcus existed in the strange country between colleague and confidant, where you shared lunches and complaints and never once the actual contents of your life.

"The house on Verrin Street," Orsen said. "The one I've been treating. It — takes people. People like me. People who learned to be invisible young." They heard how it sounded and kept going anyway, because the whole plan was to stop sounding reasonable and start sounding *witnessed*. "I'm making myself loud so it loses interest."

Marcus was quiet for a moment. He was a big man, gentle with the traps, the kind of guy who relocated spiders instead of squashing them and lied to clients about it.

"And that works?" he said. "Being loud?"

"That's the theory."

"Huh." He handed Orsen the last sprayer. "My mom used to say the opposite. She said the loud ones get eaten first." Then he grinned, because Marcus delivered wisdom like it was a joke so nobody could accuse him of caring. "Anyway. If you die, I want your parking spot."

"Deal," Orsen said, and laughed, really laughed, and for a moment the morning was just a morning, and the plan was just a plan, and nothing anywhere was writing anybody's name down.

* * *

Saturday, Orsen went bigger.

They stood in front of the Verrin Street house itself, in broad daylight, and did not go inside. That was important. The plan was visibility, not suicide. Instead they planted a sign on the lawn — an actual printed sign, laminated, zip-tied to a stake: **MISSING: DEV OKAFOR. LAST SEEN HERE. IF YOU KNOW SOMETHING, CALL.** With a phone number. Their phone number. Written large.

Mrs. Adeyemi watched from the upstairs window. Orsen waved at her, a full-armed, unmistakable wave, the wave of a person who exists.

"I see you," Orsen said, not loudly enough to carry, loudly enough to mean it. "And more importantly, everybody sees *me*."

The house did nothing. That should have been the first warning. Houses that collect children are patient institutions; they don't flinch at signage. But Orsen was high on their own audacity, riding the strange bright vertigo of being a person in public, and patience looked like defeat.

Sunday they called their sister.

That hadn't been in the plan, exactly. But somewhere between the precinct and the lawn sign, *visible* had stopped being a tactic and started becoming a hunger, and Orsen found themselves holding the phone before they'd decided to dial, listening to it ring, rehearsing nothing.

"Hello?" Her voice was older than Orsen remembered. Everything about her was older than Orsen remembered; that was what nine years of silence bought.

"Hi," Orsen said. "It's me."

A long pause. Long enough that Orsen heard a television in the background get turned down, then off.

"You sound different," she said finally.

"I'm working on it."

Another pause, softer. "Working on what?"

"Being findable." Orsen sat down on the kitchen floor, back against the cabinet, the way they used to sit as a kid when the shouting started, except now the kitchen was quiet and the sitting was a choice. "Can I tell you something weird? And you're allowed to think I'm crazy. You've earned that."

"Orsen—"

"There was a friend I had. When we were little. Before you went to Dad's. A girl in a yellow raincoat. Paloma." The name came out whole, unbroken, the first time in thirty years it had been spoken aloud in that apartment. "You used to say I talked to her through the wall."

Silence. Then, very carefully: "I remember. You'd sing this counting song. It used to scare me, because you'd sing it and the fighting would just... keep going, like nobody downstairs could hear you anymore. Like you'd gone somewhere." A breath. "I thought you'd invented a way to leave without leaving."

Orsen closed their eyes. "That's exactly what I did."

They talked for two hours. It was clumsy and uneven and Orsen cried once, briefly, furiously, the way people cry when they've been holding a door shut for decades and someone finally opens it from the other side. By the end, his sister had extracted a promise — Sunday calls, real ones — and Orsen hung up feeling scoured and new and so aggressively *perceivable* that surely, surely, no waiting room anywhere had any further claim on them.

The plan was working. That's what Orsen believed, going to sleep Sunday night with the porch light on and the phone full of a sister's number and a case number inked faintly on the heel of one hand.

The plan was working.

* * *

Monday, the ledger disagreed.

Orsen knew before they saw it. Knew the way animals know weather — a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the morning light, the particular quality of silence that means something has already happened and is waiting for you to catch up. They drove to Verrin Street without deciding to, parked crooked, and found the front door standing open like a held breath.

Mrs. Adeyemi was in the parlor, at her small table, with the ledger open in front of her. She didn't look up.

"You shouldn't have gotten louder," she said.

"Where's Dev?" Orsen said. "Is he still singing? Is he—"

"He's fine. He's *kept*. Kept is fine, for a while." Her pen moved. "But that's not why you're here. Look."

Orsen looked.

The ledger lay open to a fresh page, and the handwriting on it was not Mrs. Adeyemi's. It was rounder, older, written in a brownish ink that might once have been something else. And the name at the top of the page, underlined twice with a flourish that managed to be both loving and proprietary, was:

**MARCUS WEBB.**

Below it, in smaller script, as though filling out a form: *nine years. lunches. jokes. knows the birthday now. knows everything now.*

"No," Orsen said.

"You wanted to be seen," Mrs. Adeyemi said, and now she did look up, and her eyes were wet and pitiless with sympathy. "So the house looked where you were looking. You shone a light on yourself, child, and light doesn't fall on one thing only. It falls on whatever stands beside you." She turned the page toward Orsen fully, gently, the way you turn a mirror. "He stood beside you. Nine years of standing beside you. Do you understand? You weren't invisible all that time. You were invisible *to yourself*. To him, you were the most seen thing in the building."

Orsen's hands were shaking. "Take my name instead. Put it back. I'll go quiet again, I'll—"

"You'll what?" For the first time, Mrs. Adeyemi's voice cracked, and underneath the bookkeeper was something old and terrified. "You'll teach yourself to disappear again? You did that once. It's why the house chose you. It's why Paloma found you. Quiet isn't protection, it's *ripening*." She pressed her palm flat against the ledger, over Marcus's name, as if she could smother it. "I feed it names so mine stays closed. That was our bargain, mine and the house's. But it writes its own entries now. It always could. I just — I never gave it anyone I—" She stopped.

"Loved," Orsen said.

Mrs. Adeyemi didn't answer. The word sat in the parlor like a third person.

Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of old paper. Somewhere upstairs, faintly, sweetly, looping — a child's voice was singing. Not Dev. Something older. Three verses, and then a pause, patient as a held breath, waiting for a fourth that wasn't coming yet.

Orsen backed toward the door. "How do I get him out?"

Mrs. Adeyemi looked at the ledger, and then at Orsen, and something in her face shifted — pity collapsing into something harder, something almost like respect.

"The books," she said. "Everything the house owns, it owns on paper. Names, verses, debts. Burn the books and the accounts come due." She pulled the ledger toward herself and closed it, both hands flat on the cover, like a woman keeping a lid on a well. "But hear me, Orsen Nakamura. Accounts coming due cuts both ways. Mine included."

Orsen was already reaching for their phone, thumb finding Marcus's name, the contact photo — Marcus mid-laugh at last year's holiday party, holding a paper crown.

One ring. Two.

Voicemail. And beneath the recorded voice, faintly, from somewhere on Marcus's end — a hallway hum, a held breath, the acoustic signature of a house that had finally, after nine years of standing beside its favorite invisible person, decided to look at who else was standing there.

Orsen ran for the van.

End of chapter.
