## 19. The Last Verse, Un sung

The walls whispered it to me twice before I understood what I was hearing.

*Hush now, hush now, count them all—*

I clamped my hands over my ears like a child, which was, I suppose, exactly what the house wanted me to feel like. But the whisper didn't come through my ears. It came through the wallpaper, through the plaster, through the soles of my boots and up the bones of my legs, patient as water finding a crack.

*—the ones who never answered when they called.*

The fourth verse.

It existed. Paloma hadn't lied about that much. It had been here all along, pressed into the house itself like a flower in a book, and the house was giving it to me freely, generously, the way a spider gives silk to a fly—not as a gift but as an invitation to wrap myself.

Marcus stood by the nursery door with the ledger open across both arms, reading names in a voice that shook and steadied and shook again. "Ruth Okafor. Ruth Okafor, born 1961, last seen 1973." Each name he spoke made one of the doors down the hall groan on its hinges, made something in the walls shift its weight. "Thomas Verrin. Thomas Verrin, born 1954—"

"Keep going," I said.

"You keep going," Marcus shot back, not unkindly. His eyes were red-rimmed and furious and terrified, all three at once, and I have never loved a coworker more. "You're the one it wants."

He wasn't wrong. The hallway had rearranged itself around me the moment we'd come back inside. Every door now faced me. Every doorknob gleamed like it had been polished that morning, for me, in anticipation. The house was a throat and I was standing in it, and somewhere below—or above; the geometry here had given up pretending—the room waited with its small bed and its chair turned toward the door.

But the fourth verse was in the walls, and only someone the house wanted could read what remained of it. Paloma had told me that too, her voice fraying at the edges like old tape. *You're the only key left, Orsen. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry.*

So I did the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than burning anything, harder than being born visible:

I listened to the whole verse. And I refused to sing it.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about refusing a song: your body wants to finish it. That's what songs are *for*. They are little machines for making bodies agree. Paloma taught me the first three verses when I was seven years old precisely because a seven-year-old's body agrees easily, hungrily, grateful for any rhythm to belong to. *One for the dark and two for the door,* I'd sung on the stairs while the shouting rose from the living room like floodwater, and the shouting had seemed farther away, and I had thought that was safety. It was just anesthesia.

Now the house poured the fourth verse into me and my lips moved without permission. My fingers tapped the rhythm against my thigh. *Three for the bed where you'll sleep forever—*

"ORSEN." Marcus's voice cracked across the hallway like a whip. He'd dropped the ledger—he caught it, barely, pages fanning—and grabbed my shoulders with both hands. "Your mouth is moving. Stop moving your mouth."

"I know," I said. My voice sounded far away. "I know how it ends, Marcus. I know all of it."

"Then don't say it!"

"That's the trick." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, breathless, giddy, the laugh of someone standing on a ledge appreciating the view. "That's the whole trick. It doesn't need me to sign anything. Singing along *is* the signature. So I learn it—" I tapped my temple, and my fingers were still keeping time, damn them, still tapping, *—and I keep it behind my teeth. All of it. Forever if I have to. A song you never sing is just knowledge. Knowledge isn't ownership."

The walls went quiet.

Not peaceful-quiet. Held-breath quiet. The hum I'd noticed on my first visit, the hallway humming like something waiting to exhale—that hum deepened until the floorboards buzzed under my boots and dust sifted down from the ceiling in fine gray threads, like the house was shedding.

Then, from everywhere, soft as a mother at a bedside, the house began again. Slower this time. Kinder. It sang the first verse, the one every invisible child learns first, and God help me, it was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing I had ever heard. It sounded like being seven years old and finally, finally warm.

My jaw started to drop.

I bit down. Hard enough to taste copper.

"No," I said through my teeth. "No. You don't get it. You had me for thirty years and you don't get one more note."

* * *

The ledger lay open on the floor between us, and I knelt and picked it up, and the thing weighed what a book should weigh, which somehow felt like the biggest lie in the whole house.

Page after page of names. Children's names in a dozen different hands, some in pencil worn soft as moth wings, some in ink gone brown, some in crayon, which nearly broke me. Dated. Always dated. Born, taken, kept. The house's books, Mrs. Adeyemi had said, and she'd been right—it was accounting, columns and balances, and every entry balanced against a child who had learned early that being unseen was safer than being seen, and had been collected for it like interest.

"Read them," I told Marcus. "All of them. Out loud."

"Why? What does that do?"

"The house keeps them because nobody witnessed them." I turned pages with fingers that wouldn't stop trembling. "Paloma told me—no. The house told me, wearing Paloma. It collects witnesses. Kids who saw too much and went quiet. Well." I looked up at him, and my eyes were stinging and I let them sting. "Two can witness. If we say their names out loud—if we *see* them—they're not invisible anymore. And the house can't keep what it can't account for."

Marcus swallowed. Nodded once. He crouched beside me, put his finger on the page to hold his place, and read:

"Ruth Okafor."

"Ruth Okafor," I repeated. "We see you. You were here. You mattered."

The wallpaper shuddered.

"Thomas Verrin."

"Thomas Verrin. We see you."

Down the hall, one of the eleven doors—twelve now; I'd stopped counting, counting was a game the house played better than me—slammed shut with a boom that rattled my fillings. Not opening. Shutting. The house was trying to close accounts.

"Good," I breathed. "Louder, Marcus. Let's bankrupt it."

* * *

We worked our way through the ledger name by name, call and response, two people kneeling in a screaming hallway doing the least professional thing either of us had ever done on a job site. Marcus's voice gave out twice and I took over; my voice cracked on the third page and he carried me. Somewhere in there we stopped shaking. There is a strange calm that comes from doing the right thing badly, loudly, in immediate danger, and I recommend it to no one and everyone.

"Dev Ramachandran," Marcus read, and his voice broke clean in half. "Dev Ramachandran, born 2016, last seen Tuesday."

"We see you, Dev." My throat closed. "We're coming, Dev. Hold on. Don't sing. Whatever you hear, however pretty it gets—don't sing along."

From somewhere beneath us, faint as a radio through a wall, a child's voice was indeed singing. First verse. Second. Third. Looping. Younger each time. I heard it and felt the fourth verse rise up my own throat like bile, offering itself, *here, use me, end it, save him, just say the words—*

And that was the trap inside the trap, wasn't it. The house didn't need my vanity or my loneliness anymore. It had found something better. It would let me trade the verse for Dev. One little song, one signed soul, one boy walked out the front door blinking in the daylight. Fair exchange. Reasonable terms. The house had always been good at reasonable terms; ask Mrs. Adeyemi, who'd been paying hers off for forty years.

I held the verse behind my teeth. I felt every syllable of it, knew it the way you know your own phone number, complete and perfect and mine—and I gave it nothing. Not a hum. Not a tap. I sat on my own tongue like a dragon on a hoard and I burned.

"Not for sale," I whispered. "Not for anything. You hear me? Nothing in this house is worth the song."

The scream started in the wallpaper.

* * *

I want to describe it accurately, because accuracy matters when you've survived something; it's the difference between testimony and nightmare. The wallpaper screamed in patterns. The roses in the print opened their paper mouths. The stripes bent and vibrated like plucked strings. It was not a human sound and it was not an animal sound; it was the sound of arithmetic failing, of a column that would not balance no matter how many times the figures were entered, of a ledger discovering a debt it could not collect.

The lights—all the lights we'd brought, the floods, the work lamps, the cheap flashlight in my pocket—flared white and held. The house hated light. It always had. It had counted on its children loving the dark of invisibility more than they feared the dark of basements, and it had never imagined one of us standing in the middle of it with every bulb blazing, refusing to dim.

Names. We kept going. Page five, page six, names going back further than the house on Verrin Street, further than the street itself—children from houses that had burned or rotted or been demolished, and I understood then with a lurch that the house was older than its address, that it had simply *been* Verrin Street for a while, the way a hermit crab is briefly a shell.

"Eleanor Moss," I read. "Eleanor Moss. We see you."

The eleventh door dissolved. Just—wasn't there anymore, wall where the door had been, seamless, like it had never been needed.

"Samuel Adeyemi," Marcus read, and stopped. "Orsen. This one's hers. Samuel Adeyemi, born 1949, last seen 1989. Her brother?"

"Her brother," I said. "Read it anyway."

"Samuel Adeyemi. We see you." Marcus's voice gentled. "We see you, Sam."

Somewhere in the house, very far away, something that might have been a woman began to cry—or to laugh; at that register, in that place, grief and relief sound identical.

The fourth verse pushed against my teeth again, one last time, sweetest yet, promising rest, promising warmth, promising the small bed and the chair and the eyes that would finally, finally stay on me forever—

Forever. That was the sales pitch. *Seen forever.* And I finally understood the flaw in the contract, the typo in the eternal lease: seen forever by *one thing*, in *one room*, is just another kind of invisible. The house had never offered visibility. It had offered a slightly larger box.

I smiled at the wallpaper with blood on my teeth from biting down, and I said, "I know the ending. I'll die knowing the ending. And you will never hear it."

The house screamed louder. The floor bucked. Down the hall, the nursery door—the real one, the original, the one that had been waiting thirty years—swung slowly open onto darkness that breathed.

And Paloma stepped out of it.

She looked wrong. Flickering, like film run through a projector with a hair in the gate. Her yellow raincoat was yellow and then gray and then yellow, and her face was her face and then it was the house's face, and then, fighting hard, it was her face again.

"*Burn the book,*" she said, and her voice was half hers and half the groan of load-bearing wood. "*Orsen. While it's weak. Burn the ledger and it can't—*" The house seized her mid-word; her head snapped sideways; she wrestled it back, and her eyes, when they found mine, were seven years old and thirty-seven years old and begging. "*It can't collect what it can't count.*"

"Paloma—"

"*Don't sing. Whatever happens next, whatever it offers, whatever you hear—*"

The lights dipped. The darkness in the nursery leaned out like a tide.

"—*don't sing.*"

* * *

Marcus was already on his feet, hauling me up by the arm, the ledger crushed against his chest. "Lighter," he said. "Tell me you have the lighter."

I did. I'd carried it since chapter one of this disaster, since the follow-up visit, some instinct packing fire into my tool bag alongside the flashlights and the gloves. Pest control. That was the job. Remove the infestation.

I looked down the hall at the nursery, at the breathing dark, at the girl in the flickering raincoat who had taught me a song to survive my childhood and then waited thirty years to teach me the silence that would save my life.

"Paloma," I said. "Come with us."

Her smile was the saddest and brightest thing in that entire screaming house. "I'm in the book, Orsen. I've always been in the book."

Behind us, the wallpaper peeled itself off the walls in long curling sheets, shrieking, and every name we'd spoken glowed through the plaster like embers looking for air.
