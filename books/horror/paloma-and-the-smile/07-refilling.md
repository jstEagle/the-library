## 7. Refilling

The water came up the chapel steps on a Tuesday, and no one but me seemed to think it strange.

I found it at half past six in the morning, notebook under my arm out of habit more than purpose, taking the long way past the chapel because the frost had made the main path treacherous. The fog had a new quality to it that week—thicker, wetter, as though the air itself were sweating—and when I reached the steps of the old chapel I stopped so abruptly that my boots skidded on the stone.

The bottom three steps were black.

Not wet. Black. Glossy, still, and utterly lightless, like lacquer poured over the granite, like something that had been poured there deliberately and was waiting to be admired. I crouched at the top step and looked down into it and saw nothing reflected back—not the grey sky, not the chapel's broken arch, not my own face. My own face most of all. I leaned closer until my breath fogged the air above it, and the surface did not ripple.

Behind me, a crow called once from the yew tree and then thought better of it.

I did what I always did. I wrote it down. *6:47 a.m., 14 December. Water on chapel steps, lower three. Colour: none. Temperature: unmeasured. Reflected image: absent.* My handwriting was steady. My hand was not. There is a difference between the two, and by that point in my life at Hollowmere I had learned to live inside it—the gap between what I recorded and what I felt, a narrow corridor where I kept myself upright.

When I came back at noon with Wren trailing me, the water had climbed two more steps.

"It's filling," Wren said. She said it the way other children say *it's raining*, flat and factual, and that frightened me more than the water did. She stood with her hands jammed in her coat pockets, chin tucked, studying the rising blackness with the narrowed eyes she'd inherited from a father who fixed boilers and said very little. "It does that when it's hungry."

"Hungry for what?"

Wren didn't answer. She never answered that question directly. Instead she said, "Miss Aldercott wants you to come Thursday. She says she hasn't got much longer and she'd rather not waste the time being polite about it."

* * *

Two children went home overnight on Wednesday.

That is the phrase the school used—*went home*—though both of them lived, according to every record I could find, at addresses that no longer existed, with guardians whose phone numbers rang through to dial tones older than the children themselves. Petra Osei from Year Four. Daniel Whitlock from Year Six. I had taught them both. I had marked their work in green pen because red felt cruel. Petra drew horses on every spare margin; Daniel could make a paper aeroplane that rode the radiator's thermal current in slow, endless circles above our heads, and I had let him, because watching it was the only ten minutes of peace any of us got.

Their desks sat cold on Thursday morning.

Not empty—cold. I touched the wood of Petra's desk and flinched; it held the temperature of the chapel steps, of deep water, of somewhere the sun had never been asked to visit. The other children filed in and hung up their coats and did not look at those desks, moved around them the way water moves around a stone, and when I took the register I read nineteen names and heard eighteen voices answer and did not correct the silence, because correcting it felt like agreeing to something.

At break I stood at the window and watched the fog press against the glass like a face too close to a windowpane.

"You're doing the face again," Wren said. She had appeared beside me without sound, as she always did. "The teacher face. The one where you're deciding whether to tell us something true or something kind."

"Can't I do both?"

"Not here." She picked at the chipped paint on the windowsill. "Petra liked your lessons. She told me. She said you were the first one who ever wrote her name right." Wren's voice did something small and terrible on the last word, a hairline crack in a wall that was holding back considerably more than weather. "You write everyone's name right. That's why it likes your book."

"Likes my—" I turned. "Wren. What likes my book?"

But the bell went, and she was gone into the corridor crowd, small and quick and feral, and I was left holding a question that had teeth.

* * *

Edith Aldercott's cottage smelled of coal smoke, lavender, and forty years of carefully rationed honesty.

She had grown visibly smaller since my last visit, folded deeper into her wingback chair, her hands—those enormous, capable hands I remembered from the hymnal—resting in her lap like sleeping birds. But her eyes were clear. Clearer than mine, that afternoon.

"You've seen the water," she said. Not a question.

"On the chapel steps. It's climbing."

"It always climbs in December. It's just never had this far to climb before." She gestured vaguely toward the window, toward Hollowmere, toward the well behind the chapel that the maps had stopped marking sometime in the eighties. "We fed it nine names in 1974 and it slept. One name a generation since, little sips, enough to keep it dreaming. Voss calls it administration. I call it what it is." Her mouth thinned. "Murder with paperwork."

"Edith—"

"I'm dying, Freya. Let me be rude while I still have the breath for it." She laughed, a dry rustle, and then the laugh ran out of her and left something rawer underneath. "The solstice is Sunday. Dawn. If the well isn't given back what it was given—if the names aren't spoken home—it stops sipping. It takes freely. It takes until it's full, and it has been starving for fifty years, and there are ninety-two children in that school."

My recorder was in my pocket. It was always in my pocket now. And through the wool of my coat I felt it grow warm—not hot, not mechanical, warm the way a hand is warm—and I knew, with the certainty of the truly exhausted, that he was listening. Thomas. The boy whose desk I taught from. The voice on my tapes who answered questions nobody had asked aloud, who had said *you write us down, nobody ever wrote us down* in a tone I have no adequate word for except *homecoming*.

"He knows," Edith said, watching my hand move to my pocket. "He's known since you started writing. Names spoken aloud bind, girl, but names written down—" she smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever been the cause of, "—written down, they can be carried. He's been waiting fifty years for someone to carry him."

"Then we do it Sunday. We go to the well at dawn and we say the names."

"We need all nine. I gave you the hymnal. Seven names torn out, and Thomas makes eight." She closed her eyes. "The ninth—" 

Her breathing changed. Not laboured; eased. As if some effort she'd been making for half a century had quietly become unnecessary.

"Edith?"

"The ninth name was mine to remember," she said, and her voice had begun to drift, gentle as snowfall, "and I kept it safe all this time, I kept it in the place where I keep—" her lips moved, shaping something, "—Margaret. Margaret Ilbery. Thomas's sister. We sang her second so he wouldn't be alone down there, oh, we were kind, we were so *kind*—"

"Edith." I was on my knees by the chair. "Edith, stay with me."

She opened her eyes one last time and they were looking at something over my shoulder, something in the corner of the room where the shadows pooled, and whatever she saw, she was not afraid of it. Her expression was the one I imagine we all hope for: recognition, and relief, and a love old enough to have outlasted its object.

"Margaret," she said again, softly, like setting down a heavy thing after fifty years of carrying it. "There you are."

And then she was gone, mid-sentence or mid-something larger than sentence, her hands still resting in her lap, her face turned toward the corner with an expression of perfect welcome.

I sat on the floor of a dead woman's cottage with a recorder full of ghosts and a hymnal missing nine pages, and I did not cry. I want that recorded. I did not cry, because crying is a loosening and I could not afford to loosen; everything in me was compressing instead, grief and terror and fury folding down and down into something small and dense and burning, the way pressure makes diamonds out of things that would rather have stayed soft.

* * *

That night I played the tapes back in my room with the door locked and the lamp turned low, and Thomas's voice came through the hiss clearer than it had ever been, a boy's voice, patient and worn thin:

*Dawn on the solstice. All nine. Say us home or it feeds without asking.*

"I know," I whispered. "I know. I have seven names and Margaret. I have eight."

A pause, long enough that I checked the machine was still turning.

*You have nine,* the voice said. *You've had nine since the day you arrived.*

And beneath the hiss, beneath the dormitory sounds I had recorded—a cough, a bed creaking, wind against glass—I heard, very faintly, a child laughing, and the sound was coming from the direction of the well, and it was getting louder.

Sunday dawn was thirty-six hours away. The water, when I checked the chapel steps at midnight, had reached the door.
