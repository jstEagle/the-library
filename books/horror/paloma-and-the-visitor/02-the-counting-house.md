## 2. The Counting House

The station had eleven doors.

Ada knew this the way she knew her own pulse — not as information but as fact, worn smooth by repetition. Eleven doors between the broadcast booth and the fire exit at the back of the building: two for the booth itself, one to the hallway, three along the corridor where the transmitter cables ran like exposed nerves under the floor grating, one to the supply closet that always smelled of warm dust and burnt solder, one to the men's room Marcus never cleaned properly, one to the break room with its dead vending machine, and one, finally, the heavy steel exit door with its push-bar and its sign that read THIS DOOR ALARMED, though no alarm had ever sounded in the nine years Ada had worked there.

Eleven doors. She counted them every night when she arrived, and every night before she left, and sometimes in between, during long stretches of dead air or weather reports, her lips moving silently behind the microphone, tallying a building she could not see from inside the booth. If the count came to eleven, the night was manageable. If it came to ten, or twelve — if a door had gone missing, or multiplied — then something was already wrong, and the wrongness would find her eventually, the way water finds the low place in a floor.

Tonight the count was eleven. She had checked twice.

* * *

People who didn't know her — which was most people, since most people existed only as voices in her headphones — assumed Ada Galloway was calm. It was the voice, probably. She'd been told since childhood that she had a radio voice, low and even, a voice like a hand laid flat on a frightened animal's back. Her grandmother had said it differently. *You have a keeping voice,* Ruth Galloway used to say, sitting on the porch of the house on Delmar Street with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting out a storm that had been coming for sixty years. *A voice that holds things in place. Don't waste it.*

Ada had wasted it anyway, or tried to. She'd left Hollow Creek at eighteen with a duffel bag and a scholarship and the specific, ferocious hunger of someone escaping a house where the lights were never allowed to go off. She had studied communications in a city where nobody counted anything, where doors opened and closed without ceremony, where you could leave a room dark and nothing happened. For almost four years she had been nearly free.

Then her grandmother died — Tuesday, October the ninth, alone in the house on Delmar Street, found the following morning by a neighbor with her radio still playing, tuned, as always, to the overnight frequency — and Ada came home for the funeral and never quite left again. She told herself it was the job. The station in the city had downsized; the station in Harlow Bend, forty minutes from Hollow Creek, was hiring for overnights, which nobody wanted, which suited her fine. She told herself a lot of things. She was good at it. Telling yourself things was a kind of ritual too.

But the compulsions had come back with her, the way birds come back to a chimney they were fledged in. She kept the lights on. All of them. Her little rental house on the edge of Harlow Bend burned with light all night, every bulb in every socket, because her grandmother had taught her that darkness was not empty — darkness was a mouth, and light was how you kept your hand out of it. She counted doors. She counted windows, too, though less urgently; windows were eyes, her grandmother said, and eyes could be trusted more than mouths. She salted the thresholds on the first of every month, an unbroken line across each doorway, whispering the words her grandmother had whispered, words that weren't quite Latin and weren't quite anything else, sounds that had come down through four generations of Galloway women like a gene you carried in the throat instead of the blood.

And she never, ever answered a knock after midnight without counting to thirty first.

She had never told anyone this. Not the two therapists she'd seen in college, both of whom had used the word *anxiety* in that gentle, diagnostic way, both of whom she'd stopped seeing once it became clear they wanted to take the rituals away rather than understand why they worked. Not Marcus, who thought her habit of leaving every light burning in the station was an eccentricity, maybe a fear of the dark played for charm. Not her listeners, who knew her only as the steady voice between midnight and five, the woman who played requests for truckers and insomniacs and shift workers and the grieving, who said *you're not alone out there* every hour on the hour, and meant it, God help her, meant it so hard some nights it felt less like a slogan than a prayer she was making on behalf of everyone including herself.

Because here was the thing Ada had never said aloud, not once in thirty-one years: she had always half-believed.

Not believed the way the faithful believe, all sunlight and certainty. Half-believed the way you half-believe in the monster under the bed even as an adult, even knowing better — the belief lives in the body, not the mind. Her mind was a modern, reasonable thing. Her body knew otherwise. Her body knew that the counting mattered, that the salt held, that the lights were a wall, that somewhere beneath the ordinary skin of the world there was a thing with an appetite, and that the Galloways had spent generations standing between it and everyone else, passing the duty down like a casserole recipe, except the dish was never done and the oven was never cool.

Her grandmother had tried to explain it to her exactly once, the summer Ada was nine. They had been shelling peas on the porch, and Ada had asked — children ask, that's their whole job — *Grandma, what are we keeping out?*

Ruth's hands had gone still in her lap. A long time they stayed that way, long enough that Ada looked up from the peas and saw her grandmother's face doing something complicated, grief and love and fear braided together so tightly you couldn't tell which strand was which.

*Nothing you need to meet,* Ruth had said finally. And then, quieter, almost to herself, in the voice she used for talking to the porch boards or the dark: *So long as somebody keeps the count.*

Ada had asked what count. Ruth had put another handful of pods in her lap and said, *Shell, baby,* and that had been the end of it, the way most important things end in families — not with answers but with a change of subject and a full bowl.

* * *

At 2:14 a.m., the board lit up again.

Ada watched the line blink red and did not move. The studio around her hummed its small electric hum — the console, the transmitter feed, the refrigerator in the break room that had a dying compressor and sounded, if you listened too long, like slow breathing. Outside the booth's glass, the ON AIR sign glowed its patient crimson. Rain had started sometime after one, a soft persistent static against the roof, and the parking lot beyond the window was a smear of sodium light and wet asphalt.

The caller ID showed a local number. They usually did. That was part of what made last night's call so easy to file away — a local number, a local man, a strange story. People said strange things on the overnight airwaves. It was practically the format.

But her hand hesitated over the button, and the hesitation itself frightened her, because her hands never hesitated. Her hands were professionals. Her hands had taken ten thousand calls.

She let it ring six times. Then she answered.

"Harlow Bend overnight, you're on the air."

For a moment there was only rain, and the breathing sound of the refrigerator, and then a woman's voice, older, unhurried, with the flattened vowels of someone who'd grown up in the valley and never left it.

"Is this Ada? Ada Galloway?"

"That's me." Keep it level. Keep it warm. "Who do I have tonight?"

"You know me, honey." A small dry sound that might have been a laugh. "You used to. You'd sit on my steps and I'd give you lemon drops out of the tin on the hall table. You always took two."

Ada's mouth went dry. She knew the voice. Of course she knew the voice. Mrs. Aldous, from three houses down from Delmar Street, the woman with the lemon drop tin and the plastic-covered sofa and the photograph of her late husband on the mantel turned slightly toward the room as if he were watching television with them. Ada had sat on those steps a hundred summers. She had taken two lemon drops every time, because Mrs. Aldous insisted, because one was stingy and three was greedy, and this memory arrived now with such vividness — the tin's lid rasping open, the dust-fuzz sweetness on her tongue — that for a moment she was nine years old and the world was intact.

"Mrs. Aldous," she said carefully. "It's good to hear from you."

"Is it?" The voice was gentle. Almost sorry. "That's kind of you to say, considering."

"Considering?"

"I've been dead eight days, sweetheart." No drama in it. No performance. Just a woman stating the weather. "Heart, in my sleep. Harold found me — well, Harold couldn't, he's been gone twenty years, but you know what I mean. The girl from the church found me. I heard them say the date at my own service. Lovely flowers. Too many lilies. Lilies smell like funerals, which I suppose is fitting."

Ada's hand found the fader and pulled her mic down, an old reflex, cutting herself from the air while the caller kept talking into the void. Her other hand gripped the edge of the console. The console was real. The console had weight and temperature and a scratch near channel four from a coffee mug in 2019. She held onto these facts.

Last night's caller had described his funeral. She had checked the obituaries at 4 a.m., hands shaking so badly she'd had to rest her wrist on the desk to type, and there he'd been: Dale Pruitt, 54, passed suddenly Tuesday, survived by two daughters, services Saturday. Dale Pruitt, who had called her show at 1:40 in the morning and chatted about his daughters' softball seasons and the new gravel on his driveway and then, casually, terribly, mentioned the view from the casket, the ceiling tiles of the funeral home, the exact number of chairs — forty-one — and how his youngest cried into her sleeve so nobody would hear.

Ada had filed it as a prank. A cruel, elaborate prank. Someone who'd known Dale, someone reading the obituary aloud for shock value. It was the only sane explanation and she had wrapped it around herself like a coat.

Now Mrs. Aldous was wearing the same coat.

"Why are you calling me?" Ada asked. Her voice came out steady. She was distantly proud of that, and distantly horrified — the steadiness was automatic, deeper than choice, the keeping voice doing its work whether she willed it or not.

"Same reason Dale called, I expect." A pause, filled with rain. "We wanted to hear a familiar voice. It's quiet where we are, honey. Quiet isn't right for us. We're not meant to be quiet yet."

"Where are you?" Stupid question. Necessary question.

"Close." The word landed softly and heavily at once, like snow. "Closer than we were. That's partly why I'm calling. To pass along a message, since I've got the chance and you've got the reach." The voice gathered itself, and Ada heard — or imagined she heard — other voices underneath it now, faint, layered, a congregation murmuring behind one speaker. "Tell them we're coming back. Tell them we're coming home, and they should leave the doors the way they like them. Tell them—" and here the layering thickened, the voices rising like water coming up through floorboards, many mouths saying one sentence in ragged unison, "—tell them we remember the count."

The line went to dead air tone.

Ada sat very still. The ON AIR sign bled its red onto the glass. Down the hall, past the supply closet and the transmitter room, the steel exit door stood shut, and she found herself counting it — *one* — and then counting backward through the whole building, *two, three, four*, all the way to eleven, checking, checking, the way her grandmother had checked, the way her great-grandmother must have checked, the way women in her line had been checking for longer than the town had had a name.

*They remember the count.*

Her grandmother's phrase. Her family's private liturgy. Something no stranger should know, something Ada had never spoken on air, never written down, never told a living soul outside the kitchen on Delmar Street.

The rain thickened. Somewhere in the building a fluorescent tube buzzed and flickered, and Ada got up — legs unsteady, professional smile nowhere, no one to see anyway — and walked the corridor slowly, touching each doorframe as she passed, whispering the threshold words under her breath, re-salting nothing because there was no salt here, just the touch and the whisper and the count, until she stood in front of the alarmed exit door with her palm flat against cold steel.

Through the little wired-glass window, the parking lot lay empty under the lights. Rain silvered down through the cones of illumination. Nothing moved.

But the night felt attended. That was the only word she had for it, and it was her grandmother's word, dredged up from some overheard conversation decades old: *the night feels attended tonight, Ruthie.* As if the dark were a room and something had entered it and was standing politely, patiently, the way callers stood in queue, waiting to be put through.

Ada pressed her forehead against the door. The steel was cold enough to ache.

"They remember the count," she said aloud, to no one, to the door, to whatever listened on either side of it. "What does that mean? What am I supposed to do?"

The building hummed. The rain fell. And in the break room, faintly, through two walls, the dying refrigerator breathed in and out, in and out, like something sleeping just below hearing, like something that had been asleep a very long time and was, night by night, call by call, beginning to stir.

When she went back to the booth, the phone was ringing again.

Line two. Line three. Line four. All of them at once, blinking red in the dark like a row of small urgent hearts.

Ada looked at them for a long moment. Then, because she was her grandmother's granddaughter, because she had a keeping voice and a keeping name, she reached out — hand trembling now, no hiding it, no one to hide it from — and answered the first call.

Outside, far to the north, past the county line, past the dark fields and the sleeping town of Hollow Creek, the old radio tower stood above its sealed well, its beacon turning, turning, turning against the clouds.

And dawn was still hours away.
