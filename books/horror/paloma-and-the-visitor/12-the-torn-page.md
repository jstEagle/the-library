## 12. The Torn Page

Marcus Delgado had spent eleven years learning to read a signal the way other men read weather. He could tell you the difference between a cold front rolling in off the ridge and a truck's ignition interfering with the AM band. He could tell when the tower was in a good mood. That was the phrase he'd always used, privately, to himself, in the little cinderblock room beneath the transmitter shack where the equipment hummed its one long note: the tower's in a good mood tonight. He had never let himself think too hard about what it meant that a tower could have moods at all.

Now, at four in the morning, with Ada's voice still echoing somewhere behind his sternum — *it's holding something in, Marcus* — he sat in that room with the tower's maintenance schematics spread across the workbench, and the tower was in a very bad mood indeed.

The hum had changed. Anyone else would have missed it. But Marcus had fallen asleep to that hum for a decade, and he knew its pitch the way a mother knows a cry. It had dropped. Not much. A quarter tone, maybe less. Like something enormous shifting its weight in its sleep. Like something settling in closer to the walls.

The schematics told him nothing useful. They were the county's drawings, 1971, all utility and conduit, the tower rendered as a stick figure with lightning rods for hair. But the tower was older than 1971. Everyone in Hollow Creek knew that, the way everyone knew the creek ran backward in spring. The original structure went up in the 1840s, before radio was radio — before there was anything to transmit. Marcus had always filed that fact under the town's usual folder of charming nonsense. A tower before radio. A church without a congregation. A well with a lid like a coffin lid.

He'd filed a lot of things in that folder. The folder was full now. It was splitting at the seams.

He pulled the county drawings aside and went looking for the older ones. Every station kept its history in boxes, and Hollow Creek's station had been the town's attic for a hundred and fifty years. He found the box he wanted on the third shelf of the storage room, under a dead raccoon trap and a stack of hymnals water-stained into soft gray bricks: *Hollow Creek Municipal Records, Transfer — 1971.* Inside, wrapped in wax paper gone brittle as autumn, were the founder's drawings.

They were beautiful. That was the first thing that unsettled him. No county surveyor had drawn these; these were the work of someone who loved the thing they were building. Ink on linen, hand-ruled borders, the lettering ornate as scripture. The tower rose from the page in careful section — but it wasn't drawn as a tower. It was drawn as a *needle.* A needle driven into a body, and the body was the hill, and beneath the hill, in cross-section rendered with the delicacy of an anatomist, was the well.

The well was not a well. Marcus sat with that for a long moment, the workbench lamp buzzing, the hum in the walls dropping another quarter tone. The structure beneath the hill had been drawn in layers, like a diagram of an ear. Chambers. Channels. A central cavity that the draughtsman had shaded so carefully it seemed to pulse off the page. And running down through all of it, from the surface to the shaded heart, a single vertical shaft — and the shaft was labeled, in that ornate hand, not *well* and not *shaft* and not *cistern* but *throat.*

The founders had built a mouth. The tower was the needle. The town — he turned the page, and there it was, the plat map of Hollow Creek itself, every street and lot drawn in the same loving ink — the town was laid out around the hill in concentric rings, like ripples, like the sound-pattern a struck bell makes, and at the center of the pattern, at the exact point where all the streets converged if you extended them far enough, stood the tower.

A town built as an instrument. He'd read enough of Ada's grandmother's ledger by now — she'd left it with him two nights ago, pressed into his hands with the cassette tapes and a look he'd never seen on her face before, a look like a woman handing over a diagnosis — to know the word the founders used for what they'd made. *The Keeping.* He'd assumed it meant keeping something out.

The drawings were suggesting it meant keeping something *in* the way a farmer keeps a herd in.

* * *

He worked until the windows went gray. He photographed every page with his phone, hands steadier than he felt, and then he did what he always did when the paperwork ran out and the questions didn't: he went up the tower.

The stairs were open ironwork, shivering under his boots, the fog of the early morning beading on the rails. The fog had been coming off the hill every night that week, thick and low and warm, warm like breath, and the town had taken to shutting its windows against it. From the platform at the top, Hollow Creek was a scatter of yellow windows in a white sea, and the fog moved through the streets in a way fog should not move — in lanes, in currents, following the concentric rings of the old plat map, circling the tower like water finding a drain.

Marcus put his palm flat against the tower's leg. The metal was warm. Not sun-warm; there was no sun yet. Warm from inside, warm like a throat that has been speaking for a long time.

"Okay," he said out loud, because the alternative was not saying anything, and not saying anything had stopped working around the time the dead started calling the request line. "Okay. What are you."

The hum answered. Not in words. In pitch. It dropped again, that quarter tone, and this time he felt it through his palm, through his boots, through the soles of his feet and up into the roots of his teeth, and it was not a machine's hum. Machines don't lean. This leaned. Toward him. Toward the sound of a voice, the way a plant leans toward a window.

He thought of the backward recordings. He thought of Ada's grandmother's voice resolving out of the static, patient and clear, reciting numbers. He thought of the dead callers, how polite they were, how *patient,* how they never demanded, only asked, only waited at the edges of headlights with their hats in their hands. And he thought — with the particular cold that comes when a thought arrives already knowing it's true — that everything in this town that spoke had been speaking *toward* the hill. The dead called the station. The static resolved in the tower's recordings. The hymns at the church, he'd heard them Sunday through the open windows, and they'd been pitched low, pitched *down,* the whole congregation singing into the ground.

The town wasn't praying. The town was feeding.

He climbed down faster than he'd climbed up.

* * *

The tower's base was concrete, poured in 1971 over the older foundation, and the older foundation was stone — fieldstone, fitted without mortar, the kind of work that outlasts the countries that made it. There was an access hatch, a rusted steel door set into the concrete skirt, and Marcus had opened it twice in eleven years, both times for inspections, both times finding nothing but a crawl space and a smell of cold mineral dark.

The door was already open when he got there.

Not broken. Not forced. Open the way a door is opened for you. The hinges had been oiled recently — the smell of it, machine oil, sharp and new. Someone had been here. Or something had wanted him to think so.

He stood in the fog with his flashlight in his hand for a full minute, and he will never be able to explain, afterward, why he went in. Except that the hum had dropped again, and it was not a threatening sound. It was a *welcoming* one. It was the sound his grandmother's stove had made when she was baking bread, the sound of a house that expects you.

The crawl space was bigger than the county drawings said. Of course it was. The concrete skirt was a shell, a false wall, and behind it the fieldstone curved away into a chamber that had no business existing under a municipal radio tower — a round room, maybe twelve feet across, the stone walls dry as paper in a climate where everything rotted. Someone had kept it dry. Someone had kept it for a very long time.

There was a shelf. There was a lamp — an actual oil lamp, brass, polished, full. There was a chair.

And on the shelf, in a row, were the ledgers.

Not one ledger. Nine of them. Leather-bound, spines cracked, arranged in chronological order by a hand that cared about order the way the draughtsman had cared about his ink. Marcus knew the earliest one on sight. He'd held it. Ada had it now, or what they'd all thought was the whole of it — the founders' ledger with its lists of keepers, its Galloways always last, its dates and its terrible gentle word, *renewed, renewed, renewed.*

The volume on the shelf was its twin. Same binding, same hand. And tucked into the front of it, loose, folded twice, was a page torn from a book Marcus had held in his own hands two nights ago. The tear matched the gap in the back of Ada's ledger exactly. He knew it the way you know your own front door.

He didn't touch it at first. He stood in the cold dry dark with the lamp unlit and read what he could by the spill of his flashlight, and what he could read was enough to make him sit down in the chair, because the chair was there, because someone had put a chair there for exactly this.

The torn page was not a list of names. It was instructions.

The founders' hand, the ornate loving script, and it said — he read it three times, he would read it forty times in the days to come, he would read it aloud to Ada with his voice breaking on the third pass — it said that the thing beneath the hill was not a prisoner. It said the word *prisoner* appeared exactly once, crossed out with a single elegant line, and above the crossed-out word, written in the same ink, the correction: *livestock.*

It said the Keeping was a keeping the way a shepherd keeps. It said the founders had come to this valley because the thing was already here, old before them, asleep in the stone, and it dreamed, and its dreams leaked upward as voices, and the voices could be *caught* — caught in bells, caught in hymns, caught in wire, when wire came — and the caught voices were nourishment, and the nourishment kept the thing asleep, and the sleeping kept the dreams coming, and the arrangement, the page said, the arrangement was *generous to all parties.* The dead got to finish their sentences. The town got to keep its dead close, warm, speaking. And the thing below got its meal, served nightly, in voices.

The tower was not a lighthouse. The tower was a *straw.*

And the ritual — the renewal, the blood of the line spoken into the tower at the turning of the season — the page was explicit about this, in a hand that had begun to shake, in a hand that had pressed so hard the pen had torn the paper in two places: the ritual did not seal anything. The ritual was the *seasoning.* The blood of the line was not a lock. It was a mark of quality, a promise, a vintage — it taught the thing below what its food was supposed to taste like, generation after generation, so that the herd would stay sweet, so that the dreams would stay rich, so that the voices caught in the wire would keep coming up warm and willing and *familiar.*

The last lines were different ink. Older, or more desperate. Marcus had to hold the page close to the flashlight to make them out, the letters small and crowded into the bottom margin like a man whispering after the sermon:

*We did not build a town. We built a farm, and we are the fence, and the fence is fed first. God forgive us. God forgive us. It is not enough to keep it asleep. We have made it love the taste of us. Find the other way. There is no other way. Find the other way.*

The page was dated. The date was 1849. And beneath the date, in the same cramped whisper, a single word that Marcus recognized from the ledger, from every page of the ledger, the word that had followed every Galloway name like a shadow follows a body:

*Renewed.*

* * *

He came out of the crawl space into a morning that had no right to be as beautiful as it was. The fog was burning off the hill in gold rags. The town below was waking — smoke from chimneys, the school bus grinding up the far road, Reverend Hale's church bell ringing eight o'clock the way it had rung eight o'clock for a hundred and seventy years, and Marcus stood there with the torn page folded in his jacket, against his chest, and heard the bell for what it was.

Not a call to worship. A dinner bell.

He thought of the dead callers. He thought of them with a grief that surprised him, that doubled him over at the waist so he had to put a hand on the tower's warm leg. All those voices on the request line, all those funerals described in loving detail, all that patience at the edges of headlights — he had thought they were haunting the town. They were the town's *produce.* They were what the farm yielded. The dead didn't come back wrong because the ritual had failed. The dead came back because the ritual had *worked,* for a hundred and seventy years, and what Marcus had been hearing on the overnight frequencies, what Ada had been taking calls from, was not a haunting.

It was a harvest coming in.

And the hum beneath his hand — the hum he'd slept beside for eleven years, the hum he'd called the tower's good mood — shifted again, dropped again, leaned again, and this time he understood the lean, and understanding did not help. It was leaning toward the town. Toward the yellow windows. Toward the voices. It was hungry in the patient way that only very old things are hungry, the way that has outlasted every urgency, and it was being fed, it had always been fed, it was being fed *right now* by every word spoken in Hollow Creek that drifted downhill on the morning air — and the page in his jacket said *find the other way* in a hand that had torn the paper, and the page also said *there is no other way,* and both lines were written by the same man on the same night, and Marcus stood in the gold-ragged fog and realized that Ada Galloway was scheduled to walk up these stairs in three days, and that everything she'd been told about what waited at the top of them was a lie told by dead men to keep the farm running.

He got in his truck. He drove toward her house with the page against his chest like a second heartbeat, and behind him the tower hummed, and the hum was not a machine's hum, and it had never been a machine's hum, and as he pulled onto the county road he could have sworn — he would swear it later, under the transmitter's changed pitch, to anyone who asked — that the hum followed the shape of a melody. A lullaby. The one Ada's grandmother had been humming on the tapes, backward and forward, patient and clear.

It wasn't a warning. It had never been a warning.

It was a song you sing to something while it eats.
