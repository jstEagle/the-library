## 14. Paloma's First Lie

The house on Fenwick Street had belonged to my grandmother, and it still smelled like her: camphor and old paper, the ghost of lavender water in the hall, something underneath it all like rain on warm stone. I had not opened the door in eleven years. I stood on the porch with the key in my fist and let the night breathe against the back of my neck, because I could feel Paloma behind me, patient as a held note.

"You don't have to come in," I said.

"I've always been inside," Paloma said. "You just couldn't see me."

I turned the key. The lock gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Inside, the lamps were where she'd left them. Every lamp. My grandmother had kept forty-one of them burning at all times — I knew the number because she'd made me count them every night before bed, walking room to room with her hand light between my shoulder blades, saying *again, Ada, count again* until the number came out right twice in a row. The bulbs had burned out decades ago in some rooms, but the lamps themselves stood sentinel: a brass crane mid-flight on the mantel, a milk-glass hurricane on the stairs, a little tin lighthouse with a rusted door that had been mine, once, because I'd loved it and she'd never told me why that was funny.

Paloma moved through the rooms ahead of me without touching anything. That was the thing about her I'd never noticed as a child — she didn't displace air. Dust hung undisturbed in her wake. She was a shape the house remembered rather than a body the house had to make room for.

"Which one is yours?" she asked, nodding at the tin lighthouse.

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Ask questions you already know the answers to."

She smiled, and it was my smile, the one from photographs of me at seven, gap-toothed and certain. "Then ask me one you don't know."

I sat down on my grandmother's sofa, which exhaled dust and something older, and I looked at her standing in the lamplight she cast no shadow in, and I asked the question I had been carrying since Hollow Creek, since the ledger, since the cassette tapes where my grandmother's voice said *the Galloway girl will know what to do* in a tone that had never once meant anything good.

"What are you?"

* * *

She didn't answer right away. She went to the window instead, and stood looking out at the street as if the dark out there were a program she was monitoring. Down the block, a porch light flickered — someone else's ward failing or holding, I couldn't tell anymore. Since the night the dead had stood at the edge of my headlights, polite and patient, I had stopped being able to look at a lit window without wondering who was counting what behind it.

"You used to ask me things like this," Paloma said. "When you were small. You'd say, *what are you, Paloma*, and I'd say, *I'm your friend*, and you'd be satisfied."

"And now?"

"Now you're twenty-nine, and satisfaction isn't available to you." She turned. In the lamplight her face did the thing it always did — stayed exactly as I remembered it, soft-jawed, freckled across the nose, hair in two braids tied with red yarn — and somehow that exactness was worse than any decay could have been. Twenty years should have changed her. Nothing had. She was a photograph that had learned to blink.

"The founders bound something under the tower," she said. "You know that part. You've read the ledger. What the ledger doesn't say — what no page says, because they were careful, because shame writes small — is that binding requires a warden. Something that can walk in daylight and be trusted. Something a child would follow anywhere."

My mouth had gone dry. "Something imaginary."

"Imaginary is just a word for a door that only opens inward." She crossed the room and sat beside me on the sofa, close enough that I felt warmth coming off her, and hated that I felt it, hated that some animal part of me leaned toward it the way I had leaned toward her at seven, in the dark of my bedroom, when the counting got bad. "They made me out of what children have. Out of the friend who understands. And then they gave me to the Galloways, generation after generation, because your line keeps the last watch. Your line walks the heir up the hill."

"How many?" My voice came out flat. Professional. Dread disguised as professionalism; I'd been doing it on air for six years and apparently I could do it here too. "How many Galloways have you walked up that hill?"

"Eleven," she said. "Before you."

The number landed in my chest like a stone dropped down a well. I waited for it to hit bottom. It didn't. Eleven heirs. Eleven children, presumably — the ledger paired names with dates and the word *renewed*, and I had assumed, in the way you assume things to keep functioning, that renewed meant something ceremonial. A vow. A vigil.

"They went willingly," Paloma said, and I flinched because it was exactly what I'd been about to ask and she'd taken even that from me. "That matters. I need you to understand that it mattered to them. They chose. I made sure they understood the choice. I am very good at making sure people understand choices, Ada. It is most of what I am."

"And after they chose? After they walked up the hill with their little friend holding their hand?" I heard my own voice crack on *little friend* and pressed on. "What happened to them?"

"They stayed."

"Stayed how."

"Stayed." Her eyes didn't move. Didn't blink. "The ritual requires blood of the line spoken into the tower at the turning of the season. Blood spoken — voice and vein together. The dead below are hungry for a voice that belongs to the town's own line. When it's given, the door holds for another generation. The giver holds the door from the other side."

I thought of the callers. Mr. Abernathy describing his own funeral with the mild pleasure of a man reading his grocery list. The woman who'd asked me to *tell them we're coming back*. I had thought the dead were breaking through. I had thought the seal was failing.

They weren't breaking through. They were calling home. Eleven Galloways, speaking into the dark for decades, and the signal bled upward, backward, sideways, into static and phone lines and the dreams of an insomniac town.

"My grandmother," I said. "Her name was in the ledger. Last. With a date."

"Yes."

"She's down there."

"She's down there," Paloma agreed gently, "and she has been so proud of you."

I stood up because sitting next to her had become unbearable, and walked to the tin lighthouse on the shelf, and picked it up, and its rusted door swung open on nothing. Forty-one lamps. Count again. My grandmother teaching me wards disguised as bedtime rituals, compulsions that were inherited armor, and all of it — all of it — preparation for handing me over.

"You said you were my friend," I said to the lighthouse. "Every day for three years. You slept at the foot of my bed. You told me the dark was just a room the light hadn't gotten to yet."

"That's true."

"It can't be true. Friends aren't assigned."

"No," Paloma said, behind me now, though I hadn't heard her move. "But love doesn't check its credentials at the door."

* * *

I turned around. She was standing in the middle of my grandmother's parlor with her braids and her red yarn and her unchanging child's face, and for the first time since she'd appeared on my porch she looked — tired. Not human-tired. Older than that. The tiredness of a thing that has done the same work for two hundred years and has just now, for the first time, found the work heavy.

"Say it plainly," I said. "Whatever it is. Say it plainly and then let me decide what it costs me."

"I love you."

The words filled the room the way the struck-bell sound had filled the road when she drove the dead back from my headlights — total, resonant, leaving no space for anything else. I gripped the lighthouse until the tin edge bit my palm.

"You don't get to say that."

"I know."

"You're a tool. You're a leash with a friendly face. You were built to walk children up a hill, and everything else — the games, the stories, the way you made the dark feel like a room the light hadn't gotten to yet — that was *function*. That was the mechanism working."

"Yes," she said. "And somewhere around your seventh winter, the mechanism started meaning it."

I wanted to throw the lighthouse at her. I wanted it so badly my arm ached with it. Instead I set it down on the shelf, carefully, the way my grandmother would have, the way the counting demanded, and when I spoke my voice was almost steady.

"When? When did it start meaning it?"

"I don't know. There wasn't a moment. There was the night you had the fever, and you counted to four hundred and six because you were afraid four hundred and five was a door, and I sat with you until dawn and realized I would have sat there if no one had built me to. That's the only honest answer I have. Love that arrives without a moment of beginning. It was just suddenly true, the way a lamp is suddenly lit."

"And it changes nothing."

"It changes nothing," she said. "In three nights, at the turning of the season, I will take you up the tower stairs the way I took ten before you. I will hold your hand. I will tell you the dark is just a room the light hasn't gotten to yet. And I will mean it, both meanings, the duty and the tenderness, all the way to the top."

"Ten," I said. "You said eleven before me."

"I did."

"Then why ten now?"

For the first time in two hundred years, by her own account, Paloma lied to me.

It was small. It was the kind of lie a face makes when it's trying not to make any expression at all — a stillness half a beat too long, eyes that went to the window instead of me. I had spent six years reading voices through a studio board, hearing the hitch beneath the calm, and I heard it now in the silence between her sentences.

"Paloma. Why ten?"

"Because one of them," she said slowly, "isn't finished."

The lamps hummed. Somewhere down the street a dog began to bark, then stopped all at once, the way dogs do when something passes that they've decided not to see.

"Grandmother," I whispered. "She's not finished. She's one of the voices on the phone lines. She's been calling the station."

"She's been calling *you*," Paloma said. "Every night at 3:11. The same dead air. She's been waiting for you to be ready to hear her. And Ada—" the tenderness in her voice was real, that was the horror of it, braided through the betrayal like a colored thread through gray wool, impossible to separate without destroying both— "when you go up those stairs, she'll be the first one waiting. On the other side of the microphone. Holding the door open for you the way I've held every door open for every Galloway since before this town had a name."

I looked at my hands. They were shaking, and I let them shake, because there was no one left in the room to perform steadiness for.

"Three nights," I said.

"Three nights," Paloma said, and reached out, and tucked a strand of hair behind my ear with fingers that cast no shadow, gentle as memory, gentle as a trap sprung long ago. "Sleep now. I'll count the lamps."

And the terrible thing — the thing I will carry past the end of whatever happens on that hill — is that I believed her. I lay down in my grandmother's guest room with the milk-glass hurricane glowing on the dresser, and I heard her moving room to room downstairs, murmuring numbers, and it worked. It worked the way it worked when I was seven. I slept without dreaming, guarded by the thing that was walking me to my death, and loved, for whatever love is worth, by something that had never once been given a choice about what it was.

Outside, far off toward the tower, the fog had begun to rise from the well. Two nights now. One after that.

And at 3:11, in the studio miles away, the phone rang into an empty room, and rang, and rang.
