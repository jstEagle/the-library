## 3. What the Frost Knows

The frost came to Hollowmere House the way grief comes to a family—first at the edges, then everywhere at once.

Ansel noticed it on the third night of December, during his rounds. The windows of the east ward had bloomed overnight with patterns too deliberate for weather: ferns and feathers and long branching rivers of ice that seemed less grown than *drawn*, as if some patient hand had been working all night with a needle of cold. He stood in the corridor outside Room 4 with his clipboard against his chest and watched the frost move. Not melt. Move. A tendril extended itself along the glass while he watched, slow as a second hand, reaching toward the center of the pane like a man feeling his way through a dark room.

He told himself it was condensation. He was very good at telling himself things; he'd had decades of practice. Detachment wasn't just professional habit for Ansel Amberdale—it was architecture. He had built himself out of corridors and doorways, out of polite distances, out of the careful grammar of clinical notes. *Patient resting comfortably.* *No distress observed.* He had learned young that the safest place in any house was the hallway, and he had lived there ever since, in every house, including this one.

But the frost did not respect hallways.

* * *

The recordings were worse now.

He had taken to reviewing them in the staff room at dawn, after the counting stopped, when the light came up gray over the frozen gardens and the coffee machine made its small asthmatic sounds. Nine patients, nine channels. He would sit with his headphones on and the notebook open and transcribe what he heard, because transcription was a kind of control, because ink on paper could not reach into your chest.

The voice came every night now. Low, unhurried, genderless in the way that deep water is genderless. It moved from bed to bed like a nurse making rounds, and it asked its questions, and the sleepers answered gladly, in that warm slurred voice people keep only for their most private hours.

*"And where did you keep the photographs?"*

*"In the shoebox. Under the sweaters."*

*"And who gave you the sweaters?"*

And so on. All night. It never asked anything cruel. That was the thing Ansel couldn't make anyone understand—it wasn't cruel. It was *interested*. It asked about first bicycles and dead husbands and the smell of particular kitchens, and the patients answered like children showing a visitor their treasures, and by morning they were always a little lighter. A little thinner behind the eyes. Mrs. Calloway, down to eleven days now by Marisol's arithmetic, had begun leaving sentences unfinished, as if she'd given away the endings along with everything else.

Ansel transcribed. He drank his coffee. He kept his voice in the hallway of himself.

Then, on the fourth of December, channel six—Mr. Aldous, sleeping soundly—the voice said:

*"And who else sleeps in this house?"*

Silence. Long enough that Ansel's hand went still on the pen.

*"There is a man who walks the halls,"* Mr. Aldous said, dreamy, agreeable. *"The night nurse. Tall. Careful. He doesn't talk to us much."*

*"Does he have a name?"*

*"Amberdale. Like the color of beer, he says. He makes jokes like that so you won't ask him anything real."*

Ansel took off the headphones. Put them back on. Cross-talk, he wrote in the margin, and underlined it twice, and hated how many times he had written that word lately, how it was becoming the load-bearing wall of his sanity.

*"And where does the tall nurse come from?"* the voice asked, patient as frost.

*"I don't know,"* Aldous said. *"But he hums sometimes. When he thinks no one's awake. Same four bars, over and over. Never the fifth."*

Ansel sat in the gray light with his heart going hard and strange, because it was true. He hummed when he worked. Four bars, over and over. His mother's song, or what remained of it—the part she'd taught him before the part she wouldn't. He had never once hummed it consciously. He had certainly never done it within hearing of the patients.

Had he?

* * *

By the sixth of December, the voice had stopped asking the patients about themselves.

It still made its rounds—he could hear it moving down the channels, one to nine—but the questions had changed direction entirely, the way a searchlight swings off the road and onto the house beside it.

*"Your mother,"* it said to Mrs. Voss in channel two, *"did she have a kitchen with a blue door?"*

*"Everyone's mother had a blue door once,"* Mrs. Voss said fondly.

*"And in the kitchen, a table with a wobble. And a radio that only liked one station. And a woman singing while she peeled apples, singing a song with a hole in it, a song that stops—"*

Ansel ripped the headphones off.

He stood in the staff room shaking, the headphones dangling from his fist like something venomous. Because he knew that kitchen. Not remembered—*knew*, the way you know a bruise. Blue door. Wobbling table. Apples. His mother standing at the counter with her back to him, singing, and the song stopping mid-phrase every time, cut clean, like a thread snipped with scissors, and little Ansel waiting for the rest and the rest never coming, and never asking why, because even at five he had understood somehow that some questions are doors you don't open after dark.

He had never told anyone about that kitchen. There was no one left to tell.

The frost on the staff room window had formed, overnight, into shapes. He made himself look. Letters—or almost letters, ice-ferns arranged with an ambiguity that might have been accident if accident weren't so persistent. He read them the way you read clouds, knowing he was projecting, unable to stop:

**H E R K I T C H E N**

and below, smaller, in a scrawl of feathered frost:

**W H A T   D O E S   T H E   S O N G   S P A R E**

He wiped the window with his sleeve. The words smeared into ordinary ice. Outside, the gardens lay white and silent, and the sky had the flat pewter look of a sky that has agreed to hold its breath until spring.

*What does the song spare.* He turned the phrase over and over like a stone with something underneath it. Spare. As in save. As in leave untouched. As in the last verse of a lullaby being the one verse the dark can't—

He didn't finish the thought. Finishing thoughts had become dangerous work.

* * *

That night he did rounds early and fast, checking vitals with the brisk tenderness he'd perfected over fifteen years, keeping his voice low and neutral, humming nothing. In Room 7, Mrs. Ibarra slept with her mouth slightly open, counting—she'd started at forty tonight, which Marisol said meant days—and between numbers her lips shaped words too soft for the recorder to catch clearly.

In Room 6, Mr. Aldous was not asleep.

He was sitting up against his pillows in the dark, eyes open, fixed on the window where the frost had climbed halfway down the glass in ropes and braids. His oxygen cannula traced a thin line across his face. When Ansel came in, the old man didn't turn his head.

"It's learning you," Aldous said. His voice was dry as paper in winter. "You hear it too. Don't lie to me, boy. Lying feeds it."

"Mr. Aldous, you should be resting—"

"It started with us because we're thin already. Doors half-open, see? Easy to slip questions through." Now he turned, and his eyes in the dark were wet and terribly clear. "But you're a locked house, you are. So it's picking the lock slow. Asking everyone in the building about you. Building you out of other people's answers, the way frost builds a picture out of breath."

Ansel's mouth had gone dry. "What picture?"

Aldous raised one trembling hand and pointed at the window.

Ansel looked. The frost there was different from the others—not ferns, not letters. A figure. Small. A child seen from behind, standing in a doorway, one hand raised as if waving goodbye or reaching for someone just out of frame. The proportions were childlike, artless—and yet the set of the narrow shoulders, the way the head tilted slightly upward toward whoever it was reaching for, snagged something in Ansel's chest with a hook he recognized from thirty years away.

"That's not me," he said.

"No," Aldous agreed softly. "It's you the way it remembers you. It doesn't have your memories yet. It has our *answers* about you. It's making a copy from the outside in." He began, gently, to cry—not sobbing, just leaking, the way old men cry, as if the body had gotten tired of holding the level. "When the copy's finished, son, it won't need to ask you anything. It'll just knock, and you'll answer, because it'll sound exactly like home."

Ansel reached for the call button, for the chart, for anything with a procedure attached. "Let me get you something for—"

"It already has your half," Aldous whispered.

Ansel froze. "My half?"

"The song. Your mother's song. You've been humming the front half of it down these halls for three nights like a fool ringing a dinner bell." Aldous's hand found Ansel's wrist with surprising strength, cold fingers closing around the pulse point. "She cut the song in two to keep a thing out—that's old knowledge, my grandmother knew songs like that, songs you don't finish. But a cut song is a door held shut with one hand, boy, and it's been listening to your half every night, learning the tune, learning the *shape of the missing part*—" His grip tightened. "It doesn't want the whole song. It wants the hole in it. The hole is a door too."

Down the hall, faintly, through two closed doors, a sleeper's voice rose and fell, counting. Thirty-one. Thirty. Twenty-nine.

And beneath it—so low Ansel might have imagined it, except that Aldous heard it too, and his face went the color of the frost—a second voice, patient and pleased, humming four bars.

Over and over.

Never the fifth.

Aldous released Ansel's wrist and sank back against his pillows, exhausted, his eyes drifting toward the window and the small frozen child in the doorway. "Find the rest of the song," he murmured, already sliding under. "Before it finds it first. Before it learns to sing the ending *to* you, in your own—"

He was asleep. Or zero-bound. Ansel couldn't tell anymore where one ended and the other began.

He stood a long moment in the dark of Room 6, his own pulse hammering under the fading print of those cold fingers. Then he walked quickly—not running, nurses don't run—to the supply room where he'd stowed, three weeks ago and without knowing why, the single box he'd brought from his mother's estate. The box he had never opened.

His hands were steady until they touched the lid.

From somewhere above him—second floor, empty wing, no patients, no beds—the humming stopped.

And in the silence that followed, clear as a bell rung once in an empty church, a voice that was almost his mother's said his name.
