## 8. Every Bed Counts Down

**Ansel**

The counting began at 2:14 in the morning, all nine rooms at once.

I knew it before I knew it—the way you know a storm has changed direction by the taste of the air. I was in the nurses' station with the recordings playing back, Marisol's ledger open on my knee, when the sound came up through the floor like water rising through a well. Not one voice. Not nine voices. One voice in nine mouths, all of them counting backwards from different numbers, and all of them—this was the part that made my hands go still on the desk—descending at the same rate.

Room 1: *four hundred and six, four hundred and five.* Old Mr. Voss, who hadn't spoken a full sentence since October.

Room 4: *two hundred and eleven, two hundred and ten.* Delia Chen, whose lungs were paper.

Room 9: *ninety, eighty-nine.* Little Tomas, eleven years old, the only child Hollowmere had ever agreed to take.

Different sums. Same tempo. Same destination. Zero at dawn, and dawn was three hours away.

Marisol was already in the doorway. She had her coat on over her scrubs and the ledger clutched against her chest like a shield, and her face in the green emergency light was the face of someone watching a house burn from the inside.

"It's never done this," she said. "Three winters. It's never done *all of them.*"

"Because it never had a reason to." I stood. My legs felt borrowed. "It's not counting them down, Marisol. It's counting *me* down. They're just the clock it's using."

The frost had started at the windows again, but it was wrong tonight—too fast, too deliberate, ferning across the glass in strokes like handwriting, like the quick confident script of someone taking dictation. And the doors. I heard them before I saw them: a sound like a held breath released, and when I pulled at the handle of the east exit the door didn't budge. Not locked. *Frozen.* The frame had grown ice inward, a seamless weld of white, thick as a wrist, and when I put my palm against it the cold went through me like a question.

The west door was the same. The windows of the ward itself had gone opaque, white ferns layered over white ferns until the night outside was only a rumor. Hollowmere had closed its mouth.

"Back door," Marisol said, and we went, and it was the same, and the kitchen windows, and the little window over the linen closet that had never once in its life frosted over—it was white as a wedding.

We ended up in the corridor between rooms 1 and 9 because it was the only place left to be, and because the patients were calling for us. Not screaming. That was the terrible part. They were counting in the gentle, unhurried voices of children saying their prayers, and between numbers they said our names.

*"Ansel,"* said Delia Chen, kindly, from behind her door. *"Two hundred and four. Ansel, it wants to know you."*

*"Marisol,"* said Mr. Voss. *"You burned the pages. It remembers the pages."*

Marisol's hand found mine in the dark. Her fingers were freezing, but her grip was alive, human, furious.

"Don't answer them," she whispered. "Whatever they say. Whatever voice comes out of their mouths. Don't answer *anything* after dark. You know the rule."

"I know the rule."

"Say it back to me."

"Never answer aloud."

"Never answer aloud," she repeated, and then, softer, in the voice of a woman confessing to a priest she doesn't believe in: "Ansel, I have to tell you what I burned. Before it makes me tell you anyway. Half that ledger wasn't patients."

* * *

**Marisol**

I had sworn I would carry it into the ground. Three winters I had carried it, and the winter before those, and the winter before that, back to the year I was nineteen and my sister Lucia stopped answering her own name.

But the corridor was full of counting, and the frost was writing on the inside of the windows now, and I understood that secrets were a currency this house had stopped accepting.

"Lucia was a nurse too," I said. "Before you. Before me. She worked the winter shift at Hollowmere in—God, it must have been eighty-one. She used to hum while she worked. She had a song from our grandmother, from the funeral, the one they play when they're sending someone across the water. She sang it to the dying. She thought it was a kindness."

Ansel had gone very still. In the green light his face had the look of a man reading his own name on a document he was not supposed to see.

"The song had four verses," I said. "Everyone knows the first three. They're in every hymnal in three counties. *Sleep, my heart, the river's wide, the ferryman is kind.* That one. But there was a fourth. Our grandmother taught it only to Lucia, and Lucia sang it only to herself, and one night in February she sang it in room 6, to a woman named Ada Brandt, and Ada Brandt reached zero at dawn, and Lucia—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I let it. "Lucia walked out into the snow in her nurse's shoes and they found her at the gate in the spring, when it melted, standing up, frozen, facing the house. And she was smiling. And when they buried her, her mouth was still moving. Not much. Just enough. Just enough that at the graveside, my mother leaned close, and came back white as the casket, and said: *she's answering someone.*"

The counting rolled over us. *One hundred and one. One hundred.* The numbers were falling faster now, or the night was getting shorter, or both.

"So when I saw the frost spelling out a kitchen, when I heard the recordings asking about a mother and a song—" I took his wrist. Under his skin his pulse was going like a bird in a box. "Ansel. It doesn't want your patients. It has never wanted the patients. They're practice. They're *tuning.* It's been learning the shape of you the way I learned Lucia's face, and the song is the door, and your mother cut off the last verse because she knew—she *knew*—that the last verse is the part where the singer says goodbye, and the thing in the cold has been waiting forty years for someone to say goodbye *to it.*"

"And if I finish the verse," he said slowly, "it takes the goodbye. It takes—"

"It takes what answers. It takes what *belongs* to the song. And you belong to it, Ansel. You've belonged to it since before you could talk. That's what it's been cataloguing. That's what it's been *counting.*"

Room 9 went quiet.

Then Tomas's voice came through the door, and it was not Tomas's voice. It was low and patient and old as the underside of a lake, and it said, in the accentless tone of something that has never needed a mouth:

*"Who sang you to sleep, Ansel?"*

* * *

**Ansel**

The question went through me like frost through a window—not fast, but total, every pane of me whitening at once.

Because I knew the answer. That was the horror of it. I had known it all along, the way you know the layout of a house you lived in before you were four. There was a kitchen with a blue enamel stove and a crack in the linoleum shaped like a river, and there was a chair pulled up to the counter, and there was a woman with flour on her hands and my whole name in her mouth, and there was a song, and the song had a sound like a river being kind, and she sang it every night of my small life until the night she stopped, mid-verse, mid-*word*, and never sang again, and never said why, and I had spent thirty years calling that silence *grief* when it was *protection.*

She had cut the last verse off to keep something from answering her.

And I was standing in a frozen corridor with the question hanging in the air like a held note, and every cell in my body wanted to say *my mother, her name was—* wanted it the way the drowning want air, because the answer wasn't just information. The answer was *belonging.* I could feel it waiting behind my teeth, warm, whole, the entire lost country of my childhood folded up inside four words, and all I had to do was open my mouth and be home again.

"Ansel." Marisol's hand on my arm, her voice cracked down the middle. "Look at me. Look at *me.*"

I looked at her. Behind her, frost was climbing the walls in ferns and feathers, and in the frost, in every window of every door, the same portrait was forming over and over: a small boy in a kitchen chair, watching a woman's back, waiting for a song.

"Whatever it's offering," she said, "it's offering it in *her* voice. Isn't it. You can hear her. You can hear her about to sing."

I could. God help me, I could. The kitchen was so close. The flour, the river-shaped crack, the blue stove ticking as it cooled. I had spent my whole life at arm's length from everyone—patients, colleagues, Marisol, the whole warm breathing world—because I thought the thing I'd lost was people. It wasn't. It was one kitchen. It was one song, three verses and a silence. And it was right there, one honest answer away, and the voice knew it, and it said, through nine doors, through the walls, through the frost, gently, the way you'd speak to a child at bedtime:

*"Just answer. And everyone wakes up. The counting stops. The doors open. You can go home, Ansel. You can go all the way home."*

My lips parted.

The cold rushed toward my mouth like water toward a drain.

And somewhere below us, in the foundations of Hollowmere House, in the place where the counting came from, the number reached *one*—all nine rooms, all nine voices, one breath, one beat of silence like the whole night inhaling—

—and I did not answer.

I did the other thing. The thing my mother had been too afraid to do for thirty years.

I started to sing.

* * *

**Marisol**

He sang the third verse. The one everyone knows. *Sleep, my heart, the river's wide, the ferryman is kind.* His voice was terrible—flat, cracking, a nurse's voice, not a singer's—and it was the bravest sound I have ever heard, because he was singing it *alone*, refusing the harmony that the walls were offering him, refusing to let the voice in his mouth, and the frost on the windows shivered like a struck bell.

And then he stopped, at the edge of the silence where the fourth verse should be, and the whole house leaned in to hear what he would do.

The counting reached zero. Every bed. At once.

The dark after it was the darkest I have ever stood inside.

And Ansel Amberdale, with his eyes full of a kitchen he was losing even as he stood in it, opened his mouth to finish the song.
