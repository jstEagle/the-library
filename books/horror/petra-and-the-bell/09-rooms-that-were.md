## 9. Rooms That Were

Noor had stopped sleeping in the master bedroom on the fourth night, though she could not have said precisely why. The mirrors there were the worst offenders — the tall cheval glass with its tarnished silver frame lagged so badly now that she sometimes caught her reflection still standing when she had already turned away, a stranger wearing her posture, waiting for her to leave before it did something else. So she had moved her cot to the east parlor, where the only mirror was a small hand-glass she kept face-down beneath a folded towel, and where the house's attention felt thinner, distracted, like a host who has stepped out of the room.

It was in that parlor, at half past three in the morning, that she heard her own footsteps going up the stairs.

She was awake — she was almost always awake now, sleep coming to her in shallow, salt-tasting intervals — and she heard them clearly: the particular creak of the seventh stair, the one that dipped in the middle, the one *she* always avoided by hugging the banister. But she was sitting up on the cot, blanket pooled at her waist, and the footsteps were not hers. They were above her, unhurried, deliberate, moving along the upstairs corridor toward the north wing.

Her first thought, absurdly, was of Maren, who had taken the blue guest room and whose warmth Noor had begun to distrust the way you distrust a fire that burns too clean. But Maren slept like the dead — Noor had checked, more than once, telling herself it was for security — and these footsteps had no weight to them. They were the sound of footsteps remembered rather than performed. The house playing back a recording of someone walking.

Or someone walking one second ahead of her.

Noor rose without deciding to rise. Her body made the choice; her mind trailed after it, cataloguing objections it had no intention of enforcing. She took the flashlight from the crate of instruments — the laser measure, the moisture meter, all the little secular reliquaries of her trade, none of which had given an honest reading since Tuesday — and followed the sound into the hall.

The corridor stretched long and dim under the papered ceiling, doors on either side standing open onto rooms she had measured and photographed and entered into her survey as *bedroom, vacant; bedroom, vacant*. At the far end, where the blueprints showed a wall and the county records agreed with the blueprints, stood a door.

It had not been there yesterday.

That was the thought her rational mind offered, and it was true, and it changed nothing. The door was simply there now, set flush in the plaster as if it had always been, dark-stained oak with a brass handle gone green at the touch-points. Thirteen panels, she counted, running her thumb down them without touching — thirteen recessed rectangles arranged in a column, and she understood, with a lurch that had nothing to do with vertigo, that this was the door the arithmetic had been hiding from her since her first day. Twelve doors in the survey. The thirteenth had been waiting to be found, or waiting for her to be ready, which amounted to the same thing in a house like this.

From behind it came the smell of bread baking.

Not the sour mineral breath the cellar exhaled, not the pond-smell of the capped well seeping up through the floorboards on humid nights. Bread. Yeast and butter and woodsmoke, warm and specific, the smell of a kitchen in the last hour before morning. It was such an ordinary smell that Noor's eyes stung, and she hated herself for it, and she pressed her palm flat against the thirteenth panel anyway.

The wood was warm. Not sun-warm — there was no sun, and this was the north wall — but warm the way a body is warm, blood-warm, and beneath her palm she felt something that might have been a pulse, slow and vast, once every few seconds, or might have been the house settling, or might have been her own heartbeat conducted through the timber and returned to her slightly out of time.

The handle turned without resistance.

* * *

The hallway beyond was lit by gaslight.

That was the detail that stopped her, flashlight beam useless against the amber glow that filled the space — actual gaslight, mantles glowing softly inside frosted shades, the kind of light that flickered with a faint hiss she could hear if she held her breath. The wallpaper was different here: deep oxblood damask, unfaded, unpeeled, the pattern of climbing roses so crisp it looked printed yesterday. The floorboards underfoot were pale pine, scrubbed, smelling faintly of beeswax. There was no dust anywhere. In four days of surveying Bellhaven House, Noor had breathed enough dust to line her lungs with plaster, and here there was none, and the absence of it frightened her more than anything the mirrors had done, because dust was time, and this hallway had been excused from time entirely.

Behind her, the door stood open. She looked back at it twice. It remained, a dark rectangle opening onto her own century, her cot visible in the parlor below through nothing — she couldn't see the parlor, actually, only darkness, but she told herself the door was there, that the way back existed, that houses could not simply swallow their exits.

The hallway bent left, following the exterior wall, and the rooms opened off it one after another, and Noor recognized them.

That was the horror of it, or the first layer of it. She had spent days measuring these rooms — or rooms where these rooms should be — and had recorded water damage, collapsed ceilings, floors rotted to the joists. The northwest bedroom, in her photographs, was a cave of black mold and fallen plaster. Here it was furnished in white iron and chintz, the bed made with hospital corners, a washstand holding a pitcher and basin, a child's rocking horse mid-rock, frozen at the top of its arc as though the child had just leapt off. A window seat stacked with books. Rain against the window — real rain, she could hear it, though the night outside the house had been dry for a week.

Each room was occupied by its era. A study lined with ledgers, inkwell wet, a fountain pen uncapped and lying across a page as if the writer had stepped out to answer a summons. A bathroom with a clawfoot tub, steam still rising off water someone had drawn and not yet entered. A nursery. A linen closet with shelves of pressed sheets that smelled, when Noor buried her face in them for one shameful second, of lavender and of something underneath the lavender, something mineral and cold, like the air over deep water.

She walked the length of the hallway with her heart hammering in her ears and her flashlight hanging dead in her hand, because what use was a flashlight in a place already lit, already lived-in, already *kept*?

At the end of the hall, the rooms opened into one another, walls dissolved, until she stood at the threshold of a kitchen that was also a dining room that was also a morning room, all of it suffused with gaslight and the bread-smell grown rich and close, and there was a table.

A long table, dark wood, set for a meal. White cloth. Three places laid with ironstone plates and heavy silver, glasses polished to a shine. A tureen at the center with steam rising from under its lid, curling and reforming, curling and reforming, never thinning, never dissipating — steam that had been rising, Noor understood, for a very long time.

And a woman was setting the table.

She stood with her back to Noor at the far end, laying out cutlery with small precise movements, adjusting each fork a degree, each knife a degree, the way Noor herself adjusted instruments on a site — the way Noor squared her tape measure case on the dashboard every morning, the way she aligned her pens parallel in their tray. The woman wore a high-collared dress of dove-gray cotton, sleeves rolled to the elbow, hair pinned up in a coil that had come partly loose, strands escaping at the nape of her neck. She hummed as she worked. Four notes, descending, then the same four notes again, a fragment of something that sounded like a hymn played at the wrong speed.

Noor knew those four notes.

She could not have said from where. They lived somewhere below memory, in the flooded lower floors of her, and hearing them now produced a sensation she had no honest name for — homesickness was the closest word, homesickness for a place she had never been, grief for a loss she had never suffered, the feeling of standing in a doorway of a house you grew up in and finding the furniture rearranged by strangers.

The woman reached for a fourth plate.

There were only three places set. Noor watched her take the plate from the stack, watch her carry it to the near end of the table — the end closest to Noor — and lay it down with the same precise care, and square the cutlery beside it, fork a degree, knife a degree, and Noor's skin went cold from the scalp down, because the plate was set for her. Had always been set for her. The place had been waiting, kept warm, kept *ready*, through rot and vacancy and decades of dust in the world outside this hallway, and the woman had known she was coming, had perhaps always known, had been setting this table since 1911 with one place left empty at the near end.

"You'll want to wash," the woman said, without turning. Her voice was low and pleasant and worn smooth, like a stair tread polished by feet. "Supper's nearly ready. Your grandfather will be down directly."

*I don't have a grandfather here,* Noor thought, and could not say it aloud, and could not move, and the woman continued laying out the meal, lifting the tureen lid to release a breath of fragrance — roast meat, root vegetables, gravy, Sunday dinner, the smell of every safe childhood ever cooked — and humming her four descending notes.

And then, between one note and the next, the woman paused. Her hands went still on the tablecloth. Her head tilted, very slightly, the way a person tilts when they hear their name called from another room, or the way a dog lifts its head before the thunder arrives.

"Noor," the woman said.

Not *you'll want to wash.* Not *supper's nearly ready.* Her name. Her name in this woman's mouth, in that worn-smooth voice, spoken with an intimacy that curdled the homesickness instantly into terror, because strangers did not know her name, and hosts did not use it like that, like a key fitted to a lock, like a word that had been practiced.

The woman began to turn.

Noor saw the profile first — the line of the jaw, the corner of the mouth, the loose strand of hair at the nape catching the gaslight — and the profile was wrong in the way that matters most, wrong because it was *right*, familiar the way your own handwriting is familiar, the way your mother's hands are familiar, a face assembled from features Noor saw every day in mirrors that lagged a second behind her life. The cheekbone. The brow. The small scar —

She did not stay to see the rest.

She turned and ran, and the hallway ran with her, doors streaming past on either side, the nursery, the study, the white-iron bedroom, the rain still falling against windows that belonged to another sky, and behind her the woman's voice followed, not angry, not raised, worse than that — gentle, patient, infinitely willing to wait:

"Your place is set, dear. It's always been set."

The thirteenth door stood where she had left it. She threw herself through, slammed it, leaned her whole weight against the oak, and the wood was cold now, dead cold, ordinary cold, and the gaslight did not leak through the seams, and the bread-smell faded from the air in seconds as though it had never been, leaving only the house's usual breath of stone and stagnant water.

Noor slid down the door to the floor and sat in the dark corridor of her own century, shaking, flashlight rolling from her grip.

It was some minutes before she noticed that her hands were trembling too hard to be only fear. Because in the last instant, before she fled, before the turn completed, the woman had lifted her head fully toward her — and Noor had seen the eyes catch the gaslight, and they had not been a stranger's eyes, and they had not been Ivy Bell's eyes from any photograph in the cellar archive.

They had been watching her with recognition. With relief. With the expression of someone who has waited a hundred years at a window and finally seen, coming up the road, the one person she was waiting for.

And Noor, pressing her palms flat against the cold floorboards, realized two things at once, both unbearable, both true.

The first was that she had run from that face not because it was unknown, but because some drowned part of her had known it instantly, had *loved* it, the way you love a face from before language.

The second was that the woman had called her by name — and Noor had never told anyone in this house her name.

Maren had read it off the work order. Elias had used it exactly twice. But the woman in 1911 had said *Noor* the way you say the name of a child you've fed and bathed and sung to sleep, and downstairs, in the parlor where her cot sat under the face-down hand-glass, Noor's own reflection was at that moment climbing the stairs she had just descended, one second ahead, going back into the rooms that were — going home, the way you go home, without needing to be asked twice.

Noor got to her feet. She would find Maren. She would make Maren tell her everything, tonight, with the bell between them and the well's cold breath rising through the floorboards — because the ledger had named *a keeper who remembers*, and Noor was beginning to understand that the house had not chosen her at all.

The house had *kept* her. All along. And something in the thirteenth room had been setting her place at the table since before she was born.
