## 1. The House That Counts Its Doors

The bus from the coast let me off at the bottom of the hill at 6:47 in the evening, and the driver didn't pull away right away. He sat with his hand on the lever, watching me in the mirror as I got my bags down, and when I looked back at him he smiled the way you smile at a stranger you've been told about.

"Petra," he said, as if confirming it with me. As if I'd named the place and he was checking the spelling.

"That's right."

"Nobody comes back here," he said, and then, quickly, as if he'd said too much: "Not to stay, I mean. Folk visit. Folk don't come back." He pulled the lever and the doors hissed shut, and the bus went on down the valley with its lights swinging, and I stood in the road with two suitcases and a field recorder in a padded case and the smell of the place coming up at me like a tide.

Petra smells the same. That's the first thing nobody warns you about a hometown — not that it will have changed, but that the change will have happened around something that hasn't. Woodsmoke and cut hay and, underneath, the cold mineral breath of the limestone the whole valley sits on. Twenty years away and my body knew the smell the way a hand knows a stair rail in the dark. My lungs opened before I decided to let them.

I counted the houses on the way up. Not because I meant to. Because I have always counted, and because in Petra counting is a kind of greeting.

Eleven houses on the lower lane. Nine on the church side of the square. The well with its iron cup on a chain. The tavern with its sign — *The Last Sheaf* — repainted so recently the gold leaf was still sharp. I counted windows as I passed, out of the habit my grandmother put in me before I could read, tapping each pane with one finger: *one for the living, one for the dead, and one for the one who isn't spoken of.* I'd thought, for years, that it was a nonsense rhyme she'd made up to keep a restless child occupied. Standing in the square at dusk, I found my finger moving against the glass of my own reflection and had to stop myself, and the stopping itself felt like a small betrayal.

The village was warm. That's the strange thing I keep coming back to, the thing I want to set down plainly before the rest of it complicates: they were warm. Doors opened. Mrs. Adeyemi was on her step and said my name — *Isandro* — in the round, unhurried way she used to say it when she gave me plums off her tree, and she said it like no time had passed, and that was the wrong part. Twenty years is time. Twenty years is a man's whole face changing. She said my name the way you say the name of someone you've been keeping current.

"You're the folklorist now," she said, pleased. "Your grandmother would have liked that. The songs." And then her eyes went past my shoulder, down the lane, the way you check a road for a car you've already heard, and she said, "You'll want to get settled before dark," and was gone inside, and the latch came down soft as a held breath.

* * *

My grandmother's house stands at the top of Church Lane, last before the wall, and it has always seemed to me less like a house than like a sentence someone started and never finished. Grey stone, slate roof, and the door — I stood in front of it for a long moment with the key in my hand — the door is the reason I named this chapter of my life the way I have, though I didn't know it yet.

The house counts its doors. That was the game. My grandmother taught it to me on the first evening of every visit: we would walk the house together, her hand on my shoulder, and we would say the doors. Front door, one. Back door, two. Cellar door — and here she always paused, and her hand always tightened — cellar door, three, and we don't count it twice.

Three doors. A house that size should have had three. But there was a fourth, and the fourth was the cellar, and the cellar was locked, and the lock was not a padlock you could cut or a bolt you could slide. It was a door that had been *finished*. I understood even at six that some doors are closed and some doors are *done with*, the way a sentence can be done with, the way a grave is done with.

The key she left me was on a ring with a paper tag in her handwriting, faded to the color of weak tea: *For Isandro. The house, not the cellar.* As if she'd known I'd try.

Inside, the house was cold in the particular way of a house that has been kept ready rather than kept lived-in. Sheets over the furniture in the front room, but ironed sheets, hospital corners, sheets changed recently enough that they smelled of the linen closet and not of dust. A jar of plum preserve on the kitchen table with a cloth over it. Someone had been coming. Someone had been *maintaining*.

I set my bags down in the hall and did what I always do in a new place, which is the thing my profession calls an acoustic survey and what my grandmother called *listening to the house*. I stood still and let the building tell me its dimensions. Floorboards speaking in the front room. A draft with a high note in it, up the stairwell, like a flute with a crack. The tick of the kitchen clock, which was still running, which meant someone wound it, which meant someone stood in this kitchen within the last week and wound my grandmother's clock and left.

I counted the windows from inside. Eleven panes downstairs, nine up. The numbers of the lanes. I don't know if that means anything. I've stopped assuming things in Petra don't mean anything.

* * *

I unpacked the recorder on the kitchen table because the kitchen has the best light and the least echo, and because unpacking it is a ritual that steadies me the way other men are steadied by prayer or tobacco. The Sony D-100 with its windshields. The two small condensers in their shock mounts. The spare batteries, the notebook, the little bottle of contact cleaner for the jack that's been temperamental since Lofoten. I laid it all out like a surgeon's tray, and I noticed that my hands were shaking, and I decided it was the cold.

The house disagreed. The house has an opinion about everything, I'm learning. As I laid out the microphones the draft in the stairwell stopped — not faded, *stopped*, the way a held breath stops — and the silence had a texture to it, a listening quality, and I stood there in my grandmother's kitchen with my equipment gleaming and felt the way you feel when you realize the phone has been off the hook for some time and someone has been on the line the whole while, not speaking, just present.

"Hello," I said to the kitchen. My voice sounded wrong in it. Too loud and too flat, like a voice played through a wall.

The clock ticked. The draft resumed. Whatever it was, it had apparently gotten what it needed, which was confirmation that I was here, and that I was the one.

* * *

I went out at dusk to walk the boundary, because that was the other ritual, the evening circuit my grandmother and I made, and because a folklorist arriving in a village that sings should hear the village singing. That was the whole thesis of my grant proposal, the thing I'd written in a warm office two hundred miles away: *Petra's harvest songs represent an unbroken oral tradition of unusual antiquity; the present researcher proposes comprehensive documentation.* I'd been chasing songs for fifteen years — work songs in the Hebrides, laments in the mountains of Epirus, a wedding cycle in the Carpathians that took me three summers — and Petra had always been the white whale at the center of the map, the place I was from that I had somehow never recorded. The place that sang and that I had left before I was old enough to hold a microphone.

The village sang that first evening. I'll give it that.

I heard it before I saw it: a low, woven sound from the direction of the churchyard, men's voices and women's braided together, and I came around the wall expecting a service and found instead the harvest-keeping, the old rite the church tolerated because it was older than the church. Half the village was there, standing among the graves in the last red light, and they were singing the harvest song — the one I'd come three hundred miles for — and it was more beautiful than I had any right to expect, and I had my recorder in my hand and my thumb on the button and I did not press it.

I want to be honest about why. It wasn't reverence. It wasn't shyness. It was that the song was doing something to the air. I've recorded trance singing in Thrace and I know what it sounds like when a song opens a door, and this was not that. This was a song *closing* one. Every verse ended on a falling figure, a little downward step, like a lid coming down, and the whole village sang the lid coming down with their eyes open and their faces pleasant, and between the verses the silence was so complete I could hear the limestone breathing, that faint subterranean hiss the ground makes here on warm evenings, the sound I'd forgotten I knew.

They sang it through once. All the way through, every verse, and the last note settled on the churchyard grass like something set down carefully, and then they turned and saw me at the wall, and every face did the same thing.

Not hostility. Not fear, exactly. What every face did was *rearrange*. It was like watching a room of people remember to be natural. Old Mr. Fenn got there first — he'd always been quick — and he raised his hand and said, "Isandro Zhou. Back among us," in a voice of perfect warmth, and the warmth was real, that's what I want the record to show, the warmth was real, and underneath the warmth was a calculation happening so fast and so smoothly that I could only see the wake of it.

"You know the song," I said. "It's the one I've come to record."

"We know it," he said.

"Would you sing it again? For the microphone? Tomorrow, perhaps, in daylight —"

And the whole churchyard did the thing that I have spent twenty years as a folklorist without ever once encountering, the thing that has no name in my profession because it has never needed one. They all went quiet at the same instant, on the same beat, as if the silence between verses had simply extended itself and taken them with it. And then Mrs. Adeyemi, who'd been kind to me an hour before on her step, said gently, as if explaining to a child why he can't have the knife:

"Once, love. Songs here are sung once."

And they smiled at me, all of them, and began to disperse among the graves toward their suppers, and the last light went off the valley, and I stood at the churchyard wall holding an unrecorded song in my ears and trying to write down the melody from memory before it faded, and finding — this is the part I underlined twice in my notebook that night, sitting up in my grandmother's kitchen with the clock ticking and the draft fluting in the stairwell — finding that I could not.

The melody would not stay in my head. I could remember that it was beautiful. I could remember the falling figure at the end of each verse, the lid coming down. But the tune itself slid out of my memory like water off slate, and I sat there with a pencil in my hand and a blank stave and the horrible scholarly excitement of a man who has found the thing he didn't know he was hunting — a song that resists being kept — mixed with something older and colder, a child's knowledge rising up through the floor of the adult, which is this:

There was a verse my grandmother used to hum that she never sang with words. And she only ever hummed it when she was walking me past the cellar door. And she walked faster when she hummed it.

* * *

I got the front room ready for sleep — the sheets off the furniture, the window open to the hay-smell — and I did one last circuit of the house with the lamp, because the house counts its doors and so do I.

Front door, one. Locked.

Back door, two. Locked.

Cellar door, three.

I stood in the kitchen with my hand on the cellar handle and the paper tag's ghost in my pocket — *the house, not the cellar* — and I want to write down exactly what stopped me, because I've gone over it all night and I still don't know. It wasn't fear. I've slept alone in charnel houses in Sicily with a recorder running. It was something more like *etiquette*. The feeling you get at a door marked private, except the mark isn't a sign, it's the door itself, the whole door, every grain of it, saying: *not for you. Not yet.*

And underneath the etiquette, faint as the limestone breathing, something else. Not a sound. A *readiness*. The way a stage is ready before the play. The way a bed is turned down.

I went up to bed. I left the lamp burning in the kitchen because I am forty-one years old and I still leave a lamp burning, and because the dark in this house has always had a shape to it, and the shape has a name I gave it when I was six, and the name is the Cellar-Keeper, and I have not thought of that name in twenty years, and tonight it came back to me whole, the way the smell of the valley came back to me whole, the way Mrs. Adeyemi said my name whole, as if nothing had ever been allowed to change it.

I fell asleep to the clock ticking and the draft fluting, and the last thing I noticed, in that border country where you stop taking notes, was that the draft in the stairwell had changed its note.

It was lower now. Slower. Like breathing.

Like someone in the house, somewhere below, settling in to wait — patiently, the way it had always waited — for morning.
