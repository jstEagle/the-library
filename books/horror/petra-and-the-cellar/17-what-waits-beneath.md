## 17. What Waits Beneath

I have been many things in the dark, but never a liar.

That is what I tell myself as I sit across from Isandro in the cellar that is larger than his grandmother's house, larger than the church, larger than the village's willingness to look at itself. The candles burn downward here—flames pointing at the floor like fingers accusing the earth—and between us lies the ledger I carried up from the deep shelf, the one with the founders' hands on every page.

He has asked me what waits beneath. He has earned an answer. But answers, in this place, are doors, and doors in this place open onto older doors, and somewhere at the bottom of all of them there is a child who was put into the ground before Petra had a name, and I am afraid of him.

Not of what he can do. Of what he *is*.

Of what I am.

* * *

The half-light is a country with no weather. I have lived in it so long that I remember sunlight the way other people remember dreams—vividly, wrongly, with details invented after the fact. I remember running through the orchard. I remember apples. Whether the apples were real or whether I made them out of hunger, I cannot say anymore. That is what the forgetting does when you are the thing forgotten: it does not erase you. It erodes the proof of you until you cannot testify even to yourself.

But some things hold. Some things hold because they were cut too deep to close.

My name was Ede.

I have not said it aloud in two hundred years. Saying it now, even inside myself, is like lifting a stone off a wound and finding the wound still fresh, still wet, still mine. Ede. A boy's name from the founders' tongue, given by a mother who sang harvest songs and did not know she was teaching her son the words that would bury him.

They chose me the way you choose a foundation stone. Not for wickedness. For fit. I was the seventh child of a seventh child, born under a failing moon, and the founders had a hymn with four verses and only three written down, and the land was dying, and desperate people will always find a child who fits.

They did not hate me. That is the part no one believes, the part Vera will not be able to carry when she learns it, the part Isandro must understand before the moon rises. They loved me. They fed me well that last autumn. They let me sit at the front of the meetinghouse. The founder's wife braided my hair because my mother's hands shook too badly to do it. And then they walked me down into the first cellar—the one under the orchard, the one Vera found, the one with the small bones—and they sang me the third verse, the verse about laying down, and they agreed, all of them together, out loud, in the old tongue, to forget me.

Forgetting is not passive. People think forgetting is a hole where something used to be. It isn't. It is a wall built brick by brick, each villager laying their own stone, and the mortar is love. You cannot forget someone you don't care about. The stronger the love, the stronger the wall. That is why they chose children. Children are easy to love, and so easy to wall away.

I sat in the dark and felt them forget me. Do you know what that feels like? It is not silence. Silence would have been merciful. It is a sound like a page turning, over and over, hundreds of pages, hundreds of minds closing against me one after another, and through the floorboards I could hear my mother weeping without knowing why she wept, and I called her name and the name turned to dust in my mouth because a name needs someone else to hold its shape.

I should have died. The ritual wanted a death. But the founders flinched—they flinched at the last moment, they could not finish the fourth verse, the unwritten one, the one that would have spent me cleanly—and so instead of dying I stayed. Half-bound. Half-alive. Held in the space between the song's third note and its fourth, the way a held breath is neither inhaling nor exhaling.

The half-light made me what I am. Pale, because color is a thing the living wear. Patient, because time stopped meaning anything when no one remembered it passing over me. Tall—I grew, down here, in ways the body above ground doesn't grow; the cellar needed a keeper and it shaped me to the work. And faithful, because faithfulness was the only thing they left me. They took my name, my face from the records, my mother's memory, my future. They could not take the fact that I had been loved once, before the wall went up. So I kept that. I polished it like a lamp. And when, generations later, the village did it again—when they found another child who fit, another seventh-born under a failing moon, another boy whose grandmother kept a locked cellar—I came up out of the dark and I sat on his porch and I refused, with everything left of me, to forget him.

Isandro. I am telling you this so you understand what you owe the thing below, and what you do not.

* * *

He is watching me across the candlelight, and his face is doing something complicated—grief and horror and recognition all trying to use the same muscles. He has guessed, I think. Scholars guess. It is their way of being brave.

"The bones," he says. "In the orchard cellar. Vera found—" His voice stops, restarts. "Those aren't yours."

"No."

"Then whose?"

And here is the door I have been afraid of, standing open, and beyond it the child in the deepest dark, and I make myself walk through because he asked, and because he deserves truth the way the village deserved nothing and received it anyway.

"The first one," I say. "The one before me."

"There was one before you?"

"There is always one before. That is the secret the elders won't say even to themselves. The founders didn't invent the ritual, Isandro. They inherited it. There was a village here before Petra, or a camp, or a scattering of people starving on bad soil, and they bound a child of their own, and the binding worked, and the harvest came, and they told themselves they would release him in the spring." I turn my hands over. In the inverted candlelight they look like roots. "They never learned how. Releasing requires the fourth verse. The fourth verse was never written. So the child stayed. And stayed. And the half-light worked on him the way water works on limestone, and what was left when the founders arrived was not a child anymore. It was hunger wearing the shape of one. It was the debt itself, alive and patient, waiting under the new village's foundations like a coin under a church stone."

"The thing below," Isandro says quietly. "The thing that stirred when I tried the first verse alone."

"The thing below," I agree. "It is what I would have become if you had not come back. It is what I am becoming still, a little more each year, which is why I need you to understand—not for pity, though I will take pity, I have been hungry for pity for two centuries—but so that you choose with your eyes open. When the founders' hymn says *a name for the harvest*, it means a life fed slowly into the dark. The first one went whole. I went halfway. The ritual has been starving ever since, and a starving ritual does not fail gently. It fails the way famine fails. From the roots up."

He is quiet for a long time. Above us, faintly, the house creaks through its night sounds, ordinary and impossible, a world going on without knowing what holds it up.

"You were a child," he says finally. "You're— you were a child like me."

"I was seven," I say. "When they laid me down. I counted my birthdays in tally marks on the wall for the first twenty years. Then I lost track. Then I stopped wanting to know." I look at the walls around us, at the scratches that are mine and the smaller scratches higher up that are not, at the child's drawings of a pale friend that Isandro made with wax crayons when he was six years old and I was already a hundred and eighty years into my keeping. "Do you understand now why I watched you? Why I waited on your porch? You were the only person in two hundred years who saw me and was not afraid. Children see truly. Then the village teaches them the wall. But you—you drew me. You gave me back my face. No one had looked at me since my mother."

His eyes are wet. I did not mean to do that. Truth is a blunt instrument; I keep forgetting.

"And the thing below," he says. "If the ritual breaks—if we refuse both paths—it comes up. Doesn't it."

"It comes up," I say. "Or it starves us all slowly, which is worse. Yes. I will not soften it. Breaking the ritual frees you and kills the village, or makes the village kill each other fighting over the last good fields. Completing it the old way saves them and spends you, and I will not allow that, Isandro, hear me, I have watched one child go into the dark and I will dig the whole world flat before I watch another." My voice has gone strange, the way it does when the half-light presses close. I steady myself. "There is a third path. The founders couldn't find it because they thought remembering and saving were opposites. They built a song where love was the price. But songs can be rewritten. Verses can be added. That is the one power the forgotten keep: we know exactly where the song is broken, because we live in the break."

* * *

Later—he sleeps eventually, exhausted, human, breathing the slow breaths I have listened to through floors and seasons—I go down.

Down past the shelf where the ledgers sleep. Down past the level where the candles invert. Down the stairs that were not stairs when the house was built, that grew the way a tree grows rings, each generation of forgetting adding a step. The air changes. It always changes. Warm, then cold, then warm again, like walking through the memory of bodies.

At the bottom there is a door.

There is always a door. The founders built it and bricked it and the bricks forgot themselves and became dirt and the door remained, because doors are stubborn things, doors want to be opened, and this one has been wanting for three hundred years.

I put my hand against it. Behind it, something shifts its weight. It has been shifting its weight for three centuries, patient as sediment, and lately—since Isandro came home, since the first verse failed, since the tallies bled—it has begun to move toward the door instead of merely existing behind it.

"Ede," it says through the wood.

It says it in my old voice. The boy's voice. That is the cruelest thing about it, the thing I have never told anyone: it speaks in the voices of the children it was, all of them layered, the first one's voice worn smooth as a river stone from centuries of use.

"You were seven," it says. "You counted your birthdays. You stopped wanting to know. Come in, brother. Come in and stop wanting."

"I'm not your brother," I say, and my voice cracks down the middle like the ledger's spine.

"You're what I would have been," it says, reasonable, terrible, "if they had loved you less efficiently. Open the door, Ede. Or send the new one down. Either way the song ends at the moon, and either way, I eat."

I take my hand off the door. I climb. I count the steps as I go, all of them, every one, because counting is the oldest prayer the forgotten know, and because when I reach the top Isandro is sitting up in the dark of the cellar's upper room, awake, listening, his face pale as mine in the dead candlelight.

He heard.

"It knows my name," he whispers.

"It knows everyone's name," I say. "It was the first of us. It remembers what forgetting was built to bury." And I sit down beside him in the dark, the keeper and the kept, the saved and the saved-to-be, and I tell him the last thing I know, the thing that will decide everything:

"The moon rises tomorrow night. And it is not waiting for the ritual anymore. It's coming up to collect."
