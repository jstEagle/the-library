## 19. The Unwritten Verse

The stairs went down farther than the house had gone up.

Isandro counted them out of habit—counting was the last clean thing he knew how to do—and lost the number at forty. The torchlight Vera carried behind him threw their three shadows long and wrong against walls that sweated. Not water. Something thicker, slower, like the stone itself was bleeding patience.

The Cellar-Keeper went first. It always went first now. Its pale back caught the firelight and gave none of it back, and its bare feet made no sound on steps that groaned under Isandro's boots and Vera's. Above them, muffled by earth and by distance that shouldn't have existed, he could hear the village: dozens of voices, torch-crackle, the low argument of a congregation deciding whether it was also a mob.

"They'll follow," Vera said. Her voice was steady in the way of someone who has decided to be steady and will pay for it later. "Some of them. Marta and her people want the old bargain finished before moonrise."

"I know." Isandro touched the folded paper in his breast pocket. His own handwriting on it, four days of work, ink gone soft where his thumb kept returning to it. "That's why we're singing first."

The Cellar-Keeper stopped at the bottom of the stairs and turned. In the torchlight its face was the same face Isandro had drawn in crayon at six years old—the too-smooth cheeks, the eyes like wells with something kind at the bottom—but there were new lines now, faint as cracks in glaze, and Isandro understood with a lurch that those lines were his. Twenty years of watching him from the half-light had worn themselves into the thing's face like weather into limestone.

*It knows what's down here,* the Keeper had told him, in the wordless way it told things—a pressure behind the sternum, an image pressed flat against the inside of his skull. *It is older than me. I am what it leaves behind when it takes a child.*

And below them, at the true bottom of the cellar that was larger than the house that held it, something was already rising. He could feel it the way you feel a storm through floorboards.

"Whatever it shows us," Isandro said. "Whatever faces it wears. Don't answer it. That's how the bargain works—it asks, and if anyone answers with a name, the harvest takes the name and gives back nothing but another year of silence." He looked at Vera. "You carry my name tonight. If I falter—if I start to answer it—you sing my name once. Just once. Can you do that?"

Vera's jaw tightened. "I've been carrying your name since I was nine years old," she said. "I just didn't know whose it was." She lifted the torch higher. "Let's go meet it."

* * *

The deep cellar was round, which no cellar in Petra was, and older than any stonework Isandro had seen in the parish archive. The founders hadn't built this. They had found it, the way you find a well, and then spent two centuries pretending they'd dug it.

Tally marks covered the walls—not scratched but *grown*, ridges in the stone itself, thousands upon thousands, spiraling inward toward the center of the floor. Isandro recognized the hand immediately because it was his own. Every mark matched the tallies in his grandmother's ledger, the ones cut from the pages, the count of every day some remembered one had waited in the dark. The wall was keeping the count the village refused to keep.

In the center of the floor, a circle of candle stubs stood burned down to pools of wax—centuries of wax, layer over layer, a geological record of vigils. And rising out of that circle, unhurried, came the first remembered one.

It did not walk so much as assemble, the way a figure comes together out of fog. It was tall. It was thin. And it wore faces.

Not masks—faces, moving across its own surface like reflections crossing water. Isandro saw a man with a founder's collar and a founder's severe mouth. A woman with wheat-colored braids. An old elder he'd spoken with only yesterday, Aldous Crane, alive and upstairs and yet here, rendered in the thing's gray flesh, mouthing words without sound. And then—

His grandmother.

She looked up at him from the thing's face with her exact eyes, her exact patient half-smile, and said in her exact voice: "There you are, love. You came home after all."

Isandro's whole body tried to answer. His lungs filled. His throat opened around the first syllable of *Grandmother*, and it was Vera's hand on his arm—her fingers digging hard enough to bruise—that stopped it.

"It's not her," Vera said loudly, to him, to herself, to the dark. "It's not anyone. It's the shape of everyone we agreed to forget."

The thing's face rippled. His grandmother slid away beneath the skin like a coin dropped in a well, and other faces surfaced: children, he realized. Dozens of children. Some in clothes from two hundred years ago, some in clothes from fifty, one in a school uniform so recent it made Vera make a small sound beside him. All of them had been remembered ones. All of them had been sung down into this circle and laid away from memory, and all of them had come here, to this, wearing each other forever.

When it finally settled on a face to speak with, it chose a boy of about ten, ordinary and freckled, and its voice was the voice of every villager who had ever sung the founders' hymn, layered and doubled, a choir collapsed into one throat.

**A NAME FOR THE HARVEST,** it said. **THE OLD BARGAIN. THE ONLY BARGAIN.**

"No," Isandro said.

The word fell into the round room and the room took it the way dry ground takes rain. The candles flared. The tally marks on the walls seemed to lean inward, listening.

**A NAME,** the thing said again, patiently, the way you'd explain weather to a child. **ONE IS ENOUGH. IT HAS ALWAYS BEEN ONE. GIVE US THE PALE ONE—IT IS ALREADY OURS, ONLY HALF-SPENT. OR GIVE US YOURSELF, AND GO HOME REMEMBERED, LOVED, FED BY THESE FIELDS UNTIL YOU DIE OF OLD AGE IN A WARM BED. EVERY CHILD BEFORE YOU CHOSE.**

"That's not true," Isandro said, and his voice cracked on it, and he let it crack. "They didn't choose. They were ten years old."

**THEY CHOSE,** the thing said, and for one flicker its face was honest—was nothing, was hunger with a history—and Isandro felt the truth of it like cold water. Somewhere under the coercion, under the hymn and the elders and the chalk marks on doors, there had always been a moment when a frightened child in the dark was offered warmth in exchange for being erased, and had said yes, because ten-year-olds cannot imagine that being forgotten could be worse than being cold.

He thought of the Cellar-Keeper. Of twenty years of vigil. Of a child who had said yes and then, alone in the half-light, century after century, had quietly decided to say no—to keep loving the one it was supposed to forget, to keep the count, to wait.

"We're done choosing between your options," Isandro said.

**THEN THE HARVEST FAILS,** the thing said, almost gently. **AND THEY WILL COME DOWN HERE WITH TORCHES AND TAKE WHAT THEY CAME FOR. THEY ALWAYS DO, IN THE END. I AM VERY OLD, BOY. I HAVE NEVER LOST.**

"You've never been sung to," Isandro said. "Not properly. Not all the way through."

He took the folded paper from his pocket.

* * *

The founders' hymn had four movements: the call, the binding, the forgetting, and a final verse left blank for two hundred years—a space in the manuscript where the ink simply stopped, as if the scribe had looked up from the page and seen what he was writing and could not go on.

Every scholar who'd studied the fragment assumed the final verse was lost. Isandro had understood, four nights ago, hunched over the ledger with the Cellar-Keeper's cold presence at his shoulder, that it was never written because it was never *decided*. The ritual was a question asked of every generation, and each generation had answered it the coward's way: renew the forgetting, sing the child down, buy another year. The blank verse wasn't missing. It was waiting. It was homework assigned to whoever finally refused.

He had written it in four nights, in pencil, in a notebook that now lived in his breast pocket, and he had rewritten it eleven times, and he still didn't know if it would work, and he was going to sing it anyway, standing in a hole full of hungry dead with a mob gathering overhead, because it was the only true thing he had ever composed.

"The call," he said aloud, and his voice shook, and he sang anyway:

*"We called you down, we called you kin,
we called you out of air—"*

The candles guttered. The thing's assembled face turned toward him fully, and every borrowed face on it frowned.

*"We wrote your name in wheat and sin
and left you sleeping there.
But wheat is cut and sin is paid
and sleep is not a grave—"*

The room began to resist. He felt it as pressure, as cold climbing his shins, as the tally marks on the walls scraping like the room was trying to close its fist. The thing raised one gray hand and the founders' hymn rose to meet him—the binding verse, the forgetting verse, sung backward and inside-out, trying to fold his song into the old shape, trying to make his voice another instrument of the bargain.

*"—and love is not the debt we pay,"* Isandro sang, louder, gripping the melody like a rope over a drop, *"to fields we will not save."*

Vera's voice joined him. She didn't know the verse—he'd only shown her the first line—but she caught the tune from him the way she must have caught everything else about him, half-remembered, and carried it, and her voice was a schoolteacher's voice, clear and used to making itself heard over children, and the two of them together pushed the sound outward until it hit the round walls and came back doubled.

The thing screamed. There was no other word. Every face it wore screamed with a different mouth.

**YOU CANNOT UNBIND WITH A SONG. THE SONG IS THE BINDING.**

"You're right," Isandro said, panting. "That's exactly right. The song is the binding. So we're changing what it binds."

He looked up—at the Cellar-Keeper, standing at the edge of the candle-circle, motionless, its well-dark eyes fixed on him with an expression Isandro had no word for except *waiting*, twenty years deep.

"The third movement," Isandro said to it. "The forgetting. Sing it with me. But sing it *backward*."

The Cellar-Keeper tilted its head. And then, slowly, it smiled—the first smile Isandro had ever seen it make, crooked and human and terrible, the smile of someone who has waited two hundred years to be asked the right question.

It opened its mouth, and what came out was not sound. It was memory. It poured into the room the way light pours under a door: every day of the vigil, every tally, every night it had sat on the porch of his grandmother's house keeping the half-light burning, every time it had watched him through windows he couldn't see through, every year it had chosen him again over its own release. The forgetting verse, reversed, became a remembering so vast and so patient that the walls themselves seemed to blush with it, the tally marks glowing faintly like embers.

The thing in the circle staggered. Faces sloughed off it—the founders, the elders, his grandmother, falling away like petals—and underneath, for one naked instant, Isandro saw what it actually was: small. A child's outline, hollowed, holding two centuries of stolen names inside it like stones in a belly.

**IT HURTS,** the child-thing said, in a single small voice, and Isandro's heart broke cleanly in half, because that was the whole horror of Petra, wasn't it? Not the monster. The ten-year-old at the bottom of it, still waiting for someone to finish the verse.

"I know," Isandro said. He stepped forward, into the candle-circle, which was madness, which was suicide, and knelt so he was looking slightly down at the small gray shape instead of up at its tower of stolen faces. "I know it hurts. I was you. They did it to me too—they just didn't get to finish."

**THEN FINISH IT NOW,** the child whispered. **GIVE ME YOUR NAME AND REST.**

"No," Isandro said gently. "I'm going to give you something better. Hold still."

He began the unwritten verse.

* * *

*"Here is the verse that was never penned,
the line the founders feared:
that what we bind we may befriend,
and what we lost—we hold it near.*

*No more a name to feed the field,
no more a child to spend;
the harvest is the hands that yield,
the neighbor, and the friend.*

*So take my name—not as your due,
but as a gift, twice-said:
remembered once by me and you,
and never more unfed.*

*Forgetting was the founders' lie—
that love must end to live.
We lay the old bargain down to die,
and this is what we give:"*

He ran out of words.

He had known this would happen. He'd written eleven drafts and every one ended in the same place, at the hinge of the ritual, the point where the old bargain demanded a payment and the new verse had to offer something instead—and he had never been able to find the thing big enough. A name wasn't enough; names had fed this thing for two hundred years and made it what it was. Love wasn't enough as a word; the word was worn smooth as a river stone, it meant nothing in this room.

The silence stretched. The candles leaned. Overhead, faintly, he heard the mob reaching the orchard, boots on the old cellar hatch, voices calling his name—not to save him. To spend him.

And then the Cellar-Keeper moved.

It crossed the circle in three silent strides and knelt beside him, facing the small gray child-thing, and Isandro saw what it meant to do and grabbed its arm and it was like grabbing a pillar of cold moonlight.

"Don't," he said. "Whatever you're about to give it—don't. Not you. Two hundred years, you don't get to—"

The Cellar-Keeper looked at him. And in its well-dark eyes Isandro saw the whole shape of what it intended, and the beginning of the end of the world as he understood it, and it smiled that crooked human smile again and pressed one pale finger, briefly, against his lips.

Then it turned to the first remembered one, drew breath it did not need, and spoke.

It spoke Isandro's name.

Twice.
