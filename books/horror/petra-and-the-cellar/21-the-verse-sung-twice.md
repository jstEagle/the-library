## 21. The Verse Sung Twice

The silence afterward was not silence. It was the sound of a held note finally allowed to end.

I lay on the cold stone of the deep cellar with Vera's hand locked around my wrist and the last syllables of the new verse still ringing in my teeth, and I listened to the thing below die. That's the only word for it. Not a scream, not a collapse—a *dissolving*, like sugar in warm water, like a name said so many times it wears smooth and means nothing. The walls of the cellar, the ones scarred with centuries of tally marks, went dark by degrees, the way a fire goes down when no one feeds it. The scratches stayed. The blood in them dried to ordinary rust.

The first remembered one had worn the founders' faces when it rose—stern, patient, hungry faces out of a portrait gallery—and I had watched them come apart mid-sentence, the old bargain dying on its own lips. *A name for the harvest.* I had answered with a verse instead. A verse that said: *we remember you, and we keep you, and we owe you nothing but the singing.*

It had no answer for that. It had never been offered remembering without a price.

* * *

Climbing the cellar stairs was like being born slowly. Each step, the air changed. By the time I reached the orchard door, the night had a different weight to it—lighter, ordinary, smelling of apples and woodsmoke instead of the deep mineral dark of the earth. The harvest moon hung over the orchard, enormous and orange, and I braced myself for it to matter, for it to be a clock still ticking.

It was just a moon. It went on being just a moon. It set behind the western hills at the usual hour, indifferent, and I stood in the grass and watched it go with tears running down my face for no reason I could have explained to anyone who hadn't been underground.

The villagers were still there. Torches guttering in the damp, faces half-lit, and every one of them looking at me—and here was the thing, the thing I will never stop marveling at—*knowing* me. Not the polite, careful blankness I'd walked into three weeks ago. Not the village that had agreed, generation after generation, to lay me down from memory. They knew my grandmother's laugh. They knew I'd broken my arm falling out of the Osei barn when I was nine and that Vera's mother had splinted it. Old Tomas wept openly, saying my name—*Isandro, Isandro*—the way you'd test a well rope you'd thought was cut.

The name held. I could feel it holding, the way you feel a knot holding weight. Spoken twice in the old tongue, carried by Vera, sung once by a whole village in a verse I'd written in a cellar by candlelight—and now it was in them the way water is in a sponge. They could not unlearn me again. The song had seen to that.

The Cellar-Keeper stood at the edge of the torchlight, and the village looked at it too, and this was the harder thing. Because they remembered it as well—remembered it as the boy it had been, two hundred years ago, the first child after the first, the one who'd gone down into the half-light and stayed so long it forgot how to be a child. A woman near the front—Maren, I'd learn her name later, Maren Kovač, who had been my grandmother's closest friend—made a sound like something breaking and reached out her hands.

The Cellar-Keeper looked at her hands and did not take them. Not yet. It looked at me instead, and its face—pale, smooth, ageless for two centuries—did something I had never seen it do.

It looked frightened.

* * *

We walked back to my grandmother's house together, the three of us, because Vera refused to leave and the Cellar-Keeper refused to be more than a step behind me, and the village let us go. Behind us I heard the torches being put out one by one, and someone starting the harvest song—the whole song, all the verses, including mine—and other voices joining, ragged and off-key and unafraid.

In the kitchen, Vera lit the lamp and put water on for tea as if it were any night, as if her hands needed a task more than I needed tea. The Cellar-Keeper stood in the doorway between the kitchen and the hall, and I realized it had never been inside this house. Twenty years on my porch, and it had never crossed the threshold. It hadn't been invited. Or it hadn't believed it was allowed.

"Sit down," I said.

It looked at the chair the way you'd look at deep water.

"It's a chair," I said. "You've kept a ritual burning for twenty years so I'd have somewhere to come home to. Sit in the chair."

It sat. Carefully, like a man in a boat. And in the lamplight, for the first time, I saw it clearly—not the pale figure of my childhood, not the tall shape on the porch, but a face. A young man's face, roughly my age, roughly the age he'd been when the village laid him down, weathered now by something new. His skin had color in it. Faint, uncertain, like dawn deciding whether to commit.

"It's starting," he said. His voice was still the voice I knew, low and resonant, but it cracked on the second word. "The duty's gone. The half-light's gone. I can feel the years catching up." He held out his hand and turned it over, and we all watched a faint line appear at the base of one finger—a crease, a wrinkle, a *year*. "I don't know how fast. I don't know how much."

Vera set the tea down and took his hand and looked at the line the way she'd looked at her students' first wobbly handwriting: with fierce, unsentimental tenderness. "Then we'll find out," she said. "One at a time."

He looked at me. "Isandro. If I begin to age—if I age all the way—" He stopped. He had kept vigil over a half-finished ritual for two hundred years; he had never once had to think about dying, and now the thought was in the room with us, sitting in its own chair.

"Then you'll do it here," I said, "with people who know your name." I realized, with a lurch, that he didn't have one. Not anymore. The founders had taken it when they took everything else. "We'll fix that first."

* * *

He slept that night—slept, for the first time in centuries, in the room off my grandmother's kitchen that had been a pantry and was now a bed, and I lay awake in my own room listening to the sound of him breathing and found I was crying again. Grief and joy in the same breath, arriving together, refusing to separate. I grieved for the boy in the ledger's missing pages. I grieved for the child who'd become a keeper because no one remembered him either. And I was so full of something like happiness that it hurt to hold it, the way your hand hurts holding something hot.

In the morning the village came to us.

They came the way people come after a funeral—awkward, bearing food, not knowing what to say. They came with records: parish books, farm ledgers, a photograph from 1974 that had always had a smudge in the corner that no one had ever looked at twice, and the smudge was me, age six, on my grandmother's hip. They came with my childhood, piece by piece, and laid it at the kitchen table like recovered treasure. The teacher before Vera had kept a roll book with my name in it. The doctor's old assistant remembered the arm I'd broken. Maren Kovač sat across from me for two hours and told me about my grandmother—things I'd known and forgotten I'd known, things I'd never known, the whole lost continent of her surfacing all at once.

And they came for him. That was the part I hadn't expected. They came shyly, in ones and twos, and stood in the kitchen doorway looking at the pale young man who was less pale than yesterday, and they said, "We remember you. We're sorry. We remember you." And each time they said it, something in his face settled, like silt after a flood.

By the third day he had a shadow of stubble. By the fifth, his hands looked like a working man's hands. He ate—ravenously, embarrassingly, three helpings of Maren's stew—and slept eight hours a night and dreamed, he told me, his first dreams, and woke from one of them shaken because in it he had been *old*, and he hadn't known whether to grieve or rejoice.

Vera took to calling him by the name I'd given him on the second night. I had lain awake turning it over, and what I chose was not grand. *Aldous*—old, and kept, and ours. When I said it to him the first time, he repeated it twice, out of habit, out of the old grammar of names—and then stopped, alarmed, and I laughed for the first time in three weeks, because the binding was broken and a name said twice was only a name said twice, nothing more. Just a man, learning his own name.

* * *

The moon passed. The harvest came in—late in the low fields where the blight had bitten early, thin in the orchard's oldest rows, but it came in. The village had braced for ruin and gotten a hard, ordinary autumn instead, and I don't know which frightened them more.

On the morning the last apples came in, Aldous stood in the orchard by the filled-in mouth of the old cellar and did not age a year in a moment, did not crumble, did not vanish. He just stood there, in the cold sun, a man of indeterminate age watching villagers haul crates, and after a while Vera came and stood beside him, and then Maren, and then others, until there was a loose ring of people around the buried door—not a ritual, not a congregation, just a village standing over its own grave and choosing to stay.

He wept, I think. I was too far away to be sure, and I have never asked, because some things you get to keep.

* * *

That evening I set up my recorder in my grandmother's kitchen, the same machine I'd carried into the village three weeks ago to record songs from a place that wouldn't repeat itself, and I played back the tape of the new verse—the verse sung once by torchlight, sung once again by the whole village in the days after, sung now whenever anyone wanted, because the rule was dead and the song was alive.

Aldous sat on the porch, where he had always sat, but the door was open behind him now, and the lamplight fell out onto the boards, and he was learning to be bored, which I counted as the surest sign of recovery. Vera was at the table correcting a student's essay and occasionally reaching over to touch the tape recorder as if to confirm it was real.

I thought about the final verse I'd written in the cellar, the one that had refused the old bargain. It wasn't finished. I knew that the way you know a song isn't finished—there was a rest at the end of it where something should be, a held breath. The founders had left their verse unwritten because they couldn't imagine an ending that wasn't a price. I had written mine to refuse the price. But refusal isn't the same as arrival. The song still needed its last line—the one that says not *we will not pay* but *here is what we have instead*.

I looked at Aldous through the window, aging one ordinary day at a time, and I thought I knew what the line was. I thought it had been sitting on my porch for twenty years, waiting for me to write it down.

Outside, the first true cold of autumn came down the hill, and Aldous turned his face toward it, and on his cheek, in the last of the light, I could see the faint beginnings of an old man's weather—years arriving like guests, one by one, all of them welcome.
