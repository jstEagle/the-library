## 8. Verse One, Verse Two

Marguerite Voss had kept a great many secrets for a great many years, and she had developed a private taxonomy for them. There were the small secrets — the ones you kept from neighbors, like the fact that she watered her roses with leftover tea and had done so since the Nixon administration. There were the medium secrets — the ones you kept from doctors, like how she hadn't slept lying down in fifty years and had become, through sheer practice, an expert at napping upright in armchairs with a teacup balanced on her knee as an alibi. And then there were the large secrets. The load-bearing ones. The ones you kept from everyone, including yourself, on the days when keeping yourself was the priority.

The song was a large secret.

She had known, of course, that this day would come. The moment she'd watched the pest controller's van crunch up the gravel drive — a sturdy woman with a clipboard and the posture of someone who believed the universe could be balanced like a ledger — Marguerite had understood that the arithmetic was finally going to catch up with her. That was the thing about people like Elodie Kavanagh. They counted things. And sooner or later, they counted their way to the truth.

So when Elodie came down from the attic that evening, gray-faced and holding one of her glue boards at arm's length as though it might apologize, Marguerite had the sherry already poured.

"You look like you've seen a ghost," she said pleasantly.

"I don't believe in ghosts." Elodie set the board on the kitchen table. The paint flakes sat in their little spiral, dried to a crisp orange-brown, like autumn leaves arranged by something with too much patience and no understanding of nature whatsoever. "I believe in infestations. I believe in entry points and food sources and population pressure. I do not believe in—" She gestured at the spiral. "Whatever this is."

"Sit down, dear."

"I'd rather stand."

"Of course you would. Sit down anyway. This is a sitting-down story, and I'm ninety-one years old, and if I have to tell it standing up we'll be here until Thursday." Marguerite pushed the sherry across the table. "Drink that. It's not poisoned. If I wanted to poison you I'd have done it before you found the attic, and I'd have used something classier than sherry."

Elodie sat. She did not drink. Marguerite respected that; it showed sense. She took a small sip of her own glass, mostly for ceremony, and folded her hands.

* * *

In 1974, Marguerite Voss was twenty-nine years old and beautiful in the way that made painters weep, or so the painter told her, twice a week, every week, for eleven months.

His name — his real name, the one he'd worn when he still bothered wearing names — had been Aurelian. He had arrived at Hollowmere in April with a case of brushes, a trunk of paints, and a smile that Marguerite would spend the next five decades trying, unsuccessfully, to describe to herself. It wasn't a cruel smile. That was the trap of it. Cruel smiles you could run from. His was a *generous* smile, the smile of a man who wanted to give you exactly what you'd always wanted, and who happened to know what that was better than you did.

"He was commissioned to paint my portrait," she told Elodie now. "My father arranged it. Father was dying and sentimental and thought a portrait would be a fine thing to leave the family. He was wrong about most things that year, God rest him, but he outdid himself with that one."

"Aurelian," Elodie repeated. "That's not a name. That's a cologne."

Marguerite laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, and it felt strange in her throat, like a bird that had been caged so long it had forgotten how wings worked. "Yes. Exactly that. He smelled of turpentine and violets and something underneath that I never identified. Something like cold stone. Like the inside of a church in winter."

She remembered the first sittings fondly, which was its own kind of horror, and she knew it. He had been charming. He had been *attentive*. He had asked her questions no one had ever asked her — not what she liked but what she *longed* for, not what she feared but what she dreamed about at three in the morning when the house creaked. And when she answered, he listened the way nobody listens: completely, hungrily, as though her answers were the only food in the world.

"The first verse came in June," she said.

Elodie's eyes narrowed. "Verse."

"I'm getting there. Patience is free, dear, and I'm spending mine generously." Marguerite turned her glass slowly on the table. "He sang while he painted. All painters hum, I suppose, or whistle. But he sang properly — a lullaby, old-fashioned, the kind grandmothers sing. Three verses. He taught me the first one because he said a portrait sits better if the subject knows the song. Said it helped me hold still. And it did. Oh, it did. I'd sing along and the hours would go by like minutes and I'd wake in my bed with no memory of climbing the stairs."

"And?"

"And I started dreaming true." Marguerite met her eyes. "You're a practical woman, so I'll say it plainly. Whatever I dreamed, happened. I dreamed my father's roses bloomed white, and they did, overnight, every bush. I dreamed the vicar would lose his hat in the wind, and it went into the duck pond at Sunday service in front of everyone. Small things at first. Then less small. I dreamed my father comfortable, at the end, and he went in his sleep smiling, which the doctors called remarkable." Her voice thinned. "I thought I'd been given a gift. That's the cruelest part. I thanked him."

Elodie was very still. Marguerite had spent fifty years reading people — it was one of the few skills the long vigil had sharpened rather than dulled — and she could see the pest controller doing what she did best: counting. Filing. Refusing, for the moment, to believe, but logging everything against the day she'd have to.

"The second verse he taught me in September," Marguerite continued. "By then I'd stopped leaving the house much. I didn't want to waste the dreams on groceries. He said the second verse deepened the sitting. Made the portrait 'truer.' I sang it. And the next morning—"

She held up her hand, palm flat, over the table's surface. In the overhead light, the shadow it cast was faint. Wrong. She'd had fifty years to get used to it and it still made her stomach dip, like a stair that wasn't where your foot expected.

"—I lost my shadow. Not all at once. It faded over a week, like a photograph left in the sun. By October I cast nothing at all. Photographs too — I simply don't appear in them. My father's funeral picture, I'm standing right there between my cousins, and there's a gap in the row shaped like a person. Everyone assumed grief had made me faint and they'd caught the empty space." She smiled thinly. "Grief did make me faint, as it happens. Just not that day."

"What does that have to do with—" Elodie began, and then stopped, because she was clever, and Marguerite watched the cleverness arrive like a slow train pulling into a station. "The portrait. The attic portrait. The faceless man."

"Yes."

"That's him. That's Aurelian."

"It's what's left of him. Or what he left of himself. Or—" Marguerite waved a hand. "Theology is above my pay grade, dear. I've had fifty years to wonder and I've settled on: it doesn't matter what he is. What matters is what he wants, and what he wants is simple. He wants to be finished."

* * *

Here was the shape of it, as Marguerite had assembled it over decades of sleepless upright nights:

Aurelian had been a painter once, in the way a leech is an animal — technically true, missing the point entirely. He had discovered, somewhere in whatever century he'd actually come from, that a portrait could hold more than likeness. That if you sang while you painted — sang the song, all three verses, the whole of it — the painting would take the subject entire. Body, memory, future. Everything the person was and would be, poured into pigment, and the painter left holding the brush and the ownership papers both.

But there was a catch, because there is always a catch, and Marguerite had come to find the catch almost comforting. Orderly, even. The song required a singer. The painter could not finish a portrait alone; the subject had to complete the third verse themselves, willingly, knowingly. Consent, in the oldest and most terrible sense. The final verse was the signature on the contract. Sing it, and you belonged to the canvas forever — young, preserved, perfect, *painted*. A life without decay, without change, without end.

Without anything else, either.

"He showed me," Marguerite said quietly. "Near the end, when I'd grown suspicious. He showed me the others. There were others before Hollowmere, Elodie. A house in Prague. A chateau in the Loire valley. He keeps them, I think, or they keep each other. Portraits of people who finished the song. They're beautiful. That's the worst thing I can tell you about them — they're the most beautiful paintings you will ever see, and every single one of them is a person who said yes."

The kitchen clock ticked. Forty-one bulbs burned overhead, in their fixtures, in the lamps, in the hall, in the cellar, in the attic — forty-one, always burning, because darkness was a door and he liked doors.

"So I refused," Marguerite said. "November of 1974. He'd finished everything but the last touches — the portrait needed only the third verse sung over it to close. And I stood in front of the easel and I told him no."

"What did he do?"

"He was very understanding." Her mouth twisted. "That's the thing people never understand about monsters, the good ones. They're never furious. Fury means you matter enough to lose. He simply... adjusted. He explained that the song had already changed me — two verses' worth of change, sunk into me like dye into wool — and that I couldn't leave the house now. The verses had made me part of the sitting. Step past the property line and the two verses would unwind themselves, and everything they'd done would come undone with them. My father's peaceful death. Every dream I'd ever dreamed true. And worse — the unwinding goes backwards through the dreamer. I'd have aged those fifty years in an afternoon, and then some, because the song takes interest."

Elodie glanced at her hands, her face, doing the math Marguerite had watched a thousand visitors fail to do. "You don't look ninety-one."

"No," Marguerite agreed. "I look sixty-five and lie about it. He holds me where I am. As long as I stay, and as long as I never finish the song, I'm kept. Fresh. Preserved." She let the word sit there, ugly as it was. "Like jam."

"And if you die?"

"Then the contract passes to whoever's nearest. Which is why I live alone, why I have no family left that I'll speak to, why I haven't so much as hugged a person since Ford was president. I am a cork in a bottle, Miss Kavanagh, and I have spent half a century making sure nothing else gets corked in after me."

* * *

"Fifty years," Elodie said. "How? How do you keep from singing it? It's in the walls. I heard it tonight — dusk, the plaster, two voices. One of them was yours."

"My voice, yes. He likes to use it. Thinks it flatters him." Marguerite's jaw tightened. "You learn to hum around it. That's the trick, and it took me years to master. The song wants to be finished the way water wants to run downhill — it will pour itself toward the ending through any open mouth. So you never leave your mouth open. You hum. You hum *past* the third verse, around it, the way you'd step around a hole in the floor in the dark. You learn where the verse begins the way you learn where the hole is — by falling in once or twice."

She had fallen in twice. She did not tell Elodie about the first time, in 1979, when she'd woken with the melody in her throat and gotten four words into the third verse before biting down hard enough to taste blood. She did not tell her about the week that followed, when the portrait in the attic had briefly acquired hands — hers — folded in its lap, waiting. She did not tell her about the second time either, in 1991, or the price of it.

Some secrets stayed in the largest category. Even tonight.

"Why keep me fed?" Elodie asked suddenly. "You said earlier — you stayed to keep the thing contained. What did you mean?"

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment. The forty-one bulbs hummed their steady electric hymn.

"Because he starves," she said. "That's the other thing nobody tells you. He needs the song sung near him — needs voices, needs dreaming, needs the verses moving through a house like blood through a heart. Starved, he gets inventive. He reaches. Fingerprints on ceilings. Doors that weren't there yesterday. He starts looking outward for singers." She turned her glass a quarter-turn. "So I stay. I hum around the third verse every dusk, out loud, where the walls can hear. I keep him fed on almost. On nearly. On the promise of me, forever, just out of reach. Fifty years of keeping a monster on a diet of anticipation." She smiled, and it was a tired, proud, terrible smile. "I am very good at it. I deserve a medal. I'd settle for a nice biscuit tin."

Elodie stared at her. "You're joking."

"I'm ninety-one, dear. Joking is the only muscle that hasn't gone."

"That's not funny. None of this is—" Elodie pressed her palms flat to the table, steadying herself against the unsteady world. "The glue boards. The paint flakes. The mirrors. You're telling me this house has been running on one old woman humming off-key for fifty years, and now it's—"

"Now it's hungry," Marguerite said gently. "Yes. Because I'm old, and tired, and lately — don't look at me like that, I'm allowed my confessions — lately I've been humming a little closer to the edge. Curiosity, maybe. Or exhaustion. Or the simple human weakness of wondering, after fifty years, what the last verse sounds like when it lands." She sighed. "And then you arrived. With your clipboards and your counting. And I thought: here is someone who might actually do something besides hum."

"Do what?"

"That depends entirely on you. There are two roads out of this house, Miss Kavanagh, and I've watched someone try each of them." Marguerite rose, joints crackling like green firewood, and carried her glass to the sink. "One man tried to burn him. And one man tried to count him. You'll want to hear about both."

From the ceiling above the kitchen — above the plaster, above the joists, from the direction of the attic where dust refused to settle — came a sound like a brush being loaded with paint. Slow. Wet. Patient.

Marguerite did not look up. She had learned, long ago, that looking up was a form of applause.

"But first," she said, rinsing her glass, "you should know about the van in the carriage house. Tomas Reyes drove it. Sweet man. Terrible singing voice."
