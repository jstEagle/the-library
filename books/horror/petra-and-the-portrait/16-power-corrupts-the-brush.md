## 16. Power Corrupts the Brush

Elodie Kavanagh had a rule about voices that came out of things that shouldn't have mouths: you didn't answer them.

She'd developed it around hour three of the previous night, when the portrait in the attic had begun — politely, she had to give it that — clearing its nonexistent throat. She'd gone downstairs, made tea, counted the bulbs (forty-one, all burning), and told herself that whatever lived in a canvas could wait until morning like everything else with an ego.

But morning came, and the voice was still there. And this time it said her name.

"Elodie Kavanagh," it said, in a tone like warm honey poured over gravel. "Pest controller. Third generation. You keep your grandfather's ledger system, which is charming, and you balance every job to zero, which is rarer than you think."

She stood at the attic threshold with her tea going cold in her hand and her professional dignity doing its best impression of a spine. "I don't take calls before nine," she said. "And I especially don't take them from wall art."

The faceless man in the portrait did not turn toward her, because he had no face to turn. But the wet brush in his painted hand lifted, just slightly, the way a man raises a finger when he's about to make a point he's been saving all evening.

"I've been watching you work," he said. "You count doors. You count bulbs. You count the seconds between lightning and thunder, don't you? Even indoors. Especially indoors."

"That's not watching. That's stalking."

"It's *appreciation*." The brush dipped toward the palette at the canvas's edge — a palette Elodie was almost certain hadn't been there yesterday — and gathered a curl of something gray-gold. "Do you know how long it's been since anyone balanced their books against me? Tomas tried fire. Fire is so *loud*. Marguerite tries silence, fifty years of humming around the truth like a woman stepping over the same floorboard. But you — you look at the impossible and reach for a pencil. Do you have any idea what a relief that is?"

Elodie took a sip of tea. It was cold. She drank it anyway, because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't trembling.

"Here's my problem with flattery," she said. "In my experience, anything that wants something from me starts with it. The wasps nest under the Hendricks porch complimented my hat. Metaphorically. Through the medium of stinging me."

"And did you destroy the nest?"

"Of course."

"And felt nothing?"

"...Satisfaction."

"Mm." The brush moved. And on the canvas, where there had been only the faceless man and the suggestion of a dark room behind him, a new image began to bloom — painted fast, faster than any human hand, each stroke arriving already finished like fruit dropped ripe from a tree.

It was a kitchen. Her kitchen, back in the flat on Corben Street, down to the chip in the blue tile by the kettle that she'd never gotten around to fixing because fixing it would mean admitting she noticed it.

"What is this," she said quietly.

"A demonstration," said the painter. "Watch."

* * *

The painted kitchen filled in stroke by stroke, and then — this was the part Elodie's brain kept trying to file under *optical illusion* and failing — it moved. Steam rose off the painted kettle in painted curls. Rain ticked against a painted window. And a painted figure stood at the counter making tea, and the figure was her, or nearly her, rendered in oils with a tenderness that made her chest hurt, because nobody had ever looked at her that carefully, not even her.

"You're wondering what this has to do with you," the painter said. "Fair. Let me show you what it costs you now to see a thing like this."

He gestured — or the brush gestured; the grammar of it was unclear — and the painted Elodie turned her head, and her painted eyes were tired in a way Elodie recognized from bathroom mirrors after bad jobs. The kind of tired that arithmetic couldn't fix.

"You are forty-one years old," the painter said. "You have a bad knee that tells the weather. You have eleven thousand pounds in savings and a van with a hundred and sixty thousand miles on it, and you will do this work until your hands shake too badly to hold a drill, and then you will do a smaller, sadder version of it, and then—" the brush flicked, casually, cruelly, "—well. You know how that ledger balances. Everything does."

"Gee, thanks. Very motivating. Any chance you do funerals?"

"I do *everything*," the painter said, and for one moment the honey went out of his voice and left something underneath it, vast and patient and old as varnish. Then the warmth returned, smoother than before. "But that's rather the point. Look again."

The painted kitchen changed. The painted Elodie set down her cup and straightened, and Elodie watched her own posture correct itself — knee uncomplaining, shoulders easy, the particular looseness of a body that has stopped bracing. Painted rain became painted sunlight. The painted woman smiled at something out of frame, and it was not a polite smile. It was the real one, the crooked one, the one Elodie's mother used to say looked like trouble.

"This is Tuesday," the painter said. "Next Tuesday. Or any Tuesday you prefer. Time, Elodie, is simply a surface. Most people are painted onto it and can't move. I move. And I can teach you to move."

"I'm listening the way I listen to telemarketers."

"Then let me be concrete." The canvas rippled — actually rippled, oil going fluid as pond water — and the kitchen dissolved into something else. A garden. A garden Elodie had never seen but knew instantly, because Marguerite Voss was in it, and Marguerite was young.

Not elderly-young. Not well-preserved-young. *Young*. Twenty-four, maybe twenty-five, in a summer dress the color of buttercups, kneeling in a border of poppies with dirt on her hands and laughing at someone outside the frame — laughing with her whole self, head tipped back, unguarded in a way that made Elodie's throat tighten, because in two weeks of knowing the old woman she had never once seen that face. The Marguerite upstairs was careful the way frozen things are careful.

"1974," the painter said softly. "She was my model. My collaborator, if we're generous. And this—" the garden shimmered, seasons turning like pages, the poppies dying and returning, dying and returning, while Marguerite stayed exactly, perfectly, impossibly twenty-five, "—this is what I gave her. Every year she doesn't finish the song, I paint another spring. No aches. No weather in the bones. No ledger running down toward zero. Fifty years of Tuesdays, all of them warm."

The garden faded. In its place: Marguerite as she was now, rendered without mercy and without cruelty either — just true. Small and bent and papery, sitting in the manor's yellow kitchen counting bulbs under her breath.

"She thinks she's imprisoning me," the painter said. "Fifty years of humming around the third verse, and she believes she's the jailer. Isn't that wonderful? Isn't that the gentlest delusion you've ever heard?" He paused, and the pause had weight, like a hand settling on a shoulder. "I could give you what I gave her. More. Command over reflection — you've seen what the mirrors do; imagine *aiming* it. Command over form — your knee, Elodie. Your hands. Command over time itself, or the painted portion of it, which is all of it that matters. All I ask is the song. Three verses. Sung willingly, sung whole, sung *to me*."

* * *

Elodie sat down on the attic stairs. Not because her legs gave out — she told herself it was tactical, conserving energy, keeping her center of gravity low — but mostly because her legs gave out.

Here was the problem, and she was honest enough with herself to name it: it wasn't a bad deal.

That was the trap of it, obviously. She knew that. Anything that sounded fair coming out of a faceless man in a haunted attic was wearing a costume. But God, it *sounded* fair. She ran numbers for a living. She knew what her ledger looked like — the slow attrition of a body doing heavy work, the savings account that grew a few hundred a year if nothing went wrong, the flat she'd never own, the holidays she planned meticulously and never took. She had built her whole life on the principle that everything balances, and the painter was offering her the one thing no honest arithmetic ever could: a way to fudge the totals. To write herself a better column and have it stick.

And the proof was real. That was the worst part. Marguerite's stolen springs weren't a trick; they were *inventory*. Fifty years of youth, sitting in some celestial stockroom, and the painter was holding it up going *look what's on the shelf.*

"You're quiet," the painter observed. "Quiet is good. Quiet means you're not lying to me yet."

"I'm quiet because I'm doing math," Elodie said. "And I hate that I'm doing math."

"Math is honesty. I respect honesty." The brush hovered. "Ask your questions. I'll answer all of them. That's more than your last exterminator got, and look how fondly he's remembered."

"Tomas Reyes. What happened to him?"

"He refused the same offer you're considering, but rudely, and with accelerant." A pause. "He's still here, in a sense. Ask him sometime. He's very close."

Elodie filed that away — *very close*, which meant reachable, which meant the mirrors, which meant later — and asked the question that actually mattered. "Marguerite. If I take your deal, what happens to her?"

"Ah." The brush lowered, and the painted faceless man leaned forward in his frame, and even without features Elodie felt the full attention of him settle on her like dust. "There it is. The clause you actually care about. You want to know if her account transfers."

"I want to know if she gets her years back. The ones you took."

"They weren't taken. They were *invested*." The painter's voice went gentle, coaxing, the voice of a man explaining a perfectly reasonable contract. "She agreed, Elodie. 1974. She signed the same deal you're looking at, and she's honored it for half a century, and in return she has not aged a day past the age I painted her. If you finish the verse, I keep my arrangement with her. Springs forever. Or—" the brush tilted, sly, "—if you'd rather be generous, you could take command yourself. Free her. Age her gently, or not at all. Your choice. That's what power *is*, Elodie. Choice. Real choice, instead of the arithmetic kind, where every option costs."

The attic was very quiet. Somewhere below, a bulb buzzed — bulb thirty-something, she'd have to check the count — and the sound seemed to come from very far away, from a normal world where infestations were rats and portraits were just expensive ways to prove you'd once had money.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

The painter sighed, and the sigh moved the dust motes in the attic air, which should not have been possible, and which Elodie noted with the distant, hysterical calm of a woman whose worldview had been in freefall for days and had recently stopped screaming.

"Then I paint without you," he said. "I'm already painting you, you know. Have been since you walked in. The figure on my canvas — mid-stride, always a step ahead? She's nearly done. Mouth's the hard part. Mouths are where people keep their songs." The brush rose, dripping gray-gold. "Refuse, and I finish her myself, and she walks out of this house ahead of you, and everything you might have chosen gets chosen *for* you. That's all refusal means here. It's not no. It's just letting someone else hold the brush."

Elodie looked at the canvas. At the painted version of herself, caught mid-stride, dynamic, alive, waiting — she could feel it waiting — for a mouth.

She thought about next Tuesday. About the knee. About the crooked smile, rendered in oil, aimed at someone out of frame who had made her laugh.

"Let me be clear about something," she said, standing, knees cracking in protest, tea abandoned on the stair. "I don't sign contracts in the first meeting. I read the fine print. I check references. And right now my reference list includes a vanished exterminator who's 'very close,' and a client who's been twenty-five for fifty years and counts lightbulbs like rosary beads, and neither of those inspires confidence."

"Sensible," said the painter, and she could hear the smile in it, wide and wet. "Take your time. I have so much of it."

She was halfway down the stairs when he added, lightly, almost as an afterthought:

"Oh — and Elodie? When you talk to Marguerite tonight, ask her what happened to the last person I offered this deal to. Ask her what she *did* to keep him from saying yes."

The words landed cold, all the way down, and Elodie stood on the dark staircase with forty-one bulbs burning somewhere below her and understood, with terrible clarity, that the fine print wasn't in the contract at all.

It was in the house. And it had been walking around on two legs the entire time.
