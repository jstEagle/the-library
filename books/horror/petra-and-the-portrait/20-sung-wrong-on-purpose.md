## 20. Sung Wrong, On Purpose

The attic smelled of linseed oil and old snow.

Elodie stood in the middle of the floor with frost notation climbing her boots like ivy that had learned to read, and the portrait leaned out of its frame the way a dog leans over a fence — eager, patient, certain of the treat. The faceless man's brush hovered at the canvas's edge, and on the canvas, the painted Elodie was nearly finished. Nearly whole. She had hair, hands, the particular set of shoulders Elodie got from her mother and resented mildly. Everything was there except a mouth.

A blank oval where her mouth should be. The painter had saved it for last, the way you save the best bite.

"Right," Elodie said to the attic, to the frost, to the thing in the frame. "Let's talk terms."

The house held its breath. Forty-one bulbs burning overhead — she'd counted them on the way up, twice, because counting was her rosary and her armor and the only prayer she'd ever learned. Somewhere below, Marguerite Voss was standing in the hallway with her hand flat against the wall, humming around the shape of a verse she'd dodged for fifty years, keeping it fed, keeping it here, keeping it *hers*, God help her.

And somewhere inside the mirrors — inside that one-second gap where reality hadn't quite caught up to itself — Tomas Reyes was waiting to find out if his fifty-year sentence commuted or doubled.

Elodie had done the arithmetic. That was the thing about her; people mistook her calmness for bravery, but it wasn't bravery, it was bookkeeping. Every job came down to a ledger. Cost of materials. Risk of bites. Probability of rabies versus probability of something worse wearing a raccoon's confidence. She had spent thirty years balancing infestations like checkbooks, and now she stood in an attic doing the final sum of her career:

Burn the portrait: lose her reflection forever. Walk through the rest of her life as a woman the world declined to acknowledge, unphotographed, unmirrored, unseen by anything that worked off light.

Sing the verse right: become the next canvas. Live forever, technically. Painted forever, also technically. The painter had shown her Marguerite's stolen decades as proof of concept, and it had been genuinely tempting for about four seconds, which was three seconds longer than Elodie liked to admit.

Or.

Option three. The one Reyes had pressed his half-painted face against the inside of a mirror to tell her, his voice arriving one second after his lips moved, so she heard him everywhere and nowhere:

*"Sing it wrong. Backwards. Flat. Feed it garbage. The pattern can't digest what doesn't resolve."*

Music, apparently, was like plumbing. It wanted things to flow in order, downhill, to resolution. Give it a note that went the wrong direction and refused to land, and you didn't just break the song. You backed the sewage up into the composer's kitchen.

"You want my mouth," Elodie said. The blank oval on the canvas seemed to lean closer. "That's the last piece. You've been very thorough — I'll give you that. Better site survey than most contractors I've met. But here's your problem."

She took a breath. Her breath fogged, because the attic was freezing now, frost notation thick on every surface, the third verse written across the floorboards in letters of ice that glittered like they knew how important they were.

"I'm a professional," Elodie said. "And professionals don't do the job the way the client wants. They do it the way the *spec sheet* says. And your spec sheet—" she tapped her boot against a frozen crotchet, "—says sing. It does not say sing *well*."

The painter's brush trembled.

Somewhere below, faintly, Marguerite's hum stopped.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about singing backwards: it is physically humiliating.

Elodie had rehearsed. In the van, in the dark, windows fogged, she'd taken the frost-notation verse — she'd copied it onto a legal pad like a work order, because if it was a work order then it was a job and jobs could be finished — and she'd worked out the reversal syllable by syllable. The verse was meant to rise. Three ascending phrases, each one landing higher than the last, each one handing you further up the ladder until the final note placed you, irrevocably, in the painter's collection. Reyes had described it as stairs. *"You climb them and there's no railing and the top step is inside him."*

So Elodie had built herself a staircase going down.

She started on the highest note she could produce — which, she'd be honest, was not high; she sounded like a kettle with opinions — and she dragged it backward through the first phrase, flat where it should be sharp, sharp where it should be flat, landing every resolution two notes shy and sliding off sideways. It was the vocal equivalent of walking downstairs in the dark and missing every step on purpose.

The frost screamed.

Not cracked — *screamed*. The notation on the floorboards shivered, and where the ice letters had been crisp and confident, they began to smear, the way ink smears when you breathe on it too soon. A crotchet melted into a blob. A slur dissolved into a scar. The pattern that had been transcribing itself across every window in the manor — the house trying to finish its own song, impatient, hungry — hiccupped.

The portrait made a sound like a jar of paint hitting a wall.

"That's it," Elodie said, and kept singing, because stopping would be mercy and mercy was not on the invoice. "That's the sound of a bad review."

The second phrase was worse, because she was running out of voice. Thirty years of shouting at raccoons had left her with range but not stamina, and the reversed verse demanded she hold a note that no human throat should hold while bending it downward like taffy. Her eyes watered. Her knees wanted to fold. The cold climbed up through her boots and settled in her chest like a tenant who didn't intend to pay.

And the painter *leaned out.*

There was no better word for it. The canvas bulged. The faceless man's shoulders came forward into the room, trailing wet color, his brush-hand reaching toward her mouth — toward the mouth he needed, the final brushstroke, the signature — and Elodie looked directly into the smooth blank of his face and sang the wrongest note of her life.

It came out flat. It came out backwards. It came out sounding, frankly, like a goose being audited.

And it landed in the frost pattern like a dropped wrench in a gearbox.

* * *

Everything happened at once, the way catastrophes do when they finally commit.

The mirrors snapped.

All of them, all through the house — the hall mirror, the vanity glass, the silvered windowpanes, the little hand mirror in Elodie's coat pocket that she'd stopped looking into days ago — snapped into real-time with a sound like ice releasing a branch. One second of lag, gone. Fifty years of lag, gone. The house stopped drafting and started describing, and the difference hit the air like pressure equalizing after a storm.

In the gap between — in that sliver of not-quite-now that had existed between every reflection and the world — something collapsed.

Elodie saw it happen in the attic's long mirror, propped against the eaves. For a single frame of time, Tomas Reyes stood in the glass, whole and half-painted, his left side rendered in oils and his right side in tired human flesh, and he looked at her — actually looked, eyes meeting hers across the impossible seam — and he smiled.

Then the paint let go of him.

It came away like sunburn peeling, like wallpaper surrendering, flakes of dried color drifting down through the mirror's surface into some place beneath reflection, and Reyes stepped forward out of the lag and into real time and he was just a man. Old. Gaunt. Wearing a pest control uniform gone gray with decades. He put one hand against the inside of the glass, and Elodie put hers against the outside, and for one moment their palms matched through four millimeters of silvered nothing.

His mouth moved. No sound came through. But she'd spent enough years reading lips through crawlspaces to catch it.

*"Nice pitch."*

Then the mirror went dark, and stayed dark, and did not show the attic at all anymore. Just black glass, and in the black glass, faintly, a spiral of paint flakes settling, coming to rest at last after fifty years of falling.

Reyes was free. Wherever free went, from here.

Elodie's chest did something complicated. She filed it under Later, because the portrait was still screaming.

* * *

The scream was the worst part, and Elodie had heard a lot of screams. This one wasn't a throat. It was drying paint — the sound of linseed oil setting all at once, everywhere, a vast chemical shriek as the portrait's surface cured from wet to dead. The faceless man recoiled into his frame, and as he went, the painting began to fail. Not burn — *curdle*. Colors separated. The painted Elodie, mid-stride forever, lost her outline and became a suggestion, a rumor, a figure in fog. The blank oval where her mouth should have been filled in — slowly, grudgingly, with ordinary paint, ordinary lips, a mouth that could never again be the missing piece of anything.

The frost notation died last. It went section by section, phrase by phrase, the corrupted verse eating backward through the score like rot through a beam, and where the ice letters melted they left only water, only ordinary meltwater, pooling on the floorboards of an ordinary attic in an ordinary — well. A manor. But a *house*. Just a house now. Just rooms.

The forty-one bulbs overhead flickered once, all together, like a sigh.

And then the silence was enormous, and Elodie Kavanagh stood alone in it, hoarse, freezing, her breath steaming, holding the last wrong note in her chest like a swallowed coal.

She let it out.

"Job's done," she told the empty attic. Her voice was gravel. "I'll send the invoice."

* * *

Marguerite was waiting at the bottom of the attic stairs.

She stood in the corridor with both hands pressed to the wall, and she was crying, and she was laughing, and she was aging — Elodie could see it happening, the fifty deferred years arriving all at once like mail that had piled up behind a stuck door. Silver flooded her hair. Lines carved themselves into her face with the frankness of a ledger being balanced. She didn't seem to mind. She seemed, if anything, relieved, the way a person looks when they finally put down something heavy and discover their arms still work.

"It's quiet," Marguerite said. "I'd forgotten quiet. I thought I remembered, but I didn't. It has weight. Did you know it has weight?"

"I did," Elodie said. Her voice was wrecked. She sounded like a drain clearing. "Ma'am, we should get you sitting down. What you're—"

"He'll be back, you know."

Elodie stopped.

Marguerite wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, an old woman's gesture now, practical and unglamorous. "Not him-him. He's finished here, maybe forever, maybe not. But the *song*, dear. Songs don't die when you sing them wrong. They just... wait. Learn the new key. Find a new house." She smiled, and it was a kind smile, and it was a terrible smile, and Elodie felt it lodge somewhere under her ribs. "You're half-painted, Miss Kavanagh. You know that. You must feel it."

She did. She'd been feeling it since the last note — a faint tightness along her left side, like sunburn from a sun that wasn't there. When she rolled up her sleeve, the skin underneath was *faintly* pigmented. Not painted, exactly. Tinted. As if someone had primed her and never come back to finish the job.

"Yeah," Elodie said. "I know."

"And your reflection will always be a half-second slow. That's the price. That's what you bought your freedom with." Marguerite tilted her head, studying her with fifty years of expertise in exactly this subject. "Does it frighten you?"

Elodie considered the question honestly, because the woman deserved honesty and because lying required more voice than she had left.

"A little," she said. "But I've worked worse neighborhoods."

Marguerite laughed — a real laugh, rusty, startling herself with it — and then she swayed, and Elodie caught her elbow, and together they went down the stairs past forty-one burning bulbs, counting the doors aloud as they went. Thirty-eight doors. Exactly thirty-eight. The number landed in Elodie's chest like a weight finding its shelf, and she hadn't realized how much the miscount had been costing her until it corrected.

Outside, dawn was doing its slow gray work on the driveway. The frost on the windows was just frost. The carriage house held a rusted van that no longer needed to hold anything else.

Elodie helped Marguerite into the passenger seat of her own van, wrapped her in the emergency blanket, and got the heater going. The old woman fell asleep before they reached the gate — actually slept, lying back, for the first time since 1974 — and Elodie drove in silence through a world that was moving at exactly the speed it appeared to be moving.

At a red light, she checked her makeup in the rearview, because habit.

Her reflection checked it a half-second later.

And as Elodie lowered her hand, her reflection paused — just briefly, just long enough — and smiled at her. Not her smile. Not yet. A smile with practice still to do, and fifty years of patience to do it in.

"Well," Elodie said to the road ahead, hoarse and dry-eyed and refusing, on principle, to shiver. "Nobody said the warranty was clean."

Behind her, in the back of the van, Marguerite slept the sleep of the paid-in-full. Ahead, the highway unspooled toward town, toward paperwork, toward the next contract — and Elodie drove, half-painted, half-second late, entirely free.

Mostly free.

She'd take mostly. Mostly was a good rate.

But she did not look at the rearview mirror again for the rest of the drive, and if you'd asked her why, she'd have told you it was because she already knew her hair looked fine.

It was even mostly true.
