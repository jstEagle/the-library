## 21. Half-Painted

Dawn came to Hollowmere Manor the way a debt collector comes to a door: politely, inevitably, and without any intention of leaving.

Elodie watched it arrive from the attic floor, where she had been sitting since — she checked her watch, then stopped checking her watch, because the watch was doing something complicated with its hands that she didn't have the emotional bandwidth to investigate. The light came through the east windows in long amber bars, and for the first time in six days, the dust settled.

That was how she knew it was over. Not the silence, not the absence of the song, not even the portrait — which hung on the attic wall now looking like what it had always pretended to be, an ugly old painting of a faceless man, the brush in its hand dry as a bone. It was the dust. All night the dust had refused to land, hanging in the air like an audience refusing to applaud. Now it drifted down in slow spirals and settled on everything: the drop cloths, the ladder, the forty-one dead bulbs Marguerite had hoarded like a dragon of incandescence, and the great curling sheets of frost notation melting off the windows into gray puddles that smelled, faintly and unpleasantly, of turpentine.

The house exhaled. Elodie felt it go out of her own chest too, a breath she'd apparently been holding since Tuesday.

"Right," she said aloud, because her voice needed testing, and because talking to herself was cheaper than therapy. "Let's do the walk-through."

She stood up, and that was when she noticed the left side of her body had opinions about color.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the first thing she appreciated about it, in a grim professional way. No garish streaks, no dripping pigment. Just a faint wash of umber across her left forearm, a suggestion of ochre along her jaw, a whisper of raw sienna at her collarbone — as if someone had primed her but never got around to the actual painting. She turned her arm in the light. The pigmentation caught it and gave it back slightly warmer than it went in.

"Huh," said Elodie Kavanagh, who had once catalogued four hundred and eleven individual mouse droppings under a bakery floor and described the experience in her ledger as *tedious but mathematically satisfying*. "Huh."

She flexed her fingers. They worked. She pinched the skin. It hurt, which she decided counted as a win. Somewhere below her sternum, in the place where fear usually kept its office, there was only a kind of hollow quiet, like a room after the furniture's been repossessed.

*You sang it wrong,* she reminded herself. *Backwards and flat, exactly like the dead man said. And it worked.*

Mostly. There was the small matter of the cost, which she would get to, because Elodie Kavanagh believed in itemizing.

* * *

The mirrors were the first stop.

There were nine of them in Hollowmere that still reflected anything — the rest were too foxed with age to bother lying about reality. Elodie walked the route Marguerite had walked every night for fifty years, past the hall mirror with its tarnished gilt frame, past the vanity glass in the blue bedroom, past the tall cheval mirror at the end of the east corridor that had spent the last week showing rooms one full second behind, drafting reality instead of describing it.

She stopped in front of the cheval mirror and looked at herself.

Her reflection looked back.

Then, half a second later, it finished looking back.

It was subtle. If you weren't watching for it, you'd miss it entirely — a fractional delay, a beat of lag, like a video call with bad wifi. Elodie raised her right hand. Her reflection raised its right hand a half-second afterward, obedient but tardy, a soldier who'd heard the order through a wall.

"Okay," she told it. "Ground rules."

The reflection waited. It was good at waiting. It had, after all, been rehearsing.

"One," she said. "You don't smile first. I've read about this. I know how it goes. You smile first, and then one day I'm not sure which of us started it, and then—" She stopped, because the reflection had tilted its head, and the tilt arrived late enough that she saw it coming, like weather.

"You're going to be like this forever," she told it. "Half a second. That's the toll. That's what singing a cursed verse backwards costs — you keep your soul, your shadow, your doors all count correctly, but every mirror in the world gets to see you coming."

The reflection mouthed something. She couldn't hear it, obviously — reflections didn't come with audio, even haunted ones — but she was getting better at reading lips that moved on delay, and she was almost certain it said: *Worth it.*

"Don't flatter me," she said. "We haven't known each other long enough."

But privately — in the ledger-keeping part of her brain, the part that had gotten her through nineteen years of infestations without a single nervous breakdown — she ran the numbers. Half a second of lag versus everything else on offer. Versus finishing the verse right and becoming the painter's canvas, his property, his next set of stolen decades. Versus burning the portrait and losing her reflection entirely, walking through the rest of her life unmirrored, unseen by even herself. Versus Tomas Reyes, who had tried the fire and learned what the fire thought of him.

Half a second. In pest control terms, it was practically a coupon.

"Fine," she told the mirror. "Truce."

The reflection nodded — late, always late now, but nodding — and Elodie chose, deliberately, not to notice that it had also smiled. Just slightly. Just first.

* * *

Marguerite Voss was in the kitchen, sitting at the table with her hands folded, watching the sunrise come through windows that no longer frosted themselves with musical notation. She had taken off the cardigan she'd worn every day since — well, since before Elodie was born, probably, though the woman wearing it hadn't looked a day over sixty until roughly four hours ago.

"Miz Kavanagh," Marguerite said. Her voice had changed overnight. It had dropped the careful, rehearsed steadiness of fifty years of nightly door-counting and picked up something older, rustier, more human. "Did you sleep?"

"Not lying down," Elodie said. "Habit's contagious, apparently."

Marguerite laughed. It was a shocking sound in that kitchen — bright and cracked and real, the laugh of a woman who hadn't needed to perform calm in half a century and had forgotten how, so she just did the real thing instead.

"He's gone," she said. "I can feel it. Like a tooth that finally comes out. You spend years touching the gap with your tongue." She touched the gap with her tongue, demonstrating, and Elodie found herself absurdly close to tears, which she filed away for later processing. "Fifty-one years, Miz Kavanagh. Fifty-one years of humming around the third verse like a stone in my shoe. Do you know what it's like to hold a note back for fifty years?"

"I have some idea," Elodie said. "I once held back a complaint about a client's ferret situation for three site visits."

Marguerite laughed again, and then the laugh changed key, and Elodie saw it happen — saw the hour arrive.

It happened the way dawn had happened: gently, inevitably. Color drained out of Marguerite Voss the way it drains out of a sunset, from the edges inward. The skin of her hands went from smooth to papery between one breath and the next, then from papery to translucent. Silver flooded her hair from the roots like ink dropped in water. Her spine curved forward by degrees, settling into itself, into the shape of all the decades she had never been allowed to wear. She did not cry out. She gripped the edge of the table and rode it, eyes wide and wet and — this was the part Elodie would carry for the rest of her life, the part she'd be turning over at two in the morning for years — *grateful.*

"Oh," Marguerite breathed. "Oh, it *hurts.* Oh, it's *wonderful.*"

"Should I— do you want me to get—"

"No. No, sit. Sit with me." She patted the chair beside her, and her hand was ninety years old now, spotted and knuckled and trembling, and she patted it anyway with the brisk authority of a woman who had run a household against a god. "I've earned every one of these wrinkles. I intend to inspect them personally."

So Elodie sat, and held the old woman's hand, and watched sixty years arrive in sixty minutes.

It was not easy. Elodie wanted to say that plainly, because the ledger demanded honesty: it was not easy, watching. Marguerite aged in surges, like tide. Between them she talked — haltingly at first, then in floods, fifty years of backlog pouring out. About 1974, and the painter who had come to the manor as a guest and stayed as a tenant of the walls. About being young and vain and lonely enough to sit for a stranger whose portraits made people weep. About the moment she'd realized the third verse was a signature — that whoever finished the song signed themselves over, body and reflection and future, to the thing holding the brush. About choosing, at twenty-nine, to never finish it, and paying for that choice with a life paused mid-note.

About Tomas Reyes. Her voice went quiet there, and shame moved across her ruined-new face like weather.

"He wouldn't take the deal either. He was like you. Arithmetic." She smiled sadly. "He counted things. I think he loved counting. So when he tried to burn the portrait and failed, and the painter offered me my freedom if I brought him another..." She closed her eyes. "I told myself it was survival. Fifty years I told myself that. It was arithmetic too, Miz Kavanagh. Bad arithmetic. I put a man on the other side of the equals sign."

Elodie thought of Reyes in the lag, half-painted, patient, teaching her the trick that saved her life. *Tell him,* Reyes had said, or his lip-synced ghost had seemed to say, *that I forgive the van more than the van forgives me.*

"He said something for you," Elodie said. "In the gap. He said — the van thing. He forgives the van more than the van forgives him."

Marguerite wept then, briefly and hugely, the way the very old weep, with their whole bodies, wasting nothing. Then she wiped her face with a handkerchief that had been pressed and folded sometime during the Ford administration, and said, "Good. That's settled. Now help me up. I want to see the garden in the morning. I haven't seen it since it belonged to me."

* * *

They walked the garden slowly. Or rather, Marguerite walked it slowly, leaning on Elodie's arm, and Elodie matched her pace, which for a woman who normally moved through properties at audit speed was its own small penance and its own small gift.

The garden was overgrown, of course. Fifty years of nobody tending it. But roses are stubborn, and something was blooming along the south wall — leggy, untended, riotously alive.

"They'll all be dead by now," Marguerite said, surveying her contemporaries in the village, the ones who'd aged naturally while she stayed painted. "Everyone I knew. Isn't that funny? I mourned them in advance for so long that now they're actually gone, it feels like paperwork."

"That's the healthiest thing anyone's ever said to me," Elodie said.

"And you'll write it all down, won't you? In your little book. The counts and the figures." Marguerite patted her arm, the sienna-tinted one, without comment. "You should. Someone should know what happened here, in proper numbers. Forty-one bulbs. Thirty-eight doors. One song, sung wrong on purpose, by a woman with paint on her jaw." She squinted up at Elodie. "You've got a bit of ochre. Right there. Very becoming, actually. Like freckles with ambition."

"Thank you. I grew them myself."

They reached the bench by the south wall, and Marguerite sat down with the enormous care of the extremely old, and looked at the roses, and the sun climbed, and somewhere in the next few minutes — Elodie could never say exactly which minute, and she checked, professionally, and hated herself a little for checking — Marguerite Voss stopped breathing.

It was peaceful. That was the truth of it, and the truth deserved recording: it was the most peaceful death Elodie had ever witnessed, and she had witnessed several, mostly of rodents, one memorably of a raccoon named Gerald. Marguerite went out with her face turned toward the roses and her hands loose in her lap and an expression on her century-old face that no one who met her at the start of this job would have recognized.

Contentment.

Elodie sat with her for a while. Then she took out her ledger, and wrote, in her neat accountant's hand:

*Hollowmere Manor. Infestation: resolved. Client: deceased, natural causes, age 80-ish going on 141. Casualties: one exterminator (previous), one portrait (permanent), one song (corrupted). Survivors: one, partially pigmented, permanently delayed. Recommendation: sell the house to someone who likes fixing up old places. They deserve each other.*

Then, after a moment, she added:

*Marguerite Voss kept the lights on for fifty-one years so the dark would have nothing to practice in. Fee: waived.*

She closed the book. She called the number Marguerite had given her on day one — a solicitor in town, one of the few people the old woman had trusted, though "trusted" had meant "paid to never ask questions." The solicitor would handle everything. Elodie would handle the van, and the drive, and the strange new business of living in a body that caught the light a little wrong.

At the door, she paused. Forty-one bulbs, all burned out or unscrewed now, their work finished. She left them where they were. Some counts you don't correct.

* * *

Three weeks later, outside a farmhouse in the next county, Elodie Kavanagh opened the back doors of her van and began loading traps.

The job was ordinary on its face. Mice, maybe rats, the farmer said, something scratching above the ceilings at night. Triple rate, the farmer said, and Elodie had learned what triple rate meant, and had said yes anyway, because apparently she was the kind of person now who said yes to things that should be nos.

Her kit was the same. Glue boards, counters, the ledger. Her routine was the same. Count the doors, check the traps, watch the client for signs of not blinking.

Only two things were different.

The first was the faint wash of umber on her left side, which she had stopped trying to scrub off and started thinking of as a scar that had chosen its own medium. Children noticed it. Old women noticed it. Once, a gallery owner in a service station had asked if she was "a piece." She'd said, "A work in progress," and enjoyed it more than she'd expected to.

The second was the mirror.

She caught it while buckling the last strap — her own reflection in the van's rearview, watching her work. Half a second slow, same as always. Same as forever. She raised her hand; it raised its hand, late, faithful, tardy.

And then, before she had decided to smile —

it smiled first.

Elodie stood very still in the farmyard light, strap in hand, and looked at her delayed self, and her delayed self looked back with a warmth she had not authorized.

"Well," she said slowly, closing the van door on it. "That's fine. That's completely fine."

Above the farmhouse, faintly, something scratched at the ceiling.

From the inside.
