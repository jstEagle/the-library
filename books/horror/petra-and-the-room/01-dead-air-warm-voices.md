## 1. Dead Air, Warm Voices

The dead called in on a Tuesday, though of course I didn't know that yet. Nobody ever knows the day the world tilts. You just notice, later, that your coffee has been pooling against the wrong side of the mug.

I run the last shift at WQOR, 11 p.m. to 3 a.m., a call-in show called *Night Signals* that has roughly the audience of a good houseplant. The station sits above a shuttered pharmacy in Halloway Bend, and my booth smells like burnt dust and forty years of other people's cigarettes. I like it that way. Empty rooms don't leave you. I learned that early, being the daughter of a mother who treated my existence like a clerical error she kept meaning to correct. When you grow up unclaimed, you develop a religion, and mine has always been documentation. If I write it down, it happened. If I timestamp it, it counts. If I file it, it can't be taken back.

So yes. I record everything. Sue me.

The first call came at 11:42.

"You're live," I said, the way I always do, warm as a heating pad. "You're on *Night Signals*."

"Odile?" The voice was a woman's, papery, with a whistle of breath behind it like wind through a screen door. "It's Marguerite Pell. From the senior center. I called last week about the heating bill and you said you'd look into the assistance program, and I just wanted to know if you ever—"

"I remember you, Marguerite." I didn't, not exactly, but I remembered the shape of the complaint. "Let me pull that up while you talk. How are you sleeping?"

"Oh, better now," she said, and laughed a little, a sound like paper tearing politely. "Everything's better now, really. It's so quiet where I am. You have no idea how quiet."

I found the file. Marguerite Pell, October 9th, assistance program referral, follow-up promised. And then my eye snagged on the note I'd written beneath it in my own handwriting, because I annotate everything, it's a sickness: *caller mentioned daughter in Ohio. Send her the number too.*

Marguerite Pell had died on October 11th. I knew this because I read the obituaries every morning, all of them, going back years. It's not morbid. It's filing. It's the only section of the paper where people are described completely, honestly, in full—no one edits an obituary to make themselves look better. Marguerite's said she was survived by a daughter in Ohio and was "at peace."

"Marguerite," I said carefully, "when did you call last week? What day?"

"Oh, honey, I don't keep track of days anymore." Another tear of laughter. "Days don't mean much where I am. But I remember your voice. You have a good voice. It carries."

I want to tell you I hung up. I want to tell you I did the sensible thing, the thing any reasonable person does when the arithmetic stops working. But here is the truth about me: when the world goes wrong, I don't run. I open a new file.

"Marguerite, I'm going to ask you a strange question, and I want you to answer honestly."

"Always honest," she said. "That's the one thing I've got left."

"Are you dead?"

A pause. The line hissed, that old analog hiss like the station itself clearing its throat.

"Well," she said at last, and she sounded almost embarrassed, "I wouldn't put it that way. I'd say I'm *elsewhere*. But the paperwork's probably in, yes. It usually is by the time I call."

"Thank you for your honesty," I said, because what else does a professional say, and I hit record on the backup deck with my elbow while I kept her talking with my hands.

*

She stayed on until 12:15. We talked about her daughter in Ohio, who she said she could see "clear as anything, better than before, actually," and about the heating bill, which she no longer had, "on account of not having a house in the usual sense." Her voice never wavered. That was the worst part. Not the content—the *calm*. Dead people, it turns out, are not dramatic. They are administrative. They call in with follow-up questions.

When she hung up, I sat in the booth with my heart going like a trapped bird and did what I always do. I opened the archive.

I have a filing system that would embarrass a librarian. Every call, dated, timestamped to the second, with a summary and a voice note—my own voice, murmured into the recorder like a monk at vespers. *Marguerite Pell, 11:42 p.m., claims deceased October 11, voice consistent with prior calls, no distortion, subject calm. Note: said "it's so quiet where I am." Note: did not deny death. Note: I did not hang up.*

That last note I wrote three times.

*

The second call came at 1:03. A man named Desmond Achebe, wanting to correct something he'd said on the air about the flood of '94. Desmond Achebe, obituary, October 8th. Heart failure, age 71, "devoted listener of local radio."

"You always said if I ever had a correction, to call in and you'd read it," he said.

"I did say that."

"Well. The flood didn't start at the mill. It started at the church. Nobody ever prints that."

"I'll print it," I said. "I'll say it every night if you want."

"That's why I called you," he said, "and not anybody living."

I filed him at 1:03:47. *Voice note: correction re: flood of '94. Caller deceased. Note: he called me because I listen. Note: I am, apparently, the archive of record for the dead of Halloway Bend.*

*

By Thursday I had eleven calls. Eleven dead listeners, each with something small and unfinished—a correction, a thank-you, a complaint about a grandchild who never visited, one man who just wanted to know if the diner on Route 9 was still open (it is; I checked, I drove there at dawn like a madwoman, and I filed the answer for when he called back, and he did call back, and I read it to him, and he sighed like a man finally putting down something heavy).

I stopped sleeping much. I told myself it was the show, the schedule, the coffee. But I knew what it was. It was the same thing that had kept me filing my mother's moods as a child—the good days in blue ink, the bad days in red, a ledger of a woman who would never once have written my name down. If I could just document it, I could survive it. If I could timestamp the impossible, the impossible couldn't swallow me whole.

Except documentation is a door that swings both ways. You think you're building a shield. You're also building a trap. Because now I *needed* the calls. I sat in that booth at 11 p.m. sharp with the backup deck armed and the archive open, and when the phone stayed silent I felt something I'm not proud of: disappointment. The dead had finally started calling me, and some starving cellar of my heart whispered, *finally, someone needs you.*

*

On Friday, the calls changed.

Not in content. In *quality*. Marguerite and Desmond had sounded like themselves, just—elsewhere, papery, calm. The Friday callers sounded like themselves played through a wall. And underneath every voice, so faint I had to isolate the audio at 2 a.m. with my headphones on and my eyes shut, there was something else. A hum. A melody, almost. Four notes rising, then a pause where a fifth note should have been, and then silence, like a sentence that stops before its own verb.

I filed it. Of course I filed it. *Note: tonal anomaly, four-note figure, unresolved. Present in all Friday calls. Not present in prior calls. Note: it sounds like a lullaby someone stopped singing on purpose.*

The last call of the night came at 2:51, nine minutes before my sign-off. No name. Just breathing, and that four-note figure, hummed slowly, and then a voice I had not heard in thirty years but would have known through concrete, through the ground, through the dark side of the moon.

"Odile," it said. "You always did write everything down."

The booth went cold in a way the booth had never gone cold, and every window in the studio—there are three, looking out over the dark pharmacy—began, very quietly, to frost.

"Petra?" I said.

And the line, warm as ever, said: "Come home. I'm on your porch. I've been waiting for you to need me again."
