## 2. The Porch Light Remembers

The porch light had been dead for six years. I knew this the way you know your own heartbeat—by its absence, by the way I'd stopped flinching when I came home after midnight and the steps rose up out of the dark like a dare. I kept meaning to change the bulb. I bought one, actually, in a fit of optimism sometime around the third dead caller, and it sat on the kitchen counter next to the salt like a promise I hadn't gotten around to keeping.

So when I turned onto my street at 2:14 in the morning and saw the porch glowing warm and yellow, I did what any reasonable woman would do.

I sat in my car with the engine off and watched my own house like it was a crime scene.

Because here's the thing about a light that turns itself on: it isn't a malfunction. Malfunctions don't wait. And that light was waiting—I could feel it through the windshield, that particular quality of attention that a house gets when something inside it is being patient. The bulb burned steady. No flicker, no moth. Just that soft domestic gold spilling down onto the boards, illuminating a figure sitting on my top step with his elbows on his knees and his chin in his hands.

He was wearing corduroy overalls. The left strap was unbuttoned. He was smiling at me crookedly, the way he always had, like we were sharing a joke that the rest of the world wasn't in on.

I want to tell you I screamed. I want to tell you I drove away, called the police, did anything resembling the sensible thing. But I'm Odile Ashgrove, and I have spent my whole life walking toward the thing in the dark with a tape recorder, so instead I got out of the car, walked up my own front path, and said:

"You're supposed to be dead."

"Hello, Dilly." Petra tilted his head. "You're supposed to be asleep."

* * *

Let me back up, because I can hear you flipping pages, looking for the part where I explain myself, and I'm going to disappoint you: there is no clean explanation. There's only what happened, and what I remember, and the gap between those two things where thirty years of forgetting used to live.

Petra was my imaginary friend. That's what my mother called him, when she bothered to call him anything—"that friend of yours"—in the tone other mothers reserved for head lice. I invented him at four, or found him at four; the distinction matters less than you'd think. He wore overalls with one strap undone because I couldn't work buttons yet, and he never aged past whatever age I decided he was, which meant that by nine he was a boy of exactly nine, frozen in amber, while I grew tall and strange and quiet around him.

And then, the summer I was nine, I stopped needing him. That's the official story. Children outgrow imaginary friends the way they outgrow shoes. Nobody asks where the shoes go.

But here's what I actually remember, now that he's sitting on my porch pulling the memory up out of me like a splinter: I didn't stop seeing Petra because I outgrew him. I stopped because of the song.

There were five of us that summer. Me, and Petra—who the others could see too, though I told no one and neither did they, because children know better than to say certain things out loud—and three kids from the houses behind mine. Danny Pell. The Marsh twins, Cora and June. We met in the strip of woods between our yards, in a clearing where someone had dragged an old tractor tire, and we sang.

It wasn't a real song. Or it was, but not one anyone taught us. It came up out of nowhere the way songs do in dreams—four verses, we somehow all knew there were four, though none of us knew more than fragments. Danny had the first verse. Cora had the second. The twins traded the third back and forth like a jump rope. And the fourth—

Nobody had the fourth. We sang the first three over and over, all summer, harmonizing in that flat unbroken way children have, and every time we finished, we'd look at each other expectantly, waiting for the last verse to arrive from wherever verses come from.

Danny vanished in August. Not ran away—vanished. His shoes were found by the creek, side by side, laces tucked in. Then Cora, in September, mid-verse, her sister swears. June lasted until the first frost. After that it was just me and Petra in the clearing, and I asked him where they went, and he smiled his crooked smile and said, "They learned the ending," and I understood, in the wordless way of nine-year-olds, that I should never ask again.

I stopped singing. I stopped going to the clearing. And I stopped seeing Petra—or rather, I made myself stop, the way you make yourself stop looking at something at the edge of the stairs. By ten I'd told my mother he was gone. By twelve I'd half convinced myself. By forty-one, I'd filed him under childhood, along with the fear of the dark and the belief that being invisible was a superpower.

Except invisibility, it turns out, is not a superpower. It's a survival strategy. And strategies have costs.

* * *

"I've been waiting," Petra said, "for you to need me again."

We were inside now. I don't remember deciding to let him in, which bothers me more than his existence does. He sat on my couch with his feet not quite touching the floor, overalls and all, thirty years unchanged, while I stood in my own kitchen holding a butcher knife against my thigh like a woman in a movie who has seen too many movies.

"The callers," I said. "On my show. They're dead. All of them."

"Yes."

"You knew."

"I collect." He said it gently, the way you'd say *it's raining*. "That's what I am, Dilly. You thought I was a guardian. Children always do. But guardians watch over. Collectors pick up."

"Picked up Danny. Picked up the Marsh girls." My voice came out flat, archival, the voice I use on air when a caller says something unbearable. "Because they sang."

"Because they sang along before they'd earned the fourth verse." He leaned forward, earnest now, and the crooked smile fell away, and underneath it was something old and tired and terribly fond of me. "The song wants finishing. It's been unfinished for a very long time. Anyone who sings it incomplete is making an offer. And the frost accepts offers."

"The frost."

"It's starting on your windows tonight." He looked toward the living room, toward the big bay window over the radiator, and I followed his eyes and felt my stomach drop through the floor. "One pane per night. It writes as it comes. When the pattern finishes—when frost covers every pane and the writing is done—the house is claimed. Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless the person inside learns the whole song. All four verses. Learns them before the pattern completes, and sings only then, deliberately, knowing what she's singing." He looked at me. "That's the rule. It was always the rule. Danny never knew it. Neither did the twins. You got away because you stopped singing at all."

"And the callers. The dead ones calling my show."

"They heard it too, long ago. Different towns, different summers, same song. They're calling you because you archive, Dilly." Something moved across his face—not quite envy, not quite pity. "Documentation is a form of prayer. Somebody up there finally noticed."

I laughed. It came out wrong, high and thin. "Noticed what?"

"That you were listening." Petra stood, and the couch cushions didn't move, and that detail slid into me colder than any of the rest of it. He walked to the bay window and pressed one small finger against the glass. "Look."

Frost was blooming on the lower-left corner of the pane. Delicate, ferning outward, beautiful the way tumors are beautiful on a scan. And threaded through it, fine as etching, were letters. I couldn't read them yet. They were still arriving.

"One pane a night," Petra said softly. "Count your panes, Dilly. Count how many nights you have. And whatever you do—" he turned, and his eyes were kind, and kindness from a collector is the worst thing I have ever seen— "don't sing along until you know how it ends."

Then the porch light clicked off, and he was gone, and I was alone in my house with a knife, a tape recorder full of the dead, and a window that was learning my name.

I counted the panes twice. Eleven.

Ten nights, if the first one was already done.

I made coffee. I sat down at my kitchen table. And I started making a list of everyone still alive who might have ever heard that song—because somewhere out there was a fourth verse, and I intended to find it before the frost did.
