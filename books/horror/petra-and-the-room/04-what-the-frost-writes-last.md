## 4. What the Frost Writes Last

The studio at 2 a.m. is a coffin with better lighting, and I've always loved it that way. Tonight I love it less, because the frost has followed me here.

It started on the drive over—one pane of the Corolla's rear window blooming white in July heat, ferning out in patterns too deliberate to be weather. By the time I keyed the mic, the station's front door glass had gone opaque, and Marisol in the booth next door was texting me: *is it just me or is the building cold*

*Don't sing anything,* I texted back. *I mean it. Hum nothing. Think about taxes.*

The ON AIR light burned red. I let it burn a while before I spoke, because once I said the words out loud there would be no taking them back, and I have spent my whole life learning that some things you only get to say once.

"This is Odile Ashgrove," I said. "If you're hearing this, you're still alive. Congratulations. That's rarer than it used to be."

* * *

I told them everything. That's the part people don't understand about documentation—it isn't cowardice, it's ammunition. Thirty years of filing timestamps and voice notes like scripture, and tonight the scripture finally paid its dividend: I had three verses of the song, transcribed in Mrs. Halloran's shaking handwriting, cross-checked against my own memory of a backyard in 1994, verified against Petra's voice on my answering machine, which I'd played so many times the tape had gone soft as taffy.

So I sang them.

On air. Alone in a freezing studio, into a microphone that suddenly seemed very small, I sang the lullaby that had eaten a neighborhood's worth of children, verse one through verse three, slow and clear, the way you'd read a warning label. My voice cracked on the second verse and I let it crack. Honesty broadcasts better than polish.

Then I said the rest of it, fast, before the cold could climb down my throat:

"Do not sing along. Do not harmonize. Do not hum it in your car or your kitchen or your head, because it hears intention, not volume. There is a fourth verse. Nobody alive knows it, and that is the only reason anybody is still alive. If something teaches it to you—and something will, something patient, something wearing a face you love—do not repeat it back until you understand what it costs. The frost on your windows is a signature line. It's waiting for yours."

Dead air. Then the switchboard lit up like a Christmas tree having a nervous breakdown.

A man in Cedar Falls who'd heard his dead mother singing in the vents. A woman whose daughter had come home from college "different," smiling too much, asking what songs she used to sing at bedtime. A kid—God, a kid, maybe twelve—who whispered, "There's frost spelling letters on my window and I think it's my name but spelled wrong."

"Spelled wrong how?" I asked.

"Like it's saving room for more."

I gave them Mrs. Halloran's rules. I gave them my archive number, told them to record everything, document everything, because documentation is a form of prayer and prayer, apparently, works—the dead had been calling me for weeks precisely because someone had bothered to write their names down. I told them to stay loud. Stay unmelodic. Sing something terrible and safe. "Tonight," I suggested, "the whole listening area should be humming 'Jingle Bells' at irregular intervals. Confuse it. It expects beauty. Give it chaos."

Someone laughed. It was the best sound I'd heard all month.

* * *

That's when every window in the building finished at once.

I heard it before I saw it—a sound like a held breath releasing, a soft crystalline sigh rolling through the walls—and then the frost came, not fern by fern, pane by pane, night by night, the way it had been teaching me patience all week, but *all at once*, every window in the station going white as communion wafers. The control room glass. The lobby doors. The little porthole window in the bathroom where nobody had ever sung anything in their life.

And in the frost, letters. Not mine. Not yet. Names—dozens of them, hundreds, the taken children, each one written small and neat in the ice like a ledger being balanced in reverse. And beneath them, room left over. Space shaped exactly like two words.

*Odile Ashgrove.*

Because here's what I understood, standing there with the mic hot and the cold pressing against the glass like a face: the fourth verse isn't a trap. It's a contract. Learn it, sing it, and the pattern completes—but a pattern needs a name to close itself, and the name can be anyone's. The children were taken because they sang blind. But sung *knowingly*—sung as a choice—the last verse is a trade. One name, freely given, and the ledger closes. Everyone else walks.

I thought about the kid with the misspelled window. I thought about Mrs. Halloran, mute for fifty years, writing verses with a hand that shook like a leaf in wind. I thought about every caller in my archive, every timestamp, every voice note filed like scripture—because documentation is prayer, and prayer without sacrifice is just wishful thinking.

"Well," I said, on air, because apparently even my martyrdom needed an audience. "Listeners, it appears we've reached the final segment."

* * *

Petra came through the wall, because doors are for people who still believe in houses.

He looked exactly as he had on my porch, exactly as he had at nine years old—overalls, crooked smile, that cowlick that never lay down. Unchanged after thirty years, which should have terrified me and did, a little, but mostly it made me sad, because I knew now what unchanged meant. It meant waiting. It meant thirty years of standing in the cold outside a life, holding a job no one had asked him to hold.

"You know the verse," he said. Not a question. He'd taught it to me himself, hadn't he? On the answering machine, sweet as syrup, coaxing. His collector's duty. His heartbreak.

"I know it," I said. "And I'm going to sing it. On air. So it's witnessed. So it counts."

"Odile." My name in his mouth sounded like the first time he ever said it, in a sandbox, in another century. "You don't have to—"

"Somebody does. That's the arithmetic. You told me yourself—someone sings, someone stays, everyone else goes free. Those kids in the frost deserve to have their names closed out. And I'm the one holding the pen." I smiled at him, and it hurt, and I let it hurt, because honest broadcasting. "Besides. I've been archiving my whole life, Petra. This is just the last entry."

I sang.

The fourth verse is short. That's the cruelest thing about it—all that waiting, all that dread, and it's four lines long, simple as a jump-rope rhyme, and it fits perfectly onto the first three like it was always meant to be there, like the song was never broken, only *paused*. I sang it clearly, deliberately, my name riding inside it like a coin dropped in a well, and across every frosted window in the building the empty space beneath the names began to fill—

—and Petra stepped into the pattern.

Not metaphorically. He stepped *into* it, into the frost on the control room glass, and the letters rearranged around him like water around a stone, and the space that had been shaped like *Odile Ashgrove* took his shape instead, took his name instead, because loyalty, it turns out, was always his true verse—the one thing he'd learned in thirty years of collecting that the collector was never supposed to learn.

"It was supposed to be me eventually anyway," he whispered, and his smile was still crooked, still nine years old, still mine. "You needed me. That was the whole job. Let me finish it."

The frost flared white. Every window at once, a silent detonation of light, and in the last instant before it swallowed him I saw him turn—not toward the light, toward *me*, toward the microphone, toward the voice that had documented him faithfully when he was nothing but an imaginary friend and a rumor in the ice—

"—goodnight, Odile," he said. "Thanks for keeping my calls."

* * *

The radio went quiet forever at 3:47 a.m.

Not static. Not dead air. Quiet—the way a room goes quiet when everyone in it has finally stopped holding their breath. Across the listening area, every window cleared at once, every name in the ice fading like breath off glass, and somewhere in the archives of KGRV-AM there sits a reel of tape containing the last broadcast Odile Ashgrove ever made, ending mid-exhale, mid-word, mid—

Well. Mid this sentence, if you're reading the transcript.

They found the studio empty. They found my files immaculate—thirty years of timestamps and voice notes, cross-referenced, indexed, complete except for one final entry, added at 3:46 a.m., a single audio file labeled simply: *PETRA — FINAL CALL.*

Play it, and you'll hear a porch light buzzing. Crickets. A screen door opening after thirty years of staying shut.

And then a voice—unchanged, unhurried, warm as a childhood summer—saying the words I've waited my whole afterlife to hear again:

*"Hey, Odile. It's me. I heard you kept my seat. Thought I'd finally call in."*

The line is still open.
