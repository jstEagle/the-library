## 13. Oren Comes Home

The knock came at seven in the morning, which was wrong, because Oren had never once in his life knocked before ten, and also because he had been gone for fourteen months and everyone said the word *gone* the way you say a name at a funeral — carefully, so it doesn't break.

Odile was still in her robe, holding coffee she'd forgotten to drink, when she opened the door.

He stood on her porch looking exactly as he had the day he walked out of Petra's Crossing with a duffel bag and a promise to send postcards. Same brown jacket. Same cowlick that never lay down no matter how much he fought it. Same chipped front tooth from the fence incident when they were nine, which their mother had paid good money to fix and which Oren had somehow re-chipped within a year, as if his face simply preferred it that way.

"Hi, Dilly," he said. "I'm home."

Fourteen months of not knowing. Fourteen months of sheriff's deputies shrugging, of missing-person flyers going soft and gray in the rain, of Odile calling every bus station between here and the coast. Fourteen months of their mother's voice in the walls reciting inventory lists, which was its own kind of answer, though Odile hadn't let herself read it that way.

She should have thrown her arms around him. She did throw her arms around him. He was solid, warm, smelled like woodsmoke and something faintly mineral underneath, like the air in a cave. He hugged back with exactly the right pressure. That was the first wrong thing, though it took her a week to understand why: Oren had always hugged like he was trying to win.

"You're freezing," she said, pulling him inside. "Where were you? Where have you *been*?"

"Walking," he said pleasantly, setting down the duffel bag, which looked empty. "It's a long story and most of it isn't mine to tell. Is there coffee? I'd love coffee."

There was coffee. There was always coffee. She poured him a cup and watched him wrap both hands around it and blow across the top, and everything about him was so perfectly, completely Oren that she started crying right there at the counter, and he reached over and patted her wrist twice, gently, the way you'd calm a horse.

*Twice.* Like a knock.

*Don't answer if it knocks twice,* Wren had told Quentin, and Quentin had told Odile, and Odile had thought it was one more piece of the strange new vocabulary everyone in this town had started speaking, like learning a language for a country you hoped never to visit.

Oren drank his coffee. Oren complimented her curtains. Oren asked after her hip, which had been bothering her since October, and suggested — kindly, helpfully — that she try walking on the north side of Main Street in the mornings, where the frost melted first.

"How do you know about my hip?" she asked.

"I've been keeping up," he said, smiling with his whole face. "You'd be amazed what I know now, Dilly."

* * *

By noon he had fixed her gutter.

This required some unpacking. Her gutter had been hanging by a philosophical argument since spring; two handymen had looked at it and quoted her prices that amounted to extortion. Oren found a ladder in the shed, went up, and had it secured in twenty minutes, whistling. When he came down he also mentioned, in passing, that the fascia board would want replacing before the next hard rain, and that the Hendersons' chimney had a crack in it too, and that the meetinghouse roof was worse than anyone thought.

"The meetinghouse," Odile repeated.

"It needs work before winter." He said it the way he used to say *we need milk*, a fact of the world, unremarkable. "Everyone's going to be gathering there soon. It should be sound."

"Gathering for what?"

He looked at her then, and she saw his eyes properly for the first time in daylight, and her stomach performed a slow, cold revolution.

They were Oren's eyes. Hazel, flecked, framed in the same laugh lines. But they didn't *move* the way eyes move. They rested on things instead of looking at them, the way a hand rests on a table. And behind all that familiar warmth there was a patience so vast it made her think of geology, of strata, of things that wait a hundred years to shift.

"For what we're here for," he said gently. "For finishing. It's all right, Dilly. Everyone's frightened of the word, but it's just a word. Completion. It sounds like paperwork. Really it's more like harvest."

She laughed. She actually laughed, because it was such an Oren thing to say — he'd worked three seasons on the Kessler farm and talked about harvest like other men talked about football — and the laugh died halfway out of her throat when she realized he wasn't joking, because he was never not joking, and this time there was nothing underneath the words but the words.

"Oren." She took his hands. His hands were warm. His hands had always been warm. "What happened to you? Where did you go?"

"I went where they sent me," he said. "And then I stayed where they put me. And then I was allowed to come home, because there's work to do." He squeezed her fingers. "You look tired. Sit down. Let me make you lunch. You've been carrying everything alone for over a year and that's over now. I'm here to help."

And God help her, she sat down. Because fourteen months of carrying everything alone had earned her a lunch made by someone else's hands, even if she was starting to suspect those hands belonged to something wearing her brother like a well-loved coat.

* * *

Word traveled through Petra's Crossing the way word always did, which was faster than the phone lines and more accurate than the newspaper. By evening, half the town knew Oren Vetch was back, and by the next morning the other half knew something was off about it, in the way small towns know things — not through evidence but through a collective prickle on the neck.

Odile watched it happen over the following week with the particular agony of someone who loves a person and cannot find them.

Because Oren was *wonderful*. That was the horror of it. He shoveled Mrs. Petraglia's walk without being asked. He sat with old Tom Aldous for two hours listening to stories about the mill, and Tom — who hadn't spoken a full sentence since his wife died — laughed twice. He organized the food pantry shelves by expiration date and got Pastor Crane to finally fix the drafty vestibule, and when the twins at the school needed a hockey coach he volunteered, and he was *good* at it, patient and funny, teaching ten-year-olds how to stop without falling on their faces.

"He seems better than before he left," Mara Aldous said to Odile over coffee, in the tone of someone reporting a symptom. "That's not possible, right? People don't come back improved."

"People don't come back," Odile said.

"No," Mara agreed. "They don't."

The strangest part — stranger than the eyes, stranger than the lunch, stranger than the way he sometimes stood very still in the yard facing the tree line for forty minutes at a stretch, which Odile caught through the window twice and chose, for her own survival, not to mention — was the meetings.

Oren called them *conversations*. He went door to door, or invited people to the diner, and he listened. He listened to the town's fears about the knocking, about the people who'd answered and been found smiling and wrong, reciting inventory lists in voices that weren't quite theirs. He listened to Pastor Crane's agonized non-answers. He listened to Tamsin Aldous — and Odile saw this happen outside the library, and had to grip a parking meter to stay upright — he crouched down to Tamsin's level and asked her questions, polite ones, gentle ones, and Odile crossed the street at a dead sprint and got between them and said, "She's ten, Oren, whatever game this is, she doesn't play it."

Oren had risen, unhurried, hands open. "I would never hurt her."

"That's what worries me. Hurting is easy to spot. You're doing something else."

"Dilly." He said her name the way our mother used to say it when Odile was being unreasonable about bedtime. "The town is frightened. Frightened towns tear themselves apart. I'm helping everyone get ready for what's coming so that nobody has to be afraid anymore. Isn't that what you'd want? Isn't that what we all want?"

And the terrible thing, the thing she chewed on at 3 a.m. staring at the ceiling, was that it *was* what she wanted. She wanted the fear gone. She wanted the knocking to stop. She wanted her mother's voice out of the walls and her brother back in his body and the whole rotten founding-era secret burned and buried. Oren was offering peace the way a salesman offers it, smoothly, with a brochure, and every day another handful of neighbors signed on, nodding along to talk of *completion* and *readiness* and *the gathering*, using words they didn't fully understand because understanding had become optional and relief had not.

"They're not signing anything," Quentin Eastwick pointed out, when Odile brought her notebook full of observations to the archive where Quentin spent increasingly desperate hours among the founders' ledgers. "Not on paper. But consent doesn't need paper. That's the trick of it. The original ritual ran on agreement — five bloodlines, yearly assent, a payment everyone pretended not to see. He's rebuilding the assent. Door to door. One yes at a time."

"So we say no."

"We've been saying no for a hundred and fifty years," Quentin said, not unkindly, pushing up their glasses. "Quietly. While writing the checks. Saying no out loud is different. Saying no out loud is what gets your pages razored out of the county record."

* * *

On the ninth night, Odile woke at 2 a.m. to the sound of her mother's voice.

Not in the walls this time. In the kitchen.

She went down the stairs with a fireplace poker and found Oren sitting at the table in the dark, and the voice was coming from him — low, conversational, their mother's exact cadence, reciting: *"Flour, twelve pounds. Sugar, four. Lamp oil, two tins. Nails, one box. Payment set aside. Payment set aside. Payment—"*

"Stop," Odile said.

He stopped. He turned his head, and in the dark his eyes caught the streetlight and held it, resting on her, resting.

"She liked lists," Oren said. "Your mother. She kept the household accounts for thirty years. Do you know why the hollow speaks in inventories, Dilly? Because inventory is how they thought of people. What a family has. What a family can spare. Who is surplus." He smiled. "I'm not surplus anymore. I'm staff."

"Give him back." Her voice cracked on it. "Whatever you are, whatever arrangement you made — give him back. Take me instead. I'll go into the hollow, I'll walk in tonight, just—"

"No." For one instant something moved behind his face — grief, real grief, Oren-shaped grief, surfacing like a drowning man's hand — and then it smoothed away. "No, you don't get to offer that. That's not how it works anymore. It's almost finished, Dilly. The terms are changing. Nobody else goes down. That's the whole point of completion — the last payment ends the payments." He stood, and pushed in his chair, and picked up her cold coffee cup and washed it, because he was helpful, because he was gentle, because he was the best houseguest who had ever lived in her kitchen. "Get some sleep. Big day soon. Everyone's coming together."

"What day?" she whispered.

"Soon," he said, and turned off the kitchen light on his way out, considerate to the last, and climbed the stairs to the guest room he'd claimed without asking, and Odile stood alone in the dark holding a poker against nothing, shaking.

* * *

She found Wren on her porch the next morning.

She'd seen the child around town these past weeks — the impossible eight-year-old who was Quentin's imaginary friend made flesh, who had waited thirty years on porches — but they'd never spoken. Now Wren sat on her top step, small and neat in a coat too thin for November, watching the road.

"He's organizing them toward the meetinghouse," Wren said, without preamble. "Five families. Five relics. The founders' pattern, rebuilt with living hands. When they gather, the hollow won't need to ask anyone's permission again. The town will have given it."

"Then stop him." Odile sat down on the step above, because her legs had opinions. "You're the keeper, aren't you? That's what Quentin figured out. Stop him."

Wren was quiet a moment. Up close, Odile could see that the child looked *thin*. Not underfed — thinner than that, like a photograph losing contrast, like a thing being remembered less accurately each day.

"I can't stop him," Wren said. "I was made to substitute, not to fight. Every rule I break costs me. And I've already broken one." A pause. "But I can tell you what he's really doing, if it helps. He's not lying to them, exactly. Everything he says is true. That's what makes it work. Completion *would* end the payments. It's just that nobody's asking what completes it." Wren looked up at her, and Odile saw something ancient and exhausted and terribly fond in that small face. "Quentin knows. Quentin's known since the ledger's last page. Ask them what the keeper's duty is. Ask them why they've been carrying those notebooks around like they weigh a thousand pounds."

Then Wren stood, brushed off the step, and walked away down the frost-silvered road, small footprints appearing behind them in the rime, and Odile sat on her porch with her heart hammering, watching her brother through the window as he cheerfully, helpfully, patiently swept her walk clean of snow that hadn't fallen yet.
