## 3. Petra of the Founding Families

The Ferryman's Rest was the only pub in Marrowfen, which meant it was also the town's post office, its rumor exchange, its court of appeals, and—judging by the dartboard scarred into a map of somewhere else—its window onto the wider world. Ansel found it on their second evening, after a day of unpacking boxes in Petra Quill's cottage and discovering that the previous tenant had left behind a kettle, three mugs, and a wall calendar from 1998 that no one had thought to turn.

Ansel needed a drink. Or at least the performance of one. In a town like this, you learned more from a barstool than from an archive, and Ansel had spent enough of their life in archives to know when the paper trail went cold and the oral tradition started.

The pub smelled of woodsmoke and old beer, low beams, a fire that had been burning long enough to have opinions. Conversation did not stop when Ansel entered, but it *dipped*—a half-second lowering of voices, the way water dips around a stone—and then resumed at a slightly different pitch. Ansel was getting used to that. New things in Marrowfen were noted, weighed, and filed away somewhere Ansel couldn't see.

They took a stool at the end of the bar, ordered a local ale they had no intention of finishing, and opened their notebook to a fresh page under the pretense of reviewing lesson plans.

*Ferryman's Rest. Friday. ~14 patrons. Fire lit though it's September. No music playing.*

That last one itched. Every pub Ansel had ever been in had some noise bleeding out of it—a radio, a jukebox, someone's phone on a speaker. The Ferryman's Rest was silent except for voices and the tick of a clock above the bar, an old brass-faced thing with hands that moved like they were wading through honey.

"You're the new teacher."

Ansel looked up. The woman behind the bar had come over without seeming to cross the distance—tall, mid-thirties maybe, dark hair tied back with what looked suspiciously like a length of twine, sleeves rolled to the elbow over forearms that had done real work. She had the kind of face that didn't perform friendliness but didn't withhold it either. It simply waited to see what you were.

"Ansel Eastwick," Ansel said. "Year Seven."

"I know." She set a coaster down with unnecessary precision. "Petra Quill. You're lodging in my cottage."

"You're my landlady." Ansel blinked. "I've been living in your house for two days and paying rent to a solicitor in Ashcombe."

"He handles it. I don't like paperwork." A small shrug. "You haven't broken anything. The kettle's temperamental. That's all I'd have said anyway."

"You could have said it at the cottage."

"I could have." Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the ground where one might take root. "But then I wouldn't get to see what you write in that notebook every time someone talks to you."

Ansel's pen stopped. Heat climbed their neck. "Habit."

"Mm." Petra Quill studied them for a moment with an expression Ansel would spend weeks trying to name—recognition, maybe, or its opposite: the look of someone seeing a thing they'd been told didn't exist. "What are you writing about me?"

"That you pay your rent through a solicitor because you don't like paperwork."

"Liar." Now the smile arrived, brief and dry as August. "You're writing that I knew your name before you gave it."

Ansel held her gaze. "Are you going to tell me why?"

"No." She picked up the empty glass of the man two stools down and carried it off, and that was the end of the interview, apparently.

Ansel wrote: *Landlady = bartender = Petra Quill. Knew my name. Watched me clock the silence. Doesn't lie well and doesn't try.* Then, after a moment, they added: *Interesting.*

Underlined once.

* * *

Over the next hour, the pub filled and emptied according to rhythms Ansel couldn't parse. Farmers, mostly, and two women from the bakery who spoke in shorthand. Nobody stayed late. By half eight the place had thinned to five souls, and Ansel noticed something else worth a line in the notebook: nobody sat at the table beneath the clock.

It wasn't marked. There was no rope across it, no RESERVED sign. It was simply avoided, the way people avoid the one paving stone that rocks. Ansel watched a man carry his pint past it to a worse seat rather than take the empty chair there, and he did it without looking at the table, which was somehow worse than if he'd stared.

At nine o'clock exactly, the clock struck.

It was not a chime so much as a toll—one deep note, brass and bronze and something underneath the metal, something that sounded less like a bell than a throat. The whole room flinched. Not dramatically. Just a collective tightening, shoulders rising a centimeter, conversations pausing mid-word, and then everyone carrying on as if nothing had happened, with the practiced smoothness of people who had done this ten thousand times.

Everyone except the woman behind the bar.

Petra Quill flinched like she'd been struck. Her hand jerked against the tap she was cleaning and foam spilled over her knuckles, and for one unguarded second her face did something terrible and private—the expression of a person hearing a sound they had spent their whole life dreading and being unable to make themselves stop dreading it. Then it was gone, wiped away so cleanly Ansel almost doubted what they'd seen.

Almost. Ansel never doubted their own eyes. That was the whole point of them.

They wrote: *9pm toll. Everyone hates it. P.Q. hates it most. Ask about the clock. Don't ask about the clock yet.*

When Petra came back down the bar, Ansel slid their glass forward. "Another? And whatever the story is with that table under the clock."

"There's no story."

"There's always a story. That's the first thing they teach you in archives."

"Is that what you are? An archivist?" She pulled the pint anyway, unhurried. "Headmaster said teacher."

"I'm both. I archive things that other people lose." Ansel turned the glass slowly on its coaster. "My family lost me, actually. Struck me out of the family Bible with a ruler and a steady hand. So now I keep records for a living. Somebody has to."

Something shifted in Petra's posture. Not softening, exactly—more like a door left ajar that had been about to close and didn't. "Which name did they strike out?" she asked quietly.

"All of them, eventually. They kept the surname. Easier for the Christmas cards they didn't send." Ansel heard their own voice go flat and careful and hated it a little. "Anyway. Marrowfen suits me. Nobody here knows who I'm supposed to have been."

"No," Petra agreed, and there was an odd weight on the word. "Nobody knows anybody here. That's rather the point of the place."

"The point?"

"Forget I said that." She wiped the bar where no spill was. "What do you want to know about the table?"

"Why nobody sits there."

"Because the clock's above it." She said it as if that explained everything, and then, catching Ansel's expression, sighed and leaned her forearms on the bar. Up close her eyes were grey-green, the color of the fen in the photographs in the school brochure. "Look. You're new, so I'll say this once, kindly, and then we can both pretend I didn't. In this town, some things aren't avoided because they're dangerous. They're avoided because they're *listening*, and it's rude to give them anything to listen to."

"The clock is listening."

"The clock keeps time. Time listens." She straightened. "You wanted the table explained. That's the explanation. It's not a good one, but it's the only one I give free."

"And the expensive version costs what?"

"A question answered honestly." Her gaze held Ansel's, level and unblinking, and Ansel felt the strange vertigo of being *seen*—not the polite new-teacher seeing, not the landlady checking for damage, but something that went through the surface presentation and took inventory underneath. Nonbinary, trans, estranged, meticulous, frightened, hopeful. All of it read in the space of a breath, or so it felt.

"Ask," Ansel said.

"Why did you really come to Marrowfen? Not the job. The job is a reason, not a reason."

Ansel should have lied. They were good at lying; years of practice, years of smoothing themselves into shapes rooms could tolerate. But something about the tolling clock and the avoided table and this woman who flinched like a struck bell made lying feel not just rude but *unsafe*, the way you don't litter in a temple.

"Because nobody in my family will say my name," Ansel said. "And I wanted to be somewhere that had never heard it. Somewhere with no expectations attached. A blank page." They laughed, short and humorless. "That sounds mad when I say it out loud."

"It doesn't." Petra's voice had gone quiet. "It sounds like you understand inheritance better than most people who have one."

"Do you have one?"

For a moment Ansel thought she wouldn't answer. Then she reached beneath the bar and came up with a rag and began polishing a glass that was already clean, the universal gesture of a person buying time.

"Quill," she said. "As in the founding family. As in the deed to the well-house out past the churchyard, which has been in my family since before the parish had records, and which will be mine whether I want it or not, because wanting doesn't enter into it." She set the glass down harder than necessary. "There's your expensive answer. Last Quill. Heir to a building I've entered four times in my life and intend to enter fewer."

"What's in the well-house?"

"A well."

"I gathered. What's in the well?"

"Water." And then, so quietly Ansel nearly missed it under the fire's crackle: "And whatever it sings back."

Ansel's pen was moving before they'd decided to move it. *Well-house — Quill deed — 'sings back'? Founding family. Four visits in a lifetime. Afraid of it AND bound to it.*

"Don't," Petra said.

"Don't what?"

"Write that down."

Ansel looked up. "It's my notebook. I write everything down. It's how I stay—" *sane, real, extant,* they didn't say. "It's how I do my job."

"I know what it's like to need a record." For the first time, something raw showed through her composure, quick as a fish surfacing. "Believe me. But some things change when you write them down. They stop being rumors and start being facts, and this town runs on the difference. You start pinning butterflies and the next thing you know the whole collection wakes up."

"That's not how documentation works."

"No," Petra agreed. "How does it work, then?"

"It makes things true. Permanently. It means they can't be taken back or smoothed over or forgotten by people who find them inconvenient." Ansel heard the edge in their own voice and didn't soften it. "I've been on the wrong side of forgetting, Ms. Quill. I don't do it to other people. Not even towns."

The silence between them stretched. Behind the bar, the fire settled a log with a sigh. Above the avoided table, the clock's hands crawled toward quarter past nine, and Ansel noticed—filed it without deciding to—that neither of them had looked away from each other since the conversation started, and that looking away first felt like it would cost something.

"You're going to be trouble," Petra said finally. There was no heat in it. If anything, there was the ghost of approval, buried deep, like a coin at the bottom of a well.

"I'm going to be a teacher."

"In Marrowfen those overlap." She glanced at the clock, and her jaw tightened. "Finish your pint. I'll walk you back—it's on my way, and the lanes flood after dark this time of year."

"I saw the drainage maps. They seemed adequate."

"The maps were drawn by a Quill." She smiled again, and this time it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "We're very good at drawing things the way we wish they were."

* * *

They walked out through streets gone silver with mist, lamplight pooling in circles that the fog ate at the edges. Petra locked the pub door with a key older than the lock, then stood a moment with her hand flat against the wood, as if listening to the building's pulse.

"The school," she said, as they turned onto Church Lane. "How are you finding it?"

"Fine. Twenty-six desks, twenty-five students. Bit of a surplus."

Petra stopped walking.

It wasn't dramatic. She just—stopped, one foot still raised over the cobbles, and for three full seconds the only sound in Marrowfen was the fog dripping off the eaves. When she lowered her foot and turned, her face had arranged itself into careful blankness, and Ansel recognized the craftsmanship of it, because they wore the same expression every time someone asked about family.

"Twenty-six desks," she repeated.

"Twenty-five students. I asked the headmaster. He said—" Ansel frowned, reaching for it. "He said something. I wrote it down. Hang on." They flipped back through the notebook, pages whispering, and found the entry from Tuesday. *Asked Headmaster Vane re: extra desk. He said:* 

The line was blank.

Ansel stared at it. They remembered asking. They remembered him beginning to answer, his mouth moving, the office smelling of chalk and polish. And the page was blank, in their own handwriting, in ink that had clearly dried decades of habit ago. Below it, in the same hand, the next entry continued as if nothing were missing: *Staff meeting 4pm. Bring registers.*

"That's not possible," Ansel said slowly. "I write everything down. Everything. I have never in my life left a blank line."

Petra was looking at the notebook the way people look at open graves. "Read me the last thing before it."

"*Asked Headmaster Vane re: extra desk. He said—*" Ansel's voice caught on the dash. "He said something about transfers. I think. I remember the word *transfers*."

"Stop." Petra's hand closed gently over the notebook, not taking it, just resting there, as if to hold the pages shut. "Listen to me. You're going to want to dig at this. It's what you're built for—I can see that already, you document like other people breathe. But there's an order to things in Marrowfen, and the order matters. Some doors you knock on. Some doors you lock." She met Ansel's eyes, and hers were bright and grim in the lamplight. "The music room at the school. Do you have a key yet?"

"No. Why?"

"When they give you one—and they will—lock it at night. Every night. Even in summer. Especially in summer." She said it kindly, the way you'd tell someone to wear a coat, and that gentleness was somehow the most frightening thing she'd said all evening. "It's an old rule. Nobody remembers why. But the room hums, and the hum carries, and there are things in this town that follow a tune home."

"What kind of things?"

"Old ones." She released the notebook and started walking again, faster now. "Promise me you'll lock it."

"I promise I'll consider it extremely seriously."

"Ansel."

It was the first time she'd used their name. It landed strangely—hearing it spoken aloud in a town that had never heard it, given freely by a stranger, no flinch, no correction, no ruler and steady hand ready to strike it out. Ansel hadn't realized until that moment how much they'd missed it, or how much it cost to miss a thing that simple.

"I'll lock it," they said.

"Thank you." Petra exhaled, and some tension went out of her shoulders, though not all of it. Not, Ansel suspected, any of the important part.

They walked the rest of the way to the cottage in a silence that wasn't uncomfortable, which was its own kind of remarkable. At the gate she paused, fog beading in her hair, and looked back at the school's dark silhouette on the hill, its windows black, its weathervane turning slowly in a wind Ansel couldn't feel at ground level.

"One more thing," she said. "The desk. The twenty-sixth one. Whatever you do, don't put anything in it. Not books, not bags, not a single sheet of paper." She turned back to Ansel, and the lamplight caught her face at an angle that made her look suddenly much older, and much more tired, and much more like someone standing guard. "It's not empty. It's waiting. And it's very patient about whose turn comes next."

She was gone up the lane before Ansel could ask what she meant, her footsteps fading into the fog, and the last sound Ansel heard that night, faint and far off across the fen, was either the wind in the well-house—or something, down deep under the earth, humming the opening bars of a song.
