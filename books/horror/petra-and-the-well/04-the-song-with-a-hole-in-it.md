## 4. The Song with a Hole in It

The music room smelled of dust and resin and something older underneath, like the memory of a smell. Ansel found it on their third day at Marrowfen Primary, following a corridor that bent where corridors shouldn't—two right turns and then a left that delivered them somewhere the floor plan in the staff handbook insisted didn't exist. The handbook was photocopied from an original so old it predated the school's current name. Ansel had already learned not to trust it.

They'd come for the piano, actually. Sleep had been arriving late and leaving early since the bus, and Ansel's archivist hands itched for something to do at six in the morning besides reread their own notebooks. The caretaker kept a ring of keys in the front office on a hook shaped like a fish, and nobody had told Ansel not to take them, which in a town like this felt less like permission and more like a dare.

The music room door was oak, heavier than any other door in the building, and its lock turned with a sound like a swallowed word.

Inside: twenty-four chairs stacked in threes, a wall of percussion instruments behind glass, a piano under a sheet gone grey with age, and shelves of hymnals. Hundreds of them. Green cloth covers, gold lettering worn to ghosts: *Songs of Fen and Field*. The school's name stamped inside each one, and beneath that, in smaller type, *Marrowfen Edition*.

Ansel pulled one at random and opened it to the index, because that was what they did. That was who they were, before anything else. Before the family Bible with their name struck through in ink so old it had gone brown, before the bus out of a town that had stopped saying their name the moment they left it, before the meticulous notebooks that were the only family tree they trusted. You could erase a person from a book. But you couldn't erase the book itself without someone noticing the hole.

Ansel had built a whole life on noticing holes.

The lullaby was there in the index. *The Well's Kind Keeping*, page 114. Listed as a "traditional cradle song, arr. Q."

Q. Ansel's thumb paused on the letter. Quill? They filed the thought away in the drawer of their mind marked *later*, next to Petra Quill's flinch when the pub clock struck nine, next to the way the headmaster had said *your predecessor* three times and given three different names.

Page 114.

The song began normally enough. Four verses of gentle, rolling melody in 6/8, words about water and watching and sleep:

*Down where the dark water sings,*
*the well keeps all your small things.*
*Sleep, my love, the well is kind,*
*it holds what we leave behind.*

Ansel hummed it under their breath, standing alone in the grey morning light, and the melody was sweet in the way old things are sweet—slightly wrong, slightly sad, like a photograph of someone smiling at a joke you'll never hear. It was the kind of song that got into the walls of a place. Ansel had heard children singing fragments of it in the playground yesterday, skipping rope to it, and had assumed it was some radio thing.

Then came verse five.

Or rather, didn't.

The page jumped from verse four directly to a different song entirely, *The Miller's Rest*, and Ansel stood there frowning, counting back through the numbers. Index said five verses. Page said four. Ansel checked three more copies off the shelf, pulling them down with increasing speed, and every single one was the same: four verses, then *The Miller's Rest*, no gap, no acknowledgment, nothing.

Except.

On the fourth copy—their fingers had gone cold without their noticing; the music room held the damp of decades—the paper told a different story. When Ansel tilted page 114 toward the window light and held it at an angle, the surface of the paper caught differently along a strip about two inches wide. A band of slight roughness, invisible head-on, unmistakable sideways. Someone had removed something from this page. Not torn—removed. Cut away with a blade so fine and so patient that the remaining page looked untouched unless you knew to ask it questions.

Ansel fetched the desk lamp from the corner, blew dust off it, and held the page flat under the light.

There. Ghost-letters. The pressure of ink from the removed verse had transferred faintly to the facing page over years of the book being closed—a mirror-image shadow of text, backwards, like writing seen through skin. Ansel photographed it with their phone, then photographed it again with the flash, then sat down on a stacking chair that groaned under them and began the slow work of reading backwards.

It took forty minutes. The transfer was partial—maybe half the words legible—but half was more than enough to establish the shape of the absence. A final verse existed once. It had been cut from this copy with surgical care, and the cut had been made *before the ink fully dried on the rest of the page*, because the ghost-text sat under the printing of *The Miller's Rest* on the facing side, not over it. Whoever did this hadn't defaced a published book. They'd been part of making it.

Ansel wrote in their notebook, in the small square hand they used for facts that frightened them:

*The Well's Kind Keeping — 5 verses per index, 4 per page. Verse 5 excised at source. Not censorship after publication — omission during production. Someone decided, before these books ever reached Marrowfen, that the song would end early.*

They looked at the words they could recover from the ghost-text. Fragments. *"...and if the well should call your..."* Something. *"...name is the..."* Something. *"...belongs..."*

A verse about names. About belonging.

Ansel closed the notebook and sat in the silence of the music room, listening to the rain start against the high windows, and tried to think about anything else. They failed. Their whole life had trained them for exactly this moment: the hole in the record, the archive that lies by omission, the family that solves the problem of a person by removing the page. Ansel knew what it meant when someone cut a verse out of a song before anyone could learn it. It meant the verse was true, and the truth was load-bearing, and somebody had decided the structure would hold better without it.

* * *

Mr. Sorrel taught music on Tuesdays and Thursdays, and he was, by a distance, the strangest thing Ansel had encountered in Marrowfen, which was saying something. He was perhaps seventy, small and neat as a folded letter, with white hair combed in strict rows and cardigans buttoned to the throat even in the damp. He spoke rarely and precisely, like a man rationing words he'd counted out decades ago. The children adored him with the particular ferocity children reserve for adults who are clearly carrying something heavy and have never once set it down near them.

Thursday morning, Year Seven music. Ansel had no official reason to be there—they taught history and English—but they'd offered to help, and Sorrel had looked at them for a long moment with pale eyes and said, "Yes. Do," in a tone that suggested the offer had been expected for some time.

The lesson was the lullaby.

"Page one hundred and fourteen," Sorrel said, and thirty voices rustled paper. "We'll take it slowly. This is an old song. Older than the school." He raised his hand, and the room went quiet the way rooms go quiet around certain people—not commanded, but *held*.

He played the introduction himself, and his hands on the piano were suddenly, shockingly young—not the hesitant movements of an old man but something fluid and sure, muscle memory sunk deep as bedrock. Then he began to hum, because he never sang the words. Ansel noticed that immediately. The children sang; Sorrel accompanied, humming above them like a second melody, a harmony line only he knew.

And he was good. He was very good. The humming was warm and controlled and full of a tenderness that seemed almost embarrassing in that bare room, and Ansel understood within eight bars that this man had been singing this song his entire life, into it, maybe, the way you speak to someone across a great distance.

The children's voices rose—*sleep, my love, the well is kind*—and Ansel watched the verse numbers tick past. One. Two. Three. Four.

Sorrel's humming stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. Mid-phrase, mid-breath, at the bar where the fifth verse should have entered—and the effect was extraordinary, because the melody *wanted* to continue. Ansel could hear it, could feel it in their own chest, the phrase leaning forward into the silence like a person stepping toward a doorway that wasn't there. The accompaniment resolved, the children's last note died, and Mr. Sorrel stood at the piano with his eyes wet and his mouth pressed shut and his hands resting on the keys as though holding something down.

"Sir?" said a girl near the front—Wren Halloway, small and watchful, the one whose whisper about desks Ansel still carried in their notebook like a stone. "You always stop there."

"Do I?" said Sorrel.

"Yes. Every time. Right there." She pointed at the piano, at the exact bar, unerring. "Why do you always stop there?"

The room waited. Rain ticked against the windows. And Ansel watched Mr. Sorrel look down at the open hymnal—at page 114, at the clean, smooth, lying paper—and saw something move across his face that was not confusion and not sadness but recognition, the expression of a man meeting an old debt in an unexpected place.

"Because the song isn't finished," he said quietly. "And I don't know how it ends."

"You hummed it fine up to there," Wren said.

"Yes." His voice was very steady, the steadiness of a wall. "Up to there."

"But how can you not know how it ends if—" Wren began, and Sorrel closed the hymnal with a soft, final sound, and said, "Instruments away," and the lesson moved on, and the question stayed in the air like smoke with nowhere to go.

Afterward, while the children clattered out, Ansel remained by the door, and Sorrel did not look up from squaring the hymnals into their stack, but he said, without preamble, as though continuing a conversation begun years ago:

"You've been in the music room before hours."

"I have."

"The books are wrong," he said. "All of them. Every copy in this building, every copy in the church, every copy in any home in this parish. They're all wrong in the same place." He squared the last hymnal. His hands were trembling very slightly, and he stilled them by pressing them flat together, a gesture so practiced it had become prayer-shaped. "I checked, when I was younger than my students are now. I wrote to the publisher. There was no reply, because the publisher had ceased trading in 1911, and the edition was printed in 1910, and every copy of the *Marrowfen Edition* went out with the same missing verse, because the plates were cut before binding. All five hundred of them."

"Who cut them?"

For the first time he looked up, and his eyes were dry now, and terribly tired. "That," he said, "is the wrong question, Mr.—Miss—forgive me. Eastwick. That is the wrong question." He turned back to the piano and played a single note, middle C, letting it decay into the damp air. "The right question is: what does a song do when you take away its ending?"

"I don't know."

"It keeps going," said Mr. Sorrel. "Somewhere. Quietly. In the dark, where no one taught it to stop." He lifted his hands from the key. The note had long since faded; the room held it anyway. "Forty years I've hummed that song, Eastwick. I stop at the same bar every time because that is where I was taught to stop, and I have never once asked myself by whom, because I am afraid of the answer." He smiled then, a small, terrible, courteous smile. "You're an archivist, I'm told. You notice holes."

"I try."

"Don't try," said Sorrel. "Notice. There's a difference. Trying is optional. Noticing—" he gathered his folder, his chalk, his careful self, "—noticing has already happened to you. I could see it on Tuesday." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "The verse had a name in it. Whatever else the ghost on the page says or doesn't say—that much I remember, from before. From when I was small, and someone still sang it whole, once, at night, thinking no one listened." His knuckles whitened on the doorframe. "A song that ends in a name, Eastwick. Think about what kind of song needs to end in a name. And think about who benefits from a town full of people who can never finish it."

Then he was gone, his footsteps precise and fading down the corridor that bent where corridors shouldn't, and Ansel stood alone in the music room with the rain coming harder against the glass and their own reflection doubled in the dark windows—themselves, and behind themselves, faintly, the shelves of green hymnals, five hundred identical books, five hundred identical wounds.

They walked home through the rain without hurrying, and outside the cottage they met Petra Quill coming up the lane with her collar up and her arms full of firewood, and she took one look at their face and said, "You found the song."

"You know about it."

"I know it stops." She shifted the wood, and wouldn't quite meet their eyes. "Everyone here knows where it stops. We just don't ask why." She hesitated on the step, rain running off her hood, and something moved behind her face—fear, or its long-married cousin, duty. "Ansel. What did the index say? How many verses?"

"Five."

Petra closed her eyes briefly, as if confirming a diagnosis she'd carried since childhood. "Come inside," she said. "There's something you should see in my mother's things. And Ansel—" she pushed the door open, and the hallway beyond was dim and smelled of old paper and older grief, "—she kept a hymnal too. Hers isn't missing anything."
