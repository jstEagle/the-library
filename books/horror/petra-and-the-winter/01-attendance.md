## 1. Attendance

The snow began at the top of the pass and did not stop until Casper Sandoval reached the bottom of her grandmother's driveway.

She had been driving for six hours through country that grew progressively less interested in her — highway giving way to county road, county road to a two-lane track where the pines leaned so close they combed the roof of the rental car. The GPS had given up outside of town, its blue line dissolving into blank white, as though Vesper Hollow were a place the map had agreed to forget. She found it anyway, the way you find anything in winter: by following the smoke.

The town sat in a bowl of hills like an egg in a nest, gray rooftops dusted white, woodsmoke standing straight up from chimneys in the windless cold. A church steeple. A water tower with HOLLOW painted on it in letters gone rust-brown. And everywhere the snow, falling with the patience of something that had done this before and would do it again long after anyone currently living was gone.

Casper pulled up before the house on Corvus Lane just as the light was failing.

It was larger than she'd expected from the photographs the lawyer had sent — three stories of dark timber and narrow windows, a steep roof holding snow like a held breath. Shutters closed over every window on the upper floors. A wraparound porch whose boards groaned when she tested them, one by one, like a man counting under his breath. Above the door, carved into the lintel and softened by decades of weather, a single letter: **S**.

Her letter. Her name, or the first third of it, waiting for her before she'd ever known this place existed.

"Okay," she said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud made them real and she wanted very badly for all of this to be real. "Okay. I'm here."

The key turned without resistance, which somehow felt worse than if it hadn't.

* * *

Inside, the house smelled of cedar and cold ash and something faintly sweet underneath, like apples going soft in a cellar. Dust sheets covered the furniture in the front parlor, ghosting the shapes of chairs and a piano and something tall and narrow that might have been a cabinet or might have been a door. Casper pulled the sheets down one at a time, and each piece of furniture beneath was polished, unblemished, waiting — as though someone had cared for these rooms right up until the moment they'd stopped, and expected someone else to begin again exactly where they left off.

On the mantel stood a photograph: a woman with Casper's same dark, heavy brows, seated at a teacher's desk, thirteen children arranged behind her in three careful rows. On the desk in the photograph lay a rolled sheet of paper, and though the image was too small to read it, Casper knew instinctively what it was. Teachers always knew.

Her grandmother. Elowen Sandoval. The woman whose blood ran in Casper's veins and whose face she had never once seen in life, only now, here, in a house that had been sitting empty with the heat off and the dust sheets on, waiting for a girl it had never met to come home.

"I never knew about you," Casper told the photograph. It felt important to say it honestly, to establish that whatever this inheritance was, it wasn't longing fulfilled. "Nobody told me you existed until the lawyer's letter."

The woman in the photograph smiled her small, closed smile and said nothing, and the fire Casper built in the grate crackled and spat, and outside the snow went on falling with that terrible patience.

Upstairs, she chose the bedroom facing the road, unpacked two suitcases into a wardrobe that still held wire hangers shaped to shoulders smaller than hers, and fell asleep beneath a quilt that smelled of lavender and something older, something mineral, like the inside of a cave.

She dreamed of a classroom. In the dream she was standing at a chalkboard writing her own name over and over, and behind her, in rows, children watched, and every time she turned around to look at them their heads turned back toward the front a half-second too late, like echoes arriving after the sound.

* * *

Vesper Hollow Consolidated School was a brick building with a boiler that clanked like a man in chains, and Casper loved it immediately, the way you can love a thing precisely because it is imperfect and yours. Her classroom was Room 4, second floor, north light, twenty-four desks in four rows of six. Twenty-four students on her roster. Eleven of them showed up on her first morning, blinking and pink-cheeked from the cold, and she spent the first hour learning names — Milo, who read under his desk; the twins Ada and Iris, who finished each other's sentences; a small solemn boy called Fen who drew wolves in the margins of everything.

They were good kids. Warm, curious, feral in the way children are before the world files their edges down. By lunchtime Casper had decided, with the reckless optimism of every new teacher in history, that this year was going to be the making of her.

It was during the afternoon lull, while the students copied definitions from the board, that she noticed the whispering.

It started near the windows — Ada leaning toward Iris, a hand cupped against her mouth, both girls glancing back at Casper and away again. Then Fen, murmuring to Milo. Then a ripple of it moving down the rows like wind through wheat, that particular hush that teachers learn to hear through walls.

"Something you'd like to share?" Casper asked pleasantly.

Silence. Eleven faces arranged into elaborate innocence.

It was Milo, finally, who cracked — because Milo was too honest for his own good and everyone knew it. He raised his hand halfway, then thought better of it, then raised it all the way.

"It's just—" He glanced around, checking his allies and finding none. "This room. Room 4. There used to be more desks."

"There are twenty-four," Casper said. "That's plenty."

"There used to be thirty-seven." His voice dropped, and the whole class leaned in despite themselves, the way people lean toward fires. "My mom says they took them out the year my aunt was in fifth grade. There was a class here that—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "There was a class that got counted."

"Counted," Casper repeated.

"That's what everybody says. That they got counted." He looked genuinely distressed now, caught between the story he'd been raised inside and the adult in front of him who might make it real by repeating it. "Nobody says what happened to them. Nobody counts to thirteen in this town. Elevators skip it. My grandma won't bake a dozen of anything. And the old part of the school—" He nodded toward the wall behind Casper's desk, beyond which, he said, the 1950s wing still stood, boarded shut since before any parent currently living had been young. "That's where they had class. Nobody goes in there."

The bell rang before Casper could answer, and eleven children exploded out of their seats with the gratitude of the rescued, and the question hung in the empty classroom afterward like chalk dust: *a class that got counted.*

That evening she stayed late grading diagnostic essays, and when she finally switched off the lights and walked the corridor toward the stairs, she passed the old wing. The doors were plywooded over, but above the boards, mounted on the wall where a room number should be, was a small brass plate, green with age:

**ROOM 4**

Her room. Her number. As though the school had simply stopped there, mid-breath, thirty-some years ago, and everything since — her whole life, her whole self — had been a delay in the schedule.

* * *

Principal Aldous Marsh kept an office that looked like a man had been defending it against cheerfulness for forty years and winning. He was seventy if he was a day, with a face like a folded map and reading glasses pushed up into wild white hair, and when Casper mentioned — lightly, professionally, over the paperwork of her first week — that some of the students had told her stories about the old wing, he set down his pen with great care, the way you set down something you're afraid of breaking.

"Sandoval," he said. Not her first name. Just the surname, tasting it. "You're Elowen's granddaughter."

"You knew her?"

"Everyone knew her." He removed his glasses, cleaned them on his tie, put them back on. Through the lenses his eyes looked suddenly very large and very tired. "Elowen taught in that building for nineteen years. Fourth grade. Room 4." He let that sit. "I'm going to tell you something, Ms. Sandoval, and I'm going to tell you once, and then we're not going to discuss it again, because discussing it is how it starts."

Casper waited.

"In this school, attendance is taken on paper. Silently. You mark the boxes, you file the sheet, and you never — *never* — speak the names aloud while doing it. No roll call. No calling out 'here' or 'absent.' You don't count your students out loud, you don't count them in loud, and if you ever find yourself short a child at the end of the day, you do not stand at the door calling their name into the hallway." His voice had gone flat and precise, a recitation worn smooth by repetition. "Am I understood?"

"That seems—" She laughed, and heard how thin it came out. "Mr. Marsh, with respect, they're children, not—"

"Not what?" He leaned forward. "Say it. Say what they're not, and say why you're sure."

She couldn't. That was the terrible thing, standing in that brown little office with the radiator ticking — she couldn't produce a single reason, only the modern, reasonable certainty that there was nothing to be certain *against*. Marsh studied her for a long moment, and something in his face shifted from warning to something almost like grief.

"Your grandmother said the same thing," he said quietly. "Her first week. Word for word." He slid a folder across the desk. "Your classroom roster. Check the archive copy against yours when you get home. And Ms. Sandoval — welcome to Vesper Hollow. Genuinely. We're glad you came." He paused at the door as she gathered her things, and added, without turning around: "We've been glad for a long time."

* * *

The house was dark when she returned, though she'd left a lamp burning in the front parlor, and the lamp was burning still — but the hallway mirror, the tall oval glass in the entry hall that had been covered by a dust sheet when she arrived, was uncovered now.

Casper stood very still in the doorway, snow melting off her boots onto the floorboards, looking at the mirror she had definitely, certainly, covered.

Then, because she was tired, and cold, and because the alternative was thinking about what uncovered meant, she crossed the hall to pull the sheet back over it. Her reflection rose to meet her — pale woman, dark hair escaping its clip, coat still buttoned wrong — and Casper lifted her hand to sweep the hair from her eyes.

In the glass, the reflection lifted its hand a half-second later.

She stopped. Held her breath. Watched the woman in the mirror hold hers a beat behind, like a swimmer surfacing late.

Slowly, deliberately, Casper blinked.

The reflection blinked after.

And in that stolen sliver of time between her blink and its answer — a gap no wider than the space between heartbeats — Casper saw, reflected in the glass behind her own shoulder, the far end of the hallway. Saw the door to the cellar standing open, though she had locked it that morning. Saw, descending the stairs into that darkness, small and patient and in single file, a line of children carrying little tin lunch pails, on their way to a class that had started decades ago and had never once been dismissed.

The reflection finished its blink. The hallway behind her, when she turned, was empty, the cellar door shut, the house silent as held breath.

On the hall table, beside the photograph of her smiling grandmother, lay a sheet of paper that had not been there when she left. An old-fashioned attendance roster, yellowed at the edges, ruled in faded blue lines. Thirteen names ran down its length, each one struck through with a single clean stroke — and beneath them, in fresh ink, in handwriting that was unmistakably, impossibly, her own:

**Sandoval, Casper.**
