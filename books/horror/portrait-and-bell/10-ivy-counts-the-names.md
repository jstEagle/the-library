## 10. Ivy Counts the Names

The piano in the Cold Ash schoolroom was older than the school.

Ivy knew this because her father had told her so on her first morning, when she'd asked why it was painted that ugly greenish-black color, like a beetle's back. *It came with the building,* he'd said. *Probably came with the parish.* He'd said it lightly, the way he said most things, but Ivy had noticed — because she noticed things, that was her whole talent, her one magic trick — that he hadn't touched it. He'd stood a careful half-step away from it while he talked, the way you stand away from a dog you don't know.

Nobody played the piano. That was the strange part. In her old school in Marlow, Mrs. Okafor played every Friday and everyone sang badly and happily. Here there was a music lesson on the timetable, Wednesday afternoons, and here was a piano sitting right against the wall under the tall window like it had been waiting a hundred years for someone to lift its lid. But Miss Prentice only brought out a box of tambourines and a triangle, and the children sang without accompaniment, their voices flat and careful, like people reading words in a language they didn't speak.

*We don't use the piano,* a boy named Colm told her when she asked. He said it the way you'd say *we don't swim in the reservoir.* Not an explanation. A boundary.

So of course Ivy went to look at it.

She waited until Wednesday afternoon, when the whole school trooped out to the field for what Miss Prentice called Nature Study and what was really forty minutes of standing around while the fog sat on the grass like something sleeping. Ivy said she needed the lavatory. Miss Prentice looked at her a moment too long before nodding — everyone in Cold Ash looked at you a moment too long; it was practically the village sport — and then Ivy was alone in the corridor, and then alone in the classroom, and then alone with the piano.

Up close it was worse than it looked from across the room. The paint wasn't just greenish-black; it was thick, applied in layers over years, and someone had painted it *deliberately*, working it into every crack and corner with a small brush, the way you'd seal a thing. The lid wouldn't open — not locked, exactly, but stuck, as if the paint had been allowed to run into the seam and harden there. The pedals were painted too. Even the casters. Someone had wanted this instrument to stop being an instrument and become furniture, become a shape, become nothing anyone would ever touch again.

Ivy crouched down to see if the underside was painted the same way.

It wasn't.

Underneath, where no one would ever look unless they were nine years old and small and stubborn, the wood was bare and pale and covered in letters.

Not writing. *Carving.* Deep cuts, made with something sharp and patient, each letter gouged into the grain and darkened by decades of dust and shadow. Names. Rows and rows of names, carved in different hands — some in big wobbly capitals, some in a fine slanted script, some so small they must have been cut by a child working very slowly with a very sharp knife, taking all the time in the world.

Ivy lay down on the cold floorboards and wriggled halfway under, her cheek against the wood, and read them.

**MARGARET COLE 1889**

**THOMAS VASEY 1911**

**EDITH PELL 1911**

Two in the same year. She frowned at that. Her father was always going on about patterns — he said history was just patterns that people were too embarrassed to say out loud — and two names in one year felt like a pattern with its coat buttoned wrong.

She kept reading.

**ALFRED TARNOW 1933**

**SUSANNA HALE 1955**

**PETER GRIEVE 1955**

Again two. Again twenty-two years apart from the ones above. Ivy counted on her fingers, which she was not supposed to do anymore because she was in the top group for arithmetic, but counting on your fingers was different when you were lying under a haunted piano and your heart was going fast. 1889. 1911. 1933. 1955. 1977. 1999. Twenty-two, twenty-two, twenty-two, twenty-two, twenty-two. Like a clock striking. Like something keeping time.

And at the end of the last row, lower than the others, cramped into the corner of the wood where the leg joined the frame:

**WILLIAM DUNN 2021**

William Dunn. She knew that name. It was on the enrollment list her father had shown her mother at the kitchen table, late at night, thinking she was asleep. She knew it the way you know a word you've seen written somewhere and never heard spoken: William Dunn, age ten, admitted September, gone October. There was a desk on the boys' side of the classroom with a fresh-carved initial inside the lid — a *W* — and nobody sat at it, and nobody had taken the books out of it, and when Ivy had put her hand flat on that desk during her first week, Colm had watched her do it with an expression she couldn't read then and could read now.

The expression was *don't.*

But here was the thing about being nine, Ivy thought, wriggling further under the piano until her shoulders ached and the dust tickled her nose. Here was the thing grown-ups never understood. When they said *don't*, what they meant was *I'm frightened and I need you to be frightened too so I don't have to be the only one.* And Ivy was frightened — properly frightened, the kind that made her stomach feel like cold water — but underneath the fright was the other thing, the engine-thing, the thing that made her find the bronze mouth in the churchyard wall and hold it up to her ear even though it hummed.

She wanted to know. Knowing was the only weapon she had that fit her hand.

There was space after William Dunn's name. A gap of clean pale wood, maybe four inches of it, and then the corner, and then nothing. Room for more. Room for a whole next line of names, if the line ever started.

Ivy took out her pencil case. She had a pencil, obviously, but also a stub of red crayon and, folded into the zip pocket where her mother couldn't find it and make her throw it away, a sheet of thin tracing paper she'd taken from the art cupboard weeks ago because you never knew. You never knew when you'd want to copy something down exactly.

She laid the tracing paper over the names and rubbed with the side of the pencil, gently, the way you rub a coin under paper to get the queen's face. The letters came up ghost-grey, backwards, beautiful. Margaret Cole. Thomas Vasey. Edith Pell. All of them marching down the page in their crooked ranks, twenty-two years between each pair of years, and always — she checked, she counted twice — either one child or two, and lately always two, as if whatever did the choosing had gotten greedier, or hungrier, or simply more efficient.

Twenty-one names. She counted them three times to be sure. Twenty-one children, carved by their own hands or someone else's, hidden under the one object in the school that nobody touched, sealed under layers of paint like a letter under a floorboard.

Twenty-one. And the newest one, William Dunn, 2021, sat at the end of the row with all that empty wood after it.

Ivy was still lying there, the tracing paper curled carefully in her fist, when she saw it.

At the very edge of the empty space, right where the pale wood began, there was a mark. Small. She had to bring her eye within inches of it to be sure it wasn't a knot or a scratch. But it wasn't a knot. It was a groove. A single shallow cut, curved like a crescent moon, fresh enough that the wood inside it was still bright, still raw, still the color of new pine instead of old shadow.

The beginning of a letter.

Not a finished letter. Not even half of one. Just the first stroke — the opening curve of a *C*, or a *G*, or an *O*. The kind of stroke you make when you've set your knife against the wood and drawn the blade toward you once, and stopped.

Waiting.

That was the word that arrived in Ivy's head, uninvited, and refused to leave. The carving wasn't abandoned. Abandoned carvings looked different — they looked *stopped*, they looked like the person got bored or got called in for tea. This looked like it was paused. Like the knife was set down somewhere close, within reach, and the hand that held it was coming back. The curve sat there in the pale wood with a patience that made her skin crawl, the patience of a mouth held open, of a chair pulled out at a table, of a bed turned down.

The newest carving was half-finished. Waiting for its letters.

*Ivy Halloran* started with an I. An I was one straight line. It would take two seconds.

She scrambled backward out from under the piano so fast she cracked her head on the pedal housing, and the pain was almost a relief because it was ordinary pain, school-pain, the kind that had nothing to do with crescent-shaped cuts in hidden wood. She knelt on the floorboards breathing hard, the tracing paper crushed in her hand, and looked at the piano's painted flank, its beetle-back shine, its hundred years of deliberate silence.

Someone was carving the names. Someone had carved them for a hundred and thirty years, generation after generation, in the same hidden place, in hands that changed but in a place that never did. Which meant someone taught someone. Which meant it was passed along, deliberately, like a recipe. Like a duty.

Which meant someone in Cold Ash already knew the next name.

Ivy thought about William Dunn's desk with its books still in it. She thought about the bronze mouth in the churchyard wall, and the humming she'd heard through the locked tower door, low and patient, like something practicing a single note. She thought about the supper at the squire's house, how her mother had come home quiet and washed her hands twice, and how every grown-up in that bright dining room had worn the same little metal button, and how when Ivy had asked her mother about it her mother had said *it's nothing, sweetheart, it's just a village thing* in the voice grown-ups used when they were hoping a thing would become nothing if they said so firmly enough.

Twenty-one names. One empty space. One half-made letter, waiting.

Her father had a word he used when he was teaching, when the class found a pattern and got excited and started shouting out answers ahead of the proof. He'd hold up his hand and say, *Careful. A pattern is a promise until proven otherwise.* The other kids laughed at it. Ivy had always liked it. Because it meant her father believed the world kept its promises, even the terrible ones. Especially the terrible ones.

Every twenty-two years. 1889, 1911, 1933, 1955, 1977, 1999, 2021.

She did the last sum standing in the empty classroom with the fog pressing flat against the windows, and the answer was so simple it barely deserved to be called arithmetic.

This year was 2043.

The bell in the tower had been melted down before her grandfather was born, and it rang anyway, once, at midnight, three weeks ago, and a boy named William Dunn had gone to service and left his books behind, and now there was a single curved cut in the wood under the piano, patient as an open mouth, waiting for the rest of a name.

Ivy smoothed the tracing paper flat against her chest, folded it small, and pushed it deep into the zip pocket with the red crayon. Then she walked out of the classroom, closed the door softly behind her, and went down the corridor to rejoin the line of children coming in from the fog — walking slowly, like a girl who had only been to the lavatory, like a girl with nothing folded against her ribs and nothing ticking behind her eyes.

Colm fell into step beside her. He smelled of wet grass and cold air.

"You were under it," he said. Not a question.

Ivy looked straight ahead. "Under what?"

He was quiet for three steps. Four. Five. Then, very low, without looking at her either, in the voice of a boy who had decided something important and hated himself for it:

"There's a space after the last one. Isn't there."

It wasn't a question either.

"Yes," Ivy said.

Colm nodded, once, like a man confirming a diagnosis. And then he said the thing that followed Ivy home through the fog, through supper, through the long grey dusk, through the moment when she heard her father's key in the front door and ran to hug him harder than she had since they'd arrived — the thing that made her hold on and count his heartbeats and think: *twenty-two years, twenty-two years, whose name is in the wood—*

"Miss Prentice keeps the knife," Colm said. "In her desk drawer. Under the register."

* * *

That night Ivy lay awake in the narrow bed under the sloped ceiling of the cottage, listening to the house settle and the fog press against the glass, and she made herself a promise the way children make promises — absolutely, silently, with the whole weight of their small stubborn souls.

Tomorrow she would show her father the tracing. Tomorrow she would tell him about the years, the pattern, the half-finished letter. Tomorrow the knowing would stop being hers alone, because a secret that size shouldn't live inside one nine-year-old; it rattled around in there like a stone in a jar.

But tonight, just before sleep finally took her, she thought of the curve of the unfinished letter, that single patient crescent in the pale wood, and she understood — with the terrible clarity that comes only to children and the dying — that whoever held the knife had not yet chosen whose name to carve.

And that the choosing was close.

Outside, far off across the sleeping village, something in the church tower gave a sound that was almost a hum, and almost a breath, and almost — though Ivy told herself she imagined it, told herself firmly, the way her mother would want her to — almost a voice, trying out the shape of a single letter, getting ready to begin.
