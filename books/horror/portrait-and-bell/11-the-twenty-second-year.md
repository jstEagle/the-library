## 11. The Twenty-Second Year

The invitation came on card stock the color of clotted cream, edged in gilt that caught the light like something wet. *Mr. and Mrs. Edmund Vasey-Hale request the pleasure of Mr. Mirabel Halloran's company at the Hall, Thursday next, seven o'clock. A private matter concerning the school.*

*A private matter.* Mirabel read the card four times at his desk in the empty classroom, after the last of the children had gone out into the thin November light. Twenty-nine desks faced him. He had taken to counting them the way other men took to prayer.

The empty one was still empty. Thomas Weir's desk. The boy who had never existed, according to the constable's immaculate paperwork. The boy whose mother had smiled at Mirabel with eyes like frosted glass and said, *We never had a Thomas, sir. You must be thinking of someone else's child.*

He put the card in his pocket and did not tell Senna. Not because he meant to deceive her — he had stopped keeping things from her the night Ivy's name had appeared, half-carved, in his mind's eye and would not leave — but because he did not yet know what he would say. *The squire has invited me to discuss your daughter's future* was not a sentence a rational man spoke aloud.

Thursday came down grey and still, the fog pressing against the windows of the schoolhouse like something listening. He walked up the lane to the Hall as the light failed, past hedgerows that seemed to lean in, past the church where the belfry stood open and dark and empty, the way it had stood since 1911, the way it had *not* stood at midnight three weeks ago when something down in the bones of the world had rung once, and every dog in Cold Ash had screamed.

The Hall rose out of the fog in stages — first the chimneys, then the roofline, then the whole pale bulk of it, Georgian and symmetrical and wrong the way a waxwork is wrong. Every window was lit. Every window had always been lit, Senna said. She'd counted once, walking home from the shop. Thirty-one windows, thirty-one lights, every evening, in a house that was supposed to be half-shut-up and failing, the way all the great houses were failing now.

The door opened before he knocked.

"Mr. Halloran." Edmund Vasey-Hale stood in the frame like a man posing for a portrait he'd been sitting for all his life. He was perhaps fifty-five, broad through the chest, silver at the temples, with the kind of handsome, weathered face that farmers trust and bankers lend to. His smile was warm. It reached his eyes the way lamplight reaches a well — you could see it go down a little way, and then there was just depth. "You came. Good. I was afraid you'd make me come to the schoolhouse, and I'd rather not discuss this in a room full of small chairs."

"I can't imagine why not."

"Because children repeat things." He stepped back. "Come in. Brandy?"

The hall of the Hall was oak and dark and hung with portraits — the same heavy jaw and hooded eyes repeating down three centuries like a stamp pressed into wax, generation after generation, until the most recent ones stopped bothering to be accurate and simply stopped. The last portrait was of a young man in a modern suit, unfinished, the hands only suggested, the face a smear of grey primer.

"Grandfather," Edmund said, following his gaze. "He sat for it in '55 and died before the third session. The painter refused payment. Said the face wouldn't hold still." He said it lightly, an anecdote, and watched Mirabel with those well-deep eyes to see what he did with it.

"I'm not here for brandy."

"No," Edmund agreed pleasantly. "You're here because your daughter's name is half-carved into a board under a piano, and you want to know who's holding the chisel."

* * *

The study was small for a house this size — a deliberate smallness, Mirabel thought, the kind of room built for conspiracies. A fire burned low. On the desk, squared neatly, lay a ledger bound in green leather, and beside it, on a square of velvet like a relic in a church, a fragment of bronze the size of a thumb, green with tarnish, shaped — Mirabel's stomach turned before his mind caught up — shaped like a mouth.

Edmund saw him see it.

"You've been in the parish archive," he said. "I know. Mrs. Tarnow told me you'd been asking about 1911. She also told me she burned a page for you. She was quite proud of that." He poured two glasses of brandy anyway, set one within Mirabel's reach, and sat down behind the desk with the ease of a man who had never in his life been interrupted. "So let's not spend the evening pretending. Let's spend it being honest, which is rarer, and which I find I want, at my age, very badly."

"Then say what you brought me here to say."

Edmund turned the velvet square a quarter-turn, aligning it with the desk's edge. A small gesture. Precise. The gesture of a man who aligned things.

"In 1911 the parish of Cold Ash owned a bell called Saint Aldhelm's, cast in 1348, and it was the finest thing in this valley. It rang for harvests and funerals and the birth of every founder's child, and while it hung, the village *prospered*. Not thrived — prospered. Our fields didn't fail. Our cattle didn't sicken. Our children didn't die in infancy, not one, not in five hundred years, while every parish around us buried half its cradles. The wool never rotted. The debts never came due. Do you know what that's worth, Mr. Halloran? Do you know what a family is worth that has never once known famine, in a country that has known nothing else?"

"Every record has an explanation—"

"Every record has an *entry*," Edmund said. "The explanation is the bell. And the bell has an appetite. One child, every twenty-two years. Taken below, given to the throat, and the count keeps the covenant whole. Twenty-two years, twenty-two tolls, and the bell chooses which child. It always chooses well, Mr. Halloran. It has never once chosen a founder's blood — not since the founders learned to feed it their *own* children first, in the beginning, to teach it the taste of the covenant. It chooses from the edges. From the incomers. From the ones who won't be missed, because the village has already agreed not to miss them."

"Thomas Weir."

"Thomas Weir," Edmund said, with something almost like respect. "Twenty-one. And this year — this year, Mr. Halloran — the twenty-second. The count completes. The cycle turns over. And the bell will choose again, and this time the choice will be the last for another generation, and Cold Ash will be safe until 1993, and my grandchildren will grow rich and unburied in the ground of their fathers."

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a clock counted with the patience of something that had counted before.

"You're confessing," Mirabel said. "You've just confessed to child murder."

"I've just described a *covenant*." Edmund's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing — it never rose, it only deepened, like water finding a lower channel. "You're a schoolteacher. You believe in ledgers and explanations and the decency of strangers. I'm offering you the chance to stop believing, which is a mercy, and to be paid very well for it, which is a courtesy. Because here is my problem, Mr. Halloran: the bell has chosen, and it has chosen *well*, and I need a man of the village to stand at the schoolhouse door on the appointed night and simply — not notice. To mark the roll in the morning and find it complete. To write the letters home that need writing. To be, in short, the schoolmaster, which is what we hired you to be."

The brandy sat untouched between them, amber and still, like a small held breath.

"And if I don't."

"Then the bell chooses anyway. It always chooses. The only question ever decided in this village, in five hundred years, is whether the choosing goes smoothly or whether it goes *badly*, and I will tell you what badly looks like, because my grandfather saw it. In 1889 a vicar — an incomer, like you, a rationalist, like you — locked the church and stood in the tower with a shotgun. The bell rang anyway. It rang *through* him. And in the spring the fields failed, and the cattle aborted, and the fever came, and Cold Ash buried forty-one of its own by August, and the founders opened the books and found the count had not been paid and paid it double." He leaned forward. "You cannot break it. You can only decide whether your daughter's name finishes carving itself quietly, or whether the whole village pays for your principles."

The room went very quiet. Mirabel became aware of his own heartbeat, and of a sound beneath it, far down, below the floor, below the foundations, a hum like a struck note that would not finish dying.

"You keep saying *the bell has chosen*," he said.

"I do."

"Chosen what? Say it. Say the actual words."

Edmund looked at him for a long moment, and for the first time something moved behind the well-deep eyes — not conscience, Mirabel thought. Arithmetic. The look of a man checking a sum and finding it still correct.

"The bell has chosen Ivy Halloran," he said. "And I am offering you a partnership — a share, a real one, a founder's share, in the prosperity of this parish for the rest of your natural life. Your wife will want for nothing. Your daughter will be educated anywhere on earth. And in exchange you will stand at your door on the appointed night, and you will hear the toll, and you will do nothing, and in the morning you will be the richest schoolmaster in England, and in twenty-two years your grandchildren will thank you for the wisdom of it. Every founder in this room's history took that bargain, Mr. Halloran. Every one of them. It's how the portraits got on the wall."

Mirabel stood. He was surprised to find his legs steady; he had expected them to fail, the way they'd failed in the constable's office, the way they'd failed at Ivy's bedside every night for three weeks while she slept and he listened for humming.

"You've made one error," he said.

"Have I."

"You think this is a bargain because you've never been outside it. You were born in this room, in that chair, and the portraits taught you the arithmetic, and you've never once done the sum from the other side of the desk." He picked up the brandy glass and held it, considering it, then set it down again without drinking. "Ivy is six years old. She loses her teeth and cries about it. She can't pronounce *February*. Last week she asked me whether the moon gets cold, and when I said yes, she asked me who knits for it. And you sat there and told me the price of her is a share in a farm and a wall of dead men's faces."

"Mr. Halloran—"

"No." The word came out of him with a force that surprised them both, and the fire flinched in the grate, and far below the floor the hum changed pitch — one note higher, attentive. "You don't get to call it a covenant. You don't get to call it *choosing*. A thing that eats children is not choosing, and a man who feeds it is not a steward, and a village that smiles and burns its ledgers is not a parish, it's a *larder*. You want to know what I am? I'm the schoolmaster. I count the children. Every morning, twenty-nine desks, and I count them, and every one of them is somebody's moon-knitting question, and I will not stand at any door and not notice. Do you understand me? I would rather this village burned to the last stone and every field in it went to salt than hand you one child to keep your portraits company."

Edmund had not moved. But his face had changed — the warmth draining out of it not like a mask falling but like water finding a lower channel, leaving the features bare and older and much less handsome, and Mirabel understood suddenly that the warmth had been the mask, and the face beneath it was the face in the unfinished portrait, the face that wouldn't hold still.

"That," Edmund said quietly, "was the wrong answer."

"It was the only answer."

"Then you should know the count doesn't need your consent. It never has. The schoolmaster is a courtesy. The bell is not." He rose, and as he did, every lamp in the study dimmed at once, gently, as if the house were drawing breath. "You had a chance to be rich, Mr. Halloran. More than that — you had a chance to be *inside*, and inside is the only safe place in Cold Ash, and it always has been. Ambrose Pell could tell you what it is to be outside. Ask him, before he dies. Ask him what he keeps."

The hum below the floor had become, unmistakably, a voice — or the memory of a voice, or twenty-one voices speaking in unison so precisely they sounded like one, and Mirabel knew, with a rationalist's cold and total clarity, that it was saying his daughter's name.

"You'll leave by the back stair," Edmund said, opening the study door onto darkness. "The servants shouldn't hear this. They're fond of you."

* * *

The fog had thickened while he was inside. The lane back down to the village was a grey corridor with no sides, and Mirabel walked it fast, hands in fists, breath sawing, and it was only at the lych-gate that he realized he had not been followed, and that this was worse — that the whole village had simply let him go, the way you let a man walk out onto ice, because the ice was where he was going anyway.

The church tower stood against the fog, black and empty and patient. And as he passed beneath it, high above, in a belfry that had held no bell since 1911, something moved — a slow, heavy shifting, like a great throat swallowing — and a single sound came down through the dark that was not yet a toll but was the promise of one:

A half-note. A breath drawn to ring.

And in the schoolhouse, when he got home and lit the lamp and checked — he could not *not* check — the board hidden beneath the piano had changed. Ivy's name was no longer half-finished.

The first letter stood complete. The *I*, carved deep and clean, in a hand far older than any chisel in the village.

Twenty-two years to the day since the last child had gone below, and the count had begun.
