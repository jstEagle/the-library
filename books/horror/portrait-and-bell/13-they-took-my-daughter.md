## 13. They Took My Daughter

**MIRABEL**

We left at four in the morning, before the fog had finished deciding what it wanted to be.

I told myself the packing was ordinary — a father loading a car in the dark, a family going on holiday. But my hands knew better. They shook as I carried Ivy's suitcase past the hall mirror, and they shook harder when I saw what Senna had done to the windows: every pane crossed with tape, and behind the tape, pressed flat against the glass, sprigs of rowan she'd cut from Ambrose Pell's garden at midnight. She wouldn't say where she'd learned that. She only said, *Ambrose kept notes*, and her voice had the flatness of someone who has stopped being frightened and started being armed.

Ivy slept in the back seat with her arms around her sketchbook. In the last week she'd drawn the church tower eleven times. Always from below. Always with the belfry empty.

"Drive," Senna said, and I drove.

The lane out of Cold Ash runs west between hedgerows older than any map I've ever taught from. I know roads. I have driven ten thousand miles of them, and I know how a road should behave — how it curves, how it climbs, how the verges narrow at a junction. This road did none of those things. It ran straight for two miles and then curved gently right, and when the curve ended we were passing the same leaning oak, the same gap in the hedge shaped like a kneeling figure, the same milestone reading COLD ASH 3.

I said nothing. Senna said nothing. The odometer ticked up honest numbers while the world outside refused to move.

At the fourth pass of the milestone, Ivy woke.

"Papa," she said, "we've been here before."

"No, love. New road."

"It's the same tree." Her voice wasn't frightened. That was the worst part. It was the patient, reasonable tone of a child correcting an adult who is pretending not to see something obvious. "It has a face. Look."

I looked. The knot in the oak did look like a face — a long, hollow-eyed face, mouth open, as if the wood were mid-scream or mid-song. I had noticed it the first time through and thought nothing of it. Now it felt like a landmark. Like a marker on a route someone else had drawn.

Senna turned in her seat. "Ivy. Put your headphones on. Close your eyes. Count backward from a hundred."

"Why?"

"Because I asked you to."

Ivy obeyed. Children are good at obeying the instructions that matter; it's the homework they resist. I heard her small voice counting under the hum of the tyres, and I watched the mirror, and in the mirror, far back down the looping road, I saw headlights. One pair. Holding steady. Never gaining, never falling behind.

"They're following us," I said.

"Or the road is," Senna said. "Same thing here."

* * *

**SENNA**

The sixth time we passed the milestone, I made Mirabel stop the car.

He looked at me like I'd lost my mind, which was fair, because I had decided to do something insane: get out, walk to the milestone, and put my hand on it. Cold stone. Real lichen. A snail shell crushed into the grit at its base — the same crushed shell, I'd have sworn, each time, arranged the same way, spiral outward.

"You can't reason with a road," Mirabel said through the window.

"You can't outrun one either." I got back in. "So we stop feeding it distance. It wants us driving. It wants us *going*. Every mile we do, it winds us tighter." I pointed back the way we'd come, toward the village, toward the tower just visible above the fog line, absurdly close for a place we'd been fleeing for an hour. "Look how near it still is. We haven't gone anywhere. We've been on a leash."

Mirabel stared at the tower. I watched him do the arithmetic he'd been refusing all week — the arithmetic that says a thing like this doesn't chase you, because it doesn't have to. It only has to be patient. It only has to own the ground.

"We go back through," he said slowly. "Not out. Through. If the loop always returns us to the Ash Road, then the exit isn't west. It's—"

"Don't say it."

"Somebody has to say it."

Nobody said it. But we both understood: there might be no way out that didn't pass the church. And the church, we now knew, had a throat.

In the back seat, Ivy's counting reached zero. She started over without being told. Good girl. Keep counting. Keep your eyes shut and your name yours.

Because that was the other thing I hadn't told Mirabel. When I'd tucked Ivy in last night, she'd shown me her palm. There was a mark on it — faint, brownish, like a stain from handling old bronze. A ring, perfectly round, the size of a shilling, printed into the skin of her hand.

She'd held the bronze fragment. Senna's find, our one weapon, wrapped in cloth in my coat pocket. But she hadn't touched it. I'd checked. Twice.

The bell had touched *her*.

* * *

**MIRABEL**

We came back into Cold Ash the way water comes back to a drain.

No decision was required. The road simply bent, kindly, reasonably, and delivered us past the schoolhouse, past Mrs. Tarnow's cottage where a single candle burned in the window at five in the morning, past the lychgate where Edmund Vasey-Hale stood waiting.

Standing. Waiting. At five in the morning, in his good coat, hands folded, as if he'd been there since the founding of the parish.

He didn't step into the road. He didn't need to. He smiled the village smile — the too-perfect politeness I'd mistaken, months ago, for decency — and inclined his head as we rolled past at walking pace.

"Master Halloran," he said, though the windows were shut. His voice arrived anyway, mild as milk. "Safe home."

Then the fog took him, and the road gave us our house, and the engine died of its own accord in the drive, and none of us suggested trying again. What would be the point? You cannot flee a place that is inside your daughter's hand.

Senna carried Ivy inside. Ivy was asleep again — or something like sleep — and did not wake when laid in her bed, though her lips moved. Counting, maybe. Or reciting. I leaned close and caught three words, repeated, in a rhythm like a toll:

*"Twenty-two. Twenty-two. Twenty-two."*

I sat on the edge of her bed until the grey light came. I went through everything we had: Ambrose's records, the diagram of the crypt's true geometry, the iron key heavy in my pocket like a piece of night. And the bronze fragment, warm. Warm, though the house was cold. Warm as a held coin. Warm as flesh.

At dawn I heard the front door open downstairs and thought — briefly, stupidly — that Senna had gone out. Then I heard her cry out, once, a sound I will hear until I die, and I took the stairs three at a time.

* * *

**SENNA**

Her bed was empty.

Not rumpled-empty. Not child-got-up-early empty. The blankets were smooth, tucked as I'd tucked them, and lying on the pillow, centered, deliberate, was a strip of pale wood about the length of my forearm.

Carved into it: **IVORY HALLORAN** — but only half-finished. The I, the V, the O were deep and clean, letters cut by a patient chisel. The R was shallow. The Y was barely begun, a single groove trailing off like a breath interrupted.

The rest of the name was waiting. Waiting for letters that would never need cutting, because the carving wasn't a record. It was a *count*. And the count had come for its twenty-second.

On the floor beneath the bed, her sketchbook lay open to the twelfth drawing of the tower. Except this one was different. In this one, the belfry wasn't empty. Something hung there — a shape drawn in strokes so dark the paper shone — and beneath it, in Ivy's careful schoolhand, a caption:

*it knows my name now*

And under that, smaller, pressed so hard the pencil had torn through:

*i counted wrong. there were twenty-one names under the piano but i am the twenty-second and the first one was a Vasey-Hale*

I stood in that room holding my daughter's drawings while my husband shouted her name into the fog, and I want to tell you I wept, or raged, or collapsed. I did none of those things. Something in me closed like a door nailed shut, and behind that door a fire lit that will not go out while I live.

They had taken her through walls, through wards of rowan and tape, through a locked door and a sleeping house. Fine. Then walls meant nothing, and neither did their patience, and neither did their count — because a count can be broken. Ambrose's diagram showed how. The founders' own records showed why. And the bronze in my pocket — the original metal, the *first* metal, the piece they'd hidden for a hundred years because they feared exactly this —

It was warm against my ribs. Almost pulsing. As if it knew it was going home.

Mirabel came back in, grey-faced, fog-wet, empty-handed. He looked at the carving on the pillow. He looked at me. And I saw the moment the schoolteacher died in him and something older stood up in its place.

"The vestry," he said. "Now. While they think we're broken."

Downstairs, the phone was ringing. It rang and rang, patient as the road, and neither of us answered it, and neither of us could have said — not honestly — whether it was ringing in the house or in the tower, or whether there had ever been any difference between the two.

The iron key was already in his fist. The bronze was already against my heart.

Above the fog, faint and terrible and *premature*, the melted bell rang once — a trial note, a tuning, a throat clearing — and somewhere beneath the church, twenty-one voices and one new small voice began, softly, to sing.
