## 14. The Village That Watches

Senna had learned, in the four days since Ivy vanished from her bed, that grief has a texture. It is not soft. It is not the gray wool of mourning she'd imagined as a girl reading novels by lamplight. It is a wire drawn thin and taut through the center of the body, and every hour it is pulled a little tighter, until breathing becomes a negotiation and sleep becomes an insult.

She did not cry in front of the villagers. That was the first discipline.

The second was this: she stopped pretending she didn't understand what Cold Ash was.

Mirabel had gone quiet in the way he went quiet before a proof — pacing the cottage, muttering dates, pulling books off shelves like a man dismantling a wall brick by brick to find the thing behind it. He believed there was a mechanism under the village, something with gears and history and a logic that could be exposed. Senna loved him for it. But she had sat across dinner tables from these people. She had watched Mrs. Tarnow smile at Ivy over the school fence. She had seen the bronze buttons catch candlelight at Edmund Vasey-Hale's supper while everyone toasted her husband's arrival with the precision of a rehearsal.

She understood something Mirabel was still working toward: mechanisms could be reasoned with. Villages like this could not. They were not machines. They were congregations.

And congregations had to be organized against.

* * *

There were three families in Cold Ash who had no founding name on their deeds. Senna had counted them the way you count exits in a burning building.

The Pratts kept the bakery on Fen Lane — Harold Pratt, broad and slow-spoken, and his wife Nell, who had once, in Senna's first week, pressed a loaf into her hands and said, too quietly, *You seem like proper folk.* The other family was newer: the Okonkwos, who had come two years ago when Daniel took work repairing drystone walls, and whose daughter Ada still played with village children in the lane without any of the founders' families objecting — because, Senna now suspected, Ada was not yet old enough to matter to the count.

Twenty-two years between children. Twenty-one names carved beneath the school piano. And Ivy Halloran, eight years old, taken four days ago, her name half-finished in someone else's knife-work.

Senna went to the bakery first, in the blue hour before dawn, when even the watchers needed bread and the lanes stood empty of faces.

Harold opened the door before she knocked twice. He looked at her the way men look at weather they've been dreading.

"Mrs. Halloran."

"You know why I'm here."

"I know why you're here." He didn't move from the doorway. Behind him, the ovens glowed, and Nell stood in the flour-dusted half-dark with her arms crossed, watching Senna with an expression that was not unkind and was not brave either. "I'm sorry about your girl. Truly. But I've got a son, Mrs. Halloran. Tom. He's eleven."

"Which is exactly why," Senna said, keeping her voice level, low, pitched under the sound of the ovens, "you should be standing with us instead of behind your door. They took my daughter because we're strangers. Do you think your name on a lease protects him forever? Do you think they'll stop at twenty-two?"

Harold's jaw worked. For one moment — one long moment in which the ovens ticked and somewhere down the lane a dog began to howl and then thought better of it — Senna saw the man underneath the caution. She saw a father doing arithmetic.

Then Nell touched his arm, barely, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

"We don't know anything," Harold said. "We never have. We bake bread. We keep our heads down. That's how you live in a place like this."

"That's how you die in a place like this," Senna said. "Slowly, and agreeing to it."

She left the loaf she'd brought — a small absurd courtesy, a counter-gesture — on the doorstep, and walked away without looking back. Looking back would have meant seeing whether the door closed, and she couldn't afford to know.

* * *

The Okonkwos were different. The Okonkwos were angry.

Daniel met her at the gate of his cottage on the hill road, where the fog came up off the valley in ropes. He was a big man gone lean with worry, and he didn't waste words.

"My wife hasn't slept since the girl went missing. Ada keeps asking where her friend is. What do I tell her, Mrs. Halloran? That the village ate her?" He said it flatly, testing the word the way you test ice. *Ate.*

"That's close enough to true," Senna said.

"Then tell me the rest of it. All of it. My grandmother told me stories about places like this. I used to think they were stories."

So Senna told him — the bell melted in 1911, the twenty-two-year count, the buttons, the constable's paperwork proving a living boy had never existed, the road that looped back on itself when they'd tried to flee with Ivy. She told it plainly, without ornament, because ornament was for people who had time to be believed gently.

Daniel listened with his arms folded and his eyes moving along the hedgerow, checking, always checking, for listeners.

"The road," he said at last. "You drove out and came back in without turning around."

"Three times. Mirabel counted the milestones. The same milestone, the same crack in it, the same dead crow."

"Then we don't leave by the road." He said it simply, and Senna felt something in her chest unlock — not hope exactly, but its skeleton. A plan-shaped absence where hope should go. "My cousin hauls timber out of the quarry past Marlow. There's a track. No milestones. If the village holds the roads, we find what the village doesn't hold."

"When?"

"Soon. Tonight if your husband agrees. But—" and here Daniel's voice dropped, and the fog seemed to lean in around them, "—there's a third family. The Pratts. Are they with us?"

"They're afraid."

"Everyone's afraid. Afraid isn't a side." He rubbed his jaw. "I'll speak to Harold myself. Man to man. Sometimes it lands different coming from another outsider."

They agreed on dusk, at the cottage, all of them, and Senna walked home through the fog feeling, for the first time since Ivy's bed had held nothing but a half-finished carving, that she was walking toward something instead of away.

It lasted eleven minutes. That was how long it took her to reach the market square, and see the constable's boy nailing a notice to the board outside the shop, and read it.

*A public meeting of the parish will convene Friday evening in the church hall to discuss matters of communal welfare. Attendance of all resident households is expected and appreciated.*

*Expected and appreciated.* The founders' language. The supper-toast language. Someone had reported the movement on the hill road already — or anticipated it. With Cold Ash, Senna was learning, anticipation and reporting were the same act. The village watched itself so faithfully that watching became prophecy.

Under the notice, in smaller print, almost an afterthought:

*The parish thanks all households for their continued generosity.*

* * *

Mirabel didn't argue with the plan. That frightened her more than argument would have. He listened to Daniel's quarry track, nodded once, and said, "We need Ambrose Pell's papers first. Whatever he kept. Evidence doesn't save Ivy, but it saves whoever comes after her, and I can't move without knowing the shape of the thing below." His hands shook as he said it. He hadn't slept either. He looked like a man carrying a beam on his shoulder, and Senna recognized the weight: guilt, dressed up as scholarship.

"She's alive," Senna said. Not a comfort. An instruction. "Whatever's down there wanted twenty-two. It doesn't spend what it counts. She's alive, Mirabel, and every hour we spend being careful is an hour she spends alone in the dark."

He flinched as if she'd struck him. Then he kissed her forehead, which was worse, and went to the sexton's cottage.

Dusk came. Daniel arrived with his wife, Grace, a small fierce woman who had spent the day, she announced, packing. Harold Pratt did not come. At seven, Daniel walked down to the bakery to fetch him, and Senna waited in the cottage with Grace, listening to the fog press against the windows like something reading over their shoulders.

At quarter past seven, the dog started again. Down the hill. Then silence.

At half past, Daniel came back alone.

His face told her before his mouth did. He stood in the doorway dripping fog, and he said, "Harold won't come. Harold says—" and stopped, and tried again. "Harold was very polite. He asked after our health. He asked whether we'd found the village satisfactory." Daniel's voice cracked on the word. "And there was a button on his coat, Mrs. Halloran. Bronze. Small. Like the ones at the squire's supper. And Nell was smiling at me from behind him. Smiling like—" He made a gesture near his own face, helpless. "Like it wasn't hers anymore."

Grace made a sound low in her throat and turned away to the window.

"They got to them today," Daniel said. "While we were planning. While we thought we had time." He laughed, short and terrible. "We had no time. We never had time. This place doesn't wait for you to decide. It decides for you and lets you call it deciding."

Senna sat down at the kitchen table and put her palms flat on the wood, because if she didn't give her hands a job they would start shaking and she could not afford shaking. Not now. Not in front of these people who were looking at her to learn whether to despair.

"Then we go tonight," she said. "Without the Pratts. Without anyone. Us and whatever Mirabel finds in Ambrose's papers."

"And the quarry track might loop too," Daniel said. "You know that. Whatever holds the road might hold the track."

"It might." She lifted her head. "But here's what I know that the village doesn't want me to know, Mr. Okonkwo. It needed a button on Harold Pratt's coat. It needed a smiling Nell. It needed a notice nailed to a board. That's not omnipotence. That's a machine working hard. Machines that work hard are machines that can break." She heard her own voice come out flat and cold and was distantly surprised by it; it sounded like her mother's voice, from a woman she'd sworn she'd never become. "They took my daughter to feed something. Fine. Then everything they love is food too. Their prosperity. Their pretty church. Their hundred years of being comfortable while children went into the ground. I am going to take it apart, Daniel. Piece by piece, if that's what it costs. Starting tonight."

Nobody spoke. The fog ticked against the glass. And then Grace Okonkwo turned from the window and said, in a voice like a struck match, "I'll get the rest of the bags."

* * *

They were loading the cart when the singing started.

It came from the direction of the church — thin at first, threading through the fog, and then gathering voices the way a river gathers rain. Hymn-singing, or something shaped like hymn-singing, rising from the church hall where the Friday meeting had been moved up, moved forward, convened early, because of course it had. The village knew. The village had always known, one step ahead, generous and punctual and watching.

Figures emerged along the lane as the cart creaked toward the quarry road. Not blocking it. Never blocking it — that would be crude, and Cold Ash was never crude. Just standing. Mrs. Tarnow outside the schoolhouse with her keys in her hand. The constable outside the shop, hatless, unhurried. Two farmers by the well. And at the corner nearest the churchyard, closest of all, Harold Pratt, holding a lantern, wearing his good coat with the bronze button catching the light, smiling at them with Nell's smile — no. With the smile Nell had worn. The borrowed smile. The congregation's smile.

None of them said a word. That was the horror of it, Senna thought later. Not threats. Silence. The silence of people who are certain, absolutely certain, that the cart will turn around on its own.

Daniel flicked the reins. The horse laid its ears back and pulled.

The lane unwound behind them. The singing swelled and fell behind. The fog swallowed the lanterns one by one, and then the last of them, and then the village, and Senna sat in the cart bed with her arm around Grace and watched the dark pour past and counted in her head — fifty yards, a hundred, five hundred — waiting for the milestone with the crack, the dead crow, the loop.

The track narrowed. Stone walls rose on either side. Somewhere ahead, faintly, the quarry's old cutting gleamed wet in the fog.

And then, out of the fog ahead, walking toward them up the track where no one should be — a mile from any house, on a road the village didn't hold — came a small figure in a nightdress, barefoot on the cold stones, moving with the slow, deliberate gait of a child asleep with her eyes open.

Ada Okonkwo.

Grace screamed her daughter's name and was out of the cart before it stopped, and Senna was right behind her, and the child turned her face up to them in the fog — peaceful, blank-eyed, smiling — and said, in a voice that was Ada's voice and yet arranged by something else, something patient and old and very much awake:

"Mummy. Daddy. You have to come back now. The bell says the counting isn't finished."

Behind them, far off through the fog, the church tower rang once — a single deep note with no bell in it, a note that came up out of the ground itself — and every dog in Cold Ash began to howl.
