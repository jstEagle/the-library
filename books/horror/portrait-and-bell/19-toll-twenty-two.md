## 19. Toll Twenty-Two

**Edmund Vasey-Hale**

The rite began at the hour when night forgets it is supposed to end.

Edmund stood in the churchyard with the founders arrayed behind him — eleven of them, the oldest blood in Cold Ash, each holding a candle that would not gutter no matter how the wind pulled at it. The candles had been lit from a flame kept burning in the vestry since 1911. His father had tended it. His grandfather before him. A hundred and twelve years of fire, waiting for this.

The tower loomed above them, its belfry empty for a century, and yet Edmund could feel it leaning over him like a face. Below, beneath the flagstones, beneath the crypt stair Ambrose Pell had so foolishly unbricked, something was stirring. He could feel it through his boot soles — a slow, tidal pressure, like a heart too large for its body.

"The count is twenty-one," Edmund said, and his voice carried without effort, as though the air itself were listening. "The count is nearly done."

Behind him, the eleven murmured assent. He did not look at their faces. He had not looked at their faces since the night he'd seen what the bell showed him — the true ledger of Cold Ash, every loaf of bread and full purse and healthy child bought with interest paid in small bones. He had wavered. He knew he had wavered. But wavering was a luxury of men whose families were not yet finished, and the Vasey-Hales had been feeding this thing since before there was a village to feed.

*It will keep us,* he told himself. *It has always kept us.*

He raised the bronze button — the last fragment of the original Bell of Saint Aldhelm, pried from the clapper's secret casting, warm as a living thing against his palm. Above him, in the empty belfry, the melted bell began to gather itself into sound.

**Mirabel**

Mirabel heard the tolling begin as he ran.

Not ringing — tolling. There was a difference, and his body understood it before his mind did. A ring is struck and dies. This rose and rose, one note swelling through the stone under his feet, through the walls of the passage, through his ribs and teeth, a note that had been building for a hundred years and did not intend to stop.

Twenty-one children stood around the suspended mass of bronze, unmoving, their faces slack as unfinished portraits. Ivy stood among them. Her eyes were open but nothing lived behind them; her name was half-carved somewhere on her, he knew it, felt it like a wound in the air between them.

*Ivy.*

She didn't turn. None of them turned. They were being counted. He could hear it now, inside the tolling — not a bell at all, Ambrose had told him, a throat, and throats do not ring, they *recite*. It was reciting them. Name by name by name, a hundred years of names spoken in voices he recognized: the boy from the empty desk, the girl from the ledger page Mrs. Tarnow had burned, all of them, all of them, and then —

*Ivy Halloran.*

The note swelled toward completion. Somewhere above, Edmund Vasey-Hale was raising his hands. Somewhere above, the village that watched was watching.

Mirabel took out the fragment Senna had pressed into his hand at the mouth of the stair — the piece of true bronze, the original metal, the one honest thing in a village built on a lie. It was heavy past its size. It hummed against his fingers, humming the same note, because it remembered what it had been.

"You were a bell," he said to it. "You rang for weddings. You rang for the dead. You never asked for children."

And he smashed it down against the iron edge of the crypt stair.

**Senna**

Above, in the churchyard, Senna stood at the lychgate with the two remaining allies who hadn't been turned, and she saw the moment it broke.

There was no explosion. That was the terrible beauty of it. The tolling simply *stopped* — cut off mid-swell, mid-name, like a singer strangled — and the silence that followed was so total that every dog in Cold Ash sat down at once, and every candle in the founders' hands went out together, eleven small deaths of flame.

Then the tower split.

Not collapsed — split. A seam opened down its whole height, hairline first, then widening, and through the seam came light. Not firelight. Not moonlight. A pale, ordinary, morning-colored light, though dawn was still an hour away, pouring out of the crack in the church as if the building had been holding its breath for a century and had finally, finally exhaled.

The founders began to scream. Senna found she could not pity them.

**Ivy**

Ivy woke the way you wake from a fever — all at once, gasping, in a place that smelled of earth and old metal.

Around her, the other children were waking too. Some of them were small. Some of them wore clothes eighty years out of fashion. One boy in a sailor suit looked at his own hands as if he'd forgotten they belonged to him, and then he began to cry, and the crying was the most human sound Ivy had ever heard, and she joined it without shame.

They climbed. That was all any of them could do — climb, up the bone-lined passage, up the broken stair, toward the seam of light splitting the tower above them. Hands reached down to help them. Her mother's hands. Strangers' hands. The whole village seemed to be there, pressed against the churchyard wall, watching the dead century walk out of the ground.

Her father came last, gray-faced, supporting old Ambrose Pell's body — Ambrose, who had died somewhere in the dark below, she would learn later, died smiling, his burden passed at last. Mirabel's eyes found hers across the rubble and neither of them spoke. There would be time. There would be, God willing, time.

But Ivy noticed what her mother did not, what none of them noticed, in that first wild hour of light and weeping:

Her father did not step back from the broken tower. He stood beside it, one hand resting on the split stone, the way a man rests his hand on the shoulder of a friend who is sleeping. And where his palm touched the rock, the stone was cold, and so, faintly, was he.

**Edmund**

Edmund Vasey-Hale knelt in the ruins of his family's work and understood, at last, what prosperity had cost.

The bell was dying — or leaving, or simply ceasing, the way a debt ceases when the debtor is devoured. He could feel it draining out of the ground beneath him, out of the fields, out of the ledgers, out of the eleven kneeling figures behind him who had fed it their own children and called it stewardship. What remained of Cold Ash's fortune was already rotting; he could smell it, sweet and grave-like, rising from the cracked earth.

He should have run. Some part of him, the part that had wavered, the part that had seen the true ledger, wanted to run.

Instead he looked up at Mirabel Halloran standing over him, and he smiled with what was left of his face.

"It isn't finished," he said. "Nothing is ever finished. Ask your father-in-the-dark what he's agreed to keep."

And the tower groaned, and settled, and held.
