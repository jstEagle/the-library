## 5. What Was Invited In

Marguerite hadn't slept. Elodie could tell by the way she stood in the kitchen doorway at six in the morning, still dressed in yesterday's clothes, her hands wrapped around a teacup she never drank from. The tape lay on the table between them like something dead.

"Play it again," she said.

"I've played it four times."

"Play it again."

He did. His own voice, thick with sleep, asking: *What do you want from me?* And then the second voice, low and patient as water finding a crack: *Only what was promised. Only what was always yours to give.*

Marguerite set the cup down. Her hand was shaking, but her voice, when it came, was level—the voice of a woman who had rehearsed this confession for decades and hoped she'd never have to give it.

"My grandfather built this house in 1929," she said. "The year the banks failed. Everyone else in the county went under. The Verrins didn't. Do you know why?"

Elodie said nothing. He had learned, in this house, that silence pulled truth out of people the way cold pulled warmth.

"There was a man who came to the door during the worst of it," Marguerite continued. "No one knows from where. My grandfather called him *the tenant*, though he never signed a lease, never paid rent. He offered a bargain. The fortune would survive—would grow—if the family gave him a place to live. Not the house." She looked, finally, at the ceiling. It seemed to cost her something enormous. "The *house*. The walls themselves. And in exchange, each heir would feed him."

"Feed him what?"

"Secrets. Spoken aloud, inside these walls. Things no one else knew. My grandfather gave him the location of every debt he owed and every man he'd cheated. My father gave him—" she stopped, swallowed. "My father gave him my mother's letters. The ones she wrote before she left. He read them into the air of the east bedroom until there was nothing left of her that wasn't in this house."

Elodie thought of the handprints. Small. Human. Pressed high on the walls where no child could reach.

"And when an heir refuses to feed it?"

"It starves. Slowly. It gets... thin. Angry." Her eyes came up to his, and he saw that behind the fear was something older and more exhausted. "I haven't spoken to it in thirty years. That's why the house is dying around me. That's why the dust is so thick. It's been starving since 1994."

"But the handprints are fresh."

"Yes." She said it simply, without excuse. "Because you started talking to it the night you arrived. You narrate everything, Mr. Voss. I've watched you do it. You speak your findings aloud like prayers. You've been feeding it for two weeks."

The word landed wrong. He almost missed it—almost let it pass the way you let a stranger's mispronunciation slide—but he was a documentarian down to the bone, and documentation meant noticing.

"You said Verrin," he said slowly. "Verrin House. But you hired me through the agency under a different name. The work order said Voss."

Marguerite went very still.

"The deed," he pressed. "I checked the county records the second night, standard practice, verifying ownership. The original grant is dated 1929. Signed by Aldous Verrin." He turned his phone toward her, the scanned document glowing between them. "But look at the witness line."

She didn't look. She already knew.

"Witnessed by Tobias Voss," Elodie said. "My great-grandfather's name. The same name as my father. The same name as mine." His voice stayed flat, professional, because that was the only way he could hold it together. "Tobias Voss witnessed the contract that invited this thing into the world. And a Voss has been coming back ever since, hasn't he? Every generation. Some cousin, some branch of the family, drawn here for one reason or another. A repairman. A surveyor. A pest controller."

"Mr. Voss—"

"Elodie." He heard his own name leave his mouth and hated how small it sounded. "You didn't hire me to kill rodents. You hired me because the letter came, didn't it? The same way it comes every generation. You just answered it faster than your grandfather did."

Marguerite's composure cracked—not all at once, but in pieces, like ice giving way. "It doesn't send letters," she whispered. "It sends *names*. Someone in the family dreams a name, wakes up knowing it, and within a year that person walks through the front door. My father dreamed 'Tobias' three months before a Voss surveyor showed up to map the property line. I dreamed yours eleven years ago. I was twenty-six. I woke up saying *Elodie* out loud and I didn't know who it belonged to." She pressed both hands flat against the table as if steadying herself against weather. "When your application crossed my desk, I recognized you before I read a single word. You have his hands. Tobias's hands. I've seen the photographs."

Elodie stood up. His chair scraped back and the sound ran through the kitchen and up through the walls and somewhere above them, faint as breath, something shifted its weight.

He walked to the window. The morning outside was gray and ordinary, the gravel drive pale with frost, the world going about its business as if houses like this were not possible. He wanted to walk out the front door and keep walking until the house was a story he told at parties. But he had tried the road once already, in his mind, and some animal part of him knew it would loop him back the way a dream loops you back to the room you're trying to leave.

"Say it plainly," he said. "No more inheritance language. No more *tenant*. What am I to this house?"

Marguerite took a long time answering. When she did, her voice was gentle in a way that frightened him more than anything the recordings had produced.

"You're the payment that's been accruing for ninety years. Every generation, the tenant takes a little—a secret here, a memory there—and the balance grows. And every generation, a Voss arrives to settle it." She rose, came toward him, stopped an arm's length away. Close enough that he could see she hadn't slept, close enough that he could see she was afraid *for* him, which was somehow worse than being afraid of the house. "Aldous wrote it into the grant itself. I found the clause when I was nineteen. *And the line of the witness shall return to render unto the house what the house is owed.*"

"That's not a clause. That's a summons."

"It's both. It was always both."

Above them, the ceiling creaked—not the settling of old timber, but deliberate, patient, the sound of something turning over in its sleep and beginning to wake. Elodie felt the house listening to them, drinking every word, and understood with terrible clarity that this conversation, this confession, was itself a meal. They were feeding it right now. Marguerite knew it. She was telling him anyway, because he needed to know, because some part of her had decided eleven years ago, when she woke speaking his name, that when he finally came she would not lie to him.

"Why tell me?" he asked. "If it hears everything—why hand it this too?"

"Because you can't fight what you don't understand." Her hand rose, hesitated, and settled on his sleeve—light as dust settling. "And because I've spent thirty years starving it alone, and I'm tired, Elodie. I'm so tired. And now that you're here, it isn't starving anymore. It's *growing*. Can't you feel it? The house is fuller than it's been since my grandfather's day."

He could feel it. He'd been feeling it for days without naming it—the sense that the rooms had deepened, that the hallways breathed, that the dark in the corners had texture and appetite. He had documented it all in his neat, liturgical handwriting. He had fed it his observations like communion wafers.

Twenty-two nights remained on the contract. He wondered, suddenly, whether the number mattered to the house at all, or whether the contract was just another ritual the family had invented to make the summons bearable.

"The east bedroom," he said. "That's where it lives. That's where the ledgers are."

Marguerite's face went white. "How do you know about ledgers?"

He didn't answer, because he couldn't—because the knowledge had arrived in his head sometime in the last week, quiet as dust, and he could no longer tell which thoughts were his.

Behind them, in the sealed room above, something began—very softly—to hum.

* * *

Marguerite waited until he'd gone to fetch his recorder, then stood alone in the kitchen with her grandfather's sin pressing down through the floorboards.

She had known this day would come since she was nine years old, the first time she'd seen the tenant's shadow move against the wrong wall. She had made her peace with it the way you make peace with winter—it comes, you endure it, you do not pretend it won't. But she had not made peace with *him*. With the specific fact of Elodie Voss, with the careful way he catalogued the world, with the way he'd said his own name last night in his sleep like a question and the house had answered like a lover.

Her grandfather had believed he was making a deal. Her father had believed he was honoring one. She alone had understood the truth: there was no deal. There was only hunger, and generations of people convincing themselves they'd agreed to feed it.

She looked up at the ceiling, at the sealed door beyond it, and spoke—for the first time in thirty years—directly to the thing in the walls.

"He's not yours," she said. "Whatever you think you were promised. Whatever Aldous signed. He's not yours."

The humming stopped.

In the silence that followed, Marguerite felt the whole house turn its attention toward her, vast and patient and amused, and she understood that she had just done two things at once: she had declared war, and she had shown it exactly where she was weakest.

From the east bedroom, in a voice like her grandfather's, like her father's, like a hundred Vosses layered over ninety years, the house answered:

*"Then we will discuss terms."*
