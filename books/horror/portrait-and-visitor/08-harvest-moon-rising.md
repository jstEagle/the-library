## 8. Harvest Moon Rising

The knock came at dusk, three polite raps, the kind of knock that expected to be answered.

Theo had spent the afternoon barricading in the way a person barricades when they've mostly read about it in books: kitchen chairs under the doorknob, Wren's heaviest dictionary against the back door, the piano—her grandmother's upright, which she'd never once heard played—dragged two feet across the parlor floor to block the front windows. It was not a fortress. It was a suggestion of one. But the work of it had steadied her hands, and there was a certain dignity in stacking furniture against the inevitable.

Through the window she watched them come up the drive.

Marguerite first, in her good coat, the green one with the fox-fur collar that Theo had always privately thought looked like the fox was still deciding. Behind her, the neighbors. Mr. Aldous Prew from the feed store. The Hollis sisters, Dot and Fern, who between them ran the church's flower fund and, apparently, everything else. Young Caleb Marsh, who bagged groceries and had once, memorably, flirted with Wren over a display of honeycrisps. They carried things: lanterns, folded cloth, a wicker basket that clinked. Nobody carried a casserole. That was how Theo knew this wasn't a wake.

She opened the door anyway, because Marguerite knocked a fourth time, and because some part of her—the part that had been raised on this porch, eating peach slices over the sink—still believed the door should be answered.

"Theo." Marguerite's voice was gentle. Her eyes were not. "You shouldn't have locked the doors, dear."

"I didn't lock them," Theo said. "I blocked them. There's a difference. Locking implies I think you'd respect a lock."

Dot Hollis made a small sound that might have been a laugh, quickly strangled by Fern's elbow.

Marguerite sighed the sigh of a woman who has explained fractions to a stubborn child for the hundredth time. "May we come in? It's nearly moonrise, and I would rather we did what must be done indoors, with tea, like civilized people."

"Like civilized people," Theo repeated. "That's very funny, Aunt Marguerite. Did you write that down somewhere? Is there a ledger entry for it?"

Something flickered across the old woman's face—not guilt, exactly. Guilt would have been easier. It was more like recognition, the look of someone seeing their own reflection in a window they'd hoped was clean.

"We're not here to hurt you," Marguerite said.

"No," Theo said. "You're here to make sure something else does. There's a difference there too. I've been learning all the differences lately."

* * *

They came in because Theo let them, which she would later tell herself was strategy and knew, in the honest hour of her heart, was loneliness. Six neighbors filing into her grandmother's parlor, arranging themselves on furniture as though they'd done it a hundred times—as though they had—and the house settling around them with a sound almost like satisfaction.

Caleb Marsh wouldn't meet her eyes. He kept adjusting his collar like it was strangling him, which Theo supposed, morally speaking, it was.

"You were at my sister's funeral," Theo said to him. "You cried."

"I did," Caleb said quietly. "It doesn't mean what you want it to mean."

"It means you're human."

"It means I'm sorry." He finally looked at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed and miserable. "Those are different things, Theo. In this town they've been different things for two hundred years."

Fern Hollis began laying out cloth on the dining table—white linen, old, soft as moth wings. Dot set the basket down and unpacked candles, matches, a small clay dish of dark soil that smelled, even from across the room, of the orchard. Of apples gone sweet past eating. Of something underneath the sweetness, patient as roots.

"Where is the chair?" Marguerite asked.

Theo's stomach dropped through the floorboards. "What chair?"

"The chair, Theo. Don't make this harder than it is."

"I don't know what—" But she did know. She'd known since the photographs, since the empty seat facing the camera in every frame like an audience member waiting for the show to start. And she knew where it would be before she walked into the dining room and saw it.

At the head of the table.

Her grandmother's chair—the one with the worn needlepoint seat, the one Grandma Crane had occupied every morning of Theo's childhood while she drank coffee and read obituaries like other people read horoscopes—had moved. It sat at the head now, turned slightly, angled toward the doorway. Waiting to watch someone enter.

And it was warm. Theo touched the needlepoint before she could stop herself, and the fabric held heat the way a lap does, the way a body does, recently risen or never yet arrived.

"Oh," she said. Very calm. Very documentary. *Note: the furniture has opinions about seating arrangements.* "Great."

"Dear," Marguerite said from the doorway, "it's time you understood what happens tonight."

"I understand plenty." Theo rounded on her. "I found the ledger, Aunt Marguerite. Church basement, third shelf, behind the hymnals nobody liked. Every generation. Every Crane. Grandmother's name in ink so fresh it was still bleeding when I touched it. Wren's name twice—once crossed out, once new. Dated tomorrow. So no, I don't need the orientation tour. What I'd like to know is why you're all standing in my grandmother's dining room helping it happen."

"Because it keeps us safe," said Dot Hollis simply, and the terrible thing was that she said it kindly, the way you'd explain rain to a child. "Because it kept my mother safe, and hers. Because the year the Cranes tried to refuse—"

"1974," said Fern.

"—the orchard took the Prews' youngest instead," Dot finished. "And the year after that, and the year after that, until the debt was honored. It isn't cruelty, Theo. It's bookkeeping."

"Bookkeeping." Theo laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "You're feeding your town to an orchard and calling it *bookkeeping*."

"We're feeding it what it's owed," Marguerite said. "There's a difference."

"There you go again with the differences."

* * *

They sealed the doors while the moon climbed.

Theo fought, briefly—she got as far as the kitchen with a carving knife before Caleb caught her wrist, gently, apologetically, and Dot took the knife away like a mother confiscating scissors. Then Marguerite did something with chalk and a phrase in a language that predated the church, the county, possibly the English language, drawing lines across every threshold, and the lines sank into the wood like water into dry ground, and Theo felt the house close around her like a fist made of wallpaper and memory.

"Not to keep you in," Marguerite said, not quite meeting her eyes. "To keep the night orderly. The ritual wants a container."

"The ritual wants a *Crane*."

"Yes," Marguerite admitted. "Both things are true."

They lit the candles. They set the table—two settings, then, at Fern's quiet instruction, a third at the head, at the warm chair. Bread. Salt. A cup of cider pressed, Dot said, from the orchard's own trees, and Theo thought: *poison, obviously,* and then thought: *no, worse, hospitality.*

The moon rose fat and amber over the orchard, and through the dining room window Theo could see the trees standing in its light, and she could swear—she documented it, later, in the margins of Wren's journal—she could swear the branches leaned toward the house. Not wind. Inclination.

Her camera sat on the sideboard where she'd left it. She picked it up because picking it up was breathing, was proof, was the only prayer she'd ever learned. She photographed the table. The candles. The chair.

The film whirred forward. She advanced to the developed roll she'd tucked in her coat pocket—the one from last night, the parlor, the extra chair facing the camera—and looked.

In the photograph, the chair was not empty.

She was sitting in it. Herself, hands folded, head tilted, smiling with a smile that used all her teeth and none of her warmth. The image was grainy but unmistakable, and it was dated—her own handwriting on the border, because apparently the photograph had decided to document itself—tomorrow's date.

Behind her, in the photo, the whole town stood in the parlor doorway. Marguerite. The Hollis sisters. Caleb. All smiling too.

"Stop looking at those," Marguerite said sharply.

"Why?" Theo's voice shook. "Afraid I'll feel real?"

"Afraid you'll feel *chosen*," Marguerite said, and then pressed her hand to her mouth, too late, the word already out in the candlelit air between them.

And God help her, Theo did feel it—a hot, shameful flicker low in her chest, the same flicker she'd felt at dinner two nights ago when the second place setting appeared, the awful animal comfort of being wanted, of mattering enough to be collected. Two hundred years of Cranes had felt it too. She could see it now, generations deep: not just fear. Flattery. The house never gave belonging. It collected people, and being collected felt, from the inside, almost exactly like being loved.

"That's how it gets you," Theo whispered. "That's how it got all of you. Not the fear. The *wanting*."

Nobody denied it. Caleb looked at the floor. Dot smoothed her apron. Fern blew out a match that had already gone out.

Outside, the moon cleared the treetops, and every candle in the house stood straight up, flames motionless, as if listening.

"It's time," Marguerite said softly. "The orchard will open for you, Theo. The doors will let you out because the doors want you to walk to it. You should know that whatever choice you make in the next hour, the walking itself is not freedom."

"I know," Theo said. She set down the camera. She picked up Wren's journal instead, and the ledger page she'd torn free, and the box of matches from beside the salt.

Marguerite's eyes went to the matches, and for the first time all evening, the old woman looked afraid.

"What are you going to do?"

Theo smiled. It was not the smile from the photograph.

"What the founders never had the nerve to," she said. "I'm going to settle the account."

She walked toward the back door, and the chalk lines parted for her like a curtain, and the warm chair at the head of the table creaked—just slightly, just once—like something leaning forward in its seat.
