## 7. Knocking

The concrete was supposed to end it.

Dara Okafor had watched them pour it herself—four yards of ready-mix, troweled smooth around the door's stone frame like a cast around a broken bone, the city engineer standing there with his clipboard and his satisfied little nod, as if a problem forty years in the ground could be fixed in an afternoon. The door was still visible, framed by the concrete like a specimen under glass, but sealed now. Nothing was getting out of that cellar. Nothing was getting in.

Three days later, the door had opened a hand's width.

Not outward. Inward. Into the dark. As though the concrete had never been poured at all, as though the wood had simply decided the wall around it was a suggestion. Dara stood at the lip of the excavation in the gray of morning and stared down at the gap along the door's edge, at the black seam of it, and felt her professional skepticism—the thing she'd built her whole career on, the thing that had carried her from job site to job site for twenty years—turn over and die quietly in her chest.

She measured it. That was the only thing she could think to do. She measured it because measuring was what she knew, because numbers had never lied to her, because a gap was a gap and a door was a door and none of it required her to believe in anything.

Two inches. She wrote it in her logbook. She came back the next morning and measured again.

Three and a quarter.

* * *

Her crew walked off on the fourth day.

It happened the way these things happen—not all at once but in a slow bleed, one man at a time, each of them finding a reason that sounded reasonable in the daylight. Marcus had a cousin in Fairview who needed help moving. Old Tom's knee was acting up again, and his doctor said no more heavy work. The youngest one, Danny, didn't even pretend. He came to her at the start of the shift with his hard hat in his hands like a man holding his own severed head, and he said, "Ms. Okafor, I'm sorry, I can't. I heard it again last night. I wasn't even on site, I was at my mom's, and I heard it. Knocking. Under everything. Like it was coming up through the floor of the whole town."

"Knocking," Dara said.

"Three knocks. Then a pause. Then three more." His voice dropped. "It's counting something. I don't know what. I just know I'm not the one it's counting."

She didn't argue. She'd learned a long time ago that you couldn't pay a man enough to stay somewhere his body had already fled from. She signed his time sheet, shook his hand, and watched him drive out of Hollow Creek with his taillights shrinking down Route 9 like a wound closing.

By Friday she was alone.

The overtime money sat in her head like a stone. Two more weeks of hazard-rate pay and the back rent was gone, the lights stayed on, Marcus's braces got paid for, and her family stayed housed through the winter. That was the arithmetic she'd been doing since the door came out of the hillside. That was the arithmetic she was still doing now, standing alone over an open grave the size of a municipal well, telling herself that a woman with a measuring tape had nothing to fear from a door that opened itself.

But that night, instead of going home, she went to the hardware store and bought a recorder. A good one, the kind birdwatchers used, with a long battery and a sensitive microphone. And a folding chair. And a thermos of coffee strong enough to strip paint.

If the town wouldn't listen, she'd listen herself.

* * *

The first night, she heard nothing but wind and the far-off complaint of a train. She almost felt foolish, sitting at the edge of the excavation in her folding chair with her recorder running, a lone woman guarding a hole in the ground like some kind of secular sentinel. She almost went home.

Then, at 2:14 in the morning, the knocking started.

Not from the door. That was the thing she would try and fail to explain later, to Gideon, to anyone who would listen. The knocking didn't come from the door. It came from everywhere underneath—out of the soil itself, out of the rock, out of the deep water table below the rock—three knocks, a pause, three more, patient as a heartbeat, as if the whole hillside were a chest and something inside it was asking, politely, endlessly, to be let out.

She didn't run. She wanted to. Every nerve in her body voted to run. But she sat there with her hands locked on the armrests of the folding chair and she listened, because somewhere in the last week she had crossed a line she couldn't name, and on the far side of it, listening felt like the only honest work left to do.

The knocking stopped at dawn. She played the recording back and the knocking was there, clean and distinct, and underneath it—she had to turn the volume all the way up, press the headphones to her ears, hold her breath—voices. Faint. Layered. Not words yet. Just the shape of words, the way a crowd sounds from three streets away.

She logged it. Two inches, three and a quarter, knocking at 2:14. Then she went home and slept four hours and came back and did it again.

* * *

On the third night alone, the door opened four more inches, and the voices found their words.

She was half-asleep in the folding chair when the chorus started, and it woke her the way a hand on the shoulder wakes you—not with sound but with presence, with the sudden certainty that she was being addressed. She fumbled for the headphones, got them on, and the voices poured in, and this time they weren't a crowd. They were one voice made of many, the way a chord is one sound made of notes, and it was reciting something. A list.

"Voss," it said. "Petrie. Halloran. Voss. Mirelle. Okafor—"

She ripped the headphones off.

Her own name. Her own name in a voice made of eleven voices, spoken out of the ground beneath a door that was opening itself an inch at a time, and her first thought—her shameful, human, animal first thought—was *not me, I'm not one of them, I only dug the hole, I only did my job.*

She sat in the dark with her heart going like a pile driver and she was ashamed of the thought. Because she'd spent the week listening to these dead people knock, and somewhere in the listening she had stopped hearing them as a hazard, a liability, a reason to walk off the job like Marcus and Tom and Danny. She'd started hearing them as a crew. Eleven workers sealed in a cellar by their neighbors, left to scratch at a door from the inside while the town above them ate their food and took their land and wrote them out of the census. She knew crews. She knew what it was to be the one who stayed when everyone else left. And she knew, with a foreman's certainty, that the knocking wasn't a threat.

It was a time sheet. Three knocks, a pause, three more. They were clocking in. Every night. They had been clocking in for forty years, waiting for somebody above ground to notice they were still on the job.

She put the headphones back on.

"—Okafor," the chorus finished, gentle now, almost kind. "Who dug us out. Who stayed. Who listened. The price of survival comes due, Dara. It always comes due. The only question is who pays it."

"The founding families," she said out loud, to the dark, to the door, to whoever was down there. "They should pay it. They took everything."

A pause. Then the chorus, softer: "They will. But you stayed, and staying is a kind of signing. You put your name in the ground, Dara. The ground keeps what's given."

"I didn't give anything."

"You gave your nights," the voices said. "You gave your attention. That's the first payment. There's only one left, and it isn't yours to make alone."

She thought of Gideon then—Gideon with his notebooks, his stacks of transcription, his voice on the radio at midnight reading names back to the dead like a man paying out a rope, hand over hand, not knowing what was on the other end. She thought of what he'd told her on the phone, that voice low and ragged: *They called me because I'd write it down. That's all. That's the whole miracle. I was the only one who would write it down.*

And she thought of Ada Voss, ninety-one years old, in a chair at the care facility, who didn't yet know that everything her family had lost was waiting for her under a hillside with a door on it.

"The last payment," Dara said slowly. "It's Ada's, isn't it. Or it's his."

"Bring them," the chorus said, and the door groaned, and in the beam of her flashlight she watched it swing inward another inch, slow as a jaw, and the black seam of the cellar breathed out air that smelled like winter and old apples and the inside of a held breath. "Bring the one who writes and the one who's owed. The ledger's almost full. We only need one more signature, and the well remembers everything."

The knocking started again—three, a pause, three—but different now, and it took her a long moment to understand what had changed.

They weren't asking to be let out anymore.

They were counting down.
