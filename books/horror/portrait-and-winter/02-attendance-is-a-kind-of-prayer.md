## 2. Attendance Is a Kind of Prayer

I had prepared for my second day at Marrow Fen Academy the way I prepare for most things: with coffee, low expectations, and a laminated sense of professional detachment. The roster sat on my desk in its manila folder, thirty-one names typed in a font that suggested the school's printer had been purchased during the Ford administration. Twenty-eight desks sat below me in neat rows, and I had decided—consciously, deliberately, the way one decides not to look down from a ledge—that this discrepancy was none of my business.

I was a substitute for permanence, a stopgap in a cardigan. Teachers who ask questions at Marrow Fen, I'd already gathered, were like tourists who ask directions in a town that doesn't appear on maps: technically permitted, quietly discouraged, and liable to end with everyone pretending you don't exist.

So I did what invisible men do. I took attendance.

* * *

The classroom at 8:47 a.m. smelled of chalk dust, wet wool, and something faintly mineral underneath it all, like the air itself had been drawn through stone. Frost still clung to the lower panes of the windows even though the radiators knocked and hissed like old men arguing about football. Outside, the fen lay flat and white to the horizon, interrupted only by the black spines of dead alders.

"Good morning," I said. "My name is Mr. Winterbourne. I'll be covering English until—" I consulted the folder, which said *until further notice*, which is the bureaucratic equivalent of *until the ice cracks*. "Until further notice."

Twenty-eight faces looked back at me. Some were curious. Most had the polished blankness of adolescents who have learned that new teachers are weather—briefly interesting, ultimately irrelevant.

I picked up the roster. "When I call your name, say 'here.' If I mispronounce anything, correct me. I have a thick skin and a thin résumé."

A few smiles. Good. Humor is a door; you prop it open early or spend the whole term knocking.

"Aldous, Peter."

"Here." A lanky boy near the window, freckles like a spilled drink.

"Baptiste, Marguerite."

"Here."

I worked down the list alphabetically, marking my own copy with a pencil, keeping a rhythm. It's a mundane liturgy, roll call. You do it enough years and it stops being information-gathering and becomes invocation—you are, in a small way, calling each soul into the room by name, confirming that the world still contains them. I'd never thought of it that way before Marrow Fen. I thought of it that way constantly afterward.

"Carrick, Daniel."

"Here."

"Corrigan, Ilsa."

"Here." The girl in the third row didn't smile at my joke or anyone else's. She had dark hair cut blunt at the jaw and eyes the color of a frozen pond—pretty, in the way that winter landscapes are pretty, which is to say you admire them while suspecting they'd let you die. She watched me the way the others didn't: not curious, but *assessing*. Like she was checking whether I was going to be a problem.

I moved on. "Delacroix, Simone."

"Here."

And then I reached the names.

* * *

The first one was "Fennimore, Tobias."

I said it, pencil poised, and looked up expecting a hand.

No hand. No face. The desk where he should have been—third row, aisle seat, directly in front of a girl with braids—was empty. Not conspicuously empty. Just a desk with a chair pushed in, the way desks are when nobody's sitting in them.

But a voice said, "Here."

It came from the general region of the empty desk. I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last defense of a skeptical mind: the voice was small, male, unremarkable, and it came from approximately three feet in front of the braided girl, at seated height, out of air that contained nothing whatsoever. It wasn't an echo. It wasn't a prank from the hallway—the door was shut, and the acoustics of the room were wrong for it. It was simply a voice, answering roll, from a place where no one was.

The class did not react.

That was the part that got under my skin—not the voice, but the silence around it. Twenty-seven students kept their eyes on their notebooks or on me, with the mild patience of people waiting for a bus. Nobody turned around. Nobody giggled. Nobody did the thing teenagers do, which is *notice everything and pretend not to*, with elbows and eyebrows doing all the talking. There was no talking. There was nothing.

I looked down at my roster. My pencil hovered over Fennimore's name.

Mark him absent, said the rational part of me, the part that had spent eleven years teaching in schools where the supernatural extended no further than the cafeteria's meatloaf. Mark him absent, note the date, mention it to the office, and let the machinery of ordinary administration grind out whatever mundane explanation exists. Kid's in the lavatory. Kid's truant. Kid's a recording, kid's a ventriloquist, kid's—

"Present," said the air, again, as if correcting my hesitation.

I marked him present.

I want to be honest about why, because honesty is the only currency I have left after that winter. I marked him present because twenty-eight children were watching me not with their eyes but with their stillness, and every instinct I possessed as a teacher told me that what happened next depended entirely on what I did in the next four seconds. It was like standing on ice and feeling it speak under your boots. You don't jump. You don't run. You distribute your weight and you pray.

"Greeley, Anne."

"Here."

"Harrow, James."

"Here."

"Hollis, Wren."

And the braided girl—the one who sat behind the empty desk—said, softly, without raising her hand, "She's here," in a tone that was not mockery and not madness but *accommodation*, the tone you use when you hold a door for someone carrying too many books.

I looked at her. She looked at her notebook.

I marked Wren Hollis present.

* * *

By the time I reached the M's, I had developed the peculiar sensation of being the only person in a room who has just been told a secret by accident. My hands were steady—I made a small project of that—but my thoughts were running in circles like a dog looking for an exit.

There were three of them. Three names on the roster that received answers from empty chairs. Fennimore, Hollis, and a boy called Osk Nadeau, whose "here" came out slightly warped, like a tape played at the wrong speed, and who occupied—according to the seating chart I'd been given—a desk beside the radiator that currently held nothing but a forgotten scarf.

Thirty-one names. Twenty-eight desks. Twenty-eight children. And yet when I finished the list and set it down, the arithmetic in the room felt wrong in a way I couldn't fix, like a word you've written so many times it stops looking real. *Spelling.* You stare at it long enough and it dissolves into noise.

"Questions about yesterday's reading?" I said, because the lesson plan Voss's secretary had left me said *Chapter Four, discussion*, and because the fastest way out of a haunted moment is forward motion. "Anyone? The symbolism of the river?"

Silence—and then hands, more hands than I'd ever gotten on a first full day, and the discussion, when it came, was good. Genuinely good. These were sharp kids, better than the ones I'd left behind in the city, and for forty minutes I almost forgot that three of my students were answers without bodies.

Almost. Because twice, during the discussion, I saw something that reassembled the dread in my chest like a man patiently rebuilding a wall.

The first time: Marguerite Baptiste, mid-sentence about the river as passage, glanced sideways at the empty aisle desk—just a flicker, the way you glance at a friend when you've landed a joke—and adjusted her course slightly, as if making room in her argument for someone else's turn.

The second time: the girl with the frozen-pond eyes, Corrigan, wrote something in her notebook. She wrote it without looking down, without pausing, the way you write when your hand knows the words already. Then she turned the page and covered it, and her expression never changed, and she went on taking notes like a model student.

I couldn't read what she'd written. But I noticed that two other students, at different moments, also wrote brief lines in their notebooks while looking at nothing, and I noticed that none of them looked surprised, and I began to understand that I was not witnessing a haunting.

I was witnessing a routine.

* * *

The bell rang at 10:15, and the class rose with the shuffling gracelessness of the young, gathering bags and coats, and the room filled with ordinary noise—laughter, complaints about homework, someone asking if the test would be hard—and I stood at my desk feeling like a man who has walked into a church service halfway through and can't find the hymnal. Everyone knew the words. Everyone but me.

"Mr. Winterbourne."

Corrigan. She had stayed behind, which students do when they need something, except she didn't approach the desk. She stopped a careful four feet away, bag over one shoulder, and looked at me with those pond-water eyes, and said, in a voice pitched exactly for me and no one else:

"Don't mark anyone absent."

"I'm sorry?"

"Whoever isn't there. Don't mark them absent." She said it evenly, without drama, the way you'd tell someone not to touch a hot stove. Not a threat. A kindness, maybe, or the closest thing to kindness the situation allowed. "It matters what the book says. The book remembers."

"The book," I repeated. My voice came out drier than I intended. "You mean the roster."

"I mean whatever writes itself down." Her gaze flicked—to my folder, to the roster inside it, and then, briefly, to the windows, where the frost had begun its slow work on the glass. "You seem like you ask questions. That's fine. Ask them. But don't write anyone down as gone. Please."

"Is Tobias Fennimore—"

"He's here," she said, simply, and the terrible thing was that she believed it, completely, the way you believe in gravity, and some part of me understood that she wasn't lying to me. By whatever accounting this school used, Tobias Fennimore *was* here. He had answered roll. He had, presumably, done the reading.

"Where?" I asked. It came out quieter than I meant it to. "Ilsa. Where is he?"

She held my eyes for a moment, and I saw something move behind hers—not fear exactly, but the exhaustion of someone who has carried a warning a long way and finally found someone worth giving it to.

"That's the wrong question," she said. "Everyone asks where. Ask *when*." And then she was gone, out the door and into the corridor's current of coats and voices, leaving me alone in a classroom with twenty-eight desks, thirty-one names, and a roster that seemed, suddenly, heavier than paper should be.

* * *

I sat down. I did the things a rational man does. I examined the ceiling for speakers and found the ancient vents I'd expected, too corroded to carry anything but dust. I checked the door frame for wires. I replayed the morning in my head, hunting for the trick—the hidden student, the rehearsed prank, the elaborate hazing ritual that boarding schools inflict on new staff like a folk custom. I found nothing. Worse than nothing: I found the memory of the class's stillness, and stillness cannot be faked by twenty-eight teenagers simultaneously. Fear can be faked. Laughter can be prompted. But that smooth, practiced non-reaction? That takes years.

That takes curriculum.

I took out my own notebook—the private one, the one I'd started keeping since the roster, though I hadn't admitted to myself yet that it was a ledger—and wrote the date, and under it: *Three names answered. No bodies. Class unbothered. Corrigan: "don't mark absent."*

Then, because my hand was moving anyway and honesty is a habit once it starts:

*I marked them present.*

I stared at that sentence for a long time. Here is what I could not get past, what cracked the skepticism I'd carried into Marrow Fen like armor plate: it hadn't taken courage to mark them present. It had taken relief. When my pencil touched the paper, the room had exhaled—subtly, collectively, the way a congregation settles when the right response is spoken at the right moment—and I had felt that exhale pass through me like warmth, and some animal part of my brain had registered: *good, you did it correctly, you may now be safe.*

Attendance is a kind of prayer. I'd thought it as a joke that morning. It wasn't a joke. Every school day, in every classroom, teachers call names into the air and wait for the world to confirm them, and at Marrow Fen Academy the world answered—and answered—and the children had learned the responses by heart, and somewhere in the order of service there was a line I hadn't been given yet, a verse that explained why the prayer needed feeding, why the book remembered, why a girl with winter-colored eyes had looked at me like a man walking on ice and told me, gently, please, don't look down.

I closed the notebook. Through the frost on the window, the fen stretched white and silent toward the horizon, and for just a moment—before I blinked, before reason reasserted itself—I would have sworn the frost on the glass wasn't ferning outward in its usual random lace.

It was forming letters. Small ones. Patient ones. As if something on the other side of the glass were practicing its handwriting, getting ready to spell a name.

Possibly mine.
