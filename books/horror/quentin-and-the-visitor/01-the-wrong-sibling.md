## 1. The Wrong Sibling

The taxi wouldn't come past the gate.

Felix noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a song you've known all your life—not the fact of it at first, but the shape of its absence, the silence where the engine should have continued humming up the gravel drive. The driver simply stopped, hands on the wheel, and looked straight ahead at the iron arch with AMBERDALE worked into the scrollwork in letters gone soft with a century of rust, and said, "This is as far as I go, I think," in the tone of a man stating the weather.

"I'll pay for the full fare."

"It's not the fare." He said it kindly. He said it the way people say things when they've decided not to think about them. "It's just—this is as far as I go."

Felix paid him anyway, because arguing felt like a luxury they hadn't earned, and stood in the road with two bags while the taillights shrank and the evening came down around them like a lid. Twelve years away, and the first thing Amberdale Orchard gave them back was a driver who wouldn't cross its threshold. The second thing was the smell: apples, everywhere, sweet and bruised and faintly rotten, though the orchard had been dead for years. Their mother had stopped harvesting the year Quentin—stopped harvesting, that was all. The trees had gone on standing in their long ranks like an audience that had been told to wait.

The house was lit.

Every window, Felix thought, standing on the drive with their bags cutting grooves into their fingers. Every single window, and the light in each one the same warm amber, the same shade their mother had used all their childhood because Quentin was afraid of the dark and white bulbs made the shadows "look like they were thinking." She had never changed the bulbs. Felix had assumed, all these years, that the house would be dim when they came back. That it would be a house in mourning. Instead it blazed like a stage set between acts, like a place waiting for its cast to arrive.

Their mother opened the door before Felix reached it.

That was the third thing. She was small in the frame, smaller than Felix remembered, her hair gone entirely white since the last visit—two years ago? Three?—and she was dressed in black, properly dressed, the good dress with the jet buttons, and she said, "Felix. You look tired. Come in, I've kept your room ready," and the words were so ordinary that they passed through Felix like a knife through water, leaving no wound, only the cold trail of what they didn't say.

No *I'm sorry*. No *he's gone*. No *my darling, my poor darling*. Just the logistics of arrival, delivered with the smoothness of a line rehearsed.

"Mother." Felix set down the bags on the step, an old reflex, keeping hands free. "I came as fast as—"

"I know." She stepped back to let them in. "Supper's at eight. The funeral director comes tomorrow at ten to go over the arrangements. I've made most of the decisions already. I thought you'd want it that way."

And there it was, the first wrongness with weight to it, because Felix did not want it that way, had crossed an ocean and two trains and a driver's refusal precisely so that they could make the decisions, could stand over the coffin, could do the one thing twelve years of absence had made impossible: be the sibling. Be present. Be the one who stayed in the room.

"I'd like to see him," Felix said. "Tonight, if I can."

Their mother's face did something small and fast, a flicker like a candle in a draft, and then it was smooth again, and she was smiling, and the smile was her company smile, the one she'd worn for the bank men and the neighbors and the priest, and Felix thought with a strange floating clarity: *she's been wearing that smile for days. Maybe weeks. Maybe since the phone call.*

"Tomorrow," she said. "You've had a long journey. There's soup."

*There's soup.* Felix followed her into the hall, and the door closed behind them with a sound like a held breath being released, and the house received them.

* * *

It was the hall that made the guilt rise, finally, the way water rises in a well when you've been drawing from it all day without looking down.

Felix had grown up in this house and had spent twelve years not thinking about it, which was a discipline, a practiced amnesia, and now the hall undid the discipline in the space of ten steps. The same wallpaper, faded to the color of weak tea. The same grandfather clock, stopped—no, not stopped, Felix realized, listening—*slowed*, its tick stretched and lazy, like a heart under sedation. The same coat hooks, and on the third hook from the door, the same green wool scarf that Quentin had worn at fifteen, that he'd worn the last winter Felix had lived here, still hanging there, still there, and their mother had dusted around it for twelve years the way you dust around a shrine.

"You kept it," Felix said, and hated how their voice cracked on the second word.

"It's where he left it." Their mother didn't turn around. She was walking toward the kitchen with her small straight back and her rehearsed calm. "Things are where he left them. It's simpler that way."

*Things are where he left them.* Felix stood in the hall with the scarf an arm's length away and did not touch it, because touching it would mean the twelve years were real, and the twelve years were the thing they had come home to atone for, and atonement, they were discovering, begins with touching.

Because that was the truth they had carried in their chest through every airport and every train station, the truth that had made the taxi driver's refusal feel like a verdict: the last time Felix had stood in this hall, they had been nineteen, and Quentin had been sixteen, and Quentin had said, in the kitchen, over the noise of their mother running water, *Come with me. I want to show you something in the cellar,* and Felix had said, *I'm leaving in the morning, Q. I can't spend my last night looking at the cellar,* and had laughed, and had gone upstairs, and had heard—had *heard*, faintly, through the floorboards, Quentin's voice down there below the kitchen, talking, the cadence of it rising and falling like a conversation, and had told themselves it was the radio, had told themselves it was Quentin talking to himself the way he talked to himself, and had packed their bags, and had left in the morning, and had never once in twelve years asked their brother what he'd wanted to show them.

And now there would never be a time to ask, because Quentin was dead, had been dead for nine days when the call came, dead in the old well field or near it, the coroner's letter said, and the letter had used the phrase *recovered from* in a way that Felix had read six times without the sentence getting any clearer.

"Your room's the same," their mother said from the kitchen doorway. "I aired it this morning."

Felix followed her in. The kitchen was warm and bright and the table was set for two, and the soup was already ladled into bowls, already steaming, and Felix understood with a lurch that their mother had known the hour of their arrival before they had, had known it the way you know the hour a train comes in, and had timed the soup to it, and something about that—the precision of it, the *rehearsal* of it—sat wrong in Felix's stomach like a swallowed stone.

"Mother, how did you know what time—"

"The 4:12 from the junction. It's always been the 4:12." She sat, and folded her hands, and looked at Felix across the table with an expression of such gentle, terrible patience that Felix sat down too, because standing felt like disobedience. "Eat. You're thin. He'd have said you were thin."

*He'd have said.* Present tense, wearing the clothes of past tense. Felix picked up the spoon.

The soup was good. It was always good. That was the unbearable thing about their mother, that her grief expressed itself as competence, that the house was immaculate, that the funeral was arranged, that the soup was seasoned perfectly, and somewhere underneath all of it, Felix kept sensing, the way you sense a current under still water, something moving that she was very carefully not looking at.

"Tell me about the arrangements," Felix said. "Please. I want to know everything."

"Cremation was considered and rejected. Burial, in the family plot, past the orchard." She recited it like a litany. "The coffin is oak. The service is Friday. Father Anselm is coming out of retirement to do it, he baptized you both. The viewing is tomorrow evening, at the house, because I wouldn't have him lying at the undertaker's in town. He's here now. In the front room."

Felix's spoon stopped.

"Here? He's—Mother, the body is here? In the house?"

"Where else would he be?" She said it simply, and then, softer, looking down at her folded hands: "He never liked the dark. I told you. White bulbs make the shadows look like they're thinking. So I keep the lights on in the front room, all of them, until Friday, until he's in the ground and it doesn't matter anymore."

The clock ticked its slow drugged tick. The soup steamed. Somewhere upstairs, in a house that should have held only one grieving woman, a floorboard settled with a sound that Felix's body recognized before their mind did—the particular creak of the fourth stair, the one that had announced Quentin coming down for a glass of water at midnight for as long as either of them had been alive—and Felix's head came up, and their mother's did not, and that, more than anything, more than the lit windows and the timed soup and the scarf on its hook, was the moment the cold started and did not stop.

She hadn't looked up. She had heard it—Felix would swear to the end of their life that she had heard it—and she had not looked up, the way you don't look at the thing you have an arrangement with.

"The stairs settle," their mother said, to her soup. "Old houses settle. Eat while it's hot."

Felix ate. It would be years before they stopped being ashamed of that—of sitting there, spoon in hand, eating soup while the fourth stair creaked again, and again, in a slow descending rhythm, one step and then a pause and then another, the pace of a boy who had always taken the stairs carefully because of the night terrors, because if he went too fast the dark came with him—and when the rhythm reached the bottom and stopped, when the silence came back, Felix set down the spoon and said, in a voice they barely recognized:

"I need to see him tonight. Mother. I need to see my brother tonight."

And their mother looked at them then, finally, fully, with those pale eyes that had always seemed to be looking at something just behind the person in front of her, and she was quiet for so long that the clock filled the whole kitchen with its slow tick, and when she spoke, she said the sentence that Felix would take with them down into everything that followed, the sentence that would open like a door:

"You always did want things before they were ready." She stood, and took the bowls to the sink, and turned her back. "The front room's at the end of the hall. The lights are all on. If you're going to do this, Felix, do it quickly, and when you're finished, come back to the kitchen, and we won't speak of it again until Friday."

*We won't speak of it again.* As if the body were an argument she'd already won. As if Quentin were a subject, closed.

Felix stood. The hall was long and amber-lit and the scarf hung on its hook and the clock dragged its slowed heart through the silence, and at the end of the hall the front room doors stood open, both of them, spilling their too-bright light out across the floorboards like something poured.

Felix walked toward it. Twelve years of absence walked with them, and the guilt, and the memory of a voice below the kitchen floorboards saying things to something that answered, and the fourth stair that had creaked under no living weight, and underneath all of it, quieter than any of it, a thought that Felix would not let themselves finish, would not let themselves think all the way through until it was too late to unthink it:

*He never liked the dark. So why did he go down to the cellar to talk to it?*

The front room was warm. The lights were all on, every bulb, every lamp, a small white sun of a room, and in the middle of it, on trestles draped in black cloth, lay the coffin, open, because their mother had known Felix would come tonight, had known it the way she'd known the 4:12, had known it the way you know a thing you've rehearsed.

Felix crossed the room. Felix looked down.

And the first thing they saw, the thing that stopped their heart and restarted it wrong, was the hands—folded on his chest, pale as candle wax, and on the left hand, where Quentin had carried a scar shaped like a crescent moon from the time he'd fallen out of the apple tree at eight years old, the skin was smooth. Unbroken. Whole.

The hands of a boy who had never fallen at all.
