## 5. Mother's Ledger

The funeral was tomorrow, and the house would not let me sleep.

I told myself it was the jet lag, the eleven hours folded into an airplane seat, the wrongness of my body's clock set against the dark pressing at the windows of Amberdale. I told myself it was grief, which is a kind of insomnia anyway, a fever that runs on absence. But the truth was simpler and worse: the house was too quiet in the way a held breath is quiet, and every time I closed my eyes I saw the casket again—the wrong hands, the missing scar, the teeth like a stranger's smile—and sleep refused me as if it knew something I didn't.

So at two in the morning I got up and wandered.

The hallway runner swallowed my footsteps the way it always had, wool gone thin and gray with decades of use. The walls were hung with the same photographs: great-grandfather Ambrose scowling beside his prize hogs; Grandmother Vera at the orchard with a basket of windfalls; my mother, Edith, young and unsmiling in her wedding dress, looking less like a bride than a woman reporting for duty. Quentin and I appeared at the edges of these images as children—two identical boys in matching sweaters, one always slightly blurred, as if even the camera couldn't tell us apart or couldn't be bothered to try.

I had been away twelve years. Twelve years since I'd left for school and then for work and then for a life that required planes and deadlines and a phone number Quentin called less and less often, until he stopped calling at all, and I let him stop, because not-calling was easier than admitting how much distance we'd let grow between us. Now there would never be another call. There would only be this house, and the casket, and the earth they'd dig tomorrow to put him in.

Except they had already dug, hadn't they. Out in the orchard. For the well.

And found a door.

I passed Mother's study without meaning to. Then I stopped, because the door stood open, and Mother's study door had been locked my entire childhood—a rule so absolute it had become invisible, like gravity. Quentin and I had tried the handle a hundred times between ages six and ten, pressed our ears to the wood, invented whole mythologies about what she kept in there. She'd caught us once, only once, and the look on her face had not been anger. It had been fear. I hadn't understood that until now, standing in the dark hall at two in the morning, looking at an unlocked door in a house where nothing was as it should be.

She was three months dead herself. Heart failure, the doctor said, though she'd had the heart of an ox according to every checkup. The lawyer had handled the estate remotely; I'd signed papers by email, sent condolences by text to a house with no one left in it. I hadn't come back for her funeral either. That guilt sat in me like a stone I'd swallowed.

The study smelled of dust and lavender and, beneath both, something older—cellar-smell, cold stone and wet earth, though the cellar was on the far side of the house.

Her desk sat under the window, neat as an altar. On it: a lamp, a pen tray, a blotter worn smooth in the center where her hands had rested for forty years. And in the second drawer down—I don't know why I went there first, except that children know where their mothers hide things, some instinct older than memory—a stack of journals bound in black oilcloth.

There were thirty-one of them. One per month, going back. The earliest was dated before my birth.

I carried them to the window seat and opened the first one, and the smell of the pages hit me like a hand across the face: ink, paper, and that same cellar-cold underneath, as if the books themselves had spent time somewhere underground.

* * *

My mother wrote like she lived—sparely, precisely, with no wasted motion and no comfort offered. The early volumes were almost boring. Accounts of harvests. Notes on the orchard's blight cycles. Prices for feed, for labor, for the mason who came in 1979 to do unspecified work "below." I might have stopped reading, might have written the whole thing off as a farmer's ledger and nothing more, if not for the entries that didn't fit.

March 1981: *Paid H. Voss, mason, £40. Second course laid. He asked no questions this time.*

June 1984: *Voss's boy came asking about the work below. Paid Voss extra to keep him home. Will not have gossip.*

September 1990: *The solstice payment made. It stayed quiet all quarter.*

I read that last line four times. *It.* Not *he*, not *she*, not a name. *It stayed quiet.* As if quiet were a service rendered. As if silence were something you paid for, month after month, the way you paid the milk man or the tax man—and God help you the month you couldn't.

I turned pages faster now, my thumb catching on the cheap paper. The entries about *it* grew more frequent as the years went on, threaded through the farm accounts like a dark wire through clean cloth.

*It scratched at the hour of the dog. Paid the church candle fund double. It stayed quiet this month.*

*Quentin asked why the cellar door has no outside latch. Told him it was old work. He looked at me the way his father used to. It stayed quiet this month.*

My mouth had gone dry. Quentin. My brother, eight years old, asking the question I'd never thought to ask, and being lied to by the same mother who fed the lie monthly into a book she kept locked from her sons.

Then I reached the summer of 2012, and everything in the room went cold.

* * *

August 14th, 2012. I remembered the date before I read it, the way you remember the date of your own wound. Quentin drowned that August. Eleven years old, in the flooded quarry past the north field, on a day so hot the whole county had gone half-mad with swimming holes and cider. I had been there. I had been twenty feet away, on the bank, and I had turned away for one minute—one minute to chase a grasshopper, to be a child, to look anywhere but at my brother—and when I turned back the water was closing over the place where he'd been, and I dove, and I dove, and I dove, and the quarry gave him back to me blue and bloated and gone.

They pulled me off his body. I remember that too. Remember screaming words I've spent twelve years pretending I never said: *it's my fault, it's my fault, it's my fault.*

Mother's entry, dated three days later:

*My boy is dead. My beautiful boy is dead and Felix lives and I cannot bear it, I will not bear it. The thing below says it can give him back. It says it knows the shape of him, has watched him grow, has heard his voice through the floor all these years. It asks only what it has always asked. A place. A welcome. An Amberdale to tend the threshold.*

*I said yes.*

I put the journal down. My hands were shaking so badly the pages chattered against each other like teeth.

Below the entry, in fresher ink, dated the same week:

*He came home Tuesday. He is warm. He laughs. He has the scar on his chin from the barn door, and he remembers the summer we got the pony, and when I hold him he holds me back, and I do not care what the priest would say, I do not care what it cost, my son is alive. God forgive me. Or don't. He is alive.*

*Felix must never know. Felix must go to school in September as planned. It is better for everyone.*

I sat in the dark of my mother's study and understood, all at once and completely, that the brother I'd grown up with for the last six years of our shared childhood had not been my brother.

Or—he had been. That was the horror of it, the thing I couldn't make sit still in my mind. The boy who came home from the quarry was warm and laughing and scarred exactly right, and he played with me and fought with me and slept in the bed across the room, and I loved him, and I never—not once, not for a single day—suspected. Because what would suspicion even have gripped? A boy who was my brother in every particular. Except the particulars had been copied. Except somewhere under the orchard something had studied Quentin for eight years through the floorboards and learned him the way a forger learns a signature.

And I had left. Six years later, I'd packed for university and hugged him goodbye on the platform and felt only the ordinary ache of leaving home. He'd waved until the train turned. I'd thought about that wave for twelve years, thinking it was the last time I'd see my twin alive, grieving it cleanly, decently, the way you grieve real things.

Now the wave meant something else entirely. Now I wondered what had stood on that platform wearing his face, watching me go, and whether it had been relieved.

No. No, that wasn't fair. I pressed the heels of my hands into my eyes. Whatever came home had been a boy, too, maybe—or had believed itself one. Had it known? Had it lain awake in the dark next to me listening to me breathe, knowing it was a copy, a replacement, a consolation prize my mother had accepted because the alternative was a grave? Had it loved me with borrowed love, genuine in its way, the way a mirror's reflection is genuinely bright?

The last volume was thinner than the others. Recent. I opened it with fingers that no longer felt like mine.

* * *

The entries in the final book were shorter. Weaker. Her handwriting deteriorating month by month, the letters leaning like tired soldiers.

*Payment made. It stayed quiet this month.*

*Solstice. Paid double as required. It stayed quiet, but it knocked twice. It has not knocked in years. I think it is getting ready for something.*

*Doctor says my heart is failing. I laughed at him. What does he know about hearts. Mine has been breaking slowly for thirty years.*

Then, dated the 3rd of last month—eleven days after Quentin's death, after the death of the thing that had been Quentin, the drowning-in-reverse, the found body in the cistern with the wrong hands—

*It is loose. I don't know how. The bargain should have held. Quentin is dead and the debt should have died with him, but it says no, it says the debt belongs to the blood, not the boy. It says it has waited long enough.*

*I am writing this so someone finds it. Felix, if you are reading: do not answer it. Whatever it says, whatever name it uses, however it sounds—do not answer. Do not knock back. Do not say yes. It needs your word. It has always needed our word. That is the whole of the law and the whole of the trap.*

*I am sorry. I am so sorry. I traded your brother for a copy and the copy for silence and the silence for time, and now the bill has come due and I am dead and you are here.*

*Do not open the door.*

That was the last entry. Below it, in different handwriting—loose, looping, familiar in a way that made my scalp crawl—someone else had written a single line:

*Thank you for keeping the light on, Mum. — Q*

I closed the journal very carefully, as if it might wake.

The house creaked above me. Footsteps, crossing the upstairs hall—slow, deliberate, with that slight drag on the left foot that Quentin had developed at nine when he broke his ankle falling off the pony, a gait he'd grown out of by fourteen, a gait that no living walker should have had, a gait I would have known anywhere, that I had known anywhere, all week, and refused to know.

The footsteps crossed the hall. Stopped directly overhead. Waited.

And in the orchard, faint through the stone and the soil and the sealed door with no outside handle, something began—very softly, very patiently—to knock.

Three times.

Our signal. Ours, Quentin's and mine, tapped through the bedroom wall every night of our childhood: *still there? still there. still there? still there.*

Still there?

I sat in my dead mother's chair with her confession in my lap, and my mouth opened, and the word rose in me like water rising in a well—*yes*, the oldest answer, the family answer, the answer I had been trained by a lifetime of love to give without thinking—

I bit it back. Barely. Blood on my tongue where I bit it.

But the knocking had heard me anyway. I felt it hear me. Felt the pause after the third knock stretch, and deepen, and turn attentive, the way a listener turns attentive when they catch the sound of breathing on the other end of the line.

Somewhere below the orchard, patiently, almost fondly, it waited to see if I would answer.
