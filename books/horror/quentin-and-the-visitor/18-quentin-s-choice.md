## 18. Quentin's Choice

There is a word for what I am and I have been trying not to say it for twelve years.

The word is *bait*.

I know this the way I know the taste of the orchard water, the way I know the exact weight of Felix's sleeping breath through the wall we shared, the way I know things I never learned while alive—because the thing that wears me has been learning them through my mouth, and some of what it learns leaks back down into the place it keeps me. A cellar under a cellar. A dark that is not empty but *attentive*. I lie in it the way a coin lies at the bottom of a well, and above me, all this time, something has been spending me.

It is using my voice now. It is using it badly.

I can hear it through the floor of the world—through the kitchen, where Felix sits with the woman with the steady hands and the axe between them. My twin. My other half, my whole childhood, the person I loved past the limit of what that word can hold. The thing is calling them *Fen*. It has my memories. It has my scar. It has the story of the broken tooth from the fence rail when we were nine, and it tells these things in my voice, and Felix is crying, and I am screaming in the dark where no sound has ever gone anywhere, and the thing does not even pause. It doesn't have to. Screaming is just weather to it.

But tonight is different. Tonight I can feel it *straining*.

The bargain is close. The solstice. The door. I have felt the house above us rearranging itself for days, hallways unspooling toward the cellar like a throat preparing to swallow, and I have felt the thing's attention narrow to a point, and I know—I know the way I know my own name, my real name, the one only Felix ever used—what it wants. It wants Felix behind the door. It wants to trade me back like a coin for a coin. And the terrible thing, the thing I have to say before I lose this sliver of myself again, is that it *can*. It can hand Felix a warm, breathing, laughing Quentin. It has my shape down to the last freckle. It has had twelve years of practice.

It has had twelve years of practice because I let it.

Because I was a coward even before I drowned.

* * *

Here is what happened in the cellar, the summer we were sixteen. I have been carrying it so long it has grown roots through me, and if this is the last of me that will ever be me, then it comes out now.

I went down for a bottle of Father's cider, on a dare from myself, because Felix dared me in the way Felix dares—by saying nothing at all, just looking at the cellar door with that expression. I took the lamp. The stairs were cold. And at the bottom, in the corner behind the preserves, there was a boy.

He looked like me. That was the first wrongness—not that he was there, but that he was *me*, a Quentin slightly older, slightly wrong, the way a reflection is wrong when the mirror is old. He was sitting on the dirt floor with his knees drawn up, and he said, in my voice, *You found me. I knew you'd find me. Nobody else looks down here.*

And I should have run. I should have told Mother, told Felix, told the priest, told anyone. Instead I sat down across from him, because he was lonely, and I have never in my life been able to leave a lonely thing alone. That is my sickness. That is the door I built in myself long before the one under the orchard.

He talked. All that summer, in the cellar, when the house was empty. He told me he had been down there a long time. He told me he was cold. He told me things about our family that no stranger could know—that Mother's sister had gone into the orchard in 1951 and come out with white hair and no memory; that Grandfather had paid a man in Ashford to forget a whole year; that the Amberdales had a debt and the debt had a door and the door was hungry on a schedule like anything alive.

And he said, one night in August, with his head tilted in a way my head does not tilt: *When the water takes you, don't fight it. It's easier if you don't fight it. And then you can come be with me, and you'll never be lonely again.*

I was sixteen and I heard that and I understood—I understood *exactly*—and I went upstairs and I said nothing. I ate dinner. I did the crossword with Mother. I let Felix tease me about the cider and I laughed. And three weeks later, at the swimming hole, when my foot went into the old drain grate and the water closed over my head, I remembered his voice, and I did not fight it.

I did not fight it.

That is the thing I have to make Felix understand, if any part of me can reach them: it was not an accident. It was not entirely the thing's doing either. It was a boy who was lonely and flattered and afraid, who was offered a way to never be lonely, and who opened his own mouth under the water and let the river in. The thing under the orchard did not take me. *I accepted delivery.* And everything Mother did afterward—the ledger, the payments, the twelve years of *it stayed quiet this month*—she did to clean up a mess her son made with his own loneliness.

Felix has spent twelve years drowning in guilt over the silence between us. The distance. The unanswered letters. I have watched them carry it, from down here, the way you watch weather from underwater. And it was never theirs. The silence was mine first. I built it in a cellar in the summer we were sixteen, brick by brick, and Felix has been living inside it ever since without knowing who laid the walls.

* * *

Now the thing is tired of wearing me carefully. I can feel it. For twelve years it has kept me folded small, a coat it wore on special occasions—Mother's death, the funeral director's back room, the long patient years of knocking politely on the inside of the world. But the solstice is tonight and the door is close and Felix is *close*, and it is spending me all at once, all my warmth and all my memories, to buy the last thing it wants.

And in the spending, something has come loose. I don't know why. Maybe it is using too much of me too fast, the way a lamp burns brightest just before the oil fails. Maybe the axe in the kitchen—the family axe, the one with our name in the haft—has cut something it shouldn't have cut. Maybe love, real love, the kind Felix has been carrying like a stone for twelve years, is simply heavier than the thing can lift.

Whatever the reason: tonight I am at the surface of myself. Tonight I can feel my own hands.

Felix is in the kitchen. The woman—Okafor, Dara, the one whose brother went into these fields before I was born—is holding the light. The ledger is open on the table; I can feel its pages like a scar on the world. And the thing is standing in the middle of the kitchen wearing my body, weeping my tears, saying *Fen, Fen, it's me, it's really me,* and Felix is reaching out with both hands, and I have perhaps one minute. Perhaps less. A minute in which I am Quentin Amberdale and not a coat.

So I take it.

I don't know how to describe what I do, because I have never done it before. It is like pushing against the inside of my own skin. It is like swimming upward through twelve years of dark toward a coin of light that is my brother's face. I put everything I am into it—every memory, every ounce of the twin-thing, that strange doubled current that ran between us before I ever went into the cellar. Felix will know. Felix has always known. That is the one power I have left, and it has never once failed: Felix knows me.

The thing staggers. I feel it stagger from the inside, feel my stolen mouth go slack mid-sentence, and in the gap—in the half-second where it is fighting me for the wheel—I speak.

*Felix. Don't open the door.*

I don't know if it comes out as sound. I don't know if it comes out as anything but a shudder in my borrowed hands. It doesn't matter. I say it again. I say it the way you say a thing when you are drowning, which is to say completely, which is to say with your whole body, which is to say: *Felix. It's me. The real one. And I'm telling you—do not trade for me. Do not take the deal. Whatever it shows you, whatever it gives you—*

The thing slams down on me like a lid.

But I got the door of myself open for a moment, and through that door I pushed the only cargo that matters. I felt it go. I felt it land in Felix like a stone dropped into still water—felt my twin go rigid, felt their hands stop reaching, felt the whole kitchen change temperature around a single understanding:

*This one is Quentin. And Quentin is saying no.*

* * *

I am fading again. The dark is folding me back down, gentle as a parent, horrible as a parent. But I have a little longer—I can feel the thing conserving, recalculating, and while it recalculates, I am going to say everything, in case this is the last surface I ever break.

Felix. Listen. I know you can hear me even if you can't hear me. You always could.

I was not taken. I went down. I want that in the record, whatever record survives—Mother's ledger, the woman's report, the story you will tell when this house is finally just a house. I went down because I was sixteen and lonely in a house full of people, and something in the cellar knew my name before I ever knew its hunger, and I chose it, the way you choose a warm coat in winter, without asking where the warmth comes from. Everything after—the drowned boy, the replaced boy, the twelve years of quiet months and paid debts—that was the interest on my choice. You have spent twelve years paying it and thinking it was yours.

It was never yours. Put it down. Put it down tonight, at the door, and leave it there.

And the other thing. The hard thing. The thing I would not say even to myself in the cellar, all that summer, because saying it would have made it real:

I would rather stay dead.

I mean it. I have had twelve years in the dark to mean it. I have felt what it feels like in here—what it feels like to be the lure, the warm coat, the voice in the walls, the open casket with the wrong hands. I have felt it use my love for you as a hook, over and over, and there is no torture in any of the stories Mother read us that is worse than that. If you open that door and trade for me, you will not be saving your brother. You will be handing it the one thing it has never had: an Amberdale who came *willingly*, in daylight, with the axe in reach and the truth in hand. You will be the best bargain it has ever made, and it will spend you the way it has spent me, and in a hundred years some other lonely child will sit in a cellar and hear your voice offering never to be lonely again.

Don't. Please. I would rather be a body in a box in the ground with my wrong hands and my missing scar, honestly dead, honestly finished, than be the thing that gets you.

You were the best half of us, Fen. You always were. I was the one who talked to things in cellars. You were the one who would have burned the whole orchard down if you'd known. Be that one now. Be the one who burns it down.

The dark is taking me back. I can feel my hands going. But I want the last of me that is me to be this: not fear, not the cellar, not the water. *You.* I want to go down holding the memory of you at nine years old, furious at me for the broken tooth, holding my face in both hands to check it was really me—and it was, it was, Fen, that was the last time anyone checked—and saying, *You're an idiot, but you're my idiot.*

I'm your idiot. I was always your idiot. And I am telling you, with the last breath I own:

Don't open it for me.

Open it because it's the truth. Or don't open it at all. But whatever you do—do it as the Amberdale who checks the hands.

* * *

The dark closes. The coat goes back on. Somewhere above me, in a kitchen that smells of lamp oil and old paper, the thing wearing my face has gone very still, because it felt what I did, and it is only now beginning to understand that it made a mistake it has never made in all its long hungry centuries:

It let the bait speak.

And the bait said *no.*

I sink. But as I go down through the layers of myself, I feel something I have not felt in twelve years—felt it in the walls, in the floorboards, in the axe-haft with our name in it, in the ledger lying open like a wound:

Felix is standing up.

Felix is picking up the axe.
