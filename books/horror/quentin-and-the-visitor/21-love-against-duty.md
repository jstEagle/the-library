## 21. Love Against Duty

The thing wore Quentin's face the way a glove wears a hand—faithfully, but with the fingers empty.

It stood at the foot of the stairwell where the rows of Amberdales waited in their alcoves, generations of them arranged like books on shelves no one had ever meant to read again. Great-grandmother Maren with her hands folded. The uncle whose name had been scraped off every document in the house. And nearest the door, newest, Pell and the two crewmen who had wandered into the orchard, their faces slack with a peace that was not peace.

And Quentin. Quentin standing apart from all of them, whole and warm and weeping, exactly as he had stood in the kitchen an hour ago when the thing had shown Felix what it could deliver.

"Say yes," it said, in his voice. "Fen. Say yes, and I walk out of here with you. We go home. We bury nothing. You get your brother back."

*Fen.* Only Quentin had ever called Felix that. Only Quentin, and only because at six years old Felix had bitten through the skin of a plum and declared it *fenomenal*, mouth purple, and Quentin had laughed so hard he'd fallen off the orchard wall.

The thing knew that. Of course it knew that. It had been drinking this family's memories for twelve years through a straw made of a drowned boy's name.

Dara's light wavered beside Felix—not from fear, she would have said, but from the way her arm was shaking. Below them, the stairwell breathed. Felix could feel it against their shins, a slow tidal warmth, like standing too close to an open oven in a dark kitchen.

"I watched you drown," Felix said.

Quentin's face tilted. Patient. Waiting. It could wait; that was its whole nature. It had waited under the orchard for longer than there had been an orchard.

"Twelve years ago," Felix went on. Their voice came out strange, level, as if someone else were speaking through them—and maybe, they thought, someone was. Maybe grief was its own kind of possession. "I was on the bank. I saw you go under the ice, and I didn't go in after you. I ran for Mother instead, and by the time we came back you were already—" They stopped. Swallowed. "You were already gone. And then you weren't. You came up the bank dripping and laughing and saying *you should've seen your face*, and I was so happy, Fen-happy, six-years-old-with-a-plum happy, that I never asked how."

"Felix." Quentin's voice cracked beautifully. It had learned to crack beautifully. "Don't do this to yourself."

"Mother asked," Felix said. "That's what the ledger is. That's what the payments were. She asked *how*, and the answer was you—or something wearing you—and she couldn't bear to lose you twice, so she built a lie around it and fed it solstices until it grew fat and quiet. 'It stayed quiet this month.' Twenty-nine times she wrote that. Like a woman noting the weather over a cage."

The thing's smile did not change. But the temperature of the stairwell changed. The breathing slowed.

"You're offering me my brother," Felix said. "Warm. Alive. Real enough to hold. And I believe you can do it—I've seen you do it. That's the worst part. You're not lying about what you're selling. You're only lying about the price."

"The price is one person," it said gently. "One person, who is standing here already, who has been half-gone since the day they watched their twin die and said nothing. Felix. *Fen.* You've been behind this door your whole life. I'm only asking you to make it official."

Dara made a sound low in her throat—not a word, just a refusal shaped like breath.

And Felix looked at their brother.

At the real one. Because here was the thing Felix had understood somewhere between the third landing and the rows of the dead: Quentin was still in there. Had always been in there. The thing hadn't eaten him; it had *kept* him, the way you keep a seed catalogued in a drawer, viable, ready to plant whenever the soil of a grieving sibling proved rich enough. Every memory it had weaponized—the nickname, the plum, the orchard wall—had come from somewhere. From him. From the boy under the ice who had spent twelve years being worn like a coat by something patient and hungry and, above all, *secret*.

That was the key. Felix had turned it over all the way down the stairs, and now they held it up to the light.

Secrets. The thing had never once broken a secret. It had lived inside one. Mother's ledger, locked in a drawer. A boy returned from drowning, never spoken of. A door sealed from the inside, unexplained to any workman, any priest, any neighbor. Twelve years of silence, paid for monthly, and the thing had grown fat on every page of it.

It wasn't fed by blood. It was fed by the *hiding*.

"No," Felix said.

The word fell down the stairwell and did not echo. The dark drank it.

Quentin's face flickered—just once, like a candle in a draft—and beneath it, for a quarter of a second, Felix saw something that had no face at all, something like the space between tree roots filled with slow water. Then the mask settled back, wounded, tender. "Fen—"

"No," Felix said again, louder, and stepped forward off the last stair onto the floor of the chamber, where the air was warm and smelled of turned earth and plums going soft. "You heard me. I'm not taking his place. I'm not taking anyone's place. But I'm not walking away either, so put your arithmetic away and listen, because I'm offering you a trade you haven't thought of."

The breathing stopped. In the silence, Felix could hear Dara's heart, or their own, or both.

"A third bargain," Felix said. "Not a swap. Not a resident. A witness."

They held up the ledger. Its cover was damp down here, swollen, the way paper gets when it has been buried a long time.

"My mother bought your silence for twenty-nine months and then died owing you. My family kept you fed with secrecy for a hundred years before that—every name scraped off, every door sealed, every child told nothing. Secrecy is the only food you've ever had. Am I wrong?"

The thing said nothing. The nothing had weight.

"So starve," Felix said. "Or eat something new."

They opened the ledger to the last written page—Mother's final entry, dated three days after Quentin's death, the ink dragged where her hand had failed—and then, below it, in the blank pages, Felix began to speak aloud. Not reading. Telling.

"Maren Amberdale, 1911, gave it her sister's name and told no one. Harold Amberdale, 1954, lost his son to the orchard and told the town the boy ran away. My mother, Edith, traded a drowned boy's shape for a living one and wrote it down only in code, in a drawer, in the dark." Their voice shook and they let it shake. "My name is Felix Amberdale. My twin brother Quentin drowned on the fourteenth of December, twelve years ago, under the ice of the mill pond, and I watched, and I said nothing, and everything that happened after happened because of that silence. And I am ending it. Here. Now. Out loud. To a witness."

They turned to Dara.

"Dara Okafor. Tell it yours."

Dara's light steadied. When she spoke, her voice was rough as gravel but it did not break. "My brother Samuel came to this land forty-one years ago with a survey crew. He never came home. They told us he took money from the site office and left. I believed it for thirty years because believing it was easier than digging. Last month I found his name scratched inside the lid of a toolbox in my father's things, with the word *orchard* under it. I took this job to dig. I'm telling the truth of it now, out loud, in front of it, and I will tell it again in daylight, to anyone who asks, for the rest of my life."

The chamber exhaled.

It was not relief. It was hunger—the vast, offended, bewildered hunger of a thing that had been offered a meal it had no teeth for. Truth, spoken freely, given without coercion, bound to be repeated: it could not swallow that. Truth hidden was meat; truth *told* was ash. Every word Felix and Dara spoke aloud was a word that would live outside these walls, in mouths neither of them controlled, multiplying, unownable. The thing's whole economy was ownership of the untold. They were dumping its stores into the street.

"You would make me a story," it said. Quentin's voice had gone thin. Then it tried another voice—Pell's, cheerful and wet. "Stories fade. People forget."

"Some do," Felix agreed. "But it doesn't matter whether they remember correctly. It matters that it's *theirs*. You've never owned a single true thing about yourself. For a hundred years you've been whatever this family needed you to be—a quiet month, a sealed door, a son who came back. I'm offering you the one thing you've never had." They took a breath. "A name. Told by strangers. Repeated until it's common. You'll be folklore. You'll be a warning children dare each other near. You will exist—in the open, in the light, in a hundred mouths that don't belong to any Amberdale. Or you can refuse, and keep your silence, and I swear to you on Quentin's grave—which I am finally, actually going to dig—that I will spend every day I have left telling everyone everything, until there is nothing left in this house that belongs only to you. No secret. No welcome. No bargain. Just an old door under an orchard that anyone may look at, and laugh at, and leave flowers on, and forget."

Silence. Long enough that Felix counted their own heartbeat past a hundred.

Then, from the row of alcoves, a sound.

Quentin moved.

Not the thing wearing Quentin—the *other* Quentin, the fragment, the seed in the drawer. He came forward out of the row like a man surfacing from deep water, and his face was his own, and he was looking at Felix with an expression Felix had last seen across a kitchen table at fourteen, when Quentin had said *you know something's wrong with me, right?* and Felix had said *don't be stupid* and changed the subject forever.

"*Fen,*" he said. His real voice. Cracked the ordinary way, the human way.

"Q."

"It's me. Right now it's me. I don't know for how long." He was crying without noise, the way he'd learned to as a kid, so Mother wouldn't hear. "Don't argue with it anymore. Take the deal. Take the witness deal—it wants it, I can feel it wanting, it's *starving*, it hasn't eaten since you started talking. Take it before it remembers it's proud."

"Is it you?" Felix asked, because they had to, because they had spent twelve years not asking questions. "Really you?"

"Bits." He smiled, and it broke Felix's heart clean in half. "Enough bits. Enough to know that if you take its swap, I spend eternity being the hook it baits you with. I'd rather rest. Fen—I'd rather rest. Let me rest."

Felix crossed the floor and took their brother's hands. Warm. Warm as promised. The thing kept its bargains to the letter; that had always been its flaw and its lure both.

"If I do this," Felix said—to Quentin, to the thing, to the dark that breathed around them—"he rests. Actually rests. His body goes in the ground under a stone with his real death date on it, and nothing wears him again. Ever. That's my price. Not a swap. A condition."

"And mine," Dara said. "Samuel too. All of them. Everyone in these walls gets named, dated, buried in daylight. Your whole collection goes home."

The breathing resumed—slower now, deeper, thinking. The water-smell thickened. Somewhere far up the stairwell, the house groaned as its rearranging halls began, grudgingly, to straighten.

"Starved," the thing said at last, and it used no one's voice, which was somehow worse and more honest. "You offer me starvation dressed as remembrance."

"I offer you existence," Felix said. "The only kind left to you. Take it or watch me burn every secret you own, one sunrise at a time, for the rest of my natural life. I'm twenty-six. I'm stubborn. I got it from Mother, and she got it from keeping you fed for thirty years without ever once letting herself say your name. Imagine what I'll do now that I'm allowed to say it."

Another silence. Then, softly, almost fond—the way the knocks had sounded, that morning, through the kitchen wall:

"Witness."

The word settled over the chamber like frost forming. Felix felt it take hold somewhere behind their sternum, a small cold weight, a hook set gently in flesh—not pain, but permanence. A vow with a pulse. Bound to remember. Bound to tell. For as long as they lived, the truth would live in them and move through them and out of their mouth, and no drawer would ever close over it again.

"Yes," Felix said. "Witness."

Quentin smiled. And then, gently, like a lamp being carried out of a room, he began to go—not collapsing, not dissolving, just *settling*, the warmth leaving his hands by degrees while his face stayed his own to the very end. Felix held on. Dara turned her light away to give them privacy, then turned it back, because some things deserved witnessing too, and that was the whole point now.

"Tell them I was funny," Quentin whispered. His eyes were already fixed on some middle distance where the ice had long since melted. "Tell them I was funny, and that I forgave you before you even started blaming yourself. You were eight, Fen. You were *eight*."

"I know," Felix said. "I know. Rest."

He rested.

What remained was a body—the true one, the drowned one, twelve years patient in the dark—and it was finally, unmistakably, only a body. The terrible aliveness went out of it like tide off a flat. Felix knelt and closed their brother's eyes with two fingers, and the chamber, for the first time in a hundred years, was merely cold.

Above them, faintly, dawn-colored light had begun to seep down the stairwell. The house had stopped breathing.

* * *

They came up into an orchard going grey with first light, carrying Quentin wrapped in Dara's coat, and behind them the earth of the collapsed stairwell lay loose and ordinary, waiting for shovels. Felix's chest ached with the cold weight of the vow, and their arms ached with their brother, and their throat ached with everything still left to say—there would be so much to say, years of it, testimony and article and book, the ledger photocopied and spread wide, names read aloud over open ground.

Dara paused at the orchard gate and looked back at the raw scar of earth.

"He'll want a stone with jokes on it," she said.

"He'll want a stone with the right date on it," Felix said. "The jokes are my department."

They walked out together as the sun cleared the hedgerow, and behind them, deep beneath the collapsed earth, in the dark that was patient now, almost fond—

three knocks.
