## 22. Sunrise

The light came up gray and then gold, the way light does when it has no interest in what it illuminates. It poured over the orchard's dead rows, over the trench where the well was meant to go, over the stone door standing open at the bottom of the earth like a mouth that had finished speaking.

Felix stood at the trench's edge with the ledger pressed against their chest, held there by both arms, the way you hold a child or a wound. The pages had gone soft with damp and handling. Somewhere in the middle of the night they had stopped being evidence and become a body—something that needed carrying, something that could not be left behind.

Dara Okafor stood beside them, saying nothing. She had not said much since they came up out of the stairwell. Her hands were still shaking, though she'd tucked them into her jacket pockets as if the shaking were a private matter. Felix understood. There were things the body did after a night like this one, and dignity was mostly a matter of where you let it happen.

Below them, in the dark at the bottom of the stairs, the rows of Amberdales waited. Or didn't. The thing had accepted Felix's bargain—*witness, not resident*—and the acceptance had felt like a door closing somewhere very far away, or perhaps like a door opening. With that creature, Felix suspected, the two sensations would always be indistinguishable.

Quentin's body had finally rested. That was the phrase Felix kept returning to, turning it over like a stone. *Finally rested.* As if rest were a place he'd been trying to reach for twelve years and had only now been allowed to arrive. They had wrapped him in Dara's tarp and laid him in the shade of the oldest apple tree, the one their mother had never let anyone prune. He looked like himself. For the first time since the viewing, he looked entirely, peacefully like himself.

"We should do it before the light gets too good," Dara said. "Before it starts feeling like a normal day."

Felix nodded. That was the trap of mornings—they arrived regardless, offering their clean gold light, their birdsong, their suggestion that the world had continued while you were down in the dark learning what your family actually was. If they waited until the morning felt real, they might convince themselves the night hadn't. And the night had. The night was the truest thing Felix Amberdale had ever witnessed.

They went back down once more, together, because the door could not be left open and it could not be closed gently. Dara had spent an hour before dawn studying the hinges—the same hinges her crew had found rusted shut from the inside—and she'd found what she was looking for: the load-bearing stones in the arch above the lintel, softened by two centuries of frost heave, held up by habit more than mortar.

"It wants to come down," she'd said. "It's been waiting for someone to give it permission."

Now Felix stood at the bottom of the stairwell one last time, holding the lantern high, and gave it permission.

The first stone came loose with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Then the next. Then the whole arch sighed and shrugged and began to fall—not fast, not catastrophically, but with the terrible patience of geology accelerated, stone sliding over stone, dust blooming up through the lantern light in slow golden clouds. Felix climbed as it fell. Dara hauled them up by the wrist at the last moment, and they lay flat on the orchard grass coughing while the doorway that had swallowed four generations of Amberdales disappeared under its own weight.

When the dust settled, there was nothing to see. Just collapsed earth, a shallow depression in the ground, and the old apple tree standing witness above it all.

Felix sat up. Their hands were black with dirt. They did not wipe them.

* * *

They buried Quentin properly at nine in the morning, just the two of them, because there was no one else left to call. Pell's family would be notified—Dara had driven to the neighbor's farm at first light to use their phone, and come back with her face set like concrete. The other crew members who'd scattered into the night had surfaced, one by one, at the gas station in town, alive, unwilling to speak about what they'd seen. One of them had already quit. All of them would.

Felix dug the grave themselves. Dara offered; Felix refused. This was the last physical labor Quentin would ever ask of anyone, and it belonged to his sibling.

The soil came up in dark, heavy spadefuls, richer than it had any right to be. Felix thought of their mother's ledger, of the entry repeated month after month across twelve years—*It stayed quiet this month*—and understood, finally, why the orchard grew so well. Why the apples had always been too sweet. Why the ground here had never once refused anything planted in it.

Everything fed on something. Their mother had simply chosen what.

They lowered him down without a coffin, because coffins were for bodies that hadn't already spent a week being worn by something else, and Felix said the words their mother used to say over winter graves—old words, half prayer and half recipe, the kind of words that assume the dead are listening and don't mind. Dara stood at the head with her cap in her hands. When it was done, when the last of the earth was patted down and the apple tree's shadow had crawled far enough to touch the mound, Dara spoke.

"My brother's name was Tomas," she said. "He was nineteen. 1987. My father worked this land before your mother bought it—did you know that? Nobody knows that. The Ambers bought it from us, and my father took the money and we moved to the city and nobody ever said Tomas's name again." She put her cap back on. "I told you I needed to see the door opened. I lied, a little. What I needed was to know he wasn't in there. And now I know. Now I can bury *that*, too."

"Where will you look?" Felix asked.

Dara looked out at the fields beyond the orchard, the wide flat acres going gold in the morning sun, and her expression made it clear she had already decided never to look anywhere again. "Nowhere," she said. "He's nowhere. That's the answer. It's a bad answer but it's mine." She turned to Felix. "And you? What are you going to do with that book?"

Felix looked down at the ledger, still clutched against their chest, still damp, still heavy. Pages of payments. Pages of dates. Pages of a mother's handwriting deteriorating year by year from careful script to cramped scrawl to something barely legible, as if the writing itself were being consumed along with everything else. *It stayed quiet this month.* *It stayed quiet this month.* *It stayed quiet this month.* And then, in the final entry, dated three days after Quentin's drowning, the sentence that had rearranged Felix's entire childhood:

*It asked for him by name. God forgive me. I said yes.*

"I'm going to publish it," Felix said. "All of it. The ledger, the house, the door, the crew, Pell. Everything. Under my name."

"You understand what that costs," Dara said. Not a question. "People don't come back from being the one who tells this story. You'll be the Amberdale who went mad, or the Amberdale who killed her brother, or the Amberdale who believes in monsters. Towns are small. Stories like this get attached to families like burrs."

"I know." Felix touched the grave mound once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder. "But the bargain I made—the third bargain—it needs a witness. It needs someone bound to remember and tell. That's not a punishment, Dara. That's the whole point. My mother starved that thing on silence for twelve years and called it peace. Silence is what it eats. So I'm going to be loud. For the rest of my life, I'm going to be the loudest woman in whatever room I'm standing in."

Dara studied them for a long moment. Then she extended her hand, and Felix shook it, and the handshake lasted longer than handshakes usually do—two people agreeing, without saying it, to carry each other's nights.

"I'll write my report," Dara said. "Structural collapse. Soil instability. One worker missing, presumed fled. One body recovered, natural causes pending." She paused. "And then I'm going to retire, and grow tomatoes, and never think about any of this except every single day."

"That sounds healthy."

"It sounds like a plan. Plans are what I have instead of healthy." She shouldered her pack. "Come on. I'll drive you to town. You shouldn't be alone in that house another hour, and honestly, neither should I."

* * *

They walked out through the orchard one last time, past the trench, past the collapsed depression in the earth that was already growing a skin of morning shadow, past the old tree with its too-sweet fruit hanging heavy and untouched. At the edge of the property Felix stopped and turned around.

The house stood against the brightening sky, windows catching fire with sunrise, looking for all the world like what it had always pretended to be: a home. A big drafty beautiful home where two twins had grown up running the halls, where a mother had baked bread on Sundays, where a father had told ghost stories by the fire that were, in retrospect, less fiction than anyone knew.

Felix had spent twelve years away from it, telling themselves the distance was ambition, was career, was the ordinary drift of adult life. Now they knew the truth: some part of them had known. Some part of them had felt the pull of the cellar through the floorboards even from three hundred miles away, and had run, and had hated themselves for running, and had run anyway. The guilt they'd carried to the funeral—that was the guilt of a survivor, and it had been accurate all along.

"I'm sorry," Felix said aloud, to the house, to the grave behind them, to the boy in the grave. "For leaving. For staying silent when we were sixteen. For every month I didn't call her and ask why she sounded so tired." A breath. "And thank you. For choosing to stay dead rather than be its lure. I don't know how you found the strength, down there, inside that thing, wearing twelve years of its lies. But you did. You were braver dead than most people ever manage alive."

The wind moved through the orchard, stirring the branches, and if it carried an answer, it carried it privately.

Dara waited by the truck, engine idling, giving Felix the courtesy of pretending not to listen.

Felix got in. The truck pulled onto the gravel lane, and the house shrank in the side mirror, and the orchard shrank, and the land itself folded away behind hedgerows and hills until it was just a green smudge on the horizon, a place where something had happened, a place that would soon be a story.

The ledger sat on Felix's lap. In the margin of the final page, beneath their mother's last confession, Felix had written a line of their own during the long gray hours before dawn, in pen that skipped and bled on the damp paper:

*My name is Felix Amberdale. I was the wrong twin, and then I was the only twin, and now I am the witness. What follows is true. Read it with the lights on.*

That was how the book would begin. Whatever publisher was brave enough or foolish enough to take it, that was how it would open—no preface, no apology, no frame of fiction to hide behind. Just the name, and the claim, and the promise.

Dara drove. The sun climbed. The world did what the world does, which is continue, indifferently, beautifully, offering its clean light to everyone equally—the guilty and the grieving and the ones who had traded their place in the world so that others could keep theirs.

Somewhere behind them, beneath the collapsed earth, beneath the roots of the old apple tree, in the dark that had learned patience the way other things learn hunger—

three knocks.

Slow. Unhurried. Patient now, almost fond.

As if it could wait forever. As if, having finally been heard, it had all the time in the world.
