## 4. The Well Refills

Maren's cottage sat at the crook of the lane where it bent toward the water, and I had been told — by the landlady at the Fox and Furl, by the postman, by a woman selling eggs from a cart who stopped mid-sentence when she saw my recorder bag — that if I wanted the old songs, Maren Ashby was the one who still carried them whole.

Her door was blue. That seemed important, though I couldn't have said why. Every other door in Hollowmere was green or black or unpainted oak gone silver with weather. Hers alone was blue, a deep evening blue, and as I stood before it with my hand raised to knock, I noticed the threshold had been freshly swept, and that the sweepings — dust, petals, a few grains of what might have been salt — had been pushed not off the step but into a neat little pile at its center, like an offering arranged for someone arriving rather than leaving.

I knocked.

"One moment," said a voice like paper folding, and then longer than a moment, long enough that I counted the panes in the window beside me — twelve of them, four by three — before the latch lifted and there she was: small, straight-backed, her white hair pinned in a coil so precise it looked architectural. Her eyes were pale gray, almost colorless, and they went first to my recorder bag, then to my face, and stayed on my face.

"You're Edith Voss's grandchild," she said. Not a question.

"Cyrus. I'm doing fieldwork on — "

"I know what you're doing." She stepped back to let me in. "You'll want tea. Everyone wants tea first. It gives them something to do with their hands while they decide how much of the truth they can afford."

* * *

The cottage interior was exactly what I'd expected from two days of interviews in Hollowmere: dark wood, dried herbs hanging in bundles from the beams, photographs of people whose faces had been turned slightly away from the camera or blurred at the moment of exposure, as though the house itself had flinched. But there were additions no other home had offered. A row of small stones lined the windowsill above the sink, each one wet. A bowl of clear water sat on the table between us, untouched, reflecting the ceiling in miniature.

Maren poured tea without asking how I took it. It was correct.

"You've been seeing the others," she said, sitting across from me. "The Hollises. The Pells. Young Tam with his fiddle."

"They've all been very generous."

"Generous," she repeated, and something moved behind her face that wasn't quite a smile. "And did they sing their songs for you?"

"Most of them. The *Loom Song*, the *May Carol*, a christening round from the Pell family — "

"And did you ask them to sing it again?"

I set down my cup. "Once or twice. For sound quality. Room tone changes, mic placement — standard practice is to record a piece more than once, ideally from different — "

"And?"

I thought of Tam Weller's fiddle stopping mid-phrase, the way he'd looked at the instrument afterward as if it had spoken out of turn. I thought of Mrs. Hollis's practiced warmth cracking, just once, when I'd asked for the second verse again — *"I think you'll find once is enough, dear"* — and the door closing with a softness that felt worse than slamming.

"They declined," I said.

"Mm." Maren wrapped both hands around her own cup but didn't drink. "Songs are like names here, Cyrus. You give one away, you don't get it back. Sing a song twice and it stops being yours. It starts being *its*."

"Its," I said. "Whose?"

She looked at the bowl of water on the table. Just looked. And I felt the temperature of the room change — not drop, exactly, but gather, the way air does before weather.

"The well refills," she said.

I waited. She let me wait. Outside, a rook called twice and went silent mid-second-call, which is not a thing rooks do, and I noted it and filed it under *coincidence* where I filed everything, because filing was what I did, it was the whole architecture of my life — every uncanny thing sorted into a drawer labeled *explainable* and the drawer shoved shut with both hands.

"Every generation," she went on, "the well refills. Goes low through the summer, low enough you can see the stones at the bottom, and then one night near the equinox it comes back up. Full to the brim. Sweeter than any water in the county. People used to come from three villages over for it."

"That's hydrology," I said, relieved to be on solid ground. "Aquifers recharge seasonally. There are chalk streams in this region that — "

"There are," she agreed pleasantly, "and none of them rise overnight. None of them rise *on schedule*. And none of them," she added, lifting her eyes to mine, "remember what they're owed."

* * *

I laughed.

I want to be honest about that, since honesty is supposedly what I'd come to Hollowmere to collect. I laughed — a short, professional laugh, the kind you deploy when an informant drifts into folklore-as-literalism, and I reached into my bag for my notebook and said something like, "That's a wonderful framing, actually. The well as creditor. Do you mind if we keep going? I'd love to record this in your own words."

And Maren Ashby looked at me with those colorless eyes and said, "Record whatever you like, child. It changes nothing. The water doesn't care about your little machine. It cared about your grandmother, though. It cared about her for years."

My pen stopped.

"What do you mean by that?"

"I mean," she said, "that Edith Voss left this village in a hurry in the spring of — well. You'll know the year better than I. And that for twelve years after, wherever she lived, there were noises. Wet feet on stairs. Scratching inside walls where there's no space for anything to scratch. And that when she came back the one time, for a funeral, she wouldn't stay the night. Drove back through the fog at two in the morning with you asleep in the back seat, and I watched the car go, and I remember thinking: *she never paid, and now it follows her.*"

The room was very quiet. The bowl of water on the table sat perfectly still, and somehow that was worse than ripples would have been — stillness that dense, that deliberate, like a held breath.

"My grandmother had arthritis," I heard myself say. "Old houses settle. Pipes knock. There are documented acoustic explanations for — "

"Cyrus." My name in her mouth sounded different than it did in mine. Older. Like it belonged to the village and I was only borrowing it. "You've been recording songs for three days. Have you written down a single word anyone's said about the well? Or have you been steering them away from it, gentle as a shepherd, every time it comes up?"

I opened my mouth to deny it and found nothing there.

Because it was true. Mrs. Hollis had mentioned the well in passing — *the children used to sing to it, before* — and I had steered her back to the May Carol so smoothly I hadn't even registered doing it. Tam Weller had started to say something about a tune "you only play walking past the water" and I had asked him about his grandfather instead. Three days of fieldwork, and I had curated Hollowmere the way you curate a photograph: cropping out everything that didn't fit the picture I'd decided I was taking.

I turned on the recorder.

It was a small, stupid act of courage, or cowardice dressed as courage — I genuinely couldn't tell you which, even now. I set the little machine on the table between us, next to the bowl of water, red light glowing, and I said:

"Tell me about the refill."

* * *

And she did. Not directly — never directly; I understood even then that directness was a currency Hollowmere did not trade in — but obliquely, in the way of people describing a river by describing what floats on it.

She told me about the year the well went dry and the vicar of that time walked out to it with a lantern and came back without the lantern and without his voice, and how the well was full the next morning. She told me about the drought of 1976, when every farm within ten miles was brown as paper and Hollowmere's fields stayed green, and how the neighboring villages stopped speaking of it because speaking of it meant thinking about why. She told me that the water was sweetest the night it rose, and that the old families drank first, always, and that the order in which they drank had never once changed in living memory.

"Which families?" I asked.

"Yours was one," she said, and drank her tea at last, slowly. "Before your grandmother. The Vosses drank deep for a hundred years, child. Prosperity sits in a family like silt. You don't see it building. You just notice, one day, that everyone else's well has run dry and yours hasn't."

"And the cost?"

"The water remembers what it's owed." She said it the way you'd recite a creed — flat, complete, closed. "That's all anyone needs to know. That's all most people *want* to know. The rest is arrangement."

"Arrangement," I said. "Of what?"

She rose, unhurried, and went to the window — the one with twelve panes and the wet stones on the sill — and stood looking out toward the lane, toward the dip in the land beyond the roofs where I knew the well stood, ringed in old stone, with its wooden cover and its iron cup on a chain.

"Near the equinox," she said. "Night of the refill. You'll hear it, if you listen. The village will be indoors with the doors locked and the lamps lit, and nobody will tell you why, because telling is half of inviting, and nobody invites anybody anymore. We learned that lesson." A pause. "Mostly."

"Maren." I leaned forward. "What happens on the night of the refill?"

She turned from the window. And for a moment — one unguarded moment, quickly shuttered — her face did something I had never seen an informant's face do in eleven years of fieldwork. It looked at me with pity. Genuine, specific, terrible pity, the pity you aim at someone standing on ice and hearing it begin to talk.

"You'll want to go home before then," she said gently. "Back to the city, back to your university, back to your drawers full of explanations. Take the songs you've got. They're good songs. Write your paper. And don't come back, Cyrus Voss. Your grandmother made her choices, and the debt followed her, and when she died—" her voice caught, actually caught, on something that was not grief for the dead woman but fear *of* her — "when she died, the debt didn't die with her. Debts like that pass down. Like houses. Like eyes."

I looked at the bowl of water between us.

It had rippled. Nothing had touched it. No draft, no footfall, no truck on the lane — the surface simply trembled, fine concentric rings spreading from the center outward, as if something far below the table, far below the floor, far below the earth itself, had exhaled.

I recorded it. God help me, that was my first instinct — I angled the microphone toward the bowl and let the machine drink in thirty seconds of silence and ringing water, and Maren watched me do it with that pitying look, and neither of us spoke until the rings stilled.

"You're your grandmother's blood," she said quietly, "right down to the bone. She recorded things too, you know. In her journals. Names."

The word went through me like cold water through cloth.

"She kept lists," Maren said. "In that house of hers. In the desk in the front room, if it's still there. And if you're the person I think you are, you'll read them. And if you're the person I'm afraid you are, you'll wish you hadn't, and you'll read them anyway."

She showed me to the door. On the threshold, in the last of the afternoon light, she pressed something into my hand — not a keepsake, nothing sentimental. A single smooth stone from the windowsill, still wet, cool as a coin from a riverbed.

"For the doorstep of wherever you sleep," she said. "Put it there tonight. Don't ask me why. Asking is half of inviting."

The blue door closed. I stood on her swept step holding a wet stone, and behind me, down the lane, in the gathering dusk, I could just see the wooden cover of the well, and — I am certain of this, as certain as I have ever been of anything — certain that the iron cup on its chain was turning. Slowly. With no wind.

I walked back to the cottage the long way, avoiding the well, telling myself the whole time that I was simply choosing a scenic route.

That night I set the stone on my doorstep, because I am a folklorist and I collect behaviors even when I don't believe them, and I told myself that was the reason.

Then I sat up late with my recordings, cataloguing, filing, drawing the drawer shut — and found myself instead playing back the thirty seconds of the trembling bowl, over and over, listening beneath the silence for whatever had breathed.

And beneath it — faint, at the very floor of the recording, where no microphone should reach — I heard humming. A child's tune. The same fragment Maren herself had faltered over two days ago, gone pale, and refused to finish.

I did not sleep. In the morning, I went to my grandmother's house, and I opened the desk in the front room, and I began to read her journals.
