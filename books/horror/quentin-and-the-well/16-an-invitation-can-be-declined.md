## 16. An Invitation Can Be Declined

The dead bird was still on the step in the morning.

Cyrus had not moved it overnight. They had watched it through the window instead, through the long hours when the lanterns of the village moved past the cottage like a slow procession of fireflies that had lost their way — three passes at midnight, two more before two o'clock, and once, near dawn, a figure who stood across the lane for so long that Cyrus's tea went cold twice before he finally walked on. The bird lay on its back on the grey flagstone, wings spread in an attitude of almost comic surrender, its small breast already busy with ants. A wren. Something had broken its neck with great care and laid it out neatly, head toward the door, like a message pinned to a board.

*We know you're asking.*

Cyrus opened the door and stepped over it. They did not bury it. Burying it would have been a kind of answer, and they were done answering questions they hadn't been asked properly.

Quentin was sitting on the porch rail when they came out, legs swinging, watching the lane with an expression Cyrus had never seen on him before. In childhood, Quentin's face had been a weather vane for Cyrus's own moods — delighted when they were delighted, solemn when the noises came. Now his face belonged to himself, and what it held was something old and watchful and very tired.

"You didn't sleep," Cyrus said.

"I don't." He said it simply, without apology. "I keep watch. That's most of what I am, when you get down to it."

"Is that what you were doing last night? Keeping watch?"

"The whole night." He turned to look at them, and his eyes caught the early light in a way that made Cyrus think of the well — of water at dusk, black and patient. "They won't come in while I'm here. They're afraid of me. Not because I'm strong." A small, terrible smile. "Because I'm *theirs*. I'm the part of Hollowmere that never learned to forget, and forgetting is the only thing standing between them and everything they've signed their names to."

Cyrus sat down on the top step, deliberately, where the dead wren could see them both. "Then tell me," they said. "You've been waiting twenty years to tell me something. You said you'd been waiting for me to keep a promise. Fine. But there's something else, isn't there? There's a reason you came back now, a reason you've been humming outside my window and standing in doorways like a bad conscience. You know how this ends. You know what the rules are."

Quentin was quiet for a long time. Down the lane, smoke rose from Maren's chimney in a thin, wavering line, and Cyrus thought: *she is dying by inches in that house, and she gave me her sister's name, and I haven't even thanked her properly.* The night before refill. The village would be lighting its lanterns tonight. Salting its thresholds. Locking its doors against things that had never once, in two hundred years, needed a door to be open.

"You asked me once," Quentin said at last, "why I never got older."

"I remember."

"You stopped asking around your ninth birthday. Do you know why children stop asking? Because they start to understand that some questions are doors, and behind some doors are rooms they can't come back from." He hopped down from the rail and crouched in front of Cyrus, close enough that Cyrus could see that his eyes were not quite any color at all — that they shifted, faintly, like light through moving water. "So I'll ask it for you. Why doesn't Quentin get older? Because Quentin isn't a child, Cyrus. Quentin is a *rule* wearing a shape you'd trust. I'm the part of the well that learned to be loved instead of feared, a long time ago, by a child who needed a friend more than they needed the truth. And rules don't age. Rules just wait."

The morning was cold enough to see breath. Cyrus's hung in the air between them, and Quentin's did not.

"And the rule," Cyrus said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "The one you're wearing. Say it."

"A name given freely," Quentin said, "can be freely withdrawn."

The words landed in Cyrus's chest like stones dropped into still water. They had spent twelve years building rational explanations out of wet footsteps and scratching walls; they had spent two weeks dismantling them again, plank by plank, until what remained was a woman's journals full of crossed-out names and a ledger in a parish chest with Edith Voss's signature beside a child's. Every piece of it had been shaped like a trap. This was the first thing that had ever sounded like a door.

"That's it?" Cyrus said. "That's all? Just — withdraw it?"

"No." Quentin held up one finger, and his hand, Cyrus noticed, cast no shadow in the slanting sun. "Three conditions, and each one is a wall. First: only the giver's blood line may speak the withdrawal. Edith gave the invitation, so it must be a Voss. It must be *you*, Cyrus. No one else's voice will be heard. Second: it must be sung, not said. Spoken words are bargaining, and the well does not bargain. Sung words are testimony, and the well cannot refuse testimony — it is, when you strip away the water and the dark, nothing but a ledger itself. An accounting. It must balance. Third—" and here Quentin's voice dropped, and the morning seemed to lean in to hear it, "—it must be sung at the well, aloud, before dawn. Before the refill completes. After the water rises, the accounts close, and whatever is written stays written for another generation."

Cyrus looked down the lane toward the center of the village, though the well itself was hidden behind cottages and the old yew. Somewhere under that stone lip, Ivy Hale's voice was singing a song no one sang twice, small and patient, keeping time with water that remembered what it was owed.

"Edith tried to spare me," Cyrus said slowly, working it through. "She offered a name to buy mine back. But the well took it as an invitation deferred — a promise of payment later. That's why the noises followed us. That's why we moved eleven times in twelve years and the scratching always found the new walls. She never withdrew anything. She just... rescheduled."

"She thought she was saving you," Quentin said gently. "That's the cruelest trick of it. The well prefers gifts that look like love. A name given out of fear is grudging, and grudging gifts sour. But a grandmother giving up a neighbor's daughter to save her own blood — the well finds that *nourishing*. It's why the prosperous families prosper. They've all learned to dress their debts as devotion."

"And if I sing the withdrawal — if I void Edith's deferred invitation — then what happens to the rest?"

Quentin was silent.

"What happens to the rest, Quentin?"

"The withdrawal only voids what Edith signed," he said. "Yours. Your debt dies with the song. But the ledger has other pages. Other signatures. The Hales' neighbors. Dev's grandfather. Names going back two centuries, every one given freely, every one still owed." His eyes were very steady. "And the well will still need to fill tonight. It always fills. If you take yourself off the account, it balances elsewhere."

Cyrus stood up. Their knees ached — they were thirty-two years old and their knees ached, and somewhere in the village an old woman was dying with her sister's name folded in a drawer, and somewhere under the ground a drowned girl was waiting to find out whether anyone would ever say her name out loud except as an apology.

"Good," Cyrus said.

Quentin tilted his head. "Good?"

"Good. Let it balance elsewhere. Let it balance on the people who wrote the entries." They heard their own voice and were startled by it — not loud, not shaking, just flat and certain, like a signature. "I spent last night listening to lanterns pass my window and deciding whether I was afraid. And I kept arriving at the same answer, which is that fear is a currency, and this village has been paying in it for two hundred years, and I am *done* being legal tender. Maren gave me her sister's name. Ivy has been singing alone in the dark since before I was born. My grandmother traded a child for me and called it love, and the least I owe either of those girls is the truth, sung, at the place where they were spent."

"It might not work," Quentin said. "The song has to be complete. Every invited name, in order, back to the beginning. Miss one, and the testimony is flawed, and flawed testimony the well can refuse. And they'll try to stop you. Tonight, at the well, they'll bring ropes and hymnals and polite faces, and they will believe — because they've always believed — that the refill wants a Voss, and that handing them one is just good husbandry."

"Then I'd better learn the whole song."

"You already know most of it." Quentin smiled, and for a moment the smile was nine years old again, gap-toothed and crooked, the smile that had shared Cyrus's tree forts and their fevers and their terror of the scratching in the walls. "You've been transcribing it for weeks. Every song they'd only sing once. Every fragment Maren hummed before she went pale. You thought you were collecting folklore." He shook his head. "You were collecting evidence. The well's own accounting, scattered through the village like torn pages, sung once apiece so no one would ever hear it whole. You're the first person in two hundred years to hold all of it at the same time."

Cyrus thought of the wax cylinders and the tape reels stacked in the parlor. Of Mrs. Aldous singing the threshing song with her eyes fixed on the middle distance. Of the choir master who stopped mid-verse and laughed and said *funny, I don't recall the rest.* Of Maren's hum breaking off like a snapped thread.

"They weren't refusing repeats," Cyrus said. "They were keeping the song dismembered. As long as no one ever hears it whole, no one can sing it whole. No one can testify."

"Now you understand the village," Quentin said. "It isn't wicked, mostly. It's *careful*. Two hundred years careful. Careful people, locking their doors, salting their steps, teaching their children songs with holes in them." He looked down the lane, toward the yew and the hidden well, and his face went old again. "Careful is how it eats. Fear keeps them feeding it, and politeness keeps them from comparing notes, and once a generation the water rises and everyone agrees, without ever saying it aloud, that it's cheaper than the alternative."

* * *

Cyrus worked through the afternoon with the curtains drawn and the door barred, though both gestures felt ceremonial rather than practical. The tapes were laid out in chronological order of recording, and beside them the notebooks, and beside those Edith's journals open to the pages of crossed-out names, and the transcription of the parish register with its terrible column of years ending the year of Cyrus's birth. Piece by piece, the song assembled itself like a body being identified.

It was not one melody. That was the first thing they understood clearly, and it explained everything — why no villager could repeat a verse, why the fragments refused to sit together in memory. The song was a round built of pieces the village had deliberately scattered: a line hidden in the threshing song, a phrase inside a christening hymn, four bars that only Maren knew and would only hum once. Sung separately, each piece was harmless, domestic, forgettable. Sung in sequence, in order of the names, they became something else entirely. They became a list. They became a roll call of the drowned, set to music, going back to a girl named Sorrel in 1789 whose entry in the ledger was written in a hand so old it had nearly returned to ink.

Cyrus sang it through alone, quietly, twice, checking the names against the register. Nothing happened. The teacups did not rattle. The walls did not scratch. Outside, the afternoon went on being an ordinary gray afternoon, and Cyrus understood that too: the song had no power of its own. It was only true. Its power began where the water could hear it.

Quentin sat in the corner the whole time, cross-legged on the floor like a child waiting out a church service, and when Cyrus faltered on the fourth verse — Ivy's verse, the newest, the one whose melody lived only in the second voice beneath his own — he hummed it until Cyrus caught it.

"How do you know it?" Cyrus asked. "Her part."

"I was there," Quentin said. "Not at the well. In the house. Ivy Hale invented me first, you know. Forty years before you were born. A lonely girl with a well at the end of her garden." His voice did something complicated. "I'm not only your friend, Cyrus. I'm hers. Or I was. What's left of her doesn't need a friend anymore. It needs a witness."

* * *

At dusk, Cyrus went to see Maren.

The old woman's house smelled of camphor and cooling tea, and she had propped herself in her chair by the window with a blanket over her knees like a fortification. She looked smaller than she had a week ago, as if the approaching refill were drawing her down along with the water table. On the table beside her lay the torn journal page, weighted under a thimble, waiting.

"You've worked it out," she said, before Cyrus had spoken. "I can see it in how you walk. People walk differently when they've decided something."

"I'm going to sing the withdrawal tonight. Before dawn. At the well."

Maren closed her eyes. For a moment Cyrus thought she had drifted off, but her lips were moving — counting, perhaps, or praying to something that had never answered her. When she opened her eyes they were wet and furious.

"I was eleven," she said. "I stood behind my mother's skirt in the churchyard and watched Edith Voss sign, and I heard the minister read the words — *freely given, freely witnessed* — and I went home and ate supper and never said a word for sixty-three years. Sixty-three years, and my sister's name in the water the whole time, and me humming half-songs so I'd never accidentally learn the whole one." Her hand found the page and pushed it across the table. "Take it. Sing her name. Sing them all. And when the water takes the ones who signed — because it will, child, the well is not merciful, it is only *accurate* — you make sure the recordings survive. Make sure someone outside Hollowmere hears it. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Don't promise me." Maren's grip, for a dying woman, was astonishing. "Promise *her*. Promise Ivy. Promises to the dead are the only ones the well respects."

Outside, the village was lighting its lanterns. One by one, down the lanes, windows bloomed yellow, and behind each lit window Cyrus could feel the careful people preparing — salt on sills, hymnals by the door, ropes coiled in sheds. They would come for Cyrus at midnight. They believed the water wanted a Voss, and they had always been willing to pay in other people's children, but a Voss offered freely would be so much tidier than a Voss taken.

Let them come, Cyrus thought, walking home through the blue cold with the torn page inside their coat, over their heart. Let them bring their ropes and their hymnals. They had forgotten — or had never known, because knowing was the one expense the village had never been able to afford — that an invitation ran both directions.

A name given freely could be freely withdrawn.

And Cyrus Voss had a song to sing, and for the first time in thirty-two years, every word of it was theirs.

Behind them, unseen, Quentin fell into step — and ahead, beneath the yew, beneath the stone lip, the water had begun, very softly, to rise.
