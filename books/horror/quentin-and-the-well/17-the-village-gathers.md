## 17. The Village Gathers

They came for me at midnight, and they brought hymnals.

I knew it before I heard the first footstep, because Quentin stopped humming. He had been at the window since dusk — he had been at the window every night since Maren's confession, a pale shape folded into the corner of the glass like frost given posture — and his silence was louder than any sound the village could have made. When Quentin went quiet, Hollowmere was about to speak.

"They're coming," I said. My voice sounded strange in my own kitchen, flat and small, as if the room had already begun to grieve me.

Quentin turned his head. Twenty years unchanged: the cowlick that never lay down, the gap in his smile, the eyes like water over stone. But something in him had aged tonight, or perhaps only settled, the way sediment settles when the current stops.

"You could go out the back," he said. "Before they finish lighting the lanterns."

"And be where? On the moor road at midnight with no car and no voice left to argue with?" I looked down at the table where I'd laid everything out with the neatness of someone arranging their own funeral: Edith's journals, the torn page Maren had pressed into my hands two days ago with her sister's name on it, the ledger's photograph Dev had taken before burning his copy, my recorder, spare batteries, and the notebook where I had copied out the forbidden song in my own careful hand, verse by verse, the way you'd transcribe a will.

The song no one sings twice. Except I intended to sing it once more than anyone dared, and then to sing it again, because that was the whole point of songs — that they survive their singers.

Quentin crossed the kitchen without touching the floor. He did that sometimes; I had stopped cataloguing it the way you stop counting your own heartbeat. He stood beside me at the table and looked at the notebook.

"Your grandmother wrote her name smaller than anyone else's," he said. "In the ledger. Did you notice?"

"I noticed."

"She was ashamed of how big the other names were." He touched the page — or almost touched it; his finger hovered a hair's breadth above the ink, the way he'd hovered above everything his whole existence, present but never permitted weight. "Ashamed people write small."

Outside, the first lanterns bloomed between the houses, orange and deliberate, moving toward the square in a procession that wanted very much not to look like a procession. I counted them through the window. Eleven lanterns. Eleven households still willing to walk in the dark toward a well that had been whispering my name for three days. I knew it had been whispering my name because the walls told me so — the same walls that had scratched and dripped and breathed for twelve years of my childhood, twelve years of noises my mother explained away as pipes, as mice, as the house settling, while the house settled around the fact of a promise my grandmother had made and the well had refused to forget.

*An invitation deferred is an invitation kept warm,* Maren had said, the last time she spoke above a whisper. *The water doesn't mind waiting. Water is nothing but patience with somewhere to be.*

I picked up the recorder. Checked the batteries. Checked them again, because ritual is what courage looks like when it's frightened.

"Stay close," I told Quentin.

"I was always going to stay close." The gap-toothed smile. "That's the whole of what I am, Cyrus. I'm the part of you that stayed."

* * *

The square was full when we reached it, and it was full the way churches are full — everyone in their best coats, everyone arranged by some invisible order of precedence, the prosperous families nearest the well and the poorer ones standing back as though proximity to the water were a privilege rather than a sentence. Lanterns hung from the iron railings that had been erected around the well sometime in the last century, railings meant to look decorative and functioning instead as a fence keeps anything in. Or out. I had never decided which, and standing there at midnight with the whole village breathing around me, I understood that no one else had ever decided either.

The well itself was lower than it should have been. That was the thing nobody said aloud but everybody glanced at: the waterline had dropped all week, inch by inch, the way a held breath drops in the chest. Refill was coming. The water was making room for whatever it intended to take in.

Dev stood near the railing, and when our eyes met he flinched. He had ash on his cuff — the ghost of his grandfather's ledger, burned in his own hearth two nights ago while begging me to leave. Now he stood among them with a hymnal clutched to his chest like a breastplate, and I understood with a clarity that felt like cold water poured down my spine: he hadn't burned it to protect me. He'd burned it because if the evidence was gone, the guilt might be too, and if the guilt was gone, he could stand here tonight and do this.

"Cyrus." It was Aldous Crane who spoke, Aldous whose family had signed more invitations than any other in the ledger, Aldous whose orchards had never failed in two hundred years of blights that killed every tree in the next valley. He wore his good wool coat and his face wore its practiced smile, the one I'd been collecting from doorways for weeks. "You got our note, then."

"There wasn't any note."

"The note was the quiet." He gestured gently, terribly, at the empty lane behind my cottage. "We noticed you didn't take it."

Hymnals opened around the square with a sound like wings. I recognized the book — the parish hymnal, the one in every pew, except these copies fell open to a page that wasn't in any pew copy I'd ever seen, a page sewn in after printing, the ink brownish and old. And the song on that page — I knew it. God help me, I knew it. It was the song Maren had hummed four bars of before going grey as dishwater. It was the melody that had lived under my childhood floors. The accounting. The well's own bookkeeping set to music, sung once a generation by everyone who owed it, so that the debt would be remembered in the shape of a tune even after the names were forgotten.

They began to sing.

It was not a good sound. Hymns are not supposed to sound like this — like a rope being paid out slowly, like something being lowered. The villagers' voices wavered and blended and found a harmony that no human throat should have found, a harmony with a second voice underneath it, and I felt my skin try to leave my body. Because I knew that under-voice. I'd heard it at the well at dusk, small and patient. Ivy's voice, or what the well kept of Ivy, singing along beneath the living the way a root sings beneath a tree.

Quentin's hand closed around mine. Cold, but a familiar cold now, the cold of a hand I'd held crossing a field at seven years old.

"They think you're the offering," he murmured. "They've thought so since you stepped off the bus. A Voss comes home after twelve years, right before refill, asking questions. They didn't need a note, Cyrus. They needed only to wait. Waiting is the one thing they've always been good at."

Ropes. I saw them then, coiled in the barrow by the railing, three lengths of good hemp, and beside them a folding chair, and beside that a board laid across the well's mouth with a rope threaded through its center — the old apparatus, older than the railings, the mechanism by which a bucket became unnecessary. I had photographed it my first week here and labeled it in my notes: *well pulley, disused.* Disused. Everything in Hollowmere was disused right up until the moment it was used.

Aldous raised one hand and the singing stopped. The silence afterward had texture, had weight, pressed against my eardrums like deep water.

"Cyrus Voss," he said, and his voice took on the cadence of liturgy, words worn smooth by generations of mouths. "Child of Hollowmere. Blood of Edith Voss, who gave freely. You come to us in the season of refill, and the water is low, and the water remembers what it's owed."

"I know what it's owed," I said. "I have the ledger. I have the register. I have Ivy Hale's name in Edith's handwriting, and I have Maren Coe's confession, witnessed and recorded, and I have—" I lifted the recorder like a relic, like a lantern of my own, "—every word any of you have said to me since I arrived. This is being recorded. All of it. Whatever happens tonight goes out of this village in my bag tomorrow, or it goes out of this village in a hundred copies, or it doesn't go out at all because none of you will be here to stop it. But it does not stay buried. Not this time. Not like the others."

Something moved through the crowd — not doubt exactly. Doubt would have been mercy. It was more like the shudder of a body deciding whether to be sick.

"You don't understand what you carry," said a woman near the front — Mrs. Tallow, whose bread I had bought, whose kitchen I had sat in. Her hymnal trembled. "You don't understand what happens to a place that lets the well run dry. My grandmother saw the year they tried to refuse. Nineteen and eleven. There was no rain and no milk and the children dreamed of drowning in their beds until the elders went back on their knees. The well isn't a hole in the ground, girl. It's a mouth, and mouths must be fed, and better it eats one name freely given than a whole village slowly."

"One name," I repeated. "Freely given." I walked forward — I don't remember deciding to; my legs simply committed treason against my fear — until I stood at the railing with the black dropped waterline below me and the whole village at my back. "Then let's talk about free. Let's talk about Ivy Hale, nine years old, whose name appears in a ledger with my grandmother's signature beside it. Was hers freely given? She was nine. Can a nine-year-old give what she doesn't understand? And let's talk about the others — forty-one names, forty-one children across two centuries, each one 'freely given' by a neighbor, a godparent, a grandmother, always by someone who prospered after. That's not sacrifice. That's commerce. You built your orchards and your clean billings and your unbroken luck on a currency of other people's children, and you dressed it in hymns so you'd never have to call it what it is."

"Hold your tongue," someone hissed, and the ropes in the barrow shifted as a man's hand closed on them.

"No," I said. "That's the one thing I won't do. Not anymore. I spent twelve years holding my tongue — twelve years of scratching in the walls and wet footsteps on stairs and my mother telling me it was the pipes, and do you know what the worst part was? Not the noises. The noises I could have survived. It was being told I hadn't heard them. Being made to doubt my own ears, my own nights, my own self. You did that. All of you, with your polite doors and your practiced smiles. You fed a child to the water and then let the child who was spared grow up thinking she was mad."

The water below me moved. Not rippled — moved, the way a sleeper turns, slow and total, the whole surface rotating once in the dark. The under-voice rose faintly through the stones, and this time I caught the words, and the words were my name, sung patiently, in the tune of the forbidden song.

"She's calling you," Aldous said softly, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was the most horrifying thing yet. "She's been calling you since you came back. The deferred invitation, Cyrus. Your grandmother offered a name to save yours, and the water accepted the offer and waited, and waited, and now the offer has come due, and you're standing at the rail of your own accord. Freely given." He spread his hands. "No ropes needed, unless you make it hard. We hoped — we all hoped — you'd understand your part. Your grandmother did. She understood, and she loved you enough to pay, and this is how a village survives, child. This is how it has always survived."

"This is how it has always *fed*," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference has a name, and her name was Ivy."

And I opened the notebook.

The crowd saw the motion and misread it — someone shouted, the ropes came out of the barrow, Dev cried out something that might have been my name or might have been a warning — but I wasn't running. I stood at the railing with the black listening water below me and the whole complicit weight of Hollowmere pressing at my back, and I did the thing no one in two hundred years had dared to do twice.

I began to sing.

* * *

My voice cracked on the first line. I am a folklorist, not a singer; I have recorded ten thousand verses and performed none. But the song didn't seem to care about quality. It cared about accuracy. Each verse was a name — that was the structure Maren's four bars had hinted at, the architecture the hymnals had tried to bury under harmony: a name, a date, a giver. *Given by* — and the giver's name too, sung equal to the child's, so that the debt attached where it belonged. The well's own accounting. I sang the first name from the ledger, 1798, a boy called Tam Osgood, given by his uncle, and the uncle's name rang out into the dark and the water below me shivered.

I sang the second. The third. The crowd's shouting faltered, because you cannot shout over a thing you were raised to fear, and every soul in that square had been raised on the terror of this exact song. Mothers had hushed children for humming it. I watched Mrs. Tallow's mouth form the words *stop, stop* without any sound coming out. I watched the ropes go slack in men's hands, because the hands holding them had begun to shake.

By the tenth verse my voice had steadied. By the twentieth, another voice had joined mine — thin, high, rising up through the well stones, Ivy's voice finding the harmony from underneath, and the duet of it was the most beautiful and terrible sound I have ever made. The lanterns guttered. The water rose — visibly, inch over inch, climbing the old flood-marks inside the shaft, black and gleaming and attentive, a congregation of its own.

And beside me, untouched by the crowd that could no longer see him, Quentin lifted his voice too — the third thread, the one that stayed — and the last verse of the song was a name I knew better than my own.

Edith Voss. Given by Edith Voss. Deferred, deferred, deferred.

I drew breath for the withdrawal.

Aldous Crane's hand closed on my shoulder, and the ropes came alive in the torchlight, and the village of Hollowmere finally showed me its true face — not polite, not grieving, not afraid for me, but afraid *of* me, and willing, at the last, to do what it had always done to keep its luck.

I kept singing.
