## 18. The Song No One Sings Twice

The ropes came first.

I saw them in the lantern light as the villagers assembled around the well — coils of good hemp, the kind used for hauling buckets, though no bucket had touched that water in my lifetime. They carried them the way you carry a thing you hope not to use: loosely, apologetically, with eyes averted. Behind them came the hymnals, small black books clutched to chests like shields. I recognized the hymnal from my grandmother's shelf. *Songs of the Hollow*, printed 1911, its spine cracked at the same page in every copy — the one song no one would sing twice for me, the one Maren had hummed a fragment of before going pale as milk.

They had come to renew the invitations. And they had come, some of them, hoping I would be what the water took instead.

Quentin stood beside me at the well's edge. He had not faded. If anything, the darkness coming off the water had thickened him, given him edges like cut paper. He was watching the crowd with an expression I had never seen on his face in thirty years of memory — not mischief, not patience, but something older and sadder. Recognition. He knew these people. He had been invented in this village; perhaps he had been invented *by* it, or by whatever slept beneath it wearing a child's shape.

"They brought ropes," I said quietly.

"They always do," Quentin said. "Last time it was chains. The time before, they just held hands."

I looked at him. "You've seen refills before."

"I've seen everything," he said. "That's what I'm for."

Dev stood at the edge of the crowd, ash still ghosting his sleeves where he'd burned the ledger copy. He would not meet my eye. His hands were shaking. I thought: *he warned me, in his way. He burned what he could burn.* It was more than most of them would do tonight.

Maren was not there. She had died at dusk, or near enough to dusk that it hardly mattered which — her last breath spent pressing the torn journal page into my hand, her sister's name written in Edith's careful schoolroom script: *Ruth Aldous, invited freely, witnessed.* Maren had gone out with the guilt finally off her chest and the name finally spoken, and I had sat with her body for ten minutes because no one else in Hollowmere would touch her until the refill was done. Old superstition. Old cowardice. The same thing, mostly.

So I stood alone at the well with a dead woman's page in my pocket and a dead child's friend at my shoulder, and forty villagers closing around me like a hand.

Old Hollis stepped forward. He was the oldest man in Hollowmere, prosperous the way all the old names were prosperous, and he wore his best coat for the occasion. "Cyrus," he said gently, the way you speak to someone at a funeral. "You shouldn't have stayed. But since you did — the water's owed, and it'll take what's nearest. That's just how it is. No one here wants it to be you."

"No one here wants it to be anyone," said a woman behind him, "but it has to be somebody."

"It doesn't," I said.

The words came out steadier than I felt. Somewhere under my ribs my heart was going like a bird against glass. But my voice held, and I watched it land on them — the small flinch, the way they looked at each other. Forty years of practice smiles, and every one of them slipped at once.

"The well takes what's invited," I said. "Freely given. That's the rule. Your rule. You wrote it down for two hundred years." I pulled the ledger from my bag — Dev's ledger, the meeting book, rescued from the floor of the old chapel where it had hidden behind loose plaster since before either of us was born. "I've read every page. And I know whose signatures are on it."

Hollis's face went through three expressions in a second: blankness, calculation, fear. "Girl—"

"Person," I said. "Get it right, if nothing else. You're about to ask me to die for you."

The lanterns guttered. Not wind — there was no wind. The flames leaned toward the well, all of them together, like flowers toward a sun that wasn't in the sky.

And the water began to rise.

* * *

It rose without sound at first. Black water climbing the old stone throat of the well, past the moss line, past the highest mark any drought had ever left, up and up until it stood a hand's breadth below the rim and did not spill. It did not move like water. It moved like something listening through a keyhole.

The villagers backed away as one body. Even Hollis. Especially Hollis.

I understood then what the rising meant. It wasn't hunger. Hunger would have been simpler. It was *attention*. Two centuries of invitations had taught the thing below to wait for the formalities — the name, the witness, the signature — and it had risen to hear whether the formalities would be observed.

They would not.

I opened my mouth to sing.

Here is what no one in Hollowmere would tell me, and what I had to learn from a torn page and a dead woman's confession and a friend who was never imaginary: the song in the black hymnals was not a hymn. It was an accounting. The well's own ledger set to melody, every invited name threaded into the verse like beads on a wire, and the reason no one would sing it twice was not that singing it once was dangerous.

It was that singing it once was *enough*. Once sung aloud, complete, the names belonged to the air. To everyone. To history. There was no taking them back into silence after that, and silence was the only thing the prosperous families had ever purchased with their signatures.

Edith had known. Edith had sung fragments of it to me as a lullaby, garbled, the names worn smooth — and I had carried it for thirty years without knowing I was carrying a list of the dead.

I sang it whole.

* * *

The first verse was Ivy.

*Ivy Hale, aged nine, invited freely, witnessed by the parish, taken at the refill of nineteen sixty-one.* The melody was simple, almost sweet — a skipping-rope rhythm, the kind children make up without knowing they're making anything up. That was the obscenity of it. The well had set its own murders to a tune a child could skip to.

The villagers heard the name and made sounds. Small ones. A woman dropped her hymnal and did not pick it up.

The second verse was Ruth Aldous, Maren's sister, 1923. Then Thomas Wren, 1889. Then a run of names from the ledger's deep pages, names I had copied out by lantern light while Quentin stood watch: *Alice Fen, the twins Sorrel and Sage Marsh, Peter Loxley, Annis Hale* — another Hale, a generation before Ivy, and I saw a gray-haired man in the crowd go white and grip his son's arm, because he had prospered too.

I sang them all. My voice cracked on the fourth verse and I let it crack. This was not performance. This was testimony, and testimony does not need to be beautiful. It needs only to be true, and spoken, and not stopped.

Quentin sang with me. Not the words — he hummed the melody underneath, low and steady, holding the pitch when my breath failed, the way he'd held my hand crossing the dark fields when we were small. He knew the song better than I did. Of course he did. He had been there for every verse.

By the eighth name, half the villagers were on their knees. Not praying — kneeling the way people kneel when the ground itself seems to demand humility. The lanterns had gone strange, their light bending inward toward the well, and the black water at the rim had begun to move in slow concentric rings, each ring widening, each one keeping time.

With the song. With my voice. With the names.

"Stop," Hollis said. His voice came out ruined. "Stop singing, girl, you don't know what you're—"

"I know exactly what I'm doing," I said, and sang the ninth name.

* * *

Here is what happened to the villagers, and I record it plainly because plainly is all I have ever been able to manage:

As each name left my mouth, it went looking for its owner.

Not the children — the children were long gone, kept in the water, kept as voices under other voices. The names went looking for the *signatures*. For the living hands that had given them. Alice Fen's name found the Fen descendants standing pale in the crowd and settled over them like frost. Peter Loxley's name found the Loxley farm lights burning comfortable on the hill above the village, and I swear the lights flickered, all of them, in rhythm with the melody.

The debt was being read aloud. All of it. At once. In front of everyone.

"You gave them freely," I said between verses, and my voice carried in the dead-still air. "That was the rule you loved, because it made it clean. Freely given. Witnessed. Signed. Well — hear them now. Freely named. Witnessed by all of you. Let anyone in Hollowmere say tomorrow they didn't know."

A young woman — a mother, I realized, with a child on her hip — began to sob. "We didn't sign anything. We weren't born. It wasn't us—"

"No," I agreed. "It wasn't you. But you kept the doors polite. You sang each song once and smiled. You raised your daughters on stories about strangers who ask too many questions. You knew the water was owed and you never asked *owed by whom, and to whom, and why is it always a child.*" I drew breath. "Silence is a signature too. It's just the one nobody has to wash ink off their hands for."

The water rose another inch. It stood above the rim now, held there by nothing, a black lens trembling over the stone, and in it — reflected upside down — I could see faces. Small ones. Dozens. Looking up at me from inside the reflection, mouths moving, singing along.

Ivy was at the front.

She was singing my grandmother's lullaby back to me — the garbled version, the one Edith had fed me across a pillow, the version with the names worn smooth. Except she sang it unsmoothed. Every name sharp. Every year exact. She had been waiting under the water for sixty years for someone to sing it right, and she was correcting me gently, patiently, the way you correct a child learning a song, and I followed her corrections, and we sang the last verses together, the living voice and the kept one, and the whole village of Hollowmere knelt in the lantern light and listened to what they had bought their prosperity with.

The final verse was not a name.

The final verse was a question. The well's own accounting ended in a question — I hadn't known that until Quentin's humming bent the melody downward, unresolved, and Ivy's voice in the water showed me the words that the hymnals had never printed:

*Who gives? Who owes? Who keeps the count?*

And the answer, the answer the well had waited two hundred years to hear spoken aloud by someone with nothing left to lose:

*"The ones who signed."*

* * *

The black water turned.

There is no other word for it. The lens above the rim rotated — slowly, ponderously, like a great eye adjusting its gaze — and the reflections in it changed. The small faces sank away. What rose in their place were the faces of the living, caught in the black mirror: Hollis, the Hales, the Fens, the Loxleys, every family whose name marched down the ledger's margin in confident ink, generation after generation, *witnessed, witnessed, witnessed.*

They saw themselves in the water. All of them at once. And the water saw them back.

Hollis screamed. I will write that plainly too, because I have never been able to decide whether it was justice or horror, and maybe it was both, and maybe both is the only honest account anyone will ever give of that night. He screamed and the others screamed and the ropes fell from nerveless hands and coiled in the dirt like dying snakes, and the lanterns went out one by one, not blown — *drunk*, the flames pulled down into nothing as if the dark itself had opened its mouth.

Only two lights remained. The moonless sky. And Quentin, who glowed faintly now, faintly and steadily, the way a nightlight glows — a child's invention standing guard over a child's accounting, humming the melody as the water gathered itself to take what the song had named.

I stood at the rim with my recorder running, red light steady, capturing all of it — the screaming, the singing, the turning of the water — because truth unrecorded is just a rumor, and Hollowmere had lived on rumors for two hundred years.

The well rose up.

And in the heart of the rising black, small and clear and terribly patient, Ivy's voice spoke my name — not Cyrus, but the name Edith had whispered over my cradle, the true one, the one I'd never told a living soul —

and I understood, with a cold that started in my teeth, that the accounting was not finished.

One signature remained. One deferred invitation, love-twisted, still open in the well's great book.

Mine.
