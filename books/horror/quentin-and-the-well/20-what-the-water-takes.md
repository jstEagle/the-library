## 20. What the Water Takes

The well did not roar. That was the first thing Cyrus would remember afterward, the thing they would return to in the small hours for the rest of their life: not a roar, but a *settling*, the sound of something enormous turning over in its sleep and finding the bed made to its liking.

The black water stood at the lip of the stone now, trembling without wind, and above it the villagers had gone quiet in a way that was worse than screaming. Ropes lay coiled and abandoned in the mud. Someone's hymnal had fallen open, pages splayed like a shot bird. The prosperous families — the Aldouses, the Cranes, old Pell with his silver-topped cane — stood nearest the rim, and it was on them that the water's attention had turned, if water could be said to attend, if attention could be said to have weight.

Cyrus stood where the song had left them, throat raw, recorder still running in their coat pocket, the little red light blinking like a pulse.

Quentin stood beside them. And Quentin was *thinning*.

It was not dramatic. That was the cruelty of it. No dissolution, no scattering of light. He simply looked less decided than he had on the porch twenty years ago, less argued-for, as though someone were erasing him with the side of a fist rather than the point of an eraser — smudging rather than removing. His outline had gone soft at the shoulders. When he turned his head to look at Cyrus, there was a half-second of lag, as if the image of him had to travel farther to reach the eye.

"You're going," Cyrus said. It was not a question. They had known since the withdrawal began, since the well's attention swung off them like a searchlight finding better prey.

"I was only ever borrowed," Quentin said. His voice was still the voice from the porch, unchanged after twenty years, which meant it was about to stop being anything at all. "You made me out of the noise so you'd have something to walk home with. I'm the noise going home."

"Don't." The word came out smaller than Cyrus intended. Smaller than they had ever let themselves sound in this village, in front of these people, in front of the water. "Don't make it tidy. Don't make it a nice little speech."

Quentin smiled. It was the smile from the summer Cyrus was seven, the one that had gotten them both blamed for the jam. "You promised me once you'd never be bored of me."

"I was eight."

"You kept every other promise you made at that well's edge. Why break the easy one?"

The water moved.

Not toward the villagers — not yet. It rose, a slow column of dark that never broke its surface tension, impossibly tall, and in it Cyrus could see reflections that were not reflections: faces arranged by year, by ledger-line, two centuries of names given freely and sung into the stone. Some of the faces were very old. Some were so young they had not yet learned what their own expressions meant. And near the top, near the place where the moonlight bent around the column and refused to enter, was a small face with a gap between her front teeth, watching Cyrus with the patience of something that had waited thirty years to be asked a single question.

*Ivy.*

Cyrus had named her aloud in the song. All of them, all the names from the torn page and the parish register and Maren's dying confession, each one spoken into the accounting the well kept of itself. But Ivy's name had come last, held longest, and the well had noticed.

"She can't stay either," Quentin said quietly. He had followed Cyrus's eyes. He always could. "Once it's paid, it's paid. She's owed her going same as the guilty are owed theirs."

"Then let me say goodbye."

"You're the folklorist," Quentin said. "Say it properly. Say it so it keeps."

---

The villagers had begun to understand what was happening to them, and their understanding came in stages, the way grief does.

First came Pell, who had signed the ledger three times across four decades and prospered each time, whose orchards had never blighted while his neighbor's rotted. He stepped back from the rim, and the water stepped with him — not following, Cyrus understood, but *arriving*, the way a verdict arrives. The black surface tilted toward him like a listening ear.

"It wasn't me," Pell said. His cane had gone from him; he didn't seem to know when. "I only witnessed. I only *witnessed*—"

"You sang," Cyrus said. Their voice carried strangely in the well-yard, flat and clear, the acoustics of a place built for one purpose. "Maren told me. You sang the invitation when the Aldouse girl was given. You harmonized. She was nine."

Pell's mouth worked. Behind him, his wife made a sound like a hinge.

The second stage was bargaining, and it came all at once, from every direction, a chorus of the prosperous discovering that wealth is not legal tender everywhere. They offered the well land. They offered it the church bell. Old Mrs. Crane, who had fed Cyrus dinner on their second night in Hollowmere with such warmth, such *practiced* warmth, dropped to her knees in the mud and began reciting the ninety-first psalm, and the water rose over her words the way night rises over a lit window — gently, totally, without malice.

Cyrus did not watch them go. Not because they couldn't bear it, but because they had decided, somewhere in the singing, that bearing it was exactly what they owed. These people had watched children go into the water for two hundred years and had called it refill, called it blessing, called it *the way things are*. The least Cyrus could do was watch the way things become otherwise.

But they did see the moment the column of water leaned, and took, and settled — saw the Aldouse patriarch vanish mid-sentence, saw the space where Pell had been fill with a silence that had texture, that had *satisfaction* in it, the way a debt paid has satisfaction. The well drank them the way it had drunk everything: completely, and without hurry, because it had learned from two centuries of this village that no one was ever coming to check.

Some of the willing went too. That was the part Cyrus had not predicted, and it undid something in them that they suspected would never re-knot cleanly. A handful of the older villagers — not signatories, not beneficiaries, just the tired ones, the ones who had spent lifetimes keeping doors politely closed — walked forward on their own feet. One of them, a man Cyrus had recorded singing a harvest song in chapter-thick dialect, paused beside them on his way.

"Fifty-one years I've slept with wool in my ears," he said. "Whatever's down there, it knows my name already. Better it takes me hearing than leaves me pretending." And he stepped past them, and the water received him almost gently, and Cyrus stood with their fists clenched and their heart breaking in a direction they hadn't planned for, because justice was supposed to feel clean, and this felt like weather.

---

Then the water stilled, and the small face surfaced.

She rose through the blackness the way a leaf rises through pond-water, unhurried, unrotted, thirty years old and nine forever. She stopped at the rim of the well, at the exact height of Cyrus's eyes, and the gap-toothed smile was shy, apologetic almost, the smile of a child interrupting adults.

Cyrus knelt. The mud soaked through their trousers and they did not care. This close, they could see that she was not quite solid, not quite present — she was the well's memory of a girl, kept the way the well kept everything, perfectly and uselessly and forever until tonight.

"You're the one who sings twice," Ivy said. Her voice was small and wet and utterly ordinary, a child's voice from a schoolyard, and it destroyed Cyrus more thoroughly than any shriek could have.

"Yes."

"Maren used to hum it wrong on purpose. So I'd know she remembered." Ivy's head tipped, considering. "You sing it right. Nobody sings it right. It hurts too much to sing it right."

"I know," Cyrus said. "I'm sorry it took us thirty years."

Ivy looked at them for a long moment. Then she looked past, to where Quentin stood fading at the edge of the lantern-light, and something passed between the two of them that Cyrus could not follow — some older acquaintance, some shared citizenship of the uncanny. Whatever it was, it seemed to settle something in her.

"Tell him," she said, "that it wasn't cold. Everyone always asks if it was cold, and it wasn't. It was like being remembered." She turned back to Cyrus, and her small face grew grave with an adult weight no child should carry. "Thank you. For saying our names out loud. We could hear them the whole time, under the water. Every year somebody new got put in, and we'd say, *listen, listen, someone's saying the list.*" Her smile came back then, gap-toothed and brief. "Yours was the loudest."

And she went down. Not dragged, not taken — she simply sank, the way a child sinks into sleep, and the water closed over the place where she had been, and the well gave one long exhalation against the stones, and was only water. Dark, deep, ordinary water, filling to its brim, full for another generation.

Full, Cyrus thought, standing alone in the mud with their knees wet and their recorder running, because it had finally been given what it was actually owed. Not names. *Acknowledgment.* Two centuries of children, counted at last.

They were crying. They noted it distantly, the way they noted birdsong, the way they noted everything now, professionally, permanently. The red light blinked. Somewhere behind them a villager was sick into the grass. Dawn was perhaps two hours off.

"Cyrus."

Quentin's voice had changed. Not faded — *nearer*, somehow, as though thinning had brought him closer to whatever he was made of. Cyrus turned.

He stood in the last of the lantern-light, and he was mostly gone. Not invisible — Cyrus could still see the porch behind him through his chest, through the place where a heartbeat would be — but legible, translucent, a boy drawn on tissue paper held up to the moon. His face was still entirely himself.

"Stay till sunrise," Cyrus said. "Please. Just—"

"I can't. The noises are done. That's what I was." He smiled, and the smile wavered like heat-shimmer. "Twelve years of scratching in your walls, and you built me a face and a name and a way to hold your hand when the house creaked. Best thing anyone ever made out of being terrified."

"The promise," Cyrus said. Their voice cracked on it. "At the well's edge. I said I'd never leave you behind. I was eight, but I said it, and you kept count, so—" They swallowed. "I'm keeping it. However that works. I don't know how, but I'm keeping it."

Quentin considered this with the seriousness he had always brought to promises, which was more seriousness than any adult in Hollowmere had ever brought to anything.

"You will," he said. "You're recording everything. That's how you keep it. You keep things by writing them down where other people can find them." He was fading faster now, his hands gone, his edges dissolving into the dark like sugar into tea. "That's why I came back on the porch. Not to scare you. To get written down before the well finished and there was nothing left to write."

"Quentin—"

"One more thing." He was nearly voice now, nearly suggestion, but the grin came through unmistakable, the jam-smile, the accomplice-grin. "When you publish it — and you will, you stubborn, terrible archivist — spell my name right. Q-U-I-N-T-I-N. You always spelled it wrong in your notebooks. Even when I was standing next to you."

And then the lantern-light had nothing in it, and the well-yard was full only of mud and ropes and the breathing of the living, and Cyrus Voss stood alone in the ruins of Hollowmere's oldest arrangement, holding a recorder full of ghosts, laughing and weeping in the same broken breath because even at the end — even *dissolving* — he had gotten in the correction.

The sky in the east had begun, very faintly, to consider the color gray.

Cyrus wiped their face with a muddy sleeve, checked the red light still burning, and started walking toward the house where Edith's journals waited on the table, where the whole true account needed ordering before dawn — and where, on the doorstep they would reach in twenty minutes, something small and pale and folded lay waiting that had not been there when they left.
