## 21. Sunrise

I don't remember deciding to stay at the well. I remember the water going still, and I remember the silence after, and I remember sitting down on the wet stones because my legs had made the decision for me.

The sky was doing that thing it does in the last hour of dark — not lightening so much as admitting that it had never really been black, only very tired. The lanterns the villagers had carried lay scattered around the well's rim like a ring of small dead suns. Some still burned. One had fallen into the water and was floating there, guttering, its flame reflected doubled in the black surface until the water took it with a sound like a swallowed breath.

The well was full.

That was the thing I couldn't stop looking at. After everything — after the song, after the withdrawal, after the water had risen black and listening and then risen *higher* — it stood brimming to the lip, calm as a millpond, holding the whole fading sky in its mouth. It had refilled. It had taken what it was owed. And what it was owed, it turned out, had been standing around it all night with ropes coiled in their hands and hymnals open to pages they'd never really read.

I counted them without wanting to. It was the kind of counting you do at a crash site, involuntary, the mind insisting on its inventory. Nine. Nine empty sets of clothes, nine pairs of shoes — most of them still standing upright where their owners had been, because the water hadn't dragged anyone anywhere. It had simply *collected*. The Hollis brothers, who'd signed the ledger in their father's hand for forty years. The Pells, old Amos and his son. The woman who ran the post office, whose name I never learned because she'd only ever handed me parcels with a smile that closed like a drawer. And at the rim, closest to the water, the Greave family — four of them — who had prospered longest and loudest, and whose signatures ran down the ledger's pages like a spine.

Their shoes were all still there. That detail would come back to me for years. The water had been very tidy about the shoes.

Quentin sat beside me on the stones. He was dimmer. I don't know how else to say it — he was the same shape, the same impossible boy-shaped absence of a boy, but the edges of him had gone soft, the way a photograph goes soft when the light behind it changes. He was humming the accounting song under his breath, the one no one sang twice, except that now everyone in Hollowmere had heard it twice, and the world had not ended, and the well had not risen up to drown the singing out. It had just listened. It had always only ever wanted to listen. That was the horror of it, and the pity, and I was too exhausted to hold both at once, so I set them down on the stones beside me like two heavy bags and let myself, for one moment, feel nothing at all.

"Cyrus," Quentin said. He said it the way he'd said it when I was six — patient, amused, the voice of someone who has all the time in the world and is choosing to spend it on you. "You're bleeding."

I looked at my hands. I'd gripped the well's rim so hard at some point in the night that the old stone had opened my palms, and I hadn't felt it, and now the blood was mixing with the well-water that had splashed up during — during whatever the last hour had been. I watched a drop fall from my finger toward the water and I flinched, some animal part of me still certain that the well took whatever it was given.

The drop hit the surface. The surface accepted it. Nothing else happened.

"It's done," Quentin said. "It's all done now. You can bleed wherever you like."

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, half sob, and I put my face in my ruined hands and laughed and wept into them while the sky went from tired grey to the pale gold that means the night has finally lost its argument.

* * *

Dev found me there when the sun cleared the eastern hedgerows.

He came down the lane from the village slowly, the way you walk toward a thing you're afraid will have changed shape while you weren't looking. He'd burned his copy of the ledger the night before — I'd seen the light of it from my window, the paper curling, the names going up one by one like small dark moths — and he'd begged me to leave, and I'd stayed, and now here he was at sunrise, walking through the aftermath of the choice he'd been too afraid to make, and I watched him understand it with every step.

He stopped at the edge of the ring of lanterns. He looked at the shoes. He took his cap off, which I had never seen him do, not in the schoolroom, not at the harvest fair, not once in all the years I'd known him.

"Nine," he said.

"Nine."

"I counted too." His voice was flat and careful, like a man walking on ice he didn't trust. "I counted before I came down. I thought — I thought if I counted twice and it was the same number, it would be real, and if it was different, I could go back to bed and none of it would—" He stopped. He looked at the well. "It's the same number."

"It's the same number."

He came the rest of the way down and sat on the stones across from me, heavily, the way the old men sit outside the church after a funeral, and for a long time neither of us said anything. The sun came up properly. It found the water in the well and turned it from black to a deep, ordinary brown, the color of any old well in any old village, and I hated it a little for that, and was grateful for it too.

"My grandfather's name is still in the parish ledger," Dev said at last. "The one in the church. The one nobody burns because it's a *record*, it's a *historical document*." He said the words the way you spit out a stone. "Two generations of Greaves and Hollises and Pells, all of it written down in a nice copperplate hand, all of it *witnessed*. And my family — my family gave a name two generations back, and my grandfather prospered, and my father prospered, and I prospered, and every winter when the other families' children went quiet I told myself it was the flu. It was always the flu." He was crying now, without any fuss, the way men cry when they've run out of the strength to do it decorously. "I burned my copy. As if that — as if paper was the problem. As if the problem was ever paper."

"The paper was the proof," I said. "The problem was the people who needed it burned."

He looked at me then, really looked, and something passed between us that I think was the beginning of whatever we became to each other after that — not friends, not exactly, not for a long time. Witnesses. Two people who had seen the same thing and could not unsee it and would therefore never again be entirely alone.

"The children," he said. "Someone has to tell the children something."

"No," I said. "Someone has to tell the children the truth. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point."

He nodded slowly. Then he looked past me, at Quentin, and I felt the old flinch go through him — the village flinch, the flinch I'd spent twelve years learning was reasonable — and then I watched him master it, watched him make himself look at my impossible friend with his whole face, the way you look at something you've decided to stop being afraid of whether or not you've stopped being afraid.

"Thank you," Dev said to Quentin.

Quentin tilted his head. "You're welcome," he said. "You almost didn't need thanking."

"I know."

"That's why it counts."

* * *

I went back to my grandmother's house as the village woke.

It woke strangely. That was the thing no one had prepared me for — that the refill would leave the village *emptier* but not silent. Doors opened. People stood in their doorways in the early light looking at each other across the lanes, and there was none of the old practiced smiling, none of the polite doors I'd spent weeks knocking against. What was there instead was rawness. The faces of people who have survived something they helped cause. Old Mrs. Tallow stood in her garden in her nightdress holding a handful of dead sweet peas and staring at nothing. The blacksmith's widow swept her step with long slow strokes, sweeping nothing, over and over. Nobody spoke much. There was a great deal to say and no one had the shape of it yet.

The oldest names were gone. That was how I came to think of it, in the days after — not *the guilty were taken*, though they were, but *the oldest names*. The names that had been on the ledger longest, the signatures that had signed and signed and signed until the signing was just what you did, the way you paid your rates, the way you kept your step swept. The well had taken them with the terrible impartiality of a debt collector. It had not asked me whether they were sorry. It had checked only whether their names were still freely given, and finding them so — finding that not one of them, in two hundred years, had ever once tried to take a name back — it had collected.

I thought of Maren, and my chest did the thing it had been doing all night, the squeezing. She had died two nights before the refill, in her bed, holding my hand, with the torn page — her sister's name, given by her own father, witnessed by my grandmother — pressed flat between our palms. She had made me promise to sing it. I had sung it. Every name. Two hundred years of names, sung aloud at the well's rim while the water rose to listen, and I would sing them again, and again, for as long as there was anyone left in Hollowmere to hear them.

The journals were where I'd left them, on Edith's desk, under the window. I sat down in her chair — her chair, with the hollow worn into the seat by fifty years of her — and I read the last entry again in the full morning light.

*I have given a name to save the child. I know what the old wives say, that a name given is a door opened, but I have told it: not now. Not this one. Take my name instead and leave the child. I have written it in the ledger and signed it and it is freely given and I will bear whatever comes of it.*

She had thought she was offering a substitution. A trade. My grandmother, who could mend anything, who could fix a leaking roof and a broken heart and a village's silence with the same competent hands — she had thought love was a currency the well would accept in exchange. And the well had accepted it, the way a river accepts a dam: it had simply gone around. It had taken her name and filed it as an *invitation deferred* — a debt against our bloodline, a promise that someday, someday, a Voss would come freely to the water. And because a deferred invitation is not a closed door but an open one, the noises had followed us. Twelve years of scratching in walls. Twelve years of wet footsteps on stairs. Twelve years of Quentin, waiting at the edge of my sleep, patient as water, for me to come and keep a promise I didn't know I'd made.

Except — and this was the part I was only now, in the exhausted morning, beginning to understand — Quentin had not been the well's messenger. He had been my grandmother's. She had made the offer *for* me, but she had also, in the same breath, in the same desperate loving breath, made me a friend. Something to stand between. Something to stand *guard*. Every noise in those twelve years had been the well calling in its debt, and every night Quentin had stood in the doorway between the noise and my bed, humming, and I had thought he was the noise.

I put my head down on her desk, on her journals, and I let myself grieve her properly for the first time — not the tidy grief of the funeral, but the whole ragged thing, the grief that has anger in it and forgiveness in it and doesn't know where one ends and the other begins.

* * *

At noon I set up my recorder at the well.

I'd thought about this all morning, walking the lanes, and I want to be honest about the fear, because the fear was real. The song no one sings twice. The accounting. The well's own ledger, set to music, the melody that Maren had hummed one fragment of and gone pale. Everything in the village's inherited wisdom said that this song, sung twice, would bring the water up out of the ground and over the hedgerows and into all our beds. Everything in me that had grown up on Hollowmere's whispers believed it.

But I had sung it once already, last night, and the water had listened, and the water had taken the guilty, and the water had *stopped*. The rule, Quentin had told me, was not that the song must never be sung twice. The rule was that the song must never be sung twice *to the same water*. The well had refilled. This was new water. New water had never heard the names.

So I set up the recorder on the well's rim, in the full sun, where anyone could see me and anyone could hear, and I pressed record, and I sang.

I sang all of it. Every name from the ledger, from the oldest entry to the newest — Ivy Hale last of all, because she was the newest, because she had waited the longest in the dark water for someone to say her name out loud. I sang the dates. I sang the witnesses. I sang my grandmother's signature and what it had cost and what it had meant. My voice cracked on the fourth verse and held on the ninth and broke entirely on Ivy's, and I sang it broken, because that was the truth of it, and the song is an accounting, and accounts must be true.

Then I stopped the recorder. I turned it over. I started a new file.

And I sang it twice.

The village heard it. I knew they did — I could see doors standing open, figures in doorways, Dev at the end of the lane with his cap in his hands. The water in the well held the sun and did not rise. The birds did not stop singing. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and lost interest. The world received the song the way the world receives any true thing sung in daylight: by going on.

Nothing happened. Nothing happened, and it was the sweetest nothing I have ever known.

I saved both files. I labeled them with the date and the place and the words *the true song, first singing* and *the true song, second singing*, and then I sat on the well's rim in the sun with my ruined hands in my lap, and I felt the thing the plan had promised and I hadn't dared believe: exhausted peace. Not happiness. Not yet, maybe not for years. But the peace of a debt discharged, a silence broken, a name — every name — given back to the air where names belong.

Quentin sat beside me with his feet dangling over the water. He was fainter still. In the full sun he was almost transparent, a boy drawn in light on light, and I found I could see the well's far rim through his shoulder.

"You're going, aren't you," I said.

"I was always going," he said. "I was only ever the bridge, Cyrus. You've crossed it now. Bridges don't sulk about being crossed."

"Will I see you again?"

He was quiet for a moment. When he answered, his voice had that patient, amused gentleness it had always had, the voice of someone with all the time in the world — except that now, for the first time, I could hear the clock in it.

"Ask the children," he said. "In a few years. Ask them what their new friends look like, and where they say they come from, and what they hum when they think no one's listening." He smiled — I felt it more than saw it, the way you feel sun through a window. "I was never the only one, Cyrus. I was just the one who stayed."

The light shifted. The stones beside me were empty, and warm, as if someone had just gotten up from them.

* * *

I stayed in Hollowmere through the summer.

Someone had to. Dev took over the schoolroom and taught the children the true song — not the accounting, not yet, they were too young for the accounting — but the other one, the old melody underneath it, the one Maren had hummed, which it turns out was never the well's song at all. It was a lullaby. It had always been a lullaby. The village had simply spent two hundred years too frightened to sing it all the way through and find out.

I recorded everything. The empty shoes — no. I didn't record the shoes. I recorded the lanes in the morning, the doors standing open, the widow's broom, Dev's voice through the schoolroom window teaching thirty children a melody their great-grandparents had died rather than finish. I recorded the well at dusk, and if you listen very closely to that recording — I have listened, many times — there is a moment, just at the edge of hearing, where a small voice sings one note and stops. It might be the wind over the water. I have decided it is not the wind. I have decided that Ivy Hale, or what the water kept of Ivy Hale, heard her name sung in the sunlight by someone who meant it, and went quiet, and that the note was a *thank you*, and that some debts, at the very end, are paid in music.

In the autumn I packed the recorder and the journals and the ledger — the parish ledger, which Dev and I had decided together would not be burned, because the burning was how they'd gotten away with it, and the truth does not need to be comfortable, it only needs to be kept. I stood in my grandmother's kitchen and looked at the doorway where Quentin had watched me for weeks, silent, patient, faithful, and I said goodbye to the empty air, and the empty air felt, for just a moment, like it said it back.

I walked out at sunrise, because it seemed right, because the story demanded it, because some symmetries you honor whether or not anyone is keeping score.

The lane east of the village climbs toward the crossroads, and from the top of it you can see all of Hollowmere laid out below — the roofs, the church, the well's little stone housing with its new wooden lid that Dev had made and that nobody locks anymore, because there is nothing in it to lock away. The morning mist was burning off the fields. Smoke rose straight from three chimneys. It looked, for the first time in my life, like a place where people simply lived.

I had my hand on the recorder case, and I was thinking about the introduction I would write — *the following recordings were made in Hollowmere between the spring and autumn of this year; the names are sung as they were given; nothing has been omitted* — when I heard it.

Faint. From the village below. From the schoolroom, I thought, or the lane near it.

Children's voices, singing the lullaby. All the way through. Twice.

And underneath them, so soft I will spend the rest of my life deciding whether I heard it at all, a new voice — a small, bright, laughing voice that did not belong to any child in Hollowmere, and did not belong to Quentin, and did not belong to anything the ledger had ever held — singing a verse I had never taught anyone.

I stood on the hill with the sun on my back and the recorder heavy in my hand, and I understood that the story was not finished. That it never would be. That somewhere down in that village, right now, some child was inventing a friend — a friendly one, this time, hopefully, this time — and that the well was full, and the names were sung, and the only thing left to do was the thing I had come to do in the first place.

I pressed record, and I started walking back down.
