## 7. The Winter That Didn't End

The snow began the first week of November 1974 and did not stop until it had taken everything.

Edith Whitlock was nineteen that winter, and she remembered it afterward the way you remember a fever—fragmentary, too bright in places, wrong in its proportions. The snow came up past the fence posts by December. It came up past the windows by January. The town of Hollow Creek, which had never in its hundred and six years been a place the weather paid much attention to, found itself buried to the second story, and the temperature fell and fell and did not stop falling, as though the cold had been waiting a very long time for permission.

Her father said it was the jet stream. Her mother said it was punishment. Old Mrs. Vane, who sold eggs and opinions in equal measure, said it was the field line, and when she said it, she crossed herself with a hand that shook so badly the blessing came out crooked.

Edith had lived her whole life within sight of the field line—the old boundary where the Whitlock farm ended and the Calloway corn began, marked by a rusted length of chain fence nobody had mended since before the war. In summer it was nothing. In summer it was weeds and a stone post and the smell of cut corn. But that winter, when the temperature hit forty below and the sky went the color of dishwater, Edith began to notice that the snow along the field line fell differently. It fell straighter. It fell as though it were being arranged.

* * *

The first thing they lost was the well.

It froze from the bottom up, which her father said was impossible, which was a word he used a great deal that winter. Then the cattle stopped drinking, because the water in their troughs was cold enough to crack the enamel of their teeth. Then Mrs. Pruitt's boy, Danny, age six, went out to fetch firewood and came back with his hands black to the wrist, and Dr. Loomis took the fingers off with a saw on the kitchen table while Danny's mother sang hymns in the parlor at a volume that was not singing.

The church filled every Sunday and then filled the days between Sundays too. Reverend Marsh preached about Job until Job ran out, and then he preached about Lot, and then he stood in the pulpit one gray afternoon with his coat still on, his breath smoking in the unheated sanctuary, and said, "The Lord giveth," and could not finish the sentence. He stood there for a full minute with his mouth open, and two hundred people watched him fail to find the rest of it, and Edith understood, in the way you understand things at nineteen—completely and uselessly—that the reverend was as frightened as the rest of them and that fear was the only sermon left.

It was Mrs. Vane who said the word first, in the church basement, over the coffee urn that no longer worked because the pipes had burst.

"Tithe," she said. "We owe a tithe."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when everyone has already thought the thing and no one has said it. Edith was stacking folding chairs. She stopped with a chair in her hands and did not put it down.

"That's superstition," said her father.

"Is it?" Mrs. Vane looked around the room, at the farmers and their wives, at the men who had not slept, at the women who had stopped setting extra places because there was nothing to put on them. "My grandmother told me. Her grandmother told her. When the winter doesn't end, when the cold comes walking, you put out what the field asks for. One from among us. The old ones called him the Tithe-Man, and he walks the field line, and he counts the town like a farmer counts stock, and if you don't pay him, he takes the whole herd."

"Nobody's seen any such man," her father said.

"No," Mrs. Vane agreed. "Not yet."

* * *

Edith saw him on the ninth of January, from her bedroom window, at four in the morning.

She had woken without knowing why, the way you wake when a door closes somewhere far off in a large house. The cold in her room had a texture now; it had stopped being the absence of warmth and become a presence, something with grain and weight, something that lay on the quilt like a second quilt. Her breath hung above her face and would not rise.

The field line was visible from her window as a pale seam in the dark, the snow along the old fence standing up in ridges like the hackles of a dog. And walking along that seam, unhurried, in no coat at all, was a man.

She could not say afterward what he wore, because he did not wear anything so definite as clothing. He was a shape of deeper cold, a man-shaped absence in the snow-glow, and he walked with his hands behind his back like a landlord inspecting a property, and where he stepped, the snow did not compress. It rose to meet him.

He stopped at the fence post. He turned—not quickly, not with any animal motion, but with the slow completeness of a weathervane coming about—and Edith understood that he was looking at the house. At the window. At her.

She did not move. She did not breathe. She had the absolute conviction, arriving whole and unargued, that if she moved he would begin counting.

He stood there for what might have been a minute and might have been an hour. Then he turned back to the field and continued walking, and Edith watched him until the gray of dawn took the seam of the field line away, and only then did she discover that her hands, gripping the windowsill, had left prints in the frost on the glass. Five-fingered prints, deep as if pressed into wax.

She did not tell her father. She told Wren.

* * *

Wren Calloway was her best friend, which was a strange thing for a Whitlock and a Calloway to be, given that their families had not spoken since the dispute over the fence in 1931, but the fence dispute had never trickled down to the daughters. Wren was seventeen, small and quick, with a laugh that carried across two farms and a habit of standing with her fists on her hips like she was daring the world to be smaller than she was. They met at the stone post on the field line, as they had since they were children, and that January they met in the barn between the farms because the stone post had a crown of ice on it like a monument.

"He's real," Edith said. "I saw him."

Wren did not laugh. That was the thing Edith remembered longest—not that Wren believed her, but that Wren was not surprised. "I know," Wren said. "My mama's been sewing."

"Sewing?"

"Every night. She took my blue coat and she's sewing the pockets shut." Wren looked down at her mittens. "I asked her why and she said, so I couldn't carry anything away. I asked away from where, and she went into the other room."

The barn was very cold and very quiet. Somewhere above them, in the hayloft, ice ticked and settled.

"They're going to do it," Wren said. "The tithe. They're going to draw lots at the church. My daddy's on the committee. He won't look at me when he comes home from the meetings. He sits with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral that hasn't happened yet."

"It won't be you," Edith said. "There's two hundred people in this town. It won't—"

"It'll be somebody." Wren said it flatly, without drama, and that flatness frightened Edith more than the figure on the field line had. "And everybody's already decided it won't be them, and everybody's already decided it won't be their kids, and so it'll be whoever's got the least. That's how lots work. That's how everything works." She kicked at a frozen lump of straw and it rang like iron. "My mama's sewing my pockets shut, Edie. You don't sew a girl's pockets shut unless you already know."

* * *

The lot was drawn on the last Sunday of January, in the church, before the whole town.

Reverend Marsh had opposed it to the end and then, in the way of men who oppose a thing until they are needed to perform it, had consented to conduct it. A black stone and a hundred and ninety-nine white ones, in a hat that had belonged to old man Pruitt before he froze in his own dooryard. One by one, in the order their names appeared in the county register, the people of Hollow Creek came forward and drew.

Edith watched them the way you watch a slow accident. Mr. Aldous drew white and sat down like his strings were cut. The Pruitt widow drew white and wept with relief, loudly, horribly, in front of everyone, and no one judged her because every person in that room knew they would do the same. Teenagers drew white and their parents gripped them. The Vane family drew white, all six of them, and old Mrs. Vane said, "See," to no one.

Then it was the Calloways.

Wren's father went up with his hat in his hands. He drew. He opened his fist.

The stone was black.

The sound the church made was not a gasp. It was smaller than a gasp and much worse—a collective exhale, two hundred people releasing a breath they had all been holding since the hat came out, the sound of a town setting down a weight and not asking where it landed.

Wren's father stood holding the stone for a long time. Then he put it back in his pocket instead of the hat, and Edith understood that he meant to keep it, to hold the fact of it, to have the one object in the world that proved what had been done to him.

Wren did not cry. She sat with her fists on her hips, in the pew, in her mother's new-stitched coat with its sewn-shut pockets, and she looked straight ahead at the reverend, and her chin was up, and she was daring the world to be smaller than she was.

Edith sat three pews back and discovered that she was gripping the pew in front of her so hard her fingers ached, and that inside her, underneath the horror, underneath the grief, in a place she would spend the rest of her life not looking at, something else had stirred. Something small and cold and practical. A voice that said: *not her. Anyone but her. What would you do, Edith? What would you do to make it not be her?*

She did not know yet. But the voice did, and it waited.

* * *

The Tithe-Man came to the edge of town the night before the payment was due.

He did not enter. He stood at the field line, at the stone post, visible from every window that faced east, and he waited with his hands behind his back, and the temperature fell forty more degrees in a single evening, and the frost climbed the inside of the glass in ferns and feathers until people had to look at him through a hole wiped in the ice with a sleeve.

The town agreed to bring the girl at moonrise.

Edith did not sleep. She lay in her room listening to her parents not talking downstairs, and at two in the morning she got up, and she did not decide anything—she would insist on this for fifty years, that she never decided—and she went down the back stairs and out through the cold that had teeth now, and she crossed the two hundred yards to the Calloway farm.

Wren was awake. Of course she was awake. She was sitting at the kitchen window in her coat, looking at the shape at the field line, and when Edith came in, blue-lipped and shaking, Wren looked at her and said, "You came to say goodbye."

"I—" Edith's breath was a solid thing in the air between them. "Wren. The locket. The one your mama gave you. With your name in it."

Wren touched it, there at her throat, a small warm shape of brass in a world with no warmth in it. "What about it?"

"Give it to me. Just for tonight. Just to hold."

"Why?"

And Edith, who had never decided anything, heard herself say, in a voice that did not sound like hers: "Because I can fix it. Because there's a way. Because he can't take what he can't name, and I know a place to hide it, and you can hide too, Wren, you can hide in our cellar, nobody comes to our cellar, and in the morning he'll have stood at the line all night and gotten nothing, and he'll have to go, he'll have to—"

She stopped, because Wren was looking at her with an expression Edith had never seen on her before. Not hope. Something before hope and more fragile. The face of a girl who has sewn-shut pockets and has just been told she might keep her hands after all.

"You'd do that?" Wren whispered. "For me?"

The voice inside Edith, the cold practical one, said: *yes, and also not for you.* And Edith, nineteen years old, terrified, in love with her own idea of her own goodness, said, "Yes."

Wren took the chain over her head. She pressed the locket into Edith's palm, and it was the warmest thing Edith had ever held, warm as a living hand, and inside it, engraved in a child's careful script, was the name *WREN CALLOWAY*, and the name was not just letters. Edith felt that. She felt the name the way you feel a held breath.

"Keep it safe," Wren said. "And Edie—" she smiled, quick and crooked, fists almost on her hips, "—if this works, you're the best person in Hollow Creek, and I'm going to tell everybody."

* * *

Edith hid the locket inside the furnace, wrapped in a scrap of wool, wedged where the firebox met the iron, and the iron took it and held it, and the house around it went on being cold.

She told Wren to come at dawn, to come to the cellar door, to come and hide until the Tithe-Man left.

Wren never came.

Edith waited in the cellar in the dark with her hands over her ears, and she heard the town move toward the field line at moonrise, dozens of boots in the snow, and she heard the sound that followed—there was no scream, there was never a scream, there was only a silence that arrived all at once over the whole east side of town, a silence like a door closing in a large house—and she waited, and Wren did not come, and at sunrise her father came down the cellar stairs with his face gray and his hat in his hands and told her that the Calloway girl had gone to the field line alone in the night. That her mother had found her bed empty and her coat gone from its hook. That the Tithe-Man was no longer at the stone post. That the snow had stopped.

That the winter, at last, was ending.

Edith climbed the stairs and looked out the window at the field line, at the untouched snow, at the stone post with its crown of ice already beginning to weep in the first thaw, and she understood what had happened, and she understood what she had done, and she understood that she would never, for as long as she lived, be able to say the true shape of it out loud.

The locket was still in the furnace. The name was still inside it. And somewhere in the cold, something that counted had been given a child without a name—and a debt without a receipt does not close.

It waits.

It waits, and it counts, and fifty years later, in a hospice ward three counties away, a nurse named August would hear the dying counting backwards from ten, and would not yet know that the number they were counting toward had a name, and the name was Whitlock.
