## 10. Frost in July

The garden died on a Tuesday, all at once, between one breath and the next.

August saw it from the kitchen window while the coffee was still brewing—the tomato plants Edith had tended for forty years, the beans climbing their twine, the marigolds Edith had planted every spring because her own mother had planted them—every leaf silvered and stiff, every stem standing at a strange brittle attention, like soldiers caught mid-salute by a flash of light. The grass beyond the garden bed wore a skin of white that steamed faintly in the July sun but did not melt.

August stood at the window with the mug cooling in their hands and did not go outside. Not at first. There was a part of them—the nurse part, the part that had learned to walk toward the worst room on the ward because someone had to—that understood this was exactly the moment to go outside, to gather evidence, to document. And there was another part, older and less professional, that understood something simpler: the garden had not died. It had been *collected*. And collections, August knew from the photographs, from the chair, from the fourth place setting that now appeared every night without fail, were not random. They were methodical. They moved inward.

They went out anyway, because the alternative was standing at the window all day, and they crouched at the garden's edge and touched a marigold head. It crumbled. Not wilted—crumbled, like a sugar sculpture left in a damp room for a century, and when August rubbed the powder between their fingers it was cold. Cold in July, in full sun, cold as if the flower had been held in a fist of winter for years before it was allowed to fall.

The well was worse. August had never much thought about the well; it sat at the back of the property under its low stone cap, older than the house, older probably than the town. When they lifted the cap and leaned over, their breath did not fog in the warm air above the stones—it fogged *below* them, rising up out of the dark in a slow white exhalation, as if the well itself were breathing. And on the inner wall of the well, three feet down, where the light reached, there was frost. Thick, feathered frost, growing in spirals, and it was growing while August watched. They could see it. The crystals extended, branched, extended again, patient as handwriting.

August put the cap back and stood there for a long moment with their palms flat on the warm stone, as if they could hold the whole property down against whatever was rising through it.

The cold had a direction. That was the thing August could not stop noticing, the detail that made their nursing brain itch and catalog even as the rest of them wanted to scream. Cold did not spread like this. Cold diffused, equalized, obeyed thermodynamics. This cold was *walking*. It had started in the house—the locked room, August was nearly certain, the room with the small cold spot shaped like a body—and it had moved out through the walls into the garden, the well, the soil, and it was moving at a pace. Deliberate. Measured. Like a countdown with a radius instead of numbers.

*Ten,* said the frost on the garden. *Nine,* said the well.

August went back inside and locked the door behind them, which they knew was ridiculous, and did it anyway.

* * *

The chair was at the foot of the bed the next morning.

August had begun sleeping with the lights on—not all of them, just the hall light and the bedside lamp, a compromise between dignity and terror that they had stopped being embarrassed about around the same time they'd started sleeping in their shoes. So they saw it immediately when they opened their eyes: the wooden chair, the one from the photographs, the one that had spent three weeks hovering at the edges of every image Edith had ever posed for, sitting on the braided rug at the foot of the bed, facing them.

Not floating. Not translucent. Sitting. Solid, ordinary, its paint gone gray with age, its seat worn pale in the middle the way chairs get worn by a single body sitting in the same place for years. It looked like it had been bought at a church rummage sale in 1962 and never left.

August did not scream. They were almost proud of that. They sat up slowly, keeping the blanket over their legs as if a blanket were a treaty, and looked at the chair, and the chair looked at them, which was not possible, which had never been possible, and which August now understood with the flat certainty of a diagnosis: the chair was looking at them. The back of it, the two slats and the round finials, had a way of orienting that was not about carpentry.

"Good morning," August said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. "You're inside now."

The chair said nothing, obviously. But the room got colder by a degree, and August chose to read that as *yes*.

They got up. They walked around it. They took a photograph of it with their phone, because documentation was the only prayer they had left, and the photograph showed the chair and the bed and August's own rumpled pillow, and in the photograph there was a second chair, fainter, standing in the doorway behind where August had been sleeping, as if the first one had an escort.

They drank their coffee standing up that morning. They did not sit in any chair. It felt important, suddenly, not to sit—not to offer the symmetry, not to give the thing the courtesy of a mirror. They ate their toast at the counter, standing, and the fourth place setting at the table behind them seemed, if anything, amused.

* * *

By the fourth day the cold had reached the road.

August knew because of the Hendersons' dog, a big dumb golden retriever named Biscuit who had spent his whole life treating the Whitlock property line as a rumor. Biscuit came to the edge of the frost line every morning now, stood with his paws exactly at the border where the white stopped and the green began, and barked. Not the frantic bark of a dog at a squirrel. A low, rhythmic, deliberate bark, three and then a pause, three and then a pause, and August stood on the porch listening to it with their skin crawling, because it took them three full repetitions to understand what they were hearing.

Three barks. Then nothing. Three barks. Then nothing.

Counting down.

They called the county extension office about the frost and got a cheerful woman who said, yes, they'd had a few reports, probably a late ground-frost pocket, very localized microclimate, nothing to worry about. August asked how a microclimate explained frost that grew in spirals and she laughed and said, honey, you'd be amazed what ground does, and hung up, and August sat with the phone in their lap and understood that the town had a callus over this. Forty years of callus. The way a family stops talking about the uncle who drank. *You'd be amazed what ground does.* They had all learned not to look.

The Hendersons did not learn not to look. Mrs. Henderson came over that evening, arms crossed against a chill that shouldn't have existed, and stood at the frost line at the end of the driveway, and said, "My tomatoes," in a voice of genuine grief, and then, looking past August at the house with an expression August had seen before on the faces of family members who had finally worked up the courage to ask about the prognosis: "It's the house, isn't it. People always said. After '74. I thought it was just—stories. The kind towns tell." She looked at August, and her eyes were wet and frightened and kind. "You should leave, honey. Whatever she left you, it isn't worth—"

"I can't," August said, and was surprised to hear it come out as fact rather than argument.

Mrs. Henderson nodded slowly, as if August had confirmed a diagnosis she'd already read in the chart. "No," she said. "I don't suppose you can." She turned to go, then stopped. "My mother used to say the Whitlock house was the coldest place in the county even in summer. She said Edith kept it that way on purpose. Said it was like—" Mrs. Henderson's mouth worked, searching for the phrase her mother had used, and found it: "Like keeping a seat warm. Only backwards."

She walked home fast, not looking back, and Biscuit trotted at her heel, and neither of them crossed the frost line again that August ever saw.

* * *

The counting started inside the walls on the fifth night.

August was in the kitchen at two in the morning because sleep had become a negotiation they kept losing, drinking tea they didn't want, when they heard it. Not the pipes. Not the house settling. It was a voice, small and dry, like paper being folded, and it was coming from inside the wall behind the stove, and it said:

"Ten."

August stood very still. The refrigerator hummed. The clock over the doorway—the one Edith had wound every Sunday—ticked with its usual dry patience.

"Nine," said the wall.

It was not one voice. That was the detail that broke something loose in August's chest. It was a chorus compressed into the space between the studs, a dozen voices, a hundred, overlapping and out of phase, the way the dying patients had sounded on the ward when they'd all begun to count in the same week, except this was worse, because the patients had been dying and this was the house, and the house was not dying. The house was *waiting*.

"Eight."

August set down the tea. Their hands were shaking now, finally, the steadiness spent, and they did the only thing that had ever worked in thirty years of hospice work, the only thing that had ever worked with the dying, which was to speak to the voice as if it were a person with a name and a dignity and a right to be heard.

"Who's there?" they said to the wall. "Can you tell me your name?"

"Seven," said the walls, from every wall now, the whole perimeter of the kitchen, the hallway, up through the floor of the room where Edith had slept for sixty years. "Seven. Seven. Seven."

"Is it Wren?" August asked, and the counting stopped.

The silence was enormous. It had texture and weight and a temperature several degrees below the silence that had preceded it, and in that silence August felt the house attend to them—felt it the way you feel a patient's family all turn to look when you say something true at the bedside. The cold pressed against the windows from the outside. The frost on the glass grew a quarter inch.

Then, from the attic, from behind the locked door August had broken open a week ago, from the room with the small cold spot shaped like a child: a single voice, not one of the chorus, separate and clear and young, said:

"Six."

And the chorus took it up again, and the house exhaled frost through every seam, and August stood in the kitchen of their grandmother's house in the middle of July with their breath smoking in the air and understood the shape of the thing at last, the way you finally understand a diagnosis you've been dodging for weeks. The countdown wasn't coming for the house. It wasn't coming for the town. It was coming for the debt, and the debt had a name, and the name was Whitlock, and every tick of it was one step closer to a door that had been left open since 1974.

They did not go back to bed. They sat on the kitchen floor with their back against the stove—the one warm thing left in the house, the pilot light a small blue defiance—and they watched frost bloom across the inside of the windows in spirals, in handwriting, in what August could have sworn, in the gray hour before dawn, were rows of small neat letters, over and over, the same word.

They got up at first light and wiped the frost from the glass with their sleeve, and the word, when it came away wet under their hand, was one August recognized from the newspaper clippings in the attic, from the missing-child poster that had yellowed for fifty years.

*Wren. Wren. Wren.*

August stood in the cold kitchen, holding their own breath so it couldn't be seen, and thought: *she's not haunting the house. She's knocking.*

The counting had reached six. August did the arithmetic they'd been avoiding, the same way they'd once calculated a terminal timeline from a chart they weren't supposed to read, and the number they arrived at was days. A handful of days. Whatever was coming, whatever the countdown was counting toward, it would arrive before the next full moon—which August, with a nurse's habit of knowing the moon the way they knew the shifts, knew would rise fat and orange over the field line in four nights.

Four nights. Six, five, four.

The chair at the foot of the bed had been joined, that morning, by a second chair. They had not photographed it yet. They would. But first they were going to find out what their grandmother had taken, and from whom, and why the whole county's winter had been bought with a stolen thing—because the walls had told them everything they needed to know about the shape of the debt, and the hymnal in the parlor, Edith's hymnal, the one she'd held at every funeral in town for half a century, had been sitting on its shelf with its spine slightly broken, slightly swollen, as if something had been pressed flat inside it for a very long time.

August took it down. The cover was cold as a headstone.

Inside, folded into the binding where *Abide With Me* should have been, was a page torn from a diary, and Edith's handwriting, the handwriting August had known their whole life from recipe cards and birthday checks, said:

*If you are reading this, then it has come back around, and I am sorry, and I was a coward, and here is what I did.*
