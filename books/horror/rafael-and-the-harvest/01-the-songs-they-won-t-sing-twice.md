## 1. The Songs They Won't Sing Twice

The bus to Hollowmere ran once a day, and only if someone had asked it to.

Yara Voss learned this from the driver himself, a heavy-jawed man who spent most of the two-hour journey from the coast in silence, glancing at her in the rearview mirror as though she were cargo he couldn't quite account for. When she'd told him her destination — *Hollowmere, please* — he had repeated the name slowly, the way one repeats a word in a language one is trying to forget, and then said nothing for eleven miles.

The road narrowed as they climbed. Hedgerows gave way to drystone walls, and the walls gave way to nothing at all — just the long, bruised sweep of moorland falling away on either side, stitched with the bronze skeletons of bracken. October had come early to this country. The light was the color of cider left too long in the sun, and everywhere the fields lay harvested and stubbled, gold-gray under a sky the color of old pewter.

"You'll be wanting the Withered Stag," the driver said at last, as the bus groaned down a final descent. "Only place to sleep. Only place for anything, really." He pulled the bus to a halt beside a leaning signpost, its paint flaked to ghosts of letters. "Mind how you go, then."

It was not, Yara thought later, the sort of thing bus drivers usually said. But at the time she was too busy hauling her equipment cases down the folding stairs to think much of it — two hard-shell cases of recording gear, a satchel of notebooks, and a canvas bag that held the letters. Forty-one letters, written in her grandmother Edith Voss's small, upright hand, all of them from the autumn of 1974, all of them addressed to a sister in Norwich, and all of them — Yara had noticed only after the funeral, sorting through the cedar chest — conspicuously, deliberately vague about the village where Edith had spent that year.

*The people here are kind in their fashion,* one letter read. *The harvest this year is the finest anyone remembers. I hardly know how to write what I've seen, and so I shan't.*

Yara had spent eleven years as a folklorist. She had recorded lullabies in fishing villages where the sea had taken half the men, funeral keens in valleys that had lost their language to the mines, plough songs from farmers who sang them the way other people said grace — without thinking, without meaning, out of habit so old it had become bone. She knew what it looked like when a community held something back. It looked like nothing at all. It looked like a song sung beautifully, completely, and then never sung again when you raised the microphone.

But Hollowmere was different. Hollowmere had been flagged in a survey she'd found in a university archive — a 1960s field collection that noted, in a marginal hand: *harvest song, local, unique melody. Informant sang it once, then refused. Would not say why. Village declined further contact.*

A song they wouldn't sing twice. In forty years of collecting, Yara had never heard of such a thing, not as a pattern, not as a *rule*. Songs wore out with repetition; that was their nature. But a village that treated a song as though singing it twice might wear something else out — some thread, some seal, some patience — that was a different order of folk practice entirely. That was the kind of thing that got a monograph written about it. That was the kind of thing that made a career.

The Withered Stag stood at the crook of the village's single street, a low stone building with a slate roof furred green with damp and a sign that swung on rusted hinges, creaking in a wind she couldn't feel at ground level. Inside, the taproom was warm and dim and smelled of woodsmoke and spilled ale and, beneath it, something faintly sweet and vegetal — the smell, she'd have sworn, of cut corn left out in the rain.

The innkeeper was a woman of seventy-odd, broad and square as a hearthstone, with white hair pinned in a coil and hands like knotted roots. Her name, according to the small card by the register, was Mrs. Hedley. She looked Yara over with pale, watery eyes that did not miss much.

"You'll be the folk lady, then."

"Dr. Voss. Yara Voss." She offered her hand. "I wrote ahead — about the harvest songs."

"You wrote." Mrs. Hedley took her hand with a grip like a vice wrapped in flannel. "Aye. I got the letter." She released it and turned to fetch a room key from a board of brass hooks behind the bar, most of which hung empty. "Room's up the stairs, end of the hall. Breakfast's at eight. Supper if you want it, but I'd not advise the trout."

"Mrs. Hedley — I wonder if I might ask you about the songs. The harvest songs, specifically. I understand there's one that's — particular to Hollowmere. That perhaps isn't sung very often."

The innkeeper's hands went still on the key rack. It was a small stillness, a held-breath stillness, and Yara had spent enough years among the old and the guarded to recognize it for what it was: the body deciding, a half-second ahead of the mind, what it would and would not give.

"There's songs," Mrs. Hedley said, without turning around.

"I'm told there's one with a melody that doesn't appear anywhere else in the county. A collector noted it down in 1963, but the man who sang it for him — a Mr. Aldous Crane, I believe — refused ever after to repeat it."

Now she turned. Her face had not changed, exactly, but something behind it had drawn back, the way a candle flame leans away from a draft. "Aldous is dead," she said. "Nine years."

"I'm sorry. But the song —"

"The song's not for sale, if that's what you're after."

"I'm not offering to buy anything. I'm a folklorist. I record songs so they're not lost. That's all. Songs want to be sung, Mrs. Hedley. That's the whole of their nature. A song that's kept silent is a song that's already half dead."

For a long moment the innkeeper looked at her — looked *through* her, almost, as though measuring her against something Yara couldn't see. Then, to Yara's considerable surprise, she sighed, a long exhale through the nose, and untied her apron.

"Supper's at seven," she said. "Come down at half six. I'll sing you your verse. One verse. And then we'll speak no more of it, and you'll eat your trout, and that'll be the end of it."

* * *

Yara spent the interval setting up her equipment in her room: the Nagra recorder, the calibrated microphones, the headphones she'd bought secondhand in 1989 and trusted more than any she'd owned since. She arranged and rearranged cables. She tested levels on the ambient hum of the room — a low, almost subliminal drone, like the building itself breathing.

Through the window she could see the village laid out below: perhaps thirty houses, a church with a squat Norman tower, and beyond them the great open field. It was larger than any single village needed, she thought — a full two hundred acres of harvested ground, the stubble standing in it like a congregation of the shorn. There were no walls around it. No fences. It simply began, as though the village had been built at the field's permission rather than the other way around.

At the field's far edge, she noticed, a single wooden chair stood alone in the stubble.

She watched it for a long time through the glass. A farmer's chair, perhaps, set out for someone resting. But there was no one near it, and no track led to it, and it faced the village — not the horizon, not the sun, but the houses, the church, the taproom where she stood. Facing her, she might have said, if she were the sort of person who said such things.

She wrote in her notebook: *Field larger than needed. Chair at far edge — odd. Ask about it.* And then, because the folklorist in her could not leave it alone: *Ask carefully.*

* * *

At half six the taproom was nearly empty. Two old men sat by the fire with pints they were not drinking, and a woman in a headscarf sat in the corner with a cup of tea, her hands wrapped around it as though the room were cold, which it was not. Mrs. Hedley had set a small table near the hearth, and on it, incongruously, a single candle in a bottle — though the room was lit by electric bulbs.

"For the singing," was all she said, when Yara raised an eyebrow at it.

Yara set up the Nagra on the table, threaded the microphone cable along the floor, and clipped the mic itself to the edge of a wooden stool, angled toward the innkeeper's chair. The red record light glowed like a small patient eye.

"Whenever you're ready," Yara said gently. "Take your time. There's no wrong way to do this."

Mrs. Hedley sat down. She looked at the candle. She looked at her own hands, which were folded in her lap like sleeping birds. And then she began to sing.

Her voice was not what Yara had expected. It was not thin, as old voices often are. It was dark and low and grainy, a voice like soil turned over, and the melody it carried was unlike anything in Yara's considerable internal archive — a descending line that seemed to fall and fall without ever quite landing, each phrase ending a half-step lower than the ear expected, so that the song felt less like music than like something being lowered carefully into the ground.

The words were dialect, old dialect, and Yara caught perhaps one in three:

*Down in the field where the tall corn grew,*
*We gave what the ground asked, and the ground gave true —*

She stopped.

It was not a dramatic stopping. There was no catch, no gasp. She simply ceased, mid-phrase, the way a clock stops — and the silence that followed had a texture to it, a weight, as though the room had been holding the note for her and now set it down.

The fire popped. The two old men by the hearth had turned their heads, very slowly, toward the corner of the room — the far corner, the one in shadow behind the woman with the tea, where there was nothing at all. Nothing but the wall, and the dark above the wainscoting, and the faint sweet smell of cut corn in the rain.

The woman in the headscarf was looking at the same corner. So, Yara realized with a slow cold turn of her stomach, was Mrs. Hedley. All of them, every one of them, looking at the same empty patch of shadow with the same expression — not fear, exactly. Not even guilt, though it was near cousin to it. It was the look of people checking whether something had *heard*.

The innkeeper rose. Her knees cracked. She reached out one root-knuckled hand and, without asking, switched off the Nagra. The red light died.

"That's your verse," she said. Her voice was steady, but her hands, Yara noticed, were not. They had begun, very slightly, to tremble. "That's more than you came for, and more than I should have given. Now you'll eat your supper, and tomorrow you'll be about your business, and Hollowmere will thank you to be about it quickly."

"Mrs. Hedley — what happens if the song is sung twice? What are they all looking at?"

The old woman's eyes came to Yara's face at last, and in them was something Yara had seen only a handful of times in her career — in the eyes of a woman in a drowned village in Wales, in the eyes of a miner who had refused to go back down — the look of someone standing at the edge of a thing so large that they cannot see its shape, only its light.

"Nothing," Mrs. Hedley said. "Nothing happens. That's the trick of it. Nothing happens, and nothing keeps happening, year after year, and you learn to be grateful for nothing." She turned away, and her voice dropped to something barely above the fire's crackle. "You've her look about you, you know. Edith's girl. She sang it twice, that last autumn. Only once more, mind — but twice."

Yara's blood went quiet. "My grandmother never mentioned —"

"No," the innkeeper said. "She wouldn't." She lifted the candle from the table, and as she turned toward the kitchen, she paused, and said, without looking back:

"Your room's the end of the hall. There's a chair in it. Leave it where it stands."

* * *

Yara did not eat the trout.

She sat in the taproom for another hour, nursing a cider she didn't drink, watching the villagers one by one take their leave. The two old men went first, shuffling out into the blue dark. The woman in the headscarf lingered, and as she passed Yara's table she stopped — stopped dead, as though she'd forgotten how her legs worked — and looked down at Yara with an expression of such raw, unguarded pity that Yara half rose from her seat.

"You'll be at Edith's house," the woman said. It wasn't a question. "The one on the lane, with the green door. You'll want to mind the photographs."

"I'm sorry — the *photographs*?"

But the woman only pressed her lips together, shook her head once, hard, as if dislodging a fly, and hurried out into the night.

Yara sat a long while after that, listening to the building settle around her. The fire burned down. The candle Mrs. Hedley had lit for the singing guttered in its bottle, and the wax ran down the glass in slow white ropes.

*She sang it twice, that last autumn.*

Her grandmother — careful, precise Edith Voss, who had labeled every reel and dated every letter and spent forty years never once mentioning Hollowmere — had sung the song a second time. And then had never come back, had never written of it again, had kept the village locked in a cedar chest like something that might bite.

Yara climbed the stairs at last, her mind turning over the verse like a stone in the pocket: *we gave what the ground asked, and the ground gave true.* A bargain-song. A payment-song. The old, old arithmetic of harvest folklore — the corn spirit, the wicker man, the first sheaf given back. But villages didn't stop singing those songs out of fear. They stopped because the songs were embarrassing, or because the church had frowned, or because the young didn't care.

They did not go pale in the middle of a verse. They did not all look at the same empty corner.

At the end of the hall, her room was cold and smelled of lavender and dust. The bed was made with military corners. The window looked out over the great field, silvered now under a rising half-moon, and the chair at its far edge was still there — or, she thought with a small cold jolt, it was *closer*. Only a little. Only, she told herself, a trick of the moonlight, a trick of perspective, a trick of a long day's travel on an empty stomach.

And there was a chair in the room. A plain wooden kitchen chair, standing against the wall near the window, exactly as the innkeeper had said — exactly where, she supposed, it had always stood. It was an ordinary thing, scarred and old, its seat worn pale by generations of sitting.

She unpacked her recorder, set her notebooks on the desk, and wrote by lamplight until her hand ached. *Verse obtained — unique melody, descending, unresolved. Village exhibits collective avoidance behavior re: song. All subjects glance at same location (taproom corner) during/after performance. Significance unclear. Grandmother connection: confirmed, unexplained.*

Then she added a final line, in smaller letters, the way one writes a thing one hopes will turn out not to be true:

*The room feels occupied.*

She looked at the chair by the window. It stood in the moonlight, patient and plain, its back to the field.

It had been facing the bed when she came in. She was almost certain it had been facing the bed.
