## 8. The Lag Widens

The lag was four minutes by the third night.

Wren knew because they timed it, because timing it was the only prayer they had left. They sat on the floor of the Aldous hallway with a stopwatch and a thermos of coffee gone cold hours ago, watching the mirror at the end of the hall the way you watch a pot you're afraid to boil.

At 9:14 p.m., Wren raised their right hand.

The reflection raised its right hand at 9:18.

Four minutes. On Monday it had been one second. On Tuesday, forty seconds. The lag wasn't growing linearly — it was compounding, doubling like something dividing in the dark, and Wren had done enough math on enough infestations to know what exponential growth meant. It meant that whatever lived in the glass was not settling in. It was hatching.

They wrote it down. *Day 5. Lag: 4 min 02 sec. Mirror: hallway, north wall. Subject motion: right hand, raised to shoulder height, held 3 sec, lowered.* The pen scratched against the page and the sound was very loud in the empty house, and somewhere behind the plaster something shifted its weight, patient as sediment.

Wren's phone buzzed on the floorboards beside them.

*Voicemail, Mara, 8:47 p.m.:* "Okay. I got your text from yesterday — 'don't call me tonight, working.' Fine. But you said that Tuesday too, and Wednesday, and now your office line goes to a number that doesn't exist? I called the county, Wren. There's no pest control license under Sandoval anymore. It lapsed. It lapsed *in March*, which is impossible, because I watched you renew it, I helped you fill out the stupid form. Call me back or I'm driving out there."

Wren listened to it twice and did not call back. What would they say? That their license existed, they could show her the paper, the laminated card in their wallet — except when they checked the wallet, the card was warm to the touch and the print had gone slightly soft, like a photograph left in the sun. That the office still answered when they walked in, but the receptionist looked through them now, her eyes sliding off like rain off wax. That the client who'd hired them had never existed, and yet here they were, fifth night running, sitting vigil in a dead woman's house with a stopwatch.

You couldn't say any of that out loud without becoming someone who needed to be committed. So Wren wrote it down instead, because the logbook didn't flinch.

*Client record: nonexistent. Key: present, brass, warm. License: softening. Conclusion pending.*

The conclusion was not pending. Wren knew what the conclusion was. The house had eaten them out of the world's paperwork the way termites eat a sill — quietly, from inside, leaving the paint intact. They were still standing. They were just no longer load-bearing.

* * *

By midnight the lag was eleven minutes, and Wren stopped raising their hand.

It happened naturally, the way you stop poking a bruise. Wren sat in the kitchen with the logbook open and realized, with a clarity that felt like falling, that every gesture they made was now a gift — that each movement was being received eleven minutes late by something in the glass, and that the something had begun to do things with those gifts that Wren had not authorized.

Because the reflection was eating.

Wren hadn't eaten since noon — a gas station sandwich, standing up, barely tasted. But at 12:03 a.m., Wren watched their own body sit down at the kitchen table in the mirror — the same table, the same chair, the same yellowed wallpaper — and eat dinner. A full dinner. Steam rose off a plate Wren had never set. The reflection used a fork in its left hand, and Wren is right-handed, has always been right-handed, and the wrongness of it went through them like a nail finding a wire.

It ate slowly. Methodically. It cut the food into small pieces first, the way children do, the way frightened people do. And halfway through the meal it stopped, fork suspended, and looked at nothing — at some point in the middle distance of the reflected kitchen — and its shoulders began to shake.

The reflection was crying.

Wren watched themselves weep over a meal they had never eaten, in a room they were sitting in, eleven minutes after the fact, and understood two things with terrible simultaneity. First: the reflection was not copying them anymore. It had not been copying them for some time. Second: it was not random, what the reflection did. The eating, the cutting into small pieces, the weeping — it had the shape of rehearsal. Someone running lines. Someone practicing a life before performing it.

*It's studying me,* Wren wrote, and their hand shook so badly the letters came out jagged. *No. Worse. It's studying how to be me. And it's better at it than I am.*

Because that was the thing Wren couldn't stop noticing, the thing that made their skin feel like borrowed clothing: the reflection cried more easily than they did. When it finished eating it sat with its head in its hands for a long time, and the grief coming off it was enormous, unguarded, the grief of someone who had finally stopped holding it in. Wren hadn't cried since the funeral — since *their* funeral, nine years ago, their mother's, where they had stood next to Mara dry-eyed and functional and catalogued the flower arrangements because cataloguing was what they did instead of breaking. Mara hadn't spoken to them properly for a year afterward. *You didn't even cry,* she'd said once, in the last real fight. *I don't think anything gets into you. I don't think anything ever has.*

Something had gotten in now. Something was in the glass wearing their face, and it was grieving on their behalf, and it was doing it better than they ever had.

Wren filmed it. Of course they filmed it. The phone's clock read 12:19 a.m.; the footage showed the reflection lifting its head, wiping its face with a dish towel, and beginning — calmly, efficiently — to wash the dishes that did not exist.

* * *

*Voicemail, Mara, 1:32 a.m.:* "I'm in the car. I'm coming out there, and before you say don't — you don't get to say don't. You've been weird since Mom died, fine, we all have our ways, but this is different. This is you disappearing. Dad called the apartment and the super says nobody's seen you in days, but your lights are on every night. Your lights are ON, Wren. Who's home if you're not home?"

Wren played it in the upstairs bedroom, lying on a mattress they'd dragged from the garage because sleeping in the house seemed less insane than leaving it. The ceiling above them was water-stained in the shape of a country no one had named.

They thought about calling her back. They composed it in their head, the way they composed everything: *Mara. I'm okay. I'm working a job. The job is strange. The house remembers things. My reflection is living my life eleven minutes behind me and tonight it cried and I need you to know that I saw it and I couldn't look away, and I think maybe it knows things about me that I don't, and I think maybe it's practicing for something, and I think when it's done practicing—*

When it's done practicing, what? Rehearsals ended in performances. That was the whole point of rehearsal.

Wren didn't call. Instead they lay in the dark listening to the house breathe — houses breathe, everyone knows this, the timbers tick and settle — except this house breathed in a rhythm that matched no one's lungs, slow and tidal, and beneath it, faintly, from the direction of the cellar door they'd broken open two nights ago, came the smell of something floral and stale. Lilies. Funeral flowers. The house was remembering a specific day and the smell was leaking through.

Sleep took them like an undertow.

* * *

Wren woke at 3:07 a.m. because the silence changed shape.

They lay still and listened. The breathing had stopped. In its place: footsteps. Soft ones, in the kitchen below, the particular shuffle-and-pause of someone moving through a dark room they know well. Wren's whole body went to ice and then, absurdly, to calm — because the footsteps sounded like their own. They knew their own walk the way they knew their own signature. Weight on the heel, slight drag on the left side from an old injury, pause at the counter where the coffee maker would be.

They got up. They went to the landing, where the tall mirror stood — the one from the master bedroom, moved to the hall days ago so Wren could keep it in sight — and they looked down the stairs into the reflected kitchen.

The lag was twenty-six minutes. Wren knew this because they'd timed it before bed. Which meant the figure moving around the kitchen below — their figure, their body, opening cabinets, filling a glass of water — was living Wren's evening from hours ago. Except Wren's evening had consisted of a sandwich, a logbook, and a mattress on the floor. The reflection in the kitchen was cooking. It had a pot on the stove. It stirred something and tasted it and nodded to itself, satisfied, domestic, unhurried — a person at home.

And then it turned its head and looked directly up the stairs, directly into the mirror, directly at Wren.

There should have been a delay. Twenty-six minutes of delay. But the eyes found Wren's instantly, across all that accumulated time, as if the lag applied only to everything else — as if the looking traveled a different road than the living.

The reflection smiled. It was Wren's smile, the closed-lipped one, the one they used for clients and neighbors and their sister on the rare occasions they saw her. Then it raised the glass of water in a small toast, drank, and went back to its cooking.

Wren backed away from the mirror, down the hall, into the bedroom, and shut the door, and stood with their back against it breathing hard, and the worst part — the part they would write down later in handwriting gone almost illegible — the worst part was not the smile or the toast or the eyes.

The worst part was that the reflection had cooked. Had made a real meal, had tasted and adjusted, had moved through the kitchen with the ease of long habit. Wren ate standing up. Wren ate gas station sandwiches over the sink. Wren had not cooked a meal in that kitchen or any kitchen in years, and the thing in the glass was doing it effortlessly, comfortably, *better* —

It wasn't rehearsing Wren's life.

It was rehearsing an improved draft.

*Voicemail, Mara, 3:41 a.m.:* "I'm twenty minutes out. I can see the streetlights from here. If you're in that house, come to the door. If you're not in that house — " a breath, shaky, furious, "— then whoever IS in that house better open the goddamn door, because I will knock it down. I've been practicing being angry at you for nine years, Wren. Don't make me use it."

Wren listened to that one three times. *Practicing,* Mara had said, without knowing what she was saying, without knowing that practice was the whole architecture of the night, that the world had split into performers and rehearsals, that somewhere below them their own face was washing a pot in a kitchen that smelled like lilies.

They started down the stairs to meet her. Halfway down, they passed the tall mirror, and in it — twenty-six minutes behind, or ahead, or beside — they saw themselves still standing at the top of the stairs, still holding the phone, still listening.

Except the reflection in the mirror was smiling.

And Wren, descending toward their sister's headlights, was not.
