## 9. 1974, First Pull

Wren did not mean to fall asleep in the house. That was the first thing they would write in the logbook, later, if there was a later: *Did not intend to sleep on site.* It sounded like the kind of thing a lawyer would circle twice.

But it was past two in the morning, and the lag had stretched to four minutes, and Wren had spent six hours sitting at the kitchen table with a thermos of coffee gone cold and the logbook open to a page that said, in their own careful hand:

*4 min 12 sec. The reflection is setting the table.*

They had watched themselves — or the thing that wore their posture, their slight left-shoulder slump, the way they tapped a pen twice before writing — lay out plates for three. Three. Wren lived alone. The reflection had set the table for three and then stood at the counter with its back turned, waiting for something, and Wren had understood with a clarity that felt less like thought than like temperature: *it is not copying me anymore. It is rehearsing.*

After that, sleep came the way water comes through a ceiling — slowly, invisibly, all at once.

The kitchen table was under their cheek. The logbook was under their hand. The last thing Wren registered was the smell of the house, which had changed over the weeks from closed-up dust to something warmer, something almost like bread, and the thought — absurd, professional, doomed — *I should document that.*

Then the light changed.

Not brightened. *Changed* — the way light changes in a photograph when someone adjusts the white balance, everything shifting half a degree toward gold. Wren opened their eyes and the kitchen was warm-lit. Not the dead fluorescent gray of an empty house at 2 a.m., but lamplight, yellow and soft, pooling on the table where their arms were folded, catching the grain of wood that was no longer bare but covered — a tablecloth, checkered, faded red.

The coffee in the thermos cup beside them was steaming.

Wren sat up very slowly. The house rule, learned over weeks of documentation, was this: move slowly, record everything, trust the instruments. But the instruments were wrong here. Their phone showed 2:47 a.m., June 14th, and the window above the sink showed full daylight, a summer morning so saturated it looked painted. The phone's clock ticked. The sunlight did not flicker. One of them was lying.

"Okay," Wren said aloud, because saying things aloud kept them real, kept them filed. "Okay. Anomaly. Note it."

Their voice sounded different in the room. Fuller. The kitchen absorbed sound differently when it had things in it — curtains, a dish rack, a refrigerator humming a note lower than any modern fridge hummed. Wren stood, and the floorboards under their boots were warm.

*Warm,* they wrote mentally, hands actually steady, reaching for the pen. *Floorboards warm as if heated from below or as if the house has been occupied continuously—*

The pen stopped.

On the counter, next to the dish rack, sat a lunchbox. Metal, dented at one corner, with a decal of a rocket ship peeling at the edge. And beside the lunchbox, a glass of milk, half-drunk, a mustache of it dried at the rim.

Wren knew the house empty. Wren had walked every room of this house empty, had measured its silences, had photographed its dust strata like an archaeologist. This kitchen had never held a lunchbox. This kitchen had been vacant since before Wren was born.

And yet the milk was drying at the rim in real time, and somewhere upstairs a screen door banged, and a woman's voice called out — muffled, cheerful, indistinct, the way voices sound through walls in childhood — and Wren's chest did something complicated and painful, because the voice was calling a name.

*Elise.*

* * *

The hallway was longer than it should have been. Wren noted that too, walking it, noting compulsively the way you hold a rail going down stairs in the dark. The wallpaper was different — small yellow flowers instead of the water-stained stripes Wren had photographed in week one. A telephone table stood against the wall with an actual rotary phone on it, cream-colored, its cord curled like a sleeping animal. Framed photographs lined the wallpaper at eye height, and Wren made the mistake of looking at one.

A family. Four of them. Father, tall, squinting slightly even in the stillness of the photograph, as if the sun had caught him mid-flinch. Mother, dark-haired, one hand on the shoulder of a boy of maybe thirteen who scowled with the magnificent boredom of adolescence. And in front, kneeling on the lawn with a dog whose breed Wren couldn't fix no matter how long they looked — the dog wouldn't resolve, kept sliding like a fish — a girl.

She was maybe ten in the picture. Gap-toothed grin. Knees scabbed. Holding the dog's collar with both hands.

Wren stood in front of the photograph for a long time. Long enough that they became aware, distantly, of sounds moving through the house — the mother's voice again, closer now; a radio playing something with horns; footsteps overhead, unhurried, a man's weight crossing a room he had crossed ten thousand times.

*This is 1974,* Wren thought, and the thought arrived without disbelief, the way you wake already knowing you're late. *This is the summer. I am standing inside the file.*

Because that's what it was. Wren had spent weeks assembling the Aldous file — county records, a newspaper microfiche, Dot Ferris's testimony transcribed in blue ink. And now the file had opened around them like a mouth. Every detail matched. The wallpaper matched a description in a 1971 real estate listing Wren had found archived. The radio song was one Dot Ferris had mentioned, humming it, trying to place the summer. The house wasn't showing Wren a memory. The house was showing Wren *itself*, preserved at the moment before—

Before what. Before the tarp. Before the cellar. Before the family buried whatever they buried and moved through fifty years of silence until the silence itself grew fingerprints and started walking in the walls.

The footsteps upstairs stopped.

Wren felt it the way animals feel weather. The whole house paused — the radio dipped, the mother's voice trailed off mid-word, the refrigerator's hum bent flat — and in that pause Wren understood that the house was not a recording. Recordings don't notice you.

Something in this house was aware that a stranger was standing in its hallway reading its photographs.

Wren did the only professional thing left. They took out the phone and photographed the family portrait.

The shutter clicked. On the screen, the image appeared — and in the image, the girl was not kneeling on the lawn. She was standing in the hallway, facing the camera, looking directly into the lens, her gap-toothed smile gone, her expression old and patient and terribly sad.

Wren lowered the phone with hands that were no longer steady at all.

"You're late too."

The voice came from behind them. Small. Clear. Unafraid.

Wren turned.

She stood at the end of the hallway where the wall should have been — where, in the empty house, there was only the sealed door to the cellar stairs. She was eleven, maybe, wearing shorts and a t-shirt with a cartoon frog on it, one knee scraped and crusted brown, barefoot, her hair cut short in the blunt way hair gets cut by a parent with scissors and no patience. She was looking up at Wren with her head tilted, and her eyes were the color of the warm light filling the hall, and she said it again, not as a question, not as a greeting, but as a fact she had been holding for a very long time:

"You're late too."

"Late," Wren repeated. Their voice came out sanded down. "Late for what?"

"For us." She shrugged, matter-of-fact, the shrug of a child explaining something obvious to a slow adult. "Everybody here is late. Mom's late. Dad's late. Danny's late." She frowned slightly, checking something. "You're the latest."

The name went through Wren like cold water. *Danny.* The boy in the photograph. The brother. In every record Wren had found — and they had found everything, they had pulled tax rolls and school censuses and obituaries — the Aldous family had two children. Daniel, born 1961. Elise, born 1963. And then nothing. No death certificate for Elise. No transfer to relatives. No school withdrawal form after June of 1974. She had simply been subtracted from the paperwork of the world, and the world had let it happen, because the world always lets it happen when the people doing the subtracting are respectable and tired and own their home outright.

"You're Elise," Wren said.

The girl's whole face changed — lit up, actually lit, the way faces do when a stranger says your name and means it. "You know me?"

"I've been—" Wren stopped. How did you say it? *I've been investigating your disappearance. I've read your file. I know your father carried a tarp to the cellar at three in the morning and your mother scrubbed the stairs until her knuckles bled.* You didn't say those things to a child with a scraped knee standing in warm light. "I've been looking for you," Wren said, and found, to their surprise, that it was true in every direction at once.

"Nobody looks for me," Elise said. Not bitter. Just reporting, the way she'd report the weather. "Danny says they told everyone I went to Aunt Carol's. Aunt Carol doesn't exist." She grinned suddenly, gap-toothed, conspiratorial. "I checked. I asked Mom who Aunt Carol was and she dropped a plate."

Somewhere upstairs, a door closed. The footsteps resumed, slower now, and Elise glanced up at the ceiling with an expression that aged her forty years in a second — a flinch, a readiness, a child's expert calibration of an adult's mood — and then it was gone, folded away, and she was eleven again and grabbing Wren's sleeve with fingers that had weight, actual weight, warm through the fabric.

"Come on," she said. "You're not supposed to be in the hall when Dad comes down. Nobody's supposed to be anywhere when Dad comes down."

"Elise—"

"You can see the good parts of the house," she said, pulling. "Before it gets to be nighttime. Nighttime here is—" She stopped herself, visibly deciding not to finish, and Wren felt the shape of the unfinished sentence settle into their bones like a splinter.

She led Wren through rooms that existed in 1974 and not in the present — a den with wood paneling and a console television showing static that somehow resolved, if you didn't look directly at it, into a baseball game. A bathroom with a pink bathtub. A bedroom with twin beds, one made military-tight and one a chaos of stuffed animals, and Wren understood without being told which bed belonged to which child, and understood also why the tight bed's owner paced his room at night in the loops Wren had glimpsed in the mirrors, carrying things, being carried by things, a boy becoming the keeper of a family's grave at thirteen years old.

And everywhere, everywhere, the light. Golden, late-afternoon, permanent. The house in 1974 was preserved at the hour before dinner, the hour when families are most themselves, and it had been preserved there for fifty years, and Wren walked through it feeling grief arrive in stages, like weather fronts.

Because this was not a haunting. Hauntings were cold; hauntings were absence. This was the opposite. This was a house that had refused to let go of the warmest hour it ever contained, holding it the way a hand holds a face, and inside that refusal a girl had been waiting — not suffering, exactly, not tortured, but *waiting*, the way you wait when you've been told someone will be right back, and fifty years is a long time to believe someone will be right back.

"How long have you been here?" Wren asked, in the den, while the static-baseball played to a crowd that murmured without words.

Elise considered the question with genuine effort, the way children consider questions adults use carelessly. "Since the stairs," she said.

"The stairs."

Her hand went to her knee — the scraped one — absently, the way you touch an old scar. "I was running. Danny dared me to run down with my eyes closed. That's all. That's the whole thing." Her voice stayed level, but her toes curled against the floorboards, gripping. "It was an accident. I need you to know that part. Everybody always thinks it wasn't, but it was. I fell. Danny cried for a really long time. He's still crying, kind of, if you listen." She looked up. "Mom and Dad got scared. Scared people do bad math. They did bad math about me."

The words landed in Wren's chest and stayed there, glowing like coals. *Scared people do bad math.* Fifty years of horror distilled by an eleven-year-old into five words.

"Elise," Wren said carefully, "do you know what year it is outside?"

"Outside," she said, tasting the word, and for a moment the golden light seemed to dim at the edges, just slightly, like a lamp on a failing current. "Outside is later. That's why you're late too." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile Wren had ever seen on a living face. "Everyone who comes here comes from later. That's how I know you're real. The fake ones never come from later."

"The fake ones?"

"Dad," she said simply. "Dad comes down every night and does it again. Mom scrubs. Danny paces. They do the same night forever." She said it without horror, the way you'd describe a tide. "But you came from outside. So you can leave." Her fingers tightened on Wren's sleeve, and now the grip had something desperate in it, something that had waited fifty years to be desperate in exactly this way. "That's the important part. You can leave. People who live here can't."

Upstairs, the footsteps stopped again. And this time they started coming down.

Elise's face went white. Not metaphorically — Wren watched the color leave it, watched the girl become, in real time, a child who had heard these footsteps before and knew precisely what followed them. She pushed Wren toward the kitchen, toward the back door, which stood open onto a summer evening that smelled of cut grass and charcoal smoke.

"Go," she whispered. "He can't know you're here yet. He's not ready. When he's ready, he'll hear you, and then—" The footsteps reached the landing. "Then nobody leaves."

"Elise, I'm coming back for you. I need you to know that. Whatever happens—"

"I know," she said. And she did know — that was the unbearable thing, the thing Wren would carry out of this house and into every day after. She knew, with the terrible patience of the abandoned, that everyone said that. "You'll be late," she said. "But you'll come. Late is okay. Late is still coming."

The footsteps entered the hallway.

Wren woke at the kitchen table with their cheek on the logbook and the ink of their last entry smeared by damp — though whether sweat or tears, the record did not say, and Wren, for the first time in their career, declined to speculate.

The house was dark and empty and 2024. The lag in the hallway mirror had widened to nine minutes.

But on the back of their hand, where a small hand had gripped it, there were five finger-shaped marks pressed deep into the skin —

— and they faced the wrong way, as if made by someone standing behind them, in the mirror, waiting to come through.
