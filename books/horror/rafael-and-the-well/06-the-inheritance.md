## 6. The Inheritance

The solicitor's letter had been waiting in the study for longer than Rosalind had been alive.

They found it on the third morning, inside the locked drawer of the grandmother's desk—the lock so old it surrendered to a hairpin and a prayer—and the envelope was heavy cream stock gone the color of old teeth, addressed in a hand Rosalind knew from birthday cards: *Miss R. Dravenmoor, upon her majority.* Not *Mr.*, not *Ms.* The grandmother had never once gotten it right, and somehow that was the most familiar thing in the whole cold house.

Rosalind sat down in the desk chair, which exhaled dust like a held breath finally released, and broke the seal.

*Dear child,* the solicitor's letter began, in the flat copperplate of Messrs. Halloway & Finch, Solicitors, Hollowmere, established 1881.

*We write further to the will of the late Mrs. Agnes Dravenmoor (dec.) to confirm the terms of your inheritance, which we set out plainly so there may be no misunderstanding at the time of execution.*

*The freehold of Dravenmoor House, together with its lands, water rights, and appurtenances, passes absolutely to you, subject to one condition precedent, which is as follows:*

*That the well house standing at the north boundary of the property remain sealed, unopened, and undisturbed, in perpetuity, by covenant of blood.*

Rosalind read the phrase twice. *Covenant of blood.* It sounded like something out of one of the ballads they'd come here to collect—except no ballad in the archive used language like that. Ballads were sly. This was legal.

*You will be asked to sign a clause to this effect before probate concludes. We would draw your attention to the fact that this clause has appeared in every conveyance of the property since 1974, and that each heir has signed it. We would not draw your attention to anything else.*

The letter ended there, signed with a name Rosalind didn't recognize, though the ink had browned decades ago. Beneath the signature, in the grandmother's hand, someone had added a single line:

*He signs his letters like a man who has seen what happens when they aren't signed.*

Rosalind set the letter down. Their hands were steady, which surprised them; they'd have expected shaking. Instead there was only a strange ringing quiet, the kind that follows a bell struck once in an empty church.

Seventeen heirs. The letter hadn't said seventeen—but the ledger had, or almost. Rosalind pulled the ledger across the desk, that terrible accounting book they'd found wedged behind the false back of the drawer, and turned to the pages after the final entry of 1974. *Well secured. Voice secured.* And then, year after year, in different hands, in different inks, the same ritual:

*1975. Clause signed. — E. Dravenmoor.*
*1981. Clause signed. — H. Dravenmoor.*
*1989. Clause signed. — M. Dravenmoor.*

And beside each name, a line drawn through it. A clean, deliberate stroke, ruled with something straight-edged, as if the names were debts being cancelled. Seventeen of them. Rosalind counted twice, then a third time, because the number mattered now in a way it hadn't an hour ago. Seventeen signatures. Seventeen crossings-out.

The last crossed-out name was the grandmother's own.

Rosalind sat with that for a long time while the light moved through the study window and failed to warm anything. If the clause had been signed by every heir—if signing it was how you kept the house, the land, the water rights, the *quiet power* the village still bent around them like grass around a stone—then why was every name struck through?

Unless the crossing-out wasn't cancellation.

Unless it was a record. A list. Not of people who had broken the covenant, but of people who had *kept* it—who had signed, and lived their comfortable invisible lives, and died, and been struck from the book the way you strike a debt paid in full. Paid in what currency? Rosalind thought of Edith's face when she'd sung the line twice. Thought of the mirrors lagging a half-second behind the world, patient as a creditor.

*In perpetuity, by covenant of blood.*

There was a second envelope beneath the first. Thinner. No solicitor's stamp. Just the grandmother's hand again, and this time the address was not formal: *For whichever of you is sitting in my chair reading this.*

Rosalind's mouth went dry. They opened it anyway. Of course they opened it. That was the whole disease of this family, they were beginning to understand—curiosity dressed up as scholarship, greed dressed up as duty, and always, always the opening of things better left shut.

***

*Child,* the letter began, and the handwriting was shakier than the birthday cards, hurried, the pen pressed hard enough in places to tear through to the wood of whatever desk she'd written it on.

*I don't know when you'll read this. I hope late. I hope you'll be old and settled and too sensible to do anything foolish. But I know our blood, and I know you'll read it young, and I know you'll think you understand, because I did too, at your age, sitting where you're sitting.*

*So let me say it plainly, the way no one said it to me until it was nearly too late.*

*The house is not haunted. I want you to understand that first, because everything else follows from it. Hauntings are accidents. Residue. Grief caught in plaster. What we have is not an accident. What we have is an arrangement, and arrangements have two sides, and we have been on the right side of ours for fifty years.*

*You've noticed the mirrors. Everyone notices eventually; the children notice first and are taught not to say. The lag is real. It grows if you feed it attention and shrinks if you starve it. Do not test it. Do not time it. Do not stand in front of a mirror wondering what it knows. It knows exactly what you're wondering, and it is flattered, and it is hungry.*

*Here is what I can tell you without breaking anything worse than silence. In 1974 my mother and her brother did a thing in the well house. I was eleven. I heard the singing stop. That is all I will put on paper, because paper has been read by eyes I did not invite.*

*What I will tell you is what it costs, and what it buys, because no one told me and I signed blind.*

*It buys safety. Real safety—not luck, but something better than luck. Accidents bend away from us. Sickness passes over this house like weather. The village does not cheat us, does not gossip about us beyond what is harmless, does not remember slights. We move through the world unwounded. You have felt it your whole life and called it good fortune. It is not fortune. It is rent being paid on your behalf.*

*And the cost—child, the cost is that it only asks for one thing.*

*Never give it your voice.*

*I mean this literally. Do not sing in this house. Do not hum. Do not read aloud. Do not speak your own name in front of a mirror. When you sign the clause, sign it silently, in your head, and let the solicitor think you strange. The voice is the only coin it accepts directly, and everything else—all this comfort, all this invisibility—is bought with voices already given. There was a woman once whose voice paid for fifty years of our peace, and if you ever learn her name, do not say it aloud either. Names are small songs. Songs are large names. I know how that sounds. Sign the clause anyway.*

*Every heir signs. Every heir lives well. Every heir dies comfortable and is struck from the ledger, and the house goes on, and the well stays sealed, and the singing stays stopped. That is the inheritance. That is all it is. I loved this house and I hated it in equal measure and I sealed more letters than this one trying to decide which of those loves to leave you.*

*Be sensible. Be quiet. Be nothing, and be safe.*

*Your grandmother,
who was struck through on a Tuesday*

***

Rosalind read it three times. On the third reading they became aware of the light going long and amber across the desk, and of the fact that they had not moved, and of the sound—or rather the absence of sound—in the house around them. No settling timbers. No wind. The silence had texture now, the way deep water has pressure. It was listening.

*Never give it your voice.*

Their throat tightened around nothing. Absurdly, they wanted to hum—to prove they could, to feel their own voice fill the room and push back against that listening quiet. They pressed their lips together. Somewhere below the floorboards, faint as a pulse under skin, came the sound of water moving where no water should be.

They looked up. The hall mirror stood visible through the study door, at the end of its corridor of darkened wallpaper, catching the last of the light.

In it, Rosalind saw themselves sitting at the desk—saw themselves look up, saw themselves rise, saw themselves walk toward the door.

Then they looked down at their own hands, still flat on the ledger, still motionless.

The reflection was walking toward them, and it was smiling, and Rosalind had not yet decided to stand.
