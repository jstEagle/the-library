## 8. The Offer

The pen was waiting for me on the study desk when I came down the next morning.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was all I had left. I had not left a pen on the desk. The night before, after the water stains had bloomed and faded and bloomed again on the ceiling like a mouth learning to speak, I had carried every writing instrument in the house up to my bedroom and locked them in my suitcase, because I no longer trusted myself to sign anything in this house without meaning to. And yet there it sat at seven-forty in the morning: a heavy black fountain pen, silver-nibbed, laid diagonally across a single sheet of cream paper, squared to the edge of the blotter with a care that felt almost tender.

The paper was blank. It did not stay that way.

Ink rose through it the way damp rises through plaster—slowly, from beneath, as if the words had been written on the other side of the world and were only now arriving. I watched the letters surface one by one, grey first, then black, then wet:

*Clause Eighteen.*

Below it, in a hand I recognized with a sickness that started in my teeth—my grandmother's hand, the same hand that had written *Well secured. Voice secured.* forty-nine years ago—the terms assembled themselves:

*The undersigned accepts the estate of Hollowmere entire: house, land, water, and voice. In consideration thereof, the undersigned agrees to keep the well house sealed, to keep the name unspoken, and to keep the song unsung. In return: safety. In return: quiet. In return: to pass unseen through all the watching world, for as long as they keep the seal.*

There was a line for a signature. There was a date. The date was today.

I did not touch it. I made tea instead, which is what Dravenmoors do when the house begins to negotiate—we perform normalcy like a warding charm—and I stood in the kitchen with the kettle screaming and watched the window over the sink, where my reflection watched me back from a half-second in the past. Except it wasn't half a second anymore. I had timed it against my watch that morning, standing in the hall with my arms crossed, counting. Three seconds. My reflected self held its crossed arms three seconds after I uncrossed mine, and then, once, horrifyingly, it uncrossed them *first*, and I stood there feeling like a glove someone else had already put their hand into.

Three seconds is not a delay. Three seconds is a conversation happening slightly out of order.

* * *

It began properly at dusk.

I had spent the day in the village archive—a shed behind the church that smelled of mouse and mildew—photographing parish records, and I found what I expected to find and did not want to find: seventeen conveyances of the Hollowmere estate across a hundred and sixty years, each with a codicil concerning "the well house and its appurtenances," each signed by an heir whose name appeared, in my grandmother's ledger, struck through with a single clean line. Seventeen signatures. Seventeen crossings-out. I had told myself the names were crossed out because those heirs had died, because estates are grim bookkeeping, because dead people get lines drawn through them all the time.

Then I photographed the last page of the 1961 conveyance and saw that the crossing-out was done in ink that matched nothing else in the ledger—ink that was still, faintly, wet-looking under the flash—and I drove home through lanes going blue with evening, and the hedgerows seemed to lean away from the car, and I understood that I was driving toward something that had already decided how tonight would go.

The house was lit.

Every lamp burning, every room gold, though I had left them dark and the electricity in Hollowmere had been temperamental since before I was born. The front door stood open onto the warm light like a mouth. Inside, the air smelled of beeswax and woodsmoke and, underneath, faintly, of well water—that cold mineral breath, iron and stone, the smell of the place where Maren Oduya had spent her last hours listening to her own heart slow down.

"You're home," said my reflection in the hall mirror.

I stopped on the threshold. My own mouth had not moved. But there I was in the glass, three seconds ahead or three seconds behind—I could no longer tell which direction the lag ran—and my reflected self was smiling at me with a warmth I have never once managed in a mirror, saying again, soundlessly but unmistakably, reading the shape of it off the lips like a woman seen through winter glass:

*You're home.*

And behind my reflected shoulder, deep in the lagged hallway where no hallway was, the figure stood at the window of the well house. Closer than before. No longer a smudge, no longer a suggestion. A woman in a plain dress gone grey with fifty years of water, her hair a soft dark cloud around a face that was patient the way stone is patient. She raised one hand—not beckoning. Showing me her palm. Empty. As if to say: *look, I am holding nothing, I never wanted anything but what was mine.*

"Come in," said the house.

Not my reflection this time. The house itself, in the creak of the boards and the settling of the beams and the low hum in the radiators, a voice made of everything the house had ever been: nursery and sickroom and solicitor's office and grave. It was not an unpleasant voice. That was the worst of it. It sounded like my grandmother reading aloud on winter evenings, like being small and safe while the weather did its worst outside.

I went in. God help me, I went in, because it was warm and I was tired and the house had been my whole childhood's idea of shelter.

* * *

The mirrors showed me my life.

That is the only way I can say it. I walked from room to room with the lamps burning honey-gold, and in every glass, the lag had widened into something new—not a delay now but a depth, a second Hollowmere visible behind the first like a coin at the bottom of clear water, and in that second Hollowmere I could see forward. Or sideways. Or into the life I would have if I signed.

In the parlour mirror: me at forty, grey coming in early at the temples, sitting in this same room with a fire lit, a finished novel on the table beside me, my name on the spine, and no one—no one at all—looking at me twice. A reviewer's face in memory, politely blank. An editor's voice on the phone, warm and incurious. The particular blessed anonymity I had chased my whole adult life, the wish to move through rooms like weather, noticed by no one, blamed by no one, safe. It was all there. It was all *mine*, arranged like a gift.

In the hall mirror: the estate thriving. The land green and fat, the tenant farms paying, the Dravenmoor name quietly respected in county offices, in archives, in the small histories where our family had always been mentioned just often enough to matter and never enough to be examined. Invisible power. The kind you never have to defend because no one can see it to attack it. The kind my grandmother had bought, I now understood, with a woman's voice and a woman's life, and had serviced faithfully every equinox for forty-nine years like a debt she was proud to pay.

In the staircase mirror—the tall one, the one that had shown me the door closing a beat late on my first night—I saw something that undid me more thoroughly than any horror could have. I saw myself *rested*. I saw myself sleeping a full night in this house with no lag in the mirrors, no figure at the well-house window, no verse caught in my throat. I saw myself walking through Hollowmere while Edith nodded to me pleasantly, while the pub landlord poured my pint without his eyes flicking once toward the hills, while children sang in the churchyard—sang anything, sang whatever they liked, because the songs were all sung out, all paid for, all safely ours.

Everything I had ever wanted. Safety, sufficiency, silence. And all it cost was a signature and a promise to keep a dead woman dead.

"It isn't much," the house said gently, in the tick of the clock and the sigh of the boiler. "It's only a name. She has so many names by now. What does she need hers for?"

* * *

I found the names at midnight.

I had gone up to bed without signing—I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here, even if only in prose no one will believe: I went up to bed without signing—and I passed the tall mirror on the landing, and the glass was full of writing.

Names. Dozens of them, rising through the mirror's silvered depths like bodies rising slowly through dark water, each written in that same grandmother-hand, each struck through with a single clean line. I read them by the light of the moon through the landing window. *Edmund Dravenmoor, 1863. Alice Dravenmoor, 1881. Thomas, 1902. Thomas again, 1904, the ink fresher, the line shakier.* On and on down the decades, heir after heir, generation after generation, every one of them offered Clause Eighteen, every one of them—

No. That was the thing my hands understood before my mind did, pressing flat against the cold glass. Not every one of them had signed. The crossed-out names were the ones who *refused*. Somewhere below the visible silver, deeper, fainter, were other names, unwritten, unsigned, unlined—the ones who took the offer, the ones whose descendants I was, the ones whose comfort I was being offered now at the same price. The mirror showed me the refusals because the house kept them the way a fisherman keeps the hooks he's lost: as proof the bargain holds, as warning of what refusal costs.

Seventeen names. Seventeen heirs who looked into the water and said no. And every one of them gone—not dead, I understood suddenly, not dead the way people die, but *unwritten*, struck from the record, erased so thoroughly that the parish registers had gaps where they should have been, gaps I had photographed that very afternoon and filed under *damaged source material*.

Refusal didn't kill you. Refusal deleted you.

My breath fogged the glass. When it cleared, the names had rearranged themselves, and at the top, fresh, wet, gleaming, waited a blank space exactly the size of my name—and beneath it, in smaller script, almost kind:

*Rosalind. You've been writing about us your whole life. Write yourself in. Stay.*

And oh, God, the sweetness of it. I stood on that cold landing with my palm against the mirror and I *wanted* it—I want to say that plainly, because a person who lies about temptation hasn't met the real thing. I wanted the rest. I wanted the anonymity like a drug I'd been craving for thirty years without knowing its name. I wanted to stop being the queer grandchild nobody mentioned, the folklorist nobody funded, the Dravenmoor who asked questions at funerals. The house was offering to make me weather. All I had to do was leave one woman in the water forever, one more year, one more signature, one more quiet.

My hand drifted down toward my pocket, toward the pen I had locked away and which I knew, with absolute certainty, was no longer there.

Behind my reflected shoulder, in the lagged hallway, the woman at the well-house window opened her mouth.

She did not sing. Not yet. She simply breathed, and the breath moved through the glass like current through water, and every crossed-out name on the mirror trembled—and in the silence between one heartbeat and the next, three seconds wide, I heard her begin the verse I had never found in any archive, the missing verse, the one the village would not sing twice:

*"They wrote their names above my grave—"*

I turned from the mirror. Downstairs, in the study, the front door of the well house groaned open on hinges that had not moved in half a century, and the smell of the well rose through the floorboards like a tide coming in.

The equinox was tomorrow.
