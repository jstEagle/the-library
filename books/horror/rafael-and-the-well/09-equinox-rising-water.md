## 9. Equinox, Rising Water

The equinox arrived without ceremony. No wind, no omen—just a grey light pressing flat against the windows at dawn, the kind of morning that seemed to have been agreed upon in advance by everything it touched.

Rosalind woke on the study floor, where they had fallen asleep among the ledger's open pages, and knew before they stood that the water had begun.

They heard it first as a sound beneath sound: a slow, patient swallowing somewhere under the floorboards, like a throat working. Then the smell—wet stone and iron and something older underneath, something vegetal and black, the smell of a place that had been holding its breath for fifty years and had finally decided to exhale.

The well was refilling.

Rosalind went to the hall mirror because there was nowhere else to go, and because some part of them—the part that had spent three weeks learning this house's terrible grammar—understood that the mirrors were the only honest witnesses left.

Their reflection stood waiting for them. It did not lag now. It arrived with them, exactly, perfectly, and Rosalind understood that too: the delay was over because the waiting was over. Whatever had been standing one second behind the world all these weeks had closed the distance. There was no longer any gap for it to stand in.

Except that the face in the mirror was not entirely their own.

It was still recognizably Rosalind Dravenmoor—the same tired eyes, the same mouth that had not sung in days—but beneath the skin of the reflection, layered like a second exposure on old film, was another face. Darker. Older. A woman's face with high cheekbones and eyes that held the particular patience of someone who has waited so long that patience has become a form of anger refined past all heat.

Maren Oduya looked out through Rosalind's reflection, and she was not threatening. That was the worst of it. She was simply *there*, present in the glass the way water is present in a well, and her expression said only: *You know what must be done.*

"I know," Rosalind said aloud, and their voice cracked on the second word, and in the mirror Maren's mouth moved a half-breath after theirs—not lagging now but *answering*, the way one voice answers another in a round.

* * *

By noon the house was weeping from below.

Water came up through the floors the way truth comes up through a family—one seam at a time, then all at once. It surfaced first in the kitchen, pooling around the table legs in a thin silver skin. Then along the hallway, finding the grooves between boards, tracing them like veins. The wallpaper began to blister. In the parlour, the piano played a single low note on its own and held it, a drone note, the tonic of a song Rosalind had been hearing in fragments since the night they arrived.

*Maren's ballad.* The missing verses. The ones no singer in Hollowmere would complete.

Rosalind waded toward the back door, boots splashing, and stopped.

Because the mirrors were all showing the same thing now. Every glass surface in the house—the hall mirror, the shattered hand-glass in their bag, even the dark window above the sink—showed the well house. Not reflected; *shown*, the way a screen shows. The little stone building at the edge of the garden, its door sealed with lead sheeting and iron bands, its seal stamped with the Dravenmoor crest. And rising out of the ground around it, visible in the mirror-world though the actual garden was merely sodden, the water—black, gleaming, climbing.

In the mirror, the seal was already broken.

"Is that what will happen," Rosalind asked the glass, "or what I'll do?"

The reflection answered with neither. It showed them instead, briefly, the ledger lying open on the study desk behind them, and the seventeen crossed-out names, and beneath them a blank eighteenth line. The line waited. The line had always been waiting.

"No," Rosalind said. "I'm not signing anything. I'm not taking anything." They swallowed. "I'm giving it back."

For the first time, the face beneath their face smiled.

* * *

The garden had become a marsh. Each step sank ankle-deep, and the water pulled at Rosalind's boots with small insistent hands, and everywhere the smell of the opened earth. The sky had gone the colour of pewter, and the light came from everywhere and nowhere, shadowless, equinoctial—the one day of the year when dark and light hold each other in perfect balance, when the scales hang level, when, if the old stories are believed, debts can be called in either direction.

The well house stood at the garden's far end, smaller than memory, squatting under its mossed roof like a thing pretending to be architecture. The lead sheeting across the door had buckled outward. Water threaded from beneath it in a dozen bright ropes, all running the wrong way—inward, down, feeding whatever rose below.

Rosalind put their hands against the seal.

The lead was cold and soft as flesh. Underneath it they could feel vibration—a hum, or a voice held at the very threshold of pitch, the way Edith had described Maren singing: *low enough that you felt it in your teeth before you heard it in your ears.*

"You were wronged," Rosalind said to the door. Their breath fogged although the air was not cold. "In 1974 my family took your land and your songs and your life, and they called it settling, and the village let them, and every year since, everyone here has slept warm on what they buried in you. I can't undo it. The law won't touch it. But I can stop holding it."

They pressed their forehead to the lead.

"I'm going to open this. And I'm going to say your name. Out loud. As many times as it takes."

The humming changed key. It was, Rosalind realized with a shudder that was not entirely fear, the opening phrase of the ballad—the verse every singer in Hollowmere abandoned mid-line. The verse that ended, according to the ledger's marginalia in their grandmother's cramped hand, *with the water.*

Rosalind took the crowbar from where they'd left it against the wall that morning, wedged it under the lowest iron band, and pulled.

The lead screamed. That was the only word for it—a long metallic shriek that was almost, almost a note, and beneath it the hum surged, and the water around Rosalind's ankles spun into a slow spiral, tightening, attentive. The first band snapped. The second peeled away like a lid off a fruit. The third Rosalind tore free with their bare hands, cutting their palms on the rusted edges, and barely felt it.

The door swung inward onto darkness and the smell of deep water, and Rosalind descended.

* * *

Steps cut into the living rock, slick and narrow, spiralling down into the well shaft. The water was already at the fourth step and rising—visibly rising, a hand's width every few breaths, black and utterly still except where it met the stone, where it trembled like the skin of a struck drum. The walls sweated. Somewhere below, in the flooded dark, the hum had become words.

Rosalind knew the melody. They had known it since the first night, since Edith's crossed self and the half-verse dying in her throat. Now, rising up the shaft, it completed itself verse by verse, and the verses told the story the ledger had confessed in accounting language:

*She came to the well house for her deed,*
*the ink still wet upon her name,*
*and the sons of the house were waiting there*
*with rope and stone and no shame.*

*They wrote her songs in a stranger's book*
*and sold her voice by the pound,*
*and the village heard and the village slept*
*and the village bought the ground.*

Rosalind reached the last dry step and stood in the dark with the water climbing their shins, cold as a verdict, and opened their mouth.

"Maren Oduya."

The name fell into the shaft and the water shivered from rim to rim.

"Maren Oduya. Your land was never theirs. Your songs were never theirs. Your name is yours, and I return it. Your voice is yours, and I return it."

And then Rosalind did the thing the whole inheritance had been built to prevent, the thing the clause forbade, the thing seventeen heirs had refused and been erased for refusing, the thing their grandmother had warned against with her last written breath—*never give it your voice*—because the grandmother had understood, all along, that the voice was the debt, and that someday someone would have to pay it.

Rosalind sang.

Their voice was not beautiful. It was rough and cracking and half-swallowed by tears they hadn't noticed starting, and it didn't matter. They sang the stolen verses back down the shaft, the ones about the rope and the stone, the ones about the stranger's book, and then they sang the final verse—the one no living throat had ever finished—and the words came to them the way the lagged reflections had come, arriving a half-second before they were thought:

*And the water rose to take her home,*
*and the water kept her name,*
*and every year at the turning of the light*
*she calls it back again.*

"So take it back," Rosalind whispered. "Take it all back. Every note. Every year. Take the house. Take the name. Take the claim. I give it freely, and I sign nothing, and I keep nothing."

The water reached their waist. It should have been dragging them down—it had the weight of a drowning century—but it held them upright, gently, the way a congregation holds a person who has finally come forward to speak.

Above them, faintly, through the well house door, the mirrors began to break.

* * *

Rosalind heard it as a sequence, not a crash: the parlour glass first, a single clean note like a finger run around a wet bowl's rim; then the hall mirror, deeper; then the window above the sink; then, one after another, every reflective surface in Hollowmere's dead house, shattering in order, top floor to cellar, like a held breath released after fifty years—like a woman inhaling at last.

The water surged once, upward, a column of black light in the shaft, and in it—for one instant—Rosalind saw her whole. Not layered beneath their reflection, not lagging behind the world, but *present*: Maren Oduya, tall in the rising water, her head thrown back, her mouth open, singing the final verse in a voice that filled the shaft and the garden and the grey equinoctial sky, a voice with all its pounds and pence returned to it, a voice worth nothing now to anyone but itself, which is to say everything.

Then the column fell, the shaft emptied downward with a roar like an exhalation, and the house above them groaned from foundation to chimney—a long structural lament, the sound of a lie losing its footing.

Rosalind climbed. Water chased them up the steps, not angrily now but thoroughly, erasing each foothold as they left it, and they burst out of the well house door into the garden as the first wall of the great house folded inward with a sound like a giant kneeling.

They stood in the rain that had begun, unasked, to fall, and watched their inheritance go down into the earth that had paid for it—stone by stone, room by room, the study and the ledger and the locked drawer and the clause and the crest, all of it sinking into the black water that rose to meet it and then, quietly, began to subside, drawing the wreckage down after it like a tide reclaiming a shore.

By dusk there was nothing above ground but a flooded depression ringed with stones, smooth as a wound healed over, and one figure standing at its edge with bleeding hands and nothing at all.

Nothing, except the tape recorder in their bag, still running, still recording.

On the tape, beneath the collapse and the rain, a woman's voice finishes a song—and does not stop singing.
