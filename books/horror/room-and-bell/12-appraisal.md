## 12. Appraisal

I woke with my cheek against the kitchen table and the taste of pencil graphite in my mouth.

That was the first wrong thing, and I want to be clear about how I knew it was wrong, because knowing is the only weapon I've ever carried. I don't nap. I have never napped. Sleep, for me, has always been a negotiation conducted at night, under specific conditions, with a signed agreement in place — dark room, white noise, no unresolved line items. Falling asleep at a stranger's kitchen table at nine in the evening would require a version of Marguerite Voss that does not exist.

The second wrong thing was the light. When I'd sat down — when I *thought* I sat down — dusk had been pooling blue in the windows. Now it was full dark, and the clock over the pantry door said 2:47, which meant either four hours had passed or the clock had finally decided to lie in a new direction. Given that every other clock in the house was stopped at 3:12, I suppose I should have been grateful for variety.

The third wrong thing was my hands.

They were still moving.

Not much. A small, precise motion, the way they move when I'm doing final measurements on a sketch — the little adjustments, the confident ticks of a professional closing out a job. My right hand held my mechanical pencil. My left held down a sheet of paper from my own field kit, the good vellum I save for formal deliverables. And as I lifted my head off the table, both hands finished whatever they were doing, set the pencil down parallel to the paper's edge — *neatly*, the way I always do, the way no one else's hands ever have — and went still.

"Okay," I said to the empty kitchen. My voice came out sanded flat. "Okay."

The four place settings were still there. They had not moved. I had the sudden, unhelpful certainty that they were watching me the way an employer watches an employee work through lunch — not hostile, just *expectant*.

I looked down at the paper.

It took me a moment to understand what I was looking at, because it was beautiful. That's the part I keep circling back to, the part I can't file anywhere. It wasn't scrawled. It wasn't the jagged automatic-writing horror-movie nonsense you'd expect from a hand possessed by something ancient and hungry. It was *my* lettering. The tight, ruled capitals I learned from my first mentor, who used to say a surveyor's hand should look like it was printed by a machine that respected tradition. Every measurement was there. Setbacks, parcel dimensions, easement notes, the well flagged as an encumbrance with a neat little reference number. Footnotes. Cross-references. It was, page for page, the finest appraisal document I have ever produced, and I have produced appraisals for banks that would sue you over a misplaced decimal.

At the top, in the header block where the property valuation goes, my handwriting read:

**FAIR MARKET VALUE: ONE (1) LIFE.**

Below it, in the comments section, in smaller script, almost apologetic:

*Appraised "as-is." Comparable properties unavailable. Valuation determined by highest and best use.*

I sat there for a long time. Long enough for the refrigerator — silent since I arrived, because the power had been shut off nine months ago, a fact I had verified personally on day one — clicked on behind me and began to hum.

I did what I always do when the world stops making sense. I checked my work.

* * *

Here is the thing about being very good at your job: it gives you somewhere to put your fear. Fear needs a container or it spreads, and mine has always fit neatly inside procedure. So I picked up the pages and I read them like I'd read any submission from a junior surveyor, hunting for errors, because if I could find an error then this was a document, and documents come from people, and people can be reasoned with.

Page one: legal description of the parcel. Correct. Correct down to the metes and bounds I'd shot myself that first afternoon, including the two-degree discrepancy in the northeast corner that I'd noted but hadn't yet resolved. Except — I hadn't resolved it yet. I'd written the note in my field book and planned to re-shoot the corner in the morning. The appraisal didn't just include the discrepancy. It included my *planned resolution*, in a margin note in my own voice: *NE corner monument likely disturbed; recommend re-establishment from original 1961 plat. See Odile Voss deed records.*

I had never seen the original 1961 plat. I didn't know it existed until that sentence told me it did.

Page two: improvements. The house, described with a tenderness I don't usually permit myself. *Solid bones,* my handwriting said. *Craftsman-era joinery, unmolested by renovation. Rooms consistent in dimension except where they are not.* That last phrase was underlined twice, and next to it, in the column where I normally record depreciation, someone using my hand had written: *Depreciation: none. Appreciation: ongoing.*

Page three was the well.

The well got its own page. Full elevation sketch, drawn in cross-section, showing the stone shaft descending past the waterline and continuing — the drawing continued — down through strata I recognized from geology surveys and then through strata I did not, rendered in fine stippled shading, going down and down until the vellum ran out of room and the shaft simply exited the bottom edge of the page without terminating. In the margin: *Depth unknown. Depth possibly not a meaningful category.*

And beneath the sketch, the comparable-sales analysis. This was the part that made me stand up so fast my chair went over backward. Because I know how comps work. You find similar properties, recent sales, adjust for differences. There are no comparable properties for this. There is no MLS listing for a house that keeps its dead warm. But the analysis was complete, three comps cited with addresses, sale dates, adjustment grids — and I recognized all three addresses, because I had appraised all three houses, years apart, in different counties, and each one had struck me at the time as odd in a way I couldn't invoice: the Adeyemi place with its stripped-bare rooms, a farmhouse where a family had sold everything and left nothing, and two others whose files I remembered only as a faint professional unease, the sense of standing in a home that was politely pretending to be empty.

All three comps had sold for the same figure. Not a dollar amount.

The figure was written as a ratio. *1:1.* One life per life, adjusted for condition.

"That's not how any of this works," I told the kitchen. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled with the particular creak of a house shifting its weight — or its attention.

I turned to the signature block. There were two lines. The first said *Appraiser* and above it, in my handwriting, was my signature — and it was my signature, the real one, the one with the little hitch in the V that comes from signing too fast on cold mornings. I touched it. The ink was dry. The ink had been dry for hours.

The second line said *Tenant.*

It was blank.

I stood in my grandmother's kitchen at three in the morning holding the most competent piece of horror I had ever seen, and I understood — really understood, down in the body, below the thinking — what had happened while I slept. The house hadn't haunted me. Haunting is for amateurs; haunting is noise, rattling chains, cold spots, the cheap theatrics of things that want to scare you out. This thing had *delegated*. It had found the one process I trust more than my own senses, climbed inside it, and used my hands, my training, my own professional voice to write up the offer. It hadn't asked me to sign anything. It had done what banks do. It had prepared the paperwork and left it on the table for review.

*Don't sign anything,* my mother's six-years-dead number had texted me. At the time I'd filed it under impossible. Now it read less like a warning from beyond and more like a note from someone who knew the counterparty's habits.

"Review period," I said aloud, because saying things aloud helps, even when the things are absurd. "Standard practice. I'll take it under advisement."

The refrigerator clicked off. In the new silence, I heard — distinctly, from the direction of the hallway — the sound of a chair being pushed back from a table.

* * *

I did not go toward the sound. I am many kinds of fool but I am not that kind. I gathered the appraisal pages, folded them into my field bag, and then stood there realizing I had no idea where I intended to go, because the front door was forty feet away through a hallway that had already demonstrated a flexible relationship with geometry, and my car was at the curb, and between here and there was a house that had just spent four hours wearing my hands like gloves.

So I did the second-smartest thing available, which was to make tea. The power worked for the kettle, apparently, because the house wanted it to, and I stood watching the water heat with my back deliberately to the room, running the situation through the one framework that still felt like mine.

An appraisal is an opinion of value. That's the whole profession — educated opinion, defensible methodology, but opinion. Which means whoever wrote that document hadn't just forged my signature. It had adopted my *methodology*. It had reasoned the way I reason. And that was worse than mimicry, because mimicry you can catch — the mask slips, the voice goes wrong. This was the thing actually thinking in my categories, reaching my conclusions, and arriving at a value of one life with the calm confidence of a number that had never once been disputed.

Highest and best use. That's the doctrine every appraiser learns: a property's value isn't what it is, it's what it could optimally become. I'd written it ten thousand times. *Valued at its highest and best use.* And the house had taken my doctrine and applied it to itself, and apparently its highest and best use was as a vessel for keeping people — my mother, God knew who else — in escrow, and the price of that use, the market-clearing price, was one life per generation, renewable.

The kettle screamed. I poured the water over the tea bag and watched it bloom brown, and my hands — my own hands, I checked, I flexed them, they answered when I called — shook only a little.

Four hours. Gone. No dreams I could recall, no gap I could feel, just the smooth continuous present of a person who had been set down like a tool after use. I thought about Mrs. Whitfield, my first boss, telling me: *The day you catch yourself trusting your instruments over your eyes, quit, because you'll die on a hill made of calibration error.* I had spent tonight trusting my eyes over my instruments, and now my instruments included my own hands, and there was nothing left to check them against except—

Except the child.

I put my palm flat against my stomach, the way I'd been doing all night without letting myself name it, that low warm second pulse I'd attributed to the house, to the well water, to stress, to anything except the obvious. The pulse answered. Steady. Indifferent to leases. Mine, in the one sense of the word that no contract had gotten around to defining.

"Not her," I said. Or maybe "not him." I said it quietly, to the tea, to the dark window where my reflection hung over the garden like a bad double exposure. "Whatever the comps say. Whatever the market will bear. That line item is not for sale."

Behind me, softly, the pantry door opened six inches and stopped. I hadn't latched it. I was almost sure I hadn't latched it.

I drank my tea standing up, facing the door, and I did not sign anything, and I did not answer the pantry, and when the first gray suggestion of a fence post appeared at the edge of the yard — a shape that had not been there minutes before, a silhouette too solid for the hour — I told myself it was a shadow and shadows are nobody.

Then the shape raised one hand and knocked, gently, on air, at the property line, like a neighbor who knows better than to cross.

"Miss Voss?" a woman's voice called, low and urgent across the dead grass. "Miss Voss, I know you're awake. We need to talk before it knocks."

Somewhere in the house, every stopped clock ticked.
