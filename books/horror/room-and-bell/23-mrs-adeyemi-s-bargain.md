## 23. Mrs. Adeyemi's Bargain

The water had reached the second stair when the knocking stopped.

I want to be clear about what that meant, because at the time I thought it might mean mercy. In my experience — and by four-fifteen in the morning I was becoming an expert in things I had no experience with — courtesy from a house is never a good sign. A house that knocks is a house that wants something. A house that stops knocking has either gotten it or decided it no longer needs to ask.

The water was warm. That was the worst part. Not cold, not shocking, not the slap of ordinary floodwater — warm, like bathwater, like something that had been waiting for me to get in. It swirled around my ankles in slow, deliberate spirals, and every time I looked down at it, it showed me something: a kitchen with yellow curtains, a hallway hung with framed photographs of people smiling at someone just off-camera, a bedroom with a crib assembled but unoccupied. My future, apparently, came furnished.

I had retreated to the staircase because retreating to the staircase felt like doing something. Surveyors are trained never to retreat; we *reassess positions*. I reassessed my position on the third stair, one hand on the banister, which pulsed faintly under my palm like a throat swallowing.

That was when I saw her through the front window.

Mrs. Adeyemi stood at the fence line again, and this time she was inside it. The gate — which I had latched, because even in extremis I latch gates, it's a professional deformity — stood open, and she was walking up the path through water that parted around her shoes as if it had been instructed to be polite to her specifically.

"You shouldn't be here," I said, opening the door before she could knock. If there was one rule left in the universe I intended to enforce, it was that nobody knocked tonight. "It's not safe. The property line doesn't—"

"The property line," Mrs. Adeyemi said, "is exactly why I'm here." She looked past me into the hall, at the water sliding across the floorboards, and her expression was not fear. It was recognition. The way you look at a photograph of yourself taken during an illness. "You've read the contract."

"Most of it."

"Then you know sunrise is the deadline." She stepped inside without being invited, and the house let her, which frightened me more than anything else that had happened, because I had begun to think of the house as hostile, and here it was holding the door like a doorman who approved of the guest. "And you know the terms. One life. Or the memories. It always presents three options, but really it's one option wearing three coats."

"How do you know what it—"

"Because in 1988," she said, "my mother sat where you're sitting."

* * *

We ended up in the kitchen, because kitchens are where you go when the world ends. There's a reason every disaster movie eventually gets around to someone making tea. The four place settings were still on the table. Mrs. Adeyemi looked at them for a long moment, then deliberately turned two of them upside down, plates clacking against wood, cups set mouth-down like small bells silenced.

"That helps?" I asked.

"It annoys it," she said. "Which helps me." She sat down in the chair nearest the window — the chair that, in every photograph in this house, stood empty behind somebody. She did not seem to notice which chair she'd chosen, or perhaps she noticed and chose it anyway, the way you'd sit on the grave of someone you'd made peace with. "My name is Funmi Adeyemi. I was born Funmi Okonkwo. We lived at number eleven, two doors down, until 1988. My mother, Adaeze, signed a co-tenancy with your grandmother in 1971. Did Odile ever mention us?"

"No. Odile never mentioned anything. Odile mentioned the weather and whether I was eating enough."

A smile crossed her face briefly, like a fish under ice. "That sounds like her. My mother and yours—" she corrected herself, gently, "your grandmother kept accounts together. Not money. Debts. When the thing in the well answered your grandmother in 1971, it answered *both households*, because both households were on Vellum Lane and the well sits on the parcel boundary. Did you know that? Have you surveyed it?"

"I surveyed the whole street in 2019 for the drainage easement," I said slowly. "The well straddles the line between this lot and number eleven. I flagged it as an encroachment issue. Nobody followed up."

"Nobody ever follows up," Mrs. Adeyemi said. "That's how it survives. Every surveyor sees it, flags it, files it, forgets it. It's very good at being paperwork." She folded her hands on the table, and I saw them properly for the first time under the kitchen light — scarred, both of them, top to bottom, fine white lines like someone had written on her skin and then tried, repeatedly, to erase the writing. "In 1988, the lease came due again. Seventeen years. It comes due every seventeen years, or whenever a tenant dies, whichever comes first. Your grandmother paid in 1988. But the debt touches everyone in the household, and my mother decided — my mother, God rest her stubborn soul — decided that two households paying separately would weaken it. Divide the invoice, she thought. Split the interest. So she went to the well and offered to co-sign."

"What happened?"

Mrs. Adeyemi turned her hands over. The scars continued onto her palms, pale against brown skin, running in lines that almost formed letters and never quite managed it. "The house accepted. Of course it accepted. It accepts everything; refusal isn't in its vocabulary, only *terms*. And for six months we were fine. Better than fine — my mother slept for the first time since her sister died. The dreams stopped. The clocks ran. Then the house sent its adjustment."

"Adjustment."

"It's a landlord, Marguerite. When two tenants split a payment, a landlord doesn't reduce the rent. It increases the units." Her voice stayed level, the way voices go level when the alternative is screaming. "The debt didn't halve. It duplicated. One life owed to each household. And the house collected from ours first, because we had offered. Because offering is consent, and consent is the only currency it truly trades in. It took my brother Chidi in March of 1989. Heart failure, the doctors said. He was twenty-four. Then it took the *memory* of Chidi from all of us, as a kind of — I don't know the word. Handling fee. My mother lived another thirty years and could not tell you she'd ever had a son. There was a gap in every family photograph, and none of us could see the gap, only the strange way our eyes pointed at empty air in pictures from Christmas 1988." She touched the back of her neck, an old gesture, worn smooth by use. "I remember him. Only me. Do you know why?"

"Because you weren't home?"

"Because I fought it," she said. "I stood where you're standing and I told it I would rather die than forget my brother, and it — negotiated. It took his face instead of my memory of him. I remember loving my brother. I remember he was funny, that he bit his nails, that he once convinced me the moon was following us specifically. I remember all of that. I cannot remember his face. I have looked at photographs of him and felt nothing, like looking at a stranger, and the photographs themselves have faded wherever his face was, so there's no help there." She laid her scarred hands flat on the table. "This is what co-signing buys. This is the family business I inherited. I'm fifty-three years old and I am the last of my line because after Chidi, nobody in my family would live on this street, and the ones who stayed were collected."

The water in the hallway had gone quiet. Too quiet. Somewhere upstairs a clock — one of the stopped ones — ticked once, experimentally, like a thing testing whether it was allowed.

"So you're warning me," I said. "Thank you. Message received. I won't co-sign."

"No," Mrs. Adeyemi said. "I'm not warning you. I'm *offering*."

* * *

I want to record, honestly, what happened in my chest when she said that, because it wasn't noble. It wasn't the swell of gratitude you'd want from a person in a story. What happened was: for about four seconds, I wanted it. Four full seconds of pure animal relief, the feeling of a rope thrown to someone drowning — and I was, more or less literally, standing in rising water that wanted me to sign an appraisal valuing a house at exactly one human life.

"You'd split it with me," I said carefully. "Half a life each."

"Half a life, or half the memories, or — here's the part the house doesn't put in the brochure — half the *time*. The debt is denominated in years, Marguerite. One life means roughly forty years of someone's remaining span, or the equivalent in memory. Two signatures, it takes twenty from each. Twenty years off my life. I'm fifty-three. I can afford twenty." She smiled, and the smile had cost her something enormous to assemble. "I have been waiting seventeen years for this house to come due again. Do you understand? I have spent half my life preparing to stand in this kitchen. I know the clauses better than the house does. I have a copy of the 1971 contract — my mother transcribed it before she lost the nerve to read her own handwriting. I know where the flaw is, if there is one. You don't have to face sunrise alone. Nobody should face it alone. That's not a platitude, that's the entire lesson of this street."

"And what happens to you," I said, "when it collects its twenty years."

"I die at seventy-three instead of ninety-three. I've made peace with—"

"And if it adjusts again?" I heard my own voice go sharp, the voice I used with contractors who lowballed a bid. "If splitting it duplicates it again? You said yourself — divide the invoice, increase the units. Your mother tried exactly this. It took your brother. It took your mother's mind. It took your hands—" I gestured at the scars. "What are those, actually? What did it write on you?"

She looked down at her own hands as if they belonged to a colleague. "Receipts," she said quietly. "Every negotiation leaves a mark on whoever holds the pen. My mother held the pen in '88. These passed to me when she died. They itch when the house is thinking about me." She paused. "They're itching now."

"Then it's already adjusting," I said. "Isn't it. You walked in here and it started recalculating. You're not offering me a rope, Mrs. Adeyemi. You're offering to tie us together so we drown at the same speed."

For a long moment she didn't answer. The water in the hall crept another inch up the doorframe, patient as arithmetic. Outside, far off, a bird made a sound it had no business making at that hour — a single confused chirp, a clock striking the wrong number.

"My mother said almost the same words to me," Mrs. Adeyemi finally said. "Before she signed. She said, *Funmi, we will not make the house pay twice.* And I hated her for it, and she signed anyway, alone, and it ate her alive over thirty years in installments." Her jaw tightened. "So yes. Part of me came here tonight because I cannot watch it happen to someone else. And part of me came here because I am tired, and lonely, and the house has been whispering my name through the fence since sundown, and I am not a saint. I want you to know that. I want it on the record, whatever record survives tonight. I am not offering out of pure love. I'm offering because the offer costs me less than watching."

"That," I said, "is the most honest thing anyone has said to me all night. Including the house."

Something moved in the walls. Not a sound, exactly — a redistribution of weight, the way a large animal shifts in a stall.

"But I'm going to refuse," I said.

"Marguerite—"

"Let me finish, because if I don't say it fast I'll talk myself out of it, and I've watched myself talk myself into things all night. This house got its hooks into my grandmother because she loved someone too much to do the math. It got its hooks into your mother the same way. And every single person who's tried to beat it has tried to beat it with *more* — more signatures, more sacrifice, more love stacked on the scale. And every time, it thanks them and adds a zero." I pulled the contract from my jacket — I'd been carrying it like a wound dressing — and flattened it on the table between the place settings. "You said you know the clauses. Good. Then help me do the one thing nobody's done. Help me *read*. Not sacrifice. Not co-sign. Read. I'm a forensic property surveyor, Mrs. Adeyemi. Finding the flaw in a deed is literally my job. Everyone who faced this thing was grieving or terrified or both. I'm grieving and terrified, but I'm also — God help me — the only person on this street who reads footnotes for a living."

Mrs. Adeyemi stared at me. Then she laughed — a short, cracked, genuine laugh, the first real sound I'd heard in that house that the house hadn't made itself.

"Odile used to say you were the stubborn one," she said. "She wrote that. In the margins of her ledger. *The little one is stubborn as a surveyor's stake.*"

"You've read her ledger?"

"I've read everything your grandmother ever wrote. My mother kept copies of her letters. They were friends, in the way two women who share a secret disease are friends." She drew a breath, and I watched her decide something — watched the decision move through her like weather. "All right. No co-signing. But hear my condition, because I get one. If you find the flaw and it requires a payment — any payment, a memory, a year, a finger — you come to me first. Not as a co-signer. As a neighbor. Because the difference between a sacrifice and a waste is whether anyone offered to share it."

I looked at her scarred hands resting beside the contract, at the four overturned place settings, at the dark hallway where my future lay furnished and ankle-deep.

"That's fair," I said.

"Good." She pulled the contract toward her and produced reading glasses from her cardigan with the unhurried competence of a woman who had waited seventeen years to use them. "Now. Clause fourteen. *Renewal upon default.* Read it with me, and read what it *doesn't* say, because this creature drafts like a lawyer who's never been audited. It assumes certain things about households. It assumes certain things about consent. And I have spent decades wondering whether anyone ever checked its assumptions."

The water in the hall began to rise faster.

Somewhere above us, behind a door that hadn't existed yesterday, a woman with my mother's voice called my name — softly, twice, the way you'd call someone in for dinner.

Mrs. Adeyemi's hands stopped itching. They went still as stones.

"It knows," she whispered. "It's changing the document."

"Then we read fast," I said.
