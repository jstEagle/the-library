## 31. Whiteout

I want to say I hesitated.

That would be the decent thing to report — a long moment at the threshold of my own mind, weighing what I was about to lose against what everyone else had already paid. But the truth is that when I said *I'll pay the difference myself*, the word *myself* came out of me with the smoothness of a signature on a form I'd been reading all night. Maybe that's what expertise does to you. You spend twenty years appraising other people's losses and eventually you learn to price your own without flinching.

The chair sat in the middle of the kitchen, ankle-deep water lapping at its legs, and the shape in it regarded me with the patience of a bank teller watching someone finally count out their cash.

"You understand," it said, in my grandmother's voice, "that this option has never been exercised."

"Then your contract has a gap," I said. "Contracts always have gaps. That's why they hire surveyors."

"It is not a gap. It is simply that no one—" The voice shifted, tried my mother's, found it insufficient, settled back into Odile's. "No one gives up the love itself. They give up faces. Dates. The sound of a voice. Those are easy; those are inventory. What you are offering is the *asset*."

"I know what I'm offering."

"Do you?" And here it did something almost gentle. It leaned forward — the shape did, the suggestion of shoulders tilting — and said, in a voice that was neither my grandmother's nor my mother's nor anyone's but its own, which turned out to be the most frightening thing yet: "When it's gone, you will not miss her. You will not know there is anything to miss. There will be no wound, Marguerite. Only smooth skin where you never remember being cut."

"Then it should be easy for both of us."

The kitchen clock — stopped at 3:12 since dusk — ticked.

"All right," it said.

And the sun began to rise.

* * *

You'd think memory extraction would feel like theft. A yanking, a tearing, something reaching in through the ear. It doesn't. It feels like drainage.

That's the only word I have for it, and I'm a woman who spent that same night cataloguing wrongnesses like defects on a punch list, so believe me when I tell you I searched hard for a better one. Drainage. Everything going down and out through some channel I couldn't locate, a slow spiral pull behind my eyes, and the first thing to go was trivia — thank God for small mercies — the smell of my mother's car, which had been a 1998 Corolla and smelled of rain on hot vinyl and the vanilla air freshener she bought in three-packs. Gone. Not faded. *Gone*, the way a room goes when the lights cut: not darker, just absent, as if it had never been furnished at all.

I gripped the edge of the kitchen table. Four place settings. I didn't know why four mattered. I held onto the number.

"That's not how this works," the shape said, almost kindly. "You can't hold numbers."

"Watch me."

The second thing to go was my mother's laugh. I felt it leave the way you feel a tooth come loose — not pain exactly, but a horrible awareness of the socket afterward, the tongue returning again and again to the absence. I opened my mouth to say her name and found the name still there, mercifully, *Marguerite's mother, born June—* and then the month went, and the day, and I stood in the rising water holding a name with no birthday attached, a label on an empty jar.

The photographs were changing on the wall. I could see them from the kitchen doorway — I had moved to the doorway at some point, or been moved, the water now at mid-shin and warm as bathwater, warm as the well, everything in this house warm, that was the trick of it, horror should be cold and this was never once cold — and in the frames, faces were going soft. Not dissolving like film in acid. More like overexposure. Light eating them from the edges inward, features bleaching toward white while the backgrounds stayed crisp: the lake house intact, the Christmas tree intact, the birthday cake with its candles intact, and where my mother had been, a brightness shaped like nothing at all.

"No," I said, and heard how small it sounded, and hated that.

"You can stop," the shape offered. From the chair it watched me with what I want to call compassion, though I suspect it was only accuracy. "At any point before sunrise completes. The original terms remain available. Your life. The child's. Or the memories of the kept ones only — you'd retain yourself, Marguerite. You'd walk out of here whole, just lighter. Most people choose that one, when they understand it."

"There are no most people. This clause has never been exercised. You said so."

"Yes." A pause, and in the pause the water rose another inch, and somewhere upstairs a door closed politely. "I find I'm curious about you."

"Put it in writing."

Something that was almost a laugh moved through the room — through the walls, through the water, a shiver of timber. Then the light strengthened at the windows, gray going to pearl going to gold at the rim of the world beyond Vellum Lane, and the extraction resumed, and I learned what it actually takes to hold onto a feeling by brute will.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about losing someone twice: the second time is worse, because the first time you at least got to keep the loss.

Grief, it turns out, is made entirely of memory. Take the memories and the grief doesn't hurt — it just stops existing, and you're left standing in a flooded kitchen at dawn feeling absolutely nothing about a woman whose name you still technically know, and the nothing is so much worse than any ache I've ever carried, because the ache at least proved she'd been real.

Her hands went next. I don't mean photographs of her hands. I mean the knowledge of her hands — flour to the second knuckle, the burn scar on her left thumb from the oven door that stuck, how she'd press two fingers to my forehead to check for fever the way doctors do, flat and businesslike and tender underneath the businesslike. Drained. I felt them go like water off a table, and I grabbed — God help me, I grabbed at the air, at the drain itself, whatever channel this was, and the shape in the chair said:

"Don't fight the current. It only pulls harder."

"Everything you've said tonight sounds like a fortune cookie written by a bailiff."

"And yet."

My mother's face was the last of her to go, because I fought for it, I will say that. I stood in the water up to my knees and I held her face the way you hold a door shut in a storm, with your whole body, with your shoulder and your hip and your cursing breath, and for a while — a real while, minutes, the sky going from pearl to pink at the windows — I kept it. Brown hair going gray early. The crease between her brows that appeared when she did sums. Eyes I have looked into across thirty years of kitchens and hospitals and one terrible hospice room, eyes that—

Gone.

Not taken. *Drained.* Down and out, spiraling, and the socket where her face had lived in me filled with smooth nothing, and I heard myself make a sound I have never made before or since, some animal note that had no language in it at all, and the shape in the chair did not gloat. That's the thing I'll say for it, if I ever say anything for it. It did not gloat. It watched me pay with the solemnity of a witness at a signing, because that is what I was doing. Signing. In the oldest ink there is.

The grandmother went easier, which broke me worse. Odile drained out of me like silt in fast water — the lavender and cigarette smell, the way she pronounced my name with the French weight on the last syllable, the specific crime of her hands dealing cards on a summer porch — and I barely fought for her, because I'd spent my whole fight on the other face, and there was a shame in that too, a triage of love, choosing who to lose more slowly. The house took the difference as a discount. Houses do.

By the time the sun's first true ray came over the fence line and lit the floodwater gold, I knew three facts and nothing else:

A woman named my mother had died six years ago. A woman named Odile had owned this house. I was standing in it.

And underneath the three facts, deeper than facts go, in the bedrock under all the drained soil, one thing remained. Not a face. Not a voice. Not a date or a smell or a scar. Just — a warmth. A directional warmth, like heat from a window you can't see, coming from nowhere, pointing at no one. The pure residue of having been loved once, thoroughly, by people whose names the water had taken.

I held onto it with brute will. I held onto it the way drowning things hold rocks.

It was enough. God forgive me, it was enough.

* * *

The draining stopped at sunrise the way a tap stops — not gradually, but with a final shudder, a knock in the pipes of me, and then silence.

The water went out all at once. Not down the stairs, not out the doors — *out*, downward, everywhere at once, the flood sinking through the floorboards as if the ground beneath the house had opened its throat, and inside of a minute I stood in a dry kitchen in a dry house with wet socks and a head full of smooth places.

The chair was gone.

The clock read 6:04 and was ticking, ticking, ticking, running forward like an honest machine, and outside the window Vellum Lane lay in ordinary morning light with dew on Mrs. Adeyemi's roses and a milk cart idling somewhere streets away, the whole world resuming its paperwork.

On the wall, the photographs hung straight and blameless. Families. Just families. A woman at a lake whose face meant nothing to me. A birthday party where every seat was occupied by exactly the people sitting in it. No extra chair anywhere. I checked. I walked the hallway checking every frame like a surveyor checking benchmarks, and every photograph showed precisely what it had always shown, and I felt nothing, and the nothing had edges, and the edges were the worst part — I could feel the shapes of the absences the way a tongue finds the socket of a pulled tooth, except there was no tooth to remember, only the smooth gum of gone.

In the study, the ledger lay open on the desk. I hadn't left it open. The final page bore my grandmother's initials beside the recurring entry, and beneath them, in fresh brown-black ink, a new line had written itself while I wasn't looking:

**RENT, PAID IN FULL. PAID BY THE TENANT'S ISSUE. METHOD OF PAYMENT: VOLUNTARY.**

And below that, smaller, in handwriting I recognized from the contract behind the pantry wallpaper — Odile's hand, though Odile was nine months dead and I no longer knew why that handwriting should matter to me, only that my chest did something complicated when I saw it:

*Thank you, little one.*

I didn't know who that was for.

I stood in the study of a dead woman's house, holding a ledger full of payments I couldn't remember witnessing, and I cried — hard, racking, reasonless crying, the kind you cry at funerals for strangers — and I want to be precise about this, because precision was the last professional habit I had left: I cried without knowing whom I was crying for. The grief was real. Its object was gone. I was mourning a hole in the exact shape of people, wearing their names like clothes left behind in an emptying house.

Somewhere in the rooms above me, a door closed. Politely. For the last time.

Or so I believed, standing there in the gold morning light with my smooth places and my one held stone.

I did not yet know about the echo.

I did not yet know that some things, once invited in, learn to wait — quietly, patiently, in the margins of ultrasound monitors and the corners of photographs not yet taken — for the next tenant to come due.

But the house knew.

Houses always keep the files.
