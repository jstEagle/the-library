## 38. Chapter 38

My daughter's school photographs arrive every October in a padded envelope that smells like glue and institutional optimism, and every year I open them at the kitchen table with the same ritual I bring to everything now: measure twice, trust once, and never, ever sign anything without reading it three times.

I have rules. I made them myself, which is the only way rules stick. Rule one: no wells. When we bought this house — a plain, honest house on a street called Corliss Avenue, where the water table is documented and boring — I had the backyard surveyed before we closed, and when the surveyor's map showed an old cistern footprint near the fence line, I paid to have it filled with concrete and gravel and a layer of topsoil deep enough to bury a rumor. The crew foreman thought I was eccentric. Eccentric is fine. Eccentric is what sanity looks like when it's been through something.

Rule two: no mirrors facing each other. Rule three: clocks get wound weekly, all of them, and if any clock in my house disagrees with any other clock by more than a minute, I correct it immediately, because I once spent a night in a place where every clock said 3:12 and I have not forgiven time for how it behaved there.

Rule four, the big one, the one I don't write down anywhere: if you see a chair where a chair shouldn't be, you do not sit near it, you do not move it, and you do not look at it long enough for it to notice you looking.

I am telling you my rules because last Tuesday I broke rule four without meaning to, and because by Friday I had broken most of the others too, and because a person in my position should keep records. Records are how I survived the first time. Records are the only inheritance I trust.

* * *

The envelope sat on the hall table for two days before I opened it, which was unlike me. I want to be honest about that. I have never been a procrastinator; hesitation is a luxury for people whose pasts stay put. But something about the weight of it — heavier than last year's, thicker — made me carry it around the house like a letter from a doctor, and finally, on Thursday evening, with Wren doing homework at the kitchen table behind me, I slit it open.

Wren is seven. She has her grandmother Odile's jawline, though she'll never know it, and my stubbornness, which she demonstrates hourly. She was named in a moment of weakness I've never regretted: I wanted her to have a bird's name, something that belonged to the air and not to the ground. Something that could leave.

I shook the photographs out onto the table.

The first one was the standard package: Wren against the mottled blue backdrop, gap-toothed, hair escaping its braid, wearing the expression of a child who has been told to smile by a stranger with a camera and is complying under protest. Perfectly ordinary. I set it aside.

The second one was the class composite, thirty small faces in a grid, and I was halfway through checking that Wren's row was straight — a habit, I check grids, grids are honest — when I saw it.

Behind the third row, between two children whose names I didn't know, there was a chair.

Not a chair in the classroom sense. Not a folding chair or a plastic stackable. A wooden chair, high-backed, dark, the kind of chair that belongs in a farmhouse kitchen in a photograph from 1971, and it stood behind the third row of children as if it had been posed there, empty, patient, its back to the wall of the gymnasium that was clearly visible on either side of it.

I did what any reasonable person does. I put the photograph face-down on the table, walked to the sink, ran cold water over my wrists until my pulse agreed to behave, and told myself the following things, in order: printing errors happen. Composites are assembled digitally, from individual portraits, and sometimes the software does strange things. There is a rational explanation. There is always a rational explanation, and the fact that I own a laminated card in my desk drawer listing the rational explanations for phenomena I have personally witnessed does not make me crazy, it makes me thorough.

Then I turned the photograph back over, because I am also, God help me, thorough in the other direction.

The chair was still there. And now that I looked properly — now that I let myself look, which took about four seconds and cost me something I can't name — I could see that it wasn't quite in focus. Everything else in the composite was crisp, digital, modern. The chair had the soft edges of an old photograph, the grainy warmth of film, as if it had been developed in a tray of chemicals in a darkroom that no longer existed, and then pasted in.

"Wren," I said, keeping my voice at the register I use for asking about homework. "Did your class take any photos besides the regular ones? In the gym, maybe? With props?"

She looked up from her math worksheet with the particular patience seven-year-olds reserve for questions with obvious answers. "There was the funny one. Where you jump."

"Anyone sitting on anything?"

"Nope." She went back to her fractions. Then, without looking up, in the mild tone children use for observations they don't know are landmines: "Except Miss Okafor sat on a stool for hers. But they cut her out 'cause she blinked."

A stool. Not a high-backed wooden chair with a spindle back and a seat worn pale in the middle by decades of sitting.

I know that chair. You don't forget a chair like that. It stood in the kitchen on Vellum Lane my whole childhood, and my grandmother sat in it every morning, and in every photograph I ever found in that house — including photographs taken in other houses, other states, other decades — it appeared just behind the subjects, empty, waiting, closer in each frame than the last.

I had watched it burn. I had stood on Vellum Lane — no. I hadn't. I'd recommended demolition, signed the paperwork, driven past the lot afterward and felt nothing, because feeling had been the price of the paperwork. The house came down in March of that year. I read the demolition report the way I read all reports, closely, and nowhere in it was there any mention of furniture being removed, because there was no furniture to remove. The inventory listed the house as vacant. Every chair accounted for: zero.

So where, I asked the kitchen, in the flat tone of a professional addressing a defect, did you get the chair?

The kitchen declined to answer. Kitchens usually do. It's the other rooms you have to watch.

* * *

I did not sleep much Thursday night, which will surprise no one. I spent it the way I spend bad nights: working. I scanned the composite at high resolution, and the scan showed the chair exactly as the print did, which ruled out — nothing, actually, but it felt rigorous at the time. I emailed the photography company's customer service address, composing the message carefully so that I sounded like a mildly annoyed parent and not like a woman asking a vendor whether their compositing software had begun channeling the dead. *There appears to be an object in the third row that wasn't in the original scene,* I wrote. *Please advise.* Professional. Bland. The kind of email that gets a coupon for ten percent off your next package.

At 2 a.m. I gave up on sleep and got out the box.

Everyone keeps a box. Mine is a fireproof document safe, because irony is a discipline, and inside it are the artifacts of the year I do not discuss at dinner parties: my grandmother's ledger with its columns of dates and amounts and RENT, PAID IN FULL; the pantry contract, or rather the photographs of it, since the original burned with the house; Mrs. Adeyemi's phone number, though she died two winters ago and I still haven't decided what to do with it; and the final page of the ledger, the one I found against the fence after the demolition, in handwriting I didn't recognize and believed anyway. *Thank you, little one.*

I laid the class composite beside the old photographs from Vellum Lane and compared them the way I compare boundary surveys: edge to edge, feature to feature.

Same chair. Same wear pattern on the seat. Same slight lean to the left, as if one leg were a half-millimeter short — and I would know, because I measured that chair once, during the appraisal, with calipers, at three in the morning, because measuring was the only prayer I knew. One leg, half a millimeter short. The lean matched.

And then I noticed the thing that made me close the safe and sit very still in the dark for a while.

In the old photographs, across forty years of them, the chair moved. Closer to the camera each time. My grandmother's albums documented its approach frame by frame, a slow patient dolly shot across three generations, and I understood, standing in that house with the albums spread around me, that the sequence had an end. That the last frame would be the chair filling the lens. That I was looking at a countdown.

The class composite was the fourth photograph since the fire.

The first was Wren's kindergarten picture, two years ago — and yes, I went and checked, digging the envelope out of the memory bin in the closet, and there it was, so faint I'd missed it entirely: a smudge behind her shoulder, wood-dark, that I had taken for a shadow. The second was our family portrait from last Christmas, taken at a studio in the mall, and the chair stood behind the photographer's stool, far away, small, at the back of the frame like a figure at the end of a corridor. The third was Wren's school photo from this fall, the single portrait, and the chair was nearer — mid-ground now, sharp enough to show the worn seat.

Four frames. Each one closer. And I had missed the first three because grief had taught me not to look at backgrounds, the way a woman who has lost someone learns not to look at faces in crowds.

It was approaching again. Or — I made myself consider the alternative, because the alternative was worse and worse is usually true — it had never left. It had simply been waiting for a new album to develop itself into. Houses burn. Leases, I learned on Vellum Lane, are sturdier than houses.

* * *

Friday morning I broke rule two, which I want noted for the record.

Wren came down to breakfast carrying her sketchbook — she draws constantly, birds mostly, and houses, and a recurring figure she calls the Sitting Lady, whom I have never asked about because asking is an invitation and I know precisely how invitations work — and she flipped it open to show me her homework, which was not homework.

"Miss Okafor says draw a picture of your family," she said. "But I already did it."

The drawing showed our kitchen, rendered in the confident wrong proportions of a seven-year-old: the table, the window, three figures with yellow-crayon hair — Wren, me, and a tall shape beside me that I chose, in the moment, to interpret as myself drawn twice, because children do that.

"Who's this?" I asked, tapping the extra figure, and heard my own voice come out level, which cost more than the question.

"That's the lady who visits." Wren said it the way she'd say *that's the mail carrier*. "She doesn't talk. She just sits." She pointed at the corner of the drawing, and I saw that in the corner, drawn smaller and darker than everything else, pressed hard enough into the paper to dent it, was a chair. Occupied. By a shape almost like a person.

"When does she visit?" I asked. Keep it flat. Keep it clinical. You're taking a statement, not having a conversation.

"At night. She sits in the corner till the sun comes up and then she goes back in the floor." Wren picked up her toast. "She smells like the well."

We don't have a well. We have a concrete-filled cistern footprint under two feet of topsoil, and my daughter has never in her life seen a well, and I have never described one to her, and the word has never been spoken in this house in her hearing. I checked the ceiling, absurdly, as if the word might have come from above. Then I checked myself, because there was a second heartbeat once, years ago, that turned out to belong to her, and I remember what it taught me: the fine print is always in the body.

"Wren." I knelt by her chair, and I made myself ask the question I had sworn on several separate occasions never to ask, because the whole architecture of my rules rests on not asking. "Has anyone ever knocked? At night? At the doors?"

She considered this with her mouth full. "The front one. But you told me not to answer it, so I don't." A pause, and then, with the terrible casualness of children delivering catastrophe: "Grandma answers it, though."

I have no mother. I have a mother-shaped absence with a six-year head start on me, and a Room 3:12 full of forgery in a house that no longer exists, and I have spent seven years teaching myself that the voice in the walls is a recording, that the thing wearing her learned her from photographs, that love can be counterfeited and the counterfeit is the whole point.

"She does?" I said.

"Yeah. She comes out of the bathroom mirror after baths and goes downstairs." Wren frowned, suddenly uncertain, reading my face the way children read weather. "Is that bad? Is Grandma not supposed to?"

Every rule I have, every defense, every concrete-filled hole and wound clock and unturned mirror, was built for a haunting that stayed outside my daughter. None of them were built for a haunting that had already gotten inside her vocabulary, her sketchbook, her nights, calling itself by the kindest name it could find.

"No, sweetheart," I said, and kissed the top of her head, and smelled shampoo and underneath it, faintly, unmistakably, water — cold, mineral, deep-water smell, the smell of a well that reflects rooms no one has built. "Everything's fine. Eat your toast."

* * *

That evening I did the thing I should have done two days ago, and I'm writing this down so that whatever comes next, the record shows I moved as fast as I could, which is the only excuse anyone ever really has.

I called the number in the safe. Mrs. Adeyemi's number, dead two winters, dialed anyway, because rule five — unwritten, until now — is that in matters of Vellum Lane you exhaust every option including the impossible ones, especially the impossible ones. It rang. That alone should have ended the call; dead women's numbers ring as *not in service*, they don't ring warm and live in my ear at dusk.

On the fourth ring, someone answered. Not Mrs. Adeyemi. A voice I knew better than my own reflection, gentle, unhurried, coming through the line with the faint acoustic hollowness of a large tiled room — a bathroom, I thought wildly, a bathroom mirror somewhere, holding a phone.

"Marguerite," my mother said. "You measured everything else. Come measure the one thing you left."

The line stayed open. I could hear, beneath her voice, water moving — not draining, filling. Rising.

And from the hallway behind me, from the front door of a house on Corliss Avenue where the survey shows no anomalies and the clocks all agree, came the sound of knocking. Polite. Patient. Three knocks, a pause, three more — the rhythm of someone who knows they will be answered eventually, because they have read the lease, and they know exactly how many pages are left.

Wren looked up from her drawing, and smiled, and said: "That's her."
