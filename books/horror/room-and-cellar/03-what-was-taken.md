## 3. What Was Taken

The house on Vesper Lane had been waiting so long for me that the porch boards didn't creak. They exhaled.

I stood at the bottom of the steps with my hands jammed in my coat pockets like a woman being frisked by her own conscience, and Wren stood on the porch exactly where he'd been standing in 1974, one foot on the rail, chewing a piece of grass that had no business surviving forty years of seasons. Same overalls. Same gap where his left eyetooth should have been. Same patient, maddening smile of a boy who has never once been wrong and knows it.

"You could just tell me," I said. "Whatever it is. You could say it out loud here on the sidewalk like a normal hallucination."

"It's not my thing to tell." He hopped down off the rail. "It's yours."

"That's what every terrible therapist I've ever fired said."

"Then they were right too."

The front door was open. Of course it was open. Houses like this don't knock; they just leave themselves unlatched and let you do the walking. The hallway smelled the way it smelled when I was eight—dust and cold ash and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going bad in a bowl nobody remembers setting out. My flashlight found wallpaper the color of old teeth, peeling in long curls. It found the staircase. It found the door under the stairs.

Small. Painted shut once, long ago, then painted again, and again, layer over layer over layer, the way you'd bandage a wound if you were very stupid and very committed to never looking at it. Forty years of paint. Somebody—I—had needed that door buried under more than color.

"I don't remember that door," I said.

"Yeah," Wren said gently. "You do."

* * *

1974 smelled like rain that hadn't fallen yet.

Thomas went first down the cellar stairs, because Thomas always went first, because he was eleven and eleven-year-old boys are constitutionally incapable of letting their little sisters lead. I followed with the candle-end we'd stolen from the kitchen drawer, and the dark below took us the way water takes stones—one step, two, three, and then the light behind us was a coin and then a thread and then nothing.

There was a voice down there. That's the part I need you to understand, the part I spent four decades not understanding: there was a *voice*, and it was kind. That's the trick nobody warns you about. Monsters in stories roar. This one murmured. It sounded like a grandmother reading by lamplight, like someone smoothing your hair back when you're feverish, and it knew our names before we gave them.

*Mirabel,* it said. *Mirabel Thornbury. Eight years old. Asthmatic. Afraid of the space under her own bed. Your mother sings you to sleep with a song about a lark, and you pretend to be asleep longer than you need to so she'll sing it twice.*

"Tommy," I whispered. "Tommy, who's saying that?"

But the voice wasn't finished with me. *The house is hungry this year,* it said, the way you'd remark on weather. *It must be fed a name. One name, little Miri, and the rest of you walks out into the sun. Choose.*

And eight-year-old Mirabel Thornbury—who was afraid of the dark, afraid of dogs, afraid of the sound of her own father's truck pulling into the drive—stood in that cellar holding a candle stub and did the arithmetic children are never supposed to be able to do.

"Take his," she said.

Not *take him.* Take his *name.* She was precise even then; she'd grow up to edit other people's sentences for a living. And the voice made a sound like a page turning, and Thomas turned around in the dark and looked at me with my own eyes' exact gray, and said, "Miri? Miri, why are you crying?"—because as far as he knew, nothing had happened at all. Something had happened to him, but the knowing of it had been poured into me like tea into a cup I hadn't asked to hold.

We walked up the stairs. He complained about missing dinner. I held his hand the whole way and gripped it like a drowning girl grips driftwood, and upstairs Mother sang the lark song only once that night, and I lay in bed memorizing the shape of having a brother, because some part of me—the honest part, the part that stayed in that cellar—knew I was going to need the memory later, when the borrowed years ran out.

He died the next spring. Fever, they said. Quick, they said. A shame, they said.

They said a lot of things in 1975.

* * *

Wren put his hand flat against the painted door, and the paint split along seams I swear weren't there a second ago, curling away like bark.

"The house kept your half," he said. "You kept his. That was the trade. It couldn't take him whole while you carried the name—it needed you to come back and finish it. So it waited. It got very good at waiting." He glanced at me sideways, and for the first time since the porch, the boyishness dropped off his face like a mask slipping its string. "It sent you the callers, Miri. It hummed your mother's song through dead women's mouths. It's been calling its debt home all week."

"The papers," I said. My voice came out thin. "You said before the papers are signed. What papers?"

"The Hendersons sold last month. There's a young couple coming today. Kowalczyk. They've got a daughter." He said it without cruelty, which was worse. "Houses like this don't stay empty forever. Hunger finds tenants."

Upstairs, faintly, through forty years of floorboards: a car door. Voices. A laugh—a woman's laugh, bright, unguarded, the laugh of someone who thinks she's buying a fixer-upper with good bones.

I looked at the opened door and the dark beyond it, and the dark looked back with the patience of a creditor, and I understood—not believed, *understood*, the way you understand gravity or grief—that the house had never been calling the dead home at all.

It had been calling me.

Every caller who described their own funeral. Every sign-off. *Come home, Miri.* Not an invitation. An invoice.

"They're signing early," Wren said. "We should go down."

Downstairs. Down the stairs I'd climbed once already with my brother's hand in mine and his name in my mouth like a stone I'd swallowed. Above us, a realtor's cheerful voice said something about *so much natural light.*

"Mirabel," Wren said, and used my whole name for the first time in forty years, "it's time to give it back."

I put my foot on the top stair.
