## 4. Choir of the Drowned

The bell rang at dusk again, and this time Lena was ready for it.

"Ready" was perhaps generous. She had a thermos of tea going cold on the surveyor's table, a torch in her hand, and a plan that consisted entirely of the words *follow it and see*. Fifteen years in property surveying had taught her that most mysteries dissolved under direct observation. Damp turned out to be condensation. Ghosts turned out to be bad plumbing. Nine times out of ten, the thing making noises in the night was a pipe.

She had never yet learned what happened the tenth time. She suspected Hollowmere House was about to educate her.

The tolling came from below — which was absurd, because bells lived above, in towers, in the sky where sensible ringing things belonged. This one came up through the floorboards like sound traveling through water, muffled and round, each note arriving slightly after she expected it, as though it had to swim to reach her.

Lena set down her tea, picked up the torch, and went looking for the cellar door.

She found it behind the linen cupboard on the ground floor — a door that had not been there yesterday, which no longer surprised her so much as annoyed her. The house kept rearranging itself the way a cat rearranges itself in a sunbeam: lazily, without apology, certain of its right to do so.

"Noted," she said to the house, and opened the door.

Stone steps descended into a dark that her torch pushed back grudgingly, the beam going soft at the edges as if the light itself were reluctant. The air changed character halfway down — cooler, wetter, tasting of riverbed and old coins. And beneath it all, the bell, closer now, resonating in her sternum more than her ears.

At the bottom of the stairs, the cellar floor was underwater.

Lena stopped on the last step and stared. The water lay flat and black and utterly still, covering the flagstones to a depth of perhaps four inches, reflecting her torch beam back up at her like a mirror laid over a grave. It should not have been there. She had read the drainage survey. Hollowmere sat on chalk; its cellars were recorded as dry as a sermon. There was no spring, no leak, no broken main within a hundred meters. The water had no source.

It simply was, the way the extra corridor simply was, the way the bell simply rang.

"You're not real," Lena told it conversationally. Talking to houses was a habit she'd developed around year six of the job, mostly as a way of staying sane in empty buildings. "You're a damp problem with ambitions."

The water did not dignify that with a response. But the bell tolled again — one long note, patient as tide — and somewhere across the flooded cellar, something moved.

* * *

Lena's first instinct, honed by fifteen years and one very bad night she did not think about, was to go back up the stairs and lock herself in the car until morning. Her second instinct, the newer and more troublesome one, was that the movement had looked less like an attack and more like an answer.

She stepped off the last stair into the water.

It was cold — properly cold, the cold of deep places — but only ankle-deep, and the flagstones beneath were firm and even. Her torch swept the cellar: vaulted brick ceiling sweating moisture, wine racks rotted to lace along one wall, and at the far end, where the cellar widened into what might once have been a cold store, the figures stood.

There were seven of them.

They stood in the water in a loose half-circle, facing the wall, and they were singing. That was the only word for it. Their mouths moved in unison, opening and closing with the bell's rhythm, though no sound came from them — or rather, the sound came from everywhere else, from the water and the bricks and the air itself, the drowned voices diffused into the room like ink into water. They wore clothing from another century: smocks, a butcher's apron, a woman's shawl gone grey with decades underwater. Water dripped steadily from their hair and hems, feeding the pool they stood in, and Lena understood with a lurch that the water had no source because *they* were the source. The cellar wasn't flooded. The cellar was full of them.

Her body did the sensible thing. It made her back up two steps, heart slamming, torch shaking so badly the beam strobed across the figures like a faulty film.

But they didn't turn. Didn't lurch. Didn't do any of the things figures in cellars were supposed to do. They stood facing the wall, mouths moving together, and the quality of their stillness was so complete, so *patient*, that Lena's terror found nowhere to attach itself and instead curdled slowly into something else.

Recognition.

Not of them — she'd never seen them, they'd been dead before her grandmother was born. Recognition of the posture. She knew people who faced walls and sang without sound. She had been one, once, fifteen years ago, standing very still and saying nothing while something terrible happened just outside her silence. These weren't monsters. Monsters moved toward you. These were waiting. There was a difference, and the difference was everything, and Lena hated that she could see it because seeing it meant she couldn't un-see it, couldn't file them under *pipes* and go home.

"All right," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "I see you."

The singing continued. The bell tolled. Water lapped at her ankles with each toll, tiny waves radiating outward from the seven figures, as though the song traveled through them into the flood.

"I'm a surveyor," she added, because apparently her coping mechanism under mortal dread was professional small talk. "I'm here to value the property. Which, I'll be honest, is going poorly. The square footage keeps lying to me."

Nothing. Seven backs, seven swaying shoulders, seven silent mouths keeping time with a bell that had been melted down before the First World War.

Lena took a step forward. Then another. The water dragged at her boots gently, like a dog tugging a sleeve — not to stop her, she thought, just to slow her down, make her feel it, make her understand where she was walking.

She was three meters away when the nearest figure broke formation.

It was the woman in the shawl. She turned — not the jerky pivot of a puppet but slowly, wearily, the way a person turns when they've been holding a position for a very long time and their body has forgotten how to do anything gracefully. Her face was bloated and pale, her eyes white as poached eggs, and Lena's torch beam shook across her and Lena did not run, mostly because her knees had unionized and voted against it.

The woman looked at her.

There is a particular quality to being looked at by the dead, Lena would think later, trying and failing to write it down. The living look *at* you. The dead look *through*, past skin and profession and the careful armor of rationality, straight to whatever it is you keep at the bottom of yourself, wrapped in oilcloth, weighted, sunk. The woman's white eyes found that thing in Lena in about two seconds flat, and Lena felt her breath go thin.

Then the woman's mouth stopped moving with the chorus. She alone fell out of rhythm. And her lips shaped — silently, deliberately, the way you mouth words through glass to someone on the other side — a single word.

*Listen.*

Not *run*. Not *join*. Not the hungry summons Lena had been bracing for since the first impossible measurement.

*Listen.*

"Oh," Lena said. Her voice cracked in the middle of it. "Oh, you're not — you're not hunting anyone. You're *testifying*."

The woman's ruined face did something that might, in better circumstances, have been a smile. Behind her, the other six sang on, faces to the wall, patient as furniture. Patient as people who had been waiting a hundred and fourteen years for someone to stop measuring the house and start hearing it.

The bell tolled again, and this time Lena heard the structure of it — heard that the melody was heading somewhere, building phrase upon phrase like testimony building toward a name, and then stopping. Cutting off. Every time, cutting off at the same place, the way a sentence cuts off when a hand goes over a mouth.

The gap at the end. The verse that was never written down.

"That's why the rooms don't add up," Lena whispered, and the water trembled around her ankles. "The house is bigger than it should be because it's *holding* something. You've been singing the evidence into these walls for a century and nobody's ever stayed to hear the end of it."

The woman in the shawl tilted her head. Almost a nod. The white eyes held Lena's, and in them Lena saw reflected torchlight and something older — a question, hanging there, unbearably polite after all this time:

*Will you?*

The bell tolled the closing phrase. The melody climbed, strained toward its ending —

— and stopped. Cut clean. The silence afterward was enormous, pressing, full of seven drowned mouths frozen mid-word.

And in that silence, from the top of the stairs, came the scrape of the cellar door being closed.

Not slammed. Closed. Gently. The way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping, or where something is being kept.

Lena spun, torch beam swinging up the stairwell, and saw the last sliver of light from the kitchen narrow and vanish. Above her, faintly, footsteps crossed the kitchen floor — unhurried, familiar, wearing the exact cadence of her own boots.

Then, through the stone, muffled and sweet and horribly patient, a voice began to hum.

It was humming the bell's song. From the beginning. And it was humming it *better* than the bell did — warmer, fuller, filling in the phrases with a confidence the bell had never managed, as if it already knew how the whole thing went.

As if it already knew the final verse.

Lena stood ankle-deep in the dead, listening to her own footsteps learn a song she didn't know, and understood that whatever came up those stairs next, it was going to know her name.
