## 7. The Chair Moves Closer

I want to say I slept well that night, but the truth is I slept like a man who has agreed to something in his sleep and will be held to it later. When I woke, the light through the bedroom window was the color of weak tea, and somewhere below me the house made a sound I chose to file under *old buildings settle* rather than *old buildings clear their throats*.

The counting had become a ritual by then. Eleven doors at night, twelve by morning. I'd stopped saying it out loud because saying things out loud gives them permission to be true. Instead I did what any reasonable man does when his house grows an extra door overnight: I made coffee and pretended the morning was ordinary.

The photographs were still spread across the dining room table where I'd left them, sorted into decades like a timeline of a family slowly being photographed to death. My grandmother's parlor, over and over. Birthdays, Christmases, Easters, one suspiciously formal Tuesday in 1974 where everyone wore their church clothes and nobody smiled. And in every image after 1962, there it was: the extra chair.

I had spent two evenings telling myself a story about it. The story went like this: the chair belonged to my great-uncle Aurelio, who died young, and the family kept his chair in the photo out of sentiment. Families do this. They keep empty chairs at tables for the dead; they set places for them on holidays. It's grief with furniture. It's normal.

It was a good story. I'd built it carefully, load-bearing wall by load-bearing wall, and I intended to live in it indefinitely.

Then I picked up the photograph from last Sunday — the one taken at my welcome-to-town potluck, which Principal Voss had insisted on hosting in the school gymnasium because apparently Marrow Bend doesn't believe in restaurants — and the story collapsed on me like wet cardboard.

The chair was closer.

Not *a* chair. Not *some* chair. The chair. The same straight-backed wooden thing with the slightly too-tall backrest, the one that had sat at the far edge of every parlor photograph since 1962, minding its own business at the periphery of forty years of family memory. In Sunday's photo, taken in Voss's gymnasium, a room my grandmother had never entered in her life, the chair stood six feet behind the assembled faculty, angled toward the camera like it had been asked to say cheese.

I checked the date on the back. Written in pencil, in handwriting I didn't recognize: *Sept. 14.*

Yesterday.

* * *

Here is what I know about denial: it is not the absence of understanding. It is the active, hourly maintenance of not-understanding, and it is exhausting work. By Wednesday I was putting in overtime.

I laid the photographs out chronologically across the living room floor, all thirty-one of them featuring the chair, and I measured. Not with a ruler — I'm not yet at the stage where I measure ghosts — but with my eyes, and then, when my eyes kept lying to me, with actual index cards placed between the chair and the nearest human figure in each photo.

1962: chair at the far wall, half-cropped by the frame.
1971: chair against the bookshelf, fully visible.
1983: chair beside the sofa.
1996: chair at the edge of the table.
2004: chair pulled up to the table, one setting's distance from Grandma Sandoval's elbow.
2019, the last photo of her alive: chair directly beside her, close enough that its shadow touched her sleeve.

And then this week. Monday's photo — Ruthie Okafor's class picture, which I'd been given as part of my homeroom materials, God help me — showed the chair standing in the hallway behind the students, visible through the classroom doorway, as if it had walked down from the parlor to check on the new teacher. Tuesday's photo, snapped by Denny Ruiz on a disposable camera he'd brought for a social studies project about "documenting our town" (oh, Denny), showed the chair at the edge of the school parking lot, sitting on the asphalt in full sun, casting a shadow that pointed the wrong way.

Wednesday morning, I found a photograph I had not taken, did not own, and could not explain. It was tucked into the frame of the mirror above the fireplace, face-out, like a greeting card. It showed my grandmother's parlor. Empty. No people at all. Just the room, the table, and the chair — now positioned exactly where a person would sit if they were posing for a portrait.

On the back, in that same unfamiliar pencil: *Soon.*

I put the photograph face-down on the mantel, which is the international symbol for *I am not dealing with you right now*, and I went to school, because attendance is sacred, or so I'm told.

* * *

At school, I tried the archive again. Voss had given me a key during my first week — "the new teacher should feel ownership," she'd said, in a voice like syrup poured over a lock — and the basement room smelled of paper dust and something sweeter underneath, something like honey left too long in a jar.

I wasn't looking for anything specific. That's a lie. I was looking for other photographs. If the chair appeared in the town's historical record, maybe there was a pattern, a date, a rule. Photographs are evidence. Photographs don't move.

Except these did, apparently, so my methodology was already compromised, but you work with what you have.

The archive boxes were organized by family name, which struck me as odd for a public school until I remembered that in Marrow Bend, the school *is* the families, has always been the families, probably legally. I found SANDOVAL between RUIZ and VOSS — alphabetical, neutral, innocent — and opened the box expecting class pictures and sports teams.

What I got was my grandmother, aged perhaps twenty, standing in front of the school with eleven other young people. Twelve of them, arranged in a line, holding hands. On the back: *Founders' Day, 1958.* And there, at the end of the line, where a thirteenth person should have stood if the line were even, was nothing. A gap. An absence shaped like a person, and every single one of them leaning slightly away from it, the way you lean from a smell.

I photographed the photo with my phone, because that's the century we live in, and when I looked at the screen, the gap looked less like an absence and more like a doorway. I put the phone in my pocket and decided not to look at it again for several hours, which is a coping strategy I highly recommend and never follow.

* * *

Denny Ruiz found me at lunch, which had become our routine. He'd sit at the corner of my desk, eat his sandwich in four enormous bites, and dispense local knowledge like a very small, very tired oracle.

"My mom says your grandma's house has good bones," he said.

"It does seem to have a lot of bones."

He didn't laugh. Denny didn't laugh much anymore, and each week he laughed less, and I was starting to chart it the way I charted the chair.

"You find more pictures?" he asked.

The question landed wrong. Not wrong like rude — wrong like a key turning in a lock I hadn't shown him. "How do you know about pictures?"

"Everybody knows about the pictures." He said it the way you'd say *everybody knows water's wet*. "My sister used to say the house takes them. Before she—" He stopped. He looked at his hands. "Before she went to the farm."

"Denny." I kept my voice level, the way you do around children and landmines. "What happens to the pictures?"

"They get closer." He shrugged, a small terrible shrug, a shrug that had been handed down through generations of Ruiz children. "That's how you know it's almost time. Grandma Ruiz says when the chair gets to the table, somebody's getting set a place."

"And whose place? Whose place gets set?"

Denny looked at me with the flat, patient eyes of a child explaining rain to a grown-up who insists it isn't falling. "Whoever's blood invited it," he said. "Your grandma never let it get to the table. Sixty years, she kept it at the wall." He hopped off the desk. "She must've been really stubborn."

"She was," I said, to his retreating back. "She was extremely stubborn."

The bell rang. Somewhere down the hall, a child laughed, and the sound bounced off the linoleum and came back sounding rehearsed.

* * *

That evening I did the thing I had been circling for three days the way you circle a dog you suspect of rabies.

I took the Tuesday photograph — Denny's parking lot shot, the chair on the asphalt, shadow pointing the wrong way — into the kitchen, along with the fireplace lighter and a metal bowl I'd bought specifically for this purpose, which tells you something about how long I'd known this moment was coming.

Let me be clear about my reasoning, such as it was. Reasoning: photographs are objects. Objects can be destroyed. Whatever is happening to the images — whatever is *approaching* through the images — cannot survive fire, because nothing survives fire, fire being nature's delete key. This reasoning has the structural integrity of a gingerbread house, and I built it anyway, and I lived in it for approximately ninety seconds.

I lit the corner of the photograph and held it over the bowl.

It burned the way photographs burn, curling and browning, the asphalt going first, then the school building, then the small figures of children frozen mid-stride. I watched the flame eat toward the chair, feeling something between vindication and nausea, and when the fire reached it — when the little wooden shape finally caught —

The smoke changed.

I don't know how else to describe it. Smoke smells like burning chemicals, like plastic and silver halide, like the acrid nothing of destroyed memories. This smoke smelled like bread. Fresh bread. The specific bread my grandmother baked on Sundays — the dense, sweet anise bread she called *pan de muerto* and refused to explain, the bread whose recipe I had searched her kitchen for after the funeral and never found.

The ash drifted down into the bowl, gray and fine, and it kept smelling like bread for a long time. Longer than smoke should. I stood in my grandmother's kitchen breathing in the ghost of her baking while the last embers of a photograph of a chair that shouldn't exist cooled in a mixing bowl, and I thought, with the clarity of the truly rattled: *it knows what comforts me.*

Because that's the thing, isn't it. Fear is loud. Dread is quiet. But this — the bread smell, the careful pencil notes, the chair's patient inching across sixty years of family photos — this was *attentive*. Something had studied my grief the way a tailor studies shoulders. Something knew exactly which memories fit me best.

I dumped the ash into the compost, washed the bowl twice, and told myself the shaking in my hands was the cold.

* * *

I didn't sleep so much as negotiate with sleep, and around two in the morning I gave up and went downstairs for water, and that's when I noticed the light in the parlor.

Not lamplight. Moonlight, early and wrong — the moon wouldn't be full for weeks, but the glow coming through the parlor window was round and heavy and orange at the edges, harvest-colored, the color of the porch lights in every photograph of this town I'd ever seen.

The parlor was empty. Of course it was. But the extra chair — the real one, the physical one, the one that had always sat in the corner near the bookshelf like a piece of furniture with a clean record — had moved.

It stood by the window now, facing the driveway. Facing the road. Facing town.

Facing the direction a guest would come from.

I stood in the doorway in my socks and looked at it for a long time, and I want to report that I felt terror, cinematic and total, but mostly what I felt was a horrible tenderness, because the chair looked *expectant*, the way a dog looks at a door before the car pulls in, and some animal part of me wanted to comfort it. Wanted to tell it: no one's coming. You've been waiting sixty years and no one is coming.

And then, from inside my own head, in a voice that was mine and also was not, came the correction:

*Yet.*

I turned on every light in the parlor, dragged the chair back to the corner myself — it resisted the way furniture resists, which is to say not at all, which is worse — and I sat up until dawn with my grandmother's photo albums open on my lap, looking for her face in every picture, looking for the moment she learned the rules, looking for the instruction manual she'd clearly read and I hadn't.

In the last album, the final page, tucked behind a photo of her garden, I found a note in her handwriting. Just one line, pressed hard enough to emboss the pages beneath it:

*If it reaches the table, don't feed it. Don't answer it. And Ines — if you're reading this, count the doors, mijo. Count them every morning. The day you stop finding twelve is the day it's done waiting.*

I looked up from the note. Through the parlor doorway, gray dawn light was creeping across the floorboards, and the chair sat in its corner, demure, patient, harmless as anything that has ever waited.

Outside, gravel crunched in the driveway.

A car door closed. Boots on the porch steps. And then — polite, unhurried, at an hour no sheriff has any business visiting — three knocks on my grandmother's front door.
