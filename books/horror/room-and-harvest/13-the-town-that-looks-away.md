## 13. The Town That Looks Away

By the third house, I stopped knocking like a teacher and started knocking like a census taker, which is to say: with the grim patience of a man who already knows the answers and is only collecting them for the record.

Denny had been gone four days. His desk had vanished before lunch on the first day, hauled out by nobody, witnessed by everybody, mourned by no one. When I'd asked Voss about it she'd given me that look — the one a librarian gives a man who asks where the fire exits are during a book burning — and told me I was "disrupting the season." Then she'd suspended me. Suspended. As if teaching were a season, like hunting or harvest or flu.

So there I was, a man with nothing but time and a town full of neighbors who suddenly couldn't look at him straight.

I'd made a list. It was a short list, because Marrow Bend is a short town — three streets deep, wedged between the river and the orchards like something the valley swallowed and never quite digested. Eleven households within walking distance. I'd written them all down in my grandmother's old ledger paper, which felt appropriate, though at the time I hadn't yet learned why that particular paper choice would come to feel like a joke someone else was telling.

The first house belonged to the Pell family. Old Man Pell answered the door in his undershirt, holding a coffee mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST FISHERMAN, which struck me as the most honest piece of self-assessment I'd seen in this town.

"Mr. Sandoval," he said. "The new teacher."

"The new teacher," I agreed. "I'm sorry to bother you, Mr. Pell. I'm looking for Denny Ruiz. He's a student of mine. He hasn't been at school."

Pell's face did something interesting. It didn't close, exactly. It *arranged* itself — the way a table gets set. The smile came up from somewhere below, professional, warm, practiced in front of a mirror by somebody's grandmother a hundred years ago and handed down like a recipe.

"Denny," he said, tasting the name. "Ruiz boy."

"Yes sir. His sister went missing two years ago. Now him. I thought maybe—"

"It keeps us fed," Pell said.

I waited. He waited. The mug steamed between us like a small truce neither of us intended to honor.

"I'm sorry?" I said.

"It keeps us fed." He said it again, same cadence, same warmth, and I realized with a small cold click that he wasn't answering my question. He was answering a different question, an older one, one I hadn't asked yet but apparently everyone in Marrow Bend had been rehearsing since before I was born. "You have a good day now, Mr. Sandoval. Mind the frost tonight. They say it'll be a hard one."

He closed the door with the gentleness of a man shutting a hymnal.

* * *

House two was the Okonkwos. Mrs. Okonkwo was watering mums on her porch when I arrived, and she gave me the smile before I'd even finished climbing her steps — which meant it wasn't a response to anything. It was just *on*, the way porch lights are on at dusk, automatic, municipal.

"Mr. Sandoval! How lovely. Are you settling in? Your grandmother's house has such good bones."

"It does. Thank you. Listen, I hate to intrude, but one of my students, Denny Ruiz—"

"It keeps us fed," she said, tilting the watering can so precisely that not a single drop missed the pots.

"You too?"

"Me too what, dear?"

"That phrase. Everyone says it. Like a password. Like a *prayer*."

She laughed then, and the laugh was real, which was somehow worse. A real laugh, warm and crinkled at the edges, coming out of a woman who had just recited a script over a missing child.

"Oh, Mr. Sandoval. You're new. New people always ask. My mother asked, when we moved here in '78. Her mother asked before that." She set down the can and patted my arm, and her hand was floury and soft and kind. "It keeps us fed. That's not a riddle, honey. It's just true. Look around. No hunger here. No want. Best soil in the county. Best yields. You've eaten at Marta Hollis's table — you know how good we eat."

"I've eaten pie at Marta Hollis's table," I said carefully, "and I've also noticed that children keep disappearing from my classroom, and those two facts sit together in my stomach like stones."

Something flickered behind her eyes then. Just for a second. Something tired, and old, and — I want to say this correctly because it matters — *sorry*. Not sorry for Denny. Sorry for *me*, the way you're sorry for a dog that barks at thunder.

"You should talk to Sheriff Aldous," she said, picking the can back up. "Or don't. Honestly, dear, sometimes the kindest thing new folks can do is just... settle in. Put down roots. Have some supper."

I walked down her steps and heard her resume humming before I reached the sidewalk.

* * *

I won't give you all eleven houses. Some mercies are structural. But I'll give you the shape of them, because the shape is the thing.

At the Delgados', a man in a wheelchair watched me through the screen door and said "It keeps us fed" before I'd gotten two sentences into my introduction, as if my face itself were the question. At the Fenwicks', both husband and wife said it simultaneously, in unison, like a couple finishing each other's sentence, except they weren't finishing each other's sentence; they were reciting the same one, from the same invisible page, and when I pointed this out they looked at each other with mild surprise, as if they'd only just noticed they were married.

At the house belonging to the Coyle twins — ninety if they were a day, twin sisters who dressed alike and finished each other's silences — I got the phrase plus a bonus verse. "It keeps us fed," said the one on the left. "And it doesn't ask much," said the one on the right. "Just what anyone asks," said the left again. "A place at the table," said the right. And then they both smiled at me with identical serenity and offered me lemonade I did not drink.

By house nine I had stopped taking notes. Not because I'd given up, but because every page looked the same. Same smile. Same phrase. Same gentle deflection toward supper, toward roots, toward the weather, toward anything that wasn't the hole in my classroom shaped like a twelve-year-old boy who used to draw monsters in the margins of his math homework and once told me, with the flat pragmatism of a child raised in a slaughterhouse, *never answer a knock after the porch light dies.*

House ten was empty. Foreclosed, according to the notice on the door, though the lawn was mowed and the flowerbeds were weeded, which told me somebody was still keeping it fed.

Which left house eleven. The last one on Birch Street, set back behind a stand of cottonwoods, with a porch swing that creaked in no wind. I knew who lived there. Everybody knew who lived there. Reverend Pike's sister, Ada Pike, widowed twice, ancient as the covenant itself, though I didn't have the word *covenant* yet — I had only the ledger, and the tin box I hadn't found, and a growing suspicion that my grandmother's name appearing twice in Voss's book meant something I wasn't ready to read.

Ada opened the door before I knocked.

That's not an exaggeration for effect. I raised my hand and the door swung inward, and there she stood, small and dry as a corn husk, wearing a cardigan the color of weak tea.

"Ines Sandoval," she said. Not a question. A reading.

"Ma'am. I'm sorry to trouble you. I'm looking for—"

"A boy. Yes. They do keep taking the boys, don't they. Sit down, child, my knees are older than your grief."

Her parlor smelled of cedar and camphor and, underneath it, faintly, of bread. I sat. She sat across from me in a chair that matched, exactly, a chair I'd been seeing in photographs all week — and I want to be clear that I'm not saying it *was* that chair. I'm saying my hands went cold anyway.

"You've been to the other houses," she said.

"Ten of them."

"And what did they tell you?"

"They told me it keeps us fed."

"Mm." She folded her hands. "And what did their faces do while they said it?"

I thought about it honestly, because she seemed like a woman who could tell the difference between an honest answer and a polite one. "They smiled. All of them. The same smile. And underneath it—" I searched for the word. "Underneath it they looked like people standing very still in a room where something is sleeping."

Ada Pike nodded slowly, and for one absurd moment I wanted to cry, because she was the first person in this town who had looked at me like I was saying words that meant things.

"My brother tried," she said. "Forty years a reverend in this town. Tried to preach against it, gently, in his way. Never named it from the pulpit — you don't name it, naming is an invitation — but he preached around it. Preached circles so wide the whole county fit inside them. And every Sunday the congregation sang louder, and every autumn the pews filled up, and every year another child went 'to the farm,' and every year he grew smaller." She looked at her hands. "Stillness isn't agreement, Mr. Sandoval. Sometimes stillness is just the shape shame takes when it's been sitting long enough."

"Then help me," I said. "Tell me what it is. What the farm is. What the chair means — my grandmother's photographs, the extra chair, the—"

"Child." She said it softly, the way you'd say it to stop a horse. "If I tell you, you will know. And knowing is a door, and doors open from both sides, and once you know, *it* knows that you know, and then you are not a neighbor asking questions anymore. You are a guest being prepared."

The clock on her mantel ticked. Somewhere outside, a crow said one thing and decided against saying more.

"But you'll tell me something," I said. "You opened the door before I knocked. You invited me in. Why, if knowing is dangerous?"

Ada Pike smiled then, and hers was not the town's smile. Hers was crooked, and tired, and entirely her own, and it was the most beautiful thing I'd seen in Marrow Bend.

"Because I'm ninety-one, and I've kept still for seventy years, and my brother is dying upstairs right now with something caught in his throat that he's been trying to cough out since 1962." She leaned forward. "Come back Thursday. Come after dark, when the porch lights die and before the moon rises. Knock twice. He wants to tell you what he couldn't preach. And Ines—" she used my first name without having been given it, and I let that pass, because everything in this town passed through doors I hadn't found yet, "—when you go home tonight, count your doors. Count them twice. And whatever number you get, believe the second count."

I walked home under a sky going the color of a bruise healing, past eleven houses and eleven smiles, carrying the loneliest thing a person can carry, which is not grief and not fear but *clarity* — the terrible weightless clarity of being the only sober man in a town where everyone else insists the drinking water is fine.

Behind me, porch light by porch light, Marrow Bend began to switch itself off, politely, in order, like a congregation bowing its head.

And ahead, in my grandmother's darkened windows, I could see the parlor lamp burning — though I had left it off that morning — and beneath the smell of woodsmoke and cold earth, faint and patient and rising on the evening air like something baked long ago and never allowed to go stale:

Bread.

Someone, or something, had left bread rising in my grandmother's kitchen.

And tomorrow was Thursday.
