## 14. A Voice Through the Wall

The suspension letter came on school letterhead, which struck me as funny in a way I couldn't explain to anyone. *Disrupting the season.* Like I'd been caught selling fireworks to minors. Like Marrow Bend had seasons the way other towns did — not spring, summer, fall, and winter, but something older with a capital S, something you could disrupt the way you'd disrupt a church service.

I taped the letter to the refrigerator because it seemed like the kind of document that should be near food. Then I sat at my grandmother's kitchen table — eleven chairs' worth of table, twelve if you counted the one only photographs could see — and drank coffee that had gone bitter while I wasn't paying attention.

Three days of nothing ahead of me. No classroom, no attendance sheet, no Denny Ruiz raising his hand to ask questions he already knew the answers to. The house and I were going to have to get reacquainted.

I should have left. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody keeps them anymore. A sane man would have packed the Corolla, driven until the corn stopped, and mailed his resignation from a town where the diner had more than one pie option. But there was a boy missing, and a ledger with my grandmother's name in it twice, and a deed in a tin box that said my blood held the deciding vote in something I still didn't fully understand. You don't walk out on a vote. That's what I kept telling myself, anyway. It sounded better than *I couldn't stop looking.*

So I stayed, and I fixed things. That's what you do in an inherited house — you fix things, because fixing is cheaper than thinking.

* * *

The plaster crack had been growing above the hallway door since Tuesday. I'm not exaggerating; I'd taken a photo of it Monday night for no reason I could name, and by Wednesday it ran from the ceiling down past the doorframe like a lightning strike frozen mid-thought. Houses settle. Old houses settle dramatically. I know this. I repeated it to myself like a mantra while I mixed joint compound in a bucket that had once held honey — Sheriff Aldous's honey, actually, unopened, because I hadn't been able to bring myself to eat anything anyone in this town had given me.

By four o'clock I had the crack scraped open and was working the compound into the gap with a putty knife, and the house was quiet in the way old houses are quiet, which is to say not quiet at all. Pipes ticked. The furnace muttered. Somewhere upstairs a board creaked its one note, over and over, like a musician practicing a single syllable.

That's when I heard the scratching.

It came from inside the wall — not behind it, not through it, but *in* it, the way a mouse sounds when it's between the lath and the plaster, except mice scratch fast and frantic and this was slow. Deliberate. Patient. Scratch. Pause. Scratch. Pause. Like someone tapping a pencil while they decided how to say something difficult.

"Mice," I said out loud, to the empty hallway, because saying things out loud to empty hallways had apparently become part of my routine.

The scratching stopped.

And then, clear as a phone call, a voice said: "Mr. Sandoval?"

Denny Ruiz's voice.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. It wasn't *like* Denny's voice. It was Denny's voice — the slight rasp in it, the way he swallowed the ends of words, the particular pitch that sat just below where you'd expect a seventh grader's voice to sit. I'd heard it every morning for two months taking attendance. I'd heard it say *present* forty-some times. I knew that voice the way you know a song you've heard on the radio too many times, and this was it, coming from eighteen inches away, from inside a wall full of horsehair plaster and sixty years of dust.

"Denny?" My own voice came out wrong. Thin. "Denny, is that you?"

Scratch. Pause. Scratch.

"Mr. Sandoval, it's cold in here."

I stood very still with the putty knife in my hand and made myself think. This is the trick of horror that nobody warns you about: it doesn't feel like horror while it's happening. It feels like a problem. A logistical problem. There is a voice in the wall and it belongs to a missing child and your brain, your stupid helpful brain, starts generating solutions instead of screams. *Cut into the wall. Call someone. Get him out.* The fear arrives later, retroactively, like a bill.

"You're in the wall," I said. Stupid. Obvious. But my mouth needed to do something while my mind raced.

"It's cold," the voice said again, and now there was something underneath it, something that made the hair on my arms stand up before I even identified it. The coldness wasn't in the description. It was in the delivery. Denny had been a warm kid. Denny complained about the heat in September and wore shorts into October. This voice described cold the way a man who has never been warm might describe it — as a concept, a rumor, something learned secondhand.

"Where are you, Denny? Really."

A long pause. Long enough that I almost convinced myself I'd imagined it, that grief and insomnia and three days of institutional gaslighting had finally cooked something loose in my head.

Then: "Can you let me into the warm rooms?"

* * *

Here's what I did, and I'm not proud of the order of operations. I didn't run. I didn't call anyone — who would I call? Sheriff Aldous, who delivered honey like a threat wrapped in gingham? Principal Voss, who'd suspended me for asking where children went? I did the thing that felt most natural and most insane in equal measure: I talked back.

"The warm rooms are locked," I said. "All of them. Since last night."

"I know," the voice said, and there was something almost apologetic in it. "I tried them."

Every door in the house had locked itself two nights ago, all twelve of them — or eleven, depending on when you counted — clicking shut one after another at midnight like a clock striking hours. I'd spent that night on the kitchen floor with a salt canister and a baseball bat, listening to footsteps pace overhead in a room I couldn't find. I hadn't mentioned the footsteps to anyone. There wasn't anyone to mention them to.

"How did you try them, Denny?"

Silence. Then the scratching resumed, but different now — rhythmic, deliberate, moving. Down the wall. Toward the front door.

I followed it. Of course I followed it. Write that down too: *he followed the voice in the wall toward the front door, alone, at dusk, holding a putty knife.* If there's a museum of poor decisions, I want a wing.

The voice traveled inside the wall the way a rat travels, or a current, and I tracked it by ear through the living room, past the photographs I still hadn't taken down — the parlor photos, the ones with the extra chair, the chair that had spent this week creeping closer to the camera like a guest working up courage — and into the front hall, where it stopped.

Directly behind the front door.

"Mr. Sandoval." Denny's voice again, and this time it cracked, actually cracked, the way a twelve-year-old's voice does, and I felt my chest seize up because that was real, that had to be real, no thing in a wall could fake the specific humiliation of a voice changing. "It's really dark in here. And there's a table. I don't like the table."

My hand was on the deadbolt. I don't remember putting it there. That's the detail that haunts me — not the voice, not the wall, but my own hand, resting on the lock like it belonged there, thumb already finding the turn-piece the way it does ten thousand mornings a year without consulting me.

"What table, Denny?"

"The long one." A pause. "There's soup."

"There's—"

"There's always soup," the voice said, and for half a second it wasn't Denny's voice at all. It was deeper, and slower, and it came from somewhere further down than a wall, like sound traveling up through the foundations, through bedrock, through whatever the town was built on top of besides corn and lies. "There's always soup, and the bowls are set, and everyone is waiting so politely."

My thumb turned the deadbolt one click. One. The bolt slid back with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

And then — I don't know what saved me. Maybe nothing saved me. Maybe some animal ledger in my brain finally balanced: *a child trapped in a wall would not ask to come in through the front door. A child trapped in a wall would not be calm about it. A child trapped in a wall would be screaming.* Denny Ruiz, wherever he was, was either screaming or silent, and this thing was neither. This thing was *patient*, and patience is the tell. Everything in Marrow Bend was patient. The town waited out its children like a farmer waits out a frost.

I pulled my hand back like the lock had burned it.

"No," I said.

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. The whole house seemed to lean in.

"No?" the voice said, and it was Denny's voice again, sweet and small and wounded, and God help me, it nearly worked a second time. "But Mr. Sandoval, I'm hungry."

"You're not Denny."

"I'm everybody," the voice said pleasantly. "That's rather the point."

* * *

I backed into the kitchen and put the table between myself and the hallway, which accomplished nothing and made me feel better, which I've learned is the primary function of most human behavior. The light outside had gone the bruised purple of early evening. The porch light — Denny had told me about the porch light, weeks ago, in his flat fatal child's way: *never answer a knock after the porch light dies* — flickered once and went out. Not dimmed. Died. Like something had pinched the filament.

At seven o'clock exactly, something knocked on the front door.

Three knocks. Polite. Spaced evenly, the way you'd knock at a neighbor's house to borrow an egg. And then Denny's voice, muffled now, coming from the other side of the door instead of the wall, calling: "Mr. Sandoval? It's me. Can I come in? It's cold out here."

I sat at the kitchen table with both hands flat on the wood and did not move. The knocking came again. Then again. Each round identical — same force, same spacing, same little pause afterward, like a recording played by something that had studied knocking but never needed to do it before. Between rounds I could hear it breathing, if that's what it was — a slow wet rhythm, in through the keyhole, out through the mail slot, the house itself inhaling.

"Mr. Sandoval." Softer now. Kinder. "You look tired. You should eat something."

"I'm fine," I said, and hated how normal I made my voice, how much effort it took to sound bored.

"You're not eating. We notice. We always notice." A pause, and then, in a tone of genuine helpfulness: "Your grandmother used to forget too. She got better at it. She practiced."

Something went through me colder than fear. "What did you say?"

"She practiced," the voice repeated, warm as pie. "Not yet, she'd say. Not mine. She was good. She held out longer than anyone." The doorknob rattled gently, experimentally, a hand testing a jar lid. "But everyone gets hungry eventually, Mr. Sandoval. Even us. Especially us."

*I practiced,* it was telling me. *Your grandmother starved it out, night after night, year after year, saying no at this exact door.* And it was also telling me it remembered her fondly. Both of those things together made a shape I didn't want to look at directly.

The knocking stopped around nine. Just — stopped, mid-round, after the second knock of a set of three, which was somehow worse than finishing. The breathing continued a while longer, unhurried, and then that stopped too, and the house settled into its ordinary noises, pipes and furnace and the creaking board upstairs keeping its one stubborn note.

I didn't sleep. Around two in the morning I heard the footsteps again, pacing the twelfth room, back and forth, back and forth, and I noticed something new: they weren't pacing anymore. They were walking heel-to-toe, slow, measured, like someone learning the dimensions of a space. Measuring it for furniture.

Toward dawn, exhausted past fear into some gray country beyond it, I dragged myself to the sink to splash water on my face, and that's when I saw the flour.

My hands were white to the wrists. Fine, soft flour, worked into the creases of my knuckles, under my nails — the flour of someone who'd been kneading dough for hours. I stood at the sink staring at my own hands like they belonged to a stranger, and somewhere in the walls, faint and far down, something laughed. Not cruelly. Almost kindly. The laugh of a grandmother watching a grandchild fail at a recipe, ready to step in and show them how it's done.

I washed my hands until they stung. They wouldn't come completely clean. And under the water, over the noise of it, I heard the voice one last time — not Denny's, not the deep one, but a woman's, old and dry and familiar in a way that broke something loose in my chest:

*"Not yet,"* it whispered from inside the wall. *"Not mine. But soon, mijo. Soon it has to be somebody's."*

I dried my hands, picked up the phone, and dialed the number Ruthie Hollis had scratched onto the back of my hand two nights ago in the orchard dark. It rang four times. Five. Six.

She answered on the seventh ring, breathless, whispering: "Ines. Ines, something's wrong. My mother's voice was just outside my window, and my mother died two years ago."
