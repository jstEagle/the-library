## 19. Ally in the Orchard

Two years is a long time to live in an orchard.

Not literally. I want to be clear about that, because if you ever end up in Marrow Bend—and God help you if you do—someone will tell you I'm exaggerating, that I lived with my aunt in Hollis Hollow for a while after my "difficult period," and that girls say all kinds of things when they're troubled. That's the version they keep in their mouths like a peppermint. The truth is less minty.

The truth is that my mother's voice called me out of bed on harvest night two years ago, and I went to the window, and I saw her standing at the edge of the yard in her nightgown, smiling up at me with her head tilted too far back, like she was trying to look at something on the roof of her own skull. And the truth is that some animal part of me—the part that knows the difference between your mother and a thing wearing your mother the way a coat wears shoulders—slammed the window and ran. Out the back, through the fence gap, into the orchard. Barefoot. October. Sixteen years old.

I've been barefoot in more ways than one ever since.

* * *

The orchard belongs to the Hollis farm, which means it belongs to Marta Hollis, which means it's the last place anyone would think to hide a runaway, because everyone in town believes the missing children go *to* the farm, happy as pigs, doing chores and getting three meals a day. That's what Sheriff Aldous told my aunt when she filed the report. That's what Principal Voss wrote in my file, probably, in that careful cursive of hers, the handwriting of a woman who has never once smudged a page: *Transferred to agricultural placement, family arrangement.*

Family arrangement. Sure. The Hollis family arranges things. That's practically their motto. It should be carved over the gate.

Here's what nobody counts on: Marta doesn't walk her orchard after dark. Marta doesn't walk it much at all anymore, because the trees make her nervous—not the trees themselves, but the rows between them, where the ground stays soft no matter how dry the summer gets, soft the way a bed is soft, soft the way something waiting is patient. She sent hired men through every fall to pick, and the men came out by four o'clock even in years when the light lasted till six, and they never said why, and she never asked, because asking questions in Marrow Bend is like knocking on a door after the porch light dies. You know better. Everybody knows better. Knowing better is the whole civic religion here.

So I lived in the orchard. First in the old equipment shed until I learned the shed had a door that wasn't a door—I'll get to that, maybe, someday, or maybe never—and then in the hollow of the biggest apple tree, the one at the center of the oldest rows, the one that fruits out of season and always has. Granny Smiths in June. Nobody in town finds that strange because nobody in town finds anything strange; strangeness was zoned out of Marrow Bend sometime around 1911, along with the saloon.

I ate apples and stolen eggs and whatever I could lift from the compost heap behind the school cafeteria, which, let me tell you, is the true curriculum of this town. I watched. Watching is a skill like any other. Two years of watching taught me the schedules of every adult in Marrow Bend, the exact hour Voss locks the archive, the fact that Aldous takes his coffee standing up because sitting down makes him drowsy and drowsy makes him honest, and the fact that once a year, give or take, a new teacher arrives in town with a rental car full of boxes and a face like a man who thinks he's starting over.

This year, the new teacher had my grandmother's chin.

Well. Not my grandmother. But a Sandoval chin, which around here amounts to the same thing, the way a key and a lock amount to the same thing.

* * *

I'd been watching Ines Sandoval for eleven days before I decided to talk to him.

Eleven days is not a long time to decide something, unless you're deciding whether to trust a stranger with the only thing you have left, which is the truth, which in Marrow Bend is also the most dangerous thing you can carry, heavier than a gun, more flammable than kerosene. People have been disappeared for less. People have been *harvested* for less, though the town prefers its other words: transferred, placed, gone to the farm, taken by weather. My personal favorite, from the year I vanished from the rolls: *resolved.*

Ines Sandoval did not act like the others. The others—young teachers, old teachers, it didn't matter—arrived with the smoothness of people who had been told a story about this town and found the story mostly accurate. They noticed nothing for six weeks, then noticed everything for one bad week, then stopped noticing, permanently, the way a radio stops picking up a station. I used to mark them in my little wax-pencil ledger, kept in a tin under the apple tree's roots: *Arrived Sept 3. Noticed Oct 12. Stopped noticing Oct 19.* Eleven entries in two years. Eleven small gravestones made of pencil marks.

Sandoval arrived and started noticing on day two.

That's not an exaggeration. Day two he asked Voss about attendance policy in a tone that wasn't hostile, just precise, and I watched through the cafeteria window—heated glass, October, me outside with my breath fogging it like a ghost with poor boundaries—as Voss gave him the covenant speech wrapped in administrative language. *Attendance is sacred here, Mr. Sandoval. We take it seriously. Families depend on it.* And Sandoval nodded the way polite people nod, but afterward, alone in his classroom, he stood at the window looking at the playground for a long time, and his face did a thing I recognized because I'd seen it in my own reflection, back when I still had access to mirrors: the arithmetic face. The face of a person counting something that isn't supposed to be counted.

By day nine he'd asked about Denny Ruiz's sister. By day ten he'd been seen walking the perimeter of the school grounds at dusk, touching the fence posts like he was checking them for fever. By day eleven, half the town was smiling at him—that smile, the fed smile, the one that says *we know something you don't and we're so glad you don't know it*—and Sheriff Aldous had left honey on his porch.

The honey was what decided me. Because I knew what the honey meant. I'd watched Aldous deliver jars before, always to newcomers, always in autumn, always with both hands, like an offering. And I'd watched what happened to the households that accepted it. Sleepwalking comes later, closer to the moon, but the acceptance starts right away. You eat the sweetness, you belong to the sweetness. It's very simple. It's the oldest trick there is, older than apples, and we live in an orchard, so.

If I didn't talk to him soon, he'd be another entry in the wax-pencil ledger. *Arrived. Noticed. Stopped.* And worse—if he was what I thought he was, if the chin meant what the chin meant—then he was the only person in Marrow Bend who could do anything about any of it, and he was sleepwalking toward the table with a jar of honey in each hand.

So I wrote him a note.

* * *

I want to defend the note, briefly, against the charge of melodrama. Yes, I cut the letters out of a newspaper, like a ransom demand, like a crazy person. But consider my options. Handwriting can be identified. I hadn't signed my name to anything in two years and I didn't intend to start. Also, cut-out letters have a certain authority. They say: *I am serious, and I am possibly dangerous, and either way you should come to the orchard.* Which was accurate on both counts.

The note said: **THE ORCHARD. CENTER ROW. AFTER DARK. COME ALONE. BRING NOTHING THAT RINGS.**

"That rings" meaning phones, keys, coins, belt buckles with attitude. Sound carries in an orchard at night, and sound carries further than sound in Marrow Bend; it carries to wherever the listening happens, and the listening happens everywhere except where you'd expect. I learned that the hard way, the first month, when I hummed to myself in the shed and the next morning every apple on the south rows had turned toward the shed overnight. Apples don't turn. These turned.

He came. Of course he came. A man who counts doors and asks about absent sisters will absolutely walk into a dark orchard because a ransom note told him to. That's not bravery exactly. It's the same sickness that got me: the inability to leave a question unanswered, even when the answer has teeth.

I heard him before I saw him—no rings, good, he'd listened—but I heard his breathing change when the rows closed in around him, that quick shallow pull people do when a place feels like being swallowed politely. Then his flashlight beam swung down the center row and caught me sitting on the low branch of the out-of-season tree, and he made a sound I'll remember as long as I remember anything, which may be a long time or may be until spring.

"You're—" he said.

"Ruthie," I said. "Ruthie Hollis. Though officially I'm resolved."

* * *

He didn't run. Credit where due. Most adults, confronted with a teenage girl materializing out of a tree in a haunted orchard, would have performed some theater of alarm and then left to report me to whichever authority felt safest. He stood there with his flashlight shaking slightly and said, "You're supposed to be at the farm," and then immediately winced, because he'd already learned enough to hear how that sentence sounded.

"I am at the farm," I said. "Technically. This is the farm. Marta owns every tree you're standing under. The difference between me and the other kids at the farm is that the other kids are indoors." I let that sit. He was smart; I could see him assembling it, the way you assemble furniture with the wrong instructions, forcing pieces together until suddenly the shape makes horrible sense. "You've been to Marta's kitchen," I said. "Tell me what she served you."

"Pie," he said slowly. "Apple."

"Out of season?"

"...Yes."

"And did she call it anything? Did she use a word you wouldn't expect with pie?"

His jaw worked. "Harvest," he said. "She said her daughter had—harvested well. Like it was a grade."

"There it is." I dropped down from the branch. He stepped back—one instinctive step, and I noted it without bitterness; I've gotten used to being a thing people step back from, barefoot girl, wild hair, eyes like someone who hasn't slept indoors in two years, which is exactly what I am. "My name's Ruthie. I told you. Ruthie Hollis. Marta's my aunt, which makes the harvested daughter my cousin, which makes me the one who got away, which makes me, as far as I can tell, the only person in this town who remembers the whole story from the inside and is still above ground and talking."

"The harvest," he said. "Denny Ruiz mentioned a farm. The missing kids—"

"Are not missing. That's the first thing you have to understand, and it's the worst thing, so we might as well start there. They're *kept*. They're at a table under a house, or under something that uses houses the way a hand uses a glove, and they're alive, Mr. Sandoval, they were alive when I saw them two years ago and I would bet every apple in this orchard they're alive tonight. Alive and seated and vacant. Being kept. Like preserves." I heard my own voice crack on that word and hated it, and pushed through anyway. "Like jam. Like something put up for winter."

The flashlight had drifted down to point at the dirt between us, forgotten. Good. Light attracts things here too, sometimes, depending on the phase of the moon and the mood of the ground.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked. And underneath the question, quieter, the real one: "Why me?"

Because of the chin. Because of the ledger. Because of the name written twice in a book I'd only glimpsed through the archive window on a day Voss left it open on her desk like an idiot, or like bait, and I'd read two lines before I had to drop flat behind the radiator: *good yield,* and beside one of the entries, a name that made my teeth ache. But I didn't lead with any of that. I led with the truth, because I'd decided somewhere in those eleven days that this man deserved the truth straight, no chaser, and because I was so tired of carrying it alone that my spine had developed a permanent ache, like I'd been sleeping on a bag of stones, which, in fairness, some nights I had.

"Because two years ago I sat at that table," I said. "Before I ran. Before my mother's voice called me and something in my blood said *no* loud enough to save me. I saw the guest, Mr. Sandoval. I saw what sits in the thirteenth chair when the moon is right. And I saw the empty places set for the ones who hadn't arrived yet, and I saw the name cards." His breath stopped. I let it stay stopped for a second. "Handwritten. Old-fashioned script. One of them said SANDOVAL. Not yours—a Sandoval, an old one, the ink brown with age. Your family didn't just live in this town. Your family *signed* for it. There's a deed, and there's a ledger, and there's a vote, and the vote runs in your blood like the chin does."

The orchard was very quiet. Even the wind was holding its breath, which I mean literally; the wind in Marrow Bend holds its breath when it wants to listen, and I've stopped finding that charming.

"My grandmother's house," he said finally. "There are twelve doors. There were eleven when I moved in."

"There are thirteen rooms if you count the one that doesn't want counting," I said. "And there's a chair in your photographs that moves. Don't ask how I know. Ask instead why a town that trades children for good soil would let a Sandoval come back and teach its children, weeks before the harvest moon." I stepped closer, close enough to see him properly in the dark, close enough that he could see me, and I made my voice as steady as I could make anything. "Either they think you'll do what your grandmother wouldn't—or couldn't—or they think you won't figure it out in time. I've watched them be wrong about a lot of things, but I've never watched them be wrong about this. So we have days, maybe. Not weeks. Days."

"Do?" he said. "Do what?"

"Unseat it," I said. "Or seat it. Those are the only two moves on the board, and one of them costs a child, and the other one—" I stopped. Because I didn't actually know what the other one cost. I knew there was a clause; I'd heard Reverend Pike preach a garbled sermon about substitution once, years ago, before the palsy took his words, and the congregation had sung extra loud to drown him out, which told me everything. "The other one, I think, costs a Sandoval. And I think you already know that, deep down, because you counted the doors and you didn't leave."

He looked at me for a long time. Above us the out-of-season tree held its impossible green apples up against the sky like small pale moons, and somewhere far off, at the edge of the orchard, a porch light died—not flickered, *died*, the way they do here, all at once, like a held note cut off—and we both heard it, and we both understood it, and for the first time in two years I was standing next to someone who understood it, and I nearly cried, and didn't, because crying makes noise.

"Okay," Ines Sandoval said. "Then teach me the rules. All of them. Starting with why the apples turn."

"They turn toward whatever listened last," I said. "Come on. We'll walk the rows and I'll show you which ones are facing your house."

He followed me into the dark, and behind us, softly, the whole orchard began—very quietly, very patiently—to turn.
