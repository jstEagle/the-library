## 23. Three Days to the Moon

There is a particular kind of arithmetic you learn when you know exactly how long you have left. It isn't like counting down to a vacation. It's like counting the boards in a floor you're about to be buried under. Three days to the harvest moon. Seventy-two hours, give or take whatever the moon decides to do about it. I spent the first of those hours doing something I'm not proud of: I sat at my grandmother's kitchen table with a legal pad and a pencil and I tried to make the horror into a list.

Lists are how I cope. I'm a seventh-grade teacher. We are a profession of lists.

*Salt. Mirrors. Doors. Ruthie. The deed. The chair.*

I looked at the list for a long time. Six items. It seemed like too few for the end of the world and too many for what remained of my nerve.

Ruthie arrived just after dark, coming up the drive the way she always did now—fast, low, hugging the fence line like the fence itself might vouch for her. She'd taken to wearing her hair tied back with a strip of blue fabric, and I'd noticed she touched it before she did anything difficult, the way other people cross themselves. She came in without knocking, which I'd told her she was welcome to do, and which she'd interpreted with the thoroughness of a teenager, meaning she never knocked even when the situation clearly called for it.

"Salt's here," she said, dropping four cardboard cartons on the table. "I bought out the hardware store. Marv asked me if I was canning."

"What'd you say?"

"I said sure. He said, 'Good, we all got to do our part.'" She sat down across from me and stared at the legal pad. "You made a *list*?"

"Dread arithmetic," I said. "It's a coping mechanism."

"It's a coping *disaster*. Salt doesn't work. You told me it doesn't work."

"I told you it failed once."

"It failed *spectacularly*. You told me. The line just—" she made a gesture with both hands, a sort of erasing motion, "—stopped being there."

She was right, and I hated that she was right, and I did it anyway, because that is more or less the defining characteristic of the Sandoval bloodline as far as I can tell. We poured salt that night. All four cartons. We laid a thick white line across every threshold in the house—front door, back door, the cellar hatch, even the little door under the stairs that had never once in my childhood opened onto anything but coats. Ruthie did the upstairs. I did the down. We worked in silence except for the hiss of salt hitting old floorboards, and when we were done the house looked like it had been frosted by a very meticulous, very frightened baker.

Then we sat in the parlor and waited to see what would happen.

What happened was this: at 2:14 in the morning—I know because I was watching the clock the way you watch a pot you're afraid to boil—there was a sound from the front hall. Not a knock. Not a footstep. A sound like sand being poured out, slowly, patiently, from a height.

I got up. Ruthie grabbed my sleeve and I shook her off, gently, and went to the doorway.

The salt line was gone. Not scattered. Not disturbed. *Gone*, the way a word goes when you look away from it mid-sentence. The floorboards were bare and clean, and in the middle of the hall, arranged with terrible neatness, was a small pile of salt in the shape of a chair.

I didn't sleep after that. Ruthie slept on the couch with a baseball bat across her knees, and I sat in the armchair where my grandmother used to sit, and I watched the little salt chair until the dawn came and took it, and by morning it was just salt again, spilled, meaningless, and I swept it up and threw it out the back door and hated myself for the small, stupid hope I'd felt sweeping it.

* * *

The mirrors were worse.

I want to be careful here, because there's a version of this story where I tell you the mirrors were the worst thing, and another version where I admit the mirrors were only the second-worst thing, and the worst thing I haven't told anyone yet, including Ruthie, including possibly myself. But the mirrors were bad enough.

It started the second morning. I was shaving, which is a thing I do, a human thing, a routine thing, and I noticed that the mirror over the sink was fogging. Not from the shower—I hadn't run the water yet. Not from my breath, which was steady. It fogged the way a window fogs when someone on the other side of it breathes.

I stood there with my razor in my hand and watched a handprint bloom in the fog. Small. A child's hand, maybe, or a child-sized something. It pressed and slid, the way a hand slides when the person behind the glass is trying to find the latch.

"There's no latch," I told it. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which I've learned is the only way my voice works anymore. "There's no latch, and there's no you, and I'm not opening anything."

The handprint stayed. I shaved around it. I want that noted in the record: I shaved around a dead child's handprint in my own mirror, because I am a man who has decided that if he stops performing the ordinary, the ordinary will stop performing for him, and then there will be nothing left between me and the chair.

By noon, every mirror in the house was doing it. The hallway mirror, the vanity in my grandmother's room, the little round one in the entry that had always been too high to see anything but the tops of heads. All fogged. All breathing. And here's the detail I keep circling back to, the one I can't put down: the fog was *warm*. I touched the glass—Ruthie dared me to, in the flat way she dares people when she's more scared than she wants to look—and it was warm the way a cheek is warm. The way a face is warm when it's been waiting a long time for someone to touch it.

"We could cover them," Ruthie said.

"With what?"

"Anything. Sheets. Towels."

"And then we won't be able to see them breathing. Is that better?"

She thought about it honestly, which is one of the things I like about her—she treats every horrible question as a real question. "No," she admitted. "I'd rather see it. Not seeing is how they got the whole town."

So we left them uncovered, all seven mirrors in the house, and that night I lay in bed listening to the soft, rhythmic fogging of the hallway mirror like a sleeper's breath through a wall, and I understood that this was the point. Not to frighten us. To *acclimate* us. Every breath on the glass was a rehearsal. Every handprint was a place setting.

The guest wasn't haunting the house. It was moving in.

* * *

On the third day—no. I'm getting ahead of myself, and dread arithmetic is nothing if not precise. The second day, the town began to watch us.

I'd noticed it before, of course. The way conversations stopped when I walked into the hardware store, then resumed a half-tone too loud. The way cars slowed on my street—not suspiciously, not aggressively, but with the gentle deceleration of people looking at something they'd already made peace with. But after the salt failed and Ruthie started sleeping on my couch, the watching changed quality. It stopped being surveillance and became something worse.

It became pity.

Marv at the hardware store gave me a discount on the plywood I bought to board nothing in particular, and pressed a bag of apples into my hands, and said, "You take care now, Mr. Sandoval," in the voice you use at a bedside. Mrs. Okafor from the diner sent over two slices of pie with her son, who wouldn't come past the porch and stood in the yard holding the plate out like a communion offering until I came and took it. Sheriff Aldous drove past the house four times in one afternoon. Not stopping. Just driving past, slow, and each time he touched two fingers to his hat brim, and each time the gesture looked less like a greeting and more like last rites.

"They think we're already dead," Ruthie said. She was watching Aldous's cruiser recede from the front window. "That's what it is. They're being nice to us the way you're nice to someone in the obituary photo."

"They think we've lost," I said. "There's a difference."

"Is there? From where they're standing?"

I didn't have an answer, and she knew I didn't, and that was the thing about Ruthie—she didn't need me to have answers, she just needed me to stop pretending I had them. She was fifteen and she'd been living in an orchard for two years and she had no use whatsoever for adult fictions. It was, I think, the most restful quality anyone has ever had.

The worst of it was Voss. On the second afternoon she came to the house—not to threaten, that was over, we were past threats, we were in the endgame now where everyone knew everyone's cards—and she stood on the porch holding a casserole dish like any neighbor, and she said, "Ines. When it's done, the school will need a new attendance ledger. I'd like you to know that whatever you decide, the children will be provided for."

"Provided for," I repeated.

"The town takes care of its own." She handed me the casserole. It was warm. Everything here was warm. "Your grandmother used to make this same dish. Chicken and dumplings. She'd sit right where you're standing and tell me, *not yet, Voss, not this year.*" Her eyes were dry and steady and infinitely tired. "Every year she said it. Thirty-one years, she said it. And every year the town ate."

Then she left. I stood on the porch holding the warm dish and understood, finally, what the pity was. It wasn't pity for the doomed. It was pity for the *late*. They looked at me the way you look at someone who has arrived too late to save the person they came for, and who is now going to have to sit with that forever. They thought I was a Sandoval, and Sandovals, in their experience, always said *not yet*, and *not yet* was just another word for *this year*.

* * *

The second night, Ruthie and I did the last of the preparations, and I use the word *preparations* with the full irony it deserves, because there is no preparation for what the deed says I have to do. There is only staging.

We went over the plan the way you'd drill a fire escape. The deed, copied out in my handwriting, folded in my jacket's inside pocket, against the heart, which is where I'd decided it belonged since it was, in every way that mattered, a sentence passed on my family before I was born. Ruthie's job was the door—she would hold the pantry open behind me, keep the mirror turned, keep the way back lit. "Not that there is a way back," she said, with her terrible fifteen-year-old precision. "But I'll keep it lit anyway."

"That's the job," I told her. "Keeping the way back lit even when there isn't one. It's called hope. It's very fashionable."

"Shut up, Mr. Sandoval."

"Mr. Crane," I corrected her, for the hundredth time, and she ignored me, for the hundredth time, because she'd decided in the orchard that I was a Sandoval first and everything else was paperwork.

We salted nothing. We covered nothing. We had learned. Instead we did the only thing that had ever worked in this house, the thing my grandmother had done for thirty-one years: we went into the parlor, and we sat at the table, and we set twelve places, and we ate dinner there ourselves, loudly, with the windows open, so that whatever was listening would understand that these seats were taken, that this table was occupied, that the invitation had not been answered.

It's an old superstition, eating loud. Ruthie made it up, or remembered it from somewhere—I never asked which, because in Marrow Bend the difference doesn't matter. We ate fried chicken and the Okafor pie and we laughed at things that weren't funny and we kept the lights on in every room and we did not answer the one knock that came at eleven, at the back door, soft as a moth, in a voice like flour sifting.

"Ines," it said. Not Denny's voice this time. Not my mother's. Something older, using the shape of a voice the way the salt had used the shape of a chair. "Ines, the table's set. Ines, we're waiting on you."

Ruthie's hand found mine across the table and gripped it, and we kept eating, and the voice said it again, and again, each time a little more patiently, a little more like family, until at some point past midnight it simply stopped, the way a host stops asking when the guest has made it clear he'll come when he's ready.

* * *

The last morning, I woke with flour on my hands.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. I went to bed—after Ruthie fell asleep, after I checked the deed, after I counted the doors, twelve, all present, all shut but not locked, the house holding its breath with me—and I did not get up until dawn. And when I woke, there was flour on my hands. Fine white flour, worked into the creases of my knuckles, dusted across my palms like I'd been kneading bread in my sleep.

I stood in the bathroom and washed it off and watched the water run white for a moment and then clear, and I looked at my face in the fogged mirror, at the handprint still bloomed there behind my own reflection, and I did the arithmetic one more time.

Three days had become one. One day had become hours. The moon would rise tonight over the Hollis farm, over the school where Voss was even now preparing her attendance ledger, over this house where twelve mirrors breathed and twelve doors waited and one pantry mirror stood between me and the table below.

I went downstairs. Ruthie was awake, blue ribbon tied tight, bat across her knees, watching me with the flat clear eyes of someone who has already survived the worst thing once and refuses to be surprised by a second act.

"Flour?" she said. She'd seen me washing.

"Flour," I said.

She nodded slowly. "Grandma Sandoval's hands," she said. "The dream. She did this too, didn't she. The night before. Every year."

"Every year," I said, "she woke up with flour on her hands, and every year she said not yet, and every year it wasn't enough."

Ruthie was quiet for a moment. Then she said the thing that I would carry down those stairs with me, the thing I would hold onto when the guest wore my mother's voice and my own face and every memory I had, the thing that turned out to be the only preparation that ever mattered:

"Then say something different this year."

Outside, the sky was already turning the color of a bruise healing, and down at the end of the drive, slow and ceremonial, Sheriff Aldous's cruiser rolled past one last time, and he did not touch his hat.
