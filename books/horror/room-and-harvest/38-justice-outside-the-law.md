## 38. Justice Outside the Law

The ledgers smelled of tallow and old rain when we carried them out to the schoolyard, and I remember thinking that was wrong somehow—that a thing responsible for so much should smell like nothing at all. Like paper. Like dust. But no: it smelled like something fed for a hundred years, kept warm in a dark drawer, turned over by careful hands.

Ruthie carried the deed wrapped in her flannel shirt like an infant.

"Careful," she said, though I hadn't touched it roughly. "It's heavier than paper should be."

She was right. The covenant deed weighed as much as a brick, as much as a stone from a foundation, as much as all the small decisions made over generations by people who wanted their fields fat and their bellies full and were willing to let someone else's child pay the interest. We set it on the pile with the ledgers—Voss's ledger, the archive ledger, three more we'd found behind the reverend's pulpit, each one fatter than the last—and Ruthie stood back and looked at the whole stack the way you'd look at a body.

"That's it?" she said. "That's everything?"

"That's everything they wrote down."

"Then there's more they didn't."

There always is. But we had what we had, and the sun was coming up on our second morning after, and the twelve were awake upstairs in the school gymnasium eating oatmeal with the blank patience of people who have been somewhere very far away and have not yet decided how to feel about being back. Denny Ruiz was among them. He had asked me twice who I was, politely both times, and both times my chest had done something complicated and unhelpful.

"Matches," Ruthie said.

I gave her the matches. She struck one against the box with the casual violence of a girl who has spent two years learning that hesitation is just fear wearing a nicer coat, and she held the flame to the corner of Voss's ledger.

Paper burns fast when it wants to. This paper did not want to.

* * *

**RUTHIE**

The flame sat on the corner of the ledger like a bird that had landed and wasn't sure it liked the perch. It didn't spread. It just sat there, orange and stupid, while Ines and I stood in the schoolyard watching it not do the one thing fire is supposed to do.

"It's waiting," I said.

"For what?"

"For permission, maybe. For somebody to say go ahead." I looked at him. He looked terrible, which was fair, because he'd spent a night in a chair under the earth letting something wear his mother's voice like a glove. "You're the deciding bloodline. Maybe it's your call."

He crouched down and put his face close to the little burning corner, close enough that I could see the ledger's edge glowing but not catching, and he said—not loudly, not like a spell, just like a tired man talking to a stubborn animal—"Go ahead."

The fire took it all at once.

Not gradually. Not page by page. One second the ledger was a ledger with a polite flame on its ear, and the next it was a column of light and heat and a sound I felt in my teeth, a sound like a long-held breath finally let out, and the other books caught without being touched, and the deed caught last and burned brightest, blue at the edges, and the smoke went straight up into the windless morning air in a line so straight it looked drawn.

I want to say I felt triumphant. I want to tell you I watched two hundred years of children's names going up in smoke and felt free. What I actually felt was cold, and hungry, and furious that there was no ceremony. No confession. Nobody came out of their houses to watch. Nobody knelt. Sheriff Aldous stood on his porch half a mile away—I could see him, a gray smudge in the gray light—and he did not come running and he did not look away either. He just stood there, holding his coffee, watching us burn his town's whole arrangement like a man watching weather.

That was the worst part. Not the fire. The ordinariness around the fire. You spend two years dreaming about the moment the thing ends and when it comes it arrives on a Tuesday morning in a schoolyard that smells like oatmeal and burning paper, and there is no thunder, and nobody says anything true, and the smoke goes up straight as a plumb line and then the wind takes it and it's just smoke.

Except the wind didn't take it. That's the part I keep circling back to. The smoke went up and then it bent—all of it, every thread of it, from every book—and it bent toward the school, toward the open window of Principal Voss's office, and it poured in through the window like water finding a drain, and then the window shut itself, gently, the way a mouth closes.

Ines saw it too. I know because he stopped talking mid-word.

We ran inside.

* * *

Voss was sitting at her desk with her hands folded, facing the window, and she was already coming apart.

I don't mean dramatically. I mean the way a leaf comes apart in late October—quietly, structurally, according to a schedule written somewhere before anyone asked it. Her edges had gone brown and dry. Fine lines ran through her cheeks like cracks in old varnish. When she turned her head toward us, small flakes drifted off her jaw and settled on her collar, and she smiled at us with lips that were mostly texture now, mostly suggestion.

"You burned it," she said. Her voice sounded like pages turning.

"We burned it," Ines said.

"I felt it." She unfolded her hands and looked at them, interested, the way you'd look at gloves you were taking off. "Do you know what I was, before? I was eleven. I was harvested in 1954 and they sent me back because the yield was better with a steward inside the school. Better selection." She said it without bitterness, which frightened me more than screaming would have. "Sixty-eight years I kept the books. Do you know how many children that is?"

Neither of us answered.

"Neither do I," she said. "That's the joke. I counted attendance every morning for sixty-eight years and I never once counted them." A flake fell from her cheekbone. She didn't brush it away. "My name was Margaret. I had a dog named Biscuit. I could show you where my house was. There's a gas station on it now."

"Margaret," Ines said, and something in his voice made her look at him fully, and I understood that this was the first time in sixty-eight years anyone had used that word in her direction.

"The covenant held me together," she said. "Like a splint holds a broken bone. You've taken the splint out. That's fine. That's correct. I only want to say one thing before—" she gestured at herself, at the browning, at the whole quiet catastrophe of her—"before I'm done. Tell the ones who woke up that they were not eaten. Tell them they were kept. It won't help. But say it anyway."

"I'll say it," Ines said.

"And tell yourself," she said, and here her voice did crack, one hairline fracture running through it, "that sitting in that chair was braver than anything any of us ever did, including me, especially me, and I include the dying." She turned back to the window. The sun was fully up now and it came through the glass and lit her like a lamp through parchment, and I could see, faintly, the shape of the desk through her shoulder. "Close the door on your way out. Not for me. Habit."

We closed the door. From the hallway, through the frosted glass, we watched her silhouette sit perfectly still while the light came through it more and more completely, until there was nothing behind the glass but morning, and then a soft sound like a book closing, and then a drift of fine gray dust settling on a chair that had held a woman for sixty-eight years and would hold no one ever again.

* * *

**INES**

Ash is honest in a way courts aren't. We swept Voss into a dustpan—an actual dustpan, from the janitor's closet, and I will carry the absurdity of that to whatever grave I get—and Ruthie said we should bury her under the oak behind the gym, and I said yes, and neither of us said any words over it, because what words were there. *Here lies Margaret, who was eleven once.* The oak will have to do the remembering.

By noon the ledgers were ash in a steel drum and the deed was ash in the same drum and I stirred them together with a rake until there was no telling covenant from record from steward. Then I opened every window in the school, all of them, top floor and bottom, and let the wind walk through the halls for the first time in living memory. It took papers off desks. It knocked a clock down in the front office. It moved through the building like a landlord doing a final inspection, and when it left, the school smelled like nothing, which is the best thing a building can smell like.

The town did not convene. That's the thing I need to write down plainly, because someday, if I'm ever able to write anything again, someone will ask how a town answers for a hundred years of trading its children, and the answer is: it doesn't. There was no court. There was no confession. Sheriff Aldous walked the length of Main Street that afternoon and shook no hands and made no arrests, least of all his own. Marta Hollis sat on her porch with a pie cooling on the rail beside her, and the pie cooled, and no one came for a slice, and she watched the road with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to. The founding families did not gather. The church stayed shut. Reverend Pike was dead and had died mid-sentence, and whatever sentence he'd been about to finish died with him.

What happened instead was smaller and stranger. All over town, quietly, people opened their windows. I saw it walking home—the Hollis place, the Aldous place, the little houses along Bend Road—windows pushed up, curtains tied back, houses breathing out. As if the covenant had also been a lid, and underneath it the town had been holding its breath for a century, and now, without deciding to, without discussing it, everyone exhaled.

Nobody thanked us. I hadn't expected it, exactly, but I'll admit I'd imagined some version of it—a nod across a yard, a plate of food left on a step. Instead they looked at me the way they'd begun looking at me even before the moon: with a vague, warm, unfocused kindness, the kind you give a substitute teacher or a mailman. Ruthie got the same look. Worse, actually—her own mother gave it to her, standing in the orchard among the trees where Ruthie had hidden for two years, and her mother said, "Oh—you're the Hollis girl, aren't you? You've grown," and Ruthie stood there and took it, and I watched her take it, and it was the hardest thing I watched all week, harder than the chair.

"They can't remember," she said afterward, on my porch, drinking tea she wasn't tasting. "That's the price. You paid it, not them."

"I agreed to it."

"You agreed to it for them. That's worse." She set the cup down. "They get to be innocent because you're not here to remind them they weren't."

The house was quiet around us. Twelve doors, all mine, none locked. Through the parlor doorway I could see the extra chair by the window, empty, ordinary, holding nothing but afternoon light. Somewhere in the walls there was no voice at all, and the silence of it was so complete it rang.

"Denny asked me today if I was staying," I said. "Through the year. He said the sub they're getting has a reputation."

"Are you?"

I thought about it honestly, which is all I have left to offer anyone. My records are fading—I checked; the state database lists Marrow Bend Middle School's seventh-grade position as vacant, filled pending, my own name dissolving out of the personnel files like sugar in rain. My grandmother's house knows me. The photographs have gone back to showing an ordinary parlor, an ordinary table, chairs in sensible numbers. Half the town would wave at me on the street tomorrow and not know why the wave felt familiar.

"I don't know yet," I told her.

Ruthie nodded slowly, and outside the window the light was starting to go gold and long, and down the road a figure was walking toward the house—small, carrying a backpack, kicking a stone along the shoulder of the road with the deep concentration of a kid who has nowhere in particular to be and all evening to be there.

Denny stopped at the gate. He looked up at the house, then at me through the screen, and he frowned the frown of a boy working hard at a feeling he doesn't have a word for.

"Mister," he called, "do you got any of those cookies? Somebody made cookies. I think it was you. I don't know why I think that."

I looked at Ruthie. Ruthie looked at me.

"Yeah," I said, opening the door. "Come on in. I think it was me too."
