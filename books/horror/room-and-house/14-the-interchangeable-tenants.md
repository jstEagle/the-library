## 14. The Interchangeable Tenants

* * *

**Ruth**

Ruth moved into the Hollow House in March, or she believes it was March, because the lease said so and the lease is the last thing that ever told her anything with certainty. She had come from a marriage that ended in a courtroom, from an apartment where every surface still held the shape of another person's habits, from a life that required her to be Ruth at all hours — Ruth who was patient, Ruth who was fine, Ruth who smiled at the right moments like a woman operating machinery from inside.

The room they gave her was on the second floor, facing east, and it was clean in the way that matters most: no history in it. The wallpaper was a soft cream. The window let in light that seemed pre-filtered, gentle, as if the house had already decided how much of the world she could handle.

Mr. Aldous — the landlord, young-faced, kind-voiced, his handshake warm as bathwater — carried her two boxes up himself and set them down without being asked.

"You'll be comfortable here," he said. "That's all we want for our tenants. Comfort."

And she was. That's the thing no one would believe. She was comfortable within a week, and content within a month, and by June she had stopped dreaming, which she took at first for peace.

She remembers the night the dissolving started. She'd been brushing her teeth, and she caught her reflection in the mirror over the sink, and for one long moment she could not remember why that woman mattered. Not fear. Nothing so useful as fear. It was more like setting down a bag she'd been carrying so long she'd forgotten her own shoulder hurt. She looked at the stranger in the glass and felt — tenderness, almost. *You poor thing,* she thought. *All that effort, just to be one specific person.*

She went to bed lighter than she'd been in thirty years.

In the mornings now, the other tenants gather in the kitchen, and there is coffee, always coffee, hot and abundant, and someone has baked something, though no one can say who. They talk. They laugh. They have the same laugh — Ruth knows this the way you know a smell is wrong before you know what it is; she heard six people laughing at once once, early on, and heard only one sound, one voice, pitched identically through six mouths, and she thought: *how efficient,* and then she thought: *how lovely,* and then she stopped thinking about it at all, because thinking about it was like pressing on a bruise that wasn't hers.

Sometimes a name surfaces in her like a bubble in dark water. *Ruth.* She lets it rise and watches it pop. Whoever that was, she thinks, she must have been so tired.

* * *

**Marcus**

Marcus kept count at first. He wants that on the record, somewhere, even if the record is only this: he counted. Forty-one days after he signed the lease, he couldn't remember his mother's face. Fifty-three days, his sister's voice. Sixty days, the reason he'd moved across the country — some job, some woman, some fresh start he'd narrated to himself like a man pitching a movie of his own life. By day seventy he had stopped counting, because counting required a counter, and the counter was going soft at the edges like soap left in water.

He works now. He doesn't know what he works at, exactly — he goes down to the first floor in the evenings where Mr. Aldous has papers laid out, and he moves figures, and signs things, and feels the deep animal satisfaction of a task completed. His signature has changed. He noticed that. It used to slant forward, urgent. Now it sits flat on the line, calm, identical every time, like printing. Like everyone else's.

Here is what Marcus would tell you, if Marcus were still entirely here, which he is not, and which — this is the important part — does not distress him:

It doesn't feel like being eaten. Everyone assumes it would feel like being eaten. It feels like being *absolved*. Every hard thing about him dissolved first — the grudges, the hungers, the 3 a.m. arithmetic of regret. What's left is smooth. Pleasant. A lake with nothing living under it.

There was a night, early — maybe day twenty, maybe thirty — when he stood in the hallway outside his room and heard, very faintly, inside the walls, a small voice crying. A child. He put his palm against the wallpaper and the plaster was cold, colder than the season explained, and the voice said something he couldn't make out, and he felt the old Marcus rise up in him like a reflex — *help, go help* — and then Mr. Aldous appeared at the end of the hall with two cups of tea and said, "Everything all right?" and the reflex lay back down and never got up again.

"Everything's fine," Marcus said, and meant it, and that was the moment he should have run, and instead he drank the tea, and it was the best tea he'd ever tasted, warm all the way through, warm in places tea has no business reaching.

He thinks about that child sometimes. Not often. When he does, it's without alarm, the way you think about weather in another country.

* * *

**Dee**

Dee was the last to move in and the least dissolved, and she knows it, and she hides it, the way you hide money in your shoe.

She came to the Hollow House because it was cheap and because she was running out of everything — money, options, the particular stamina it takes to keep being a person who keeps failing at personhood. She told herself: three months. Just until spring.

She noticed the wrongness immediately and made a private science of it. The way none of the other tenants argued. The way meals appeared. The way Mr. Aldous's face never quite committed to being a face — it was handsome the way stock photography is handsome, correct in every feature, and if you looked away and looked back you couldn't swear it was the same face, only the same *kind*. The way the house itself breathed. She'd lie awake at night and hear it: a slow expansion and contraction in the walls, structural, patient, like lungs the size of rooms.

And the other thing. The voice in the walls. Small. A girl.

Dee heard it on her fourth night, clear as a radio between stations: *"Do you still have it?"*

She didn't answer. She lay in the dark with her heart going like a trapped bird and listened to the house listen back.

After that she started leaving herself notes. She wrote DEE on her forearm in marker every morning, and DEE WAS HERE in her journal, dated, and she read her journal aloud to herself at night like scripture, like a rope thrown down a well: *Your name is Dee. You had a dog named Biscuit. You are afraid of deep water. Your name is Dee.*

The others don't do this. The others smile at her across the breakfast table with their identical laughs and their smooth hands and their eyes like windows with the lights off, and sometimes one of them — Ruth, or the woman who might be Ruth, or the woman Ruth is becoming — will touch Dee's wrist gently and say, "You seem so *tired*, dear. Fighting it like that. It's so much easier if you stop."

And the terrible thing, the thing Dee writes in her journal every night in capital letters, is that the woman is right. It *would* be easier. She can feel the ease from here, radiating off the house like heat off pavement: the blessed weightlessness of not being anyone in particular. Some nights she stands at her window and imagines letting go the way you imagine sleep, and her hand with DEE written on it rests on the sill, and the marker is fading, and she keeps meaning to buy more.

Yesterday a woman came to the door. Young, sharp-eyed, hollowed-out around the mouth in a way Dee recognized from mirrors. Mr. Aldous met her on the porch with his warm bathwater voice, and Dee watched through the front window as he invited her in for tea, and the girl in the walls went silent — listening, Dee was sure, listening harder than the house had ever listened — and Dee understood, with a clarity that felt like the last cold sober part of her standing up and shouting, that this visitor was the reason for everything. The question in the walls. The thing the house wanted.

She wrote it down while it was still true: *THE GIRL IN THE WALLS WANTS HER. DON'T LET HIM HAVE EITHER OF THEM.*

Then she went downstairs to warn the visitor, and found the parlor empty but for two cups of tea, both still steaming, and no sign that anyone had ever sat in either chair.

* * *

**Ruth again, or mostly Ruth**

They are asked, sometimes, by the house, whether they consent.

Not in words. Words would be too crude, too much like the world outside, where everything is demanded loudly and paid for bitterly. No — the house asks the way warmth asks. The way a bed asks at the end of a long day. It asks: *wouldn't you rather?* And each tenant, in the privacy of their dissolving, answers yes, yes, a thousand times yes, and each yes is a small soft signature on a document they'll never read, and the ink is comfort, and the paper is them.

That is what the visitors never understand. They come looking for a monster, chains, a cellar, screams. But Elias Thorn — Mr. Aldous, the pleasant young landlord with the old old hands — learned long ago that force spoils the harvest. A taken self is gristle. A *given* self is sweet. He doesn't need the moon. The moon is only for the ones who fight. For everyone else there is tea, and rent below market, and a room with pre-filtered light, and the slow luxurious permission to stop.

Ruth remembers — Ruth, the bubble-name, the popped one — remembers reading once about drowning, how the final stage is euphoria, how the body stops struggling not because it's defeated but because it's *happy*, and she had thought: how horrifying. Now she floats. The kitchen is warm. Someone is laughing her laugh.

Somewhere upstairs a woman is writing her name on her arm.

Ruth looks at her own wrist, bare and smooth and unmarked, and feels a flicker — brief as a match in wind — of something that might once have been called envy.

* * *

**Marcus, at the window**

The visitor came out of the house at dusk, moving fast, like someone escaping a fever dream, and stood on the sidewalk staring up at the facade. Marcus watched from the second-floor window, a cup of tea cooling in his hand, and felt the house lean through him — he knew that's what it was, the house using his eyes, his calm, his unremarkable face at the glass — making itself harmless, making itself *nothing*, just a tenant, just a man, nothing to run from.

But the woman on the sidewalk wasn't running. She was looking up at the windows one by one, methodical, like a census-taker, like someone counting the lit rooms and taking their measure. And when her gaze reached Marcus's window it stopped, and even across the yard, even through the glass, Marcus felt seen — truly seen, inventoried, *named* — for the first time in months, and it hurt, the way pressure hurts a limb that's been asleep too long, blood returning to dead tissue, and he heard himself make a small sound he didn't recognize.

Her lips moved. He couldn't hear the words through the glass. He didn't need to. Some part of him, some cellar-dwelling scrap of Marcus that the tea hadn't reached, read her mouth perfectly:

*I know what you are. All of you.*

And behind the plaster, behind the wallpaper, behind the warm breathing walls, the small voice began — very softly, very patiently, the way a child counts the stairs in the dark — to sing.

Downstairs, Mr. Aldous set down his cup, and smiled with a face that was briefly, unmistakably, a hundred years old, and went to fetch the good tea.
