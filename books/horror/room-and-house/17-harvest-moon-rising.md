## 17. Harvest Moon Rising

The moon came up the color of a peeled egg.

Sable saw it first through the window of Room 3B — the room that had been their mother's, that had been no one's, that had been waiting — and for a moment it looked ordinary, just another fat autumn moon climbing over the roofline of the house across the street. Then it cleared the eaves entirely and the light changed. Not brighter. *Thinner.* As if someone had stretched the night over a frame until the weave showed through.

Below, in the parlor, Dev was saying something about leaving. About the car. About the fact that the tenants had stopped pretending to be people an hour ago and were now standing in the hallway like coats on hooks, all of them facing the staircase, all of them smiling with the same mouth.

"Sable." Dev's voice, careful, the way you talk to someone standing on a ledge. "The moon's up. Whatever's going to happen—"

"It's already happening," Sable said.

They felt it in their teeth first. A pressure, low and tidal, like the moment before a storm front breaks — except the storm was inside the walls. The Hollow House had been still since they'd come back down from the basement, still in the way of something holding its breath, but now the stillness developed a pulse. The floorboards under Sable's feet warmed. The wallpaper — that tired floral pattern, roses gone brown as old blood — seemed to shift its weight.

And then the house began to unfold.

Not dramatically. That was the worst of it. No groaning timbers, no splintering wood. The hallway simply became longer when Dev walked down it, so that his footsteps went on past the point where the front door should have been, past the coat rack, past the window that now looked out onto a street that wasn't there. When he turned around, the parlor doorway had moved three feet to the left without disturbing so much as a dust mote. The rooms were rearranging themselves around them like hands closing.

"Okay," Dev said, very quietly. "Okay. I'm not liking this."

"Don't stop moving," Sable said. "I don't think it wants us standing still."

*It doesn't,* said a voice that was not a voice, small and dry as a moth's wing, rising out of the baseboard near Sable's ankle. Wren. She'd been close all evening — closer than she'd ever been, her presence no longer a whisper in recordings but a warmth against Sable's ribs, like a child hiding behind them. *It's stretching. It does this when he's frightened. The house is his skin, remember? And his skin is coming off.*

"Then where do we go?" Sable asked aloud, because speaking made it bearable, because fifteen years of radio had taught them that a voice in the dark is a kind of light.

*Nowhere,* Wren said. *That's the trick of it. There's nowhere to go and nothing to run from. Only the choosing. He can't make you choose. He never could. That's why he needs the moon.*

From somewhere above them — the second floor, or what the second floor had become — came a sound that stopped both their hearts at once.

Screaming.

Six voices screaming, and every one of them coming out of a single throat.

* * *

Elias Thorn stood at the top of the stairs.

He had been young when Sable arrived — young and wrong, a landlord's pleasant face stretched over something older, the way a glove looks over a hand that isn't yours. Now the glove was failing. Sable watched him grip the banister with fingers that flickered: smooth and forty, then spotted and ancient, then not fingers at all but something jointed and pale, then fingers again. His face moved like water with things beneath it. A woman's cheekbone surfaced near his jaw. A man's heavy brow rolled up under his hairline and sank away. His eyes were the only constant — wet, black, and absolutely terrified.

"You shouldn't be here," Thorn said, and the voice was six voices agreeing badly. "You should have taken the offer. It was a good offer. Nobody else got a better one."

"We're leaving," Dev said, and reached for Sable's arm.

But the hallway behind them had closed. Not with a door — with *wall*, seamless, warm to the touch, breathing faintly. Dev pressed his palm flat against it and pulled back like he'd touched a sleeping animal.

"Let us out," Dev said.

"I can't," Thorn said, and for one flickering instant the voice was only one voice, thin and old and almost human, and Sable understood with a lurch that this was true. He couldn't. The house had its own appetite now, fed for fifty years, and the moon had turned it over like a stone. "Don't you understand? It isn't me anymore. It hasn't been me since '74. I built it and it ate me and wore me and now—" He doubled suddenly, hand clawing at his own chest, and the scream came again, six voices in one throat, and Sable saw why.

His skin was opening.

Along the sternum, along the forearms, seams splitting like overripe fruit — and through the splits, faces. Pressed against the inside of him the way children press against windows. A young man with a crew cut, mouth open. An older woman with glasses fogged from the inside. A Black man in a postal uniform, eyes wide, palms flat against the underside of Thorn's skin as if knocking on a door from within. Six of them. The six tenants of 1974. The found family Sable had spent two weeks learning the names of, learning the shapes of their lives, learning how they'd sat around a radio in this very parlor on Sunday nights while Maren Vane made popcorn and little Wren fell asleep against her mother's side.

They were still in there. Fifty years, and they were still in there, and they were screaming to be let out.

"Oh God," Dev whispered. "Oh God, they're alive in there."

*Not alive,* Wren said softly. *Unfinished. He took everything they were and wore it, and what he didn't wear stayed folded up inside, waiting. Like clothes in a drawer. Like me.* A pause, and then, smaller: *Like what's left of me in you.*

Sable's hand went to their own sternum without deciding to.

"Mr. Thorn." Their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. Fifteen years of dead callers had taught them this much: you speak to the dying the way you'd want to be spoken to. "Look at me. Just you. Not the house. You."

The old man's face fought its way to the surface of itself. For a moment — one gray, shuddering moment — he was only an old man, a hundred and one years old, propped upright by the thing he'd built, weeping without tears because whatever gland made tears had been harvested long ago.

"They won't stop," he said. "All day they've been getting louder. Since the moon came over the ridge. They know. They always know on harvest night, but this year—" His jaw unhinged slightly, reset. "This year they know someone came to give them back their names."

"Then let it end," Sable said. "Open the seams. Let them go."

"And go where?" Thorn laughed, and the laugh curdled into the six voices again, and he straightened, and the terror in him hardened into something older and more familiar. Hunger. "Into what? You think being let out is mercy? They'll scatter like smoke. They'll be *nothing*. I am the only shape they have left. I kept them! Fifty years I carried them, every one, faithful as a husband!" He came down one stair. The whole house leaned with him. "You would know. You've carried something too, haven't you? All these years. How heavy is she, Sable? How heavy is the little bird in your chest?"

*Don't answer him,* Wren breathed. *He wants you talking about it. Naming it out loud makes it easier to take.*

But Sable had already learned that lesson the hard way, in a basement, over name-plates, and they held their tongue and watched Thorn descend another stair, and another, his body rippling with the trapped dead, and understood that the choice they had dodged for fifteen years was now standing on the staircase wearing a hundred-year-old suit of stolen skin.

* * *

Here is what Sable knew, in the compressed white silence of those minutes:

That the name they had lived under belonged to a four-year-old girl who died in this house in 1974, and that every memory before age six was a forgery stitched by proximity and grief, and that the person who had hosted a midnight radio show for fifteen years, who drank their coffee with too much cream, who loved exactly two people and lost exactly one, was built on a foundation of borrowed bone.

That giving the name back meant unmaking all of it. The childhood. The mother's kitchen. The first kiss, the last goodbye, the thousand small mornings that had added up to a self. Gone — not forgotten but *never having been theirs*, returned to the drawer, to the fold, to the dark.

That the alternative was standing right in front of them, offered twice now, warm as a bath: keep the name. Keep the life. Help Thorn finish what he started, harvest the child properly this time, and live easy forever in a house that would love them exactly as long as they were useful.

Fifteen years of holding. Fifteen years of thanks-for-calling and moving-on and burying the dead line under rational explanations. Fifteen years of being so tired.

Thorn reached the bottom of the stairs.

"You're so tired," he said gently, and his face was kind now, was the kindest face Sable had ever seen, was every comforting voice from every 3 a.m. call rolled into one. "I know. I've listened to your show. I know what it costs you, carrying all those voices, all that grief, night after night, and no one to carry you." He spread his arms — arms seamed and leaking faces — and the gesture was somehow tender. "Put it down. That's all I'm offering. Put her down. Let me take the weight. You don't even have to help me. Just stand there. Just stop holding. The moon will do the rest."

And God help them, Sable felt their arms begin to loosen.

It wasn't hypnosis. It was worse. It was *recognition*. Every night for fifteen years they had signed off the same way — *you're not alone, I've got the watch* — and every dawn they had driven home to an apartment where a sleep app recorded a child's voice asking questions, and they had done it all alone, and here at last was something offering to share the load, and the load was so heavy, and their arms were so tired—

*Sable.* Wren's voice, right against their heart now, not pleading, not frightened. Just present. *Look at the mail slot.*

"What?"

*The mail slot. In the front door. Look.*

Sable dragged their eyes away from Thorn's kindness. Down the unfolding hallway, past the hook-people standing silent in their rows, to the front door — which was, impossibly, still the front door, the one fixed point in the shifting house. Brass mail slot. Frosted glass. And through the frosted glass, the moonlight, and silhouetted against it, small and stubborn, a figure that could only be Dev, who had apparently gone out the back and around and was now standing on the porch pounding on the door of a house that had swallowed his friend.

They couldn't hear the words. But they could see his fists. They could see that he wasn't leaving.

*He gave you a name once,* Wren said. *On air. Remember? When you froze during the broadcast and couldn't remember who you were, and he leaned into the mic and said, "You're listening to Sable Vane, and Sable's got the watch tonight"? He made that name hold. Names given freely hold. That's the whole secret. That's why his works and mine works and Thorn's never did — he never gave himself anything. He only ever took.*

Thorn was watching Sable's face, and whatever he saw there made the kindness drop away like a mask slipping its string.

"No," he said. "No, you don't get to choose. You don't get to be *noble*—" And the house convulsed around them, walls flexing, the hook-people turning their identical heads in unison, and Thorn lunged down the last of the stairs with all six faces screaming in his skin and his hands reaching — not for Sable.

For Sable's chest.

*He's going to take her,* Wren said, and for the first time in fifty years, the child in the walls sounded afraid. *Sable, he's going to reach in and take her out the fast way, and it will kill you both, and the names will stay folded in him forever—*

Sable did the only thing left. The thing they had practiced for fifteen years without knowing it.

They spoke into the dark.

"This is Sable Vane," they said, loud, clear, broadcaster's diction, voice filling the unfolding house the way it filled a million sleepless bedrooms, "and I've got the watch tonight."

The house *stopped*.

Every wall. Every hook-person. Even Thorn, mid-lunge, frozen with his flickering hands a foot from Sable's ribs, his six mouths open. Because the house remembered this voice. Fifty years ago, in this parlor, a family had gathered around a radio on Sunday nights, and the sound of a voice claiming the watch — claiming the dark hours, promising *you are not alone* — was the last warm thing any of them had heard before the harvesting began.

In that held breath, one minute wide and deep as a lifetime, Sable Vane understood the shape of the choice at last. Not keep versus give back. Not self versus nothing.

It was simpler and more terrible than that.

Someone had to hold the watch while the names came out. Someone had to stand in the dark and speak steadily while six people screamed their way free and a child got her name returned and a hundred-year-old thief came apart at the seams. And the only person in the world who knew how to do that — who had trained for it every midnight for fifteen years — was standing in the parlor of the Hollow House with a stolen name burning a hole where their heart should be.

"Dev," Sable called, toward the door, toward the silhouette. "When this starts — whatever happens — say my name. The one you made. Keep saying it. Don't stop no matter what you see."

Through the frosted glass, the fists stopped pounding. A pause. Then, muffled, steady, Dev's voice: "I've got the watch with you."

Thorn screamed, and the house screamed with him, and the moon climbed higher, and the seams began to open.

Sable closed their eyes, put one hand over their own heart, and prepared to give back the only thing they had ever truly owned:

Nothing at all.
