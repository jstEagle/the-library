## 19. Six Goodbyes

Dawn came to the Hollow House the way dawn comes to a hospital — slowly, and with the sense that something had ended in the night, though no one would say what.

Sable sat on the front steps because the porch swing had been the first thing to stop being a porch swing. All through the small hours the house had been rearranging itself, rooms folding shut like hands, hallways shortening as if the building were exhaling after fifty years of held breath. Now, in the gray-blue light before sunrise, it was only a house. Three stories. Peeling paint gone soft as old fruit. A gutter hanging crooked over the east corner like a broken finger. Sable could see all of it at once, plainly, without the house shifting when they looked away, and that plainness felt almost obscene — like seeing a magician's face after the trick, ordinary and tired and human.

The door stood open behind them. It hadn't been locked since midnight. Nothing in the house had been locked since midnight.

Dev was asleep in the car, or pretending to be. Sable could see the shape of them slumped against the passenger window, phone still lit in their lap, the recording app open out of habit. Fifteen years of habit. Dev had driven them home from the moonrise and then refused, flatly, to drive away, and now the two of them kept their vigil in separate silences — one inside the car, one on the steps of a house that no longer wanted anything from anyone.

The grass was wet. The street was empty except for a jogger three blocks down who passed the house without slowing, without looking, without feeling anything at all, and Sable watched him go and thought: *that's it. That's what it will be now.* Just a house. A place where bad things happened once and then stopped happening, the way they stop everywhere else.

They heard the footsteps behind them and did not turn around. They already knew the weight of each one.

* * *

Maren Vane came first.

She crossed the porch boards without making a sound, though Sable could hear her presence the way you hear weather coming — a pressure change, a hush. In life she had been thirty-one years old and tired in a way that never washed out; in death she had kept the tiredness but lost everything else, and now she stood in the gray light looking less like a ghost than like a photograph someone had finally developed correctly. Her cardigan was the color of weak tea. Her hair was pinned up with a pencil, because she had always used a pencil, because scissors went missing in a rooming house and pencils didn't.

"Sable," she said.

Not Wren. She said *Sable*, deliberately, the way you'd set a cup down carefully on a table that might not hold it.

"You don't have to call me that," Sable said. Their voice came out scraped. They had been screaming an hour ago, or it might have been last year; time had gone soft at the edges since the moon set. "I gave it back."

"You gave back what was taken." Maren knelt beside them on the step, close enough that if Sable reached out they would have touched nothing but cold morning air, and somehow that was worse than touch. "What you made out of the years after — that's yours. Nobody can take that back. Not even you."

"I'm not anybody," Sable said. "That was the whole point. I unmade myself so you could—"

"Hush." It was such a mother's word, so ordinary, that Sable's throat closed around whatever came next. Maren looked out at the street, at the jogger long gone, at the milk truck two blocks over rattling through its route like the world was determined to keep being the world. "I died in 1974. I've had fifty years to think about what I'd say if I ever got to say anything. Do you know what I came up with? Fifty years, and all I have is this: I'm sorry I wasn't faster. I hid you under the floor of my room and I told myself it would only be for a minute, and then it wasn't a minute, and then it was too late, and you were already half-gone, half-made, a name with nobody in it. I left you unfinished. That's the sin I carried. Not dying. Leaving."

"You didn't leave me," Sable said. "You *made* me. Everything I am that isn't borrowed —"

"Is yours," Maren finished. "Yes. And I am so proud of it I could weep, if I still had the equipment." She smiled, and it was a real smile, crooked, with a chipped tooth Sable had never seen before and recognized instantly anyway, the way you recognize your own hands. "I have to go now, little one. We all do. That's how this works. You opened the door; we walk through it. But I wanted you to hear it from me first, before the others crowd in: you were a good child, and you are a good person, and neither of those things required a name to be true."

She stood. She was already thinner than thin, already more suggestion than shape, the dawn light starting to show through her like light through a curtain.

"Maren," Sable said — and it was strange, calling her that, having only ever known her as a voice in walls, a warmth in recordings, a grief-shaped absence. "Was I happy? Before? Was there any of it that was happy?"

Maren paused at the top of the steps. The sun was just clearing the roofline across the street, and where it touched her she began to shine instead of fade, which Sable decided was the same thing done gently.

"One evening," she said. "There was one evening. You'll see it. It kept." And she walked into the light and was not there anymore, and the porch was warmer where she had been, briefly, like a stone that had held the sun.

* * *

They came one at a time after that, because even freed, they were courteous. Fifty years in a man's skin had apparently taught them nothing about rudeness, or perhaps it had taught them everything about the value of being asked.

The second was Odell Marsh, who had been the piano tuner, and whose hands Sable remembered without ever having seen them — broad, scarred, smelling of rosin and pipe smoke. He sat down heavily on the step beside Sable as though he weighed something, which he didn't, which was its own kind of heartbreak.

"I tuned a piano in this house once," he said. "1973. Upright, in the parlor, hadn't been touched since the war. Thorn wouldn't let me finish. Said the money wasn't right. I always wondered if the piano knew." He flexed his fingers, watching the light move through them. "You know what I missed most, all these years? Not music. Silence between the notes. He never gave us any silence. Six of us in one skin, all talking at once, all wanting different things. You don't know what noise is until you've been a chorus against your will."

"I'm sorry," Sable said, because there was nothing else, and Odell shook his head.

"Don't be. Be loud instead. You've got a voice people listen to at three in the morning — that's a holy thing, whether the callers are dead or alive. Use it for the living for a while. They need it worse." He stood, and paused, and added, almost shyly: "Middle C. If you ever want to know what peace sounds like. It's the note everything else agrees to hang from."

Then he was gone, and Sable sat with the word *middle C* in their chest like a coin left in a coat pocket by someone who loved you.

* * *

Ruthie Okonkwo had been a nurse. She checked Sable over the way nurses do — a look, head to toe, cataloguing damage — and pronounced, "You're dehydrated and you're in shock and you haven't slept, and I can't do a damn thing about any of it, which is the rudest part of being dead." She crouched in front of Sable and took their non-existent pulse anyway, two fingers to the wrist, out of pure professional stubbornness. "Fifty years I watched him wear us. You know what I learned? He couldn't hold a face steady unless the person consented to be forgotten. Every single one of us, we got tired, and we let go, and that's when he could fold us in. You didn't let go. Four years old and half-dissolved and you *didn't let go*. So don't you dare call yourself nobody like it's a shame. Nobody is what he wanted to make of everyone. You chose it. There's a difference wider than the sky."

She pressed her cool fingers once, hard, against the back of Sable's hand — the closest thing to contact either of them could manage — and said, "Drink some water today. Eat something. Grief is physical work," and went into the light still giving instructions.

* * *

The twins came together, because they had always come together. Hal and Benny Ferro, twenty-two years old in 1974, delivery drivers, inseparable, and in death as indistinguishable as they'd insisted on being in life. They stood at the bottom of the steps arguing about which of them should speak first, which resolved, as it always resolved, into both speaking at once.

"—tell her the thing about the map—"

"—the *map*, Hal, tell her about the map—"

"What map?" Sable asked.

Benny — or Hal — grinned. "We drove every road in this county, back when. Made a game of it. Every road, every diner, every spot where the radio came in clear. We had a whole map of good signal. Dead zones marked in red." His grin softened. "Your show comes in clear everywhere, you know. We checked. Fifty years late, but we checked."

"We listened," the other twin said quietly. "Every night. All fifteen years. It was the best part of being dead, honestly, and we were dead in a *house*, so the bar was low, but still."

"You were our found family," Sable said. The words came out cracked. "All of you. I didn't know it, but — the callers, the dead line, all these years — you were the closest thing I had to—"

"Yeah," Hal said. "We know. Same, kid." And both twins, together, in unison, the way they'd done everything: "Take care of the radio guy. He cried in the car. We saw."

"He did not—"

"He absolutely did—"

And they were laughing as they went, both laughs at once, harmonizing, fading up the street into the brightening air, and Sable sat on the steps and laughed too, wetly, helplessly, because grief that ends sounds exactly like that.

* * *

The sixth waited until the sun was fully up.

Wren came around the side of the house, walking slowly, and Sable understood immediately that this was different, that this was the goodbye the whole night had been shaped around. The child was whole now — that was the terrible, beautiful thing. No longer a fragment, a question in the walls, a voice asking *do you still have it?* She was four years old and complete, wearing a corduroy jumper and one red mitten, and she looked at Sable with eyes that were Sable's own eyes, and were not.

She climbed the steps and sat down next to them and swung her feet, because they didn't reach the step below, and for a while neither of them said anything. The milk truck rattled past again. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Do you remember the evening?" Wren asked.

"No," Sable said. "Should I?"

"It's yours if you want it. The one Mom kept. But—" Wren frowned, working it out with the seriousness of a very young person doing very old arithmetic. "I think maybe you shouldn't take it. I think maybe it should stay mine. Because you got all the years after, and I only got the ones before, and this way we each have something true."

"That seems fair," Sable managed.

Wren nodded, satisfied, and then went quiet again, swinging her feet. When she spoke again her voice was smaller.

"I used to hate you. In the walls. I want you to know that. I heard you on the radio saying my name like it was yours, and I hated you so much, and then I listened longer, and I heard how careful you were with everybody. How you thanked the dead lady who drowned. How you never hung up on anyone mean. And I thought — oh. Oh, that's what my name grew into. That's what it did while I was waiting." She looked up. "It grew kind."

Sable could not speak. Their whole body was a closed throat.

"So here's the thing," Wren said, standing up, brushing off her jumper with brisk little pats, an adult gesture on a child's body that undid Sable completely. "You were never me. You were the one who carried me." She considered this, then added, with four-year-old precision: "Like carrying something heavy home from the store. You didn't buy it. It wasn't yours. But you got it home safe, and that counts. That counts the most."

"Wren—"

"I know." She smiled, and it was Maren's crooked smile, and it was Sable's own, and it was hers alone, and it was going away. "Me too."

She walked down the steps into the full morning sun, one red mitten raised in a wave, and the light took her the way it takes fog off a field — gradually, then all at once, then not at all, because some things aren't gone, they're just finished. The last thing to fade was the mitten. Sable would remember that forever: the red mitten, waving, longest to go.

* * *

Afterward the house was just a house.

Sable walked through it once, because it seemed important to see it emptied. The parlor smelled of dust and old wallpaper paste. The stairs creaked in the honest way stairs creak. Upstairs, six doors stood open on six bare rooms, and in the basement — Sable made themselves look — the harvest room was only a basement, damp concrete and a water heater ticking over, the name-plates gone or never having been, the dark merely dark. On the wall, faintly, where the plates had hung, were six paler rectangles, like marks left on skin after a bandage comes off. Healing shapes. Scars that had decided to become history instead of hunger.

In the parlor, against the far wall, stood an upright piano, out of tune, untouched since 1973.

Sable sat down on the bench. Their hands hovered over the keys, shaking slightly, and they pressed one note — middle C, because a dead man had told them it was the note everything hangs from — and the sound went out into the morning, flat and sweet and imperfect and entirely ordinary, and nothing answered it, and that silence was the kindest sound Sable had ever heard.

Outside, Dev honked the horn twice, impatient and alive and crying, probably, and Sable stood up from the bench of a house that no longer wanted anything, and walked toward the door, and the morning let them.

Behind them, on the parlor wall, in a house keeping its one warm memory, the pale rectangle where a seventh plate had never hung stayed bright a moment longer than the rest — and then, gently, it didn't.
