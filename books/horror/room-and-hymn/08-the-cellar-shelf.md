## 8. The Cellar Shelf

The cellar smelled like every cellar in every horror movie I'd ever worked through a shift to forget: cold earth, old wood, and the particular sweetness of something that had been wet a very long time ago. I'd been in a thousand basements in my career. I had a professional taxonomy for them. This one I would have classified, ten years ago, as *raccoon-adjacent, moisture issues, nothing that pays*.

Ten years ago I also would have classified the voice in the walls as settling joists, so my taxonomy could go to hell.

I'd borrowed a mattock and a short-handled spade from the hardware store, and Dale had thrown in a pair of work gloves without being asked. "Your grandmother bought her nails here for forty years," he'd said, bagging them. "Tell her the gloves are on the house." I didn't tell him she was dead. I don't think he needed me to. There was a way people in this town talked about the Blackwood house — a slight widening around the eyes, a sentence that changed direction midstream — and Dale's goodbye had that widening in it. *Come back and tell me how it goes,* he'd said, and then, quieter, like he was paying a toll: *if it goes.*

Father Okafor had offered to come with me. I'd said no, and he'd accepted it the way priests accept things, which is to say he let me have my lie and blessed me anyway. Marge had pressed a thermos of coffee into my hands on my porch and said, "You dig until you find it, and then you stop digging. Promise me the second part." I promised. I was already pretty sure I'd break it. People who spend a decade rationalizing noises are, it turns out, people who need to know.

The humming had been going on for three days now. Not from the nursery — from everywhere. Through the floorboards, the pipes, the stud walls, the way a refrigerator hums except the refrigerator in question was the house itself, and the song had no melody I could name and yet I kept almost naming it. It sat under my skin like a second pulse. Some nights I caught myself humming along, and that was the part I didn't tell Marge.

The journal had said *beneath the shelf, beneath the stone, where we put what we would not look at.* Grandmother's handwriting, 1974, the ink gone brown as old blood. The cellar had one shelf — a plank on brick legs against the north wall, holding forty years of paint cans that had turned to small colorful drums. I moved them out. Underneath, the flagstone was a different flagstone. That was it. That was the whole clue. The stones of the floor were gray and worn and laid in no pattern a mason would defend, but this one was darker, unweathered, its edges too clean, like a tooth set in a mouth of old ones.

I sat down on the cold floor with my coffee and looked at it for a long time.

"Okay," I told the house. My voice sounded stupid and human and I was grateful for it. "I know you can hear me. I'm going to dig now. If you have objections, this is the time."

The humming didn't change. It never changed. That was somehow the worst quality it had — the patience of it, the way a thing that has waited fifty years doesn't need to hurry for one more man with a spade.

* * *

The flagstone came up easier than it should have. Under it was loose earth, dark and soft, and it took me about forty minutes of digging to understand that the softness was because this earth had been dug before. Twice, if the layering was honest. There was a stratum of undisturbed clay, and above it, fill dirt that someone had shoveled back in with more speed than care. I found a 1973 penny at the boundary line, which was either archaeology or a coincidence, and with my track record I wasn't betting on coincidence.

The frost had been climbing the cellar walls too, I noticed. I hadn't written that down anywhere because I didn't want it to be true. Fingerprints in the frost, whorls the size of dinner plates, spiraling up the fieldstone like the house was being gripped from the outside by something very large and very gentle. Every morning there were more. Marge had a theory that it was counting down. I had stopped having theories. Theories were the currency I'd paid my rent in for ten years and I was broke.

The spade rang against something at about two feet down.

Not stone. Metal. A small, flat, metallic sound, the sound a locket makes against a spade blade, and my whole body knew it before my brain did — I actually stepped back, one foot up on the cellar stairs, heart going like I'd been caught at something. Because I had been caught, hadn't I. Caught by a dead woman's honesty, by a promise made before I was born, by the simple physics of a thing buried being a thing that can be found.

I got back down on my knees and used my hands.

The locket was in a tin — a tobacco tin, rusted at the seams, and inside it, wrapped in a scrap of christening gown gone yellow, was the locket itself. Silver, small, the size of a half-dollar. Engraved on the front, so fine I had to turn it in the flashlight beam to read it: *E.M.B. — kept, always.* Edward Martin Blackwood. The uncle I'd never known existed until a month ago. The stillborn boy whose memory the family had promised away and then, in the way of families, kept instead — kept the locket, kept the room, kept the hymn, and gave the thing behind the wallpaper nothing but a sealed door and fifty years of silence.

I held it in my palm and it was warm. Not sun-warm. Body-warm. The warmth of something that had been held.

And that's when I saw the bones.

They were beside the tin, not with it — tucked into the wall of the hole, curled, small. I'd been digging around them for twenty minutes without seeing them because the earth was dark and they were dark and grief makes you tunnel-visioned. I brushed the soil away and they came clear all at once and I made a sound I have never made before and hope never to make again.

Small bones. A ribcage the size of my fist. A skull the size of a plum, delicate, the sutures still soft-looking, the way infant skulls are.

"Not him," I heard myself say. "That's not him. He's in the churchyard. I checked. I *checked.*"

I had checked. First week in the house, before I'd found the reels even, I'd walked St. Brigid's cemetery with Marge and found Edward Martin Blackwood's stone, 1971–1971, two dates like bookends with no story between them. So these bones weren't his. These bones were something else. Something small enough for the nursery, something that had been in the room behind the wallpaper in 1974, something that had been — my mind circled the thought like a moth around a bulb, refusing to land — something that had *died* there. Or been buried there. Or had been buried *as* something, in the shape of something, because whatever had lived behind that wallpaper had needed a body the way we need names, and the family had given it one, and then taken everything back.

The journal's last entry came back to me, the one I'd read eleven times: *We gave it a shape and then we shamed ourselves for the shape. God forgive us, we buried the shape and told ourselves the debt was paid.*

The debt was not paid. The debt had been accruing for fifty years, and the interest was frost, and the frost was almost done.

* * *

I climbed the stairs on legs that didn't feel like mine and stood in the kitchen, holding the locket, and looked out the window over the sink.

The frost pattern on the glass was complete.

Except for one print.

I'd been tracking it the way Marge had taught me, mornings, with coffee, like reading a weather report written by a hand. Nine prints now ringed the window, great whorled spirals, each one arriving overnight, each one more defined than the last, until the whole pane looked like the inside of a grip. This morning the ninth had come in overnight, sharp and perfect, and there was a gap at the lower left corner exactly the size and shape of one more. A thumb, maybe. The last finger of a hand closing.

The humming was still there. Under the floor. In the pipes. In the walls where I'd spent ten years telling clients *that's just thermal expansion, ma'am, old houses talk.*

And then it stopped.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was the last professional skill I had left. The humming did not fade. It did not trail off. It stopped, mid-phrase, the way a music box stops when the lid closes, and the silence that replaced it was not the absence of sound. It was attention. It was the whole house holding its breath with me, every board and pipe and frost-whorled window focused on the small silver thing in my hand, waiting to see what I would do with it.

I had thought, in the nights I let myself think it, that the silence would be a mercy. That after weeks of the humming I'd welcome the quiet.

I would have given anything, standing in that kitchen, to hear it start again. Because the humming had been, I understood now, the sound of something keeping itself together. The sound of a thing that had been sung to every night for years, holding the song in its mouth the way a child holds a blanket, and the song was the only thing between it and whatever it became without the song. And I was holding the locket. And the locket was the last piece. And the hand outside the window needed one more finger's worth of time, and I did not know if the time was for me or for it.

The phone rang. I nearly went through the ceiling.

It was Marge. Her voice came through thin and fast, none of her usual porch-front ease. "Dominic. The priest is here. He says tonight's the longest night, and he says the pattern on his church window has nine prints too, and he says—" She stopped. I heard her take a breath, the kind you take before a confession. "He says whatever you found down there, you bring it up out of that cellar and you don't put it down until it's returned. Do you understand me? He says the house has been holding its breath since sundown and it's waiting for *you.*"

I looked at the locket in my palm. Warm. Still warm.

"Yeah, Marge," I said. "I found it."

"And the other thing?" she asked, very quietly. "Did you find the other thing?"

I looked down through the floor, through the kitchen tiles and the joists and the dark, toward the small curled bones in the earth beneath the shelf.

"Yeah," I said. "I found that too."

The frost on the window began, very slowly, to close the last print.
