## 3. Edith Marlow's Room

The solicitor's key ring had contained eleven keys, and ten of them had opened doors Ivo had already found open. That was the thing about the house — it didn't lock you out so much as decline to be closed. Every door on the ground floor sat ajar by exactly the width of a hand, as if the house were breathing shallowly and didn't want to be disturbed. He had spent his first evening walking through it with a tape measure and a notebook, because that was the kind of man he was: the kind who responded to dread with documentation.

The eleventh key was small, brass, and warm. He noticed the warmth only after he'd been holding it for an hour, and told himself it was the radiator pipe he'd been standing near. The radiator pipe was downstairs. He told himself it was his own hand heat, conducted through brass. He was very good at telling himself things. Seven years of fieldwork had made him a virtuoso of the reasonable explanation, and he could hear one being composed even now, even as he climbed the stairs to the one door in the house that was not ajar.

The door at the end of the landing was shut. Painted shut, from the look of it — layers of white gloss gone yellow at the edges, a hairline of dark gap beneath where no light showed. A door that had not been opened in a long time, or a door that had been opened so often it had learned to stay shut on its own. The brass key fit it the way a word fits a crossword clue: with a small, obscene click of rightness.

The hinge made no sound at all. That was somehow worse than a creak. A creak would have been a house being a house. This was a door that had been waiting.

Inside: dust, and paper, and a smell like the inside of an old piano — felt and wood and the ghost of hammers. Ivo stood in the doorway for a full minute with his hand on the light switch before he found it, a bakelite toggle behind the door frame, and the bulb that came on was dim and amber and buzzed like something considering speech.

The room had been wallpapered in lyrics.

Not printed wallpaper with a lyric motif — actual lyrics, handwritten, on the backs of sheets, on butcher's paper, on the reverse of a 1962 calendar, on anything flat enough to paste to a wall. Hundreds of sheets. Thousands. They covered every wall from floor to ceiling, overlapping at the edges like feathers, like scales, and every one of them bore annotations in a hand that started firm at the top of each page and deteriorated by the bottom into something like seismograph output.

He stepped inside and read the nearest sheet.

*Green grow the hollies, green grow the rind,*
*My love she is buried past finding—*

And beside it, in the shaking hand: **WRONG.**

He read another. A harvest song he knew from the Vaughan Williams collections, or thought he knew — the words were close, but the second line had been swapped with the fourth, and a syllable had been shaved off "barley" so it scanned wrong, deliberately, unmistakably wrong to anyone who knew the tune.

**WRONG**, said the margin.

Another. A lullaby this time, or the skeleton of one, its melody-line notated in a wobbly treble clef with two notes transposed.

**WRONG.**

Another. This one nearly perfect — he sang it under his breath, half a phrase, and stopped, because the room seemed to lean in when he did, the way a dog leans toward a hand. The annotation here was different. Smaller. Pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn it:

**ALMOST.**

He stood in the middle of Edith Marlow's room and felt the particular vertigo of a scholar who has found not a source but a person. Not folklore. A folklorist. Someone had lived in this room for years — decades, by the strata of the paper, older sheets buried under newer ones like geological layers — and had done exactly what Ivo did, except alone, except without funding, except in terror.

"Hello, Edith," he said, and his voice came out smaller than he intended.

* * *

The marginalia, read in order of the layers, told a story. Ivo spent the afternoon pulling sheets carefully from the walls — the oldest ones came away easiest, the paste long dead — and laying them on the single bed, which was made, military-tight, with a counterpane gone the color of weak tea.

The earliest layer was professional. Dates. Sources. *Mrs. Aldery, age 81, the hollies song, 14 March.* A good hand, a confident hand, the hand of a woman doing a job. The annotations here were the annotations of scholarship: *variant B; cf. Sharp; second line doubtful.*

Then, somewhere around what Ivo judged to be the late sixties, the hand changed.

It didn't deteriorate all at once. It tightened first, the letters getting smaller, the pen pressing harder, as if the writer had begun to believe that precision was safety. The annotations stopped being about variants and started being about errors — and the errors stopped being recorded and started being *made.*

*Deliberate transposition. If it cannot be found, it cannot be answered.*

Ivo read that line four times. He was a rational man. He had a rational man's toolkit: Ockham, Occam's razor, the whole set of blunt instruments. He reached for it now and found the toolkit lighter than usual.

The next sheet was worse. The hand was shaking now, genuinely shaking, and the lyric beneath it was a version of the hollies song he'd never encountered — every verse altered, the rhyme scheme broken on purpose, a word in the third verse replaced with a nonsense syllable that ruined the meter entirely.

**They know this one. They sang it back to me through the wall. I have made it wrong and they sang it back CORRECT.**

The capital letters had torn the paper.

Ivo sat down on the bed — on Edith's bed, in Edith's room, and he was aware of the impropriety of it and did it anyway because his legs had opinions of their own — and read on.

* * *

*They came for the songs the way a tithe-collector comes for grain. Not all at once. A verse here, a chorus there. I thought at first it was my memory failing. I am seventy-one years old and I thought: this is what age is, the songs going soft in the keeping. I wrote down what I could recall and it was wrong, all of it wrong, and Mrs. Petch at the shop sang a line back to me that I had not yet written down, and I understood.*

*It is not that I am forgetting. It is that something else is remembering instead of me.*

* * *

Ivo's pen had stopped moving. He noticed this because his notebook was open on his knee and the last thing he'd written was the word *tithe* and then nothing, a long nothing, the nib resting on the page and making a small dark period that grew as the ink bled. He looked at it. A period. A full stop. The punctuation of a man who has stopped being able to narrate.

He made himself write again. *Marginalia, sheet 34, c. 1970s. Subject describes memory as contested property.*

That was better. That was scholarship. That was a box he could put the fear in and label.

* * *

*Mr. Vetch came today. Tam. He is a boy still, or was — no, he is grown, they are all grown, the ones who were children in '68 are the old men now, and I am the old woman, and we are all of us kept. He asked me why I don't sign. I told him I had. I lied to a child who is not a child and he knew it and smiled at me with such kindness that I had to sit down.*

*He said: "You're the only one who's still fighting, Miss Marlow."*

*I said: "Fighting what?"*

*He said: "You know."*

*I do know. That is the whole horror of it. I know, and knowing is the currency, and I keep spending it and it keeps not running out, and I begin to suspect that is the design.*

* * *

The layers deepened. The room got colder as the afternoon went, or Ivo's blood did; the amber bulb buzzed on, indifferent. He worked through the strata with the tenderness of an archaeologist and the growing dread of a man reading his own mail from the future, because — and he admitted this only in the privacy of his own skull — the hand was starting to look familiar. Not the handwriting. The *project.* A woman alone in a house full of paper, documenting a thing that fed on documentation, using the only weapon she had, which was to get the facts wrong on purpose.

He had spent seven years recording things correctly. He had a filing system. He had a backup drive.

* * *

*I have made a discovery, and I hardly dare write it, because writing it correctly might give it to them. But I am so tired. I am eighty-three and I am so tired and the room hums at night when I get a verse nearly right, as if it is pleased, as if it is a dog waiting for the treat, and I hate that I have made a dog of it in my mind because it is not a dog. It is a landlord. It is the landlord of everything anyone has ever gotten exactly right.*

*Here is the discovery: it cannot take what is wrong. A song sung badly belongs to no one. I have tested it. I sang the hollies song mangled, every verse, to the empty lane, and for three nights the house was quiet, and I slept, actually slept.*

*But it comes back. It always comes back. Because I cannot help correcting myself. In the morning I remember the true version and I write it down and the ink is still wet when the humming starts.*

*I am at war with my own accuracy. Pray for me, whoever finds this. Pray that you are a worse scholar than I am.*

* * *

Ivo laughed. It came out as a single dry bark, inappropriate in the amber dark, and he pressed his hand over his mouth like a man at a funeral.

*A worse scholar.* He looked at his notebook, at the careful dates, the careful citations, the careful everything. He thought of the archive in London, of his reputation, of the paper he was going to write, of the word *rigorous* that appeared in every reference to his work. He thought of the recordings downstairs, the ones he'd set up last night, the ones he had not yet played back because he'd fallen asleep before dawn and told himself the house settling was a normal acoustic event in an old building.

He had been in this house three days. He had already begun to be careful. Careful was the first symptom. Edith's hand had started careful too.

* * *

The last layer of paper — the newest, the one still slightly tacky at the edges, though it couldn't have been, though the woman had been dead or gone for years — was a single sheet pasted over the others beside the window, positioned, he realized with a slow cold turn of his stomach, at exactly the height of a person standing at the glass and looking out.

It was the hollies song. The true version. Every verse, complete, correct, beautiful — the fullest text of it Ivo had ever seen, a text that would make his career, that would anchor a monograph, that would have the folk-song establishment weeping into their sherry.

And across it, in the shaking hand, one word, written so many times the letters had become a black smear, a wound in the paper:

**ALMOST ALMOST ALMOST ALMOST ALMOST**

And beneath it, smaller, in a hand that had gone suddenly calm — the calm of the very end, of the last page of a suicide note or a love letter, of a decision:

*I could not make the last verse wrong. It is the verse my mother sang me. I have tried for thirty years. My hand will not do it. It knows that if I ruin that verse, no one will ever have sung it to me correctly again, and I will have lost her twice.*

*So I leave it here. Correct. Perfect. For whoever comes next.*

*I'm sorry. I was going to burn it and I could not. She is in that verse. Let it be a trap for them instead of a keepsake for me.*

*Whoever you are — you will want to sing it. That is how it works. You will want to sing it because it is lovely, and because singing it correctly will feel like having her back.*

*Don't.*

* * *

Ivo stood at the window for a long time. The lane outside was empty in the way that Hollowmere was always empty, which was not the emptiness of abandonment but of attention — the village felt like a room in which everyone had gone quiet to listen.

The sheet was at his eye level. Of course it was. She had pasted it at her eye level, and she had been, by the end, a small bent woman, and he was a tall man, and the paper met his gaze anyway, which meant the room had adjusted it, or meant nothing, or meant everything, and he had run out of reasonable explanations for the evening.

He did not sing it. He wanted to; his throat ached with wanting, the way a hand aches to touch a hot stove to prove it can bear it. The melody sat in him whole, complete, note-perfect, having arrived through his eyes the way water arrives through a crack in a wall. He could feel the last verse especially, the one about the holly bearing the crown, the one her mother sang her, warm as a hand on the back of his neck.

*Don't,* she had written.

"Right," Ivo said aloud, to the room, to Edith, to whoever kept the books here. "Don't. Noted."

The room hummed. Very faintly. The note was almost — *almost* — the first note of the hollies song, and it was either the bulb buzzing, or the pipes, or the house settling, or a dog waiting for the treat.

He turned off the light. In the doorway he paused, and did the thing he would later understand was the first honest thing he'd done in Hollowmere: he took the true text of the last verse out of his memory as best he could, deliberately, the way you'd take a splinter out — by grabbing the wrong end of it, by misremembering on purpose, changing the holly to the hazel, the crown to a cap, ruining it, mangling it, making it his.

It felt like blasphemy. It felt like freedom. He couldn't tell the difference, and suspected that was the whole point.

Behind him, in the dark, the amber bulb flicked itself back on.

Ivo stood in the hallway and watched Edith Marlow's room glow through the gap of the door he had not quite shut, and he thought about the recorders downstairs, the ones running all night in the front room, the ones capturing the ambient sound of the house for the archive.

He decided he would play them back in the morning.

He decided that tonight he would sleep with the door of his own room shut, properly shut, and the key turned, and his headphones on, and if the house wanted to hum, it could hum to itself.

He was a rational man. He had explanations for all of this.

But as he lay in bed that night, listening to the house breathe its shallow breath through every ajar door below him, he found himself counting the verses of the hollies song and getting six, and then seven, and then six again, and it was a long time before he understood that the number wouldn't settle because something on the other side of the wall was counting along with him — and getting it right.
