## 5. The Village That Won't Look Up

I woke on the fourth morning to a house that had tidied itself overnight.

I don't mean that anyone had cleaned it. I mean the doors that had sat ajar — all of them, every one, at the same apologetic three inches — had drifted closed while I slept, and when I opened them again they swung back to exactly three inches with the soft insistence of a cat returning to its spot. I stood in the hallway with my tea and conducted an experiment: I propped the bathroom door wide open with a chair. By the time I'd finished my tea the chair was in the middle of the landing and the door was at three inches.

"Fine," I told the hallway. "Keep your secrets."

The hallway, I should note, was longer than it had been yesterday. I had measured it on arrival — forty-one of my paces, and my pace is nothing if not consistent after years of pacing out churchyards for survey work. This morning it took me forty-four. I wrote this down in my field notebook under *observations, structural*, and then, after a moment, crossed out *structural* and left the entry unclassified. There is a category of thing you do not want to classify too early, because once you've named it you have to decide what to do about it, and I was not yet ready to decide anything except to keep walking Hollowmere and pretend I was still doing folklore.

So that's what I did. I took my map, my clipboard, and my professional smile, and I went out to count houses.

* * *

The Ordnance Survey sheet from 1961 shows sixty-two dwellings in Hollowmere. My landlord's solicitor — Mr. Pell, a man built entirely of briefcases and apology — had told me "a dozen or so families remain," which even then struck me as arithmetic done by someone who feared numbers. I intended to establish the truth the way you establish anything: door to door, name to name, with a clipboard as shield.

By noon I had learned something more interesting than the truth.

The first house on Chapel Lane belonged, according to the woman who answered its door, to nobody. "The Winters moved away," she said. "Years back." Her name was Mrs. Aldous, and she had the particular cheerfulness of someone performing wellness for an audience. Behind her, through the doorway, I could see a hall stand with two coats on it, both damp, both recently worn.

"And where did they go?" I asked.

"Away," said Mrs. Aldous, with the finality of a slammed drawer. She wished me a lovely day and closed the door before I could ask away *where*, or why her hall stand suggested the Winters might be expected back for supper.

The next house along, number nine, was answered by a man who introduced himself as Mr. Winter.

I looked at my map. Number eleven was the one Mrs. Aldous had identified as empty. I said as much, carefully, the way you'd handle a live wire.

"Oh, they moved away," said Mr. Winter, nodding toward number eleven with real warmth. "Years back. Sad business. Lovely family."

"But you're—"

"Winters, yes." He smiled. His teeth were very even, like piano keys no one played anymore. "Different Winters."

There are no different Winters. There is no such thing as a village that swaps one family of Winters for another without anyone noticing, any more than there is such a thing as a river that changes its water and stays the same river — except, of course, that rivers do exactly that, and perhaps villages do too, and perhaps the question I should have been asking was not *who lives here* but *what keeps deciding to live here*.

I put a tick on my map anyway. Ticks were easier than thoughts.

* * *

By mid-afternoon the pattern had hardened into something I could have submitted as a paper if any journal would have taken it, which none would, because the pattern was insane.

Every occupied house claimed its neighbors had moved away. Every supposedly empty house was clean. Not tidy — clean, in the way of things maintained, glass washed, paint touched up, gardens weeded with the fussy competence of people who intend to go on needing gardens. I looked through windows into rooms with chairs set at tables, teacups upside down on drainers, coats on hooks. In one front room a newspaper lay folded to the crossword, half completed, pencil resting in the gutter of the page as though the solver had merely stepped out to check the weather and would be cross to find a stranger reading over the puzzle.

The windows themselves were the worst part. Dark behind clean glass — every single one. Not curtained; dark. I walked the length of Well Street at four o'clock on a bright October afternoon and not one window held a reflection of the sky. They held a darkness that sat just inside the glass like water behind a pane, and when I raised my hand to knock at the door beneath one of them, the darkness seemed to lean, very slightly, the way a listener leans.

I knocked anyway. Nobody came. But from somewhere inside — deep inside, further than a house has any right to be deep — I heard a sound I chose to file under *pipes*. It was rhythmic. It was patient. If you made yourself believe in pipes hard enough, it was almost pipes.

* * *

Tam Vetch found me at the well at the top of the hill, which is the kind of sentence I would once have written into a monograph with scholarly delight. Now it just reads like a threat.

He was sitting on the well's stone lip with a bottle wrapped in a paper bag, watching me mark my map, and he watched the way old men watch — without blinking enough.

"You're mapping us," he said.

"I'm mapping the houses. For the survey. Occupancy records help establish continuity of tradition—" I gave him the lecture-hall version, the soothing version, the version with footnotes implied.

"Mm," said Tam. "And what does your map say so far?"

"That about half the village is empty."

"And what do the houses say?"

I stopped writing. "Sorry?"

"The houses." He took a pull from the bottle. His hands were steady, which somehow made it worse; drunk men usually tremble, and Tam's stillness had the quality of something balanced very precisely on an edge. "You're a folklorist. You know better than to ask the neighbors. Neighbors lie the way birds sing — it's just what they're for. Ask the houses."

"I don't think the houses are talking to me, Mr. Vetch."

"Not yet." He said it comfortably, the way you'd say *not yet raining* in a place where rain is a matter of time rather than chance. Then he leaned forward and tapped my map with one yellowed fingernail, right on the little square I'd drawn for number eleven, Chapel Lane. "This one. Who told you it was empty?"

"Mrs. Aldous. And a Mr. Winter says the family moved away."

"Did he." Tam smiled, and his smile was the loneliest thing I'd seen all week, and I had spent the week alone in a house that rearranged itself. "And did Mrs. Aldous tell you the Winters' boy drowned in the mill race in 1974? Did Mr. Winter mention he buried his wife in the spring? Did either of them say who it is that weeds those gardens?"

The wind came up the hill and moved the grass in a long slow wave, all one direction, like something passing through.

"People move away," I said. "It happens. Villages hollow out. I've documented it a dozen times."

"Aye," said Tam. "They do move away. That's the trouble. Nobody ever moves *here*." He stood, joints cracking like green wood, and looked down at me with eyes that had the watery kindness of a man about to warn you off a road he can't avoid himself. "Count them again tomorrow, folklorist. Count them twice. See if the number holds."

Then he wandered off downhill, weaving gently, and I sat at the well and did not count anything for a long while.

* * *

I counted again the next morning. Sixty-two dwellings on the 1961 sheet. I walked every street, every lane, the track behind the chapel ruin, the croft by the mill. I ticked and crossed and annotated until my hand ached.

Forty-one houses: dark, clean, maintained, empty. Fourteen houses: occupied, according to their doors being answered, though I noticed now that the residents never quite met my eye — they spoke to my clipboard, my shoulder, a point slightly above my head, as if my face were a detail they'd been asked not to study. Seven houses I couldn't classify at all, because although lights burned behind the glass in the evenings — I checked, walking the lanes after dark like a burglar with a bibliography — no one ever answered, and the darkness behind those particular windows had a texture to it, a depth, like looking down a well that was politely pretending to be a room.

Sixty-two minus forty-one minus fourteen leaves seven. The number held. I checked it three times, because Tam had told me to count twice, and I am constitutionally incapable of taking advice without adding a redundancy of my own.

It was on the third circuit, outside the last of the seven, that I saw the child.

She was small — eight, maybe nine — sitting on a garden wall with her legs swinging, watching me with the frank appraisal children reserve for new animals. She wore a red coat that was too warm for the weather and she had the stillness of a child who has been told to wait somewhere and has made waiting into an art.

"Hello," I said, because one does.

"Hello," she said. "Are you the new rememberer?"

The word went through me like a needle finding a vein. I actually felt it — a cold thread from ear to sternum.

"I'm a folklorist," I said. "I collect songs and stories. For an archive. In London."

"That's rememberer," she said, with the patience of the very young explaining things to the very dense. "Mum says you'll write it all down so it's kept right." She hopped off the wall and came closer, and up close her eyes were a gray so pale they were nearly colorless, and they did not blink quite often enough either. "Is that your job? Keeping it right?"

"It's — recording it. Accurately, yes."

"Exactly right?" She was very close now. She smelled of soap and woodsmoke. "Word for word? Note for note?"

"Traditions vary between informants," I heard myself say, retreating into methodology like a man backing into a cupboard. "Variation is itself part of the record—"

"Oh." She considered this. Something in her face fell, minutely, like a small machine losing pressure. "That's a shame. It likes it exact."

"*It* likes what exact?"

But she was already stepping back, already turning, already becoming a red coat diminishing down the lane, and she called the last part over her shoulder without stopping, lightly, the way children call things that aren't jokes but haven't been taught yet that they aren't:

"He'll be pleased anyway. He says you ask good questions."

* * *

I got back to the house at dusk and locked the door, which was a gesture, since the doors had opinions of their own, and sat at the kitchen table with my map spread out and my hands flat on either side of it to stop them doing anything embarrassing.

Here is what I knew, laid out like a hand of cards I didn't want to look at:

A village of sixty-two houses, forty-one of them maintained by no one for neighbors who had "moved away" decades ago and left their crosswords half-finished. Residents who wouldn't meet my eye but spoke warmly of absent families whose dead they hadn't mentioned. An old drunk who counted houses like a man counting exits. A child who used a word I'd never heard — *rememberer* — as though it were a job title with a salary and a pension. And a pronoun. *It likes it exact.* A pronoun needs an antecedent, and the only candidate in evidence was the village itself, or the dark behind its windows, or the thing the whole arrangement existed to feed.

Because that's what it was, wasn't it. An arrangement. Forty-one clean houses isn't neglect; neglect lets paint peel. Somebody — some*thing* — was keeping Hollowmere the way you keep a set: dressed, lit, ready for the production to resume. And the fourteen residents weren't living in a dying village. They were living in a display case, and they knew it, and they had all agreed, silently, mutually, never to look up at the glass.

I thought about Edith Marlow, the previous tenant, whose sealed room waited upstairs like a held breath. I thought about the papers Mr. Pell had mentioned and hurried away from. I thought about the second voice I hadn't heard yet, and how the house had begun, in its small furniture-shifting way, to make room for something.

On the map, my forty-one crosses looked like a constellation. I found myself tilting my head, the way you do at stars, trying to see what shape they made.

They made the shape of a house. Of course they did. Forty-one empty houses arranged around fourteen lived-in ones, and the lived-in ones clustered at the center like a heart, and my rented cottage sat at the very middle of the heart, and I had drawn the whole thing without once seeing it, the way you can walk a maze all day and only see the design from above.

Someone had designed it. Someone had designed it long ago, and the village had grown around the design like bone around a splint, and I was sitting in the center of it holding a clipboard, asking good questions.

*He says you ask good questions.*

I poured a drink I didn't want and carried it upstairs past the sealed room, whose door was at three inches, and I stood there a moment in the dark of the landing listening to the house breathe its ductwork breathing, and I said, quietly, to no one, to the dark behind everything:

"I'm just here for the songs."

From below the floor, from inside the walls, from the general direction of everywhere, something that was not pipes exhaled — long, satisfied, almost fond — and the hallway light came on by itself, lighting my way to bed.

I did not sleep much. But I did dream, and in the dream I was counting houses, and every time I finished, the number was one smaller, and somewhere a voice said, kindly, *almost home.*
