## 6. First Question

I want to be precise here, because precision is the last thing I had left that week, and I spent it like a man spending his last coin on a lottery ticket.

The recorder was a Sony PCM-D100 — field archivists' workhorse, the kind of machine you can drop off a cliff and it will land recording. I'd used it in Suffolk barns, in a disused chapel in Wales where the bats had chewed through two foam windscreens, in a pub in Norfolk where the folk club met on Thursdays and the landlord's dog sang along on the choruses. It had never once produced anything I hadn't put into it. That is not a mystical claim. That is what microphones do: they are honest to the point of stupidity. They record the room. The room was me, a kettle, and forty years of other people's dust.

I set it up on the kitchen table at 11:40 p.m., checked the levels — peaking around -18 dB with my voice, ambient floor around -50 — pressed record, said aloud for the benefit of the archive, "Baseline recording, night one, Hollowmere, Ivo Crane speaking," and went to bed.

That much I am certain of. I have the habit of certainty the way some men have a stutter. You say the date, you say your name, you state the conditions, and then whatever happens afterward belongs to the record and not to you. It's a superstition dressed as methodology, and I've never once examined it closely because examining it would mean admitting it's a prayer.

* * *

Morning came gray through the kitchen window, the light of a place that hasn't decided whether to bother. I made tea, ate toast with the marmalade I'd brought from home because I already distrusted the village shop's stock rotation, and sat down with headphones to log the night's audio, as I had done perhaps four hundred times before in my career.

The first hour was perfect. Wind in the chimney doing its low organ note. A mouse or something mouselike in the wall cavity, moving with the busy small ambition of mice. At 1:12 a.m., the house settled — that creak old houses make when the temperature drops and the timbers give up their arguments for the night. I noted it. *House settling, 01:12.* My handwriting in the log book is very tidy. I remember being pleased with how tidy it was.

At 2:03 a.m., I heard myself breathing.

This requires explanation, because "breathing" sounds dramatic and what I heard was not dramatic. It was the slow, even respiration of a sleeping man, picked up by a sensitive microphone in a quiet room. I breathe through my nose when I sleep; I know this because an ex-girlfriend told me it was the only endearing thing about me. The recording showed exactly that: nasal, unhurried, roughly fourteen cycles a minute, the respiration of a man in deep sleep in the next room.

The problem — and I want you to appreciate how long it took me to find the problem, because I listened to that stretch of tape four times before I let myself see it — was that the breathing was coming from the kitchen.

From the table. From approximately eight inches from the microphone.

I did what any rational person does. I invented ductwork. Hollowmere's house had no ducts — it was heated by radiators and spite — but the human mind is a generous employer and will hire anyone for the position of Explanation, no references required. Old plumbing. A draft across the mic grille making a periodic sound. Some resonance of the house itself, breathing in its own way, ha ha, houses do that, don't they, old houses, all that timber expanding and contracting, perfectly natural, perfectly explicable, write it down and move on.

I wrote: *02:03–02:40, low periodic ambience, possibly structural/draft-related.*

"Possibly." Even my denial was hedging. Somewhere under the floorboards of my skull, a version of me that hadn't yet learned to lie was reading that word and raising an eyebrow.

* * *

Then, at 3:26 a.m., the second voice arrived.

Let me describe it technically first, because the technical description is somehow worse than any atmospheric one. It was close-miked. Not room-distant, not echoing down a hallway, not the smeared ghost-sound of something recorded through a wall. It was *close* — the proximity effect was audible, that bass warmth a mic picks up when a source is inches from the capsule. Whatever spoke, spoke directly into the microphone, into the space where my mouth had been eleven hours earlier, with the dry patience of a man who has been waiting politely for his turn.

And it answered a question.

Here is the transcript, verbatim, from my log book, which I still have, though I have since burned most things:

**[03:26:14] VOICE (M?, 60s?, calm):** "As long as it takes."

**[03:26:31] VOICE:** "Longer, if you're sensible. They usually aren't, at first."

That's it. Two utterances, seventeen seconds apart, and then nothing but my own sleeping breath until dawn.

There is no question on the recording. There is no gap where a question might have been edited out — I've done enough editing to know what a cut sounds like, and besides, who would cut it? There is no question. There is only the answer, arriving like the second half of a conversation, patient as postage, addressed to someone.

To me. Obviously to me. *How long do you mean to stay?* — that is the question it was answering; you can hear the shape of it in the reply the way you can hear the shape of a keyhole in the scratch-marks around a lock. *As long as it takes. Longer, if you're sensible.* Someone had asked this thing how long I meant to stay in Hollowmere, and it had replied with the weary authority of a landlord discussing a tenant.

The question hadn't come from my mouth. I'd have bet the house — such as it was, such as it was turning out to be — on that. But the horrible arithmetic of the situation offered only so many possibilities, and I lay on my bed that morning running them like a man counting the exits in a burning building and finding each one bricked up.

One: the voice was answering a question asked by someone else, present in the kitchen at 3:26 a.m., whose presence the microphone had otherwise failed to detect. Two: the voice was answering a question asked by me — silently, asleep, in some register below the threshold of air. Three: there was no question, and the voice simply talked, the way lonely people talk to empty rooms, except inverted.

I found option three oddly comforting for about ninety seconds. Then I thought about what kind of loneliness talks *into* a microphone positioned for a sleeping man's benefit, and stopped finding anything comforting at all.

* * *

I checked the equipment twice. I want to be exact about that too, because the number matters — twice, and then a third time that I refused to count because counting it would have been an admission.

First pass: I opened the recorder, inspected the capsule, checked for moisture, checked the windscreen seating, ran a test tone, re-recorded my own voice and compared waveforms. Everything clean. The machine was fine. Machines are always fine; that's their whole tragedy.

Second pass: I checked for interference. Radio frequency pickup is real — a cheap charger, a neighbor's CB radio, some longwave ghost bleeding into the preamp. I unplugged everything in the house, turned off my phone, moved the recorder to the far side of the kitchen, and recorded ten minutes of silence in full daylight. Playback: silence. Clean floor noise, -52 dB, nothing else. Whatever had spoken had needed neither power nor proximity nor, apparently, daylight hours to exist — but it had chosen not to use the daylight, and I logged that observation and then immediately pretended I hadn't made it.

The third check was the one I didn't count. I took the recorder outside, walked to the far end of the garden, held it up to the sky like an idiot offering a sandwich to God, and recorded another ten minutes. Wind. A crow somewhere being cynical about the morning. Nothing.

So: not interference. Not the machine. Not the room's acoustics playing tricks — proximity effect doesn't fabricate consonants. The T in "takes" was crisp. The S in "sensible" had the faint lisp of a man missing a tooth, or of teeth grown strange. These were details. Details are the enemy of denial, which is why denial works so hard to keep you at the level of impression. *A voice* is arguable. *A dry male voice in its sixties, close-miked, slightly lisping, replying to an unheard question about my tenancy* is a fact, and facts have weight, and weight pulls you down toward conclusions.

I spent the rest of that morning doing what academics do when confronted with a fact: I built it a little office and gave it paperwork. I transcribed the utterances. I timestamped them. I cross-referenced the breathing segment. I drafted a memo to myself — actually drafted it, pen on paper, in the log book — titled *Alternative Explanations, Night One,* and listed: (a) radio interference, ruled out; (b) mechanical artifact, ruled out; (c) auditory pareidolia — the mind hearing speech in noise — which collapsed the moment I played the clip for the third time and heard the toothless S again; (d) hoax. Someone entering the house at night. This one I held onto longest, because it was the only one that kept the world intact. A prankster. Tam Vetch with a dictaphone and a grudge against folklorists. The solicitor, testing the new tenant.

But the door had been locked. I'd locked it — I'm a man who locks doors in villages where everyone knows everyone, a habit that had seemed paranoid in London and was beginning to seem quaint here. And the breathing. The breathing had gone on for thirty-seven minutes, paced to mine, and no prankster lies on a kitchen table breathing in sync with a stranger for over half an hour for fun. And the voice had answered a question about *me*, with the specific weariness of someone who has answered it many times before, for many tenants, going back further than dictaphones, further than microphones, further, I suspected with a cold sliding feeling under my sternum, than questions.

* * *

In the afternoon I did the professional thing, the thing I'd driven three hundred miles to do: I interviewed a resident. Mrs. Aldous, seventy-eight, of the last-but-one occupied house, on the subject of harvest songs. She had a good voice, thin but true, and she gave me two verses of "The Barley Bride" before her mouth simply closed mid-line like a gate.

"That's all I know," she said, in the tone of a woman who knew more.

"You stopped in the middle of—"

"I stopped where I stopped." She smiled at me with tremendous kindness, the kindness you extend to a man walking toward a cliff edge who hasn't seen it yet. "You'll want to speak to Tam Vetch about the singing. He's the one with opinions."

I noted it down. I was aware, even then, of how the notebook had become a talisman — if it was written down, it was data, and data could not hurt me. Meanwhile the actual hurtful thing sat in my pocket on a memory card, lisping its two sentences, waiting.

Walking back, I passed the dark windows again — clean glass behind which no lamp ever burned, houses the village insisted were empty, though empty houses grow ivy and theirs did not — and I caught myself doing something new. I caught myself listening to the village the way I'd listened to the recording. Reading answers out of silences. *How long do you mean to stay?* the whole place seemed to be asking, politely, patiently, the way you ask a guest how long they're staying when you already know, when the answer was decided before he arrived, when the bed was made up years ago and the papers drawn and the ink drying in some drawer.

That evening I set the recorder again. Same table, same levels, same spoken slate: "Baseline recording, night two, Ivo Crane speaking." My voice sounded different to me saying it. Tighter. A man announcing himself to a room in the hope the room will announce back on schedule rather than improvising.

Before I switched off the kitchen light, I stood for a moment and said, clearly, to no one, to the air, to the microphone: "If you're going to talk, talk to me while I'm awake."

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. But the silence had a quality I recognized from interviews — the quality of a witness deciding whether to cooperate — and I went upstairs telling myself that the quality of a silence is not evidence, that I was a folklorist and folklorists collect songs, not voices, and that tomorrow I would call the university, or the solicitor, or anyone, and get a second opinion from a member of my own species.

I did none of those things. I want that on the record too. I checked the mic twice and called no one, because part of me — the part that had rented a house in a dying village sight unseen, the part that had spent seven years chasing folklore the way other men chase ex-lovers — wanted the voice to be real. Denial isn't the refusal to believe a thing. It's the arrangement of your life so that believing costs nothing yet. I had arranged mine beautifully.

At 3:26 a.m. — I checked the timestamp the next morning with the resignation of a man checking a lottery number he already knows — the recorder captured my own voice, thick with sleep, asking the kitchen a question.

It was not the question about how long I meant to stay.

It was: *"What do you need from me?"*

And the voice, dry and patient and close, close enough to have been lying beside me, said:

"Just the singing, Ivo. Just the singing, in time."
