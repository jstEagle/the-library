## 11. What Belonging Costs

The mending of my fence was the first thing I noticed, because I had not asked for it to be mended.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was becoming the one currency I still held. On the morning of my ninth day in Hollowmere I came out with my tea and found that the loose paling — the one that had swung on a rusted nail since before I arrived, the one I'd been meaning to fix and had instead begun to regard as a colleague — stood straight. New nail. Fresh wood, pale as an apology. No sawdust left behind, no footprints in the frost-soft ground, no note.

I stood there for a long time holding my tea like a man who has forgotten what cups are for.

The night before, I had recorded eleven hours of ambient sound. I had not yet played it back. Some part of me understood, even then, that playback was a door and that once you opened a door you could not pretend there was no room behind it. So I did what any reasonable folklorist does when his research material starts doing research back: I made more tea and told myself the fence was a kindness from a neighbor.

It was. That's the thing I keep circling. It *was* a kindness. That's what makes it so difficult to write about without my hands going cold.

* * *

The warmth began, as far as I can date it, on the evening I let a lie stand.

Mrs. Aldery — Winifred, though nobody in Hollowmere used her Christian name except the dead, and possibly not even them — came by with a covered dish. Shepherd's pie, still warm, the kind of warmth that means it was made for you specifically and not decanted from a larger batch of neighborly obligation. She set it on my kitchen table, looked around at my boxes and my recording equipment with the expression of a woman assessing whether a house is being lived in properly, and said:

"You'll have seen the maypole, then. In the square. Where your mother danced the year of the flood."

I had seen the square. There was no maypole. There was a rusted socket in the cobbles where something might once have stood, and a bench, and a great deal of nothing. There had also been no flood — the parish records I'd photographed in the county archive mentioned droughts, two fires, and an unusually bad winter in 1968, but no flood. And my mother had never set foot in Hollowmere until she drove me here last week, alive, complaining about the suspension on the lane.

Every fact in me lined up at the starting gate.

And then I looked at Winifred Aldery's face, and what I saw there stopped them all. It wasn't madness. It wasn't senility. It was hope — thin, worn, carefully maintained hope, the kind you see on people who have rehearsed a sentence for years waiting for someone to say it to. She wanted me to say *yes*. She wanted the maypole to have existed, wanted the flood, wanted my mother dancing, wanted some thread connecting this village to a world outside itself, and she wanted it so badly that correcting her felt less like scholarship and more like cruelty.

"Yes," I said. "Lovely."

Her whole face changed. I don't have a better word for it. It changed the way a house changes when someone turns the lights on in rooms that have been dark so long the bulbs need replacing anyway. She laughed — actually laughed — and touched my arm, and said, "I knew it. I *knew* you were one of ours."

She hummed on her way down the path. Four notes of something old, cut off before the fifth, the way they all do.

That night the shepherd's pie tasted like the best meal I'd had in years, which is its own kind of horror, if you think about it long enough. I try not to think about it long enough.

* * *

By the eleventh day, the village had organized itself around me with the quiet efficiency of ants who have received instructions.

A basket of eggs on the step, no note. My gutters cleaned — I only discovered this when rain fell in orderly ropes off the eaves instead of in the chaotic sheets I'd been enjoying. Someone had oiled every hinge in my house, including the upstairs doors I had never mentioned to anyone, including the sealed one at the end of the landing, which now opened — I checked — exactly as easily as all the others, though I still couldn't bring myself to walk through it. Old Tam Vetch appeared at my woodpile with his grandson's axe and split two days' worth of logs while telling me a story about a horse that had definitely never existed, and I listened, and I nodded, and when he said the horse had been called Duchess and had pulled the mail through the flood year, I said, "Duchess. Of course."

Tam looked at me with such naked gratitude that I had to turn away and pretend to inspect the log pile.

Here is what I understood, standing among the split wood: seven days ago these people had been polite the way furniture is polite. They answered questions. They gave me their songs up to the second-to-last verse and then developed sudden interests in their boots. The child who'd asked if I was "the new rememberer" had been the closest thing to warmth I'd received, and she'd been an anomaly, a crack in the glass.

Now they brought me food. Now they fixed things I hadn't broken. Now Mrs. Petchy down the lane waved every morning with her whole arm, and the Hollis brothers, who hadn't spoken a full sentence to me in a week, spent an afternoon rehanging my gate and refused payment with genuine offense.

And the difference — I tested this, God help me, I ran the experiment twice — was entirely conditional on my silence.

On the twelfth day, feeling the particular itch of a scholar who has noticed his own corruption and wants to at least document it honestly, I corrected someone. Deliberately. Mr. Hollis the elder mentioned the chapel bell that rang the hour "all through the flood," and I said, mildly, conversationally, "There's no bell in the ruin, is there? Just the stump of the tower."

The temperature of the conversation dropped four degrees. Not metaphorically — I would almost swear to it. Mr. Hollis finished his sentence, agreed with everything I said in the flattest possible tone, tipped his cap, and walked away, and the next morning there were no eggs, and the gate — I checked — had developed a squeak overnight, a small vindictive squeak, as if the village had reached into my house and un-oiled exactly one hinge as a reminder.

So I apologized. I sought him out and said I'd been mistaken, of course the bell rang, everyone said so, I must have misread the records. Records! As if records mattered. He brightened instantly, completely, forgave me with a laugh, and by evening there was a rabbit stew on my step.

That was the day I sat down with my notebook and wrote, in capital letters, at the top of a fresh page: **THEY ARE BUYING SOMETHING FROM ME AND I KNOW NOT WHAT.**

Then, underneath, smaller, because honesty is a habit that dies hard: **Or I am selling.**

* * *

Let me describe the texture of the belonging, because I want to get it right, and getting it right is apparently the only thing I'm still allowed to do.

It wasn't just the food and the repairs. It was the way conversations changed shape. Before, villagers spoke to me the way you speak to a meter reader — cooperative, minimal, watching the door. Now they lingered. They told me things. Not true things — almost nothing true — but *warm* things, detailed things, the shared furniture of a communal past: the flood, the maypole, the bell, the harvest of '61 that "everyone remembers" because the cider went wrong and half the village sang until dawn. They built the past in front of me like children building a blanket fort, and they wanted me inside it, and inside it was — I will not lie to you, whoever you are reading this — inside it was *lovely*.

I have been alone a long time. Seven years of archives and rented rooms and academic conferences where the only intimacy on offer is someone disagreeing with your footnote structure. I know what loneliness does to a person; I've read the literature; I've annotated it. None of the literature mentions how good it feels when a whole village decides you're theirs. When Mrs. Aldery calls you "love" and means it. When Tam claps your shoulder hard enough to stagger you and says, "You're a proper Hollowmere man now, whatever they say."

"Whatever who says?" I asked.

Tam's face did something complicated and closed, like a shop pulling down its shutters. "Nobody," he said. "Nobody says anything." And he cut off the fourth note of the song he'd been humming, and went home.

* * *

The fourth night of the warmth, I dreamed of my mother's kitchen.

I want to be careful here, because dreams are unreliable narrators and I am a folklorist, professionally committed to the position that most hauntings are memory wearing a costume. But this dream had details. The yellow of the cabinets — a specific institutional yellow, the yellow of 1980s council kitchens. The chip in the linoleum by the stove shaped, if you squinted, like Portugal. The smell of onions and toast. My mother at the counter with her back to me, humming, and in the dream I knew the song, knew it the way you know your own hands, and I woke with the melody complete in my head for the first time since childhood, all eight bars, note-perfect.

I lay in bed with my heart going like a trapped bird and thought: *the house grows rooms from remembered ones.* Except whose remembering? Mine was buried deep under twenty years of forgetting. Nobody living knew that kitchen. Nobody living could have —

I got up and wrote it down, because writing things down is how I hold the line between experience and evidence, and the line has been moving.

In the morning there was a loaf of bread on my step, still warm, and beside it a jar of honey, and beneath the jar a scrap of paper in a child's careful hand: *You sing nicer when you're happy.*

I stood on my step in the gray light holding that note and felt two things simultaneously, which I am learning is the native condition of Hollowmere residents. The first was a warmth so pure and human it nearly undid me — a child had noticed I was happier, and had acted on it, with bread. The second was the coldest thought I have yet had in this village: *they can tell.*

They can tell when I'm happy. They are monitoring my happiness the way a farmer monitors rainfall. My contentment is a crop. And it ripens fastest when I stop correcting them.

* * *

I did the arithmetic that evening, sitting in Edith Marlow's doorway — not inside, never quite inside, but in the threshold, which had become a kind of study for me, the one place in the house where the air felt honest.

Eleven names on the deed. Eleven signatories across sixty years. Edith Marlow, twelfth line, blank. And a village that had spent decades perfecting a single social technology: the manufacture of a past agreeable enough that anyone living in it would eventually prefer it to the truth. You don't need to force a scholar to sign. You just need to make him comfortable. Make him loved. Feed him, mend his fence, remember his mother dancing at a maypole that never stood, and wait. Belonging arrives priced in truth, and the price is collected slowly, in installments, so the buyer never feels the total.

I thought about Edith, thirty years alone in that wallpapered room, marking verses WRONG WRONG ALMOST in a shaking hand, refusing the shepherd's pies of her era, correcting everyone, being hated for it, staying hungry so the thing that fed on correct memory would stay hungry too.

I thought about how tired she must have been. How very, very tired.

And then — and this is the part I record with shame, because a folklorist should know better, because *I* should know better — I thought about how nice it would be to put the notebook down. To let the flood be real. To dance, in the village's telling, at a maypole under a bell that rang through water. To belong somewhere, anywhere, even if the somewhere was stitched together out of lies by something patient in the walls.

The recorder on the shelf beside me — I'd taken to keeping one running during these sessions, professional habit, or perhaps insurance — clicked softly. Not recording-anything click. More like a throat cleared.

I did not play the tape back that night either. But as I switched off the lamp, I heard it, faintly, from the direction of the sealed room: four notes of a tune, hummed low, stopping just before the fifth.

The same four notes Mrs. Aldery always stops at.

Except this voice kept going. Past the fifth note. Into the sixth, the seventh, the eighth — my mother's lullaby, complete, patient, note-perfect, ending on a little rising figure like a question.

Like it was asking whether I'd like to hear it again.

Whether I'd like to sing along.

* * *

In the morning I found the chapel key on my step, tied with green ribbon, with no note at all. An iron key, older than the parish records, heavier than it needed to be.

I turned it over twice. Behind me, the whole length of the lane, every window of every occupied house had someone standing at it, looking not at me but slightly above me, toward the ruin on the hill, as if forty invisible chairs had just been arranged somewhere and they were all waiting to see who would sit.

I put the key in my pocket, because I am a folklorist, and a key to a locked ruin is a primary source.

But I did not go up that day. And all through breakfast, the bread tasted faintly of ash, and the honey tasted like the smell of a room that has been closed for thirty years, and somewhere above the village — I would have sworn to this under oath — a bell that did not exist rang once, softly, like a host checking that dinner is nearly ready.
