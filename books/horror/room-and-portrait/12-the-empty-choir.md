## 12. The Empty Choir

The chapel at Hollowmere had no roof, no bell, and — as far as I could determine — no particular objection to any of this. It stood on the low rise east of the village green like an old man who has stopped explaining himself to strangers: walls of honeyed stone going soft at the corners, windows that were once arched and were now simply gaps with opinions about the weather. Ivy had taken the south face entirely. The north face had been left to the lichen, which was doing a more restrained job of it.

I'd been meaning to visit since my second day in the village. Every folklore survey worth its grant money includes the sacred architecture, and besides, I told myself, it would be useful. A chapel is where a community keeps its songs. That's practically the definition.

I want to be honest about what I was doing that morning, because honesty became a currency in Hollowmere and I spent mine slowly, then all at once. What I was actually doing was looking for something complete. Eleven days in that village and every song I'd collected came to me amputated — verses dropped mid-line, choruses abandoned like hot pans, old women who went silent so abruptly I checked their pulses. Tam Vetch had told me, with the gravity of a man passing on state secrets, that a song sung twice is an invitation, and the village had apparently decided that even once was pushing it. My archive was a museum of broken instruments. I wanted one whole thing. Just one.

So when Mrs. Aldous mentioned — in the way villagers mention things, sideways, while talking about something else entirely — that "the old place" still had its plaster, I put on my boots and went up the hill before she'd finished her sentence about the vicarage garden.

* * *

The path to the chapel was the kind of path that makes you complicit. It was worn into a shallow trench by centuries of feet, all of them presumably heading the same direction I was, and walking it felt less like exploring and more like joining a queue that had been standing for four hundred years. The grass either side grew tall and silvered with seed heads. Nothing else did. I noticed that later, and wished I hadn't: the verge was all grass, no wildflowers, as if the ground here had been asked the same question too many times and had stopped offering answers.

The door was long gone. I stepped through the empty frame into a nave full of light and bird droppings and the particular silence that ruins have — not absence of sound but presence of held breath.

And there were the chairs.

Forty of them. Wooden, plain-backed, the kind you'd find in any village hall in England, except these had been arranged not facing the altar — there was no altar, just a raised step and a window-shaped hole in the east wall — but facing the west wall. All forty of them. Rows of eight, five deep, squared up with a precision that made my skin prickle, because ruins don't do precision. Ruins slump. These chairs had been set straight, recently, deliberately, like an audience waiting for a performance that had been postponed rather than cancelled.

I walked down the aisle between them — because of course there was an aisle; the chairs left one, exactly wide enough for one person — and I touched the nearest backrest. The wood was worn smooth. Not weathered-smooth, not sun-bleached smooth. Worn the way a church pew gets worn, or a banister, or the handle of a tool used daily by generations of the same family. The varnish had been rubbed through to bare wood in two patches, shoulder-blade height, symmetrically placed.

Every chair. Forty chairs, each polished by backs, each bearing those two pale ovals where shoulders had leaned, year after year, decade after decade.

Generations of people had sat here, facing that blank wall, long enough and often enough to wear through varnish. And the chapel had lost its roof — I could see the sky through the rafters' ghosts, could see the rain stains running down the interior plaster like tear tracks — and yet the chairs had been kept straight. Kept occupied. Whatever congregation this was, they hadn't stopped coming when God left. They'd stopped needing Him.

There was dust on the floor, thick and grey, drifted against the walls. There was no dust on the chairs. I crouched and looked properly, the way you learn to look when your profession is noticing. No dust. In a roofless building, in a ruin that birds used as a latrine, forty wooden chairs sat clean as furniture in a showroom.

I stood up too fast and my knee cracked, and the sound went up into the open sky and didn't come back. No echo. I noticed that too. A stone box with no roof should give you echo the way a drum gives you noise. This room swallowed sound the way the village swallowed songs.

Then I looked at the wall they were all facing.

* * *

The west wall was the best-preserved surface in the building, which should have been my first clue, because everything else was peeling, stained, surrendering. This stretch of plaster — maybe twelve feet across, from floor to the height where the roofline would have been — was intact, pale, almost tender-looking. And it was covered in writing.

Not painted. Scratched. Scratched into the plaster with something fine and hard, thousands upon thousands of strokes, dense as engraving, covering every inch of that panel in lines of text that resolved, as I got closer, into words. Into verse. Into a song.

I have spent my life around old documents. I've read marginalia in books that survived plague and fire, inscriptions on graves of children, last letters home from wars. I know how to look at human marks on surfaces without flinching. But I flinched. I'll admit it plainly: I stood in that ruined chapel and flinched away from a wall, because the writing was wrong in a way I couldn't immediately name, and my body knew it before I did.

Here's what was wrong. The letters were uniform. Scratch-writing is never uniform — hands shake, tools slip, plaster crumbles unevenly. This was different. Every letter the same depth. Every line the same spacing. As if the words hadn't been carved by many hands over many years but had grown there, the way frost grows on glass, following rules. And yet — and this is the part I keep returning to — the edges of some strokes were worn smooth. Smoother than the plaster around them. People had traced these letters. With fingers. Repeatedly. For a very long time.

A song, scratched into a wall, that no one in the village would sing for me. Traced by fingertips until the grooves went soft.

I stepped closer and began to read.

It was a harvest song. Or it wore the clothes of one — the opening verses were familiar enough, cousins of a dozen songs I'd collected in three counties, the standard inventory: the reaping, the binding, the last sheaf, the feast. But the language darkened as it went, the way a path darkens underfoot when it turns from gravel to mud. By the fourth verse it wasn't about the field anymore. It was about keeping. About what must be kept and what must be given to be kept. There was a line — I wrote it down that night and I can still see it, though I've since done my best to mangle it beyond recovery — a line that went:

*What we remember, we hold. What we hold, holds us.*

And then the final verse, the one nobody in Hollowmere would finish for me, the one Tam Vetch begged me never to learn:

*Sing it once for the living, sing it twice for the dead,
sing it never for the Listener who stands behind your head.
Give Him all your yesterdays, He'll lend you all your tomorrows,
and the debt comes due at morning with the sun and all its sorrows.*

I read it twice before I caught myself.

I caught myself on the second reading — physically caught myself, hand rising to my own mouth like a man stopping a cough in church — because I realized I had been reading aloud. Under my breath, barely voicing it, but aloud. Once already. And my eyes were going back to the top of the final verse to run it again, and some animal part of me had understood, before the professional part caught up, that I was standing in front of the only complete version of the forbidden song in Hollowmere, and that I was one careless recitation away from singing it twice.

An invitation. Sung twice. In the one room built for inviting things in.

I backed away. Actually backed away, heel over toe, like leaving a dog's territory, until I bumped a chair and sat down hard in the third row because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me.

And here is the thing I have never told anyone, the thing I set down now only because setting it down wrongly is the only way I know to keep it: sitting in that chair, facing that wall, I felt — for perhaps three seconds — completely, catastrophically at home. Warmth spread through my chest like brandy. The light through the empty windows turned gold. Somewhere behind my sternum a voice that was not quite a voice said, with enormous tenderness: *there you are. We kept your seat.*

Three seconds. Then it passed, the way a fever passes, leaving me shaking and cold and furious at myself. But those three seconds were real, and they were the most dangerous thing that happened to me in Hollowmere, more dangerous than the voice on the tapes, because the tapes had frightened me and this had *comforted* me. For three seconds I understood why forty generations of people had come up this hill and sat in these chairs and faced this wall and traced these letters with their fingertips until the plaster went soft.

Belonging. On loan. At interest.

* * *

I gave myself ten minutes to recover my dignity, then did what I always do when frightened: I worked. I photographed the wall — the images came out strange, overexposed in the centre as if the plaster itself were emitting light, but legible. I measured the chair rows. I counted the wear patterns again and confirmed what I'd suspected: the middle rows were worn deepest. Rows three and four. The seats of honour, or the seats of habit. The people who came most, sat closest.

And then, because I am thorough even when terrified, I examined the rest of the walls, and found the smaller things.

Names. Scratched small, near the floor, in the corner where the west wall met the north — dozens of them, hundreds, in hands that varied wildly, some careful, some childish, some barely legible. Dates beside some of them. 1887. 1903. 1941. 1968 — that date appeared eleven times, crowded together, as if a whole family had knelt there in the same season. And beneath the names, always, the same two words, repeated like a refrain:

*remembered correctly*

Not a prayer. Not a hope. A specification. These weren't memorials — there were no death dates, no *in loving memory*. They were claims. Each name was a declaration: this person is remembered, and remembered right, and therefore held. A ledger written into the fabric of the building. The chapel wasn't a church at all. It was a strongroom, and the deposit slips were scratched into the walls.

I found Edith Marlow's name at the end of the 1968 cluster. Small, precise, pressed deep into the plaster by someone pressing hard enough to hurt her finger. And beneath it, unlike every other name, no *remembered correctly*. Instead, scratched so finely I had to put my nose nearly to the wall to read it:

*not signed*

Two words. The only defiance in a wall full of compliance. I put my fingertip in the groove of the *n* and held it there, the way generations had held their fingers in the song, and I thought: you sat in one of these chairs, Edith. You faced this wall. You learned this whole song — you must have, you annotated every verse of it upstairs in your sealed room, WRONG, WRONG, ALMOST — and you refused to give it back to them whole. You are the reason the wall hums when I read aloud. You are the crack in the strongroom door.

Something moved behind me.

Not a sound — the room ate sounds. A pressure. The specific pressure of attention, of being looked at by a room. I turned and the forty chairs were exactly as they had been, the aisle exactly as wide, the light exactly as gold and sourceless. But the warmth was back, faintly, at the edge of perception, like a fire lit in a distant room of a large house. And the air in the chapel had acquired a quality I recognized from my own hallway at night, from the recordings, from the space just behind my left shoulder where the breathing lived: the quality of being awaited.

Patiently. Without hurry. The way furniture waits.

I gathered my bag. I walked up the aisle — the aisle, the one path, because the chairs permitted nothing else — and out through the empty doorway into the ordinary grey afternoon, and the relief of the open hillside was so violent it was nearly vertigo. Behind me the ruin sat in its silence with its clean chairs and its soft-worn letters, holding its congregation's place.

Halfway down the path I stopped, because a thought arrived with the force of a physical blow, and I want to record it exactly as it arrived, unpolished, the way it hit me:

*They don't face the altar because there isn't one. They face the wall because the wall is the altar. The song isn't written on the wall. The wall is written in the song. And every Sunday — every year, every generation — they came here and sang it once, and traced it with their fingers, and went home alive, and the thing behind the plaster counted.*

One song. Complete. The only one in the village. Kept in a locked room of stone, in a building with no roof, guarded by nothing but the village's terror of ever letting it reach the air twice.

And I had photographs of it in my bag. Every verse. Every word. Digitized, portable, ready to be played back — played *twice*, played forty times, played at three in the morning in a quiet house with good speakers —

I stood on the hillside with the wind flattening the grass and understood, with total clarity, that I was carrying the most dangerous object in Hollowmere home to the house that answered doors, and that some part of me — the part that had felt warm in the third row — had known exactly what I was doing when I raised the camera.

That night I set the recorders running, as I had every night, out of habit, out of science, out of the stubborn belief that a machine between me and the dark made the dark manageable.

At 3 a.m., the tape caught me talking in my sleep.

But I didn't know that yet. That night, I only knew that I dreamed of sitting in a warm room, in a comfortable chair, facing a beautiful wall, while someone with my mother's voice read me a song I already knew by heart.

And in the dream, I sang along.
