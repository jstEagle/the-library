## 17. Neighbors at the Door

I had learned to sleep with the recorder running the way other men learn to sleep with a gun under the pillow — not because it protects you, but because afterward you can prove what happened to you.

So when I woke at midnight to a sound like weather made of people, my first instinct was professional: check the levels. My second instinct was human, and it told me to stay in bed and pretend the house was small again, the way it had been when I arrived, before I'd started measuring the hallway twice a day like a man taking his own pulse.

The sound was coming from outside. From the lane. Not shouting — nothing so useful as shouting. It was the sound of many people standing still. If you've never heard forty-odd villagers assembled in silence at midnight, I can't describe it for you, and if you have, I'm sorry, and we should probably compare notes over something strong.

I went to the window with the lamp off.

They filled the lane from the gate to the bend where Tam Vetch's cottage sat. Every one of them. The Hollises from the farm with the collapsed barn. Mrs. Pargeter, who hadn't spoken to me since I'd sung her husband's song wrong on purpose and she'd known, somehow, that I'd done it on purpose. The solicitor, Mr. Quill, standing slightly forward of the others the way a pen stands slightly forward of a hand. And down at the front, small and solemn as a chess piece, the child — the one who'd asked if I was the new rememberer — holding what looked like a candle, though no flame moved in it.

They were all smiling.

That was the worst of it. Not torches, not masks, not the pitchfork choreography you get in films. Smiles. Forty faces tilted up toward my dark window, each wearing the expression of someone greeting a relative off a train, and every smile identical in its patience, as if they'd all been issued from the same drawer.

I counted to thirty, which is how long it takes a rational man to exhaust his explanations. Sleepwalking: no, they were too arranged. A village festival: no, festivals make noise, and this was silence with edges. A dream: I pinched the skin of my wrist hard enough to leave a mark I'd find in the morning, blue as ink, evidence filed.

Then Mrs. Pargeter raised her voice — warm, unhurried, a woman calling across a garden fence — and said:

"Mr. Crane. We've come to ask a favor."

* * *

I did not open the window. I want that on the record, wherever records go after they're burned. I stood back from the glass like a man avoiding a mirror he doesn't trust, and I answered through the wall, which felt absurd and also like the only sane act available.

"What favor?"

"A song," she said. "Just the one. Just once more." A ripple went through the crowd — not movement exactly, more like the way water acknowledges a dropped stone. "It's been so long since anyone sang for us properly. Since Edith, really. And tomorrow's the anniversary, and it would mean such a lot."

"Just once," said Mr. Hollis, and his voice had the flat comfort of a hymn.

"Once," agreed someone else, and then another, and then the word came back along the lane like an echo that had gotten organized: *once, once, once*, until it sounded less like a number than a promise being rehearsed.

And there it was. The whole trap folded up neat as a solicitor's letter and handed to me with a bow. Once more, they said, meaning twice. Because a song sung twice is an invitation — old Tam had told me that in the Black Lamb with beer shaking in his glass and terror coming off him like heat off a road — and whatever they were, whatever had them by the memory, they needed *me* to sing it. They couldn't do it themselves. That was the thing about this village that I'd been circling for days without landing on: none of them ever finished a verse. They hummed. They began. They stopped mid-line like clocks with the pendulum removed. The singing had to come from outside, from someone unsigned, someone whose voice still belonged to him.

Which meant the entire smiling congregation in my lane was not a mob.

It was a key, and I was the lock.

"I'm tired," I called down. "It's midnight. Come back some other—"

"It has to be tonight," Mrs. Pargeter said, still warm, still patient, and for just half a second the warmth thinned and I heard underneath it something dry and vast and familiar — the second voice, wearing her throat like a borrowed coat. Then it sealed over again and she was only a lonely widow asking a kindness. "You understand, don't you, Mr. Crane? You're a folklorist. You know what songs are *for*."

I knew what songs were for. I'd built a career on knowing. Songs are for carrying things — names, griefs, instructions, warnings — across distances that paper can't survive. What I was only now learning, at one in the morning in my dressing gown, was that some songs are also for carrying *things*. And that the thing under Hollowmere had spent sixty years waiting for someone to carry it a delivery.

"I'll think about it," I said, which is what cowards say and what survivors say, and sometimes those are the same sentence.

"That's all we ask," said forty voices, gently, together.

* * *

I shut the window. I latched it, which was theater — I'd seen what came through walls in this house — but theater keeps the hands busy while the mind works. I stood in my bedroom listening to the lane.

They didn't move.

That's the detail I keep returning to, the one I'd give back every other memory of this place to unhear. They simply stayed. No shuffling, no murmuring, no cigarette ends winking out one by one. They stood in the lane in the cold — and it was cold; frost had begun feathering the glass, though the forecast, I'd checked, said mild — and they waited with the perfect contentment of furniture. A chair does not sigh. A sideboard does not check its watch. And forty neighbors do not, under any ordinary circumstance, stand motionless in a frozen lane for hours, smiling at a curtained window.

At half past one I made tea in the kitchen because I couldn't bear the bedroom anymore, and the kettle's noise seemed almost obscene against that silence, and I caught myself moving slowly, quietly, like a burglar in my own house, ashamed of disturbing them.

At two, Mrs. Pargeter spoke again. Not louder. She didn't need to be; the night carried her voice up to the glass the way water carries a leaf.

"We don't blame you, you know. For the wrongness. We know you've been learning it from Edith's papers." A pause, fond and terrible. "She got so tired near the end. All those years changing the words. You mustn't follow her example, dear. It's such a heavy way to live, always correcting. Much easier just to sing it true."

There it was — the offer inside the request inside the siege. They weren't only asking for a song. They were offering me relief. Put down the manuscript, Ivo. Stop annotating. Sing it straight, sing it once, and then — the word floated up unbidden from somewhere below the village, below the soil — *belong*.

I thought about Tam Vetch, dying by inches in his cottage with sixty years of guilt packed around him like insulation. I thought about the reel-to-reel behind Edith's wallpaper, her voice thin as wire: *it owns what is remembered correctly. Stay unsigned. Keep it hungry.* I thought about my mother's lullaby, which I could no longer hear even inside my own head — a hole in my memory shaped exactly like her voice, and the thing downstairs had taken it as payment, casually, the way a landlord takes a deposit.

"No," I said to the kitchen. Quietly. But the house has good ears, and I suspect the lane does too.

The smiles outside did not falter. I checked. I went back up to the window at three, and they were all still there, still tilted toward me, and the frost had climbed the lane itself now, silvering the hedges, and not one of them was shivering.

* * *

Somewhere around four I stopped watching and started working, because work is what I do instead of screaming. I sat at the kitchen table with Edith's manuscript and I read her marginalia by lamplight — WRONG, WRONG, ALMOST, WRONG — and I practiced, silently, mouthing the words, mangling her mangles further. If they wanted a song from me, they would get one, eventually, on my terms, broken past recognition. But that was for sunrise. That was the plan forming at the edge of me like frost at the edge of a pane. First I had to survive the night, and the night's weapon was not violence.

It was kindness. It was the slow, patient application of belonging, and I will tell you honestly, sitting alone in that kitchen with forty people loving me through a door, that it nearly worked. There is a specific loneliness to being the only person in a valley who remembers anything straight, and it hollows you in shapes that a warm voice fits into perfectly. When Mrs. Pargeter said *you mustn't follow her example*, some traitor chamber of my heart whispered: but look how tired you are. Look how easy it would be.

I put the kettle on again. Ritual against ruin.

At five, the child's voice came up through the frost, small and clear, cutting through the collective warmth like a pin through satin.

"You don't have to," she said.

The lane went very still — stiller than still, if that's a thing, and in Hollowmere it turned out to be a thing. The other smiles held, but something behind them shifted, the way a congregation shifts when a stranger speaks during the sermon.

"You don't have to sing," the child repeated. "He can't make you. He can only *ask*." And then, quieter, in a tone I'd swear was meant for me alone, threaded up through the glass and the frost and the forty bodies between us: "Don't sign. Don't sing it right. Please."

Someone — Mr. Quill, the solicitor, his bureaucratic calm finally showing a seam — said, "Mara, dear, go stand with your mother," and the child said, "I am standing with my mother," and I looked, and there was indeed a woman beside her, smiling, and the child did not take her hand.

Then the lane resumed its silence, and I understood that I had just watched the smallest crack run through the largest wall, and that both sides of it were now aware of me as a participant rather than a prize.

* * *

Dawn came gray, the way it does here, like a decision made reluctantly. By the time the light was strong enough to show colors, the lane was empty. No footprints in the frost — I went out and checked, barefoot on my own step, breath smoking, and the frost lay unbroken from my gate to the bend, pristine as a page nobody had written on. As if forty people had stood on it all night without weighing anything at all.

On my doorstep, someone had left a loaf of bread, still warm. Beside it, a jar of honey, and a note in Mrs. Pargeter's round hand:

*No hard feelings, dear. We'll call again this evening. Do think about the song. It's only once.*

I picked up the loaf. It was warm as a living thing. I carried it inside, set it on the table next to Edith's manuscript and the unsigned deed, and stood looking at the three of them together — bread, battle plan, contract — the whole of my situation arranged on one surface like a still life titled *Last Week*.

Then I noticed the fourth object.

A cassette tape, unlabeled, sitting against the honey jar where it definitely had not been when I brought the loaf in. Old-style casing, the plastic gone amber with age, and on the back, in a shaking hand I knew better now than my own —

Edith's.

One word, underlined twice:

*ALMOST.*

I looked out the window at the empty, unmarked lane, at the village pretending to be asleep, at the sun climbing like a witness arriving late, and I thought: they've stopped asking nicely. Whatever comes next won't come through the door.

It will come through the wallpaper.

I put the tape in the player and pressed down.
