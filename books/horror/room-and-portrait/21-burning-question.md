## 21. Burning Question

I want to be clear about something before I tell you what I did, because I've noticed that when people describe the moment they finally lost their temper at the impossible, they tend to dress it up as a decision. It was not a decision. It was three in the afternoon, I had slept four hours in two nights, and I had spent the morning listening to a voice on my own equipment explain, in the tone of a man reading a shopping list, that my mother had liked to hum while she peeled apples, and that I had inherited the habit without knowing it.

That was the sentence that did it. Not the breathing. Not the questions I never asked. The apple-peeling.

I took the recordings out of the archive boxes — all of them, the cassettes, the minidiscs, the DAT tapes, the little dictaphone memory cards I'd transferred to labeled plastic sleeves like a good archivist, like a man who believed in metadata — and I carried them into the back garden, and I built a fire.

Not a ritual fire. A fire like a normal angry man builds. I dragged the old galvanized bin from behind the shed, tipped in the kindling that had been sitting there since before Edith Marlow's time, probably, doused the whole business in a quarter bottle of paraffin I found under the sink, and fed it tape after tape after tape. I did it with the particular satisfaction of a man who has finally found a problem that responds to fire. There is a reason the phrase exists. *Solved with fire.* Some problems deserve it.

The first cassettes went up beautifully. I stood there with my sleeves rolled and my hands smelling of paraffin and watched the plastic blister and curl, and I said — out loud, to the garden, to the village that wouldn't look at me — "There. That's the archive gone. That's your evidence gone. Burn the lot of you."

The fire took the kindling. It took the bin's wooden lid, which I'd balanced on the edge. It took a stack of old newspapers I'd thrown in for ballast, and the paraffin tin itself, and eventually — this took some doing — the galvanized bin's plastic handle, which melted and dripped and stank.

It did not take the recordings.

I want to describe this precisely, because precision was the only weapon I had left and I was about to learn it wasn't a weapon at all. The tapes did not burn. They did not *refuse* to burn, either, which would have been dramatic and almost forgivable. They simply came out the other side of the fire. I'd watch a cassette go in — a proper one, the October baseline, the night of the breathing — and the flame would lap over it, and the plastic would go soft and shine, and then the fire would move around it. Around it. The way water moves around a stone in a stream. The cassette would sit in the heart of the flames, glowing faintly, unharmed, while everything it touched charred to nothing, and after a while I'd fish it out with a stick and it would be cool. Cool. In a fire that had melted the handle off a metal bin.

I burned eleven tapes that afternoon. Eleven came out.

By the fourth one I had stopped swearing, which is how you know a man has moved past anger into something worse. By the seventh I was sitting on the wet grass with my back against the shed, feeding tapes into the bin with the weary diligence of a man doing a job he knows is pointless because the doing of it is the only thing keeping him upright. The fire didn't care. The fire was, if anything, *helpful*. It burned the labels. It burned the plastic sleeves. It burned the paper inventory sheets I'd spent a week typing. Every trace that the recordings had ever been catalogued, organized, understood — gone. The recordings themselves sat in the ashes like eggs in a nest, blackened at the corners, pristine within.

The DAT tapes were the worst. They're small, you know. Delicate things. You'd think fire would love a DAT. I put the whole sleeve in — the one with the second voice answering questions I hadn't asked, the one with my sleeping self negotiating for *one more year* in a voice that was mine the way a mask is a face — and the fire bent around it politely, the way a crowd bends around a man carrying something breakable.

I sat in the smoke and laughed. It came out of me in one ugly bark and I couldn't stop it for a good while, and the garden didn't mind, and the house at my back didn't mind, and the dark windows of the neighboring houses — the clean glass behind which nobody lived, behind which nobody had lived for thirty years — didn't mind either.

"You can't have them," I said, when I'd got my breath back. My voice sounded scraped. "You hear me? They're mine. I made them. They're my work and they're my property and they are *mine*."

The garden said nothing. The fire popped. Somewhere over the wall a bird made a noise that I chose, for my own sanity, to believe was a bird.

And then, from behind me — from the direction of the house, but not from any room in particular, the way the voice always came, as if the house itself were a speaker cabinet — the voice said:

"They were never yours."

I didn't jump. I'd like to report that I didn't jump, and for once the record supports me. I was too tired to jump. I turned around slowly, and there was nothing there, obviously, just the kitchen door standing slightly ajar in the way every door in that house stood slightly ajar, as if the house were permanently about to say something.

"They're my recordings," I said. "I set the machines. I pressed record."

"You did," the voice agreed, and it sounded — and this is the part I still can't write down without my jaw tightening — it sounded *fond*. Patient, dry, amused, the voice of a man watching a child try to return a gift. "You pressed record very well. Better than the last one. She was all thumbs. But Ivo — may I call you Ivo? I notice you flinch when I do — you have a mistaken idea about ownership. You think a thing belongs to the one who makes it. In the village where I do my business, a thing belongs to the one who remembers it correctly."

The fire crackled. The tapes sat in the ashes. Eleven of them, and the DATs, and the minidiscs, all of it unburnt, all of it *kept*, and I understood then — properly understood, in the stomach, not the head — that I hadn't been destroying an archive. I'd been tidying it. The fire was a courtesy. The fire was the house letting me feel useful.

"Then explain it," I said. I meant it as a challenge. It came out as a request, and I heard it come out as a request, and I hated myself for it. "Explain why they won't burn. Go on. You love explaining things. You've been dying to explain things to me since the night I arrived."

A pause. The voice does pauses the way other people do applause — it knows exactly how long to hold them.

"I could explain it," it said. "It's not complicated. You'd find it almost disappointing. It would take perhaps a minute, and you would understand your situation completely, and you would never again waste an afternoon on fire."

"Then tell me."

"It's the one thing I can't give away."

I stood up. My knees cracked. The smoke was getting into my eyes and I told myself that was why they were watering. "Everything else you give away. My mother's lullabies. My childhood kitchen. Things I haven't thought about in twenty years — you hand them to me free, wrapped, gift-wrapped, like you're *courting* me. But the one thing I actually want to know costs money."

"Everything I know about you was given to me," the voice said. "That's the difference. I deal in what's given. I can return a gift, Ivo, but I can't make one. And an explanation of my own nature — well." A sound that was almost a chuckle, dry as paper. "That's the one item in the shop that isn't for sale. It's only ever traded."

"For what?"

"You know the currency."

I looked at the fire. I looked at the tapes. I thought about what the currency was, because I did know, and I'd known for days, and every hour I spent not knowing it out loud was an hour I could pretend the transaction hadn't already begun.

"A memory," I said. "One true memory."

"One true memory," it agreed, "freely given. Not taken. Not lost. *Given.* There's a difference, and the difference is the whole contract, and I'm being more generous than I have been with any resident of this parish since 1968, and I would thank you to remember that."

I crouched by the bin and picked up one of the cassettes. The October baseline. The breathing that wasn't mine, paced to mine, patient as tide. It was warm now — the first warmth any of them had taken from the fire — and I held it and thought about throwing it anyway, into the hedge, over the wall, into the well at the bottom of the garden if there was a well, and I knew with absolute certainty that it would come back. It would come back the way the hallway came back longer at night. It would come back and it would be *slightly more itself* each time, the way a thing gets more itself the more it's remembered.

"Why this one?" I asked. "Why won't this one burn? What's on it that you're keeping?"

"You know what's on it."

"Say it anyway."

A long pause. The fire had burned down to a sullen orange and the smoke was going flat and gray, and the light was starting to go out of the afternoon, and I realized I'd spent four hours in this garden arguing with the air.

"Your voice is on it," the voice said. "Asleep. Agreeing. That's a memory I hold in trust, Ivo, not one I own — you made it, even if you don't remember making it. And things held in trust don't burn. They only transfer." The almost-chuckle again. "You've been trying to burn your own signature. I did enjoy watching, though. You've got your mother's temper. She once threw an entire radio into the sea at Whitstable because it played a song she didn't like."

"I don't remember that."

"No," it said, gently, horribly. "You wouldn't yet. That's the sort of thing I keep for special occasions."

I put the cassette in my pocket. I don't know why. It seemed important, in the moment, not to leave it in the ashes — to carry at least one piece of the evidence on my person, as if I were still a man who might one day hand evidence to someone. There was no one. I knew there was no one. The village had stopped leaving meals at my door a week ago and the word SINGER had been on my fence in white paint for three days, and the solicitor's letter about forfeiture sat unopened on the hall table where I could see it through the kitchen window, a thin white rectangle, patient as everything else here.

"I'm not giving you a memory," I said.

"Of course not. Not today." The voice was already receding — it receded the way the hallway shortened, not by moving but by becoming less there. "You'll want to think about which one, though. I'd start now, if I were you. It's easier to choose when the choice is hypothetical. By the time it's not hypothetical, you'll find you've already made it."

The kitchen door swung — not shut, never shut, just *closed*, the way doors close in that house, to within an inch, like an eye mostly closed.

I stood in the garden until the fire went out on its own, which it did quickly once there was nothing left it was permitted to take. Then I gathered the eleven tapes and the DATs out of the cold ashes, every one of them cool as river stones, and I carried them inside, and I put them back in their sleeves, and — God help me — I put them back in alphabetical order, because my hands needed to do something and that was the something they knew.

It was while I was reshelving, kneeling on the floor of the front room with the boxes around me, that I noticed the smell. Smoke, yes, that was mine, that was in my clothes. But under the smoke, something else. Something from the kitchen, drifting through the mostly-closed door. Apples. Butter. Sugar going amber in a pan.

Someone was peeling apples in my kitchen. Someone was humming while they did it.

And the hum — I stood very still in the front room with a cassette in each hand and let it reach me — the hum was a tune I knew. A tune I had known my whole life and had lost a week ago, taken as payment for a refusal, gone from my memory as clean as if it had never been. It was my mother's lullaby. Note-perfect. Being hummed by something in my kitchen, in my house, in a village where nobody had cooked a meal in thirty years.

The voice had said it couldn't make gifts. It could only return them.

It was returning one.

I stood in the doorway and watched the kitchen — the wrong kitchen, my mother's kitchen grown into the corner of a house in Hollowmere, the window showing a garden that wasn't this garden — and on the table, beside a bowl of peeled apples browning gently in the evening light, sat the deed, and the pen, uncapped.

* * *

I didn't sign. I want that on the record too, since the record is apparently the only thing in this village that keeps itself. I stood in the doorway for I don't know how long — long enough for the light in the wrong window to change, long enough for the humming to work its way through the whole song and start over, patient as tide — and then I did the only thing I could think of that wasn't signing and wasn't running, because there was nowhere to run to and we both knew it.

I said: "That's not how it goes."

The humming stopped.

"You've got it wrong," I said, and my voice shook, and I leaned into the shake, I *used* it. "The third line. You're humming the third line where the second should be. My mother never got it wrong. Not once. So either you're not her, or you don't know her as well as you think, or—" and here I made the leap, the one I'd been circling for days without letting myself land on it, the one Edith Marlow had spent thirty years circling from the other side, "—or you can't. Or you can only ever give back what was remembered *correctly*, and the moment I remember it wrong, it stops being yours."

The kitchen was very quiet. The apples sat in their bowl, going brown at a speed I chose not to watch.

"You've been reading her notes," the voice said at last, and the fondness was gone, and what replaced it was worse: interest. "I did wonder when you'd get there. She nearly managed it, you know. Nearly. Would you like to know how close she came? I could tell you. It's a wonderful story. It ends in that room at the top of the stairs."

"Costs a memory, does it?"

"Everything true costs," the voice said, almost gently. "That's the first lesson. You've just taught yourself the second."

The kitchen dimmed — not the light, the *kitchen*, the whole borrowed room going out like a lamp — and when my eyes adjusted I was looking at my own kitchen again, the honest one, the small one, the one with the damp in the corner and the solicitor's letter on the table and nothing cooking and nobody humming. The deed was gone from where it had lain. I checked the hall table. It was back there, unopened, patient.

The tapes sat in their boxes at my feet. Unburnt. Unburnable. Kept.

I sat down on the floor among them, with my back against the shelf, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding. The fire wouldn't take them because they held things remembered correctly, and correctly-remembered things were in trust, and trust only transferred. Edith had nearly found the way to make a memory belong to no one. Nearly. Her room at the top of the stairs was wallpapered with *WRONG, WRONG, ALMOST*, thirty years of almost, and she had died unsigned and the thing was still here and still hungry, which meant *almost* hadn't been enough.

But she'd left a tape. Tam had said so, in his drunk and terrified way, years ago — *she recorded everything, that woman, recorded her own funeral before it happened* — and if Edith recorded everything, then somewhere in this house, behind that wallpaper, in that room that hummed when I read aloud, there was a reel of tape with the last thirty years of *almost* on it. The difference between wrong and almost. The difference between her failure and my chance.

I looked up the staircase, into the dark at the top of it, where the door of her room stood — of course — slightly ajar.

The voice spoke one more time that night, from nowhere, from everywhere, quiet as dust settling, and it didn't sound amused anymore.

"You'll want the tape," it said. "Go and look for it, then. I'll wait. I'm very good at waiting. I've had the practice."
