## 27. The Child Who Asked

She came at the hour when the light goes thin and dishonest, the hour I had privately begun to think of as *the house's favorite*, and she did not knock. She was simply there in my kitchen doorway, small and composed, like a parcel someone had left without a note.

I knew her, of course. She was the one from my second week — the child on the lane who had asked if I was "the new rememberer," a question I'd filed under village eccentricity and tried very hard to forget, which by then I understood was not a thing you could do in Hollowmere on purpose. Forgetting here happened to you. It was never a service you could order.

"You shouldn't be out after—" I began, and stopped, because I didn't actually know what the rule was. Curfew? No one had said curfew. But every window on the lane had gone dark at dusk for three weeks running, dark in that particular way where you could feel people behind the glass, watching, waiting for something to be over.

"I know," she said. "That's why it's now."

Her name, she told me, was Wren Halloway. She was nine. She wore a coat too big for her, sleeves rolled four times, and she stood with her hands clasped behind her back in the manner of a child reciting something rehearsed, which, it turned out, she was.

"Are your parents—"

"They think I'm at Netty Sowerby's." A pause. "Netty thinks I'm home. Nobody checks. Nobody's checked anything since before my mum was born."

"Then how did you get here?"

"Walking," she said, as if I were simple. "The lane doesn't mind me. It minds *you*."

I should have sent her home. I want that on the record, wherever records go when they're blank: my first instinct, genuine and immediate, was to walk her back down the lane myself, knock on whatever door produced her, and hand her over to whatever passed for parenting in a village where all the dying had stopped in 1968. But the lane behind her had gone the color of dishwater, and the house behind me had gone quiet in the way it did lately — attentive, holding its breath — and I understood that sending her out into that would not be protection. It would be delivery.

So I made her tea, because I am English and it is the only liturgy I know, and she sat at my kitchen table with both hands around the cup and told me why she'd come.

* * *

"I'm already signed," she said.

I set down my own cup. It made more noise than I expected. "What does that mean?"

"It means my name's on the papers. All of ours are. We're born onto them." She said it the way children say things that are true and terrible — flatly, without the adult machinery of euphemism. "Mum says it's just how it is, like being born English or born left-handed. You don't remember agreeing to it because you hadn't been born yet, but the agreement was made *for* you, see. In sixty-eight. Before Gran was even married."

"Your name is on a document," I said slowly, "that you've never seen, signed by people dead before you existed."

"And their parents, and theirs." She blew on her tea, inexpertly, so that it rippled. "It's collateral. That's what Tam calls it. Collateral added to the original deed. The village borrowed something big in sixty-eight and we're what got put up against it. Every baby born after is another thing in the pot." She looked up. "You've met Tam. He cries when he talks about us. He tries to hide it but he's bad at it. He's bad at most things except knowing."

Tam Vetch, who was dying by inches in a house the size of a matchbox, who had begged me not to learn the last verse of any song, who had confessed — though I didn't yet know the full shape of that confession; that came later, and it rearranged everything — Tam had apparently been educating the children of Hollowmere in the terms of their own captivity. Or one child, anyway. One child who listened.

"What happens when you're collected?" I asked. "Do you know?"

"Nobody knows exactly." She turned the cup a quarter-turn, then another, some private ritual. "People get old here but they don't get *done*. They just keep going until the thing decides it wants what they've got. Then they go somewhere. Mum says moved away. Everyone says moved away." Her voice did something complicated on the word *everyone* — a nine-year-old performing an adult's lie, and knowing she was performing it, and doing it anyway because that was the local dialect. "But the children are different. Children aren't collected for keeping. Children are collected for *renewing*. That's what Tam says. We're how the loan stays good. When one of us turns old enough to hold a whole memory properly — to remember things right, all the way through, beginning to end — that's when we're useful. That's when we're called on."

"How old is old enough?"

"Nobody's ever found out," she said, "because nobody's ever broken it first."

And there it was. The moral weight of this entire rotten arrangement, which I had been carrying around in the abstract — eleven names on a deed, a village of the un-dying, Edith Marlow's thirty years of WRONG WRONG ALMOST — suddenly condensed into a child of nine with rolled-up sleeves, sitting at my table, drinking tea she was too young to make for herself.

She wanted me to break the contract. Before she was old enough to be collected. That was the errand. That was why the lane didn't mind her.

* * *

"Why me?" I asked. "Why not Tam? He knows more than I do. Why not your parents?"

"Tam's dying and he can't leave his chair, and even if he could—" She hesitated, and for the first time she looked nine instead of ninety. "Even if he could, he's *in* it. He signed. Everyone over sixteen signed, or was signed. Their word's no good. It's like asking a debtor to tear up the ledger — the ledger owns him too."

"And your parents?"

"Mum would stop me if she knew I was here." Flat again. "Not because she doesn't love me. Because she loves me and she's frightened, and frightened people here do what keeps things calm. That's the whole village, mister Crane. Everybody being calm at each other for fifty years so nothing gets hungry." She set the cup down with great care. "But you're not from here. You're not on the papers. You only got *asked*. And you haven't said yes yet. You're the only person in Hollowmere who can still say no."

"The solicitor would disagree. He seems to feel my signature is a formality."

"He would." Something flickered across her face — distaste, or fear, hard to say. "He's not really a solicitor. Did you know that? There hasn't been a real solicitor in Hollowmere since the seventies. He's just what the paperwork wears when it wants to walk around."

I thought about that longer than I wanted to. The man with the briefcase and the damp handshake, the appointment confirmed for sunrise, the pen uncapped on my desk like a small black promise. *What the paperwork wears.* Of course. Of course it was. Nothing in this village was a person doing a job; everything was the arrangement wearing a costume, patient as furniture, and I had been having cups of tea with the furniture for weeks.

"Why come to me now?" I asked. "Why tonight?"

"Because it's nearly time." She said it simply. "The frost started in your front room. The clocks are losing their minute. The village is practicing its welcome — I've heard them, through walls, saying hello to you over and over till it comes out smooth. Sunrise after next, the papers come due, and if you sign, then there's nobody left to break it. Ever. Because everyone after you will be born inside it, same as me, and none of them will even know there's an outside to compare it to." She looked at me with those level, unhurried eyes. "You're the last person who'll ever get the choice. That's why I waited till you were almost out of time. You wouldn't have believed me sooner."

She was right about that, and I hated her a little for it, and admired her a lot, and the two feelings sat in my chest like stones in the same pocket.

* * *

Here is what I did not tell her, sitting across that table while the house listened through its walls: that I had spent the previous night listening to myself negotiate in my sleep, in a voice not quite mine, haggling for *one more year* like a man at a market stall selling off his own future. That the voice in the recordings had sung my mother's lullaby note-perfect and then taken the melody out of my head as payment when I refused to sing it back, so that I now remembered the lullaby the way you remember a room you lived in as a baby — warm, important, entirely gone. That I had woken standing in Edith Marlow's sealed room with fresh annotations in my own handwriting on wallpaper written by a dead woman's shaking hand, and that my handwriting had said, in the corner, in letters I did not remember making: *ALMOST.*

That I was, by any honest measure, half-claimed already, and possibly the worst possible candidate for the job she was hiring for.

Instead I asked her the question I should have asked on the lane in my second week, the question whose answer I had been circling for a month like a moth around a bulb:

"Wren. What exactly happens if I sing a song wrong? On purpose. All the way through. What did Edith almost do?"

And this child, nine years old, born into debt, raised in a village of the calmly damned, leaned forward over her tea and gave me the answer that Edith Marlow had spent thirty years failing to write on her walls:

"Then it isn't theirs anymore. A song sung wrong belongs to whoever sang it wrong. That's the loophole. That's always been the loophole. Edith found it but she couldn't finish it because she was alone and it takes somebody the thing has already got its hooks in — somebody it thinks it owns." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever been given by anyone, of any age. "It's got its hooks in you, mister Crane. That makes you perfect."

* * *

I walked her home. Or toward home — to the bend in the lane where the houses began, past the point where I judged a nine-year-old could safely absorb the rest of the journey without an escort the village would notice. The frost was starting in patches on the ground, and — I checked twice, because I check everything twice now — it lay only on the side of the lane nearest my house, following me like a shadow made of cold.

At the bend she stopped and turned around.

"They'll bring me to you on the morning," she said. "For leverage. I heard them arranging it. So don't be surprised, and don't let it work, and don't sign anything to save me, because saving me that way isn't saving." She tucked her hands into her enormous sleeves. "Sing it wrong, mister Crane. Sing it wronger than anybody's ever sung anything. And when they scream — and they will scream — keep singing."

Then she went down the lane, small and straight-backed, coat flapping, and the dark took her the way the dark here takes everything: gently, completely, and with the air of something that considers itself owed.

I stood at the bend for a long time. Behind me my house hummed, one room warmer than the rest, its clocks a minute slow and losing. Ahead of me the village slept its bloodless sleep, forty chairs facing a wall, a whole song scratched into plaster, a child born into debt walking home to parents who would hand her over at sunrise as sure as gravity.

One day. Maybe two.

I went inside, took Edith's manuscript from the drawer, and began, for the first time, to practice singing badly.
