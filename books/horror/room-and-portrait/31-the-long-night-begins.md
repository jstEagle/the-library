## 31. The Long Night Begins

Midnight arrived the way a debt collector arrives: not with knocking but with a change in the quality of the silence.

I had been sitting at the kitchen table since eleven with the deed in front of me and a pot of tea I'd made strong enough to strip varnish, on the theory that if my hands were occupied holding something hot, they'd be less likely to hold the pen. The pen sat three feet away, uncapped, exactly where it had been since sunset. Neither of us had moved. We were having a standoff, the pen and I, and I want it noted for the record that I was winning, in the sense that a man standing waist-deep in the sea is winning against the tide.

At twelve o'clock precisely, every clock in the house stopped losing its minute.

That sounds like nothing. It wasn't nothing. For two weeks the clocks had been shedding sixty seconds a night like a snake shedding skin, and I had learned to sleep inside that small arithmetic of loss. Tonight they simply... agreed with each other again. The mantel clock, the kitchen timer, the dead watch in my pocket that hadn't run since I arrived — all of them ticking together, in unison, like a choir finding its pitch before the conductor raises his hand.

"Right," I said to the empty room. "Let's have it, then."

The house, which had spent weeks pretending to be merely old, stopped pretending.

* * *

The first thing it returned was small, which I understood later was deliberate. A seducer doesn't open with the good stuff.

The smell of toast. Not burning toast, not the ghost of toast — actual toast, buttered, drifting down the hallway from a direction in which there was no kitchen. My stomach responded before my brain could file an objection, which is the trouble with bodies; they'll testify against you every time.

I got up. Of course I got up. You would too. Don't pretend otherwise.

The hallway was longer than it should have been, which by now registered as normal rather than alarming — I had measured it twice with a surveyor's tape and once with a ball of string, and the string came back longer than the tape, which is the sort of result that either breaks your mind or makes you laugh, and I chose laughing, mostly. But tonight the hallway wasn't just long. It was *furnished differently*. The wallpaper I'd been meaning to strip for a fortnight gave way halfway down to paper I knew intimately: small yellow roses on cream, faded in a diagonal stripe where the sun used to come through a window that had been on the other side of the country when I was six years old.

My mother's hallway. Or rather, the hallway of the house we lived in until I was six, reproduced here in Hollowmere with the loving, slightly wrong precision of a dream. The third floorboard from the kitchen door creaked under my foot, and it creaked in the right place, at the right angle, with the right apologetic little sound, and I stood on it for a moment like a man testing ice, because if the floorboard was right then what else might be?

The kitchen at the end of the hall was her kitchen.

Not similar. Not evocative. *Hers.* The dresser with the chipped blue door. The calendar from the butcher's shop, turned to a month in a year I shouldn't have been able to read off the top of my head and could. The tea towel draped over the oven rail with the wobbly embroidered ducklings, one of which had only one eye because she'd fallen asleep mid-duck in 1979 and never went back. There was bread under a cloth on the counter. There was a cup of tea going cold beside the sink, half-drunk, with her lipstick — the coral one, the one Dad hated — faint on the rim.

She wasn't there. That was the mercy of it, or the cruelty, depending on which way you wanted to be flayed. The kitchen was set like a stage between entrances. Everything arranged as though she'd just stepped out to hang washing, and any second now—

"Don't," I said aloud, to the house, to myself, to whatever was listening with its head cocked. "Don't you dare bring her through that door."

The house said nothing. Houses that answer questions are bad enough; houses that decline to answer them are worse.

I picked up the cold tea because apparently my hands had defected along with my stomach, and I drank it, and it tasted exactly right, tannic and over-stewed the way she always left it, and I put the cup down very carefully so as not to throw it, and I walked back up the hallway without looking at the yellow roses, and I did not cry until I reached my own room, and then only briefly, and I'm counting that as a victory. On anniversary night, you take your victories where the margins are thin.

* * *

By one o'clock the house had returned the rest of the inventory.

It came in waves, the way tide comes into a cave — not all at once but in surges, each one reaching a little further up the rock. Sounds first. A dog barking three streets away from a street that didn't exist in this county. Rain on a tin roof, though there was no rain and no tin. A woman's laughter from upstairs, brief and warm, cut off as though she'd remembered something she'd meant to forget.

Then objects. Small ones, patient ones. A hair ribbon on the banister. A child's drawing folded into quarters on the doormat — mine, age five, a horse that looked like a sofa with legs, done in crayon so aggressively the paper had torn. I had watched my mother throw that drawing away. I had been *there*. And here it was, smoothed flat, kept, presented like evidence in a trial whose charge I couldn't yet read.

"You're showing me what you hold," I said to the dark hallway. "Is that it? This is the collateral display?"

The second voice answered from everywhere and nowhere, dry and unhurried, the voice of a solicitor who has never once been surprised by a client.

"I am returning what was borrowed," it said. "There is a difference."

"Borrowed implies I lent it."

"You lent everything," it said, "the day you asked your first question in this house. But we needn't litigate. Tonight is not for litigation." A pause, and I could hear — this is the part I still can't write down without my jaw tightening — I could hear that it was pleased. "Tonight is for homecoming."

I went downstairs and got the deed off the table and held it. Not reading it. That was the discipline, and God knows it cost me: the document fairly itched to be read aloud, the way a bell itches to be struck. I'd learned that much from Edith's notes, scrawled in the margins of her manuscript in handwriting that deteriorated across thirty years from schoolteacher-neat to something like seismograph output: *Never say the words back to it. Reading aloud is signing with your mouth.*

So I held the deed against my chest like a breastplate and I sat in the chair facing the front door, and I waited for the next wave.

* * *

It came at two, and it came as a voice, and it was the voice I had been bracing against since the moment the clocks agreed with each other, and it undid me anyway.

"Mvo?" my mother said, from the top of the stairs.

Not the second voice doing an impression. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. The second voice does impressions the way a photograph does a face — accurate, complete, and dead behind the eyes. This was different. This was her voice with the grain in it, the slight huskiness that got worse after the winter she had bronchitis, the way she said my name with the *o* rounded soft, the way nobody else has ever said it, including me, out loud, in thirty years.

"Ivo, love, are you sitting down there in the dark?"

"Yes," I said, and my voice came out steady, which I will be smug about forever, because inside I was a boy of six with a torn crayon drawing and no defenses whatsoever. "Yes, Mum. Go back to bed."

"It's cold up here. Come and sit with me a while."

And there it was. The whole architecture of the trap laid out in seven words. Come upstairs. Come into the sealed room, or the bedroom that had grown overnight to resemble hers, come and sit with the thing wearing her warmth, and let it pour the lullaby into you note-perfect, and somewhere between the first verse and the last your mouth would start moving on its own, singing it back, correctly, and correct singing is signature, and signature is surrender, and the deed would sign itself with my own breath while my heart broke happily in my chest.

"No," I said.

A pause. Then, gently, with infinite patience, the way she used to talk me down from nightmares: "You don't have to be afraid of me, love."

"I know," I said. "That's the problem."

Another pause, longer. When the voice came again it had shifted register — still her timbre, but the phrasing was the second voice's now, the dry legal patience bleeding through the disguise like wire through wax. "We can do this all night, Ivo. We have, in fact, scheduled the whole night. But consider: how many more times in your life will you hear that voice? I took it from you once already as payment. I can return it permanently. All you have to do—"

"No."

"—is read the deed. Aloud. Just the first line. Just to know what you're agreeing to. That's reasonable, isn't it? A man should know the terms."

"That's why you want me to read it aloud," I said. "Because knowing isn't the point. Saying is."

Silence. Then, almost warmly: "Edith taught you that."

"Edith taught me everything worth knowing in this village, and she learned it the hard way, for thirty years, alone, in a sealed room, and you never once got her to sing it back. So you'll forgive me if I take her side."

"She failed," the voice said, and there was something underneath the patience now, something old and thin and hungry, the sound of a thing that has been hungry so long hunger has become its personality. "Thirty years, and on the last verse she sang it true. They always slip on the last verse. Grief is heaviest at the end of a song."

"Then I'll grieve in prose," I said.

The voice laughed. It was her laugh. I gripped the deed tighter and stared at the door and thought about Tam Vetch's face at the end — grateful, finally light, a man setting down a weight he'd carried for fifty-six years — and I held onto that face like a handrail in a storm, because it was proof. Proof that the thing could be beaten, that wrongness was armor, that a song mangled on purpose belonged to no one.

The stairs creaked. Once. Twice. Descending.

I did not look up. I am telling you honestly, as a scholar and a coward and a man who survived this night: I did not look up. Whatever stood on those stairs wearing her voice stayed above the fourth step, and at some point the creaking stopped, and the cold that had pooled around my ankles drained away toward the kitchen, and the house exhaled.

* * *

Three o'clock brought the lullaby itself, restored, and this was the worst wave of all, worse than the kitchen, worse than the stairs, because it didn't ask anything of me. It just played.

It came from everywhere — the walls, the pipes, the space behind my own ears — sung in her voice, note-perfect, every ornament exactly where she put it, the little rise she did on the third line that I've never heard anyone else do because the song, as far as the world knows, doesn't exist outside our kitchen. It was flawless. It was a museum piece. It was my childhood taxidermied and mounted under glass, and it was being performed for me like a bribe.

And here is the thing I will confess only here, in this account that maybe no one will ever read: some traitorous cellar of me *sang along*. Silently. In the dark behind my ribs, where no microphone could reach and no contract could touch, some part of me that is six years old and misses her beyond the reach of language harmonized with that perfect, terrible rendition, and wept, and was happy.

But the mouth stayed shut. The mouth is the border checkpoint, and the mouth held.

When the lullaby ended, the house let the silence sit for a long time. Then the second voice, back in its own dry register, said:

"You're stronger than Edith."

"Don't flatter me."

"It isn't flattery. It's assessment. She lasted four hours past midnight on her anniversary night before she wept. You've lasted three hours and haven't touched the pen." A pause. "Of course, she had thirty years of practice at refusing, and you've had a fortnight. Which makes your performance either admirable or doomed, depending on how the remaining hours go."

"How many are there?"

"Sunrise is at 6:47," it said. "But you know that. You've known it since the solicitor called. Three hours and forty-seven minutes, Ivo. The house has so much more to give back. We've barely opened the boxes."

Somewhere upstairs, a door I had nailed shut in week two — quietly, with a hammer, feeling ridiculous — unnailed itself with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

I looked at the deed in my hands. Blank where a signature should be. Eleven names above Edith Marlow's empty line, and below it, waiting, a twelfth line with my name printed neatly at the margin in ink that hadn't been there yesterday.

Three hours and forty-seven minutes.

I turned my chair to face the stairs, held the deed against my chest, and settled in to endure.

From the top of the stairs, in a voice that was getting better at being hers with every passing hour, my mother said: "Ivo, love. Come and see what I found in the attic."
