## 2. Fingerprints

Ada Nystrom had a system, and the system had never failed her.

Step one: assess. Step two: exclude. Step three: identify. Step four: eliminate. It was not glamorous, but neither was pest control, and glamour had never once paid for diesel. The system worked because it did not care what you *wanted* the problem to be. Rats were rats. Mice were mice. And whatever Mrs. Ellery kept calling "the visitor" was, by process of elimination, going to turn out to be something with fur and a digestive tract and a very ordinary fear of steel wool.

So on her second morning in Hollowmere, Ada backed her van up the lane to Mrs. Ellery's cottage, unloaded forty pounds of equipment with the ease of twenty years' practice, and got to work.

The kitchen was the epicenter. That much was clear even before she opened her kit. Mrs. Ellery had left a bag of flour open on the counter — deliberately, Ada suspected, the way some clients left out saucers of milk for things they didn't want to name — and the flour was printed all over with small handprints. Five fingers each. Neat as a signature.

"Rats don't have hands," Mrs. Ellery said, watching from the doorway with her arms folded.

"Mice have paws," Ada said, kneeling, "and those leave four-toed prints with a tail drag. Squirrels are bigger but messier. These—" She stopped herself from saying *these aren't anything* and said instead: "These are consistent. Whatever made them has a regular gait."

"A gait." Mrs. Ellery tasted the word like it might be off. "It walks, then."

"Everything that leaves prints walks," Ada said, and pulled on her nitrile gloves. "That's actually good news. Things that walk can be tracked, and things that can be tracked can be excluded from premises."

She dusted the counter with talc-based tracking powder, laid down her first trap line along the skirting boards — snap traps, live traps, glue boards at intervals she'd calibrated over two decades — and set a small camera on a shelf, angled at the flour bag. Then she went through the house room by room with a torch and a notebook, cataloguing every print she could find.

By noon she had thirty-one entries.

That was the first thing that bothered her, though she wrote it down without comment: *prints too uniform.* Thirty-one sets, and not one smear, not one partial, not one drag mark. Every print was complete. Every finger registered. Even a house cat, walking across flour, left smudged toes and the occasional double-step. These prints looked less like evidence of movement and more like — she hunted for the word while eating a cheese sandwich in her van — like stamps. Like someone pressing a rubber hand into the flour, once per location, tidy as an office worker punching a clock.

*Someone.* She noticed she'd written *someone* and crossed it out and wrote *the animal* above it, which was worse, because now the page showed both words and she'd have to look at them together.

* * *

The second thing that bothered her arrived at four o'clock, when she opened the cupboard under the sink.

She'd checked it that morning. She knew she'd checked it, because checking under-sink cupboards was step one of any kitchen assessment and Ada did not skip steps. She'd found nothing but bleach bottles and a mousetrap so old it qualified as an antique. She had closed the door. She remembered closing the door, because the hinge squeaked and she'd made a mental note to oil it.

Now the door stood slightly ajar, and inside, pressed into the fine dust on the shelf between the bleach and the antique trap, was a handprint.

Five fingers. Right hand. Small — smaller than hers, child-sized almost, though the proportions were wrong in a way her eye caught before her brain agreed to look at it directly. Too long in the palm. Fingers of equal length, all five, no taper, no thumb-differentiation. A hand drawn by someone who had been told about hands but had never needed one.

And it was *inside* the cupboard. Behind a closed door. On a shelf sixty centimetres off the ground, which meant whatever made it had opened the door, reached in, pressed its palm neatly into the dust, closed the door again —

— except the door wasn't fully closed. It stood ajar by perhaps three centimetres, exactly as if someone wanted it to be found.

Ada crouched there for a long moment, torch in one hand, notebook in the other, feeling the particular cold that comes not from fear exactly but from the moment a professional explanation packs its bags and leaves without giving notice.

Then she did what she always did. She measured it. Fourteen centimetres, palm to fingertip. She photographed it. She noted the height, the orientation, the absence of disturbance to the surrounding dust. She wrote: *access method unknown* and then, because the notebook deserved honesty even if she didn't feel like giving it any, she added: *door was shut this morning.*

"You're doing great," she told herself quietly, in the tone she used for nervous clients. "Very normal Tuesday."

Mrs. Ellery appeared in the doorway behind her. "Found something?"

"Just confirming activity patterns," Ada said, standing, knees cracking. "I'll need to leave the traps a few nights. I'll come back tomorrow evening to check."

"The visitor likes evenings," Mrs. Ellery said. "Dusk mostly. Though it's been later lately. Bolder." She said it the way people report weather. "You'll want to be gone before dark."

"I'll be gone before dark," Ada agreed, because it was easier than asking the obvious question, which was: *gone where? There's nowhere to go. You're at the end of a lane in a village that isn't on my satnav.*

* * *

Over the next three days, the prints multiplied with a patience that Ada found more unsettling than speed would have been.

They appeared on the inside of the airing cupboard, among the folded towels, arranged in a little row like laundry waiting to be claimed. They appeared on the bathroom mirror — not smeared, not breathed-on, just printed, five neat fingers at exactly the height where a person would rest a hand while leaning close to their own reflection. They appeared on the lid of the piano no one played, on the spine of a book on the second shelf, on the window glass of the back bedroom, always, always at shoulder height of a standing adult.

Not floor level. Not along skirting boards, where a quadruped travels. Shoulder height. As if the maker of these prints walked upright, or wanted very badly to seem like something that did.

Ada catalogued forty-six new prints over three days and built, in her notebook, a little table. Location. Height. Orientation. Left or right. And in the margin, accumulating like silt, the notes she didn't read aloud even to herself:

*No weight displacement in flour beneath wall prints.*
*Prints on ceiling beam, 2.3m. Orientation suggests upright stance.*
*Sealed pantry (latch confirmed fastened 22:40). Prints inside, 06:15.*
*All prints same size. All prints right hand.*

Every single one a right hand. Forty-six prints and not one left. She sat in her van with this fact for a long time, eating an apple she forgot to taste.

A right-handed world. Or — and here the thought arrived uninvited, sliding under the door of her mind the way the visitor apparently slid under doors — a thing that only had the one hand, and was showing it to her. Over and over. *Here. Here is mine. Where is yours?*

"That's anthropomorphising," she said aloud, to the van, and bit the apple hard enough to hurt.

But that night, back at the rented room above the Hollowmere Arms, she lay awake doing the arithmetic she'd promised herself she wouldn't do. Shoulder height of a standing adult. She was one metre seventy. The prints sat at one metre fifty-five to one metre sixty. Which meant either the thing was shorter than her, or it stooped, or —

Or it was standing where she would stand. Reaching where she would reach. Measuring itself against her, print by print, like a child tracing round a parent's hand to see how much growing was left to do.

She slept maybe three hours, and dreamed of flour, and woke with her right hand curled into a fist against her sternum as if holding something in.

* * *

On the fourth day, needing a control sample if nothing else — *compare the abnormal to the normal; establish your baseline* — Ada canvassed the neighbours. Hollowmere had perhaps sixty houses strung along two lanes and a green, and word travelled faster than any animal she'd ever tracked. By the time she knocked on the third door, people were already saying, "Oh, you're the one dealing with Mrs. Ellery's visitor," in voices that were kind and careful and closed, like doors left politely ajar.

Mostly they told her nothing useful. One man offered her rhubarb. A woman asked whether she was Ellery's niece, and when Ada said no, just the contractor, the woman's face did something complicated and apologetic, and she said, "Well. Good luck with it," in the tone reserved for terminal diagnoses and bad weather at weddings.

It was the sixth house, the last on the lane, that gave her the neighbour.

He was in his garden — a wide, weedy plot running down to a drystone wall — and he was standing still. Not gardening-still, not thinking-still. Standing the way furniture stands. He was a big man, grey-bearded, in a cardigan the colour of wet slate, and he was facing Ada's rented room across the lane, and he did not turn around when she said good afternoon.

"Hello?" she tried again. "Sorry to bother you. I'm doing pest work at Mrs. Ellery's, and I wondered if you'd seen anything — droppings, damage, tracks—"

"I see it every night," the man said. He hadn't turned. His voice was mild, conversational, aimed at the middle distance. "From my window. It sits on your roof ridge sometimes. Sometimes it stands in the yard. Last night it stood in the yard from eleven until the birds started, facing your window."

Ada's professional smile held. Barely. "Facing my — you mean an animal? A fox, maybe? We do get foxes standing and staring, it's territorial—"

"It doesn't move," the man said. "All night. It doesn't shift its weight. It doesn't turn its head. It just watches your window, and around three it raises its hand." He lifted his own right hand, slowly, and pressed the palm flat against the air, shoulder height. "Like this. Holds it there till dawn."

The garden was very quiet. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Did you get any sleep?" the man asked then, and finally turned, and his eyes were ordinary and tired and full of something that was almost pity. "Only you've got your light on till four. I see that too."

"I keep irregular hours," Ada said. "Occupational."

"Mm," he said, and turned back to his watching, and Ada walked away with her notebook hugged to her chest like armour, telling herself: *old man, lonely, exaggerating, probably watches everything, probably told the last contractor the same story,* and the telling worked for nearly nine minutes, until she remembered that she hadn't mentioned to anyone which window was hers.

* * *

That night she decided to stay and watch.

This was, she told herself, sound methodology. Direct observation beats inference; you cannot build an exclusion strategy on hearsay. She set up in Mrs. Ellery's kitchen with the camera running, a thermos of coffee, her notebook, and the flour re-laid in a fresh, pristine drift across the centre of the floor — a clean page, waiting.

Mrs. Ellery had gone to her sister's in the next valley, pressing the key into Ada's hand with a look that said, plainly, *you poor fool, and also thank you.* The cottage ticked and settled around Ada the way old houses do, and outside the window the light went from gold to bruise to black, and the hills became a suggestion, and the lane became a rumour.

At half past ten, the flour was empty. Untouched. A perfect white field with no tracks crossing it, and Ada sat looking at it and felt the specific vertigo of a scientist whose control group has begun lying to her.

At midnight, the camera's red recording light blinked steadily, faithfully, recording a kitchen in which nothing happened.

At one, she caught herself listening to the walls. Old houses make noises; she knew the taxonomy of house-noises better than anyone alive — expansion ticks, pipe knocks, the dry scuttle of mice in cavities, the papery flutter of a trapped bird. This was none of them. This was quieter than noise. This was the sound a house makes when it is being listened to from inside, and holding very still.

At two, she wrote in her notebook: *Nothing observed. Flour undisturbed. Camera active. Will review footage in morning.* Her handwriting had gone slightly strange, slanting downhill, and she stared at it for a while because it reminded her of something she couldn't place — some other handwriting, from some other time, in a farmhouse she did not let herself think about — and then she put the notebook face-down.

At three, according to the clock on the microwave, she must have drifted, because the next thing she knew the kitchen was colder and the window over the sink was full of dark.

Not night-dark. Night-dark has depth, texture, stars, the orange bleed of a distant porch light. This was a gathered dark, a dark with edges, occupying the window the way a person occupies a doorway — politely, completely, waiting to be acknowledged. It was roughly her height. It had the posture of someone who has knocked, heard no answer, and decided to wait rather than presume.

Ada did not move. Twenty years of professionalism poured through her like cold water: *don't run, don't scream, observe, catalogue, breathe.* Her heart was performing percussion and her hands, resting on the table, had gone entirely still, because some ancient part of her understood that stillness was the correct answer to whatever question was being asked.

The shape raised its right hand and pressed it, palm-flat, against the outside of the glass.

When it withdrew, the print remained on the pane. Five fingers. Equal lengths. Shoulder height of a standing adult.

And then the mouth of it — if it was a mouth, a darker darkness within the dark, moving — shaped something at her. Slowly. Deliberately. Three syllables, maybe four, formed with the exaggerated clarity of someone speaking to a foreigner or a child, and Ada, watching, felt the shapes of the sounds assemble themselves in her own head without passing through her ears at all, and they were:

*A-da.*

Her name. Not shouted. Not threatened. Said the way you say a name when you've been looking for someone a long time, and have finally, finally found them, and are being very polite about it.

The kettle clicked. Just the thermostat, cooling, an ordinary mechanical event, and the shape was gone — not fled, not dissolved, simply *not there anymore*, the way a word is gone once you stop saying it — and the window held only night, and one perfect handprint, and Ada Nystrom sitting alone in a stranger's kitchen at three in the morning with her coffee gone cold and her system, her beautiful four-step system, lying in pieces around her like a dropped toolkit.

Assess: done. Exclude: impossible. Identify: she looked at the print on the glass and knew, with the certainty that lives below rationalism in the cellar of the mind, that the identification ran the other way.

Eliminate: 

She couldn't finish the word. She sat until the birds started, and then she packed up her traps — untouched, every one — and drove back to the inn with the windows up and the heater on in June, and lay on the bed with her clothes on, watching the ceiling.

Somewhere across the lane, she knew without looking, an old man in a wet-slate cardigan was watching her window, and for the first time in twenty years Ada Nystrom did not blame him for it.

Because at dusk that evening — she'd hear the villagers speak of it soon enough, but already, faintly, through the inn's thin glass, she had heard it begin — somewhere over the ruined tower where no bell had hung since 1911, something was ringing.
