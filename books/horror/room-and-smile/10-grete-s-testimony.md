## 10. Grete's Testimony

Grete Ashcombe lived in the last cottage before the lane gave up and became a footpath, a low stone house with a garden so aggressively cheerful—marigolds, nasturtiums, a scarecrow wearing a proper waxed jacket—that it looked like an argument against everything else in Hollowmere. Ada had walked past it four times now on her rounds, and each time Grete had been out there, deadheading flowers with the focus of a surgeon, not looking at her.

Today, Grete was waiting at the gate.

"You took your time," she said. "I've been lucid since Tuesday. It doesn't keep."

Ada stopped on the path. In nine days in Hollowmere she had learned that the villagers' courtesy was a kind of weather—uniform, ambient, impossible to argue with—but Grete's voice was different. Dry as seed husks. Impatient. *Alive* in a way that made Ada's chest ache with something she refused to call homesickness.

"Lucid," Ada repeated.

"Old age is a radio that drifts off station." Grete pushed open the gate. "Most of the time I get static and 1962. But some mornings I come through clear, and I know what I know, and I say it while I can. Come in before it goes. The kettle's on. I made a cake, which should tell you how serious this is. I haven't baked since my husband died, and he'd have called this cake 'a cry for help.'"

The kitchen was small and yellow and smelled of ginger. A tabby cat occupied the fourth chair at the table like a committee member who could not be removed. Grete poured tea without asking how Ada took it—strong, no nonsense—and set down a fruitcake dense enough to have its own gravitational field.

"Mrs. Ellery sent you round last week," Grete said. "I watched you walk past my window three times and not knock."

"I was working."

"You were hiding behind your clipboard." Grete said it without malice, the way you'd note that it was raining. "Sit down, Ada Nystrom. I've got a story, and you're going to want to be sitting."

Ada sat. She was beginning to understand that in Hollowmere, people did not ask questions so much as arrange circumstances until the answers arrived on their own.

* * *

"My name is Grete Ashcombe," the old woman began, "and in the summer of 1974 I was seventeen years old, and I was in love with a girl named Liss Vane, though neither of us ever said so out loud because it was 1974 and Hollowmere, which between them accounted for roughly all of the silence in England."

Ada's tea paused halfway to her mouth.

"There were three of us that summer. Me, Liss, and a girl from the farm up past the ford—Maren. Maren Nystrom." Grete watched her over the rim of her cup. "Yes. That name. I see it land. Good. I haven't got the patience to tell it twice."

"Maren is my mother's name," Ada said carefully.

"I know whose name it is. I've known since you got out of that van with your mother's chin and her way of standing like she's about to leave any room she's in." Grete cut two slices of cake, gave one to the cat, who accepted it as tribute, and pushed the other toward Ada. "Eat. You're thin. Fear burns through a body like a furnace and nobody feeds it properly."

Ada ate. It seemed unwise not to.

"The church still had its things back then," Grete said. "Silver on the altar, silver in the cases. And in the case by the north wall there was a reliquary—a little box, this big—" she measured a hand-span, "—silver, with saints stamped round the lid, heavy as sin and twice as old. Nobody knew what was inside. Father Ambrose said it held a relic of the parish founding, but he said a lot of things. He was the sort of vicar who believed in God the way other men believe in the weather. Present, occasionally inconvenient, not to be discussed indoors."

"What was actually inside?"

"We didn't know then. We know now. Or we guess, and our guesses have been confirmed rather thoroughly." Grete's hands went flat on the table. "It was Liss's idea. Everything was Liss's idea; that was the whole trouble with loving Liss. She was the funniest person in three parishes and she could not bear a locked thing. A dare, she called it. Just to hold it. Just to take it out into the field and open it under the moon and see what a box that old was keeping so carefully."

"And you did."

"We did. Midsummer, 1974. We climbed in through the vestry window—Maren went first, she was always the brave one about actual physical danger, it was everything else she was scared of—and we took it." Grete smiled, and it was a terrible smile, full of love and rust. "And I want you to understand something, Ada. Nothing happened when we opened it. That's the part nobody believes. We sat in Barrow Field with the lid off and the moon coming up, and there were pages inside, old pages, soft as cloth, and they were blank. Blank as boiled milk. Liss laughed. Maren laughed. I laughed, because they were laughing. We put it back—or we meant to. Liss kept it. Said she'd return it Monday. Said the church owed us a bit of excitement anyway."

"But she didn't return it."

"That same night," Grete said, "the bell rang."

* * *

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, a wood pigeon said the one thing wood pigeons know.

"It had been silent since 1911," Grete went on. "Everyone knows that story—the bell cracked, they melted it down for the war effort that never needed it, and the tower's been empty ever since. Empty tower, no bell, no rope. And at dusk that night it rang. One toll. Clear across the valley. My mother dropped a whole trifle." She said this with the gravity the event deserved. "Next dusk, again. One toll. And the night after that. Every dusk since, fifty years, one toll, and I have heard every single one, and I will tell you what I learned about them, Ada, because I paid attention when everyone else was learning to look away."

"Which is?"

"They're counting." Grete leaned forward. "Not hours. Not days. They're counting *something else*. And the count changes when certain people are in the village. When Liss stayed, after—we'll get to that—the tolls came closer together. Like a clock that knows its appointment."

Ada set down her fork. "Tell me about Liss staying."

Grete looked out the window at her marigolds for a long moment. When she spoke again her voice had gone quieter, the dryness soaked through with something older.

"Liss kept the reliquary eleven days. On the eleventh day she told me and Maren she was going to give it back, and she walked up the lane toward the church at dusk, and the bell rang while she was still in sight of us—rang early, rang wrong—and she turned around and waved, and she never reached the church door. She just... wasn't in the lane anymore. Not gone like vanished. Gone like *collected*. Like something had finished reading a page and closed the book." Her jaw tightened. "We ran to the tower. Empty. We ran to her house. Her mother was setting the table for supper. And here is the thing I need you to hear, because it's the thing that explains Hollowmere entire: her mother wasn't frightened. She was *relieved*. She said, 'Well. Now the rest of us are safe.' Because that's the arrangement, you see. That's what this village has been since before the church, probably since before the village. It gives the visitor a name, now and then, one name, freely given or freely taken, and in exchange it leaves everyone else alone. Generous, isn't it. Like a landlord who only evicts one tenant at a time."

"And Maren?" Ada asked. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "My mother."

"Maren left." Grete turned back from the window. "Two weeks later. Packed a bag, wouldn't say goodbye properly, wouldn't come to Liss's—there wasn't a funeral, there was never a funeral, you can't bury a page—and she went, and I stood at the gate and watched her go and I thought, good, run, be somewhere the bell can't reach." She shook her head slowly. "But it followed her. Not the bell. The quiet. Have you noticed it about your mother, Ada? In the photographs, in the stories? The silence that travels with a person? Houses go quiet when she enters them. Radios drift. Dogs won't settle. Fifty years of rooms holding their breath around her, because she was the one carrying the debt, and debts make a sound only the debtor can't hear."

Ada thought of the farmhouse. Of the noises in the walls her mother had hummed to like pets. Of twenty years of explanations, each one thinner than the last, a woman papering over a hole with words.

"She never talked about Hollowmere," Ada said. "She never talked about anything. I used to think she was just—closed. Private."

"She was keeping a secret so large she couldn't see around it." Grete refilled Ada's cup, unasked. "And then she had you, and I imagine she thought—hoped—that distance would dilute it. That a child born far away would be just a child. But the visitor doesn't work in distances. It works in names. And you, my girl, are named after nothing. Did you ever wonder about that? No grandmother, no aunt, no saint. Just Ada. Four letters, plain as a fencepost. Your mother chose the plainest name in the book because she was trying to build you a fence too."

Ada's throat was tight. "You said the pages were blank."

"They were blank when we opened them." Grete met her eyes, and for a moment the lucidity in her face was almost cruel in its clarity. "They don't stay blank. Whatever holds that book writes in it. Names go in when they're given. And they come *out*—" she tapped the table, once, softly, "—when they're taken. Liss's page filled up the night she was collected. I've seen it since. Don't ask me how. And your mother's page has been filling for fifty years, slow as frost on glass, letter by letter, every year she carried it. There isn't much room left on it, Ada."

"So the debt passes." Ada heard herself say it flatly, scientifically, the old reflex reaching for the clipboard even now. "If the carrier dies, it transfers. To next of kin."

"Oh, you clever cold little thing." Grete said it fondly. "Yes. That's why you're here. You think Mrs. Ellery found you through a pest-control directory? Half this village has been praying for twenty years that Maren's girl would come home before Maren's page filled up. Not because we want you to pay it." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the word. "Because if anyone can end it instead of paying it, it might be you. You've spent your whole life learning to look straight at things other people rename. That's not a flaw, dear. That might be the whole point of you."

The cat stood, stretched, and left the table with the air of a creature whose scene was done.

Outside, far off across the fields, the light was beginning to go amber. Dusk was coming. It always came. Ada realized she had started measuring her days by it, the way the village did—not by clocks but by the space between tolls.

"One more thing," Grete said, and now she gripped Ada's wrist, her hand surprisingly strong, her eyes suddenly wet and fierce. "When the moon fattens—and it's fattening, look at it tonight, look at it—you'll feel it pulling at your name. Everyone will start knowing you. Doors opening. Old nicknames. That's not welcome, Ada. That's *reading*. It reads you a little more each day, and when the moon is full it finishes the sentence." She pressed something into Ada's palm—small, hard, folded in a scrap of cloth. "So don't waste the nights you've got left being careful. Be quick. Ask your mother what she was protecting you *from*, not what she was protecting you *of*. And whatever you decide to do with that book—"

The first toll came through the walls, muffled and vast, shaking the windowpanes and the tea in Ada's cup and something older than both.

Grete closed her eyes. "Too late tonight," she murmured. "Go home, Ada. Go and find out what your mother kept under the floorboards."

Ada stared at her. "How did you know there's a farmhouse? How did you know about—"

But Grete's face had already drifted, gently, like a radio sliding off station, and the old woman smiled at her with warm, unfocused courtesy and offered her another slice of cake she no longer remembered baking.

Ada walked out through the marigolds with the small hard object in her fist. At the gate, she unfolded the cloth.

Inside was a brass key, green with age, and a paper label in her mother's handwriting:

*For Ada. Don't use it unless you must. If you must — forgive me.*
