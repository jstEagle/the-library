## 15. The Harvest Moon Rises

Ada woke in Mrs. Ellery's spare room with flour on her pillowcase and no memory of how it got there.

She lay very still for a long moment, cataloguing. Flour: present. Memory: absent. Panic: rising, thank you, noted, filed. She had spent twenty years teaching herself that fear was just a mislabeled thing — a draft, a mouse, a pipe settling — but there was no renaming this. The flour hadn't been carried in. It had been *left*, the way a cat leaves a dead bird on the doorstep: not a threat exactly, more an offering. A reminder that something considered her worth feeding.

Or worth keeping.

"Right," she said to the ceiling, because saying *right* out loud was cheaper than screaming, and she'd always been frugal. "Two nights."

The harvest moon would rise tomorrow evening. She'd counted it against Grete's warning — *come back before the moon fattens* — and against the almanac page Tallow had left, face-down, on the vicarage step like a man who wanted you to know he'd been there without wanting to be caught having been there. Two nights. Which meant today was for the one thing Ada Nystrom had spent her whole adult life avoiding:

Homework about feelings.

* * *

The Hollowmere parish archive lived in a room behind the vestry that smelled of cold stone and older paper, and it was kept by a woman named Dora Petch who was approximately as old as the stone.

"You'll want the cartularies," Dora said, before Ada had finished explaining what she wanted. "Third shelf. Mind the damp on the left side, the wall weeps when it rains and it's been raining since 1987."

"You knew what I was going to ask?"

Dora looked at her over half-moon spectacles with the mild patience of someone who had outlived everyone who might have surprised her. "Girl, I've filed every scrap of paper this parish has produced since decimal currency came in. When a stranger starts asking about the reliquary, they're either a journalist or a Nystrom." She turned back to her tea. "You've got her chin. Third shelf."

Ada decided, generously, not to be unnerved. It was becoming a full-time position.

The cartularies were ledgers the size of tombstones, bound in leather gone the color of strong tea, and the third shelf held four of them spanning the parish's founding through the early 1800s. Ada hauled down the oldest, blew off a sediment of dust that she suspected predated the concept of dust, and began to read.

The handwriting was beautiful in the way only things written by candlelight could be — each letter leaning into the next like villagers shoulder-to-shoulder at a funeral. Latin, mostly. Her schoolgirl Latin was forty years stale and had never been good, but the structure of legal documents is a skeleton you can hang any language on, and after an hour she had the shape of it.

It wasn't a land charter. Or rather, it wasn't *only* a land charter.

*In the year of our Lord eleven hundred and sixty-one,* it began, *the free men and women of the hollow mere do set down their names, that the ground may know them and the ground may keep them.*

Below, in two careful columns, ran a list. Not signatures — names, written out whole, and beside each name a second word or phrase in smaller script. Ada squinted. Some were occupations: *miller, smith, keeper of geese*. But others were stranger. Beside one entry, *Edric called Stillwater*. Beside another, *Maud who does not sleep*. Beside a third, simply, *the one who waits at the ford*.

True names. Or something adjacent to true names — the name underneath the name, the one your mother used when you were small and feverish, the one nobody else ever learned because nobody else needed to. Ada felt the hair on her arms lift, one follicle at a time, in what she recognized with professional detachment as an orderly and well-managed fashion.

Every villager. Every founding family. Written into silver-bound pages and sealed inside a reliquary, so that the parish's identity — its actual, literal identity — sat in a box on the altar for four hundred years.

Until three girls took it home in a canvas shopping bag in 1974.

"Oh, Mum," Ada said quietly, to no one, in an empty room, which was where she did most of her talking to her mother these days.

* * *

She worked through the morning, and the morning worked back.

The later cartularies told the rest. Every generation, the covenant was renewed — new names added, old ones struck through with a single line that somehow didn't obscure them, only *quieted* them, the way a held breath quiets a room. And every renewal was witnessed the same way. Not by the vicar. By an entry that appeared in each book, in different hands across six centuries, always identical in wording:

*Given freely, taken gladly.*

Ada read that phrase eleven times. On the twelfth reading, she noticed something she should have noticed sooner: the phrasing was wrong. Backwards. You'd expect *taken freely, given gladly* if the parish were describing a gift. But *given freely, taken gladly* described something else entirely. Something being handed over. Something doing the taking.

And then, in the 1911 cartulary — the year the bell came down — she found the marginal note. Different ink, hurried, pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages:

*Bell recast & sold. Vicar says the ringing will stop. Vicar is wrong. It does not ring from the bell. It rings from the LIST. As long as the names are held, it calls. We have made ourselves a door and sold the hinges.*

Ada sat back. The vestry clock ticked with the smugness of clocks everywhere.

The bell wasn't the summons. The bell had never been the summons. The reliquary was — the list of names inside it, every soul in Hollowmere written down and therefore *ownable*, and whatever owned the list had been calling the roll at dusk for fifty years, patient as arithmetic, waiting for someone to bring the register home.

Her mother had been carrying the register around for fifty years. Wrapped, eventually, in her daughter's baby blanket. Kept warm. Kept *waiting*.

Ada rubbed her eyes until she saw constellations. Then she did what she always did when the world stopped making sense: she made tea, laid out her evidence in a grid on the vestry table, and started looking for the loophole.

* * *

The loophole arrived at four o'clock, wearing cardigans.

Grete let herself into the vestry without knocking — doors in Hollowmere had apparently given up on the concept — carrying a tin of shortbread and the expression of a woman delivering a verdict.

"Dora said you'd found the covenant," Grete said. "Dora also said you'd got the wrong end of it, which is typical of Dora, who reads the words and not the spaces between them."

"The spaces between them," Ada repeated.

Grete set down the tin, opened it, and offered a biscuit with the ceremony of a sacrament. Ada took one. It was excellent. Grief and terror were, she was discovering, no defense whatsoever against good shortbread.

"Sit, girl. You look like your mother did, the week after. All edges." Grete lowered herself into the chair opposite with several audible negotiations. "Now. What have you concluded?"

"That whoever holds the reliquary holds the village's names. That the entity — the visitor, the thing under the tower, call it what you like — can't ring for us directly anymore because the bell's gone, but the list still works. That it's been trying to get the reliquary back, or get me to bring it back, and that Tallow wants to hand over my name to buy his congregation another generation." Ada ticked the points off on flour-dusted fingers. "And that I'm holding the only object in England that a monster wants, which makes me either the most important person in Hollowmere or the most doomed, and I genuinely can't tell which."

"Mm." Grete nibbled her own biscuit. "All true. All useless. Here's what you missed." She leaned forward. "Read me the witness line. From the renewals."

"*Given freely, taken gladly.*"

"Now read it again and think about what it doesn't say. It doesn't say *taken freely*. It can't take freely. Nothing about that thing takes freely — that's the whole of it, girl, that's the entire creature. It hoards what it's *given*. Liss gave herself. Your mother's friend gave herself. Every name in that book was surrendered by somebody — frightened, tricked, tired, loving, it doesn't matter how, but *given*. It has never once in six hundred years managed to simply reach out and take." Grete's eyes, milky at the rims, were absolutely clear at the centers. "Because it can't. Because a name isn't property. It's a gift or it's nothing."

Ada's tea went cold in her hands while the idea assembled itself.

"It can't take the reliquary," she said slowly. "That's why it's been leaving prints. Why it wrote my name in flour instead of just—" she gestured vaguely at the concept of horror— "*doing* whatever it does. It needs me to give it. To carry it up the tower myself. To open it and say yes."

"It needs you to want to," Grete corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is everything. It can't even ask outright. Asking would be pressure, and pressure isn't giving. So it leaves flour. It rings bells. It wears your aunt's face. It makes belonging smell so sweet you'd walk up those stairs on your own feet, smiling." Her mouth twisted. "It's very good at it. I nearly went in '74. I stood at the bottom of the tower steps for an hour, and the only reason I didn't climb was that my mother called me in for supper, and I was a greedy little madam." She paused. "Greed's saved more lives than courage, in my experience. Nobody warns you."

"So if I don't give it—"

"If you don't give it, it waits. It's been waiting since 1974; it can wait past Friday. But it won't need to wait, will it? Because Tallow will arrange a giving. He'll dress it up as mercy, he'll find the words, he'll get some poor soul to say yes on behalf of everyone else, and the list will grow by one name and the village will sleep another thirty years." Grete brushed crumbs from her cardigan with terrible gentleness. "Unless."

"Unless?"

"Unless the giving goes the other way." Grete reached across the table and tapped the oldest cartulary, right on the founding line. *That the ground may know them and the ground may keep them.* "A covenant cuts both ways, girl. They gave their names so the ground would *keep* them. Protect them. Belong to them as much as they belonged to it. Whoever holds the list holds the village — you said it yourself, and for once you were right. If the reliquary is returned *to the parish* — freely, openly, given back to the people whose names are written in it — then the debt ends. The keeping returns to the kept. And the thing under the tower is left holding fifty years of borrowed shapes and nothing else." She smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "It cannot take what is freely returned. But oh, it will hate watching you do it."

Ada stared at the ledger. The plan was forming the way plans did for her — not as inspiration but as pest control. Find the entry point. Cut off the food supply. Remove the reason anything wanted to be in the house at all.

"I'd have to speak my own name," she said. "Up there. Into the open. My real one — the one Mum used. The one nobody living knows."

"Yes."

"And if I'm wrong about any of this, I'll have handed it to the thing on a plate."

"Yes," Grete agreed cheerfully. "Lovely, isn't it? A proper gamble. I haven't seen one since the raffle of 1968, and that was rigged."

Ada laughed — actually laughed, a rusty sound that startled them both — and then the laugh died in her throat, because through the vestry window she saw the light going amber over the square, and the villagers coming out of their houses all at once, unhurried, unbidden, faces tilting toward the empty tower the way sunflowers tilt toward a sun that wasn't there anymore.

The dusk toll began. One. Two. Three.

But this evening, something was different. This evening, as the fourth toll faded, heads began to turn — away from the tower, one by one, across the square, until every face in Hollowmere was pointed at the vestry window.

At her.

They were smiling. All of them. Warmly. The way you smile at someone you've been expecting.

"They know you're close now," Grete said softly, not turning around. "Two nights, girl. Write your name somewhere safe tonight — your real one, in your own hand, where you alone can find it. Tomorrow, learn it by heart. And Ada—" the old woman's grip closed on her wrist, bird-boned and surprisingly strong, "—when they start greeting you like a daughter come home, and part of you *wants* it, and that part gets louder — remember that the wanting is the trap. The love is real. The wanting is theirs."

Outside, the last toll hung in the air like a held breath.

Then, faintly, from the direction of the empty tower, something began to hum — a lullaby Ada hadn't heard in thirty years, in a voice she'd know anywhere, singing the name that only her mother had ever used.

* * *

That night, Ada sat in Mrs. Ellery's kitchen with a pen and a strip of paper, and wrote, in her own careful hand, the name she had never given anyone.

She folded it small and hid it inside the lining of her coat, over her heart, where the warmth of it felt less like a secret than like a seed.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she'd learn it by heart.

Tonight, upstairs, something in the spare room was already practicing saying it.
