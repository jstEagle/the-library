## 16. What the Lady Wants

Toby had learned the house's new rules the way you learn the rules of a nightmare: by falling into them.

The hallway outside his bedroom used to end at the linen closet. Now it ended at a door with his grandmother's handwriting on a card taped to it — *TOBY'S ROOM* — except his room was behind him, forty feet back, and when he opened the door anyway, it opened onto the kitchen, where the clock said 4:12 and also 11:58, depending on which way you tilted your head.

"Stop doing that," his mother had said, when she caught him tilting his head. "Toby. Stop *doing that*."

But the lady in the wall did it all the time. Tilted her head. Smiled like the numbers were a game she'd already won.

He found her in the dark television in the sitting room, the way he always did now, since the power died and the screen went the deep grey of a pond under ice. She stood at the back of the glass, small and out of focus, like someone standing at the far end of a swimming pool. She wore a uniform — pale blue, the kind nurses wore in old photographs — and her hair was pinned up in a way that made her look like a woman from a film about the war.

"You're not a ghost," Toby said. It wasn't a question anymore. He'd figured it out around two in the morning, lying awake, listening to the count come up through the floorboards from Grandpa's room. *Twenty-two. Twenty-one.* Ghosts didn't help you. Ghosts didn't point.

The lady in the wall raised one hand and drew a shape on the inside of the glass. Her finger left a trail, like writing on a fogged-up window. An arrow. Then another arrow. Then a little stick figure, and next to the stick figure, a shape Toby knew from the maps in the back of his atlas: a chapel.

"The chapel," Toby said. "Quentin said the chapel."

The lady nodded. Then she did the thing she did that made Toby's stomach go strange — she pressed her whole palm flat against the inside of the screen, and for a second her face came close, close enough that he could see she had tired eyes and a small scar above her eyebrow, and she mouthed something. Three words. He couldn't hear them through the glass. He never could. But he was starting to be able to read her lips, the way you get good at reading a teacher across a loud cafeteria.

*Get them out.*

* * *

He found Quentin first, because Quentin was the one who listened. She was in the hall with the salt in a cereal bowl, pouring a fresh line under the front door while his mother stood at the window with the curtains pulled almost shut, watching fog that wasn't weather.

"Quentin." His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "She wants to show me something."

Quentin went still the way she went still when Grandpa's numbers changed. "Who wants to—"

"The lady in the wall." He said it fast, before his mother could hear and before Quentin could do the face adults did, the gentle face that meant *we'll talk about that later, sweetheart.* "She's been showing me pictures. She drew the chapel. And Quentin — she drew *you*. She drew you walking, and then she drew the chapel, and then she drew all of us, and she crossed out the house."

Quentin set down the cereal bowl very carefully, as if it might go off. "Show me."

The sitting room had changed while they were gone. The furniture was the same, but the walls had shifted the way walls do in dreams — the doorway to the hall was now where the bookshelves had been, and the bookshelves were where the window had been, and the window was a long grey rectangle showing a hallway that did not exist anywhere in Wrenfield House. In the reflection of the dark TV, the room looked *almost* right. Almost. Toby had stopped trusting almost.

He knelt in front of the screen and knocked twice, the way you knocked on a friend's door, and the lady came.

Quentin made a sound. It wasn't a scream. It was smaller and worse than a scream — a single syllable of air, like someone had knocked all the wind out of her and only a little of it came back.

"That's—" Quentin's hand went to her mouth. "That's my mother."

Toby looked from the glass to Quentin and back. The lady in the wall had both hands pressed to the inside of the screen now, and she was mouthing something over and over, urgent, her tired eyes wet.

"What's she saying?" Toby asked. "What's she saying, Quentin?"

Quentin sank down beside him on the carpet, close to the glass, and for a long moment she didn't say anything at all. The count drifted down through the floorboards from upstairs. *Nineteen. Eighteen.* The house groaned somewhere deep in its bones, a sound like a ship in ice.

"'Get them out,'" Quentin said finally, her voice shaking. "She's saying *get them out*. She's been saying it since — Toby, that's my mother. That's my mother's face. She's been dead since I was nineteen."

The lady shook her head. Slowly. Deliberately. She pointed at herself, then drew on the glass — a year. *1991.* Then she drew a door, and a stick figure running through it, and then she drew the running figure inside a shape Toby recognized now, a shape he was starting to hate: a rectangle. A mirror. Then lines radiating out from it, like the figure was caught in a web.

"She didn't die," Toby said slowly, working it out the way you worked out a sum in school, each number landing with a little click. "She's *in there*. She ran out of Wrenfield House in 1991 and she didn't get out, she got *caught*. She's in the glass."

Quentin's face did something Toby had no word for. He was ten, and he had already seen more faces do more things in the last two weeks than most people saw in a life, and this one was new: hope and horror in the same expression, fighting each other to a draw.

* * *

They gathered everyone in the great hall, because it was the biggest room and the safest — one door, salted twice, and the great mirror covered in three bedsheets nailed to the frame, which his mother had done herself with a hammer at four in the morning, weeping and swearing and hitting the nails like each one was a promise.

Grandma sat in the wingback chair with a quilt over her knees, looking smaller than Toby had ever seen her. His mother stood with her arms crossed, which was her armor. Grandpa was upstairs, counting. *Seventeen. Sixteen.* The count had slowed, Toby noticed. It used to lose a number every night. Now it lost one every hour. Like something pacing itself. Like something saving its breath for the last part of a race.

"She's Quentin's mother," Toby said, before the adults could start talking over each other, which they always did. "The lady in the wall. She's Quentin's mom, and she's trapped in the mirrors, and she's been trying to help us this whole time. She drew me a map to the chapel. She wants us to leave."

"Sweetheart," his mother started.

"She *showed* me." His voice cracked and he hated it but he kept going, because there wasn't time to be embarrassed, there might not be time for anything. "She showed me the year. 1991. She showed me running out of the house and getting caught in the glass. Quentin knows her face. Ask her. *Ask her.*"

Everyone looked at Quentin. Quentin's jaw was tight, and her eyes were red, and she looked like someone who had been crying quietly in a hallway and had decided to stop.

"It's her," Quentin said. "It's my mother, Marion. She worked here before I was born. She left in the middle of a count and she never told me why, and she died when I was nineteen, and I buried her, I *buried her*, I stood at the grave—" Her voice caught. "But it's her. The scar. The way she holds her hands. It's her."

The silence in the great hall was the kind that has weight. The sheets over the great mirror stirred, though there was no draft.

Grandma Vivian spoke from the wingback chair, and her voice was the old voice, the iron voice, the one from before Toby had ever seen her afraid. "Marion Iversen." She said the name like she was spitting out a stone she'd been holding under her tongue for fifty years. "The nurse. The one who ran." Her eyes went to Quentin, and something moved in them that wasn't quite blame and wasn't quite anything Toby could name. "You have her face, you know."

"Grandmother," Lorna said sharply.

"It's true. She has her face." Grandma's hands twisted in the quilt. "And now she has her mother in the walls. Of course she does. This house *collects* us."

The house chose that moment to demonstrate. The floor tilted — not much, two degrees, three, the way a boat leans — and the door to the kitchen swung open all by itself and showed not the kitchen but the east staircase, going up, and at the top of the stairs a rectangle of grey light that Toby knew was a mirror, uncovered, waiting. His mother slammed the door. The floor leveled. Everyone breathed.

"The chapel," Quentin said. "My mother drew the chapel. Toby, why the chapel?"

Toby hesitated. This was the part he'd been carrying alone since two in the morning, the part that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. Because after she drew the map, the lady had drawn more. She'd drawn all of them — stick figures, Grandma and Mum and Quentin and Toby — walking to the chapel. And then she'd drawn one more figure, standing apart, facing the house. And she'd drawn a rectangle around that figure. And then she'd looked at Toby with those tired eyes and mouthed the words he could read now as easily as his own name.

*One has to stay. One has to be willing.*

He didn't want to say it. If he said it, it would be real, and if it was real, then one of the four stick figures was going to be drawn inside a rectangle, and he was ten years old and he did not want it to be anyone, he wanted everyone to walk out the front gate into the normal fog and keep walking until the house was a story they told at Christmas.

But the count came through the floor again. *Fifteen.* And the lady in the wall had carried her message for thirty-three years, alone in the glass, waiting for someone who could hear, and Toby thought: if she could wait that long, he could say one true thing out loud.

"She drew one of us staying," he said. "Facing the house. In a mirror. She said — she mouthed — *one has to be willing.*" His eyes were burning and he wiped them with his sleeve, angry at them. "Somebody has to take her place. That's the trade. That's always been the trade, isn't it? That's what Grandpa said. The glass always offers a trade."

Nobody spoke. His mother's arms were wrapped around herself now, holding her own sides, as if she was afraid she'd come apart at the seams. Grandma had gone very white, and her lips were moving, and Toby realized with a cold slide in his stomach that she was counting. Not the count from upstairs. Her own count. A private one. Fifty years of numbers.

Then Quentin knelt down in front of him, so their eyes were level, and she took both his hands, and her hands were cold but steady.

"Toby. Listen to me. You are not staying anywhere. Your mum is not staying. Your grandmother is not staying." She said each name like she was nailing it down. "Nobody here is a trade. Do you understand? Your mother spent thirty-three years in that glass finding a way to get *everybody* out. She didn't draw that picture so we'd pick someone. She drew it so we'd know the price before it was asked. That's different. That's the whole difference in the world."

"Then what do we do?" Toby asked. His voice was very small.

Quentin looked past him, at the covered mirror, at the sheets stirring in their non-draft, and Toby watched her think. He watched her the way you watch weather come in.

"She said *one has to be willing*," Quentin said slowly. "She didn't say one has to be *taken*. Willing is a door that swings both ways." And then she went quiet in a way that scared Toby more than the tilting floors, because she looked like someone who had just understood the last question on a test and didn't like any of the answers.

* * *

They left the great hall at grey noon, though the fog outside the windows made noon look like evening, and the fog was wrong in a way Toby couldn't explain to anyone who hadn't seen it: it didn't move. It stood against the glass like wool pressed to a window, and once, passing the front door, Toby had seen a shape in it, standing, patient, the way you stand outside a shop before it opens.

The lady in the wall traveled with them. Toby carried the television.

It had been his idea, and it had felt brilliant for exactly as long as it took to say it: *she can come with us, she can show us which halls are real.* His mother had carried it down from the sitting room, grunting, the cord wrapped around her arm like a snake, and now Toby walked at the front of the little procession holding the plug end in one fist like a torch, and wherever the screen faced, the lady was there, drawing arrows on the inside of the dark glass.

The house fought them the whole way.

It had stopped pretending. Doors opened onto stairwells that went down where the cellar should have been and up where the attic should have been. The corridor to the front hall stretched — Toby counted his steps, and it was ninety steps for a hallway that had always been twenty. Behind them, doors they'd already closed were open again. And everywhere, in every polished surface — the brass of the door handles, the dark of the windows, the black glass of the television — the count followed them, faint now, like a voice through water. *Fourteen. Thirteen.* Not from upstairs anymore. From *everywhere*. From the walls themselves.

"Chapel's on the grounds," his mother said, breathless, half-dragging the TV over a threshold that tried to rise under it like a living thing. "East lawn, past the yew trees, it's not even — it was a two-minute walk, when I was a girl—"

"Nothing here is two minutes anymore," Grandma said.

The lady's hand flashed on the screen. Toby read her fast now. *Left. Not the big door. The servants' door.*

"The little door," Toby said. "By the scullery. She says the big one's a trick."

It was a trick. They could all feel it — the big front hall door had begun, very quietly, to breathe, its panels swelling and settling like a chest. They went the other way, through the pantry, through the scullery that smelled of salt and old iron, and the little door opened onto the east lawn and the fog and the yew trees, and the chapel stood beyond them, small and grey and stone, its one arched window catching what little light the day had left.

They made it eleven steps across the lawn.

Then the fog closed, and the yew trees were gone, and the chapel was gone, and they were standing on the lawn in a circle of pale grass no bigger than a living room, and beyond the circle, in every direction, was Wrenfield House. Not the real one. A reflection of it, vast and dark and perfect, its hundred windows all showing the same thing: a small circle of people on a small circle of grass, seen from the inside of the glass.

The television screen lit up white. The lady was there, both palms flat, mouthing fast, urgent, over and over, and this time Toby didn't need to read her lips, because the words came up through the ground, through his shoes, through the soles of his feet and into his teeth, in a voice that was almost his mother's voice and almost Quentin's voice and almost, horribly, his own:

*Twelve.*

*Eleven.*

*Ten.*

The lady in the wall stopped mouthing. She looked at Toby — only at Toby, out of all of them — and she drew one last thing on the inside of the glass, quick and clear, the way you write something you've had thirty-three years to practice.

A stick figure. Standing in front of a rectangle. And beside it, two arrows: one pointing down, one pointing up.

*Answer it forwards,* she mouthed. *And don't look away.*

Behind them, across the lawn that was no longer a lawn, the front doors of the reflected house swung open, and something in the doorway began, very patiently, to count.
