## 1. The Wrong Twin

The urn rode in the passenger seat like a small, polite guest.

Hazel had buckled it in somewhere outside of Bakersfield—somewhere past the last stretch of orange groves and the first brown hills that meant the coast was close—and had not touched the seatbelt since. It seemed important that it stay secure. It seemed absurd that it mattered at all. Four pounds of ash and ceramic, cold through the canvas bag, and still Hazel drove the whole way with one hand hovering near it at every hard turn, as though Rosalind might be jostled, as though she could feel it.

*She can't feel anything,* Hazel told themselves for the hundredth mile. *She couldn't feel anything for fifty years.*

The rental agreement was folded in the door pocket, soft as cloth from handling. Six months on the Ravensworth house in Hollow Creek, month to month after that, signed by a lawyer named Corinne Ashe whose voice on the phone had been brisk and kind in the way people are kind when they are also counting something. The house was being sold. The estate needed an occupant until closing. Hazel had said yes before they understood they were saying yes, the way you say yes to a doctor's question you didn't hear.

They had not been back to Hollow Creek since 1989. They had not spoken to Rosalind since 2019, and even then only by letter, and even then only because their mother, dying, had made them promise.

*You'll want her when I'm gone,* their mother had said, gripping Hazel's wrist with a strength that frightened the nurses. *Twins come back around. You'll see.*

Hazel hadn't wanted her. That was the truth they'd carried across three decades and two coasts like a stone sewn into a coat lining: they had not wanted her, and now she was dead, and there would be no second chance, no reconciliation scene, no letter answered too late. There was only an urn and a funeral home called Pell & Sons, and a town that had stayed exactly the size of Hazel's memory of it, which was to say: small enough to drown in.

The sign came up out of the fog the way signs do in coastal towns—suddenly, rusted, apologetic. WELCOME TO HOLLOW CREEK. POPULATION 1,204. Someone had hung plastic flowers from the post, sun-bleached to the color of old skin.

Hazel took the turn onto Mill Road without deciding to. Their hands knew the way. That was the worst part, they thought—the body remembering what the mind had spent thirty years burying. Left at the feed store, right where the road bent around the creek itself, up the hill past the church with its leaning steeple, and there it was: the Ravensworth house, gray-shingled and patient at the top of the rise, windows dark, porch sagging gently at one corner like a mouth gone slack.

It looked smaller than it should have. Houses did, when you'd been hiding from them since childhood.

Hazel sat in the rental car in the gravel drive for a long time, engine ticking, fog pressing against the windshield from all sides like something leaning in to look.

"You're home," they said to the urn, because someone had to say something, and it wasn't going to be the house.

The urn said nothing. But the house—Hazel would swear later, standing in a courtroom of their own memory—that the house exhaled. A long settling sigh through the eaves, the sound an old building makes when it has been holding its breath.

* * *

Pell & Sons Funeral Home occupied a converted Victorian on Cypress Street, white clapboard gone ivory, a wheelchair ramp bolted over steps that had once hosted wedding parties. Inside smelled of lilies and carpet cleaner and, beneath both, faintly and irretrievably, of the thing it actually was.

The man who met Hazel in the entryway was perhaps seventy, silver-haired, wearing a suit the color of a dove's back. His name tag read DELL PELL, and his handshake was dry and gentle, the handshake of a man who had spent fifty years touching the hands of the bereaved and had learned exactly how much pressure grief could bear.

"Ms. Ravensworth," he said. "We're so sorry for your loss."

"Thank you." Hazel's voice came out flat, rehearsed. They had practiced this sentence on the drive down—I'm sorry for your loss, thank you, she was my sister—and it still sounded like lines from a play they'd never auditioned for.

"If you'll follow me, we can go over the arrangements. Your sister's—" He paused at the office door, hand on the frame, and smiled with genuine warmth. "Rosalind, we've got her settled in the viewing room whenever you're ready. She looks very peaceful. Very much herself."

Hazel stopped.

It wasn't the words themselves. It was the ease of them. Dell Pell spoke about Rosalind the way a man speaks about a neighbor he's known all his life—not a client, not a stranger's remains shipped in from Sacramento, but *Rosalind*, definite article implied, a fixture of the town the way the steeple was a fixture, the creek, the fog. As if she had died here. As if she had lived here, these fifty years, walking these streets, buying bread, growing old.

But Rosalind had left Hollow Creek at nineteen and never come back. Had lived in Phoenix, then Portland, then a series of apartments that shrank as her money did. Had died alone in a studio above a laundromat, and the police had needed four days to reach anyone, and the anyone they reached was Hazel, who stood in the doorway of that studio and saw the twin bed, the single mug, the wall calendar still turned to a month that had already ended.

Rosalind had not been *herself* anywhere in decades. Hazel knew that better than anyone alive.

Except—

"Dell," Hazel said carefully. "When did you last see my sister?"

He looked up from the folder he'd opened, and for a moment—a flicker, no longer—something moved behind his eyes. Not confusion. Recognition of the question, maybe. The look of a man hearing a word from a language he'd promised himself he'd forgotten.

"Oh, you know how it is in a town like this," he said, and his voice was smooth again, warm again, and the flicker was gone so completely Hazel wondered if they'd imagined it. "Everyone knows everyone. Sit, sit. Let's talk about Thursday."

They talked about Thursday. Hazel signed things. The service would be small—Dell said it gently, the way you tell someone a tumor is operable—Thursday at eleven, the church on the hill, graveside after, weather permitting. Hazel asked about cremation paperwork; Dell produced it, already filled out, already dated, in a handwriting that was not his own. Hazel noticed this and did not say anything. There were so many things, they were beginning to understand, that one did not say anything about in Hollow Creek.

At the door, Dell shook their hand again and held it a half-second longer than the first time.

"It's good to have a Ravensworth back in the house," he said. "It's been too long. She always said someone would come back."

"My sister hasn't been here since 1989," Hazel said. "She told me herself. Many times."

Something happened to Dell Pell's face then that Hazel would spend the rest of the week trying to name. It was not guilt. It was older than guilt, and more patient. It was the expression of a man watching water rise around his ankles and knowing, with terrible calm, exactly which river it had come from.

"No," he agreed softly. "I suppose she wouldn't have. Not the one you knew."

He let go of Hazel's hand. The door closed behind them with a click that sounded, in the fog-thick street, very much like a lock turning.

* * *

That night Hazel slept—or lay down and let the dark happen to them—in their childhood bedroom, in a sleeping bag because the mattress had rotted through sometime during the Clinton administration. The room was exactly as they'd left it at fifteen: band posters gone brown at the edges, a bookshelf of paperbacks swollen with damp, the window seat where they used to sit and watch the fog come up the hill like a tide with intentions.

And in the corner, the chalkboard.

Their mother had never taken it down. The green board with its wooden tray, the stubs of chalk worn to nubs, and on it, untouched by forty years of dust that lay thick on everything else in the room, a drawing.

Hazel had forgotten the drawing. That was a lie they told themselves; they had buried it, which is different, which takes work. Two stick figures holding hands under a scribbled sun. One tall, one small. Over the small one, in a child's careful block letters: ROSALIND'S FRIEND. And beside it, smaller, almost shy: SHE NEVER LEFT.

Hazel stood looking at it for a long time, the flashlight trembling slightly in their grip.

They had drawn that when they were eight. They remembered the afternoon of it—the long rain, the power out, the two of them playing in this room while the storm leaned on the house. Except they hadn't been playing with Rosalind. Rosalind had been downstairs with their mother. Hazel had been alone, drawing, talking aloud to someone the way only children and certain kinds of lonely adults are permitted to, and the friend had been there, the way the friend was always there, patient and sun-warm and slightly wrong in ways Hazel's eight-year-old mind had declined to examine.

*What does she look like?* their mother had asked once, indulgent, drying dishes.

*Tall,* Hazel had said. *Like me but taller. She wears a yellow dress even when it's raining. She says she's been here longer than us.*

Their mother had laughed. Everyone had laughed. That was the arrangement with imaginary friends: the adults smiled, the child kept the secret, and everyone agreed not to notice what the child was actually saying.

Hazel clicked off the flashlight. In the sudden dark, the chalkboard was a pale rectangle, and the two figures seemed to stand out from it, and the words SHE NEVER LEFT seemed less like a caption than a sentence being served.

From downstairs, distinctly, came the sound of the front porch step creaking under weight.

Hazel stood absolutely still. The house ticked and settled around them. Fog pressed at the window glass. Somewhere down the hill, the creek ran over its stones, patient as ever, keeping whatever it kept.

"Just raccoons," Hazel said aloud, to the dark, to themselves, in the firm voice of a person listing evidence. "Old houses make noise. Old houses are full of animals."

The house was quiet for the rest of the night. But in the morning, when Hazel went out to the car for the urn—they had not yet decided where she should rest, and some part of them suspected the decision had already been made long ago, by other parties—they found the porch swept clean.

Not clean the way a broom leaves a floor. Clean the way a host prepares a room. The dead leaves and gull droppings of decades gone, the boards pale and bare, and in the center of the porch, drawn in white chalk that had no business surviving a coastal night's damp, a hopscotch grid.

Ten squares, numbered in a child's uneven hand. The chalk was fresh. The chalk was bright. And at the top square, where a child would write the prize, the thing you hopped toward, someone had written a single word:

**HOME.**

Hazel stood on their own porch with the urn heavy against their chest and did not move for a long time. Below the hill, the fog was lifting off the town, and Hollow Creek emerged roof by roof into the gray morning, and every window of every house, Hazel could not help noticing, seemed angled toward the Ravensworth place on the rise. Watching. Waiting to see whether they would pick up the chalk.

They did not. They stepped over the grid, carefully, the way you step over a threshold, the way you step over a grave, and went inside, and locked both locks, and told themselves the shaking in their hands was the cold.

Behind them, on the porch, in air that had not stirred all morning, the chalk dust rose gently from the boards and hung there, suspended, as if the word were considering rewriting itself.
