## 3. What the Chair Remembers

I didn't decide to sit in it. That's the part I keep trying to explain to myself, and the explanation keeps coming back wrong. I went down to the cellar with my phone flashlight and the ledger under my arm, and the chair was there in the middle of the floor, and my legs simply folded, the way they do when you've been standing at a graveside too long. My body made the decision. The house had been waiting for exactly that.

The wood was warm.

That's the first thing I want you to understand, because it's the thing that undid me. Not cold, not damp, not the cellar chill that had been crawling up my ankles since I opened the door. Warm. Body-warm. As if someone had just gotten up from it, or as if it had been holding a shape for someone for a hundred and forty years and finally, finally had something to hold again.

The ledger slid off my knees. I heard it hit the dirt floor and I didn't reach for it, because by then the room had stopped being the room.

* * *

There were nine of them. I knew that number before I knew anything else — nine founders, nine families, nine names I'd grown up hearing on street signs and plaques: Harrow, Delacroix, Osei, Villalobos. My own name among them, which should have surprised me and didn't, because some part of me had always known we weren't from *here* so much as we were *of* here.

They stood in a circle in this same cellar, except it wasn't a cellar yet. It was a foundation. Raw earth, lantern light, June air moving through the open stairwell above them like breath. And in the center of the circle, two chairs.

Not one. Two.

They faced each other, close enough that the people sitting in them could have held hands if they'd reached. One was plain pine. The other was this one — dark wood, carved along the arms with a pattern I recognized now as writing, though not in any alphabet I knew. I understood it anyway. That's the horror of borrowed memory: it comes pre-translated. The carvings said *two made one* over and over, the way a hymn repeats its chorus until the words stop meaning anything and start meaning everything.

A woman sat in the pine chair. I couldn't see her face — the memory kept her turned away, the way dreams protect you from the worst of it — but I knew her hands. Long fingers, ink-stained. A ledger rested on her lap, and she was writing in it even now, even here, documenting her own unmaking. *Documentation is a kind of prayer,* she'd written somewhere in those pages, and sitting inside her memory I finally understood it wasn't a metaphor. Writing it down was how you held the door open. She was keeping the ritual legible so it could be finished later, by whoever came after, whenever the house grew hungry enough.

And across from her, in the dark chair —

Me. Or the shape of me. The memory wouldn't give me a face either, but it gave me the feeling of being looked at, and the feeling was unbearable, because whoever sat there wasn't a stranger. It was the other half of something. A twin. There had always been twins in this town's founding; that was the whole architecture of it. Nine families, and in each one, a pair. You don't build a binding on one soul. One soul is a closed door. Two souls are an archway, and you can walk through an archway.

The woman began to speak the words. I felt them move through my borrowed throat, syllables that tasted like iron and honey, and around the circle the other eight joined in, and the archway began to open. I can't describe what that felt like except to say it felt like the moment before a sneeze stretched out forever — every nerve in the body leaning toward a completion that never comes.

Because it never came.

Halfway through the ninth verse, the woman in the pine chair stopped writing. Stopped speaking. The memory shudders there, skips like a scratched disc, and what I got instead were fragments: shouting above us, through the floorboards. A name being called. Someone's child, sick, dying, maybe already dead. The circle breaking. Lanterns swinging. Hands pulling the woman up out of the chair while she clawed against them, still chanting, still trying to finish, and the dark chair emptying behind her as her twin rose too, and the archway slamming shut mid-word.

Mid-binding. They left it half-done. Nine verses, and they got through four and a half, and then life — ordinary, stupid, grieving human life — interrupted, and they walked away from an open wound and called it a foundation.

The house has been hungry ever since.

* * *

I came back to myself on the cellar floor with my cheek against the dirt and the taste of iron and honey in my mouth. My phone was dead. Not drained — dead, screen cracked down the middle as if something had pressed it flat. The ledger lay open beside me, and when I looked down at the page it had fallen open to, the handwriting had changed. The old ink-stained hand was gone. The words were mine. I recognized my own slanted capitals, the way I cross my sevens.

*I waited for the one who left.*

I scrambled backward until my spine hit the stairs. My heart was going so hard I could hear it in my ears, feel it in my teeth. This was the part where I told myself I'd imagined it. Grief does this. Sleep deprivation does this. I'd catalogued all the reasonable explanations by day three of photographing the chair, and I recited them now like a rosary, and none of them explained why the chair was warm, why my mouth tasted like a ceremony from 1887, why the ledger was writing itself in my hand.

Then I thought about Rosalind, and everything reasonable fell away at once.

She died eleven days ago. Single-car accident on Holloway Road, the curve by the water tower, at 11:47 at night. The police report said she lost control. The report said there was no evidence of braking. Rosalind drove that road ten thousand times; she used to take it at speed with her knee and laugh about it. She did not lose control. Something took it from her, and I had spent eleven days not letting myself finish that sentence, and now the sentence finished itself:

*The house chose her.*

Not me. That was the thing that made me sick, there on the stairs, hugging my knees like a child. The house had wanted me. I was the twin who left — I moved to the city at nineteen, I missed holidays, I let months go between calls, I was the open door, the absent half of the archway, the vacancy the ritual had been waiting a century and a half to fill. Every photograph in this house had been quietly correcting itself toward me. The chair moving closer to the camera in each new picture wasn't haunting. It was *approach*. It was invitation. It was a hand extended across a table, patient beyond all human patience.

But I didn't come home. So the house settled for the next best thing. The other half of me. The twin who stayed.

Rosalind's ashes were upstairs in my grandmother's bedroom, in an urn my aunt had chosen because it was "tasteful." Eleven days ago my sister drove past this house on a road she'd driven her whole life, and something in the ground beneath it leaned toward her like a plant toward light, and took the wheel.

I have never in my life been a violent person. I want that on the record. But I got up off those stairs and I kicked the chair — full force, both hands braced on the wall, boot heel into its carved arm — and the carvings said *two made one* and the wood didn't even scuff, and somewhere below the floor, below the foundation, below the raw earth the founders had poured their half-finished prayer into, something laughed.

Not cruelly. That was the worst part. It laughed the way family laughs. Warm. Welcoming. Like I'd finally come home.

I picked up the ledger. If documentation was prayer, then the book was the altar, and altars can be burned. Four and a half verses bound this house; somewhere in these pages was the rest, and somewhere in these pages, maybe, was the way to unmake it. I memorized as I read, every inversion, every reversal, my lips moving in the dark like the ink-stained woman's, and outside the small high window I could see the sun sliding toward the horizon, and I counted days on my fingers like a child doing arithmetic he doesn't want to do.

Solstice was in six days.

And the last page of the ledger, in fresh ink, in my own handwriting, had added a single line since I'd come down the stairs:

*Bring your sister. We only need one of you.*
