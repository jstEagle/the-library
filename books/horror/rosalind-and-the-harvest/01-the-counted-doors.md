## 1. The Counted Doors

The bus let Wendell off at the bottom of Main Street at four in the afternoon, and the first thing she noticed about Hollow Creek was that the town did not look up when she arrived.

She had lived in enough small places to know how a newcomer registers: the pause of hands in gardens, the slow turn of heads behind screen doors, the way conversation dips and swells around a stranger like water around a stone. Hollow Creek gave her none of that. A man raking leaves kept raking. Two women on the post office steps kept talking—though their voices dropped so low she caught only the shape of the words, not the words themselves—and neither glanced her way. A dog watched her from a porch with mild, incurious eyes, as if strangers came through often enough to be boring.

Wendell Eriksen had spent thirty-one years perfecting the art of not being looked at. She dressed in colors the color of nothing—oatmeal, slate, the gray-green of old money's envelopes. She spoke softly and only when spoken to. She had chosen teaching, absurdly, because it was the one profession where being overlooked was an asset; children remembered lessons better than they remembered teachers. In eleven years she had worked at five schools, and in every one of them she had been the teacher whose name parents got wrong at conferences, whose face did not surface when former students tried to recall their favorites.

She had thought of it as safety. She had never quite admitted it was also hunger.

Hollow Creek, then, felt less like a new town than like a mirror held up to her own strategy. The place was prosperous—that was the word the job posting had used, *prosperous*, twice, as if apologizing for it—and prosperity here wore its collar buttoned all the way up. The houses along Main were large and beautifully maintained and almost entirely shuttered against the October light. Lawns were mowed to the millimeter. Porches were swept. And yet there were no bicycles abandoned in driveways, no toys in yards, no jackets slung over porch rails. It was a town that had been tidied the way a hotel room is tidied: thoroughly, professionally, by someone who wanted no evidence that anyone actually lived there.

The school sat at the top of a low hill at the end of Main Street, and it was the only building in town that seemed to be waiting for something. Wendell shouldered her bag and walked toward it, past a hardware store whose window display had been arranged with museum care, past a diner called the Founding Table where every booth was full and every booth was silent. She heard forks. She did not hear voices.

*Prosperous but quiet,* she wrote later, in the notebook she kept for observations she didn't yet understand. *Like a house where someone has just told everyone to hush.*

---

Principal Alden Marsh met her in the front office of Hollow Creek Consolidated School, and his handshake was warm and dry and lasted exactly one second longer than comfortable.

"Miss Eriksen. We're glad to have you." He said it the way a man says *we're glad to have you* when he means *we're glad to have anyone*, and Wendell recognized the desperation under the courtesy because she'd heard it from four other principals in four other towns. Rural districts bled teachers. Nobody wanted to move somewhere with one grocery store and no cell signal past the ridge.

"I'm glad to be here," she said, which was true in a way she couldn't have explained yet.

Marsh was perhaps fifty-five, tall without being imposing, with the particular posture of a man who has spent decades standing in doorways watching hallways. His office was immaculate except for one detail: a small leather-bound book that lay on the corner of his desk, closed, with a fountain pen resting across its cover like a paperweight that had opinions.

"You'll want to see your room," he said. "Fourth grade. Nineteen students. Good group. Quiet."

"Quiet," Wendell repeated.

"They always are, here." He smiled, and the smile reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Best-behaved district I've ever served. You'll see."

He walked her through the building himself, and Wendell catalogued it the way she catalogued everything: wide corridors, waxed floors the color of honey, bulletin boards with construction-paper cornucopias and hand-traced turkeys. The classrooms were bright. The library was better stocked than any rural school library she'd seen—new spines, uncracked, arranged in perfect rows. There was a computer lab with machines still in their plastic. Everything was funded. Everything was fed.

And everything was empty in a way that had nothing to do with occupancy. The halls smelled faintly of floor wax and, beneath it, something older and sweeter that she couldn't name—like apples left too long in a cellar, like hay going soft.

"Your classroom," Marsh said, opening a door onto a room with windows facing west, over the playground, over the hills beyond. "Nineteen desks. Seventeen will fill, most days."

Wendell turned. "I'm sorry?"

"The attendance runs about seventeen out of nineteen." He said it lightly, the way a man mentions weather. "It varies. Some years eighteen. Never nineteen, if you follow me. Don't take it personally—it's not your teaching. It's just how the number settles."

"How the number settles," Wendell said.

"These hills," Marsh said, spreading his hands, as if the hills explained everything, which she would come to learn they did, though not in the way he meant. "Families come and go. Farm kids stay home some days for harvest. You'll have your roster Monday."

He showed her the staff lounge, the gymnasium, the boiler room door he touched briefly with two fingers as they passed, a gesture so quick and habitual that Wendell might have missed it if she hadn't spent her whole life studying the ways people touch things they're afraid of.

At the end of the tour, he checked his watch—a heavy gold thing, older than him—and said, "I'll walk you out. I do my rounds at six."

"Rounds?"

"Insurance," he said, and locked the front doors behind them with a key from a ring of maybe forty keys, each labeled in faded typewriter font.

---

Her rental house stood three streets down from the school, on a lane called Voss Road, in a house that belonged to a woman named Agnes Voss, whom Wendell would not meet for another week and who was, even now, ninety-one years old and sitting in a parlor across town deciding things. The rent had been suspiciously reasonable. The house was furnished—good furniture, walnut and brass, dust-sheeted until two days ago. On the kitchen table someone had left a welcome basket: apples, a jar of honey, a loaf of bread still warm when she pressed it, which meant someone had been in the house within the hour.

There was no note.

Wendell ate standing at the counter, looking out the window at the yard, which was fenced in white picket and utterly without weeds. Beyond the fence, the hills went gold and violet in the failing light, and the town below her made no sound at all. No dogs barking. No cars. She could hear the wind in the maples and nothing else, and the silence had a texture to it—not empty silence but *held* silence, the silence of a room where people are listening.

She had grown up in noise. Her mother had filled every room of every apartment with radio and television and running conversation, talk that went around and around and never landed anywhere, and Wendell understood now—understood with the clarity of thirty-one years and one dead mother—that the noise had been a wall. Her mother had built it brick by brick, broadcast by broadcast, and Wendell had inherited both the wall and the instinct to hide behind it. They had moved eleven times in her childhood. Always suddenly. Always at night, sometimes, the car packed before dawn. Her mother never explained, and Wendell had learned not to ask, and the not-asking had hardened into a personality: a woman who packed light, signed short leases, and never fully unpacked.

She unpacked now. All of it. She hung her coat in the closet and put her books on the shelves and set her mother's photograph on the mantel, and when she stepped back and looked at the room with her things in it, she felt something she had not felt in any of the eleven apartments or five schools or three states of her adult life.

She felt like she'd been here before.

Not the house—the feeling. The feeling of arriving somewhere that had already agreed to her presence, that had made space, that had left bread on the table. Towns didn't do that. Towns made you earn belonging over decades, and usually you never earned it at all. But Hollow Creek had set a place for her the way you set a place for family, and the wrongness of that should have frightened her, and part of her registered that it should, and the larger part of her—the tired, hungry, invisible part—simply exhaled.

*I'm home,* she thought, and immediately distrusted the thought, and wrote it down anyway.

---

At six o'clock, from her kitchen window, she saw Principal Marsh come out of the school at the top of the hill, and she saw him begin to walk the building.

She told herself she wasn't watching. She was washing a dish. The dish happened to face the window. But she watched, and what she saw was this: Marsh walking the perimeter of the schoolhouse slowly, counting under his breath—or moving his lips as if counting—and pausing at each exterior door. At each door he stopped, laid his palm flat against the wood, waited for something, and then wrote in the leather book he'd carried out with him. Door after door after door. The east entrance. The gym doors. The loading dock. The little blue door near the boiler room, which he touched longer than the others, and at which he stood so still that in the dusk he became part of the wall.

There were more doors than a school that size needed. She counted as he counted: fourteen exterior doors on a building that should have had six.

When he finished, he stood in the middle of the parking lot, turned once, all the way around, surveying the building the way a shepherd surveys a fold, and then he raised his head and looked directly at her window.

Three streets away. Dusk falling. He could not possibly have seen her; she had not turned on a light. But he looked at her window the way a man looks at a window he knows is occupied, and he nodded—once, courteously, the nod you give a neighbor—and put the book under his arm and walked away into the dark, and the streetlights of Hollow Creek came on all at once, every one of them, as if switched by a single hand.

Wendell stood at the sink with the water running over her hands long after the dish was clean.

*Insurance,* he'd said. People insure against loss. You count what you have so you know when something is missing. She knew that arithmetic intimately; her mother had counted too, in her way—counted the exits in every restaurant, counted the cars parked on the street below their windows, counted the days since the last time they'd moved, as if some clock were always running. Wendell had thought her mother paranoid. Standing in the held silence of Hollow Creek, watching a principal bless fourteen doors at dusk, she wondered for the first time whether her mother had ever been paranoid at all, or merely from somewhere.

She went to bed early. The house creaked in ways houses creak, settling, cooling, ordinary. She lay in the dark and listened to it and found the sounds comforting rather than strange, which was itself strange, and she fell asleep faster than she had fallen asleep in years, deeply, dreamlessly—

—except that at the very bottom of sleep, in the last warm inch before morning, she dreamed of a porch. Not this porch. An older porch, gray wood, a swing on chains. Someone was sitting on the swing, and the someone was a girl, and the girl had a crooked braid, and the girl said, in a voice like a jump rope slapping pavement:

*You came back.*

Wendell woke at dawn with the words still in her ears and no memory of the dream, only the residue of it, the way you retain the temperature of a hand after it lets go. She showered. She dressed in oatmeal and slate. She ate an apple from the basket—sweet, dense, the best apple of her life—and she walked up Voss Road and up the hill to the school, and the town again declined to look at her, and she again felt, shamefully, gratefully, at home.

Her classroom smelled of chalk and apples. Nineteen desks stood in rows. On the chalkboard, in handwriting that was not hers, someone had already written the day's date and the words:

**Welcome, Miss Eriksen.**

Below that, smaller, in the same hand:

**Seventeen today.**

She stood very still in the empty room while the buses groaned up the hill outside, and she told herself it was the custodian, of course, a welcoming tradition, a bit of local charm. She picked up the eraser to clear the board, and paused, and did not erase it. Something in her—the same something that had slept dreamlessly and woken with a voice in its ear—wanted the words to stay.

The first bell rang. Feet thundered in the hall, seventeen sets of them, and Wendell Eriksen, invisible for thirty-one years, went to stand in her doorway to greet them, and the last thing she noticed, before the children came pouring in with their round faces and their careful manners, was that the classroom had a second door.

A small one. In the back corner. Painted the same color as the wall.

She was almost certain it hadn't been there yesterday.
