## 13. Truth

Rosalind came to the kitchen window at dusk, as she always did now, standing in the yard with her crooked braid and her patient, terrible stillness. Wendell had stopped locking the door against her weeks ago. It hadn't helped anyway; Rosalind didn't use doors.

"You've decided," Rosalind said through the glass.

"I haven't."

"You have. You just haven't said it out loud." She tilted her head, and the gesture was so exactly the one Wendell had invented at seven years old—borrowed from a girl she'd never met, a girl who had been erased before Wendell was born—that Wendell's chest ached with it. "You're going to keep it. The truth. Fold it up small and put it somewhere no one can find it. That's what you do. That's what your mother taught you. That's what this town taught you before she ever could."

Wendell set down the mug of tea she wasn't drinking. "I'm trying to save Theo. I don't need a lecture on my character flaws from—"

"From what?" Rosalind smiled, and there was nothing cruel in it, which made it worse. "From the thing you made out of me? Say it. Imaginary friend. You can say it now. We both know I was never imaginary, but you built me anyway, didn't you? A friend who couldn't leave. A friend who couldn't tell anyone else about you. A friend who kept things in." She pressed one palm flat against the windowpane. Her hand left no fog on the glass. "They love you here, Wendell. The casseroles, the kindness, Mrs. Pruett's pies. They love you the way you love a lock—because you keep things in."

The words went into Wendell like a splinter working toward bone.

"That's not fair," she said.

"No," Rosalind agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about 1974 was fair either. But you wanted fair, and instead you got loved, and you're going to have to choose which one you can live with." She stepped back from the window, and the dusk took her by degrees, braid first, then the pale smudge of her face. "Test them. One neighbor. One truth, told plain. See what love does when you stop being a lock."

* * *

Wendell didn't sleep. She sat at her kitchen table with the ledger photocopies spread out under the lamp—the names, the harvests traded for them, the neat administrative columns that turned children into line items—and she thought about locks.

Her mother had been a lock. Thirty years of moving towns every time someone asked too many questions, thirty years of teaching Wendell to answer everything with a smile and nothing: *We're private people, honey. Some things are family business.* Wendell had thought it was temperament. Shyness, maybe, or damage. She had built a whole self out of it—a woman who could stand in front of a classroom and still be nobody, a woman whose invisibility felt like a talent rather than a wound.

Now she knew it was engineering. Her grandmother had smuggled a stolen name out of Hollow Creek inside a seven-year-old's imaginary friend, and the price of carrying it was never, ever being seen.

And the town had offered her the same bargain within two weeks of her arrival. Casseroles on the porch. Mrs. Pruett waving her over for coffee. The job offer from the school board—*a real future here, Ms. Eriksen, we think you're one of us.* And then Dale Hutchins, the feed store man, saying with a warm hand on her shoulder: *Whatever you found in those yearbooks, some doors are better left counted and closed.*

Complicity dressed as community. Silence wearing an apron and bringing pie.

At four in the morning she made her choice, and it surprised her how easy it was once she stopped trying to be clever about it. No strategy. No evidence folder. Just the truth, spoken plainly, to one person, and whatever came after.

She chose Maren Okafor.

Maren lived three houses down, a widow who ran the pharmacy and had lost her husband the ordinary way, cancer, eight years back. She was the one who'd brought the first casserole. She was the one who'd told Wendell, on her second week in town, *You look like you're holding your breath, hon. Nobody holds their breath forever in Hollow Creek.* Of everyone, Maren seemed most like a person who might already know something and hate it.

Wendell walked over at seven, while the mist was still sitting in the hollows between houses and the town smelled like woodsmoke and cut hay—prosperous, peaceful, fed.

Maren answered the door in her pharmacy vest, coffee in hand, and smiled the smile Wendell had come to dread because it was genuinely kind. "Ms. Eriksen. Early for house calls. Everything all right?"

"No," Wendell said. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to let me finish before you decide what to do about it."

Something flickered behind Maren's eyes—not surprise. Recognition. That was the first crack in Wendell's heart, though she didn't feel it break yet. Maren stepped aside and let her in, and they sat at the kitchen table where the casserole dishes had been returned clean, and Wendell put the truth on the table like a stone.

"Theo Marsh is going to disappear," she said. "Not run away. Not transfer. He's going to be taken, the way nineteen other children have been taken since 1974, one every few years, quiet as paperwork. This town trades its children for its harvests. There's a ledger in the school basement with their names in it, and payments recorded beside them—good soil, full classrooms, money that doesn't run out. Rosalind Vane was the first. Your records say she never existed. She existed. I've seen her. She stands on my porch every evening, and she is thirty years dead, and this town killed her and called it prosperity."

Maren's coffee cup sat untouched, steam rising off it in a thin thread.

"My grandmother signed the first page," Wendell went on, because she had promised herself she would tell all of it, even the parts that shamed her. "She tried to strike Rosalind's name out and couldn't, so she hid it instead—in me, in a child's imaginary friend, and my mother ran with it for thirty years and died tired. And now Agnes Voss is about to sign away the deed, and there are three days until the new moon, and Theo is due. And every person in this town who has ever brought me a pie or shaken my hand or told me I belong here knows, or half-knows, or has decided not to know. So. Now you know. What happens next is yours."

The silence afterward lasted long enough for the kettle on the stove to click itself off.

Maren looked at her, and Wendell watched her try—she would always believe this—to watch her genuinely try to find a version of the world where what Wendell had said wasn't true. She saw the moment Maren failed. She saw the moment Maren chose anyway.

"Oh, honey," Maren said softly. "You shouldn't have said that out loud."

"Maren—"

"It's not—" Maren stood, wrapped her arms around herself, and for one naked instant her face was grief, pure and old, the face of a woman who had been carrying something heavy for decades and had just been asked to set it down in front of a witness. Then it closed, smooth as a drawer sliding shut. "Theo's parents are good people. The school is a good school. Whatever you think you found down in that basement, you're new here, and new people misread things. I'd hate for you to hurt yourself. Professionally, I mean. People talk."

"They talk," Wendell said. "I know. I've heard them talking about me since the day I arrived. Every kind word had a hinge in it."

Maren walked her to the door. At the threshold she touched Wendell's arm, and her hand was trembling, and that almost undid Wendell more than anything else—the trembling, the terrible human trembling of a woman doing something she hated because she believed she had no other choice.

"You seem like a good teacher," Maren said. "Go home, Ms. Eriksen. Don't come to the store for a few days. Let things settle."

Wendell walked home through the mist. Halfway there she realized she was crying, and she couldn't have said whether it was heartbreak or relief, and then she understood it was both, in exactly equal measure, and that this was what liberation actually felt like: not lightness but amputation. Clean, necessary, and bleeding.

Behind her, Maren's porch light stayed on a long time. Watching her go. Or watching the road, for whoever Maren needed to call.

* * *

By morning, her classroom was stripped.

Wendell stood in the doorway of Room 4 with her keys still in her hand and understood, with a strange floating calm, that she had expected this and had somehow still not prepared for it. The desks were there. The chairs were there. But everything that was hers was gone—the posters she'd put up, the reading corner cushions, the jar of pencils with the students' names taped to them, the wall of Theo's drawings she'd been quietly collecting since September because he drew the eyeless girl every day and she couldn't bear to throw them away. Even the attendance chart. Even the little paper suns the kids had made the first week, each one with a word written inside: *kind*, *funny*, *loud*, *mine*.

Gone. As if the room had been professionally un-loved overnight.

A yellow sticky note sat on the empty desk at the front, square and precise:

*Ms. Eriksen — Please collect personal effects from the office. Your contract has been voided per Section 12(b), grounds: conduct detrimental to the welfare of the district. Final pay issued Friday. — R. Aldous, Principal*

Section 12(b). She'd signed it without reading it, like everyone signs everything, like this whole town signed everything—names on paper, names having weight, names given away.

In the office, a single cardboard box waited on the counter. Inside: her spare sweater, a photo frame, the novel she'd been reading aloud to the class—*Charlotte's Web*, bookmark still in place at Templeton's triumph—and a sealed envelope with her final check. Someone had packed her things carefully. Tenderly, even. That was the part that made her laugh, alone in the empty office, a short ugly sound with nothing behind it. They were gentle. They were gentle the way you're gentle closing a door on a room you don't want to hear anymore.

Principal Aldous appeared in his doorway across the hall. He looked like he hadn't slept either, and he held his counting book against his chest the way another man might hold a Bible.

"You tested them," he said. Not accusatory. Almost admiring.

"I tested one."

"One's enough. Word travels fast in a town that doesn't say anything." He came closer, lowered his voice. "For what it's worth, I argued against voiding the contract. I lost. I usually lose lately." His thumb moved along the edge of the book, back and forth, a man soothing himself with inventory. "Three days, Ms. Eriksen. After the new moon, if you're still here, we can talk about reinstatement. Things get simpler after a collection. They always do."

"Is that what you tell yourself? That they get simpler?"

He flinched. Actually flinched, a small involuntary motion, and for a moment she saw the steward underneath the administrator—a man who had inherited a mouth and spent his life feeding it so it wouldn't eat him.

"I tell myself I count the doors," he said, "and the doors stay counted, and nobody else gets taken early. That's the whole of my religion. It's a small one. It fits in a book." He stepped back into his office. "Take care of yourself, Ms. Eriksen. Genuinely. I mean that in the only way left to me."

* * *

She carried the box home through streets that had already changed temperature toward her. Mrs. Pruett was watering her mums and did not wave. Two men loading feed sacks outside Hutchins' store paused to watch her pass, unhurried, their faces pleasant and absolutely blank, and one of them touched his cap—an old courtesy that felt, today, like a warning shot fired politely into the air.

Let them look, Wendell thought, and was startled by the ferocity of it. Let them see me. That's the whole point. That's what none of you can afford.

At home, Rosalind was waiting on the porch steps, sitting properly, feet together, like a child called to a photograph. The morning light went straight through her shoulders.

"Well?" she said.

"They stripped my classroom. Voided my contract. My neighbor won't look at me." Wendell set the box down on the boards and sat beside her, close enough that if Rosalind had been warm, their arms would have touched. "You were right. They love a lock."

"And?"

Wendell looked out at the town—at the fat fields, the tidy roofs, the church steeple white against the ridge, all of it grown fat on a ledger of children—and felt the strangest thing rise in her, which was not despair. It was space. Thirty years of held breath, finally leaving her body.

"And now I'm free," she said. "Nobody's paying me to stay quiet. Nobody's pretending. There's nothing left to take from me except what matters, and what matters was never theirs." She turned to Rosalind, and her voice didn't shake. "Two days until the new moon. Agnes hasn't signed. Theo is still sleeping in his bed—I checked this morning. So we stop testing and start building. Get me the janitor. Get me the principal's schedule. Get me every name in that ledger, because in forty-eight hours, this town is going to hear all of them read out loud, in the basement, in front of the thing that took you—and I want you standing next to me when it does."

Rosalind looked at her for a long moment. Then she smiled—not the patient, terrible smile from the window, but something younger, something that belonged to a girl with a jump rope and a library card, a girl who had once been real enough to lose.

"There she is," Rosalind said. "The granddaughter Edith promised me."

Down the street, faint but unmistakable, the humming behind the school walls had begun again—and this time, it was singing words.
