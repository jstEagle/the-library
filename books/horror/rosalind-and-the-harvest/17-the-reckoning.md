## 17. The Reckoning

The basement of Hollow Creek School smelled like every basement in every school I'd ever known—boiler dust and floor wax and the ghost of decades of lunches—but tonight it smelled like something under that too. Something older. Wet earth. Turned soil.

We came down at eleven o'clock, an hour before midnight, because Agnes Voss said the reckoning had to begin while the day still had a finger hooked into it. She walked ahead of us on her cane, ninety-one years old and steadier on those stairs than any of us, as if she'd been rehearsing this descent for fifty years. Maybe she had. The principal—Hollis, I had to keep reminding myself to call him Hollis now, not *the principal*, because tonight he was just a man with a confession in his mouth like a stone he'd swallowed whole—carried the ledger wrapped in a wool blanket, the way you carry a thing that might wake up.

The janitor, Mr. Pruitt, carried a folding chair for Agnes and said nothing at all. He hadn't said much since he'd unlocked the boiler room door with a key on a chain around his neck, a key I'd watched him touch every morning for two months without ever seeing him use it. He'd been down here before. He'd been down here in 1974, when he was seventeen and mopping floors after the harvest dance, when he'd heard the humming start and the doors begin to count.

The room beyond the boiler was small and wrong. Not wrong the way a horror movie is wrong—no dripping walls, no impossible geometry. Wrong the way a room is wrong when it has been *kept*. The concrete floor was swept clean. A single bulb burned overhead without flickering. And on a plain wooden table, in the exact center of the room, sat a space shaped exactly like a ledger.

Hollis unwrapped the book and set it down, and the room exhaled.

I felt it against my skin, a pressure change, the way air moves before a storm. Behind me, Theo's mother made a small sound—I'd almost forgotten she was there, standing by the stairs with her arms wrapped around herself, because I had told her that if she wanted her son back, this was where he would come back from—and Pruitt put a hand on her shoulder without looking away from the table.

The ledger was open to the first page. It had been open to the first page since I'd found it three nights ago, wedged behind a false panel in the records cabinet, and in three nights it had not gathered a single speck of dust.

*In consideration of the harvests, the health, and the fullness of these classrooms, the founding families of Hollow Creek do covenant—*

And below the covenant language, the names. Columns of them. Dates beside each one. 1974. 1979. 1984. Every few years, one name, entered in a hand that changed over the decades but never changed its carefulness. Whoever wrote these entries always understood what they were writing.

Beside the first name—*Rosalind Vane, taken in payment of the founding debt*—there was a smudge. A correction attempted and abandoned. My grandmother's ink, dragged through the letters until they bled, and then nothing. She'd tried to strike the entry and the ledger had refused her, and so she'd done the only other thing she could think of: she'd smuggled the name out. Folded it into her daughter's imagination. Carried it across the county line inside a seven-year-old girl's invented friend, because no one counts what a child makes up.

"We should begin," Agnes said.

Her voice didn't shake. I kept waiting for it to shake, and it never did, and somehow that was worse than shaking would have been. Shaking is a body arguing with itself. Agnes's body had finished arguing decades ago.

Hollis cleared his throat. "There's a—" He stopped. Started again. "There's an order to it. My father showed me. Confession first, then the names, then—" He looked at me. "Then whatever happens."

"Then whatever happens," I agreed.

He stepped forward to the table, and I watched a man prepare to unmake himself.

* * *

"I am Marcus Hollis," he said, "son of Dale Hollis, son of Everett Hollis, steward of the count." His voice echoed off the low ceiling and came back flat, absorbed, as if the room were drinking it. "Since 1974, my family has kept the inventory of this building. We counted the doors each night so the covenant would know its holdings were intact. We scheduled the collections. We filed the transfers. We told the parents their children had moved away, and we told ourselves it was mercy, and then we told ourselves it was duty, and then we stopped telling ourselves anything at all."

Theo's mother made the sound again, louder.

"I collected four," Hollis said. "Four children, in twenty-two years. I knew their names. I knew which desk they sat in. I signed the paperwork that erased them, and I went home and ate dinner, and in the morning I counted the doors." His hands were flat on the table on either side of the ledger, and I saw that they were trembling now—the stone finally cracking open. "I am guilty. Not of the bargain. Of the keeping of it. I confess it here, in front of witnesses, and I ask—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I don't ask forgiveness. I ask to be recorded saying it. That's all testimony is. Someone hearing you say the true thing out loud."

"It's enough," Agnes said. "It's more than most of us ever gave."

She rose from the folding chair without using her cane, which frightened me more than anything else that night, and stood across the table from Hollis, and looked down at the ledger the way you look at a grave you dug yourself.

"My name is Agnes Voss," she said. "My sister married a man named Vane, and in the autumn of 1974 they had a daughter, eight years old, who liked to jump rope double-time and read past her bedtime and draw horses that looked mostly like dogs." Her voice stayed level. Her knuckles on the cane did not. "When the founding families met to settle the debt—the debt our grandparents incurred, the prosperity we'd been spending like inheritance—there were two girls of the right age in Hollow Creek. Rosalind Vane. And my granddaughter's daughter, Wendell Eriksen, who was seven."

I had known this was coming. Agnes had told me on her porch, three days ago, in the flat voice of a woman reading her own indictment. But knowing a thing and hearing it spoken into a room where the walls hum are different acts entirely.

"They chose Rosalind because her father was from away," Agnes said. "Because his people wouldn't stay to ask questions. Because mine would." She turned her head and looked at me, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "My sister begged. My brother-in-law packed the car that same night and drove until dawn, and I stood in my kitchen and let them go, because the alternative was admitting what I had consented to. I consented. I want that in the record. I was thirty-nine years old and I knew exactly what the paper meant and I signed my portion of it anyway, because the corn was failing and my husband's heart was bad and the town needed the harvest, and Rosalind was only eight, and eight seemed—"

For the first time, her voice broke.

"—eight seemed far enough away," she finished. "That's what I told myself. Distance. As if a child could be far away from anything, in a town this size, where everyone knows everyone's name." She laid her hand flat on the ledger, on the smudged entry, on the name beneath the smudge. "Rosalind Vane. Say it back to me, girl. All of it. It has to be both of us or it's nothing."

"Rosalind Vane," I said.

The bulb overhead dimmed—not flickered, *dimmed*, like a held breath—and the humming behind the walls, the humming I'd been hearing for weeks and pretending was pipes, went silent.

Then the singing started.

* * *

It came from everywhere and nowhere, thin as thread, high as children's voices are before they break. Not words. Not yet. Just the melody, circling, patient, the way children sing when they're playing a game whose rules they all know and you don't.

"The names," Hollis whispered. "Read the names. All of them. In order."

I opened the ledger wider and my hands were steady, which surprised me, because I had spent my whole life being the invisible one, the quiet one, the woman who arranged her existence so that no room ever turned toward her. Standing in a basement about to speak forty years of stolen children aloud to a listening house—that was the loudest thing I had ever done. And my hands were steady. Maybe steadiness isn't the absence of fear. Maybe it's what's left when you finally stop trying to disappear.

"Daniel Okafor," I read. "Taken 1974, second payment of the founding debt."

The singing shifted. Found a rhythm.

"Maren Kowalczyk. Taken 1979."

Behind the wall to my left, something knocked—once, twice, a small fist-sized sound, and Theo's mother pressed both hands over her mouth.

"Peter Lund. Taken 1984."

I kept going. Name after name, year after year, and with each one the room grew colder and fuller, the way a church fills, the way a room fills when the dead arrive politely and take their seats. I could feel them. I can't explain it better than that. Not ghosts pressing at the edges of my sight, nothing so cinematic—just a sense of *attendance*. Nineteen names in the ledger. Nineteen children who had once raised their hands in classrooms above this room, and every one of them present now, drawn down through the floors by the sound of their own names spoken by someone who wasn't asking anything of them.

That was the thing, I realized somewhere around the eleventh name. Nobody had ever said their names *for* them. The ledger said them as transactions. The parents had whispered them in empty bedrooms. The town had scrubbed them from yearbooks and rosters and memory. But no one had stood in front of the record and said *this child existed, this child was taken, this child is owed*—because there was no court for it, no statute, no form to file. Trading children for harvests isn't a crime anywhere in the books. So we had built our own tribunal down here among the boilers: a guilty steward, a complicit witness, a penitent founder, a grieving mother, and me. Testimony as exorcism. If the law wouldn't hear it, the walls would.

"Sarah Beth Pruitt," I read, and Mr. Pruitt made a sound like a hinge, and I understood—he'd named his daughter after her, or she'd been his cousin, or some thread of grief ran through his family too, back to 1974, back to the boy with the mop listening to the humming. "Taken 1996."

On and on. The singing rose with each name, harmonizing now, and the cold deepened until my breath showed, until frost feathered the edges of the table, until the ledger's pages stirred though there was no wind.

And then I reached the last entry before the blank pages, and my throat closed.

"Theo Marsh," I said. "Scheduled. New moon. Payment pending."

The singing stopped.

The silence afterward was the worst sound of the night—a whole room of the dead holding still, waiting, and I understood with total clarity that everything depended on the next sixty seconds. The covenant was a contract, and contracts run on consideration, and the consideration had failed. The town had promised a tithe and the tithe was sitting asleep at the school's locked front door, and the dead were owed, and debts unpaid curdle into something else. Unless—

Unless the debt was paid differently. Unless the original creditor stepped forward and canceled it.

Agnes was already moving. She pulled the ledger toward her, both hands, and bent over the first entry, over the smudged and bleeding name, and she spoke directly into the page as if the page were a face.

"Rosalind Vane," she said. "My niece. My sister's daughter. Eight years old in October of 1974, and I let them write your name in this book, and I have not signed my own name to anything since, because I knew—if I ever signed anything again, it should be this." She lifted her head. "I release the debt. Do you hear me? Whatever was owed to the founding families, whatever this covenant bought us—corn, health, full classrooms, fifty years of a town too comfortable to look at itself—I return it. Every year of it. Let the crops be thin. Let the budgets tighten. Let the prosperity rot back into ordinary dirt. You are released, Rosalind. You are released, all of you. The account is closed."

She took the pen from her coat pocket—the same kind of pen, I'd have bet anything, that she'd used in 1974—and she signed her name beneath her confession, on the last written page of the ledger, and the moment the ink touched paper the book began to burn.

* * *

There was no fire.

That's the part I will never be able to make anyone believe, and I've stopped trying. There was no heat, no flame, no smoke. The pages simply began to lift—each written line rising off the paper like ash off a log, curling upward in slow spirals, glowing faintly gold at the edges as it went. The covenant language went first, dissolving word by word. Then the columns of names, each one brightening as it rose, each one *loosening*, and as the names lifted I heard them—heard the children, all nineteen, saying their own names in their own voices, some laughing, some crying, one of them singing hers like a jump-rope rhyme.

*Rosalind Vane.* Hers rose last. It hung in the cold air above the table longer than the others, turning slowly, and I swear—I will swear it on any stack of Bibles you bring me—it looked at me. Not the name. The girl inside the name. And the look wasn't thanks, exactly. It was recognition. *You came back,* it said, the way she'd said it on my porch the first night. *You came back.*

Then it rose through the ceiling, through the floors, through the roof, and the singing went with it, fading upward and outward, and the cold broke all at once like a fever.

The ledger lay open on the table, blank. Every page. Blank as the day it was bound. Hollis touched it with one finger and flinched, then laughed—a cracked, disbelieving sound—and touched it again. "It's just paper," he said. "It's just paper now."

Above us, through the concrete, through the floors, came a new sound. A door opening. Then another. Doors all through the building, swinging wide on their hinges, every counted door in Hollow Creek School unlocking at once—and under the doors' long echo, faint but unmistakable, footsteps. Small ones. Going up the stairs.

Going home.

Theo's mother was already moving, already running for the stairwell, and Pruitt after her, and I stood one moment longer in the emptied room with Agnes, the two of us alone with a blank book and a single burning bulb.

"Whatever comes up those stairs," Agnes said quietly, gripping her cane, "whatever walks out of this building tonight—we receive it. That's the last part of the reckoning, girl. What was taken doesn't just get returned. It gets *welcomed*."

The footsteps reached the top of the stairs. The exit door groaned open onto the November dark.

And out on the lawn, in the moonless light, the missing children of Hollow Creek were standing in the grass—nineteen of them, and one more, a girl of eight with a crooked braid, looking up at the school windows as if she'd never seen them from the outside before.
