## 6. What the Mirrors Keep

Jude did not sleep well in strange houses. This was a professional trait as much as a personal one — he had learned, over eleven years of surveying other people's properties, that a house at night told you what its owners concealed by day. The creak of a settling joist, the drip of a pipe run badly, the way wind found gaps no inspector had flagged: all of it was testimony. He had trained himself to listen.

Hollowmere House did not creak. That was the first thing he noticed when he lay down in the guest room Wren had prepared for him on the second floor, east-facing, above the garden. The house made no sound at all. No settling, no ticking of cooling timber, no whisper of water in the walls. It was like lying inside something holding its breath.

He had set his recorder on the nightstand out of habit, though he hadn't spoken into it since dinner. He'd set his watch on top of his folded shirt. He'd placed his notebook beside the lamp, squared to the edge of the wood, because a notebook left askew bothered him in some deep, unexamined way he had never thought worth examining.

The room itself was ordinary enough — good proportions, a plaster cornice, a bed that smelled faintly of lavender and older, dustier things beneath it. But there were two mirrors, and this struck him only now, in the dark, as an excess. One was the tall cheval glass in the corner, its oval face turned toward the window. The other hung above the marble fireplace, rectangular, gilt-framed, so old that the silvering had begun to decay along its lower edge, blooming there like frost or mold.

In daylight both had shown him exactly what they should. Now, with the lamp off and moonlight coming through the east window in a long pale wedge, he could not stop looking at them.

He told himself it was the lag. Of course it was the lag. He had measured it that afternoon — 1.3 seconds, consistent across four trials, reproducible, documented — and a measurable thing was a manageable thing. He had written *mirror delay, E wing hall, ~1.3s, investigate* in his field book with a hand that had been almost steady. Almost. Wren had watched him do it without comment, arms crossed, her expression carrying something he'd first taken for skepticism and now, thinking back on it in the dark, recognized as pity.

He should sleep. He had a full schedule tomorrow: the cellar, the roofline, the outbuildings, another session with Edith Vane, who had spent their first interview asking him questions instead of answering his — whether he loved anyone, whether he wrote things down, whether he believed a house could be grateful. He had answered honestly, because he always answered honestly; it had never occurred to him until recently that honesty might be a kind of currency, and that someone might be collecting it.

He closed his eyes. Counted breaths. Got to forty-one.

It was the silence that broke him. A house this old, this large, should breathe. Hollowmere didn't. And beneath the silence — not audible, exactly, but present the way pressure is present before weather — there was a quality of attention that he had been trying all evening to dismiss as imagination.

He sat up. Swung his legs to the floor. Told himself he was going to get a glass of water, which was even true, mostly.

The cheval glass caught him as he crossed the room.

He stopped.

The mirror was not showing him the room.

It was showing him a room — the same room, he realized slowly, the same window, the same fireplace, the same wedge of moonlight laid across the same floorboards — but furnished differently. A four-poster bed stood where his single bed stood, hung with dark curtains tied back. A washstand with a porcelain basin occupied the corner where the cheval glass itself now lived. A dressing table. A candle burning on the mantel, its flame perfectly still, giving light that somehow did not brighten anything beyond the glass.

And there were people.

A woman sat at the dressing table with her back half-turned, brushing out hair so long it pooled in her lap like spilled ink. Her brush moved in slow, even strokes. She wore a high-collared gown the color of cream, and she was humming — he couldn't hear it, but he could see it in the small motion of her shoulders, the rhythm of the brush, a song conducted entirely in silence.

Jude's heart was going hard against his ribs. He did the thing he always did, the thing that had made him good at his work: he observed, and he recorded, and he refused to conclude before the evidence concluded for him.

He reached for his notebook. His hand shook slightly. He wrote: *11:52 p.m. Cheval glass, guest rm. Contents do not match room. Furnishings circa—* He stopped, looked again, amended: *—late Victorian? Woman at dressing table. Alone.*

She wasn't alone. As he watched, a man entered the mirror's frame from the left — from the direction of the door, Jude understood, the door that in the real room was behind him. The man was tall, bearded, dressed in shirtsleeves and waistcoat, carrying a book. He crossed to the woman, bent, kissed the crown of her head. She leaned back into him without pausing her brushing. An entire marriage conducted in eight seconds of silent pantomime, tender and unremarkable and utterly impossible.

Jude raised his phone and photographed the mirror. The flash fired.

The image on the screen showed nothing but an empty room — his room, the real one, the cheval glass reflecting the window and the pale wedge of moonlight and, at its edge, the blurred shape of a man holding up a phone.

He lowered the phone. Looked back at the glass.

The couple was still there. The woman had stopped brushing. She was looking directly at him.

Not toward the mirror. Not vaguely, the way people in photographs seem to look past you. At him. Her eyes found his across whatever distance separated the glass from wherever she was, and she tilted her head — a small, curious motion, the way a bird considers something bright — and then she smiled.

Jude stepped backward. His calf hit the bed and he sat down on it harder than he meant to, the mattress catching him with a sound that seemed enormous in the silent house.

The woman returned to her brushing. The man settled into a chair with his book. The scene resumed as if he had never existed, as if his interruption had been a fly to be waved away and forgotten.

*Documented,* he wrote, and his handwriting had gone ragged. *Observed me. Aware of observation.* Then, after a moment, because accuracy mattered even now, especially now: *Or I am dreaming.*

He pinched the inside of his wrist, hard, the way you do when you are thirty-four years old and embarrassed to be doing it. It hurt. The room stayed. The mirror stayed.

He got up, walked to the cheval glass, and turned it firmly to face the wall. The gesture felt absurd and necessary, like hanging a sheet over a window in a haunted story, and he hated himself a little for how much better it made him feel.

Then he turned around and saw the fireplace mirror.

*

The gilt-framed mirror above the mantel showed the dining room.

He knew it was the dining room because he had surveyed it that morning — the long mahogany table under the tarnished chandelier, the sideboard with its faded runner, the three tall windows now shuttered for the season. In the mirror the shutters were open. Daylight fell through them, gold and heavy, the light of late afternoon in some summer decades gone. The table was set for nine. Candles burned in the chandelier, pointless against the daylight, lit anyway, because that was what one did.

Nine people sat at the table.

They were mid-meal. A man at the head — gray-bearded, broad, with hands folded around a wineglass — was speaking, his mouth moving through some long anecdote that had everyone else smiling. Beside him a younger woman laughed and covered her mouth. Two children sat together on one side, a boy and a girl, the boy sneaking something from his plate to the girl under the table while an older woman pretended not to see. A servant stood at the sideboard with a tureen. Another figure, half out of frame, was pouring wine.

Jude knew these faces.

He had spent three hours that afternoon photographing the portrait gallery on the ground floor, cataloguing each frame for the inventory: *oil on canvas, unsigned, c. 1860s; oil on canvas, attributed to—*. The gray-bearded man at the head of the table was the founder himself, Aldous Vane, whose portrait hung at the gallery's center, painted with a surveyor's instruments on the table beside him. The laughing young woman was his daughter, the one in blue. The children were the grandchildren from the double portrait in the stairwell, the boy forever frozen mid-fidget, the girl forever solemn.

They were all here. All of them, moving, alive, eating dinner in a house that had been empty of everything but Edith Vane and Wren for longer than Jude had been alive.

His first coherent thought was: *recording.* His second was: *don't.* Some instinct older than professionalism told him that photographing this would be different from photographing the woman at her dressing table. That this was private. That he was standing at a keyhole.

He stood at the keyhole anyway. He simply didn't raise the camera.

The meal went on. Dishes changed — the servant moved among them, the tureen emptied and was replaced. Wine was poured. The founder told his story to its end and the table laughed, and the sound of it reached Jude as nothing, as pure absence, and somehow that was worse than hearing it. Laughter with the sound removed is just the shape of joy, a hollow cast of it, and watching it made him feel like a thief of something that had already been stolen once.

He checked his watch. 12:19 a.m. He looked back. The meal continued. Nobody aged. The candles burned down not at all.

*How long has this been playing?* he wrote. *On loop? Or—* and here his pen hesitated over the page — *or is it live somewhere? Somewhen?*

Because that was the question, wasn't it. A recording repeats. A recording, eventually, shows its seams — jumps, stutters, the same laugh twice. He watched for twenty minutes, taking notes in a shorthand only he could read, timing the scenes against his watch. The dinner did not repeat. It progressed. Slowly, the way a real dinner progresses, with pauses and lulls and the slow migration of conversation from one end of the table to the other. The children grew restless. The girl was sent from the table — he saw the mother's stern gesture, the girl's sliding from her chair, her exit through the mirror's frame — and returned five minutes later with a doll she was not allowed to bring to the table and was allowed to keep anyway, because the father's eyebrows said *just this once.*

This was not a recording. This was a place. And the people in it were not images.

They were residents.

The word arrived in his mind with the weight of something official, something stamped and filed, and he wrote it down before he could flinch from it: *residents.*

Then the girl with the doll looked at him.

She was perhaps seven years old, in a white nightgown under her dinner dress, clutching a doll with porcelain cheeks. She had come back to her chair and climbed onto it and was arranging the doll beside her plate with great seriousness, and then, in the middle of this ceremony, her eyes lifted and found him through the glass.

Children notice things adults have trained themselves not to. That was all it was, he told himself. Children stare. She's staring at a flicker of movement, a shadow at the edge of her vision, the way children stare at—

She waved.

Small fingers, opening and closing. Not a greeting, quite. More like the wave you give someone through a train window — *I see you, you see me, we are both going somewhere.*

Jude raised his hand before he could decide not to. It was a reflex, the politeness of being seen, and the moment his palm lifted he felt the obscenity of it — waving at a dead child, a child dead a hundred and fifty years, a child preserved like a fly in amber and performing her dinner for whoever happened to be watching —

But she wasn't performing. That was the thing that undid him. She had glanced at him the way a child glances at anything new, held his gaze for three seconds with the frank appetite of the young, and then gone back to her doll. He was furniture to her. He was the odd painting on the wall, the stranger at the window. Whatever attention the house paid him, the dead themselves had better things to do.

All except one.

At the far end of the table, in the last chair, half in shadow where the chandelier's light gave out, sat a man who had not touched his food.

Jude had taken him for part of the composition — another uncle, another cousin, sunk in private thought the way men sink at dinners. But now that Jude looked properly, he saw that the man's plate was untouched, his glass full, and his posture wrong in a way Jude couldn't immediately name. Everyone else at the table leaned into the meal, into the light, into one another. This man sat very straight, very still, facing forward.

Facing out.

His face was difficult to see — the mirror's old silvering swallowed detail at its edges, and the shadows did the rest. But he was looking directly through the glass at Jude, and he had been, Jude understood with a cold spreading certainty, the entire time. Since the first moment Jude had appeared at the edge of the frame. Perhaps since the first moment Jude had entered the house.

The man did not wave. He did not smile. He did not move at all, except that as Jude watched, he raised one finger — slowly, deliberately, the way a librarian silences a noisy reading room — and touched it to his lips.

*Quiet.*

Then he pointed. Not at Jude. Past him. Downward and to the left, toward the corner of the real room, toward the floor beneath the guest room, toward the cellars, toward—

The mirror fogged.

Not gradually, not like breath on cold glass, but all at once, every inch of it, as if the surface had exhaled. When it cleared — seconds later, heart slamming, pen useless in his sweating hand — the dining room was gone. The mirror showed only the dark reflection of the guest room: the bed, the lamp, the notebook, and Jude himself, standing pale in his shirtsleeves with his hair mussed, looking like exactly what he was, a man alone in an old house at nearly one in the morning, frightening himself.

Both mirrors now showed him only the truth. He checked. Twice.

He sat on the edge of the bed for a long time afterward, writing. He wrote down everything — times, positions, descriptions, the sequence of the girl's glance, the man's gesture, the direction he had pointed. Writing steadied him. Writing always steadied him; it was the one ritual he trusted, the act of fixing a thing to paper so that it could not later claim to have been otherwise. If it was written, it had happened. If it had happened, it could be understood. If it could be understood, it could be survived.

He was on his fourth page when he noticed that his hand had written a sentence he had no memory of deciding to write:

*He knows I'm here. He has been waiting for someone to document them.*

He stared at it. Read it again. It was his handwriting, unmistakably — the narrow, upright letters, the particular way he crossed his sevens. But he had no memory of writing it, no memory of the thought forming, and that absence frightened him more than the mirrors had. The mirrors were the house behaving strangely. This was Jude behaving strangely.

He drew a line through the sentence. Then, because destroying data offended something fundamental in him, he wrote beneath it: *struck — uncertain provenance. Possible fatigue. Verify tomorrow.*

Verify. Yes. Tomorrow he would go down to the dining room in daylight and stand where the table stood and measure the sightlines to the fireplace mirror and confirm that none of this was possible, which was the first step, always, toward understanding how it was possible anyway.

He turned the lamp off. Lay down. Did not sleep.

Around three, he heard footsteps in the corridor outside his door — slow, uneven, the shuffle of someone old moving with care. Edith Vane, he assumed, making one of the nocturnal circuits Wren had mentioned, checking the house the way a lighthouse keeper checks the lamp. He listened to her pass. The footsteps paused outside his door. Held there. Moved on.

And beneath the shuffle of her slippers, so faint he might have invented it, he heard her speaking softly to someone. To several someones. A whole quiet roll call of names, murmured down the dark hallway like a mother counting heads.

When he finally slept near dawn, he dreamed of the dining room, and in the dream he was seated at the table with the others, and his plate was empty, and everyone was very kind to him, and no one would tell him what he was waiting for.

He woke at eight with the notebook open on his chest to a fresh page, and at the top of it, in his own handwriting, in letters neater than he ever managed before coffee, were the words:

*Ask Edith about the man at the end of the table.*
