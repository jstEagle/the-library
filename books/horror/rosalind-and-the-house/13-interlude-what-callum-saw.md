## 13. Interlude: What Callum Saw

I am in the walls the way a voice is in a shell.

That is the first thing I would tell you, if you could hear me — and you can't, no one has been able to for six years, though I keep talking anyway because the alternative is to stop, and stopping feels like the one door I was never given. I am in the walls, in the glass, in the grain of the banister my palm wore smooth when it was still a palm. I am not dead. That is the cruelest part, and I need you to understand it before anything else: the house does not kill what it keeps. It keeps. The way a jar keeps. The way amber keeps.

My name is Callum Vane. I was nineteen years old when I came back to Hollowmere with a can of kerosene and a righteous fire in my chest, and I have been twenty-three now for a very long time.

* * *

Let me tell you about the night I arrived, because I remember it perfectly — that's the house's gift, perfect memory, every second of the last six years preserved at full fidelity with nowhere for any of it to go.

The kerosene was in my left hand. The matchbox was in my jacket pocket, over my heart, and I remember thinking that was fitting — I'd carry the flame where the anger lived. It was October. The elms along the drive had gone the color of old brass, and the windows of Hollowmere were lit, which they shouldn't have been; Gran Edith kept the east wing dark to save the wiring. Lit windows meant someone was home who wasn't supposed to be.

I went in through the pantry door because I'd been going through that door since I was four, since before Wren was born, since the house knew the particular rhythm of my step on the flagstones. And here is the thing I did not know then, the thing I have had six years to learn from the inside: the house recognized me. Not the way a dog recognizes its family. The way a lock recognizes its key.

The founders built this place as a vessel. I know that now the way you know your own heartbeat — not learned but installed. They wanted to preserve what was loved, and preservation requires material, and material requires a bearer, someone whose remembering could be drawn out slowly, like thread from a spool, and woven into the rooms so that the dead could go on being warm at dinner, being young in photographs, being *here*. Every generation the house waits for its bearer. For sixty years after the founders' last rite failed — interrupted, unfinished, a sentence stopped mid-word — Hollowmere stood hungry and patient, dust refusing to settle, mirrors lagging by fractions of seconds, waiting.

I walked in carrying fire, and the house thought: *there. Finally. Someone who cares enough to destroy us.*

You cannot burn down something that has already decided you belong to it.

I got as far as the front hall. I struck the match. I remember the sulfur smell, the small bloom of light, my own face reflected in the long mirror opposite — and my reflection struck the match a half-second after I did, and smiled while doing it, and I had not smiled.

Then the hall exhaled. There is no other word. The air moved inward instead of out, the match flame bent toward the staircase like grass in wind, and the house pulled me off my feet the way you'd draw a splinter from skin. No pain. That's important too. There has never been pain here. Only a vast, gentle, absolutely irresistible attention, closing over me like water over a stone dropped in a well.

The last thing I saw from outside myself was the match going out on the flagstones, unburned, and the kerosene can standing upright by the door, and my sister's bicycle leaning against the porch rail where she'd left it that morning.

Wren was fourteen. She never knew why I didn't come home.

* * *

Here is what keeping feels like, from the inside.

Imagine your body is a house you can no longer leave, except the house is also your body, and also there are no doors. Imagine you can see through every mirror and every dark window at once — not sequentially, all at once, the way you can feel all five fingers without thinking about them. Imagine you can hear everything said under this roof, every floorboard's complaint, every pipe's swallow, every word spoken softly in the garden as if the night made it private. The house is not deaf. The house is me, now, and I am the house, and neither of us gets to look away.

In the first year I screamed. I want to be honest about that. I screamed until screaming lost meaning, which took — I don't know how long. Time moves strangely here. The clocks all run, I can hear them, but they don't apply to me the way they used to. I think a year. Then I wept. Then, around the time Wren came back — she was eighteen, Gran had broken her hip, and Wren packed a bag and moved into the room next to mine, the room where my bed still sat under the window where I'd pinned my star charts — then I went quiet.

Because here is the other thing, the thing that makes this bearable and unbearable in the same breath:

She stayed. Because of me.

Gran told her — I heard it through the wall of the sickroom, Gran's voice papery and fierce — *you stay so he isn't alone.* And Wren, who had spent four years hating this house for eating her brother, who had every reason to strike the match herself, put her hand flat against the wallpaper of my old room and said, "Okay." Just that. Okay. Six years of her life, traded for my loneliness.

So I watch over her. It's the only agency I have left, and I take it the way a starving man takes crumbs — gratefully, obsessively, ashamed.

* * *

Tonight it is raining, and Wren is asleep, and I am watching her the way I have watched her every night for six years, and I need you to understand that this is tenderness. Whatever it looks like from outside — and I know how it looks, I've seen enough ghost stories reflected in these mirrors to know — it is tenderness. It is the only shape my love can take now.

Her door is open two inches because she never closes it fully. She says it's for the cat, but the cat has been dead three years and she still leaves the gap, and I think — I hope — some part of her knows I'm here, some animal part, the part that sleeps better in houses than apartments, the part that talks to empty rooms. She leaves the gap and I come through it as a faintness in the hallway mirror, a warmth along the baseboard, a settling of the house around her sleep like a blanket being tucked.

She sleeps on her left side, facing the window, always has, even when the window faces nothing but the elm. Her breathing goes slow and deep around eleven and if the day has been hard — if Gran has been confused, if the doctors' letters have come, if the loneliness got its teeth into her in the kitchen where she thinks no one can see — her breathing stays shallow past midnight, and I do what I can, which is nothing, which is everything I can do: I make the radiator tick in a slow rhythm, four beats and a rest, the rhythm of the song I used to drum on her headboard to annoy her. She never wakes. But her breathing changes. It finds the rhythm. Six years of evidence and I will swear it anywhere: her body knows the song.

Tonight her breathing found it fast. Good day, then. Or a day she decided to call good.

There's a man in the house now.

I felt him arrive three days ago the way you feel a new tooth — pressure, wrongness, attention demanded. Jude Ravensworth. Surveyor. He carries instruments and a camera and notebooks, and he photographs everything twice, and I watched him do it in the front hall mirror and felt something I hadn't felt since the match: fear. Not for him. For the notebooks.

He doesn't know yet what documentation means here. He will. The house reads. The house has been reading me for six years — every memory I own, it owns; every name I loved, it files. And this man arrives with his pockets full of paper, writing himself down page by page, offering himself up in his own handwriting, and the house has turned toward him like a plant toward a window. I can feel its attention shift when he enters a room. The mirrors lag a little more each day. He measured it — of course he measured it, he's the measuring kind — one second, then two. He thinks he's studying us.

He's courting us. He just doesn't know he's the dowry.

And Wren — Wren watches him with an expression I recognize because I wore it once, at seventeen, looking at a girl across a church pew. She watches him and hates herself for watching him and watches him anyway. My sister, who gave this house six years on my account, is beginning to look at a man the way people look at exits.

I should be glad. God knows I should be glad. If she loves him, maybe she'll leave. Maybe she'll finally walk out the pantry door and not come back, and the promise Gran wrung from her will break, and she'll be free, and I will be alone in the only way that matters — not solitary, but unwitnessed. Unloved in real time. A photograph no one visits anymore.

But I'm not glad. I'm terrified. Because I've read the ledger — yes, I can read the ledgers, they're mine now too, all those shelves in the room behind the pantry wall, the thirteenth room, the one the surveyors drew and the world forgot. I've read the founders' hand. I know what the rite requires. A willing bearer, given or volunteered, memories poured out to line the vessel, and the kept ones released — that's the promise written in the margin, the promise the founders never got to test because their bearer died mid-rite, mid-sentence, leaving the house forever hungry and half-full.

A willing bearer. Given by another.

And there is a man in the house with a notebook full of himself, falling in love with my sister, and the house is patient, and the house is kind, and the house will ask him so gently he might say yes.

* * *

Rain on the east roof. Wren turns over. In the mirror on her dresser — she keeps it covered with a scarf during the day, I know why, I've never blamed her — the scarf has slipped, and in the strip of exposed glass there is a room from 1974: Gran at thirty, laughing at something my grandfather said, the table set with the good plates, the candle flames bending slightly toward the window as if listening. They're always at dinner, the kept ones. Always mid-laugh, mid-story, mid-life. Grandfather's chair is empty tonight. It's been empty forty years, ever since he signed the papers and the house folded him into its upkeep, and Gran refuses to sign because as long as she holds the deed she believes — needs to believe — some kind part of him is anchored somewhere, waiting for her.

He is. I can feel him the way you feel a limb asleep. He's in the plumbing, mostly. In the steady pressure of the well pump. He hums when the water runs.

The figure at the far end of the 1974 table looks out of the mirror at me. She always does. Great-great-aunt Ottoline, the founders' daughter, the first keeper, the one whose death interrupted the rite. Her mouth is mid-word — it has been mid-word for a hundred and ten years — and her eyes find whatever part of the house I'm occupying, and she watches me watch her, and sometimes I think the word she's trying to finish is *wait.*

Wait. Wait. Wait.

I don't wait well. I never did. Ask anyone — ask Wren, who at nine years old watched her brother climb the elm in a thunderstorm because the lightning was *right there* and waiting felt like cowardice. The house has cured me of that. The house is nothing but waiting, patient as geology, and I have become fluent in it against my will.

But tonight, tonight I am not waiting patiently. Tonight I am pressing myself against the inside of the glass like a child against a shop window, because the surveyor is awake. Third night running. He's sitting on the floor of the east wing hallway with his notebook open on his knee, testing the mirror again — raising his right hand, watching his reflection raise it late, writing the number down. Two point five seconds. His handwriting is small and exact and slanted forward, eager, and I know that handwriting. I have known it for six years from a shelf in a locked room, from a survey dated 1911, from drawings of this house signed in a hand identical to his, because the house chose him long ago the way it chose me, the way it chooses its bearers, reaching backward through time to arrange itself around the person it intends to keep.

He writes something else. I lean closer — closer is meaningless, I am everywhere and nowhere, but habit survives the body — and I read it upside down through the notebook's cover, because the house lets me read what's written under this roof, because everything written here belongs to it, and therefore to me:

*If the delay is increasing at a constant rate, extrapolation gives a divergence event within approximately nine days. What happens at zero?*

Oh, Jude, I thought, with all the love I have left, which is considerable, which is the only thing about me that hasn't been used up. Oh, Jude. You don't want to know what happens at zero.

At zero, the house stops rehearsing and starts performing.

I know, because I've felt it gathering. The mirrors aren't lagging anymore — they're *learning*. Each delayed second is a lesson, each repeated gesture a rehearsal, and somewhere behind the glass something is practicing being Jude Ravensworth, getting better at him nightly, and when the lag reaches zero the reflection won't follow him anymore. It will lead.

Unless someone finishes the sentence first. Unless the rite completes — freely, inverted, rewritten — and the vessel empties instead of fills.

I have six years of silence saved up. I have watched my sister sleep two thousand nights. I have exactly one chance to be more than a witness, and I have spent all of it, every hour, learning the house's grammar from the inside — every clause of the founders' ledger, every loophole in the margins, every place where the ritual says *given* and does not say *taken*, says *bearer* and does not say *unwilling*, says *preserve* and never once, not in a hundred and ten years of careful founder's script, says *only*.

Tomorrow the surveyor will find the thirteenth room. Tomorrow he will see his name on a ledger, half-filled, and he will understand what I understood at nineteen with a match in my hand: that the house has already decided, and resistance is just another way of arriving.

But tomorrow night — tomorrow night, when he comes back with his questions, when he stands in the dark hallway asking the empty air what happens at zero —

I am going to answer him.

It will cost me. Speaking costs; the house takes payment in memory, and I am down to my last few years of them, the good ones, the ones with Wren in them. I will pay anyway. I will spend my sister's ninth birthday and the smell of the elm bark and the girl across the church pew, all of it, every coin I've hoarded in the dark, to push one sentence through six years of glass and wallpaper and rain:

*Don't give it what it asks for. Give it the opposite. I'll show you where the flaw is — I've been living in it.*

The radiator ticks. Four beats and a rest. Down the hall, Wren's breathing finds the rhythm, the way it always does, the way it will tonight and every night I have left to keep it.

The rain thickens. In every mirror in Hollowmere, the dinner party of 1974 raises its glasses, and great-great-aunt Ottoline's mouth opens on the same unfinished word, and for the first time in a hundred and ten years, I think I know what she's saying.

Not *wait*.

*Hurry.*
