## 16. Reading Callum's Ledger

Jude did not sleep. He had stopped pretending to, somewhere around two in the morning, when the mirror above the washbasin had begun to smile at him without his permission — a small, patient thing, the way a dog smiles when it has learned that smiling gets it fed.

So he worked instead. Work was what he knew. He spread the founders' ledger across the desk in the blue room with the lamp turned low and his own notebook open beside it, and he read the way he read everything: twice, then again, cross-referencing margins against measurements, measurements against dates, dates against the small marginalia of dead men who had believed they were building a house and had instead built a mouth.

The ledger was written in three hands. The first was formal, architectural, confident — Elias Vane, 1841, laying out the grammar of preservation in language so dry it might have been a drainage specification. *The vessel shall be fed by consent, freely rendered, of one who bears the memory of those to be kept.* The second hand was shakier, later, corrections crowding the margins like weeds through pavement. The third hand belonged to no one Jude could name, and it appeared only once, near the end, in ink that had gone brown with age:

*It must be given. It cannot be taken. This is the mercy and this is the trap.*

Jude read that line eleven times.

Outside, the house breathed. He had learned to hear it now — not sound exactly, but pressure, the way a room feels different when someone is standing behind you. The mirrors in the hallway had gone quiet since Edith's collapse, all of them angled toward Wren's door like flowers toward a sun that hadn't risen yet. He could feel them even from here, even through walls. Waiting. The house had made its offer through Edith's mouth two nights ago, and it was not an offer that expired. Houses like this didn't expire. They accrued.

He turned back to the rite itself, the operational heart of the thing, laid out across four pages in Elias Vane's immaculate drafting script. And there — for the first time, because grief and terror had until now been reading over his shoulder and skipping lines — he saw it.

*The bearer shall be named by another.*

Not chosen. Not volunteered. *Named by another.* The rite required that the memory-bearer be surrendered — given up by someone who loved them, the way Edith's husband had been given, the way Callum had been taken when he came burning with hate rather than love and the house had simply amended the paperwork. That was the flaw Jude had assumed: the rite demanded a betrayal as its fuel. Someone had to hand another person over. That was why it had never been finished. That was why the house sat here starving for a hundred and eighty years, keeping its residents alive past all decency, because every generation had looked at the price and refused to pay it in the only currency accepted.

But Jude had spent twenty years reading contracts. Contracts were not about what was said. Contracts were about what was *not forbidden*.

He set down the ledger and pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes until he saw constellations.

*Named by another.* The clause specified the manner of naming, not the manner of choosing. A bearer given by another — yes. But nowhere — he checked, page by page, finger under each line like a man searching a floor for a dropped ring — nowhere did the text require that the bearer be unwilling. Nowhere did it say the giving could not be self-inflicted. Nowhere did it say the one who named could not be the one who was named.

The rite demanded a gift. It had never specified who had to do the giving.

Jude laughed once, a short ugly sound in the empty room, and the lamp flickered as if in answer. Of course. Of course. The founders hadn't built a trap at all — or they'd built one with a door in it, and then died, or lost their nerve, or loved their children too much to use it, and the door had rusted shut behind them while generation after generation of Vanes read the same four pages and saw only the word *another* and wept and signed nothing and let the house go on eating time instead.

Edith had refused for forty years because she thought signing meant surrendering someone she loved.

She had never once considered that she could sign herself.

And neither, until this moment, had Jude.

He sat very still. The house sat stiller. Somewhere below him, in the thirteenth room behind the pantry wall, shelves of ledgers stood in their patient rows, each spine bearing a name, each half-filled book a life interrupted mid-sentence. Callum's was there. He had seen it. He had touched it — warm, like everything in that room, warm as skin, warm as something freshly buried that hadn't finished being dead.

He should go down now. He should take the ledger, find the final line, begin.

Instead he found himself thinking about Wren's hands. The way she held a teacup like it might be listening. The way she had said, three nights ago in the garden, *I stopped using mirrors years ago*, and how her voice hadn't trembled, and how he had wanted — absurdly, professionally, catastrophically — to document the exact pitch of it. He had burned his duplicate photographs tonight. All of them, in the kitchen sink, one by one, watching the edges curl orange. It had felt like amputation and relief in equal measure. He had kept only the originals, and he understood now, with the terrible clarity of the sleepless, that he would have to burn those too, before the end, because the house read what he wrote and owned what it read, and he did not want it owning her face.

He thought about the blank pages. He had dreamed of them last week without understanding the dream — himself standing in a field holding notebooks full of nothing, upright somehow, held vertical by a voice he couldn't see calling a name he couldn't remember was his.

The dream hadn't been a warning. It had been a preview.

* * *

Near dawn he went down to the thirteenth room.

The passage behind the pantry wall was exactly as the founders' drawings promised — eighteen inches wider than any surveyor's conscience, dry as paper, breathing faintly against his cheek as he descended. The door was warm. It was always warm. He had stopped flinching from it, which frightened him more than the warmth ever had.

Inside, the ledgers waited in their rows, and the air had the quality of a held breath, and the lamps along the shelves — there were no lamps, and yet there was light, sourceless and brownish, the color of old varnish — lit themselves around him the way candles seem to lean toward a person entering a church.

Callum's ledger lay on the reading stand, because the house wanted him to read it. Jude knew that now. The house had been curating his research for weeks, leaving doors ajar, letting him find the archive, feeding him the survey drawings in the precise order that would bring him here standing. It thought it was reeling him in. It did not know — could not know, he prayed, could not *document* — that it was handing the executioner the blueprint of his own gallows.

He opened the book.

The first pages were a boy's handwriting, round and furious. *Day one. I don't know where I am but I can hear Wren through the kitchen window.* Then the handwriting changed — tightened, aged, though the entries ran in unbroken sequence, days passing inside a body that wasn't passing anywhere. *Day forty. She talks to the plants I planted. She uses my trowel. She doesn't know I can see her.* *Day two hundred. The mirrors are windows if you're on this side. I watched her sleep last night. I'm sorry. There is nothing else to watch.* *Day nine hundred. I love her so much it has become a kind of architecture.*

Jude read standing, because sitting felt like agreement.

Toward the middle, the entries shifted register — Callum had begun studying the rite from the inside, the way Jude studied everything, and his notes grew technical, desperate, brilliant. *The ledger fills as the bearer remembers. Every documented memory becomes a page. That's what documentation is — prayer, or payment, I can't tell which.* And then, in handwriting gone thin as wire: *I tried to write myself out. I wrote I renounce, I revoke, I refuse. The house filed it under additional material.*

And then, near the end, the entry that stopped Jude's breath:

*I understand it now. The rite needs a giver and a gift. Everyone reads it wrong. Everyone thinks the gift is the person. The gift is the remembering. If someone walked in here willing — if someone offered their memories freely, all of them, complete — the transaction would close clean. No theft. No cage. The kept ones go free because the debt is finally paid in full, in the only coin minted for it.*

*But no one will. Because everyone who understands it is already inside.*

*If you're reading this — and someone will be, the house makes sure of that — know that I almost volunteered. I was going to. I had the pen. And then I thought of Wren alone in a house with no one in it, and I couldn't make the trade. Forgive me. Or don't. Just tell her the garden was for her.*

The entry ended there. The remaining pages were blank except for faint impressions, words written and erased, written and erased, a man rehearsing his own sacrifice for years and never performing it.

Jude closed the book gently, the way you close a coffin.

* * *

He climbed back up through the pantry passage as the house was turning gray with dawn, and he stood in the kitchen while the light came up over the garden Callum had planted, and he made his lists.

This was how he processed everything; it always had been. Lists. Specifications. So:

One. The rite could be completed by a volunteer. The text permitted it. The house, he suspected, would permit anything that ended its hunger, but he would not rely on suspicion — he would rely on the clause, verified three times, photographed twice. Old habits. Let them die with him.

Two. It had to be him. Not because the house preferred him — though it did, God help him, it had chosen him years ago, had reached through a county archive and matched its founder's handwriting to his like a lock accepting its key. It had to be him because the alternative was Wren, and the house would take Wren the moment the papers signed themselves, and Jude had spent six weeks learning exactly one thing worth learning: that some things are worth more than a complete archive of yourself.

Three. He could not tell her. This was the hard clause, the one he kept trying to annotate and kept striking out. If he told her, she would fight it — she would do something reckless and brave and Wren-shaped, and the house would adapt, and the mirrors would turn, and someone else would pay. If he told her, she would also try to stop him, and she might succeed, and then they would both lose. Some mercies had to be administered without consent. He hated himself for the thought even as he filed it.

Four. Timing. Edith was failing. The papers would sign themselves soon — he had seen the pens twitching in their tray like sleeping insects dreaming of flight. The rite had to be finished before the signature, or in the same breath, or the house would take what it wanted by force and his volunteering would be moot. Two days. Perhaps three.

Five. Last night. He wanted one last night. This was not strategic. He entered it on the list anyway, because he was an honest surveyor even of his own ruin, and because he had learned from Callum's ledger that the difference between a martyr and a coward was often just one evening spent telling the truth.

He took out his notebook — his real one, the one the house had been reading for weeks, the one that had become a confession it owned page by page — and he wrote nothing in it. Instead he memorized. Wren's name. The weight of her laugh. The exact gray-green of the light in the east hall at seven in the morning. He put them somewhere the house couldn't follow, in the place behind his eyes that no ledger had ever reached, and he held them there like a man cupping water in his hands, knowing he would carry it only as far as the well.

Below him, faintly, he heard Edith cough. Once. Twice. The sound of a clock running down.

In the pantry, behind the false wall, the thirteenth room breathed in, and held it, and waited.

Jude closed his notebook, and went upstairs to make Edith's breakfast, and smiled at Wren when she came down, and told her nothing at all.
