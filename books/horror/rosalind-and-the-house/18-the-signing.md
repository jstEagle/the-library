## 18. The Signing

Edith Vane signed her name at 4:17 in the afternoon, and every clock in Hollowmere House stopped at the same instant.

I know the time because I had written it down before it happened — a habit now so deep in me it felt less like practice than like prophecy. I had filled three notebooks in the days since I'd found my own name half-inscribed on a ledger in the thirteenth room. I had told no one. I had burned nothing, confessed nothing, and slept, when I slept at all, with the covers pulled over the mirror on Wren's dresser so that the thing wearing my reflection could not watch me dream.

The signing itself was almost gentle. That was the worst of it. I had expected — I don't know what. Thunder in the walls. The mirrors running with black water. Instead there was only Edith in her bed, propped on pillows the color of old cream, her hands trembling so badly that Wren had to steady the page for her, and the pen — my pen, the good archival pen I'd brought in my instrument case, the one I used for permanent records — moving across the line in a slow, careful arc.

*Edith M. Vane.*

Fourteen letters. I counted them. I count everything now. It's how I pray.

"There," she said, and her voice was light, almost girlish, the voice of a woman setting down a weight she'd carried for forty years. "There. That wasn't so difficult, was it?"

Wren did not answer. Wren was looking at the window, where the afternoon light had gone the color of weak tea, and at the long mirror on Edith's vanity, where Edith's reflection was — for the first time in the six days I had been in this house — perfectly, exactly, terribly synchronized with her body. Pen lifting. Hand lowering. Mouth forming the same small satisfied smile at the same moment.

The lag was gone. I had measured it that morning: five seconds, sometimes seven, the delay widening like a crack in ice. Now there was nothing between the woman and her image at all. They moved as one.

They moved as one, and it was the reflection, I realized with a cold that started in my teeth and spread downward, that was leading.

* * *

The pens began writing at 4:19.

Not one pen. All of them. I heard it first as a sound I couldn't place — a dry, whispering friction, like wind through dead grass, coming from everywhere at once — and then I understood that it was the sound of nibs on paper, and that it was coming from every room in the house.

I ran. I am not ashamed to say I ran, a grown man, a surveyor with instruments and credentials and a lifetime of rational method, running down the corridor of a dying woman's house because the stationery had come alive. I flung open the door of the east sitting room and found the fountain pen on the writing desk standing upright on its nib, rocking gently, dragging itself in small arcs across a sheet of Wren's grocery lists. It had already covered both sides. It was writing names.

*Harold Vane. Harold Vane. Harold Vane, Harold Vane, HaroldVaneHaroldVane* — the name collapsing into a scrawl, then resolving again, patient, insistent, a hand practicing its own signature for forty years.

In the library, the pencil cup had emptied itself. Six pencils lay on the floor, each one rolling in slow circles, each one tracing letters into the dust — and the dust, the dust that had refused to settle on the sills since the day I arrived, was settling now, everywhere, in grey sheets, like the house exhaling after holding its breath for a century. The pencils wrote in the falling dust. I couldn't read what they wrote. I didn't stay to learn.

In the pantry — the pantry whose back wall was warm and breathed faintly, the pantry that stood before the door to the thirteenth room — every pen in the drawer had burst from its container and was writing on the inside of the drawer itself, on the wood, in ink that was not ink, in a script I recognized with a sickness that went all the way to the marrow of me.

It was my handwriting. All of it. Every line in the house was in my handwriting, because the house had read my notebooks, had learned me through my own records, and now it wrote as me, with my hand, in my name.

I stood in the pantry with my heart going like a trapped bird and read what the pens had written on the drawer's inner wall.

*Jude Ravensworth. Bearer. Jude Ravensworth. Bearer. Jude—*

The last line was different. The last line was fresh, the ink still glistening:

*He has read the ledger. He knows the flaw. He has not told her.*

I tore the drawer out of its frame. I broke every pen in it with my heel. The sound they made when they broke was not the sound of plastic and metal. It was a sound like a held note ending.

Then I went to find Wren, and I lied to her face, and it was the last lie I will ever have the strength to tell.

* * *

"Edith wants to see the garden," Wren said. "She hasn't been outside in a month. She says the light is different today. She says she wants to see the roses Callum planted."

We wheeled her out through the French doors at ten past five, Edith wrapped in a blanket the color of a storm cloud, her face turned up to a sky that had gone strange — not dark, not bright, but the flat, sourceless grey of a photograph, of a memory, of the inside of a mirror. The roses were blooming out of season. I noted it and hated myself for noting it and noted it anyway.

"She's peaceful," Wren murmured to me as we crossed the lawn. "I've never seen her peaceful. Six years, Jude, and she's been afraid every single day — afraid of the mirrors, afraid of signing, afraid of sleeping — and today she asked me to brush her hair." Her hand found mine, brief and warm and devastating. "Whatever's happening, whatever this house is doing — she's not afraid anymore."

The pens had stopped. The whole house had gone quiet in the way a held instrument is quiet, a violin between movements, everything strung and waiting. I could feel it waiting. I could feel it in the soles of my feet, through the grass, up through the bones of the earth itself: Hollowmere was not dying with Edith's signature. Hollowmere was being *born*.

"Jude." Edith's voice, thin and clear as a struck glass. "Come here where I can see you."

I came. Up close, in that grey light, she looked less like a dying woman than like a finished one — every line of her face settled, resolved, as though someone had gone over her features in ink.

"You found the ledger," she said. It was not a question.

My mouth went dry. "Mrs. Vane—"

"You found it, and you read it, and you found the flaw in it, and you have not told my granddaughter." She smiled. It was the kindest expression I have ever been the object of, and it terrified me more than anything the mirrors had ever shown. "You have her hands, you know. The way you held the pen. Harold held it like that. Like the writing mattered more than the words."

"Edith, please—" Wren started forward.

"No, love. Let me finish. I've been rehearsing this for forty years and I'll not be interrupted at the end." Edith's eyes came back to me. Behind her, in the long window of the drawing room, her reflection did not turn away when she did. It stayed fixed on me. It was smiling wider than she was. "Harold signed, Jude. Forty years ago. He signed because I asked him to, because the surveyor told us it was only paperwork, because we were young and the money was good and we thought a house was a house." Her voice did not waver. That was the horror of it. It was steady as a plumb line. "And the house took him into the upkeep. That's what the ledger calls it. The upkeep. He's been keeping the floors level and the mirrors fed for forty years, and every night he comes to the glass and looks at me, and he is *kind*, Jude. He is so kind. He has never once asked me to stop refusing to sign. Do you understand? He could have made me sign. Whatever they are in there, they can reach through the glass a little. He could have. And he never did, because he loves me, and love is the only thing in this house that was never written down."

The roses were turning. All of them, slowly, on their stems, their faces following us like a congregation.

"So when I sign," Edith said, "the papers sign themselves. That's how it works. That's what the founders built. A signature is a kind of door, and they have been waiting behind it for a hundred and forty years with all their names in their mouths. And when the door opens, the house will need a new bearer to hold what's inside — because a vessel must be held from within, Jude, that's the whole of the rite, that's the one line the founders never got to write —" and here her voice did break, just once, a hairline crack down the middle of the steadiness — "and it has already chosen. It chose you the day you photographed the front gate twice. It has been writing you into itself ever since. Every page. Every note. Every careful little measurement of a house that was measuring you back."

"Then I'll refuse," I said. My voice sounded far away to me, a voice heard through glass. "I'll walk out. I'll burn my notebooks."

"You could," Edith agreed gently. "And the house would take the one who promised to stay. It has been very patient with her, Jude, six years patient, because I asked it to be, because my signature was the lock on that promise. But I've signed now. And the house keeps what is loved by consuming what remembers it." She looked past me, at Wren, and her old face was unbearable with tenderness. "It remembers her, love. It has been remembering her since the day she was born in the east bedroom. You are the last thing it has left to take."

* * *

We got Edith back inside by six. The light had not changed. I do not believe it was going to change again; I believe the sun had been dismissed.

The mirrors were waiting for us in the hall.

There are eleven mirrors in the main corridor of Hollowmere House — I had catalogued them on my first day, photographed each one twice, recorded their dimensions in a hand I no longer fully trusted. They had lagged for six days, one second, then three, then five, then seven, a procession of delayed ghosts trailing us through the house. I had learned to move ahead of my own reflection like a man outrunning his shadow.

Now they did not lag at all.

Now they did not follow.

Wren saw it before I did — she had stopped using mirrors years ago, she'd told me, but you cannot unlearn the peripheral eye — and she made a sound low in her throat, and her hand closed on my sleeve, and we stood in the corridor of Hollowmere House and watched our reflections walk toward us from the far end of the hall.

Not walking with us. Walking toward us. Out of step, out of phase, out of *sync* — my reflection's stride a half-beat off from my own heartbeat, its arms swinging where mine hung still, and behind it, in mirror after mirror after mirror, receding down the corridor like a corridor itself, more of me, and more of Wren, and behind *them* — oh, God, behind them — the others. The dinner table from decades ago. The family at their candles. The faces from the portraits, moving, alive, preserved, each one looking directly out of the glass, each one looking at Wren, and one — the one at the very back, the one whose face was Harold Vane's face, worn and patient and heartbreakingly gentle — looking at Edith, and mouthing, over and over, without sound:

*It's all right. It's all right. It's all right.*

"Jude." Wren's voice was very small. "Jude, they're not — those aren't reflections anymore, are they."

"No," I said.

"They're standing in the glass. Like it's a window. Like they're in a room and the glass is the wall and we're—" She stopped. I watched her arrive at it, watched the understanding move through her like cold water through cloth. "We're the ones in the mirror. That's what it's been building. That's what the lag was. We were the delayed ones. We were the copies all along, and tonight the original comes through."

The reflections stopped, all of them together, at arm's length from the glass. My own face regarded me from an inch away, separated from me by silver and six days of documentation, and it did not wear my expression. It wore an expression I had never once permitted myself: it looked *relieved*. It looked like a man at the end of a long shift, setting down his instruments.

Then, all around us, from every room, from every drawer and desk and writing case in Hollowmere House, the pens began to write again — and this time I could hear what they were writing, because it was being written in my voice, in the rhythm of my own thoughts, in the private shorthand of my notebooks, and the house spoke it aloud through a hundred nibs at once:

*The bearer consents. The bearer has read the flaw. The bearer comes at dawn.*

And my reflection smiled my smile, and mouthed, silently, in perfect sync with me for the first and last time:

*Tell her. Or I will.*

Wren turned to me, her face white in the grey light, her hand still fisted in my sleeve — the hand I had already begun, without admitting it, to memorize.

"Jude," she said. "What flaw? What have you been hiding from me?"

Behind her, in every mirror in the house, the dead leaned closer to the glass to hear my answer.
