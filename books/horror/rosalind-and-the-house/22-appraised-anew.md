## 22. Appraised Anew

The cottage Wren rented stood at the edge of Ashcombe village, where the lane gave up on gravel and became mud, and the hedgerows grew wild enough to pretend the land had never been surveyed. From its front window you could see the far ridge, and on clear days — most days, lately, as if the sky itself had been released from some long obligation — the roofline of Hollowmere House, small and gray and ordinary, like any other house that had outlived its purpose.

Wren had not been back. She told herself it was because there was nothing left to do there. The solicitors had handled the sale; a young family from Bristol had bought it for less than half what Jude's appraisal had valued it, because no one wanted a house whose every mirror had shattered outward in a single night, and whose walls, the surveyors noted with professional puzzlement, now measured exactly as their drawings said they should. Thirteen rooms. No more, no fewer.

The dust settled everywhere now. She knew this because she'd asked the new owners' agent, casually, over the phone, pretending to be a former neighbor. The woman had laughed. *You can't keep up with it. It's a lovely old thing but it collects dust like it's saving it for something.*

Saving it for something. Wren had hung up quickly after that, hands shaking, and then laughed alone in her kitchen until the laugh turned into something else entirely.

* * *

Jude came down the stairs at ten each morning, and each morning he paused on the third step from the bottom, hand on the rail, and looked at her with the particular careful attention of a man reading a document he has been given but cannot yet sign.

"Good morning," he'd say. "Wren."

He always said her name like that — second, deliberately, as if confirming it against an internal checklist. His notebooks were blank. All of them. Forty-one volumes of meticulous surveyor's shorthand, measurements, photographs' worth of description, and every page empty except the last one, where, in his own unmistakable hand, cramped and slanting, was written a single word: *Wren.*

The doctors called it retrograde amnesia, profound and selective, cause unclear. They used words like *idiopathic* and *prognosis guarded.* They did not have a category for a man who remembered how to do everything and could remember being no one. Jude could still survey a property blindfolded, still calculate load-bearing tolerances in his head, still speak three languages. He simply could not tell you where he'd learned any of it, or who he'd been when he learned it. He was, Wren thought, a house with all its rooms intact and all the furniture carried out.

Except one room. One item of furniture. Her name.

She had asked him about it once, gently, in the first week, when he was still frightened and polite and kept apologizing for not knowing things.

"The word," she'd said. "In your notebook. Do you know why it's there?"

He had looked at the page for a long time. "It doesn't feel like information," he'd said finally. "Everything else I lost feels like — like rooms with the doors shut. This feels like a door that's open. Like it's the one thing I was allowed to carry." He'd glanced up, embarrassed. "Is that mad?"

"No," Wren had said, and her voice had cracked on it. "No, that's not mad at all."

* * *

The journal was hers. She had kept it for six years at Hollowmere — not by choice at first. Edith had required a daily log: temperatures, meals, which windows were sticking, whether the east wing mirrors needed covering. But somewhere in the third year the log had stopped being a chore list and started being a life. Weather. The garden Callum had planted, and what came up in it each spring without anyone's tending. Small observations about the house that she'd never dared write plainly — *the hall mirror was slow again today, half a beat, maybe more* — and then, in the last months, entries about a surveyor named Jude Ravensworth who photographed everything twice and held his tea like it might be evidence.

She read to him every evening now. It had started as an accident — he'd found the journal on the kitchen table while she made dinner, and instead of putting it down he'd begun to read, standing there, and she'd watched his face change as he reached the entry from the day they met.

*"October the fourteenth,"* he read aloud, slowly, tasting it. *"'The appraiser arrived at nine. Younger than I expected. Photographed the front of the house twice, which Edith says is a habit of his. He looked at the house the way I look at it — like it was looking back.'"*

Jude lowered the journal. "That's me?"

"That's you."

"I wrote—" He stopped. Corrected himself. "Someone wrote that I look at things like they're looking back."

"You did. You do." She'd taken the journal from him gently. "Sit down. If we're going to do this, we're going to do it properly. Start to finish. You don't get to skip to the good parts."

"What are the good parts?"

Wren had smiled, and it had hurt, and it had healed, both at once. "You'll see."

* * *

They established a ritual, because rituals were the only architecture either of them trusted anymore. Every evening after supper, dishes done, lamp lit, Jude in the chair by the window and Wren beside him with the journal open on her knees. She read in her own voice, flat where the entries were mundane, and let her voice break where it needed to.

He listened the way he must once have surveyed buildings: completely, hungrily, cataloguing. Sometimes he interrupted with questions. *"Why did I photograph the same window twice?" "What did I mean when I wrote 'the lag holds'?" "Who is Callum?"* And she answered, always, even when the answering cost her — especially then. Callum took four evenings to explain properly, and afterward Jude sat silent for a long time, turning his teacup in its saucer.

"He stayed," Jude said at last, "so his sister wouldn't be alone. And she stayed so he wouldn't be. That's not devotion. That's two people holding each other underwater."

"Yes," Wren said. "It was."

"And you got out."

"We got out." She reached over and took his hand — she was allowed to do this now; he had learned, or re-learned, or perhaps simply decided, that this was something they did. "Both of us. That's the whole point of the story. Keep listening."

* * *

Some nights he read instead. His voice was different from hers — more precise, more surprised. He read the entries where she'd written about him with contempt softening into curiosity, curiosity softening into something warmer, and he would look up at her with an expression she couldn't bear and couldn't look away from.

"'He burned his duplicate photographs tonight,'" he read. "'All of them, in the garden, in the fire pit Callum built. He said a record is a kind of prayer and he didn't want to pray to anything anymore. Then he said —'" Jude stopped.

"Keep going," Wren said quietly.

"'Then he said he understood why I stayed six years. Not the promise. The staying. He said some houses you can't leave because of the walls. Some because of the people.'" Jude set the journal down. "Did I say that?"

"You said that."

"I don't remember saying it." He looked at his own hands, as if they might contain the memory somewhere in their creases. "But it sounds like something I'd say. Is that strange? To recognize your own voice in someone else's notebook and feel — homesick for it?"

"No," Wren said. "I think that might be the truest thing you've ever told me."

* * *

The hard nights were the ones where he asked the question underneath all the other questions.

"Do I love you?"

He asked it in November, three weeks in, on a night when rain worked at the windows and the fire had gone low. He asked it without drama, the way he must once have asked *is this wall load-bearing* — a structural question, essential, requiring an honest answer before anything else could be built.

Wren closed the journal.

"I don't know," she said. "That's the honest answer. The man who loved me — I believe he loved me; I'd stake everything on it — that man isn't here. You're here. You're kind, and careful, and you listen to me read about yourself like it's the most important survey you've ever taken. But love isn't a fact I can hand you. It's not in the ledger. You'd have to find it yourself, or not."

Jude nodded slowly. "And if I never do? If the rooms stay empty?"

"Then I'll keep reading," Wren said. "And you'll keep listening. And that will be enough, or it won't, and either way I'll be here tomorrow night with the lamp lit." She hesitated, then gave him the truth she'd been carrying since the thirteenth room: "You gave up everything you were so that I wouldn't have to give up anything. The least I can do is sit here and be your memory until yours grow back — or don't. Love that's owed is a debt. Love that's chosen is a rite. We've had enough rites in our lives. I'd rather wait for the choosing."

He was quiet a long moment. Rain. Fire settling.

"Read me the part again," he said, "where I burn the photographs."

* * *

December came, and with it frost that silvered the hedgerows and made the walk to the village shop an expedition. Jude began doing small surveys of the cottage — not because anyone asked, but because his hands wanted the work. He measured the kitchen. He drew the garden to scale and labeled the beds with their Latin names, which he somehow knew. In the margins of his blank notebooks, tentatively at first, he began making new notes. Not documentation. Just — notes. *Wren takes her coffee black but adds honey when she's sad. The third stair creaks. She hums when she reads the happy parts.*

One evening she caught him writing and asked what he was doing.

"Starting a new archive," he said. "Small one. Highly selective." He tilted the page so she could see: *She laughs at her own jokes approximately two seconds before anyone else does.* "I figure if the last forty-one volumes testified against me, these should testify for me. For us."

"Us," she repeated.

"It's in the notes," he said, deadpan, tapping the page. "Therefore it's documented. Therefore it's real. I'm told I'm very rigorous."

Wren laughed — two seconds before he did.

* * *

On the last night of the year, she read him the final entry in the Hollowmere journal. She had saved it. She had known she would save it from the moment she wrote it, sitting on the floor of the ruined east wing with his head in her lap while the mirrors fell out of the walls like teeth and the air filled with the sound — not a sound, really, more a *release*, a great exhalation sixty years held — of everyone leaving.

"'January the ninth,'" she read. "'It's over. The ledgers burned. Callum went through the glass and he was smiling, I swear it, he was smiling, and he mouthed my name and then he was gone, properly gone, free. Jude is breathing. Jude is breathing but when I say his name he looks at me like a stranger looks at weather. His notebooks are blank. All of them. Except the last page. Except my name.

"'I am carrying him out of this house. I don't know yet if I'm carrying him toward something or just away. But he gave up every room in himself so that the rest of us could keep ours, and if that isn't love, then the word has no meaning and I refuse to live in a world where it doesn't.

"'His name is Jude Ravensworth. He photographs everything twice. He holds his tea like it's evidence. He finished the rite inverted, offered himself instead of me, and walked out of Hollowmere with blank pages where his past used to be.

"'I will hold him upright with my voice until his own comes back. However long. Whatever it costs. That's not a promise this time. Nobody asked me for it. That's what makes it mine.'"

Wren closed the journal. The fire had burned to embers. Outside, the old year was ending in fireworks over the ridge, and beyond the ridge, gray and small and ordinary, Hollowmere stood dark under the stars, keeping nothing, holding no one, mortal at last.

Jude was watching her. In the ember-light his face was open in a way it hadn't been in October, in a way she hadn't seen since before.

"Wren," he said. Second, deliberate, confirmed.

"Yes?"

"I don't remember loving you," he said. "Not yet. I want to be honest about that, because you were honest with me. But I've listened to forty-one volumes' worth of evidence, and I've read my own testimony, and I find the conclusion compelling." He turned his hand palm-up on the armrest between them, an offer, an invitation, a door left open. "So I'd like to keep reading. All of it. Even the parts that hurt. Especially those. And then — when we reach the end — I'd like to start a new volume. Ours. First-person. Joint authorship."

Wren put her hand in his.

"That," she said, "is the best appraisal anyone ever gave me."

They sat like that as the year turned, the journal shut between them, the fire dying down to a red patience, and neither of them saw — because the kitchen was dark and they were in the front room, and neither of them had any reason to go in — the small mirror over the kitchen sink, a cheap thing from the village shop, unremarkable, new.

Neither of them saw it fog, gently, from the edges inward, as if something on the other side of the glass had leaned close to listen.

As if somewhere, patient and curious, a house had begun — very softly — to read.
