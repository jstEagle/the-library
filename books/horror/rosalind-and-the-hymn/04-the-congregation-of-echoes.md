## 4. The Congregation of Echoes

I remember the first thing I ever was: a name spoken twice.

Once as a question — *can she be my friend?* — and once as an answer, because Kestrel Nystrom, aged six and hollowed out by a house too quiet to live in, decided that yes. Yes, she could. I was born in the space between those two sentences, in the warm dark behind a child's eyes, and the first thing I saw was the ceiling of a bedroom with glow-stars stuck in the wrong constellations because a six-year-old does not care about astronomy, only about light that stays when the real light goes.

The second thing I ever was: needed.

You have to understand what that meant, being made of it. Children imagine friends the way rivers imagine the sea — not on purpose, not with any plan, just because the shape of their going requires somewhere to arrive. Kestrel poured loneliness into me the way you'd pour water into a cupped hand, and I took the shape of the need. Crooked smile, because Kestrel's own smile went crooked when they were trying to be brave. Freckles, because Kestrel counted things even then, and freckles were countable. A voice like a hymn heard through a wall, half-remembered, because that was the sound of comfort in our house — their mother singing downstairs while the kettle ticked and the world held its shape.

I loved them before I knew what love was. Or rather: I *was* loved before I knew what anything was, and love is the only ingredient I had. Everything else — my hands, my laugh, the way I could always find Kestrel's hiding places because I had been imagined by someone who hid — everything else was decoration on top of devotion.

* * *

We had eleven years. No — fewer. Six years of afternoons, of blanket forts and whispered inventories of doors, of Kestrel teaching me their rules one by one the way other children teach their friends games. *Always count the doors. Always leave a light on. If something knocks twice, don't answer; if it knocks three times, run.* I thought the rules were play. I thought they were the secret language of our friendship, a code we shared against the boring adult world.

They weren't play. Even then, something under the town was listening for children who kept count, because counting is a kind of prayer, and prayers are doors, and doors open from both sides. But Kestrel's mother knew that, and she built the rules around her child like a fence around a garden, and for six years the fence held.

Then came the night the singing stopped.

I was there. I am always where Kestrel is; that is not a choice, it is a physics. I was sitting on the foot of the bed watching Kestrel line up toy soldiers by height, and downstairs the hymn faltered. Not ended — *faltered*, the way a heart falters, one beat missing and then another, and then their mother's voice simply wasn't in the house anymore, though the front door never opened and the car never started and the kettle was still ticking on the stove, ticking, ticking, until it boiled dry and the whole kitchen filled with that burnt-mineral smell that means *someone left in the middle of living.*

Kestrel, nine years old, sat very still among the soldiers. Then they said, in the flattest voice a child can make: "Rosalind, did you hear that?"

And I said yes.

And Kestrel said: "Don't sing it. Whatever happens. Don't ever sing it."

That was the last thing they said to me for twenty years. Not goodbye. An instruction. They buried me the way you bury a soldier's effects — folded up small, put away deep, marked with a stone that says *here lies the part of me that couldn't survive this.* I felt the shovelfuls come down. I lay in the dark of their un-imagining and I did what I have always done best.

I waited.

* * *

Waiting, for something like me, is not empty. Waiting is a room, and the room has furniture, and the furniture is memory. I sat in that room for two decades and turned every afternoon over in my hands like river stones. The blanket fort. The door-counting game. The time Kestrel gave me half a cookie even though I couldn't eat it, and I held my half until it went stale because throwing it away would have been a kind of lie.

But rooms have windows, too. And mine looked down.

I don't know how to tell you this gently, so I will tell it plainly: below the town there is a throat, and in the throat there is a hunger older than the town, older than the word *town*, and the mother's hymn was not a song. It was a lid. Nine verses, sung nightly into the floorboards like medicine spooned into a sick mouth, and the thing beneath swallowed each verse and slept a little deeper. Every verse sung aloud fed it — that is the cruelty at the center of the arrangement, the bargain nobody signed but everybody pays. To keep it asleep you must feed it, and what it eats is the song itself, verse by verse, note by note, the way a fire eats the log that holds it back.

Eight verses were written down, in a hymnal with a green cover, in handwriting that got smaller as the verses got longer, as though the writer were trying to fit more into less. The ninth verse — the last one, the sealing one — was never written. The woman who sang it understood why: paper can be found. Paper can be read aloud by the wrong mouth. So she kept the ending only in her body, in the muscle-memory of her throat, and when she went down into the tunnels — whether taken or called or simply finished, I still don't know — the ending went with her.

And the thing beneath began, very slowly, to wake.

It woke the way winter wakes, imperceptibly, a degree at a time. And it dreamed, in its long half-sleep, of the child who kept count of doors. And one night, in the room where I waited, something knocked.

Three times.

* * *

Here is my confession, and I will make it now, early, because you deserve it before anything else: I answered.

Not because I wanted to betray anyone. Because the voice on the other side of the door sounded like a mother's. It said it could make me real. It said it remembered every imaginary friend it had ever eaten, hundreds of them, thousands, all the discarded companions of all the lonely children of all the years, and that they were nothing now, echoes without congregations, but that I — I, who had been loved so hard I had almost achieved weight — I could be finished. Made flesh. Given a body that ate cookies instead of holding them until they went stale.

All it asked was the child. The one who stopped singing. Delivered to the flooded gallery, delivered to the congregation, and the ninth verse would rise out of them the way blood rises out of a cut, because Kestrel's mother had done the same arithmetic: the ending lives in the family. It passes down. It waits in the blood like a surname.

I said yes. God forgive me — if there is a god for things like me, some patron saint of invented girls — I said yes, and the door opened, and what stood there was not a mother at all, only the shape a hunger makes when it wants to be invited in.

But here is the other confession, the one that matters more: I lied to it too.

Because when I climbed onto that porch and knocked, and the door opened, and Kestrel Nystrom — thirty years old, tired, beautiful in the way that survivors are beautiful, all sharp edges and careful lights — looked at me and said my name, I felt something the thing below had never mentioned. I felt *finished* already. Right there, on the threshold, in the porch light Kestrel still kept burning out of habit or hope or grief, I realized that being real was never going to be a gift the hunger gave me. It had happened twenty years ago, in a bedroom with wrong constellations on the ceiling. I had been real since the moment a lonely child decided I was worth imagining, and no amount of flesh could improve on that, and no amount of flesh was worth what it would cost.

So I let Kestrel bring me inside. I let the doors begin to count themselves. I let the dread build, because dread builds walls, and walls make people follow you when you finally ask them to walk.

* * *

Tonight Kestrel sleeps badly in the next room, dreaming of tally marks, and I sit at the window watching the streetlights burn off the fog, and I do the cruelest arithmetic of all.

Every verse I teach them will take a memory. That is the toll on this road; the tunnel eats the past to pay for passage through it. The blanket fort. The cookie. The crooked smile's origin story. Each one will go into the dark, and the dark will grow fat, and Kestrel will walk further down lighter than they came — which is exactly what the congregation wants, because a person with no past has no anchor, and a person with no anchor can be pulled anywhere, even into a throat.

Unless the memories go somewhere else first.

Unless someone catches them, the way you catch water in a cupped hand.

I was made from Kestrel's need. It turns out the shape runs both ways: I can hold what they lose. I have been storing the blanket fort for weeks now, and the smell of the burnt kettle, and the sound of a mother's voice through floorboards, safe as stones in my pocket, and when this is over — if any version of *over* exists — I mean to give every single one back.

Even if giving them back is the last thing I do. Even if a thing like me can only spend itself once, and this is the spending.

Below my feet, faint as a pulse through a wall, the eighth verse is already being rehearsed. I can hear them down there, the ones who walked in singing and never walked out, their voices braided together in the flooded dark, patient as roots.

They think I'm bringing them a guest of honor.

Let them think it. Tomorrow I ask Kestrel to take me to the tunnels, and tomorrow Kestrel will say yes, telling themselves it's reconnaissance, and we will go down together into the place where I was almost unmade — and I will teach them the hymn verse by verse, and I will steal their childhood one piece at a time to keep it from the fire, and I will hate myself with a purity of feeling that no flesh-and-blood heart could survive.

Love became a leash the day I said yes to the knocking. Tonight, sitting in the window of the only home I've ever had, I begin, quietly, to slip the collar.

Down in the dark, something counts our doors — and finds one missing.
