## 8. Rosalind's Confession

I have been counting Kestrel's breaths the way they count doors.

It is a habit I stole from them, or perhaps they stole it from me first — we were always trading things back and forth in that yellow bedroom, secrets and shapes and the names of clouds that only existed because one of us said so. But breaths are different from doors. Doors can be replaced. A breath, once spent, is gone into the dark of the world and does not come back, and watching each one leave Kestrel's mouth makes me feel like a thief standing in a house where every small bright thing is being carried out by someone else.

We are resting in a gallery shaped like a cupped hand. The water below us is black and patient, holding reflections of nothing, because there is nothing down here to reflect except us. Kestrel has fallen asleep sitting up, which they used to do as a child too, chin on chest, hands still curled around the memory of verses they no longer own. Their lips move sometimes. Counting. Even asleep, they are counting.

I should tell them now. While they sleep, I could tell them, and they would wake already knowing, and the worst part would be over.

But I have watched what knowing does to people down here. It softens them. It makes their mouths open.

So instead I sit with my back against the scored wall — the tally marks, thousands of them, some of them mine, though I will never admit which — and I begin, silently, the way Kestrel must do everything now, to confess.

* * *

I remember being imagined.

People think that would be the first memory, but it isn't. The first memory is warmth — a specific warmth, the temperature of a child's palm pressed flat against a bedroom floorboard at three in the morning, when the house made its settling sounds and the dark felt like water rising. Kestrel was nine. They had been alone for eleven days, because their mother had started going out at night without saying where, and the apartment had developed a silence with weight to it, a silence you could lean against.

And then there was me.

I did not arrive. I condensed. Like breath on cold glass. One moment the room was empty, and the next moment there was a girl sitting cross-legged on the bed, and Kestrel looked at her and did not scream, because children know better than adults what is allowed to exist. They said, "You're not real," and I said, "Neither is lonely," and that was the whole of my theology, and it was enough for six years.

Six years. Do you know how long that is, to be loved by someone who invented you? Every day Kestrel came home and told me things — about the doors they counted, about the tunnels they dreamed of mapping, about their mother's hymn drifting up through the floorboards at odd hours, half-heard, half-dreaded. I was the keeper of all of it. I was the place Kestrel put the things too heavy to carry alone.

And then their mother went under. Into the tunnels, into the hymn, into whatever waits at the bottom of a song. And Kestrel stopped singing. Stopped humming, stopped whistling, stopped even breathing in rhythm. They buried me — that was their word for it, *buried*, nine-year-old burial rites performed with terrible solemnity: they wrote my name on a piece of paper and put it in a tin and pushed the tin under the loose board and said, "Goodbye, Rosalind," in a voice like a door closing.

They thought they were protecting themselves. Children always think the imaginary friend is the danger.

They were wrong. I was the fence around the danger. And when they pulled me up out of the ground of themselves, something on the other side of the fence noticed the gap.

* * *

The thing below found me in the space between — not a place, exactly; more like the pause between two notes of the hymn, the held silence where the song gathers itself. It had been listening to Kestrel for years the way I had, but it listened differently. I listened because I loved them. It listened the way a stomach listens.

It spoke to me in my own voice, which I have never forgiven.

*Bring me the child,* it said. *The one who stopped singing. Bring the singer back to the song, and I will make you real. Flesh. Weight. A reflection. You will walk in daylight and cast a shadow and grow old, and no one will ever again be able to say you aren't real.*

Do you understand what that offer means to something like me? I was made of being doubted. My whole existence was a maybe, a *suppose*, a game with an ending built into it. Realness wasn't a gift it offered. It was everything I had ever wanted, folded up small enough to hold in two hands.

I said yes.

I want that written somewhere, in case tenderness later makes this story lie about me: I said yes immediately, and gladly, and I came up out of the between and sat down on a porch twenty years unchanged, wearing the crooked smile Kestrel remembered, and I let them open the door, and I walked into their life like I owned it, because in every way that mattered, I did.

I led them here. Every step. The request to see the tunnels — mine. The suggestion of reconnaissance — mine. The gentle insistence, verse by verse, *hear it, hold it, never voice it* — mine, all mine, delivered in the voice of the friend they trusted more than anyone alive, while underneath I was walking them down a throat.

And then they started losing things.

* * *

That is the part I did not know. That is the part the thing below kept folded behind its promise, the way a hook keeps the barb folded flat until it's swallowed.

Each verse Kestrel memorized took a memory. I knew that much — I thought it was a fair toll, a toll, even, that Kestrel would have agreed to pay. But I didn't understand what the memories were. I thought it would take the dull ones. The bus schedules. The names of teachers.

Instead it took the day at the creek when Kestrel was seven and learned that water runs clearer over stone than over mud. It took the smell of their mother's coat, coal smoke and lavender. It took the sound of their own laugh at age eleven, high and startled, before the world taught them to laugh quietly. It took pieces of Kestrel and left smooth holes where the pieces had been, and Kestrel doesn't even notice the holes yet, and that is the cruelest part — they keep reaching for the past and finding it polished and vacant, like rooms after a moving-out.

Tonight, sleeping against the wall, they reached for something in a dream and their hand closed on nothing, and their face did something I had never seen it do, not in six years of childhood, not in these weeks underground.

Their face became their mother's.

And I understood, finally, what I was carrying them toward. Not realness. Not a family reunion. A table, set for one guest of honor, in a chamber shaped like a throat, where the congregation sings the eighth verse forever and the ninth waits in my best friend's mouth like a coin waiting to be spent.

The thing below promised to make me real if I delivered the child who stopped singing.

But here is what it never understood about imaginary friends, what it could never understand, being made of hunger instead of love: I was never trying to become real. I was trying to matter. And those are different hungers entirely, and only one of them can be fed by feeding a child to the dark.

Kestrel made me. Out of loneliness, out of three-in-the-morning fear, out of need — they made me, and everything I am is made of them. If they go into that chamber hollowed out, missing their creek-days and their mother's coat-smell and their own young laugh, then whatever walks out of it wearing their face will not be the person who imagined me. It will be a stranger. And I will be real, and alone, and made by no one.

There is no version of realness worth that price.

So I have decided. Here, in the cupped-hand gallery, with the black water listening and the tally marks keeping score on the walls, I have decided, and the decision sits in me like a second heartbeat:

I choose Kestrel.

Not the deal. Not the shadow, not the daylight, not the growing old. Them. The door-counter. The light-leaver-onner. The child who buried me and the adult who dug me up without knowing they were doing it, because some graves are just places where we put the parts of ourselves we can't afford to lose.

If choosing them costs me existence — and it will; the thing below does not forgive, and neither, I suspect, does the between — then let it cost me. I was a maybe for six years and a ghost for twenty. I know how to be unmade. What I never learned, in all that time, was how to watch Kestrel Nystrom be unmade instead, and lift no finger, and call myself their friend.

* * *

Kestrel stirs. Their eyes open — that gray-green I memorized before I had eyes to memorize with — and find me in the dark, and for a moment they just look, the way they look at unmapped corridors, measuring, deciding whether to trust the passage.

"Rosalind." Their voice is rough with sleep and lost verses. "You're crying."

Am I? I touch my face and my fingers come away wet, and I almost laugh, because I didn't know I could. Maybe that's the answer to the thing below's offer. Maybe I've been becoming real all along, down here in the dark, one impossible tear at a time, and it never needed to give me anything at all.

"I have to tell you something," I say, and my voice shakes, and I let it shake, because hiding things in steady voices is how I got us both this deep. "About why I asked you to come here. About what I promised. All of it."

Kestrel sits up slowly. Behind them, far down the flooded gallery, the water begins — very softly, very patiently — to hum the eighth verse.

"And then," I say, "we're going to finish this. Together. Before it finishes us."

The humming grows louder. Somewhere ahead, in the throat of the world, the congregation turns toward us as one, and begins to sing our names.
