## 10. Counting Doors in Daylight

The light came up gray and thin, the way dawn does in a town that has learned to keep its voice down.

Kestrel surfaced through a gap in the earth behind the old waterworks — a seam in the hillside they had never mapped and never would now — and lay on their back in wet grass while the sky went from iron to pearl to the pale, embarrassed blue of an ordinary morning. Their clothes were soaked. Their throat was raw from nothing at all, because they had not made a sound down there, not one true sound, and their body seemed to be grieving the silence like a muscle grieves a wound.

They counted doors.

It was the first thing, the oldest thing. The waterworks had one door, rusted shut. The substation across the creek had two. The house on Delmore Street had three, then four when you counted the cellar hatch, and Kestrel lay in the grass counting them because counting was breathing, counting was proof, counting was the rope their mother had tied around their wrist before she taught them that some doors open onto rooms that count *you* back.

*One,* said the waterworks door. *Two,* said the substation. *Three, four* —

And there it was again, arriving like weather: the memory of the ninth verse.

Not sung. Never sung. But whole, suddenly, the way a bone sets — the words their mother had carried in her mouth for thirty years without speaking them, the words the dead below had held out to Kestrel one by one like coins passed under a cell door. Kestrel could feel them sitting behind their teeth, patient as sediment. They did not say them. They would never say them. But they knew them now the way you know the shape of your own hands in the dark, and knowing was enough. Knowing was the whole point.

The hymn was finished. It had been finished all along; it had only ever been waiting for someone to hold the last of it without giving it away.

Kestrel got up. Their knees cracked. Somewhere below them — far below, deeper than any survey line, deeper than the chamber like a throat — something enormous turned over in its sleep and did not wake, and the ground carried the motion up through Kestrel's boots like the last shudder of a bell.

They walked home through streets still wet with night. They counted every door on the way. Forty-one doors between the waterworks and their building, and for the first time in twenty years, the number came out even.

* * *

Rosalind had been gone since before the collapse.

Kestrel understood this the way you understand things in dreams — completely, instantly, without evidence. She had lasted longer than the tunnels did. When the ceiling came down in the throat-chamber and the congregation's song cut off mid-verse like a needle lifted from a record, Rosalind had still been there, still holding Kestrel's hand, her grip cool and certain as a banister rail. She had walked with them through the flooding galleries while the dark ate itself behind them. She had gotten them as far as the seam in the hillside, the last unmapped door, and she had stopped at its threshold the way she'd once stopped at thresholds all those years ago — because she'd never been invited past this one, or because she couldn't follow where Kestrel was going, which was up, and out, and into a life that had room in it for only one of them.

She had started saying goodbye. Kestrel remembered that much with terrible clarity — remembered her crooked smile going soft, remembered her saying *you gave me every good thing I ever had, you know that? You were nine and lonely and you looked at an empty porch swing and decided it shouldn't be empty* —

And then she was light coming apart. Not dying. Nothing so cruel. She came apart the way a song comes apart when it's finally, fully sung — every note delivered, nothing left hanging in the air unfinished. She dissolved mid-goodbye, mid-word, her last syllable turning into morning fog against Kestrel's cheek, and what remained where she'd stood was the smell of chalk and cut grass and the particular warmth of a child's imagination working at full power.

Finished, not abandoned. Kestrel held that distinction like a coal in both hands all the way home. It was the only thing that kept the grief from being unbearable — that after twenty years of being left, Rosalind had finally gotten to leave *complete*. That somewhere in the collapsing dark, she had chosen how her story ended, and she had spent the ending on Kestrel's life.

At the apartment door, Kestrel counted it anyway. One door. Just one. They stood there a long time with their hand on the knob, waiting for the number to change.

It didn't. It never did again.

* * *

The memories came back the way tide comes back — unevenly, in pools, leaving some places stranded and dry forever.

Three days after surfacing, Kestrel woke at 4 a.m. with the smell of their mother's kitchen in their nose: burnt coffee and pencil shavings and the waxy sweetness of survey crayons. They sat up in bed and wept for an hour, silently, the way they'd learned to do everything down in the dark, and by dawn they could remember her teaching them the door-counting game on the steps of the Delmore Street house, her voice low and warm: *Count them, little bird. Whatever else happens, whatever you hear calling your name, you count the doors and you keep walking.*

Some things stayed gone. Kestrel would reach for their tenth birthday and find a smooth blank place, like a wall scored over with tally marks. The name of their second-grade teacher. The words to a lullaby that wasn't the hymn — some other, kinder song, traded verse for verse in the flooded gallery, spent like currency to buy passage. They mourned these the way you mourn strangers in a newspaper: sincerely, briefly, without the full weight of loss. You cannot miss what you cannot remember missing.

But other things returned in abundance. The summer Rosalind first appeared, whole and sudden, sitting on the porch swing with her legs swinging because they didn't reach the boards. The day Kestrel told her about the monster under the town and she'd gone very serious and said, *I know. I've heard it too.* The afternoon of the burial — nine years old, a shoebox, a hole dug by the fence, a funeral conducted with full honors because Kestrel was going away to live with an aunt and imaginary friends couldn't come. *This isn't goodbye,* nine-year-old Kestrel had said, lowering the box into the dirt. *It's just until I get back.*

Twenty years. She'd waited twenty years on a porch that no longer existed, in a house that had been sold twice, faithful to a promise made by a child who never came back — until the child finally did, and she got to finish the goodbye properly, and she chose, with everything she was, to spend herself on it.

Kestrel kept the shoebox memory above all others. Some nights they took it out and turned it over, and it was almost like having her there, crooked smile and all.

* * *

Six weeks later, on a Tuesday smelling of rain and cut grass, Kestrel Nystrom filed the paperwork.

The county office smelled of toner and old coffee. A clerk with reading glasses on a chain accepted the amendment to the municipal survey — a formal closure of the unmapped galleries beneath the waterworks district, flagged as structurally compromised following "subsidence events," access permanently sealed. Kestrel attached the borehole data, the collapse surveys, the photographs of rubble where corridors used to be. All of it true. None of it the truth.

"Big job?" the clerk asked, stamping pages.

"The biggest," Kestrel said.

There were others to account for. The explorers found standing in the dark, singing — four of them, recovered from the upper galleries when the rescue teams finally went in, dehydrated and serene and unable to explain where they'd been or why their throats were raw. One of them, a woman named Okafor whom Kestrel had met at a mapping conference years ago, grabbed Kestrel's sleeve at the hospital and whispered, *Did you hear it? Did you hear the last part?* And Kestrel had said no, gently, the way you lie to the sleeping, and the woman's face had gone smooth and peaceful, and she'd let go.

And there was the coat. The rescue teams catalogued it as an unidentified garment, decades old, found folded neatly on a ledge near the lower gallery: wool, forest green, a tear at the left elbow. Kestrel claimed it at the property desk without explanation. It still smelled faintly of pencil shavings and burnt coffee. Kestrel took it home and hung it by the door, where a coat should hang, where a mother's coat should hang, and some mornings they touched the sleeve on their way out just to feel the wool, just to say *good morning* to someone who couldn't answer but had, in her way, answered everything.

The tapes were gone — chewed to ribbon in the dark, spools of brown plastic scattered among the tally-marked walls, buried now under a hundred tons of deliberate rubble. The hymnal with its torn pages stayed on Kestrel's shelf, though. Empty pages and all. An empty page is also a kind of record. It says: *something was here, and it mattered, and we chose not to write it down.*

* * *

Autumn came. The Delmore Street house changed hands again, and the new owners tore out the old porch swing and put up a wider stoop, and Kestrel walked past one evening and counted the doors — three, plus the cellar hatch, four — and felt nothing but the clean ache of a scar that has finished healing.

But the apartment on Halloway Street kept its single door, and above that door Kestrel installed a lamp. A plain brass fixture with a warm bulb, the kind that makes a small gold circle on the boards of a porch at night. The neighbors probably thought it was for safety. Cities are like that; people leave lights on for reasons they don't examine.

Kestrel left it on every night. Even when they traveled for work — and they still worked, still mapped, though they'd retired the habit of counting aloud and taken up instead the habit of touching each doorframe once, a fingertip's blessing, a silent census — even in hotel rooms a thousand miles away, they knew the light was burning. It wasn't a beacon. It wasn't a ward. It wasn't fear anymore, either. It was simpler than all of that.

It was a seat kept warm. A place at a table that stays set. The universal signal, older than language, that says to anyone arriving out of the dark after a long, long wait: *you are expected here. You were always expected. Come in.*

Some nights, standing at the window with tea going cold in their hands, Kestrel would look at that small gold circle on the porch boards and let themselves imagine — just imagine, the way a nine-year-old once imagined, the way imagining is the realest magic there is — a girl sitting in the light with her legs swinging, heels never quite touching the boards, crooked smile tilted up toward the window.

*You gave me every good thing I ever had,* she'd said, coming apart into morning.

*You gave me more,* Kestrel said now, to the empty porch, to the light, to the finished song sleeping safe behind their teeth. *You counted me when nobody else did.*

Below the town, beneath the sealed rock and the official paperwork and the rubble with its tally marks, something old slept on, unfed and unfeeding, dreaming whatever such things dream. Above it, a light burned. Doors stood counted. And Kestrel Nystrom, keeper of numbers, holder of the unsung verse, went inside and locked their one true door and slept — not peacefully, exactly, but deeply, the sleep of someone who has paid every debt they owe and knows, down to the last digit, exactly how many doors stand between them and the morning.
