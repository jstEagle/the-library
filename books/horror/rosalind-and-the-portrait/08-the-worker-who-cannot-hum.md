## 8. The Worker Who Cannot Hum

Tomas had always been the one who sang.

Not well — that was Petra's honest assessment, delivered once at a crew dinner when he'd been showing off — but constantly. He hummed on the scaffold. He whistled in the portaloos. He sang half-remembered pop choruses into the echo of the pump house and made them sound like anthems. His mother, three valleys over, said he'd been humming before he could walk. It was the thing people knew about him. Tomas sings.

So when he opened his mouth on the fourth morning after the door came open, and nothing came out — not a note, not a shape, just air moving through a throat like wind through an empty pipe — he did what any sensible man would do.

He tried again.

* * *

The chamber was still sealed now, tarped and fenced with orange mesh, but the site hadn't gone back to normal. Nothing about Rosalind went back to normal. The villagers watched them from doorways with a patience that felt less like hostility than like waiting — the way you wait outside a sickroom. And the well, which had been dry for as long as anyone could remember, had started doing something no one wanted to name.

Tomas noticed it first because he was the one sent to check the pumps.

It was early, mist still sitting in the bowl of the valley, and the old well stood at the center of the village square like a stone tooth. He walked past it every morning. That morning he stopped, because there was a sound coming out of it — faint, wet, rhythmic — and when he leaned over the low rim to look down, the darkness below wasn't empty anymore.

Water. Maybe a foot of it, black and utterly still, lying at the bottom of a well that had been dry since his grandfather's grandfather.

He should have reported it immediately. Instead — and this was the part he would turn over and over later, looking for the moment it went wrong — he cupped his hands around his mouth and called down into it, the way boys do, to hear the echo.

"Hello!"

The well answered: *"Hello."*

But it wasn't his voice. It was close — same pitch, same flat local vowels — but smoothed somehow, the way a face looks smooth underwater. Like someone had taken the sound of him and ironed it.

His skin went cold all over. He stepped back from the rim, heart hammering, and told himself: interference, acoustics, the shaft acting like a pipe organ, any textbook explanation. He was an engineer's employee; he believed in textbooks.

Then, without deciding to, he hummed.

It was reflex more than anything — the little descending phrase he always hummed when he was nervous, four notes, unconscious as breathing. He got through two of them before his throat simply closed. Not choked. Closed, deliberate, like a hand wrapping around his voice box from the inside. The third note never happened. He stood there with his mouth open, pushing air, and nothing came.

And down in the well, the black water rose.

He saw it happen. He will swear to this until the day he dies: the level climbed — smoothly, silently, an inch up the mossy inner stones — in perfect time with the two notes he'd managed. As if the water had been drinking, and those two notes were the swallow.

Tomas sat down hard on the cobbles and didn't get up for a long while.

* * *

By noon the whole crew knew, because Tomas couldn't hide it and eventually stopped trying. Petra made him repeat it twice, taking notes in her flat engineering shorthand, her face carefully neutral in the way Isandro had already learned meant she was rattled.

"So your voice—" she began.

"Works fine." His own words came out rough and ordinary. Speaking was untouched. "Talking's fine. Shouting's fine. I can't carry a tune. I can't even find one. It's like—" He groped for it. "You know when you go to say a word and it's just gone? It's like that, but for every song I've ever known. There's a hole where they used to be."

Petra wrote something and underlined it.

Isandro stood at the edge of the group, saying nothing, watching Tomas the way he'd learned to watch the villagers — for what they did with their hands, their eyes, their silences. The young man kept touching his throat. Not rubbing it. Measuring it, almost, like a man checking whether a door is still locked.

That evening Isandro found him alone at the site fence and asked, gently, if he'd sing for him. Just try.

"I can't."

"I know. Try anyway. I want to see what happens."

Tomas looked at him for a long moment — a look Isandro would come to know well in the weeks ahead, the look of a man deciding how much truth another man can hold — and then he walked to the well, stood at its rim, and hummed.

Or tried. Two notes again, the same two, forced out through whatever was clamped inside him. Thin, airless, barely melody at all.

The water rose an inch.

Isandro heard it this time — a soft lap against stone, obscene in its smallness — and felt the hair rise along his arms. He made Tomas do it a third time, and a fourth, hating himself for it, needing the pattern. Each attempt cost Tomas something visible; each attempt, the water climbed. Never more than an inch. Never less.

"It's not random," Isandro said quietly, mostly to himself. "It's payment. You sing, it drinks."

"Drinks what?"

Isandro looked at the young man's grey face and didn't answer right away, because the answer had arrived whole in his mind and it was terrible in its simplicity. Every partial performance of the harvest song — every dusk for generations, cut short before the last verse — had been a meal left at a door. Something behind that sealed stone had been fed on sung sound for two hundred years, starved of the ending, kept alive on scraps. And now the door had been disturbed, and it had begun reaching further. It had reached into a young man's chest and drunk the music out of him, and left the speaking, because speaking wasn't food.

"It drinks songs," Isandro said. "And it's started taking them from us instead of waiting for us to bring them."

* * *

Maren knew before anyone told her.

She came to the square at dusk — the hour when the village had always gathered, before, to sing the harvest song once and leave before it ended — and found Tomas sitting on the well's rim like a boy at the edge of a grave. The villagers who drifted after her stopped in a loose ring, keeping distance from the well itself, and Isandro watched Maren read the scene in one sweep: the boy, the water line marked in chalk by the crew, the folklorist standing guard over both.

"How high?" she asked. Her voice was flat.

"Two feet," Petra said, arriving with her clipboard. "Steady rate. Roughly correlated with—"

"With singing." Maren said it like a diagnosis. She looked at Tomas, and something in her face cracked — not anger. Grief, old and rehearsed. "Your grandmother sang at my wedding," she told him. "Whole family full of voices. And now look."

"Maren," Isandro started.

"You'll be quiet." She didn't raise her voice; she never needed to. "This is what comes of prying. This is what comes of doors opened and tapes running. The founders sealed that thing away and starved it, and the price of the starving was silence where silence mattered, and you—" her finger moved between Isandro and Petra like a compass needle finding north, "—you've been feeding it from the other side of the wall."

"We haven't sung anything," Petra said.

"No," Maren agreed. "But it's learning the kitchen now, isn't it? It knows where the food lives."

She turned to the villagers. "No singing. No humming. No whistling. Children included. If it wants music, we give it nothing." A pause, and then, quieter, in the tone of a woman laying a flower on a grave: "We should have counted better."

They dispersed into the blue evening, and the village of Rosalind went silent in a way Isandro had never heard a place go silent — not peaceful, but held, like a breath. Somewhere a dog barked and was shushed. Somewhere a mother caught a child mid-croon and clamped a hand over the small mouth, gentle and terrified at once.

Tomas stayed on the well rim.

Isandro sat beside him. For a while neither spoke, and the black water lay below them, still as poured ink, two feet up the stones and patient.

"My mum says I hummed before I could walk," Tomas said finally. His voice was steady, which somehow made it worse. "It's the only thing I've ever been. Not good at anything else, really. Just — the singing one." He swallowed. "When I reach for it now, there's a shape where it was. Like a tooth socket. And I keep thinking — is it gone, or is it held? Because if it's held somewhere, then somewhere it can be given back."

Isandro's blood ran cold, because he understood — too late to stop it — exactly what kind of hope that was, and who else might be listening to it.

"Don't," he said. "Whatever you're thinking. Don't go looking for it."

"Looking for what?"

"The thing that took it doesn't give things back. It keeps accounts."

Tomas smiled, and it was the saddest thing Isandro had seen in Rosalind, sadder than the sealed door, sadder than the extra bolt thrown from within. "Funny," the young man said. "Down in that water, when I called hello and it answered me — it sounded like it was practicing."

He stood, brushed off his trousers, and walked away toward the lights of the crew camp, and Isandro sat alone with the well.

Below him, faint as a thought, the black water moved. Not rising. Turning. And in the turning, if he bent close enough — he did bend close enough, God help him — he could hear it, threaded through the soft lap of water on stone:

Four notes. Descending. The little nervous phrase Tomas had hummed his whole life.

Practiced once. Practiced twice.

Getting better.

* * *

Isandro didn't sleep. He sat in his room at Maren's inn with the tape recorder running, listening to the village not-sing, and near midnight he played back the ambient recordings from his first nights — before the door, before everything — hunting for something he couldn't yet name.

He found it on the second reel. Beneath the wind, beneath the counted doors, there was a thread of sound so faint he'd missed it a dozen times: the harvest song, sung at dusk by thirty voices, cutting off clean before the final verse. And underneath the cutoff, under the silence where the ending should have been, another sound. Low. Wet. Patient.

A voice, following the melody. Half a beat behind. Learning.

He sat very still in the dark, the headphones pressing the sound into his skull, and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding. Thirty voices, once a year, for two hundred years, each performance a lesson, each missing verse a wall. And now the wall had a crack in it, and the student behind it had graduated from listening to taking — had reached up through the well and emptied a living man of his music in a single morning.

It wasn't haunting the village. It was studying it.

And tomorrow, Isandro thought, switching off the recorder, he would go and see Auntie Sefu — because if the thing behind the door was learning the song note by note, stolen voice by stolen voice, then somewhere in that old woman's guarded memory lay the only thing it didn't have:

The verse that was cut.

The verse that finished the song.

The verse that, sung whole, opened the door.
