## 14. Tomas Sings

Tomas hadn't slept in nine days.

Not because of the dreams — though there were dreams, thin ones, like the memory of a memory — but because every time he closed his eyes he could almost hear it: a note, held low and warm, somewhere just under his own heartbeat. His voice was still there. He could feel it sitting behind his sternum like a swallowed stone. It simply would not come out.

He'd tried everything. Humming in the shower until his chest ached. Whispering scales into his cupped hands at three in the morning while Maren's house creaked through its counted rounds overhead. Once, drunk on two glasses of the inn's terrible plum brandy, he'd stood on the green and opened his mouth at the sky and produced nothing at all — not even breath shaped like sound. Just air. The village dogs had looked at him with something close to pity.

The doctor in Vell had called it conversion disorder. The doctor had never heard the well water say his name.

That was what broke him, in the end. Not the silence itself, but the water. Three nights ago he'd gone down to the old well because he couldn't stand the walls of his room anymore, and he'd leaned over the lip, and the black water — rising now, rising every day, patient as arithmetic — had rippled once and spoken back to him in *his own voice*, clear as a bell over clean snow:

*You left it down here.*

And God help him, some part of him had wanted to answer.

* * *

He told himself he wasn't going to do it. He told himself that all day Friday, walking the survey lines for Petra Vasquez with his instruments and his clipboard and his mouth full of nothing, and he told himself again Saturday morning when Isandro Oyelaran caught his arm outside the parish hall and said, quietly, "Whatever you're thinking of doing tonight — don't."

"How did you know I was—"

"Because I'm thinking it too," the folklorist said. "Every hour. That's how it works. It doesn't threaten you, Tomas. It *offers*. A threat you can refuse. An offer just sits there, being reasonable, until one bad night you take it." His grip was strong and his eyes were red-rimmed and exhausted. "It has your voice. If you sing to it, you're not talking to yourself. You're feeding it a whole meal through its favorite straw."

Tomas had nodded. He'd meant it. He'd walked back to Maren's, eaten the stew she put in front of him without tasting it, listened to her count the doors — eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, nineteen, twenty, twenty-one, twenty-two, twenty-three, and always, always, the pause before the last number where her voice went flat and careful, *twenty-four* — and then he'd lain in the dark listening to the well fill.

He could hear it now from anywhere in the village. That was new. The water had a sound like slow applause, like a crowd far away clapping for something only it could see.

At half past midnight he got up.

* * *

The streets were empty and ankle-deep in places. That was the other thing nobody said out loud: the water wasn't staying in the well anymore. It came up between the cobbles in threads, silver-green in the moonlight, and where it pooled it held reflections of things that weren't there. Tomas waded past the bakery and saw his own face in a puddle, smiling when he wasn't.

The well sat in its little ring of leaning stones at the bottom of the square, older than the church, older than the names on the graves. The excavation crew had fenced it with orange plastic mesh that someone had already torn down. The moon was up and fat and the water inside the well was higher than Tomas had ever seen it — six feet down now instead of forty, black and utterly still, a coin of perfect darkness.

He put both hands on the cold stone lip and looked down at himself looking up.

"Please," he whispered. His voice worked fine for words. Words were allowed. Words were the crumbs they let him keep. "I just want it back. One note. One note and I'll go."

The water didn't move.

He knew it was listening anyway. He could feel the attention come up out of the dark like heat off a road, that pressure against his eardrums he'd first felt the day the door was opened, the day he'd walked into the chamber because he was young and the foreman's warning had sounded exactly like superstition. He remembered the chamber now in fragments: the fused hinges, the bolts thrown from the inside, the walls covered edge to edge in carved notation that Petra photographed like it was a treasure map and not a warning label. He remembered standing in the middle of it feeling *welcome*, which should have been the worst part, and wasn't.

"Come on," he whispered. "You have it. I know you have it. Give it back."

Stillness. Then, so gently he might have imagined it, the surface trembled.

And his own voice rose out of the well.

*Tomas.* Not mocking. Not cruel. Tender, the way his mother used to say it when he was small and feverish, the way no one had said it since she died. *Tomas, you sound tired.*

"I can't—" His throat closed. Opened. "I can't sing. You took it."

*I kept it safe,* said the water, in his voice, warm as bathwater. *You dropped it down here and I picked it up. That's all. Come and see.*

"That's not true."

*Isn't it?* A ripple crossed the coin of dark water, and in the ripple he heard — faintly, beautifully — a hummed note. His note. The low warm one that lived under his heartbeat. *It's right here, Tomas. It's been here the whole time, waiting for you to want it enough.*

"I don't want it enough," he said, and heard the lie crack in half as he said it.

*No,* the water agreed kindly. *You want it more than that.*

* * *

Later — much later, when people asked him why, and they would ask, Maren and Petra and the folklorist with his silent mouth and his hungry notebook — Tomas would try to explain that it hadn't felt like temptation. Temptation implies a choice made in full knowledge, a devil with a contract. This was smaller and worse. It felt like remembering. Like the water was holding up a mirror to a version of him that had never lost anything, and saying *this is the real one, this is who you're supposed to be, everyone else is asking you to live as less than yourself.*

The thing behind the door had spent two hundred years learning their names from half a song. And it had learned, in all that time, exactly one lesson about human beings: that they would walk into the dark for the promise of being whole.

Tomas climbed onto the well's stone lip.

The water was close enough now that he could have touched it with his foot. He didn't touch it. He just sat there, straddling the cold rim, looking down into the black, and the black looked back with his own face wearing an expression of infinite patience.

"One note," he said. "If I give you one note, will you give me mine back?"

Silence. But it was a different quality of silence — the held-breath silence of something that has waited two hundred years and can wait one more minute.

His mother's voice, next. Out of the water, out of nowhere, gentle as a hand on his hair: *Sing, love. It's only singing. Everyone sings.*

And that was the trap laid open, and he saw it, he genuinely saw it — Isandro's voice in his head, *it offers, it offers* — and he stepped off the rim anyway, or maybe he never decided to, maybe wanting is a kind of falling — and he hummed.

One note. Low. Warm. The note under his heartbeat.

It came out ragged and cracked after nine days of silence, barely more than breath, and it was the most beautiful sound he had ever made in his life.

The well answered instantly. The water didn't ripple; it *rose*, a smooth inch of black climbing the inner stones, and out of it poured his stolen voice — not echoing, *harmonizing*, taking his single thread of sound and weaving it into the chord it had been starving for. The note swelled. The puddles in the square began to shiver in rings. Somewhere above, in the leaning houses, shutters banged open and lamps flared green.

And beneath the square, beneath the water, beneath everything — metal began to move.

Tomas felt it through the stone under his knees before he heard it: a deep grinding shudder, tectonic, patient. The sound of a bolt the width of a man's arm beginning, after two hundred years, to slide.

*Yes,* sang the water in his voice, in his mother's voice, in a chorus of voices he almost recognized from the graves on the hill. *Yes, that's it, that's the sound, more, Tomas, more—*

He gave it more. Of course he gave it more. He opened his mouth and pushed the hum louder, sobbing now, weeping and humming at once, because it felt like being born, it felt like the nine days of silence peeling off him like wet paper, it felt like—

Hands closed on his collar and hauled him backward off the rim.

* * *

They hit the cobbles together, Tomas and the folklorist, Isandro's knee cracking against stone, and the hum tore out of Tomas's throat mid-note and left him gasping. The well screamed.

There was no other word for it. The water convulsed and every voice it had ever taken came up at once — his, his mother's, Auntie Sefu's, Maren's, a hundred years of harvest singers — a chord of fury and loss that bent the air. The green lamplight stuttered in a rhythm Tomas suddenly recognized with his skin: the song's meter, the counted pattern, the tempo of a lock turning.

"Get him away!" Isandro's voice, raw, dragging Tomas up by the arm. "Get him off the square — MOVE!"

Petra Vasquez materialized out of the dark with her headlamp blazing, grabbed Tomas's other arm, and between them they hauled him backward across the flooded cobbles while the well churned behind them like something boiling. At the edge of the square Tomas twisted around for one last look and wished he hadn't.

The water had gone still again. Perfectly, obscenely still. And rising out of it, faint as breath on glass, his own face — smiling, satisfied, unhurried.

Then, in his voice, sweet and reasonable and carrying easily across the water:

*Thank you, Tomas. That was the first one.*

Behind them, deep under the earth, something enormous finished moving and settled into place with a boom he felt in his teeth. One bolt. Slid free.

Isandro stopped dragging him. Stood there in the ankle-deep water, breathing hard, staring back toward the well, and Tomas watched the color leave the folklorist's face as he did the same arithmetic everyone in Rosalind would be doing by dawn.

Two hundred years, six bolts, one gone. Five left between them and the dark.

And the well, which had risen an inch for a single broken note, was already climbing for the second.
