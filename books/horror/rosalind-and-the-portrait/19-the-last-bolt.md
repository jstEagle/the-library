## 19. The Last Bolt

Tomas woke with the melody already in his mouth.

Not in his head — in his mouth. It sat on his tongue like a stone he'd swallowed whole and never passed, warm now, humming against his teeth. He lay in the dark of the crew quarters above the survey office and felt it there, patient as a seed, and he understood with a clarity that felt like falling that it had been waiting for him to sleep.

They had taken the song from him at the door. Nine days ago he'd walked into that chamber because he was twenty-three and the foreman's warning sounded like an old man's fear, and he'd come back out again with his boots wet and something missing — the part of him that made music. He couldn't hum. He couldn't whistle. When he tried to sing along to the radio, what came out was air shaped like nothing, and every time he failed, somewhere below the village the well rose another inch.

He knew this because Isandro Oyelaran told him, gently, in the yellow light of the inn kitchen, holding a measuring stick like a man holding bad news. *It drinks through you, Tomas. Every time you reach for the song and can't find it, you leave the tap open.*

So he had stopped reaching. Four days of not reaching. Four days of pressing his lips shut when the melody pressed up from underneath, four days of counting his own breathing the way Maren counted doors, and it had almost worked.

Almost.

Now the song was in his mouth, and it was not asking anymore.

He sat up. The room was cold in the wrong way, cold from below, and through the floorboards he could feel a vibration like a held note — the well, filling, filling, black water climbing the old stones by inches while the village slept around it with lamps burning green at their edges. Petra had rigged floodgates at the cistern outflow two nights ago, great iron sluices from the mitigation works, and they were holding. Everyone said they were holding.

Tomas put his feet on the floor and the vibration came up through his soles and into the stone in his mouth, and the stone sang one note without his permission.

He clapped both hands over his lips. Too late. Far away, under everything, he heard water move — a long, glad swallow.

*No,* he thought. *No, no—*

But even inside his skull the thought had a tune to it now, and the tune was the harvest song, all of it, every verse including the one cut away before his grandfather's grandfather was born, the verse Isandro had learned silent and spoken like a key, the verse that lived scored into the chamber walls in notation older than the parish itself. The thing behind the door had been feeding him the whole song for nine days, note by note, through the hole it had made in him. It hadn't stolen his voice. It had hollowed him into a pipe.

And a pipe only wants one thing.

He got up. He didn't decide to get up; his legs simply went, and he followed them down the stairs past the sleeping bunks, past Vasquez's maps pinned glowing under a work lamp, out into the street where the fog stood waist-high and green-lit, every lamp in Rosalind burning that drowned aquarium color since the night Petra photographed the walls.

The well was at the end of the lane. It was always at the end of the lane now. However else the streets arranged themselves in the fog, the lane ran downhill toward the well, and tonight it ran downhill like a throat.

Tomas walked it.

* * *

He heard them before he saw them — voices low and urgent at the wellhead, lantern light swinging. Isandro, wrapped in a coat over his pajamas, and Petra Vasquez in her field jacket with a clipboard she wasn't reading, and between them old Maren holding a lamp with both hands like an offering.

"—forty-one centimeters since midnight," Petra was saying. "That's triple the rate. The gates are taking it but the outflow channel's silting up, it's—" She stopped. She'd seen him.

"Tomas." Isandro stepped forward, hands out, the way you approach a dog standing on ice. "Tomas, go back inside."

"I didn't mean to—" His voice came out cracked and ordinary and he hated how ordinary it sounded. "I woke up and I was already walking. I couldn't stop walking. It's in my mouth, señor, the whole song, it's been teaching me in my sleep and I can feel it, I can feel it wanting to come out—"

"Get him away from the wellhead," Maren said, and her voice wasn't cruel but it was flat as a bolt head. "Bind his mouth if you have to. We did it before, in my grandmother's time. There are ways."

"There are no ways," Isandro said. "There's only distance and there isn't enough of it anymore."

The water was visible now. That was the worst of it — you could see it, six feet down the shaft, black and utterly still, and on its surface, faint as breath on glass, moved shapes that might have been reflections of the three people leaning over it except that there were five of them, then seven, faces surfacing and sinking, mouths opening. The well was showing them singers. It was showing them choir after choir of the dead, standing in rows, singing the song with its ending, and the water carried the sound up faintly, sweetly, in voices that knew harmonies no living throat had managed in two hundred years.

Tomas's mouth filled with the first note.

He bit down on it. The note went anyway, muffled, a hum through clenched teeth, and the black surface shivered with pleasure.

"Down," Petra snapped, and hauled him back from the rim by the collar, and Isandro got an arm around his chest, and for a moment the three of them were just men grappling in fog while an old woman held up a lamp, and it would have been almost comic if the water weren't rising to meet them, if the ground beneath their feet weren't thrumming like the body of a struck instrument.

"The gates," Petra gasped. "If I drop the sluices fully open, drain it faster than it fills—"

"It'll fill faster," Isandro said. "It's been matching us. It matches everything we do. That's what it does, Petra, it learns—"

"Then we stall it. We don't win, we stall." She was already moving, wrenching free of the tangle, running for the gatehouse at the cistern where the iron wheels waited. "Isandro — hold him. Whatever happens, whatever he says, whatever voice comes out of him — hold him."

She ran. Her boots hammered the planking of the cistern walk. And Tomas, bent double in Isandro's grip, felt the thing behind the door notice her running feet and find their rhythm, and begin, softly, to keep time.

* * *

The wheel was stiff with two hundred years of disuse and Petra threw her whole weight against it, once, twice, and on the third turn it broke loose with a scream of rust and began to grind. Above her the sluice gears engaged, tooth by tooth, and far below, in the dark channels under the village, iron doors dragged themselves open across the outflow tunnels.

The sound the well made when the draining started was not a sound water makes.

It was a sound a mouth makes when the meal is pulled away.

The thrumming underfoot stuttered. The black surface dropped — a hand's width, two — and the faces in it stretched and thinned as they sank, mouths still open, still singing, the harmony bending flat, bending desperate. In the fog the green lamps guttered all together, once, like a flinch.

"Keep turning," Petra shouted to herself, because there was no one to shout it to, and turned. The wheel fought her. Somewhere in the mechanism something was resisting — not rust now, not friction, something deliberate pushing back against the gears, and the chain trembled in its housing like a held breath. She braced her boot against the housing and hauled, and the sluice came down another foot, and the well groaned, and dropped another hand's width.

Through the fog she could hear Isandro shouting her name. Then she could hear Isandro's voice saying other things, closer, right beside her ear though no one stood beside her — *Petra, let go, Petra, you're hurting it, Petra, your son asked about you today, he wants his mother, let go* — and she knew it was the water, wearing the folklorist's voice like a coat, and she did not let go. She had spent her whole career believing that things which spoke were things that could be reasoned with, and the last three weeks had killed that belief slowly, and this was the funeral: she turned the wheel and said nothing and let the thing say anything it liked.

The well dropped a full meter. For one clean moment the shaft showed bare stone, wet and ancient, and the singing stopped altogether, and the silence was so enormous that Petra heard her own heart.

Then, from the bottom of the dry shaft, from beneath the stone, from behind the door sealed from the inside, came a knock.

Three knocks. Patient. Almost polite.

And a voice — not through the water, not through the air, but up through the soles of her boots, through the bones of her legs, a voice that had learned speech the way a drowning man learns the shore — said:

*The boy is still full.*

* * *

Tomas felt it the moment the draining began: the stone in his mouth cracking like river ice, the pressure releasing, and with the release, horribly, the hunger — his own hunger, his true one, the ache of nine days without melody, rising up in him like a second self. Because that was the trap, he understood now, standing bent in Isandro's arms while the well sank and the knocking started. The thing hadn't just hollowed him. It had left the wanting. It had taken the music and left the need for it, the way you'd gut a fish and leave the shape, and the shape wanted filling more than anything he had ever wanted in his life, more than his father's approval, more than the foreman's daughter's smile, more than air.

*Sing,* said the water, in his own voice, from six feet down, from four, from the shrinking surface that still found him however far they dragged him back. *You remember how. I kept it safe for you. I kept every note. Sing and be whole.*

"He's fighting it," Isandro gritted. His arms were locked around Tomas's chest from behind, and Tomas could feel the folklorist's heart going like a bird's. "Tomas. Look at me. Look at the lamp. Maren, raise the lamp — look at the light, not the water."

Maren raised the lamp. Her face above it was terrible and calm, the face of a woman who had counted doors for forty years waiting for exactly this night. "Boy," she said. "My grandmother bound a singer in 1911 with a hymn sung backward and a rope of braided hair, and it worked, because she never stopped talking to him. So I'm going to talk to you, and you're going to listen, and you are not going to sing. Do you hear me? You are not going to sing."

"I don't want to," Tomas sobbed, and it was true, and it changed nothing. "Señora, I don't want to, but it's in me, it's been pouring into me every night, it's finished, the whole song is finished in me, and it only needs one word of permission—"

"Then we'll take the permission too."

"You can't. Nobody can. It's not words, it's—" and here his voice broke into something younger, something from childhood, from the harvest festivals where he'd stood in the front row because he had the best ear in the village, "—it's wanting, señora. You can't bind wanting. It IS the wanting. It's been wanting for two hundred years and it learned that from us, it learned wanting from every song we sang it half of, and now it wants through me—"

The knocking came again. Faster. Not polite anymore.

And the bolts — they all heard them, even Maren, even through the fog and the lamplight and the grinding of the sluices — the great interior bolts of the door began to draw. One inch. Another. A sound like a coffin lid being unmade from within, iron dragging through iron, slow, immense, unhurried, because it had two hundred years of patience and it was spending none of it.

"No," Maren whispered. "No, the founders threw those bolts. Those bolts were thrown from the inside, they were meant to hold from the inside—"

"They were thrown by people who never meant to open it again," Isandro said. His voice had gone very quiet, the voice he used in the archive, the scholar's voice, and somehow that was the most frightening thing yet. "It's not breaking out, Maren. It's unlocking. It remembered how the lock works. It's had two hundred years of our songs to learn the mechanism, and now it has a boy full to the brim with the key."

The third bolt drew back with a boom that rolled up the lane and set every green lamp swinging.

The fourth held. The fourth held, trembling, and through the gap beneath the door — through the crack of the first three bolts, a darkness thin as a knife blade — came air. Moving inward. All of it, every breath of fog in the lane, every exhalation in the village, streaming gently, steadily, toward the well, toward the shaft, toward the seam of the door, a draft the size of a horizon flowing into a space the size of a throat.

The darkness was inhaling.

"It's making room," Isandro breathed. "Dear God. It's making room for a song."

* * *

Petra reached the wellhead at a dead run, soaked in sweat, palms shredded from the wheel, and took in the scene in one engineer's glance: the drained shaft, the trembling fourth bolt, the boy convulsing in the folklorist's grip, the old woman with her lamp, the draft pulling the flames sideways toward the stone.

"How long?" she demanded.

"On the current rate? Before dawn." Isandro's arms were shaking. "Maybe less. It's accelerating. It knows it's close."

"Then we're not done stalling." She pulled the radio off her shoulder, keyed it, and her voice came out level, professional, the voice that had argued her way past three superiors and a village's worth of dread to get the floodgates built. "Control, this is Vasquez. Open the secondary culverts. All of them. Divert the river intake into the outflow channel — yes, into it, I know what I'm saying, I want the whole system running backward, maximum counterflow, now."

Static. Then a distant, doubting voice. Then compliance, because Petra Vasquez had spent eleven years building a reputation for never saying anything she couldn't prove, and tonight she was spending that reputation like the last coin of a dying currency.

Below them, deep in the earth, the river turned.

They felt it come — a shudder through the bedrock, a change in the draft, and then the well, which had sunk to bare stone, coughed. Water burst up the shaft in a brown roaring column, river-water, cold and stupid and carrying silt and sticks and one very surprised eel, water that had never heard a song in its life, water that knew nothing, that remembered nothing, that poured into the chamber approaches and choked the throat of the hill with honest dumb flooding.

The draft stopped.

The inhale caught, gurgled, became something like choking. Inside the door, the fourth bolt's trembling slowed — not stopping, never stopping, but slowing, the mechanism fighting the pressure of meaningless water the way a singer fights a flooded lung.

"That buys hours," Petra said. "Hours, not the night. The culverts will silt or blow a seal by morning and when they do—"

"When they do, it finishes opening." Isandro lowered Tomas gently to the ground. The boy knelt in the fog, gray-faced, empty-eyed, the whole song sitting finished and patient behind his teeth, and everyone looked at him and no one said the obvious thing, which was that hours might as well be seconds, because the key was already cut and the lock already turning and all that remained was for some small human failure — sleep, exhaustion, a single unguarded breath — to let the song out.

Maren set her lamp down on the wellhead stones. Very carefully, the way you set down something you will not pick up again.

"Then we don't guard him till morning," she said. "We finish what the founders started. Tonight. While the water holds it deaf."

"How?" Petra asked.

Maren looked at Isandro. And Isandro, who had memorized a verse without singing it, who had starved himself of melody for weeks, who had spoken a key instead of playing it, went very pale and very still, because he understood what she was asking, and what it would cost, and whose throat the answer would have to pass through.

"The verse is a key for one voice alone," he said quietly. "Spoken, not sung. But a key only turns a lock that's already open." He looked down at the well, at the brown water churning over the hidden door, at the fourth bolt grinding its slow inch. "Someone has to close it from the outside. And the door takes a voice freely given to fuse the bolts."

The fog pulled toward the seam of the world. The fourth bolt drew back another inch.

"And I think," Isandro said, "it gets to choose whose."
