## 7. The Chair Moves Closer

The first rule of urban exploration is that you document everything. The second rule is that you never assume the documentation is wrong. Cameras don't lie, I told myself for years. Cameras are the one honest thing in a world full of people who'd rather you didn't exist.

I've stopped saying that out loud.

It started three days after the root cellar, on a Tuesday, with my own apartment—which should have been safe ground, forty square meters of rented certainty on Delmore Street where the only ghosts were the previous tenant's cigarette smoke and a radiator that sang in B-flat. I'd been reviewing footage from the tunnel approach, editing down the good takes, when I noticed it in the background of a shot I hadn't even meant to take: a frame from when I'd set the camera on the kitchen counter to make tea.

There was a chair at my table.

Not my chair. My chairs were two aluminum folding jobs from a thrift store, the kind that clank when you breathe near them. This was wood—dark, spindle-backed, its seat worn pale in the middle by someone who had sat there so long and so often that the sitting itself had become part of the object. It stood at the head of my table like it had been invited.

I checked the timestamp. 11:47 p.m., the night after Rosalind said *someone should remember correctly*. I checked every other frame. In the ones before midnight, no chair. In every frame after, the chair, patient as punctuation.

I did what any reasonable person does. I photographed the chair directly, then looked up from the camera to compare.

No chair. Just my table, my folding chairs, my cold tea going bitter on the counter.

But the photograph had it. The photograph insisted.

* * *

Here's the thing about being erased—I know the mechanics of it better than most. When you're nonbinary in a family that keeps photo albums organized by "the girls" and "the boys," you learn early how records work. You learn that erasure isn't usually violence. It's bookkeeping. Someone decides what goes in the ledger, and everything else simply stops existing—not because it's destroyed, but because nobody writes it down, and unwritten things have a way of becoming unbelievable.

Rosalind Crane wasn't haunting me. I understood that now, standing in my kitchen holding a camera that kept insisting on furniture. Hauntings are personal. Hauntings want something *from* you. What was happening was bigger and colder than that. The erasure itself was moving. It was a current, and I had waded into it, and currents don't care about your intentions—they just carry you toward the low place.

The room wanted witnesses. And it was done waiting for me to visit.

* * *

Thursday, the café on Wexler. I go there most mornings because Marisol makes the kind of coffee that makes capitalism feel briefly forgivable, and because the window seat lets me watch people exist in public, which is my favorite spectator sport. I took a picture of the fog on the glass—just fog, just light, the ordinary beauty of a December morning—and didn't look at it until I got home.

In the reflection, behind my own smudged ghost-self, there was a table I've sat at four hundred times. And at the head of it, where nobody ever sits because the radiator blocks it—

You already know. Of course you know. The chair.

Except this time it wasn't empty. There was an impression in the cushion of shadow, the suggestion of someone recently risen or about to arrive, the way a bed looks when the sleeper has just stepped out. Almost sitting. Nearly here. The photograph held its breath around her.

I put the phone face-down on my desk and made myself breathe. Four counts in, six counts out, the way my grandmother taught me when I was small and the house creaked. *Houses talk, Ivo. Doesn't mean they're talking to you.*

Grandma. Whose house this all came through. Whose photographs had been accruing a chair since 1998, the year I turned nine, the year she caught me singing a skipping song in the yard and went white as the frost and made me promise—cross my heart, hope to die—to never sing it again.

I hadn't remembered that until this week. That's the other thing about erasure: it works on memory too. Somebody edits the ledger, and the gaps fill in with nothing, and nothing feels exactly like peace until you look too closely.

* * *

Friday morning, frost on my bedroom window.

My apartment is fourth floor, heated, sealed, the windows newer than the building code requires. Frost does not form on my windows. Frost especially does not form in patterns.

These weren't fern-patterns, not the random lacework frost usually makes. These were lines. Rows of them, marching across the glass like staff paper, and threaded along them—shapes. Marks. Half-letters, half-notes, the writing of someone who learned letters and music at the same time and never fully separated them. I pressed my palm against the coldest cluster and the cold bit back, and underneath the biting, faint as a radio between stations, I heard humming.

I knew the tune. God help me, I knew it. Three verses' worth, from the cellar, from the woman who sang them like she was setting a table for guests who kept dying on the way over.

I did not hum along. I have some rules left.

Instead I got my camera and documented the window, because documentation is my prayer, and then I documented the frost on the *inside* of the hallway window, and the frost on the stairwell glass, and the frost blooming across the windshield of my car in rows, in lines, in that same patient handwriting, following me out into the world like a dog that has decided I'm its person now.

A man scraping ice off the truck next to mine gave me a sympathetic look. "Cold snap," he said. "Whole block's got it."

His windshield was clean. His breath fogged; his glass didn't frost. But he believed me, because it's easier to believe weather than to believe the alternative, and I let him believe it, because I'm a coward in small ways even when I'm stubborn in large ones.

* * *

Saturday night I did the thing you're not supposed to do. I went looking for the pattern instead of away from it.

I laid out every photograph on my floor—the estate inventory shots from Grandma's house, the tunnel series, the café reflection, the apartment frames—and arranged them chronologically. And there it was, plain as a plot on a map: the chair getting closer. Not in space. In *certainty*. In Grandma's oldest photos it was barely there, a wooden suggestion, out-of-focus at the edge of rooms. By 2010 it had edges. By 2019 it had grain, wear, a history written into its seat. In the café shot it had an occupant-shaped absence. And in the last frame—the one I'd taken that morning without thinking, of my own front door, because apparently I now compulsively photograph doors—

The chair was in my hallway. Facing me. Close enough that whoever sat in it could have touched the camera.

Almost sitting. She was almost sitting. Every image was a countdown and I could read the numbers now: not days, exactly. Verses. We were three verses in, and everyone knows songs have endings.

I sat down on my floor amid the evidence of my own haunting-by-bookkeeping and laughed, once, a sound with no humor in it, because I finally saw the shape of the whole thing. Rosalind hadn't sent for me. Rosalind hadn't done anything except sit in her chair for a hundred years, waiting for someone willing to look at her. It was the *erasure* that moved—it was the town's old lie, still doing its work, still tidying inconvenient women out of existence, and it had snagged on me the way a current snags on anything that refuses to float past. I was a witness. Witnesses get dragged toward the room. That's not haunting. That's gravity.

And the frost, the frost was hers—or was it? I thought of her face in the cellar, courteous and terrible and so tired. *Someone should remember correctly.* Maybe the frost was the song trying to write itself somewhere the ledger couldn't reach. Maybe every window in this town was a page somebody had been scribbling on for a century while everyone praised the view.

My phone buzzed. Marisol, sending me a photo from the café: *you forgot your gloves, weird fog art on the window today, thought you'd like it.*

Fog art. Rows of lines. Marks threaded along them.

She'd photographed it because it was pretty. She'd seen it and found it pretty, and tomorrow she'd find it pretty again, and the day after, and none of it would mean anything to her because meaning is a debt and she owed nothing here.

I stared at her message for a long time. Then I typed: *Don't sing anything in the café. Even humming. Promise me.*

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared.

*ivo what did you get into*

I looked at the last photograph on my floor. My front door. My hallway. The chair, close enough to count the spindles, close enough to see that the seat's pale worn patch was fresh now, warm-looking, as though someone had risen moments ago—as though she had stepped out of the image entirely and was standing, at this very second, in whatever room stands adjacent to mine, listening to whether I'll learn the ending.

The frost crept another inch across my window, laying down new lines, and in the new lines I could almost read the beginning of a verse I hadn't been taught yet.

The last one. It was coming to me whether I went to it or not.

Sunday, I'm going back to the house. Sunday, I'm going to ask her to teach me the ending—and pray I'm strong enough to keep my mouth shut while she does.
