## 2. Grandmother's House

The solicitor handed Silas the key in a padded envelope, as though it might bite.

"Rosalind's cottage," he'd said, not meeting their eyes. "Per the will. There's no rush, of course. Take your time."

There was always a particular quality to a sentence like that, Silas thought now, standing before the cottage with the key cold in their palm. A hollowness where warmth should have been. *Take your time* meant *we would rather you didn't go at all*. The whole town had that quality—the butcher who wrapped their bread without comment, the woman at the post office whose smile flickered when Silas gave their name. Winterbourne. As if the name itself were a draft under a door.

The cottage sat at the end of Larkspur Lane, where the lane gave up pretending and became a footpath, and the footpath surrendered to hedgerow, and the hedgerow to the dark shoulder of Hollowmere Wood. It was smaller than Silas remembered from childhood visits, or perhaps they had been smaller then, and everything had seemed vast by proportion. Grey stone. A slate roof furred with moss. Windows like half-closed eyes beneath their lintels.

A gift, the will called it. But standing there, Silas felt less like an heir than a caretaker arriving for a shift no one else would work.

They let themselves in.

The air inside was cool and still, the air of a house that had been waiting rather than emptying. Dust hung in the light from the front windows in slow, turning constellations. Rosalind's furniture remained exactly where it had always been: the wingback chair by the hearth, the dresser with its chipped blue jugs, the long table in the kitchen where Silas had once done homework while their grandmother shelled peas and hummed songs without words.

Silas set down their bag and stood in the middle of the sitting room, listening to the silence settle around them like silt.

"Grief is a room," their therapist had said, back when Silas could still afford a therapist. "You don't get over it. You learn where the furniture is."

But this was Rosalind's room, full of Rosalind's furniture, and Silas kept reaching for things that weren't there anymore—the smell of her cardigan, camphor and lavender; the click of her knitting needles; her voice calling them in from the garden before dark. *Before dark*, always. She had never once said why.

Now, running their fingers along the windowsill, Silas found the first answer, or the shape of one.

Salt.

A fine white line of it, poured along the sill with a steadiness that spoke of ritual rather than housekeeping—unbroken, precise, renewed so many times that a crust had formed, fused to the painted wood. Silas touched it. Rubbed it between finger and thumb. Salt, ordinary table salt, laid down like mortar.

They checked the other windows. Every one. Then the doors: the front door, the back door, even the little door to the coal cellar that hadn't opened in living memory—all of them sealed at the threshold with lines of salt, thick and deliberate, the way a child draws a wall against monsters, except this was an old woman's hand, and old women do not draw walls against nothing.

"Grandmother," Silas said aloud, and the word fell into the quiet and did not echo. The house absorbed it. Houses like this, Silas was beginning to understand, absorbed things.

* * *

They spent the afternoon unpacking into Rosalind's world. Their clothes in her wardrobe, which smelled faintly of cedar and something older underneath, something mineral and cold, like the air off a well. Their books on her shelves, between her books—gardening guides, a Bible with a cracked spine, and a run of cheap paperback novels about hauntings, bought, Silas supposed, the way one buys books about diseases one has already been diagnosed with.

In the kitchen drawer beneath the telephone shelf—a drawer Silas had never once seen her open—they found the key.

It was small, brass, older than the others, tied to a paper tag gone soft and brown with handling. On the tag, in Rosalind's upright, careful hand: *For whoever comes after.*

Silas stood holding it for a long moment. The kitchen clock ticked. Outside, the light was beginning to lean toward evening, going amber, then copper, then the bruised violet of early dusk, and Silas noticed—with a small cold start—that they had unconsciously begun to move through the house drawing curtains. All of them. Without deciding to. Their hands had learned something their mind refused to name.

The drawer the key fit was in the bureau upstairs, in the narrow back bedroom Rosalind had used these last years, after the stairs became unkind. The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Inside lay a journal. Black cloth boards, swollen with damp decades, a rubber band around it that disintegrated at Silas's touch. And beneath it, a second thing: a photograph, face-down, of thirteen children in school uniforms arranged on the steps of a building Silas recognized instantly—Hollowmere School, Room 4's wing, the door behind them numbered with a four that had since been painted over.

Thirteen children. Silas counted twice. Thirteen smiling faces, squinting into a sun that had set forty years ago.

One of them, at the end of the second row, was a girl of perhaps ten, with dark hair cut bluntly at the jaw and a gap between her front teeth. Silas knew the face. They had grown up watching it soften and silver in the mirror of family albums. Rosalind, aged ten, grinning her gap-toothed grin, standing slightly apart from the others—not excluded, exactly, but positioned as though she had arrived late, or as though the photographer had asked her to stand just there, just so, at the edge of the group, where the shadow of the doorframe reached her shoulder.

Silas turned the journal over in their hands. The cover was warm. Not sun-warm; the bureau stood in a cold north-facing room. Warm the way a hand is warm. Warm the way something held for a long time is warm.

They did not open it. Not yet. Some instinct older than rationalism told them that once read, it could not be unread, and the daylight was nearly gone, and there was a rule forming in the marrow of them, inherited perhaps in the blood along with the house: *do not open strange doors after dark.*

So they carried it downstairs instead, set it on the kitchen table, and made tea they didn't drink, and watched the last light fail over the hedgerows.

That was when the knock came.

Three knocks, spaced evenly, unhurried. Silas opened the door to find a woman on the step, mid-seventies perhaps, built like weathered timber, with a headscarf and a canvas shopping bag and eyes that went immediately past Silas's shoulder into the lit interior of the house—and flinched.

"You've the lamps on," the woman said. "All of them."

"I'm Silas. Rosalind's grandchild."

"I know who you are." She shifted the bag. "Mabel Crowe. I taught at the school, years back. I brought you—" she lifted the bag slightly, revealing a covered dish, "—a pie. People bring pies. It's what we do instead of saying anything true."

Silas took the dish. Mabel Crowe did not come in. Silas understood, watching her feet stay planted on the step, that she was not going to cross the threshold, and that this was not rudeness.

"The salt," Silas said. "On the thresholds. Did my grandmother—"

"Your grandmother," Mabel said, "was the most careful woman I ever met. Careful in the way soldiers are careful." Her gaze slid to the curtained windows. "You'll want to keep those drawn after dark. All of them. Every night."

"Why?"

Mabel Crowe looked at Silas for a long moment, and Silas saw her decide something—saw the decision happen behind her eyes like a door being closed, or almost closed.

"Because she did," Mabel said. "Forty years, every night, and never once missed. Whatever else you think about Rosalind Winterbourne—and people here think plenty—you can't say she wasn't faithful to it." She stepped back off the step, into the blue dusk. "Eat the pie while it's fresh. And mind the house doesn't mind you."

"What does that mean? *Mind the house doesn't mind me?*"

But Mabel was already walking away, fast, her shadow stretching long and thin ahead of her toward the last of the light, as though even her shadow preferred not to follow her home in the dark.

* * *

Silas ate a slice of the pie standing at the counter because sitting felt too much like settling in. It was good. That somehow made it worse—kindness tasting of dread.

Upstairs, they checked the bedroom window's salt line, found it intact, and drew the curtain anyway, double-checking the overlap at the center where the fabric met. In the gap, before it closed, the wood stood black and dense under a moon coming up fat and white. Somewhere out there, the school sat empty. Somewhere out there, thirteen desks waited in a room no one would explain.

Silas got into Rosalind's bed, under Rosalind's blankets, in Rosalind's house, and lay listening to the sounds a house makes when it is old: pipes ticking, beams settling, wind testing the eaves like fingers testing a lock.

And then, below all of it, another sound. Faint. Rhythmic. Coming, Silas realized with a slow flood of cold, from the kitchen.

Wood, shifting gently against stone floor. A soft scrape. Pause. Another scrape.

As though something were moving a chair.

Silas lay rigid until dawn, telling themselves it was the house breathing, the floorboards, the wind, the ordinary physics of old buildings. Rationalism was armor; they had worn it all their life. But armor only works if you believe in the battle, and some part of Silas, lying in the dark listening to that patient scraping, had already stopped believing in the ordinary.

When morning came, grey and thin through the curtain seams, Silas went downstairs.

The kitchen was exactly as they'd left it. Except that the journal was no longer on the table.

It sat on the chair nearest the door. Open. Turned to face the room, as if someone had been reading it and stepped away only moments ago—or as if it had been left there deliberately, for Silas to find, the way one leaves food out.

The page it lay open to bore a single line, in Rosalind's upright, careful hand:

*It came in because I asked it to.*
