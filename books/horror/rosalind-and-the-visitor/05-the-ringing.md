## 5. The Ringing

I had spent the better part of three days pretending to be a functional adult, which in my case meant keeping the radio on even when I wasn't broadcasting, just to hear a human voice that wasn't mine. The dead had gone quiet since Tuesday — or rather, they'd gone *patient*, which was worse. Mrs. Aldous's last words to me had been "He's counting you now," and then the line filled with that soft static that I have come to understand is not static at all but breathing, if breathing can be done by something with no lungs and no reason for courtesy.

So when dusk came on Thursday and the bell rang, I was, at least, not surprised.

I want to be clear about what I mean by *the bell*. There is no bell. There has been no bell since 1911, when the parish of Rosalind melted its own bell down to forge — well, we'll get to what it forged. The tower stands empty above the churchyard like a tooth socket. Children dare each other to climb it and come back down pale and quiet, having found nothing but pigeons and cold.

And yet at seventeen minutes past four, as the light went the color of weak tea, the whole village heard it ring.

It came from everywhere. That's the detail I keep circling back to, because every other detail I could explain away if I tried hard enough — resonance off the hills, a truck backing up somewhere, mass hysteria among forty-one people who should know better. But it came from everywhere. From under the floorboards of my studio. From inside my own skull, where sound has no business being. One note, low and bronze and warm as a hand on your shoulder, held so long I checked my watch twice to confirm that time hadn't simply stopped out of respect.

Then it stopped, and the frost began.

* * *

I was standing at my window when it happened, which is the only reason I can describe it with any accuracy, and accuracy matters now, because I am apparently one of the last people who will remember this village as it was.

The frost did not creep. Frost creeps; I've watched it all winter, patient as bookkeeping, laying its spirals across my glass one morning at a time. This was different. This was an eruption. It came up out of the ground itself — out of the cracks in the pavement, out of the drains, out of the frozen flowerbeds outside the post office — in white ribbons that moved like they'd been waiting a hundred years for permission. Spirals, always spirals, the same spiral I'd been waking up to on my window for weeks, unspooling down Fore Street and Church Lane and the lane behind the butcher's where nobody's lived since the shop closed, growing as fast as water pours, faster, until the whole village looked like it had been drawn by someone with a very steady hand and no interest whatsoever in mercy.

People came out of their houses. Of course they did. You hear a dead bell ring and see the street turn to lace, you go outside; there's no version of you that stays in with the telly. Old Tom Merrick stood in his doorway in his slippers holding a poker like he intended to fight winter hand to hand, and I'll be honest, part of me loved him for it. The Hendersons from the farm stood arm in arm at their gate. Young Priya Nair, who runs the shop and has never once believed a word I've said about anything, stood in the middle of the road turning slowly, watching the patterns climb the walls around her, and her face wasn't fear. It was recognition. That frightened me more than the frost did.

Because here is what I understood, standing at my window with my breath fogging the glass: the patterns weren't new. They were *old*. They were the same spirals, the same count, the same geometry I'd been watching accumulate morning after morning — and tonight they weren't accumulating anymore. They were completing. Every spiral I'd ever seen had been a rehearsal, a pencil sketch, and tonight someone had finally gone over the lines in ink.

The ledger, Tom had said. Every villager's yearly tithe. And I had thought, in my weary way, that I still had time. That the frost counted in years, in winters, in the slow arithmetic of memory-for-memory. But the frost, it turns out, does not care about my schedule. The frost keeps its own books, and tonight it was calling them due.

The bell rang a second time.

This time it came from below.

* * *

I don't remember deciding to go outside. I want to say that about myself later, whoever later turns out to belong to: I didn't choose it. Or maybe I did, in the way you choose to answer a phone that's been ringing all night — not a decision so much as a surrender to the obvious. The sound came up through the soles of my boots, through the pavement, through whatever the pavement sits on, and it was pulling. Not dragging. Pulling, the way tide pulls, politely, enormously, without any interest in whether you consent.

The spirals on the road were turning. Not metaphorically. Turning, slowly, all of them, in the same direction, and the snow between them had gone strange and glassy, and beneath the glass I could see — light. A warm, buttery, impossible light, the light of lamplit windows, coming up from underneath Rosalind like the village was a lantern with the paint flaking off.

Tom Merrick grabbed my sleeve as I passed his gate. His hands were shaking and his face was gray and he said, "Don't look down the wells," which is the sort of thing that is deeply unhelpful and also the only useful advice anyone gave me all week.

"Tom, what's under us?"

"Nothing." He swallowed. "Nothing's under us. That's the point of it. Nothing's supposed to be under us."

"Then what's ringing?"

He looked at me then with an expression I'd seen on him once before, years ago, at Edith Rosalind's grave, when I'd asked him why the founders' stones all faced inward instead of east. It was the expression of a man deciding how much truth a person can carry before it becomes a weight instead of a gift.

"The half of it," he said. "You're hearing the half of it. God help you if you hear the whole."

The third ring took the ground away.

Not dramatically — no earthquake, no chasm yawning. The pavement simply became negotiable, the way a dream staircase is negotiable, and the light below came up through my boots and into my teeth, and the pull became a current, and I stepped — or was stepped — downward, through frost and stone and the smell of old iron, into the place that was not supposed to exist.

* * *

Rosalind is built on top of Rosalind.

That's the sentence. I've turned it over all night and there's no gentler way to arrange it. Beneath our village — beneath the shop and the churchyard and Tom Merrick's slippers and my little studio with its stubborn radiator — there is another village, older, identical in its bones, and it is preserved in ice the way a moth is preserved in amber, perfectly, lovingly, forever.

I came out of the descent into a street. That's the only word. A street, cobbled, lined with houses whose windows glowed with that buttery light, except the light doesn't flicker because nothing in here moves enough to disturb it. Everything is sheathed in ice — not buried, not crushed, *sheathed*, the way you'd wrap something you meant to give back someday. A bicycle leans against a wall, its wheels furred white. Laundry hangs on a line, frozen mid-swing, shirts with their arms out like people waving goodbye. A pram sits at a gate. A dog — I couldn't tell you the breed, the ice has opinions about detail — is caught mid-leap toward a ball that hangs in the air beside it, and both dog and ball are crystal, and both are beautiful, and I stood in that street and shook.

Because I knew these houses. Not their addresses — their *shapes*. The crooked chimney on the corner house. The lintel with the carved date, 1849. The gap in the hedge where generations of children had taken a shortcut. This was Fore Street. This was my street, before. Before the shop closed, before the roofs sagged, before the population slid from hundreds to dozens to the forty-one souls Tom counts every Christmas like a man counting spoons. This was Rosalind when Rosalind was alive, kept the way you keep a photograph — except photographs don't have lamplight in the windows, and photographs don't make you feel watched.

And the people. God help me, the people.

They're in the houses. In the ice. Standing at sinks, sitting at tables, reading to children, caught in the middle of ordinary evenings a century deep, every one of them sheathed and shining and *smiling*. Not rictus-smiling. Actually smiling, the small unconscious smiles of people having a nice Tuesday, and I understood with a lurch that this was the tithe made visible — that everything the village paid out over a hundred years, every memory surrendered, every name worn smooth and dropped, hadn't vanished. It had been *kept*. Filed. Preserved down here in the dark under the dark, in a museum of everyone Rosalind used to be.

The frost hasn't been eating us. The frost has been *archiving* us.

I walked that street — I don't know how long; time down here is a rumor — reading names on doors that matched names on graves upstairs, and names that matched nothing at all, names no living soul remembers, and beside one door I stopped because the ice there was clearer than elsewhere, polished, as though looked at often, and through it I saw a woman at a piano, mid-phrase, her hands on keys that no longer exist anywhere in the world, and I felt grief hit me like weather — huge, directionless, grieving for someone I have never met and cannot name and somehow cannot bear.

That's when I saw the chapel at the end of the street. Ours, but whole — bell tower intact, and in the tower, hanging in a cage of frost, the bell. Whole. Bronze-dark. Warm-looking, absurdly, like bread.

And beneath it, on the frozen steps, a journal lay open, its pages stiff as porcelain, and the handwriting on the open page was the same handwriting I'd spent three nights tracing in the founders' ledger upstairs.

Edith Rosalind had left something for me. She had left it a hundred years ago, on these steps, in a village she'd built out of ice and other people's yesterdays — and the entry began, in letters pressed deep enough to read through the frost:

*To whoever hears the bell —*

*I am sorry. We could not finish it ourselves.*
