## 9. Rosalind and the Visitor

The chapel had been waiting for me. That's the only way I can put it without sounding like a madwoman, and I've long since stopped caring about sounding like a madwoman — it's practically my brand.

The doors were open. Not ajar, not weathered loose on their hinges the way the church of St. Aldhelm's had been for as long as anyone remembered, but *open*, wide as an invitation, and the frost that coated everything else in Rosalind had left the threshold bare. A path of clean stone leading in. Somebody wanted me to walk it.

"Somebody" being generous. Something.

I stood at the bottom of the steps with Edith Rosalind's ledger under my arm and the last stake wrapped in my coat like a swaddled infant, and I thought: fifteen years of explaining things away. Fifteen years of dead callers and faulty wiring, of switchboard lights with no lines attached, of frost patterns I told myself were condensation, of Mrs. Aldous's voice saying *you'll forget me too, dear, they all do* in a tone so gentle it could only have been rehearsed by someone who'd said it ten thousand times before.

I was very tired of explaining things away.

So I walked up the path, because the alternative was standing in the cold being a coward, and I've never been able to afford cowardice on a radio host's salary.

Inside, the chapel was full of winter.

Not snow — snow would have been almost friendly. This was the frost itself, grown inward, spiraling up the pillars and across the pews in those same patterns that had been counting down outside my window since October. The windows were opaque with it, and the light that came through was the color of old ice, blue-green and patient, and every surface glittered like the inside of some enormous jewel. My breath hung in the air and did not disperse. It just stayed there, a small ghost of me, keeping me company.

At the altar, where the bell had hung before they melted it down in 1911, there was a frame of black iron. Empty. And beneath it, set into the stone floor, the pattern — the final spiral, nearly complete, its last arc open like a mouth waiting to be fed.

The other stakes were already there. Four of them, driven into the corners of the sanctuary, each one forged from the melted bell, each one humming faintly when I came near, the way a struck tuning fork hums after the sound has technically ended. They knew me. That was the worst part. They'd been waiting for me specifically, the way a lock waits for its key, and I felt less like a visitor than like a delivery.

"Well," I said, to the empty chapel, because talking to myself is a professional habit and I saw no reason to break it now. "Let's see what we're working with."

I set the ledger on the altar and opened it to Edith's final entry, the one I'd read eleven times in Tom Merrick's kitchen while his tea went cold and his hands shook:

*The ritual wants a finisher. We built it half-made on purpose, God forgive us, because a bargain sealed by its makers is only ever their bargain, and their bargain was made out of fear. Let the one who comes after choose how it ends. Let them bring something we didn't think to offer. Winter takes memory because memory is all we ever thought to give it. But winter is only a hunger, and hunger can be fed differently, if someone is brave enough, or foolish enough, or loving enough, to try.*

I'd read it eleven times and still hadn't understood it until the moment I drove the fifth stake into the open arc of the spiral and the whole floor lit up like a circuit board made of moonlight.

Then I understood it perfectly, and I wished I didn't.

* * *

Here is what the ritual showed me, once the stakes were set and the pattern woke beneath my feet: everything. Every tithe paid since 1911, every memory surrendered by every villager, year upon year upon year — a hundred and fourteen winters of small surrenders. A first kiss here. A mother's voice there. The smell of bread baking, the name of a river, the face of a friend who moved away. All of it flowing into the frost like water into roots, feeding the boundary that kept the true winter — the old one, the hungry one from before the founders, the one that took villages whole and left no ruins because it took the memory of them too — from finishing its work.

And here is what the ritual asked of me, once it had shown me all that: everything back. All of it. Not one memory per year, not a tithe, not a trickle. The whole account. Every person I had ever loved, every moment I had ever been glad, every scrap of the self I had spent forty-odd years assembling — poured into the pattern, all at once, to seal it forever.

Because that was the flaw Edith had built around. The ritual needed a willing heir, but willingness wasn't enough; the seal required a soul entire. Half-measures had gotten them a hundred years of borrowed time. To end it — truly end it, close the hunger's mouth for good — someone had to give themselves *whole*.

I knelt there on the frozen stone and thought about my life.

It's not much of a life, objectively. A late-night radio show in a village the maps keep forgetting. A flat above the post office with a kettle that takes four minutes and a window that draws spirals. No children. No spouse. A scattering of friends, most of them dead now, which in Rosalind is less final than it sounds.

But oh, the contents. My mother singing off-key in the kitchen. My father teaching me to whistle through my thumbs. The dog — I'd already given up the dog, two nights ago, a small brown shape whose name I could no longer retrieve and whose absence ached like a tooth gone suddenly missing. Miss Hartley in Year Four telling me I had a voice worth listening to. The first time I sat behind a broadcast desk and realized the dark was full of people, and that I could reach them, and that reaching them was the whole point of me.

All of it. Into the frost. Forever.

"No," I said.

The pattern pulsed, patient. It had waited a century; it could wait out my tantrum.

"No," I said again, and this time I laughed, because I'd finally seen it — the thing Edith had left for her successor, hidden in plain sight in her own careful handwriting. *Let them bring something we didn't think to offer.* She hadn't meant a different memory. She hadn't meant a bigger sacrifice. She'd meant a different *currency* entirely.

Winter was a hunger. Hunger could be fed differently.

A bargain, I know a little about bargains. I make them every night at 2 a.m., with people who call in dead and lonely and wanting to be heard. The terms are always the same: you give me your story, and I give you back your name, spoken aloud, into the dark, so that somewhere somebody knows you existed. That's the show. That's always been the show. I just never knew I was practicing.

"You want a keeper," I said to the pattern, to the stakes, to the patient blue light. "You want someone whole, someone who belongs to the village and the village belongs to them, someone to hold the boundary from the inside. Fine. But you don't need my memories for that. You need my *name*."

The frost went very still. Even the frost could be surprised, apparently. Good. I liked having the advantage; I'd earned exactly one surprise in this whole affair and I intended to spend it well.

"A name isn't a memory," I said, warming to it, pacing the spiral's edge like I paced the studio, like I talked to callers. "Memories are what happened to me. A name is what I'm *called*. It's the handle everyone else uses to hold onto me. You take my memories, I'm an empty shell walking around Rosalind, and the village loses a person and gains furniture. But you take my name—" I crouched, pressed my palm flat against the open arc, and the cold bit through my skin like teeth. "—and everyone who ever knew Beatrix Eastwick simply... doesn't. Not remembers her wrong. Doesn't remember her at all. There's no grief, no gap, no hole in anyone's heart, because there was never anything to miss. The keeper keeps nothing. The village keeps everything. And I stay."

The pattern shivered. Considering. Bargaining, even. Well, good — bargaining was my native language.

"And here's the sweetener," I said, "because I know how you operate, you great cold ledger, you. You don't just get a keeper. You get a *voice*. Someone on the frequency, every night, at 2 a.m., saying the names of everyone this village has ever lost — the tithed ones, the eaten ones, the ones existing nowhere but the airwaves. Someone making sure that even the forgotten get spoken. That's not a cost to you. That's maintenance. That's *staffing*."

The spiral began to turn.

I want to be honest, since honesty is the one luxury I have left: in that last moment, kneeling on the ice with my hand in the frost's mouth, I was afraid. Properly afraid, the way I hadn't let myself be since Mrs. Aldous called. Because I could feel what I was offering, feel the shape of it — Beatrix Eastwick, daughter of, friend of, beloved of nobody anymore, voice of the night hours, woman who liked her tea too strong and her music too sad — all of it gathering itself up to be unmade. And underneath the fear, worse than the fear, was a small treacherous voice asking whether anyone would even notice the difference. Whether a person who'd spent her whole life behind a microphone, heard but never seen, known but never *known*, could really be erased if no one had ever quite held onto her in the first place.

And then I thought: that's exactly why it has to be me. Because I've been half-erased my whole life, and I turned out fine. I turned out *useful*. I know how to live in the spaces between names. I am, it turns out, uniquely qualified for this job.

"I accept," I said. "New terms. Take the name."

The frost closed over my hand like water.

* * *

It didn't hurt. That surprised me most. It felt like setting down something heavy I'd been carrying so long I'd mistaken it for my spine. Name by name — Beatrix, Eastwick, Bea, Trixie, that awful nickname from university, the way my mother used to sing it across the playground — each one lifted out of me and laid into the pattern, and the pattern drank them, and the spiral closed its final arc with a sound like a book shutting gently.

The stakes sang. All five of them, forged from the melted bell, ringing together for the first time in a hundred and fourteen years — not the hollow toll of the bargain being sealed, but something warmer, rounder, the sound a bell makes when it's rung by someone who loves it. Once. Just once. The note rolled out through the chapel, through the frost, through the streets of Rosalind, and everywhere it passed the spirals stopped growing. Stopped counting. Lay still on the windows like lace, like decoration, like something beautiful instead of something hungry.

Under-village and over-village settled against each other like hands folding. Somewhere far above, I knew, dawn was coming to a village that would wake remembering everything — every tithe, every loss, every name — except mine.

Except mine.

I stood in the empty chapel, whole and unnamed, and found — this is the strange part, the part I'll spend the rest of eternity turning over — that I wasn't sad. I was *light*. Everything I loved was still loved; it just lived in other people now, held by them instead of me, which is arguably where love belonged all along. And I was still here. Still belonging. Keeper of the bell, keeper of the boundary, keeper of the 2 a.m. hour.

The iron frame above the altar hummed, and I felt the frequency open the way it opens every night, and I knew my show was about to begin.

Somewhere out there, a switchboard light was glowing.

"All right," I said, to the chapel, to the frost, to the dark full of people. "Who's my first caller?"
