## 10. Sign-Off

There is a particular kind of quiet that comes at 2 a.m. in a village the size of Rosalind — not silence exactly, because Rosalind has never managed true silence, not since before anyone living can remember. It's more of a held breath. The sound of a place listening to itself.

I know this quiet well. I've known it every night for as long as I've had this show, which is to say: forever, or near enough that the distinction stopped mattering around the time I stopped bothering to count.

"Good morning, Rosalind," I said into the microphone, because it was technically morning, and because saying *good evening* to a village at two o'clock felt like lying, and I have made something of a personal policy never to lie on air. Off air is another matter entirely. Off air, everyone lies constantly. That's what off air is *for*.

"You're listening to the late shift on Rosalind Community Radio, broadcasting from the little studio above the old post office, where the radiator clanks in B-flat and I've decided to consider it percussion. It's cold out there tonight. Cold everywhere, really. It's always cold here. But the frost's been lying still this week — flat, quiet frost, no patterns, no spirals, nothing climbing up your windows like it's got somewhere to be. Mrs. Petch at the shop says it's the calmest winter she can remember. She says that every year, mind. Mrs. Petch's memory only goes back about a fortnight with any reliability, which I suspect she'd agree with if you asked her, though by then she'd have forgotten you'd asked."

I let the joke sit there in the dark. Somewhere out in the village, a handful of insomniacs chuckled. I could feel them doing it, the way you can feel weather coming. That's the strange arithmetic of a place this small: you are never really alone, even when you are absolutely alone, sitting in a room above a post office with a dead microphone light and a switchboard that mostly doesn't.

The switchboard. I should tell you about the switchboard.

It's an old thing, older than me, older than the station, probably older than the post office itself if we're being honest — brass fittings, bakelite housing, a row of little amber lights that glow when someone calls in. Most nights, none of them glow. Some nights, one or two do, and I take the calls, and they're lovely calls, ordinary calls, people who can't sleep wanting to hear about the price of parsnips or whether anyone else heard that noise.

And some nights — well. We'll get to some nights.

First, the news, such as it was.

"The church bell rang again last night," I said. "Just once, just at dusk, warm and low, like a bell sounds when it's happy, if bells can be happy, which after everything this village has put them through I think they've earned the right. Father Illingworth says he doesn't know how. The bell, as you'll recall, hasn't existed as a bell for over a century — it was melted down in 1911, along with most of the village's good silver and, according to some accounts, a piano nobody liked. What rang last night, nobody knows. But everyone heard it. And everyone agrees it sounded like the end of something rather than the beginning."

I paused, because the next part required care.

"There's a question going round the village, and I want to address it, because that's my job. Address things. People have been asking each other: who used to run the late show? Before me? Because the equipment's old, and the chair has a dent worn into it — a deep one, the kind that takes years — and the mug by the mixer has initials scratched into the glaze that don't match mine. And when people try to remember who sat in that chair before me, they find they can't. Not that the memory's gone fuzzy. Fuzzy is normal. This is different. There's just a shape where a person should be, and the shape is empty, and everyone agrees — cheerfully, almost gratefully — that someone must always have done this job. Someone always has. Someone always will. So thank you for having me, Rosalind. Whoever I replaced, I hope they're resting."

That was a lie, actually. On air. My one policy, broken nightly now, because the truth was worse and the truth helped no one. The truth was that nobody remembered, and the not-remembering had edges, and the edges were smooth, worn smooth, like the dent in the chair. Whatever had been carved out of this village had been carved cleanly, lovingly, by something that knew its work.

I knew whose work it was. Of course I did. I'm the only one who does, and I only know it the way you know a dream after waking — vividly, briefly, and with the certain knowledge it will be gone by breakfast.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me do the job properly. Segment by segment. That's how you survive a night shift. That's how you survive anything, probably.

*

At half past two I read the notices. Weddings, births, the harvest fund, the fact that the Hendersons' goat had gotten into the churchyard again and eaten three wreaths, which the vicar had magnanimously reframed as "pastoral outreach." The notices are the heartbeat of the show. In a village like Rosalind, the notices are the whole circulatory system.

Then the music. Two songs, always two — "one for the ones who slept, and one for the ones who couldn't," as the show's unwritten rule goes. I played "In the Bleak Midwinter" for the sleepers, because it was February and I am nothing if not literal, and then something older for the wakeful, a folk air I found on a reel-to-reel in the station's back cupboard, unlabeled, hissing, beautiful. It sounded like someone humming to themselves while they worked. It sounded like company.

While it played, I looked out the window.

The frost lay flat on the glass, thin and even, like a sheet pulled up neatly over the village. No spirals. No patterns counting down toward anything. For fifteen years — or however long; time in Rosalind is a suggestion — the frost had been writing on these windows, growing more intricate each morning, and everyone had admired it and said *isn't nature clever* and gone about their business, because looking too closely at things in Rosalind has always been understood to be a hobby with poor survival outcomes.

Now it was still. Finished, or finished-with. A page turned face-down.

I remember — and I use that word loosely, the way you'd use "swimming" to describe standing waist-deep in water — I remember that once, the frost frightened someone in this chair. I remember fear the way you remember a smell from childhood: not the event, just the residue. There was someone who sat here and watched the patterns grow and understood them, and paid for the understanding, and paid past the point where payment means anything, and what's left of the paying is a dent in a chair and initials on a mug and a village that sleeps easier than it has in a hundred years.

I don't know their name. Nobody does. That was the arrangement. That was the whole genius of the arrangement, if you want my professional opinion, and you're getting it anyway, because it's my show.

The founders built a bargain in 1911 that needed a keeper, and keepers need keeping, and so the final stake of the ritual wasn't driven with blood or memory or anything so dramatic. It was driven with a name. Just a name. Take the name, take the person, leave the voice. The village keeps its dead accounted for and its living unbothered, and the keeper keeps — well. The keeper keeps the show on the air.

Somebody has to. Somebody always has.

*

At ten past three, the switchboard lit up.

One light. Amber, steady, patient. No number showing, which isn't unusual — half the phones in Rosalind predate caller ID and possibly electricity — but there was something about this light. The others blink when they ring. This one simply glowed, the way a window glows when someone inside is waiting up for you.

I faded the music. "We have a caller," I told the sleepless few. "Let's see who's out there."

I punched the button. The line opened with that soft click, and then a breath, and then a voice.

"Thank you."

A woman's voice. Old, or made old by the line — crackling, warm underneath the static, the way embers are warm under ash. Not frightened. Not urgent. The dead callers used to be urgent; I know that without knowing how I know it, the way I know the chair-dent took years. Urgency was the old frequency. This was something else. This was a voice calling long-distance from a place where nothing hurries anymore.

"You're welcome," I said, because what else does one say. "Though I'm afraid you have me at a disadvantage. Who's speaking?"

A pause. Static breathing.

"I don't have a name to give," the voice said, and there was something in it — amusement, almost. Tenderness, definitely. "Gave it away. Best trade I ever made."

My hand was steady on the fader. Fifteen years of night shifts teaches you that. Inside, something was ringing like a struck bell, one clear note, fading.

"Do I know you?" I asked.

"Everyone in Rosalind knows me," the voice said. "That was rather the point. They just don't know that they do." A soft sound, like laughter folded small. "You'll understand someday. Or you won't, and that's fine too. That's the whole fine point of it. Listen — I won't keep you. I only called to say thank you. To the village. For staying. For forgetting me kindly instead of remembering me badly. And to you—" the voice paused, and I had the sudden, vertiginous sense of being seen through, all the way down to the empty shape where a person should be, "—to you for sitting in the chair. It's a good chair. Wear it well."

"Wait," I said. "Tell me your—"

But the light had gone out. Gently, the way lights go out when someone, somewhere, has finally turned in for the night.

I sat there a while. The radiator clanked in B-flat. Outside, the frost lay still on the window, flat and even and finished, holding the village like a blanket holds a sleeper — lightly, warmly, asking nothing.

"Well," I said to the microphone, because the microphone was there and the dead had signed off and somebody should say something. "That was our last caller of the night, Rosalind. A friend of the station. No name given, none needed. You know how it is here. Somebody always calls. Somebody always has."

I queued the closing song — the same one every night, an old hymn tune hummed low, the kind of melody that feels less like music than like a promise kept — and leaned back in the chair, into the dent that fit me perfectly, that has always fit whoever sits here perfectly, that was worn by years I cannot count and hands I cannot name.

"Somebody always will," I said. "Goodnight, Rosalind. Sleep warm. The frost is watching over you, and so am I."

The red ON AIR light went dark. The village breathed. And in the studio above the post office, in the held-breath quiet, I stayed a moment longer — belonging utterly, remembered by no one, home.
