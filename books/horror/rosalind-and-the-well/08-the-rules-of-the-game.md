## 8. The Rules of the Game

Rosalind sat at my kitchen table like he'd been sitting there for years, which was the most frightening thing about him, and I include everything else I'd seen that night in that calculation.

He didn't fidget. Children fidget; that's how you know they're children. Rosalind sat with his hands folded on the table's edge, feet not quite reaching the floor from the chair — a detail my brain kept trying to file under *small child* and kept failing, because nothing else about him was small. His stillness had weight to it. Furniture has that kind of patience. You don't wonder what a chair is thinking.

I'd made tea because making tea is what you do when your hands need a job and your brain needs a hostage situation to look like an evening. I'd given him a mug. He held it without drinking, and the steam rose off it normally, which annoyed me more than it should have. If the tea behaved, then physics was still on duty somewhere, just selectively.

"Okay," I said. "Let's say — *say* — I believe you're real. Let's say I believe any of this."

"Good," Rosalind said.

"Don't encourage me. I'm building up to something." I wrapped both hands around my own mug. "If this is real, if you're real, then I want rules. Everything real has rules. Gravity has rules. Mold has rules. My landlord has rules, and those are the scariest ones I know."

Something moved across his face that might have been approval, or might have been the expression furniture makes when you finally sit down on it correctly.

"You always wanted the rules," he said. "Even when we played. You'd make Wren say them back before every turn."

The name landed in my chest like a dropped coin. Wren. Chapter and verse of a summer I'd spent thirty years pretending was ordinary.

"I don't remember," I said.

"You remember," Rosalind said gently. "You've just been keeping it somewhere dark so it wouldn't grow."

* * *

Here is what I remembered, or what I let myself remember, sitting across from a dead boy holding cold tea:

There was a well cap behind the Henderson property, round and rusted, set into the ground like a coin someone had flipped and never picked up. Four of us: me, Priya — no, not Priya, Priya came later, Priya was a gift of my twenties — me, and Dev, and Marisol, and Wren. We'd ride our bikes past the Henderson place, which had been empty since before any of us were born, and we'd play the listening game.

The rules as nine-year-olds understood them were simple. One person knelt on the cap. The others knocked — three knocks, slow, like a polite visitor. Then the knocker pressed their ear flat against the metal and listened while everyone else went quiet. You were supposed to report what you heard. Water, mostly. Wind in the pipe. Once, Dev swore he heard a train, which was impossible, because there hadn't been a rail line through town since the fifties, and we loved him for the lie.

And once — one time only, one time ever — Wren heard their name.

I remember the exact quality of the silence after. Not the absence of sound but its *attention*. The whole afternoon leaning in.

Wren stood up too fast, scraped their knee, laughed in a way that didn't have any laugh in it, and said, "It's just the water." And we all agreed it was just the water, because agreeing was free and the alternative cost everything.

Wren's family moved away eleven days later. No sale sign. No goodbye party. Their mother came by with a box of comics she said Wren wanted me to have, and she looked at our house the way you look at a car that's been in an accident — checking where the damage was, whether it spread.

We never played again. Not because we decided not to. Because the Henderson lot grew over, and the cap disappeared under blackberry bramble, and childhood did what childhood does, which is close over things like water closing over a stone.

I built explanations the way other people build model ships. *Families move suddenly all the time.* *Kids hear echoes in pipes; the throat of a well makes your own voice come back strange.* *A name is just a sound; any child could have heard their own name in that gurgle, the way you hear words in static.* For twelve years — no, longer, thirty years — I maintained that collection like a curator. Dusting each explanation. Rearranging them when one cracked.

And now here was the crack running through the whole display case, and the curator was out of glue.

* * *

"The game never ended," Rosalind said.

"It ended. We stopped playing."

"No." He turned the mug a quarter-turn, precise, ceremonial. "Stopping isn't ending. A game ends when it's resolved. Yours is still open. It's been open for thirty years, August. It's the longest-running game anyone's ever started down there, and it's why they're so interested in you."

"Who's *they*?"

"The well." He said it the way you'd say *the ocean*, or *the government* — a thing too large to be angry at. "It grants one listener per generation. That's the first rule. One ear, one turn, one claim. Your generation already used its turn. Wren took it."

My mouth had gone dry around the tea. "Then it's done. The turn's taken. Game over."

"That would be true," Rosalind said, "if turns were taken. They're not. They're *given*. By whoever says the words."

"What words?"

"You know the words." He watched me with that terrible patience. "Three knocks. And then whoever calls the turn says the listener's name down the pipe. That's how the well knows who it's allowed to keep. It can't take. It can only receive. Someone has to hand a name through the door."

The kitchen felt colder. I checked the window over the sink — night had gone fully black, the yard a rumor.

"Nobody said anything," I said. "Thirty years, nobody's knocked on anything."

"Thirty years, no." Rosalind's eyes didn't blink enough. I want to be fair to him here — I'll be fairer later, when I understand what he was — but in that moment, watching him not-blink, he looked like something wearing a memory of a boy. "But games can be reopened. Re-invited. Anyone who maps the way down is knocking. Anyone who speaks a name near the opening is calling a turn."

My stomach performed a slow, professional drop.

This afternoon. The survey. Priya crouched over the open floor vent in the utility corridor where my measurements met the county's nothing, reading off coordinates while I fed her numbers, and between readings she'd said my name — casually, warmly, the way she says it forty times a day, *August, hand me the laser, August, you're standing on my cable* — and the vent had been open, and below the vent was the unmapped dark, and below the dark was —

"How close?" I asked. My voice came out level, which I'm proud of even now. "How close does a name have to be?"

"Close enough to be heard," Rosalind said. "And below, they are very good listeners. It's mostly what they do."

* * *

I want to explain what happened inside me at that moment, because it wasn't fear, not first. First it was arithmetic. Ugly, fast, self-directed arithmetic.

Priya had said my name over the vent. Therefore Priya had called a turn. Therefore the well's claim — if claims existed, if boys made of memory were telling the truth at my kitchen table — was on *me*. Not her. She'd handed my name through the door, yes, but she'd handed *mine*, and whatever came up through that opening would come looking for the name it was given.

She'd done it with love. That was the part that made my eyes sting, later, when I let it sting. She'd said my name the way she always does, easy and fond, mid-joke, half-laughing about the cable. Affection, spoken straight down into the dark. If there was a trap, it had been baited with tenderness, and neither of us had known we were handling the trigger.

"There's more," Rosalind said.

Of course there was more. There is always more. Horror is a subscription service.

"Sunrise ends the night's claims," he said. "Whatever is called tonight must be answered tonight. When the sun clears the tree line behind the Henderson lot, the door closes until it's opened again. That's the mercy in it. There's very little mercy, so they wrote down the little there was."

"And the third rule." I already knew there was a third rule. Rules come in threes the way deaths come in buses.

"Anyone who hears their own name spoken from below," Rosalind said, "belongs to it."

The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere upstairs, the house ticked and settled, doing its innocent house noises, and for the first time in my life those noises sounded like a language being spoken politely around me.

"Belongs to it," I repeated. "Meaning what? Meaning it keeps you? Like Wren?"

"Meaning it *knows* you," Rosalind said. "Completely. All the way down to the names you don't use anymore. And once something knows you completely, August, you can't ever be lost to it again. Belonging isn't being taken. Belonging is being *found*, permanently, by something that never lets go."

I thought about that for exactly as long as it took to become unbearable.

"So don't listen," I said. "That's the defense? Plug your ears. Don't kneel on any caps. Don't go near vents. Simple."

"It worked for Dev and Marisol," Rosalind said quietly. "They never heard anything. They lived long lives and forgot the game entirely. Forgetting is allowed. Forgetting is practically encouraged."

"And Wren listened."

"Wren listened."

"And heard their name."

"Yes."

"And was found."

Rosalind didn't answer, which was an answer with excellent posture.

* * *

I got up and walked the house. I'm aware that sounds like panic; it was inventory. Every vent cover, open or closed. The floor register in the hallway — open, had been open for weeks, because the furnace ran hot and I liked the draft. The bathroom fan duct. The gap under the back door where the weatherstripping had given up sometime during the previous administration.

An old house breathes. An old house is full of openings, and every opening goes *down*, eventually, because everything goes down eventually, that's practically the definition of ground.

Behind me, Rosalind had followed at a respectful distance, the way you follow someone in a museum.

"Priya doesn't know," I said. "About any of it. About the game, the well, Wren, you. She thinks I had a bad day in a hole."

"Do you want her to know?"

The question was a trap with velvet on it, and I walked into it at full speed. "If I tell her, she runs. Or worse, she stays and starts researching, and Priya researching is a force of nature with a laptop. Either way she's in it deeper."

"If you don't tell her," Rosalind said, "she's in it anyway. She called the turn. She just doesn't know she's holding the rope."

"She didn't *mean*—"

"The well doesn't weigh meaning." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "It weighs sound. A name is a name. It was given kindly — that will matter to it, actually. Kindness matters down there more than you'd think. But the giving counts regardless."

I stood in the hallway with my hand on the open floor register, feeling warm air rise against my palm, and I understood — with the clean, cold clarity that only arrives when it's least welcome — that the trap had already sprung. Hours ago. In a utility corridor, in a joke, in love. The night wasn't coming for us. The night had *receipts*.

"What happens at sunrise if nobody answers?" I asked.

"Then the claim lapses," Rosalind said. "Until someone opens the door again. Which is why tonight is going to feel very long, and very quiet, and why you're going to want to check every sound twice."

"And if somebody answers?"

He looked at me the way you look at a child who has asked where the other sock goes.

"August," he said, using my name deliberately, and I flinched before I could stop myself, because after tonight I was going to flinch at my own name forever, "something already spoke from below this evening. While you were in the tunnels. Did you hear it?"

I hadn't. I'd been two hundred yards of concrete away, headlamp on, laser whirring, professionally oblivious.

"But it's down there," I said. "Saying it. Right now."

"Saying it patiently," Rosalind agreed. "They're patient the way furniture is patient. They'll say it all night. And every hour, they'll say it a little closer to the surface."

From beneath the floorboards — faint, unhurried, muffled by sixty years of joists and subfloor and my entire rational worldview — came a sound I would spend the rest of my life unable to unhear.

Not my name. Not yet.

Counting.

"One," said the house, in a voice like water finding a crack. "Two. Three."

Rosalind closed his eyes. "It's counting toward sunrise," he said. "Or toward forty. Down there, those are the same number."

I grabbed my boots. Because there was a well cap behind the Henderson property, and a game that had never properly ended, and I had spent thirty years believing that things left buried stay buried — and I was done letting something count toward me in the dark.

"If it wants a listener," I said, pulling the laces tight, "it should know I'm coming down to end the game at the source."

Rosalind stood, small and still and sorrowful at the edge of the light.

"That," he said, "is exactly what it's hoping you'll say."
