## 12. The Name Below

There is a particular quality to running through a tunnel that you entered voluntarily.

I want to be clear about that, because later — when I tell this story, when I finally tell it whole — I don't want anyone thinking I was dragged down there. Nobody dragged me. I laced my boots, I checked my headlamp batteries twice like a responsible adult, and I climbed down into a hole in the world because I thought I could end it at the source. That's the explorer's disease, really. We believe every problem has an origin point, and if we can just reach it, touch it, document it, it becomes manageable. A thing with coordinates can't hurt you.

I have since revised this theory.

* * *

Wren's smile stayed with me as I walked away from him, which sounds impossible, but so does everything else about that night. He hadn't tried to stop me. That was the worst part — he'd just stood there by the well cap with his hands in his pockets, thirty years old inside a nine-year-old body, watching me go with the patient expression of someone who knows the bus schedule better than you do.

"Take your time," he'd said. "It already knows where you are."

I told myself that was a bluff. I told myself a lot of things on the walk down. The tunnel narrowed past the point where any municipal engineer would have signed off on it, the corrugated pipe giving way to hand-cut stone that no county record acknowledged, and I kept moving because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant hearing Rosalind's voice in my head: *You can't un-ring a bell, August. You can only decide what the ringing means.*

Rosalind had refused to come with me. "I'm not allowed below," he'd said, standing at the drain mouth with his hands behind his back like a schoolboy outside a headmaster's office. "Not anymore. I gave up my floor rights a long time ago." He wouldn't explain. He never explained anything fully; he dealt in fragments, in almost-memories, in sentences I kept feeling I'd said first. It was like being haunted by my own footnotes.

So I went alone, which was either brave or stupid, and I've made peace with the fact that those are frequently the same word.

The stone passage sloped downward in long, gentle switchbacks, and the air changed the way air changes when you're descending toward water — cooler, wetter, carrying that green-mineral smell of pond bottoms and old rain. My headlamp beam caught marks on the walls. Chalk, mostly. Children's chalk. Hopscotch grids drawn in places where hopscotch had no business being played, tally marks in clusters of five, and once — I stopped for this one — a careful crayon rendering of four stick figures holding hands around a circle, with a fifth figure standing slightly apart.

I didn't count the figures' fingers. Some part of me understood, even then, that counting them would be an answer to a question I wasn't ready to ask.

"Keep it together, Underhill," I muttered, and my voice came back off the stone wrong. Not echoed — answered. There was a half-second delay, like the tunnel was considering my words before returning them, and in that half-second I felt the specific vertigo of realizing you're not alone in a conversation you started.

I walked faster.

* * *

The well cap was smaller than I remembered.

That's the thing nobody tells you about returning to childhood places: memory inflates them. At nine, the cap behind the Henderson property had been a massive iron disc, heavy as a planet, the kind of object that anchors a whole cosmology of dares and rules. Now it sat in a round stone chamber maybe eight feet across, and it was — what, two feet in diameter? Rusted to lace at the edges, bolted down with bolts gone soft as candle wax. Someone had scratched a game board into its surface. Nine men's morris. The pieces were still mid-game.

I crouched beside it and put my palm flat on the iron, the way we used to, the way the rules required. Cold soaked through my glove immediately, cold with texture to it, cold like something gripping back.

"Okay," I said aloud, because saying things aloud makes them real, and real things can be handled. "I'm here. Game's over. Everyone grew up, everyone moved, whatever you are, your players are gone. Consider yourself closed."

Silence. The good kind, even — the ordinary kind, the silence of deep places that have never heard a voice and don't care about one. For maybe forty seconds I let myself believe it had worked. I let myself build the whole explanation right there in the dark, brick by beautiful brick: settling groundwater, acoustic weirdness, a childhood game that had imprinted on me the way children imprint on anything, and everything since — Rosalind, the counting, Wren — a stress response wearing the costume of the supernatural. I was so good at building that structure. Twelve years of practice. I could have taught a masterclass. *Denial Architecture 401: Advanced Load-Bearing Rationalizations.*

Then the well said my name.

Not "August."

* * *

I need to describe what it did to me, and I need you to understand that the problem was not fear. Fear I know. Fear is a tool; I've used it plenty, crawling through collapse zones and flooded culverts, and it's useful, it sharpens you, it says *move.* What happened when the voice came up out of that shaft was not fear. It was recognition, and recognition is so much worse.

The voice was small. That's the detail that undoes me even now. Not booming, not demonic, none of the movie stuff. It sounded like a child reading aloud from a book they were proud of, careful and clear, each syllable placed like a stone in a wall. And it said:

"*Margaret Eleanor Underhill.*"

My deadname. The name I retired eleven years ago, folded up and buried with honors, the name of a person who never quite existed and whose nonexistence I built my whole self on top of. Two people ever knew that name was mine to begin with — my mother, who died before I transitioned, and a county clerk who stamped a form and forgot me forever. No one living knew it. No one *could* know it. I had burned the paperwork myself, in a coffee can, on a night I remember as the truest of my life.

And the well said it like it was introducing me. Like it was reading me back to myself.

Here's the horror, and I've turned it over enough times now that I can hold it without gloves: it fit. Hearing that name rise out of the dark, in that patient child's voice, in that place — it didn't feel wrong. It felt like being *seen.* Completely, exhaustively seen, every layer at once, the nine-year-old and the twenty-one-year-old and the tired explorer and the kid who invented a friend because the real ones kept leaving, all of it known and held and *pronounced* by something that had been keeping my file since before I knew I had one.

Do you understand how seductive that is? How dangerous? Most people go their whole lives without being fully seen by anyone, not even the people who love them. We show each other rooms, never houses. And something under my hometown opened every door at once and said *there you are,* and some starving cellar-deep part of me wanted to answer to it.

That's how it gets you. Not with claws. With accuracy.

"*Margaret Eleanor Underhill,*" it said again, softer now, coaxing, and beneath the words I heard the other sound I'd been refusing to hear since the porch: the counting. Forty. Starting over. Except this time the numbers weren't numbers, they were — no. They were numbers. I was doing it again, reaching for the explanation, and the explanation was thinner than paper, and I watched my own hands reach for it anyway because the alternative was admitting that a hole in the ground knew the name of the person I used to be.

"It's not my name," I said. My voice cracked in the middle like I was nine again. "That's not — I'm August. August Underhill. Whatever you think you have, it's out of date. It's *retired.*"

The silence that followed had a quality I can only describe as *interest.*

Then, gently, almost kindly, the voice said: "*We keep what we're given.*"

And I ran.

* * *

I wish I could give you a dignified account of the retreat. I can't. I scrambled, I knocked my shoulder against stone, my headlamp strobed off the walls while I took switchbacks two at a time going the wrong direction and had to double back, and somewhere in there I lost a glove and couldn't make myself care. Behind me — not following, worse than following, just *present* — the name kept coming. Not shouted. Spoken at conversational volume, at intervals, the way you'd call a child in from dinner who you knew would come eventually.

"*Margaret.*"

A switchback.

"*Eleanor.*"

The chalk marks on the walls, passing in my beam — the tallies, the hopscotch, the four figures holding hands. In the second version of the graffiti — because yes, I passed it again, or it passed me, the geometry down there being less a layout than a mood — the fifth figure had moved closer to the circle.

"*Underhill.*"

I stopped correcting it. That's the part I hate most, the part I'll carry: somewhere around the third repetition, some animal floor of my brain stopped fighting the name and started flinching toward it, the way you flinch toward your own name called across a crowded room. Eleven years of becoming August, and one minute underground nearly undid it, because the well doesn't argue with who you are. It just offers you the older draft, complete, annotated, and waits to see which version you reach for.

Identity is supposed to be armor. I'd built mine carefully, deliberately, out of truth and community and a chosen name worn smooth with use. But armor only works against attacks. This wasn't an attack. It was an *invoice,* itemized, addressed correctly, and there is no armor against paperwork that's accurate.

The storm drain grate appeared ahead of me, gray with pre-dawn nothing — no, not dawn, I corrected myself, still hours early, the gray was just the streetlight bleeding in — and I hauled myself up through it with hands that shook badly enough that I skinned both palms, and I lay on the asphalt of Delmore Street breathing like a landed fish while the town of Harrow's Bend slept on around me, oblivious, its pipes and culverts and unmapped veins all quietly connected to a chamber where something knew me better than anyone alive.

The counting resumed under the road. Patient. Unhurried. Forty, starting over.

I got up. I wiped my face with my remaining sleeve. And I started walking home, because there was exactly one person in the world I trusted, she was probably awake, and I had already decided — with the cold, clear, cowardly clarity that comes after you've heard your own grave pronounced — what I was going to tell her.

Nothing. Nothing true, anyway.

* * *

Priya was on the couch with her laptop and a bowl of cereal at 4 a.m., which should have been funny and wasn't. She looked up when I came in, took one look at my face, and closed the laptop halfway — not all the way, Priya never committed fully to pretending she wasn't investigating something.

"You look like you lost a fight with a landslide," she said.

"Tunnel collapsed behind me. Had to move fast." I set my ruined glove on the counter, evidence, corroboration. I'd gotten good at staging evidence over twelve years; it's amazing what a torn glove can certify. "I'm fine. Mostly fine. Scuffed."

She studied me the way she studies survey data, looking for the anomaly, the reading that doesn't match the others. I poured water I didn't drink and let her study. This was the arrangement, unspoken, years old: Priya noticed things, and I offered explanations, and we both pretended the exchange was honest.

Except — and here's where the night's lesson sat in my chest like swallowed ice — except Priya has never once believed my explanations. I realized it standing there with the glass in my hand. She accepts them. She lets them pass. But she doesn't *believe* them, because Priya believes data, and my data has never matched my stories. She's known for years that something in me files things under a different system. She's just been kind enough, or scared enough, or loyal enough, to let the filing stand.

"The Henderson site," she said slowly. "August, I found something today. In the archives. You're going to want—"

"Not tonight." I heard my own voice do it — the gentle deflection, the loving lie already taking shape. "Tomorrow. I promise. Tonight I just—" I made my shoulders drop, made my breath come out slow, performed exhaustion like I'd performed it a hundred times. "Tonight I need it to just be a bad day. Can it be a bad day?"

Something crossed her face. I named it concern at the time. Later I'd understand it was the look of someone deciding, consciously, to accept a lie from a person they love — and what that decision costs, and what it might mean she already suspects.

"Yeah," she said. "It can be a bad day." She stood, squeezed my arm, and paused at the hallway. "For what it's worth," she added, casual as weather, "the vent in the kitchen's been making noise again. Counting-ish. Probably pipes."

Probably pipes. She said it the way I say everything, and I watched her say it, and I understood with total clarity that the well had heard her say my name over that vent hours ago — casually, affectionately, *August, your tea's getting cold* — and that the game keeps meticulous records.

"I'll check the pipes tomorrow," I said.

We both knew I wouldn't. We both, I suspect, knew the other knew. And upstairs, as I lay down without sleeping, the counting under the floor reached forty, paused — listening, always listening — and began again, and I lay there rehearsing lies for the people I loved most, because I had learned tonight the one fact that reorganizes everything:

The well doesn't need to take you by force. It just needs to say your name right.

And it had said mine perfectly.
