## 13. Locks and Lies

*AUGUST*

I came up out of the storm drain at 4:47 in the morning with mud to the collarbone and a lie already half-built in my throat, which is a skill I'd like to report I'm ashamed of, except that I'm not sure I am.

The walk home took eleven minutes. I used all of them. I rehearsed on the empty sidewalk like a kid practicing a confession in front of a bathroom mirror, except backwards — instead of *how do I tell them*, it was *how do I make sure I never have to*. The streetlights buzzed. Somewhere a dog was losing an argument with a raccoon. Normal sounds. Town sounds. Sounds that had never once, in thirty-one years of living here, come from below.

I let myself think about Wren for exactly as long as it took to cross Maple Street, and then I folded him back up and put him away, because there would be time for falling apart later, and later was a country you could only visit if you survived the night.

The porch light was on. Priya's light — she leaves it on when she knows I'm out, has done since our second survey together, when I came back at 3 a.m. soaked to the waist and she said, "I wasn't worried, I just like paying the electric company." It's the closest she comes to saying things.

Rosalind sat on the steps beside the light, patient as furniture, and looked at me with those nine-year-old eyes that had watched me crawl into the dark after promising him not to.

"You went down," he said.

"I went down."

"He told you about the trade."

"He told me about the trade."

Rosalind nodded slowly, and for a moment he looked less like a child than like something very old wearing a child the way you'd wear a coat borrowed from a smaller friend. "And?"

"And I need Priya gone before sunrise." I wiped my hands on my thighs, accomplishing nothing. "Not asked. Not suggested. *Gone.*"

"You're going to lie to her."

"I'm going to *save* her," I said, and heard how it sounded, and said it again anyway, quieter: "I'm going to save her."

* * *

Inside, the house had that 2 a.m. stillness where even the refrigerator hum sounds like eavesdropping. Priya was asleep on the couch, laptop open on her chest, screen dimmed to a spreadsheet of county parcel data — of course she hadn't waited up, and of course she'd waited up. Her glasses had slid halfway down her nose. There was a mug of tea on the floor that had gone cold hours ago, and next to it, face-down, my field notebook, the one I'd left in her car three days ago.

My stomach did something complicated.

"Priya." I touched her shoulder. "Hey. Up. We need to talk."

She surfaced the way she always does — instantly, completely, no groggy middle. One second asleep, the next looking at me with total attention, which is either a gift or a weapon depending on what you're about to say to her. Her eyes went from my face to the mud on my clothes and back.

"Okay," she said. "Before you start: yes, I read your notes. No, I'm not sorry. And August—" she paused, and I watched her decide, deliberately, to be gentle, which from Priya is its own kind of alarm bell, "—what happened to you down there?"

Here it was. The door marked TRUTH, standing wide open, lit like an exit sign.

I walked past it.

"The tunnels are structurally unsound," I said. My voice came out steady, which felt obscene. "Worse than unsound. The sections under the old Henderson parcel are collapsing — I saw fresh subsidence, cracks running the length of the main passage. If we'd been forty feet deeper when it let go, we'd be a news item. A small one." I made myself smile. "I've been doing this twelve years, Priya. I know bad ground when I'm standing on it. This is bad ground. And it's not just the tunnels — it's the whole area. The substrate under this neighborhood is compromised. Which means—"

"Which means I should leave town," she said.

"—tonight," I finished. "Yes. Tonight. Today, technically. Before—"

"Okay."

I stopped. "...Okay?"

"Okay." She closed the laptop. She stood up, stretched until something in her back popped, and picked up her cold tea like this was a Tuesday. "I'll pack. Can I shower first? I smell like a gas station bathroom and I refuse to drive twenty miles smelling like a gas station bathroom."

That was it. That was the whole performance. No argument, no *are you sure*, no *show me the survey data*, no *since when do you care about substrate*. Priya, who once spent six weeks disputing a parking ticket on principle. Priya, who made me re-measure a tunnel twice because my numbers were off by four centimeters and she could *feel* it.

She agreed too easily.

I want to be honest about what I felt in that moment, because it wasn't relief. Relief would have been the correct emotion, the emotion a smart person feels when their plan works on the first try. What I actually felt was the specific vertigo of a floor that gives slightly under your weight — not enough to fall through, just enough to tell you the floor has opinions.

"Great," I said. "Shower. Pack. I'll make coffee."

"Coffee," she agreed, and went upstairs, and the house made room for her footsteps the way it had been making room for everything all week, doors drifting open ahead of us like a butler with too much initiative, and I stood alone in the kitchen and told myself the plan was working.

Told myself. Past tense of trust. Future tense of doubt.

* * *

I made coffee I didn't drink. I stood at the counter and listened to the shower run upstairs and tried to construct the feeling of victory and couldn't find it anywhere in the inventory.

Because here's the thing I figured out somewhere between the coffee maker and the window, standing in the gray pre-dawn with my reflection ghosted faintly against the glass: the lie only protects her if she believes it.

And Priya has never once believed my explanations.

Twelve years. Twelve years of *bad day* and *it's nothing* and *old pipes* and *I'm fine, I just didn't sleep*. She accepts them the way you accept a gift you're going to regift — politely, promptly, without ever being fooled. I've watched her do it. She nods, she changes the subject, she makes tea, and behind her eyes there's a little ledger where she writes down every explanation I've ever given her, filed under a heading I've never been allowed to read. She doesn't push because pushing would make me lie better, and she's decided — God help her — that whatever I'm not saying, I'm not saying for a reason she can live with.

Until now. Until I asked her to leave town based on *substrate*, and she said okay in under four seconds.

Either I'd finally found a lie good enough to fool her.

Or she believed me exactly as much as she's always believed me — which is to say, not at all — and she was leaving anyway. Not because the ground is dangerous. Because *I* was scared, and she could see it, and she loves me more than she loves being right.

I genuinely could not tell you which option frightened me more.

The shower shut off. I heard her moving around upstairs, drawers opening, the particular thud of her hiking pack being dragged off the closet shelf. Packing sounds. Leaving sounds. Every one of them landed in my chest like a nail driven by someone very gentle.

This is what loyalty looks like, I thought. Loyalty isn't staying. Loyalty is trusting someone enough to go when they ask, even when you know they're lying, because you've decided their lies are load-bearing. She was carrying my lie out of town on her back like furniture, and she knew it was furniture, and she carried it anyway.

And underneath that thought, colder, patient as furniture itself, came the other one:

If she doesn't believe the lie, then the lie isn't protecting her. The truth is protecting her — the truth she's guessed at, the shape of it, the weight of it in my voice. Which means the thing keeping her safe tonight isn't my cleverness. It's her faith. And faith, I remembered, looking at the floor vent across the kitchen, the one with the dark gap beneath it, is exactly the kind of currency certain parties accept.

Something down there had heard her say my name over that vent. Something down there had heard her choose to stay in a house full of wrongness because leaving me alone was worse. If love was the trap — and Rosalind had been clear that it was, that affection spoken aloud was an address — then Priya driving away might not matter at all. You can't outrun a claim. Ask Wren's family, who moved away overnight in 1974 and left a son-shaped hole behind them.

No. Stop. She'd be twenty miles out by sunrise. Distance mattered. Rosalind had said distance mattered, hadn't he? He'd said—

He'd said the well keeps what it's given. He'd never actually said anything about mileage. I turned that over and found the absence where the reassurance should have been, and my hands went cold around the warm mug.

Footsteps on the stairs. I arranged my face into something reassuring, which is to say I arranged it into a lie, which is to say I didn't arrange it at all.

Priya came down dressed for driving, hair damp, pack over one shoulder. She looked at me for a long moment — long enough that I felt audited — and then she crossed the kitchen and hugged me. Hard. The kind of hug that transfers information.

"Call me when the sun's up," she said into my shoulder. "Just to say the sun's up. That's all. You don't have to say anything else."

"I will," I said, and it was the truest thing I'd said all night, and we both knew it was also a promise I might not be able to keep, and she held on one second longer anyway.

Then she pulled back, picked up her keys, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame.

"For the record," she said, not turning around, "the substrate under this neighborhood is fine. I checked the geological survey last week. But you looked at me just now like a person who's counting the hours until something, so I'm going. Drive safe, August. Lock the door."

The door clicked shut behind her. Her headlights swept the living room wall and slid away.

I locked the door. Then the deadbolt. Then, absurdly, the little chain, as if the thing below had ever once been inconvenienced by hardware.

From the top of the stairs, in a voice like a child reading the last line of a story he already knows the ending to, Rosalind said:

"She didn't believe you."

"No," I said. "She didn't."

"Are you going to tell me why that makes it worse?"

I looked at the floor vent. The house was very quiet. Somewhere far beneath the quiet, so far it might have been memory, something began to count.

* * *

*PRIYA*

Twenty miles out of town, at the exact spot where Route 9 crosses the reservoir and the trees finally stop pressing against the road, I pulled onto the gravel shoulder and put the car in park and sat there with my hands at ten and two like a student driver, because I needed something to hold.

Let's establish the facts, since apparently nobody else in this partnership is interested in facts.

Fact: August Underhill came home at five in the morning covered in mud and wearing a lie like a raincoat two sizes too big.

Fact: I have known August for six years. I have surveyed ninety-some tunnels with them. I have seen them handle cave-ins, flash floods, a sheriff with a grudge, and one memorable raccoon situation, and they have never once looked at me the way they looked at me in that kitchen. People look at you like that when they're memorizing you. When they think the picture might have to last.

Fact: the substrate under this neighborhood is fine. I pulled the geological survey last Thursday for the overlay project, mostly out of boredom, partly because August had been weird all week in a way that made me want data. Clay over shale, stable water table, boring as toast. Whatever August saw down there, it wasn't geology.

So. Either my best friend has lost their mind, or my best friend is trying to get me out of town before something happens, and they're willing to burn six years of credibility to do it.

I got the field notes out of the glove box. I'd taken them from the couch before packing — sue me — and I'd skimmed them while my conditioner set, and I'd told myself the thing I saw was a trick of the light, and now, parked above black water with the sky going gray at the edges, I opened them properly.

August's handwriting is beautiful. Architectural. Every measurement legible, every note dated. For eleven pages it stayed that way.

On page twelve it started to change. Not degrade exactly — *lean*. The letters tilted downward, line by line, like the words were walking downhill. By page fourteen the sentences were sliding off the bottom edge of the page entirely, continuing onto the desk in a script I'd never seen August use: older, looping, written by a hand that learned penmanship a hundred years ago. The measurements kept going. They were still accurate. That was the part that made my skin try to leave my body — whoever was writing, they still knew the numbers.

The last legible line in August's own hand read: *It knows the layout of the house.*

I put the notes on the passenger seat. I looked at the road behind me, the one leading away, sensible, survivable, twenty miles of ordinary morning between me and everything.

Then I thought about August setting out a second cup of tea some mornings and pretending they didn't know why. About the way they'd flinched last month when I said their name over the floor vent, quick, like a man touching a hot stove, and laughed it off, and I'd filed it in the ledger where I file everything. About the hug. About *call me when the sun's up* — not *when you've slept*, not *tomorrow*. When the sun's up. Like sunrise was a deadline. Like they were asking me to confirm they'd survived until a specific hour.

You don't leave a person alone with a countdown.

I turned the car around.

The lie was terrible and I loved them for it, and I was going to drive back through that gray morning and stand in their kitchen and say the sentence I should have said an hour ago: *I don't believe you, I never believe you, and I'm not going anywhere, so you'd better start telling me the truth.*

Behind me, in the east, the sky was thinking about getting lighter.

In the rearview mirror, for half a second — long enough, only long enough — the road behind me seemed to tilt downward, the whole highway sloping gently toward town, as if the land itself had leaned in to listen.

I drove faster.
