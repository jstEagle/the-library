## 22. The Well Keeps What It's Given

Grief, it turns out, has a housekeeping schedule.

Nobody tells you that. The movies would have you believe grief is all rain on windows and dramatic monologues in parked cars. In practice it's mostly laundry. It's remembering to buy oat milk for someone who isn't there anymore and doing it anyway because your hands don't know how to stop. It's setting out a second cup of tea at seven p.m., watching it go cold, pouring it out, washing the cup, and doing the whole thing again tomorrow like a small liturgy with no congregation.

I've been keeping that liturgy for six weeks now. Rosalind never drank tea — I'm not sure he could drink anything, being mostly memory held together by good intentions — but he used to sit across from me while I drank mine, patient as furniture, and I find I'm not ready to stop setting his place. Maybe I never will be. Priya doesn't tease me about it, which is how I know she understands. She just refills the kettle when she sees me reaching for it, and we let the empty chair have its cup.

The house is quieter now. Not silent — houses are never silent, and ours especially so, given its history of running every tap at once like a choir warming up — but quiet in the way a room is quiet after an argument has finally ended. The floorboards have stopped counting. The water tastes like water again, chlorine and old pipes and nothing else. Some mornings I still hold my glass up to the light and check it anyway, the way you check a healed bone by pressing on it. Just to feel sure.

Priya tells me I should stop checking. Priya also sleeps with her boots by the bed and a crowbar under it, so I take her advice with appropriate seasoning.

* * *

The moving truck came on a Tuesday, which felt right. Nothing terrible ever happens on a Tuesday; Tuesdays are structurally incapable of drama. A boxy white truck idled in front of the old Henderson lot — the one where the well house used to stand before 1974, then didn't, then stood again in every way that matters — and a man in a polo shirt directed two teenagers as they hauled furniture into the little blue house that had been vacant longer than I'd been alive.

I was coming back from a survey. A real one, this time: storm drains with actual city records behind them, measurements that matched the maps, graffiti that aged forward instead of backward. I'd gotten careful about what I went looking for. You learn, eventually, that curiosity is a kind of knocking, and some doors are better left unknocked. I map the real tunnels now. The unmapped ones can stay unmapped. There's a whole geography down there that exists only because someone once agreed to imagine it, and I have retired from imagining.

But I took the long way home past the Henderson lot, because I always do. Call it a pilgrimage. Call it a scar I like to press on. Either way, that's how I happened to be standing on the sidewalk when the girl came around the side of the blue house at a dead run, arms out like a small airplane, hair flying, pursued by absolutely nothing except the joy of running.

She skidded to a stop when she saw me. Kids do that — they assess strangers with a speed and honesty adults have paid a lot of money to unlearn. She looked me over: the field bag, the boots, the general aura of someone who spends their free time in holes in the ground.

"Are you an explorer?" she asked.

"Sort of," I said. "More of a mapper."

"My dad says there's tunnels under everything." She said it the way kids say things they've been told and have decided to believe, which is to say, with total conviction and no sense of consequence. "He says the whole town is sitting on them like a lid."

"Your dad sounds like fun at parties."

"He's boring." She delivered this verdict without malice, purely as data. Then she leaned in, lowering her voice to the register children use for information of genuine importance, and said the thing.

"We have a friend already. He was on our porch this morning before we even had a porch. He knows all the best hiding places."

The world did the thing it does now, when it does it at all — a slow tilt, like a photograph sliding off a wall. I kept my face very still. Twelve years of explaining things away teaches you muscle control if it teaches you nothing else.

"Oh yeah?" I said. "What's his name?"

She frowned, and the frown was the worst part, because I knew it. I'd worn that exact frown at nine years old, standing in front of my mother, trying to explain why I couldn't come inside until Rosalind got his turn on the swing. *He doesn't have one yet,* nine-year-old me had said. *He's waiting for me to give him one.*

"I forgot to ask," the girl said. "He's polite, though. He waited on the steps. He said he'd wait as long as it takes."

*Patient as furniture.* My own phrase, handed back to me through a stranger's mouth, six weeks after I'd buried it in a collapsing tunnel along with the only person who'd ever earned it.

"What's your name?" I asked.

"Mabel."

"Mabel. Can I tell you something? Explorer to hiding-place expert?"

She nodded solemnly, arms crossing, all business.

"Some friends are only pretend," I said, gently, crouching to her level. "And the important thing — the most important thing you'll ever learn about friends, pretend or otherwise — is to remember which ones you made up."

She considered this with the gravity of a supreme court. "What happens if you forget?"

I thought about Wren, thirty years unchanged at the bottom of a hole, smiling with too much practice. I thought about a boy made of borrowed sentences who spent himself holding a door and never asked to be remembered, only to be named. I thought about a counting voice reaching forty and starting over, and about how the difference between a game and a trap is only whether anyone remembers it's supposed to end.

"Then sometimes," I said, "they forget too. And then nobody remembers whose turn it is."

Mabel looked at me for a long moment. Then she shrugged with the magnificent resilience of the seven-year-old, yelled "BYE EXPLORER PERSON," and ran back around the side of the house, arms out, flying.

I stood on the sidewalk a while longer than a person reasonably stands on a sidewalk.

* * *

That night I told Priya about it. All of it — no editing, no softening, no structural-damage fiction. That's the household rule now, the one we built out of the wreckage of the last lie I told her. We tell each other everything. It turns out honesty is less a virtue than a load-bearing wall; you don't appreciate it until you've watched what happens when someone pulls it out.

Priya listened with her feet tucked under her on the couch, the crowbar within reach out of habit rather than expectation, and when I finished she was quiet for exactly as long as it took her to decide something.

"So we watch the house," she said.

"We watch the house," I agreed.

"And if the boy comes back—"

"He'll come back."

"—then we introduce ourselves. Formally. Both of us." She said it simply, the way she says everything that matters, and I felt the familiar ache-and-gratitude combination that is loving Priya in a single sensation. She wasn't saying *we'll fight it*. She was saying *you won't be alone on the porch*. After everything, that's still the whole offer, and it's still enough.

"You know," I said, "most roommates just split the utilities."

"Most roommates haven't heard a child counting under the floorboards at 2 a.m." She reached over and took my hand, hers warm and calloused from climbing gear. "We're not most roommates. We're a whole thing. A unit. Ask anyone."

"There's no one to ask."

"Ask the chair. The chair's seen everything."

The chair, holding its cold cup of tea, declined to comment.

* * *

Here's what I know now, six weeks out, that I didn't know at nine or at twenty-one or at any point during twelve years of building explanations like sandbag walls against a flood that was coming from underneath:

The well keeps what it's given. That's not cruelty; it's just physics, the way gravity isn't cruel. And what it was given — what *we* gave it, four children playing at the edge of the woods, plus one child's frightened wish that her friends would never, ever leave — that debt didn't die with the Henderson house or the demolition in '74 or the sunrise we climbed out into. Debts like that don't die. They rest. They wait for the next pair of hands to lift the cap.

Rosalind bought us time. He spent his name — the true one, the pre-Henderson one, the currency the well couldn't mint — to void the oldest clause and take our place below. But a negotiator can only negotiate once per generation, and names, however powerful, are not erasers. Somewhere beneath the blue house and the demolished well house and the unmapped tunnels that were never dug at all, something is patient again. Something counts.

I hear it some nights. Not under the floorboards anymore — those are clean, I've checked, salted, mirrored, burned the proper names over the proper flames. No, these days I hear it in the space between waking and sleeping, that border country where explanations haven't clocked in yet. *Thirty-seven, thirty-eight, thirty-nine.* And then, always, before forty: silence. A pause with a shape to it. The pause of something listening back.

I used to think the horror was the voice. I know better now. The horror is the arithmetic — the knowledge that every game played at the edge of the woods adds a player, and every player owes a turn, and somewhere a ledger is kept in a hand older than the town, and the entries never close. They only wait.

* * *

Three weeks after Mabel, I passed the blue house again and saw chalk drawings on the Henderson driveway. Hopscotch. Four squares, drawn by small hands, and beside them, in different chalk, in a looping hand that made my stomach drop through the sidewalk and keep going, a fifth square. Drawn slightly apart. Drawn waiting.

I stood there until my legs remembered how to work. Then I took out my keys, found the piece of chalk I carry now — yes, I carry chalk, Priya carries a crowbar, we contain multitudes — and I knelt on the driveway and drew a sixth square. Connected to none. Belonging to no game. Just a square, alone, with a stick figure inside it and a smile, the way you draw a friend who is only pretend, the way you draw proof of authorship.

*This one's mine,* the drawing said. *I made him up. Remember.*

Because that's the loophole, if there is one. Names are the one currency the well cannot mint — Rosalind told me that, and he paid everything he had to make it true. Which means the reverse must hold too: the well cannot mint friends either. It can only collect the ones we make ourselves and fail to claim.

So I claim mine. Every night, second cup of tea. Every morning, the name said aloud in the kitchen, quietly, like watering a plant: *Rosalind.* Mine. Made up. Accounted for.

The girl Mabel will forget, probably. Children forget; it's their superpower and their vulnerability, same as it was mine. Her parents will paint over the chalk. The fifth square will fade or won't. And some evening years from now, a kid on that porch will mention a polite boy who knows all the best hiding places, and maybe someone will be standing on the sidewalk to say the words, or maybe there won't be, and the game will open its patient eye and note that a turn is owed.

But tonight, the house is warm. Priya is arguing with a playlist that deserves it. The second cup steams across from me, untouched, faithful. Outside, October is putting the woods to bed, and the tree line stands ordinary and dark and only trees.

Somewhere below — under the blue house, under the hopscotch, under the roots of everything we agreed to imagine — patient as furniture, something counts to forty.

And begins again.
