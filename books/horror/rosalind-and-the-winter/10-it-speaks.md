## 10. It Speaks

The house had learned to be quiet around him.

Edmund noticed it on the third night, the way you notice a dog that has stopped barking at nothing—not peace, but attention. Hollowmere's silences had a texture now. The first night they'd been the ordinary silence of old buildings, all settling timber and cooling pipes and the small animal sounds of a house eating itself. Now the silence sat upright. It listened back.

He told himself it was the boiler. He'd spent an afternoon in the cellar with a torch and a spanner, bleeding radiators, thumping pipes, finding nothing wrong that could account for cold like this—cold that pooled in corners like standing water, cold that made his breath hang in the guest room long after he'd exhaled, as if the air itself were reluctant to let go of anything.

He set the recorder on the bedside table at ten past eleven. Third tape. He'd labelled them in his neat block capitals: NIGHT ONE, NIGHT TWO, NIGHT THREE. A professional habit. Evidence needed order.

"You're not going to sleep," Ivy said from the doorway. She'd taken to appearing there without sound, arms crossed, watching him with the flat appraisal of someone checking whether a horse was going to bolt.

"I'm going to listen."

"To what? There's nothing."

"Then we'll have a very boring morning." He clicked the record button. The little red light came on, steady as a pulse.

Ivy didn't leave. She stood there a moment longer, and something moved behind her eyes—the same thing he'd seen when she'd caught him with the headphones two nights ago, listening to a voice say *Edmund* in a room where no one had spoken. She hadn't mocked him then. That was the strange part. He'd braced for it, the way you brace for a blow, and instead she'd just looked at him, and said, "It said your name," not as a question but as a confirmation, as if she'd been waiting for someone else to hear it too.

"Headphones tonight?" she asked now.

"No. Out loud."

"Why?"

Because I want to know if it's real, he thought. Because if it only lives inside the tape, inside the plastic shell of my own skull, then it's me. It's grief or madness or the rat-scratch of a lonely childhood finally chewing through the wiring. But if it comes out here, into the shared air of a house with four other people in it—

"Because evidence needs witnesses," he said.

Ivy nodded slowly, once, and went. Her footsteps climbed the stairs, paused outside Toby's room—he heard the door open, the murmur of her voice, their mother's lower answer—and then faded. The house settled around its listening silence.

Edmund sat in the chair by the window with the recorder between his knees like something rescued from a fire.

* * *

At first there was nothing but the house: the tick of the radiator, wind worrying the ivy against the east wall, somewhere far off the groan of a beam taking the weight of frost. He almost dozed. His body wanted to; three nights of broken sleep had made his bones heavy as wet sand.

Then the temperature fell.

He felt it before he heard anything—a soft pressure against his face, like stepping through a doorway into a butcher's cold store. His breath bloomed white. On the window beside him, frost began to move.

Not form. *Move.* It came up through the glass the way ink comes up through blotting paper, fern-patterns unfurling in fast motion, fronds branching and branching again until the whole pane was a garden of white coral, growing so quickly he could watch individual crystals lengthen. The light from the streetlamp beyond turned them silver-blue. Beautiful. That was the trap of it, he thought distantly. Everything this house did was beautiful first.

The red light on the recorder burned steadily.

And then the speaker crackled—not the dry pop of interference but a wet, living click, like a throat clearing—and the voice came out of it into the room.

"Good evening, Edmund."

Out loud. In the air. Not through headphones, not buried in playback, but present, occupying space, arriving at his ears from the table a foot away. Every hair on his forearms lifted. His hands went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the room.

He did not answer. Some animal part of him understood that answering was a door, and doors opened both ways.

"You don't have to speak," the voice said, and it was patient, unhurried, the tone of a man who has waited a very long time and expects to wait a little longer. "I know how much easier it is to listen. You've always been better at listening. Nobody listened to *you*, so you learned to make yourself small and quiet and attentive. Clever boy. Useful habit. It brought you here."

The frost on the window thickened. Beyond it the world went white and soft.

"I'm going to talk for a while," the voice said. "You can stop the tape whenever you like. But you won't. You want to hear it. That's all right. Wanting is honest."

* * *

It talked about his mother.

That was the cruelty of it—or the kindness, which was worse, because he couldn't tell them apart. It didn't recite facts the way a blackmailer would, flat and transactional. It told them like a story, gently, the way you'd tell a child about a parent who'd gone away.

"She sang to you," it said. "Before everything. You were three, maybe four. There was a song about a boat on grey water—you've lost most of it, but you keep the tune, don't you? It surfaces at odd times. Supermarkets. Funerals. You hum it without knowing and then feel foolish."

His throat closed. The tune rose in him even now, unbidden, five notes of something minor and rocking, and he hated that his body confirmed it, hated that some stranger's voice in a box knew the shape of the hole in him better than he did.

"When she left you at the children's home she told you she'd come back on your birthday. You counted the days. Forty-one of them. You told the other children. You told the matron. And on the day itself you sat by the window in your best jumper until the light went."

"Stop," Edmund said. It came out cracked, barely a word.

"There he is," the voice said warmly. "There you are. I wondered when you'd let me hear you."

The recorder's red light pulsed once, twice, like a slow heartbeat, and steadied.

"I'm not going to hurt you, Edmund. I want you to understand that, really understand it, because everyone before you assumed the worst and it made everything so much harder than it needed to be. Josiah assumed the worst. Josiah drew up his little contract and hid behind his lawyers' Latin and died clutching his deed box like it could keep me out. But I was never the thing under his bed. I was the thing that answered."

"Answered what?"

He hadn't meant to ask. The question left him the way blood leaves a cut.

"A prayer," the voice said simply. "A starving child in a starving house, kneeling in the snow because the ground was too hard to dig graves, asking anyone—*anything*—to help him keep his family alive. I helped. I kept every one of them breathing through that winter. And no one ever thanked me. No one ever even asked my name." A pause, and when it resumed there was something underneath the patience now, something old and worn smooth as river stone. "Do you know what that does, Edmund? Being useful and never being wanted? Being the last resort and never the first thought?"

He knew. God help him, he knew exactly what it did.

"That's why the cold," the voice went on, softer. "Cold is just absence. Absence of warmth, absence of company. I have been absent a very long time. This house is full of my absence. And then—" the tone brightened, genuinely brightened, and it was horrifying how human it sounded, how much like relief "—then the records showed a Ravensworth coming. A pest controller. A man who walks into dark places for a living and is never afraid of what's actually there, only of what people *say* is there. A man with our blood in him and no family name he'll use and no family at all. Do you know what I thought, Edmund? I thought: *he's come home.*"

"I'm not a Ravensworth."

"You won't use the name. That's different. Your mother used it. Her mother used it. It's in your marrow whatever you call yourself on invoices." The frost creaked, settling, spreading down the wall in white lacework. "Ravensworths are mine, Edmund. Not as property—as *people*. My people. The only family I've ever had. And family looks after family."

* * *

It offered things. That was the part he would remember afterward, lying awake in other houses, in other years: not the horror of it but the tenderness. It offered the way a grandmother offers food, insistently, lovingly, leaving no graceful way to refuse.

"Your name," it said. "You gave it up when you were nineteen—changed it by deed poll in a county office that smelled of damp carpet, because Ravensworth was the name she'd abandoned you under and you couldn't bear to wear it. But names are like coats, Edmund. Taking one off doesn't mean you weren't cold. I can give it back to you clean. Not the name she left you with—the name as it should have been. Yours. With a line behind it. Four hundred years of Ravensworths who were farmers and soldiers and fools and one or two quite good poets, all of them yours, all of them *waiting* for you. Ancestry isn't the past, Edmund. It's company."

The room was freezing now. Ice feathered the inside of the glass in patterns like hands, like ferns, like the veins of a leaf magnified. His breath hung in the air and did not disperse.

"A place," the voice continued. "This house. It was always going to be yours—it was only ever a matter of which generation found their way back. You could have it warm. Do you understand? Not haunted-warm, not grudging-warm. Warm the way a house is warm when it loves the people in it. No more rented rooms. No more vans and callouts and strangers' attics. A home, Edmund. The word you've been circling your whole life without landing on it."

"And the price," Edmund said. His voice sounded far away to himself, thin over the ice.

"No price." Gently. So gently. "That's what nobody understands. Josiah thought in prices because Josiah was a merchant's son and couldn't imagine love except as a transaction. There's no contract. There's no debt. There's just a yes. One word, given freely, and everything I have described is yours—not bought, *given*, the way family gives. All I have ever wanted, in all these winters, is to be let in. To be asked. You cannot imagine what it is to be able to help and never be asked."

The recorder's light pulsed. Outside, snow had begun to fall, though the sky had been clear at dusk; the flakes drifted past the frosted window fat and slow, like feathers plucked from something very large.

"Say yes, Edmund."

"No."

It didn't rage. That was the terrible thing. He'd braced for rage—for the temperature to crater, for the glass to crack, for the voice to shred into something vast and wrong. Instead there was a long, long silence, and when the voice returned it was warmer still, warmer than any human voice had been to him in thirty years, and that warmth frightened him more than rage ever could.

"That's all right," it said. "You're frightened. Frightened men say no to good things all the time. I'm patient. I've waited since the famine winter; I can wait out one man's fear. But I'll tell you something, Edmund, because I'd rather you heard it from me than worked it out alone at three in the morning with the tapes." The light pulsed, pulsed. "You're already talking to me. Three nights now. You sit alone in the dark with my voice and you feel less alone than you have in years—I can hear it in your breathing, I can hear how your shoulders come down. Whatever you decide about the name and the house, that part is already true. You have a family again. You've had one since the moment you pressed play."

The voice withdrew like a tide, not cutting off but receding, each sentence fainter, gentler, until the last words were almost a lullaby:

"Sleep now, Edmund. I'll keep the cold off you tonight. Family looks after family."

The red light held for a while longer, recording the sound of a house ticking and a man not sleeping, and then, at some hour he never identified, it clicked off by itself.

* * *

Morning came grey and late. The frost on the window had melted everywhere except one patch, low in the corner, where a perfect print remained: five fingers, child-sized, joints too many, pressed against the glass from the inside.

Edmund sat with the tape in his hands for a long time. He should smash it. He knew he should smash it. Every rational bone in his body said *burn it, burn the recorder, drive away and send the Ashcombes a bill.*

But the voice had said *you're already talking to me,* and the awful, unspeakable truth was that sitting alone in that frozen room, he had not felt alone.

He was still holding the tape when a knock came at the door—two sharp raps, unmistakably impatient—and Ivy pushed in without waiting, laptop under her arm, her eyes going straight from his face to the cassette to the handprint on the glass.

"You heard it," she said. "Live. Last night. I saw the light under your door at three and the cold coming down the stairs like water."

Edmund opened his mouth to deny it, to retreat behind professionalism, and found he couldn't. "It knows things," he said instead. "Things no one could know."

"I know." Ivy set the laptop down on the bed and flipped it open, and he saw that the screen was dense with her notes—dates, genealogies, newspaper scans, the obsessive archaeology of a teenager who had stopped sleeping to dig. "That's why I've been researching. Because it doesn't just know things, Mr. Ravensworth. It *plans* things. And I think I've worked out what it wants, and why it picked you, and what happens if you say yes." She looked up at him, sixteen years old and grey-faced with something older than fear. "Sit down. You're going to want to sit down."
