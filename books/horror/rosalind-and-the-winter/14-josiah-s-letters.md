## 14. Josiah's Letters

The cellar smelled of cold stone and old paper, a dry papery rot like the inside of a dead tree. Edmund had brought two lamps, the good torch, and a thermos of coffee gone lukewarm from the drive to town and back. Ivy had brought a magnifying glass from her desk and a notebook with a pencil stub, and she stood at the top of the stairs for a long moment before descending, as if the dark below were water she had to decide whether to enter.

"It's just a cellar," Edmund said. He didn't believe it. He said it anyway, because one of them had to.

"It's the cellar," Ivy corrected, and came down.

The box sat where Marion said it would, on a shelf of brick beside the coal chute, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark. Edmund's fingers left prints in the dust — ordinary five-fingered prints, the right number of joints in every one, and he found himself counting them twice, a habit he was going to have to break or it would break him.

They carried it up to the kitchen, into the light. Marion made tea nobody drank. Toby sat at the far end of the table with a colouring book, and every few minutes his crayon stopped, and his eyes went to the window, and Edmund made himself not look at the window, because the last time he'd looked there had been nothing there, and nothing was somehow worse than something.

The oilcloth crackled open like old skin.

Inside: a bundle of letters tied with ribbon that had been red once and was now the colour of dried blood, a leather deed case swollen with damp, a prayer book with a broken spine, and at the very bottom, wrapped separately in a linen cloth gone the yellow of old teeth, a portrait miniature no bigger than a playing card.

Edmund lifted it into the light.

The man in the miniature had the Ravensworth jaw. Edmund knew it because he'd spent forty years avoiding mirrors and still couldn't escape it — that heavy hinge of bone below the ear, the deep-set eyes that made every expression look like a withheld one. Josiah Ravensworth. Painted in a style that made his skin look like candle wax, his collar white as frost. Around the edge of the miniature, in faded gilt script too fine to read without the magnifier, ran a single line.

Ivy read it first, her lips moving. Then she went pale in a way that Edmund was beginning to understand was not a figure of speech in this house. It was a physiological event. The colour left her face like water down a drain.

"It says," she said, and stopped, and started again. "It says *He bargained for us all.*"

* * *

The letters were the real work. There were eleven of them, written across a single winter more than a hundred and eighty years gone, and Josiah Ravensworth had possessed the handwriting of a man who'd learned his letters from a sermon — cramped, slanted, driven hard into the paper as though the pen were a nail and the page a coffin lid. The ink had faded to the brown of dried mud. The first three were nearly legible. The rest degraded, as though the hand that wrote them had grown less certain of its own existence with each passing week.

Edmund spread them across the kitchen table in chronological order, weighted at the corners with the salt cellar, a spoon, Marion's reading glasses, Toby's crayon box. Ivy took the magnifying glass and the earliest letter and began to read aloud, slowly, translating the archaic spelling as she went.

"*December the third. The snow has come early and the wheat failed and we have eaten the seed corn, which is to eat the year to come. There are nine mouths in this house and by Candlemas there will be nine graves unless God or something nearer provides. I have heard the old stories. I had always taken them for stories.*"

Edmund sat down slowly. "Keep going."

"*I went out on the night of the third, past the boundary stone, and I called into the dark as my grandmother's grandmother taught her children to call in famine years, though she also taught them never to mean it. I meant it.*"

Ivy's voice had gone very quiet. In the corner, Toby's crayon had stopped again. Marion stood at the counter with her back to them, and her shoulders were up around her ears, and she did not turn around.

"*Something answered,*" Ivy read. "*It did not come out of the wood or up from the ground. It was simply nearer, all at once, the way a thought is nearer than a shout. It asked what I would give. I said I would give anything that was mine to give. It said that was well, for it wanted only what was mine — my name, and the name of my blood, and the right to be called by it. It asked to be family. I said yes. God forgive me, I said yes, and the granaries of the next farm over rotted and ours did not, and the wells froze to the east and ours ran sweet, and we ate that winter, every one of us, and I have not slept a whole night since.*"

The kitchen was very cold. Edmund looked at the boiler in the corner, ticking away faithfully, doing its job, and understood with a lurch that the cold was not coming from anywhere. It was simply present, the way a listener is present.

"The fourth letter," he said. His voice came out rough. "Go on."

The fourth letter was worse. The fourth letter was Josiah discovering the terms of what he'd agreed to, the way a man discovers the terms of any contract — too late, and in the dark, and with the signature already drying.

"*I have tried to unsay it,*" Ivy read. "*I have stood at the boundary stone and renounced it till my voice broke. It does not hear renunciation spoken from warmth. It says — and I set down its words exactly, that whoever comes after me may know — it says that a bargain may be refused only by the blood that made it, and only in this manner: that a Ravensworth, of his own free will, knowing the full price, must remain under this roof through one whole night of winter, from dusk to dawn, and refuse every gift that is offered, and accept nothing, and take nothing, and say no to everything it sets before him. Not flee. Not sleep. Not accept. Endure.*"

She stopped.

"And if he doesn't?" Marion asked. She still hadn't turned from the counter. Her voice was thin as paper.

Ivy read the next line, and Edmund watched her read it twice before she could make her mouth work.

"*If the blood flies from the house, the debt does not die. It waits. It is very good at waiting. It will sit at the hearth of my children's children like an uninvited kin, and it will grow stronger with every winter it is fed, and it will need only one of my line to say yes, and it will take his name and his face and his place at the table, and the house will be warm forever, and no one in it will be anyone at all.*"

* * *

Edmund read the rest himself, alone, in the parlour, with the door open and Ivy in the kitchen within shouting distance — an arrangement she had proposed and he had pretended not to need.

The later letters were the diary of a man being slowly unmade by knowledge. Josiah had lived to a great age, the parish records would later confirm — eighty-one years, buried in the churchyard at St. Cuthbert's with a fine headstone — and every winter of those years he had sat up through the long nights with his back to the fire, refusing. Refusing the warm drafts that came from rooms with no fire in them. Refusing the voices of his dead mother asking to be let in. Refusing, in his sixty-third winter, a vision of his drowned son alive and grown and calling him Papa through the kitchen door. Each refusal had cost him something. Each one had held the thing at bay another year.

The final letter was different. The handwriting was enormous, the loops wild, the ink blotted — a dying man's hand, or a desperate one. Edmund had to hold it close to the lamp and decipher it word by word.

"*To the last of my blood, whoever you are, if any of you ever read this:*

*I am dying in the spring, which is a mercy, for it does not come to a man in spring. It waits for the cold. It is patient as frost and it has learned my face and my voice and it sits with me in the evenings now like an old friend, and it is the most terrible thing I know, that part of me is glad of the company.*

*Hear me. It will tell you it is family. It will say it in a voice that makes the word feel like a fire on a cold night. It will offer you everything you ever wept for. It knows what you wept for. It has had a hundred and eighty years to learn this family's griefs, and it keeps them like a ledger, and it will open the ledger to the page with your name on it.*

*It cannot lie about the terms. That is the one mercy and the one trap. It may not take what is not given. It may not touch what is not opened to it. But it will make the giving feel like coming home, and it will make the refusing feel like dying, and you must refuse anyway, all night, until the sun.*

*And if you will not — if you run — then know that you are not escaping. You are delivering. You are the postman of your own destruction, carrying the debt forward to a child not yet born who will one day stand in a cold house and hear his name spoken kindly in the dark.*

*I refused it for sixty years. It was the only thing I ever did well. Do better than I did, or do not do it at all, for a half-refusal is a full consent.*

*J. Ravensworth*

*P.S. — It is lonely. God help me, I believe it is lonely. Pity it if you must. Do not answer it.*"

Edmund sat for a long time with the letter in his hands.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled. And in the quiet he became aware, gradually, the way you become aware of a smell, of the cold in the room — not the ordinary cold of a badly insulated parlour but a cold with attention in it, a cold that was *reading over his shoulder.*

He did not turn around. He put the letters back in their bundle, tied the ribbon, and said, out loud, to the empty air behind him, in the flattest voice he owned:

"I'm not talking to you."

The cold did not leave. But it changed — the way a listener changes when the speaker finally acknowledges the audience. It became, if anything, more satisfied.

* * *

He found Ivy on the back step, wrapped in a duvet, watching the garden go blue with dusk. She didn't ask what he'd read. She'd read it too; he could see it in her face, the particular grimness of a sixteen-year-old who has just learned that the world is exactly as bad as she'd privately suspected.

"So it's you," she said. "It's been waiting for you specifically. A hundred and eighty years, and you walk in with your clipboard."

"Apparently."

"And the terms — you have to stay. One whole night. Refusing everything."

"That's the shape of it."

She pulled the duvet tighter. "My mum's going to tell you to run. She'll be kind about it, but she'll tell you to run, because she can't lose anyone else and she'd rather it was you than us, and she'll hate herself for that too." Ivy said it flatly, without cruelty, the way you report weather. "And you'll have to decide which of us you believe. The letter or my mum."

Edmund looked out at the garden. The frost had begun to form on the grass already, though the temperature was barely below proper freezing, and it was forming in patterns — not the random lace of ordinary ice but ferns, branching ferns, all reaching toward the house like roots growing in the wrong direction.

"Ivy," he said. "The last letter. The postscript."

"The lonely thing. Yeah."

"It's a trick."

"Maybe." She hugged her knees. "Or maybe it's both. Maybe it's lonely *and* it's a trick. Those aren't mutually exclusive. My gran was lonely and she was also the most manipulative person I've ever met."

He almost laughed. It came out as a breath that wanted to be a laugh.

Behind them, in the kitchen, Toby began to speak. Not loudly. Just a child's voice, conversational, answering someone. Marion's voice rose sharp with fear, and then there was the sound of a chair going over, and Ivy was through the door before Edmund had fully stood.

By the time he reached the kitchen, Toby had stopped. Marion held him against her chest, rocking, her eyes over the boy's head fixed on Edmund with an expression he'd spent his whole professional career seeing on the faces of clients and had never once learned to look at without flinching.

"He was saying yes," she whispered. "He kept saying *yes, uncle, yes.*"

The window over the sink had frosted from the inside, in a single perfect handprint, child-sized, five-fingered, pressed flat as if in greeting — or in receipt.

And Edmund understood, with the cold settling into his bones like a tenant taking possession, that the letter had told him everything except the one thing that mattered most. It had given him the rules. It had not given him the strength.

Dusk was coming. The solstice was two nights away. And the thing in the cold had stopped waiting politely, and had started asking the children.

He would have to go first.
