## 7. Photographs Moving

The morning after the chair scraped across the kitchen floor, Sable did not go downstairs until nearly ten.

She told herself it was because she'd slept badly — which was true — and not because some animal part of her had decided that as long as she stayed in her grandmother's old bedroom with its quilted curtains and its smell of cedar and dried lavender, nothing below could require anything of her. She lay listening to the house settle around her, cataloguing every creak the way a soldier catalogues distant gunfire, trying to sort the ordinary from the otherwise. The furnace ticking. A branch against the north gutter. The particular groan of the porch boards contracting as the sun warmed them. All ordinary. All fine.

At 9:47 she heard Gideon's boots on the stairs, his knock at her door, two raps, polite as a meter reader's.

"Miss Joubert. I'm going to walk the fence line before the light goes flat. There's coffee on."

He said it the way he said everything, flat and competent, as though the world were a set of figures that would balance if only you added them correctly. She had decided by now that this was either genuine temperament or armor so old he'd forgotten it was armor, and she found she didn't care which. It was simply restful to be near someone who behaved as though the universe were legible.

She came down eventually. She made herself toast she didn't eat. And then, because grief is a taskmaster even when dread is standing behind it, she went back to the photographs.

Her grandmother's albums filled two shelves of the parlor cabinet: black pages with corner mounts, pictures going back to the forties, older ones loose in a biscuit tin, oldest of all wrapped in wax paper at the bottom like something being kept from spoiling. Sable had been through them twice already since arriving — once in the first raw week, hunting her grandmother's face in every crowd, and once more slowly, after the chair began appearing, with Gideon's flashlight and a magnifying loupe he carried for reading survey markers.

They had catalogued forty-one photographs showing the kitchen table. In every one, going back to a faded sepia print of women in shirtwaists and men with mustaches like paintbrushes, there was the extra chair. Plain wood. Straight-backed. No cushion, no ornament, sitting at the table's long side where no one ever sat, while everyone crowded toward the ends and the window side as though the middle of the bench were cold.

Gideon had pronounced it double exposure. He'd said it kindly, in his way — *the emulsion slips, old plates ghost, I've seen it on boundary stones half my career* — and Sable had wanted to believe him enough to almost do it.

This morning, sorting the tin, she stopped believing him.

* * *

It was the 1962 picture that did it. Her grandmother as a girl, maybe nine years old, gap-toothed, standing beside her own mother at the table's end. Sable had looked at this photograph three times before. She knew it the way you know a song from childhood. The chair had been there, far side of the table, small and patient as furniture.

Now it was nearer.

Not much nearer. A hand's width, maybe less, in the photograph's terms — but she had looked at this image often enough, recently enough, with the desperate attention grief gives to faces, to know that the gap between the chair's front legs and the table edge had been wider. She was sure of it the way you are sure of a moved object in your own bedroom: not with proof, but with the whole body's certainty.

She sat down hard on the parlor floor with the album open across her knees and pulled the other albums into her lap, working backward through time — or forward; she couldn't have said which direction the truth ran. The 1978 harvest dinner: chair closer than she remembered. The 1990s pictures, her mother young and laughing in a doorway that no longer existed: closer still. The last Christmas, four months before her grandmother died, everyone crammed into the kitchen's warmth, and the chair — the chair stood at the near end of the table now, its back almost touching the frame's edge, close enough that if the camera had been a person standing in the doorway, the chair could have been pulled out for them.

Sable laid the photographs on the floorboards in chronological order, a timeline forty feet of pine wide, and knelt over them, and felt the hair rise on her arms.

It wasn't approaching the table. It had never been approaching the table. Tables don't move. It was approaching the camera. Approaching the eye. Approaching whoever stood where the photographer stood, looking in.

Approaching whoever held the photograph.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the empty parlor, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "Okay."

* * *

Gideon came back at noon smelling of dry grass and fencerow dust, and found her still on the floor, cross-legged among the decades, a cup of untouched tea gone cold at her elbow.

He crouched, read the layout without asking, and she watched him do what he always did: reach for the rational rail and grip it. "Prints shift," he said. "Humidity warps the paper. Corner mounts let a print drift inside the frame—"

"The prints aren't mounted. These are loose." She picked up the 1962 and held it out to him. "Look at it. Then look at the Christmas one. Tell me the paper warped forty feet."

He took it. He looked. She watched his jaw do the thing it did when a measurement refused to come out right — a small tightening, a professional swallowing of the objection. He turned the photograph over, checked the back, checked the corners, held it to the window light as though the answer were a watermark.

"When did you last look at this one?" he asked.

"Three days ago. Maybe four."

"And it was—" He stopped himself. She appreciated that. Most people would have finished the sentence with something soothing.

"It was farther away," Sable said. "They're all farther away. Or they were." She gathered the timeline into her lap, all of it, every decade, and held the stack against her chest like a shield that weighed eleven ounces. "It's getting closer, Gideon. Night by night. To the camera. To whoever's holding it."

"That's not how photographs work."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

He sat down on the floor across from her, uninvited, which was unlike him, and for a while neither of them spoke. Dust moved in the window light. Somewhere upstairs a door eased shut on its own weight, gently, the way doors do, or the way they pretend to.

"We should take them out of the house," Gideon said finally.

"They're hers."

"They're paper."

"You didn't hear the chair last night." She hadn't meant to say it. It came out low and fast, an accusation she immediately regretted, because his face did something complicated — not mockery, worse, a kind of careful sympathy that made her feel hysterical instead of believed.

"I heard the house cooling down," he said quietly. "Old houses talk."

"Then why are you sitting on the floor?"

He didn't have an answer for that. She watched him understand that he didn't, and watched him file the understanding somewhere he couldn't quite reach it again.

* * *

That night she took three photographs up to her room. She told herself it was to protect them — from damp, from mice, from whatever else she wasn't naming — and the lie was thin enough to see through, but she climbed the stairs with them anyway, the way you carry a lit candle through a drafty house: carefully, hating yourself a little, unable to stop.

She propped them on the dresser facing the bed. The 1962. The harvest dinner from '78. And the last Christmas, the chair nearest of all, its straight back almost filling the lower right corner, as though it had been photographed from just above the seat itself.

She left the lamp on. She was thirty-one years old and she left the lamp on, and she slept anyway, eventually, the way the body betrays you into sleep no matter what the mind has scheduled.

She woke at 3:12 by the clock radio's green digits, for no reason she could name, and knew before she turned her head.

The room felt occupied. Not haunted — occupied. The difference mattered enormously at three in the morning. Haunted is a story. Occupied is a fact, the way a cold spot on the mattress beside you is a fact.

The photographs were still on the dresser. But the Christmas one had turned, very slightly, on the flat surface — rotated a few degrees, the way a card turns when someone nudges it — so that it faced the bed more directly. Faced *her* more directly. And in the photograph, though she made herself look only once and then look away and then make herself look again, the chair had come forward. Its front legs now broke the plane of the table's shadow. It stood, in the image, in the foreground, close enough to touch the lens. Close enough that she could see the grain of its wood, the wear on the seat's near edge, worn pale in the shape of someone who had sat there a very long time.

Waiting to be sat in. Or waiting for her to sit.

Sable got up, crossed the cold floor, and put the photograph face-down under her grandmother's Bible, which was not a superstition she practiced but seemed, at 3:14 in the morning, like the kind of thing that couldn't hurt.

She did not sleep again.

* * *

In the morning she found Gideon in the kitchen with his transit case open and his laptop out, drafting the appraisal narrative, and she put the three photographs down in front of him between the coffee pot and the sugar bowl.

"Measure it," she said.

He looked up. Whatever he saw in her face — she'd been crying in the night, briefly, furiously, the way you cry alone at 4 a.m., and had not fully repaired it — kept him from arguing. He took out a steel rule, the kind he used on site plans, and measured the distance from the chair's front legs to the table edge in each print, and wrote the numbers on a legal pad in his small engineer's hand.

Then he went quiet for a long time.

"These can't all be the same scale," he said at last. "Different cameras, different distances—"

"So normalize them. You're a surveyor. Triangulate off the window frame, the door casing, something fixed. Do the math." She heard her own voice come out level and cold and was grateful to it. "Tell me the chair isn't moving."

He did the math. It took him twenty minutes and two cups of coffee and she sat across from him watching his pen move, watching the professional machinery grind against the impossible, watching it lose. When he finished he turned the pad around so she could see the column of figures, and the figures descended, step by step, decade by decade, night by night where the dates allowed comparison, toward zero.

"If it keeps this rate," he said, and stopped, and rubbed his mouth.

"How long?"

"Based on the last three nights' change?" He wouldn't look at her. "Days. Less than a week."

* * *

She burned the 1978 harvest dinner that afternoon, in the yard, in the iron wash kettle her grandmother had used for scalding chickens, and she wanted it to feel like a victory and it did not.

She used matches and a page of newspaper and the photograph caught slowly, curling at the edges first, the emulsion blistering silver before the flame took the paper proper. She stood over it with her sleeves pushed up and her heart going hard, daring the house to object.

And the smoke rose, and turned, and came back down.

There was no wind. She checked — looked at the trees, at the tall grass along the fence line, all of it still, the whole afternoon holding its breath — and yet the smoke bent deliberately, unhurriedly, and drifted past her face, and it smelled of Sunday dinner. Roast and onions and gravy thickened in the black skillet, and underneath it the cinnamon of pie crust, and underneath *that*, faint and unmistakable, the lemon polish her grandmother had used on the kitchen table every Saturday night of Sable's childhood, working the cloth in circles, humming.

The smell of being loved. That was what the smoke smelled of. That was what it had chosen to smell of, out of every smell in the world, and Sable stood over the burning photograph with her eyes streaming — from smoke, she told herself, only smoke — and understood that this was not a haunting the way stories had hauntings. Nothing here rattled chains or moaned in attics. This thing had studied her. It knew what she was hungry for. It was not trying to frighten her away.

It was setting the table.

The photograph burned to a lace of ash. She stirred it with a stick until every fragment separated, and carried the kettle to the far corner of the yard, and dumped the ashes into the weeds by the stone wall, and walked back to the house without running, which cost her everything she had.

Inside, Gideon was waiting in the kitchen. He'd seen the smoke from the window — of course he had — and he didn't ask what she'd done, and she loved him a little for that, in a way she filed away to examine later, if there was a later.

"There's something you should see," he said.

He led her to the parlor cabinet, to the biscuit tin, to the wax-paper bundle at the bottom — the oldest photographs, the ones from before living memory. He unwrapped them onto the table with hands that were not quite steady, and Sable looked down at the sepia faces of women in shirtwaists and men with paintbrush mustaches, gathered around the kitchen table in a year no one alive could remember.

And there, at the near end of the table, closest to the camera of any photograph in the house —

— the chair stood empty, drawn out slightly from the table, its pale worn seat catching the light.

And on its back rail, carved small and deep, letters she had never been able to see in any other print because no other print had brought the chair this close:

*S.J.*

Sable Joubert's initials. Or her grandmother's. Or her great-great-great-grandmother's, in a photograph taken before anyone thought to ask.

Behind her, in the kitchen, unhurried, distinct, a wooden chair scraped six inches across the floorboards — toward the doorway. Toward her.

Gideon reached out and took her hand, and neither of them pretended afterward that either of them had done it for any reason but the true one.
