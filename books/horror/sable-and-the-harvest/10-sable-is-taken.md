## 10. Sable Is Taken

The afternoon had gone gold and long, the kind of October light that made even the Joubert farmhouse look gentle — gilding the clapboard, warming the kitchen windows to amber, laying soft stripes across the floorboards where the curtains didn't quite meet. Sable stood at the counter sorting the last of her grandmother's linens, and for a little while — an hour, maybe more — she let herself pretend the house was only a house.

Gideon was out back with his transit and his tape, running lines for the well survey he'd promised himself he'd finish today. She could hear him through the screen door: the metallic click of the tripod leg, the scratch of his pencil on the field book, once or twice the low murmur of him talking to himself, which he did when he thought no one was listening. He'd been doing it all week. *Baseline offset, four feet six. Recalculate.* A man building a small, orderly world out of numbers because the big one had stopped making sense.

She understood that impulse better than she wanted to admit.

The ledger lay open on the kitchen table where she'd left it that morning, its columns of dates and offerings marching down the page in her grandmother's careful hand. *Flour, salt, a lock of hair. Milk poured at the mouth before dawn. Do not speak its name after dark.* And beneath them, in ink gone brown with age, the entry she could not stop reading:

*It will not be me. It will be the one who comes home.*

Sable folded a tablecloth that smelled of cedar and dust and set it in the box with the others. Her hands were steady. That was the strange part. Nine days in this house, nine days of photographs with their patient extra chair, of frost on the inside of windows in warm weather, of Gideon's instruments lying to him in voices of perfect confidence — and her hands had gone calm as a surgeon's. Fear, she was learning, eventually ran out of places to live in the body. What remained was something quieter and worse: attention. The house had been paying attention to her since the day she arrived. Somewhere in the last week, without deciding to, she had begun paying attention back.

Outside, the light began to fail. October dusk came early and came all at once this far north, the gold draining out of the fields like water from a pulled plug, the tree line going black and dense against a sky the color of a bruise healing. Gideon's voice drifted up from the back acreage, calling measurements to himself, and then, gradually, stopped calling anything at all.

That was when she smelled it.

Sunday dinner.

Roast chicken and sage dressing. Onions cooked down sweet in butter. Gravy thickened just past smooth, the way her grandmother made it, the way nobody else in the world had ever made it, with that last splash of pan drippings and a secret spoonful of apple jelly Sable had found out about when she was nine and sworn never to tell. Yeast rolls. The particular scorched-sweet smell of pie heating too close to the oven's top element because the middle rack was always full.

It rolled through the kitchen like weather. It came from nowhere — the stove was cold, the oven empty, the refrigerator humming its dumb electric hum over nothing but condiments and Gideon's beer — and it filled every corner of the room at once, so thick she could taste it at the back of her throat. Her stomach clenched with a hunger that had nothing to do with food and everything to do with grief.

"Grandma?" she said, and hated how young her own voice sounded.

The house answered with silence, but it was not an empty silence. It was the silence of a held breath. Of someone standing just outside a doorway, listening to see if they've been heard.

Sable turned slowly. The kitchen looked exactly as it had a moment ago — same table, same ledger, same box of linens, same gray evening pressing at the windows. Except the photograph.

It lay on the end of the table nearest the hallway, though she had left it, she was almost certain, on the far side, near the window. It was the 1974 picture, the one taken at Easter, everyone squinting into spring sun on the back steps: her grandmother a girl of nineteen with her mother's arm around her shoulders, her grandfather holding a hat he never wore, great-aunts and uncles arranged along the railing. And there at the edge of the frame, half-cropped by whoever held the camera, the wooden chair. Straight-backed. Spindle-turned. Sitting on the grass where no chair should be, patient as furniture, patient as a grave marker.

Except that now it wasn't at the edge.

It sat in the center of the photograph. Centered, composed, deliberate — angled slightly toward the viewer, the way a host angles a chair toward a guest they are inviting to sit. Toward her. However she moved around the table, the chair's facing followed, subtle as a compass needle finding north.

"Sable."

Her grandmother's voice. Not remembered — heard. It came from the hallway, from the dimness beyond the kitchen door, pitched exactly as it had been pitched across sixty years of Sunday dinners: warm, dry, faintly amused, the voice of a woman who had seen everything twice and been surprised by nothing. *Come set the table, honey. Company's coming.*

Every instinct she owned told her to run. Her feet carried her to the hallway instead.

* * *

Gideon felt the change in the air before he saw anything wrong with it.

He was kneeling at the well mouth with the anemometer, logging readings he had already decided were garbage, when the wind died. Not eased — died, all at once, the way a machine dies, mid-motion, the tall grass along the fence line freezing upright. And in the stillness the temperature climbed. Ten degrees in under a minute. Warm air rising off the ground around him in a slow, steady column, carrying smells that had no business existing within five miles of this property: roasting meat, sage, bread, woodsmoke, cinnamon.

He stood. Checked the instrument. Checked it again. The reading held: air moving *upward*, out of a well that had been dry since before the county kept records, exhaling at a rate he could have mistaken — if he were a man who mistook things — for breathing. Slow in-gather. Long release. In-gather. Release.

"Gideon?"

Her voice, from the direction of the house. But wrong somehow — flattened, as if she were speaking through a wall, or speaking while walking away from him down a long corridor. He grabbed the flashlight off the tripod and crossed the yard at a pace he told himself was brisk and professional and knew was not.

The kitchen was empty.

The ledger lay open on the table. The box of linens sat half-packed. The screen door stood ajar, swinging gently in a wind that had not existed thirty seconds ago, and the whole room was warm and redolent of Sunday dinner, and on the table, face-up in the last of the light, lay the old Easter photograph.

He picked it up. The extra chair filled the center of the image now, rendered in sharper focus than any other object in the frame — sharper than the people, sharper than the house behind them, as though the camera had known all along what the picture was actually about. And the chair was not empty. He hadn't been able to see it before; the resolution wasn't there, the grain too coarse. But the grain was fine now. Impossibly fine. And in the chair, rendered with the clarity of something seen from very close, sat a shape. A suggestion of shoulders. The pale oval where a face would be.

It was looking out of the photograph.

At the space where Sable had been standing.

"Sable!" He was moving now, through the hallway, checking rooms he'd measured a dozen times, rooms whose dimensions refused to agree with their exteriors, shouting her name into the parlor, the pantry stairs, the cold little bedroom with the quilt still folded on the bed. The house gave back his voice in echoes that arrived too late and too slow, as though sound itself had to travel farther than the walls allowed.

He came back to the kitchen breathing hard. Set the photograph down. Picked it up again. And in the instant his fingers touched the paper, he heard her — clearly, close, from everywhere and nowhere, the way a voice sounds in a dream:

"—and I'm telling you, we can leave tonight, we can leave right now, just get in the truck—"

Mid-sentence. Cut clean, like a radio station dying between words.

The kitchen went cold. The smell of dinner thinned, frayed, and was gone. Outside, the wind resumed as if it had never stopped, rattling the bare branches along the fence line, and somewhere down the road a dog began to bark, then stopped all at once, mid-note, as if reconsidering.

Gideon stood alone in the kitchen of the Joubert farmhouse, holding a photograph of a family with one chair too many, and the chair was empty now.

Whatever had been sitting in it had gotten up.

He looked at the ledger, open on the table, and read the last line again in the failing light. *It will not be me. It will be the one who comes home.*

Then he looked at the well survey in his field book — the exhalation log, the impossible temperatures, the column of figures he'd labeled *anomalous* in his neatest hand — and understood, with the terrible clarity of a man whose instruments have finally told him the truth, that the appraisal was over. There was nothing left to value here except what the house was already taking.

"Sable," he said quietly, to the empty room, to the listening walls. "I'm coming to get you."

Behind him, unseen, the oven dial turned itself a quarter-inch toward *on*.
