## 2. The Inheritance Clause

The boxes had been sitting in the hallway for eleven days, and August Lockridge had developed a talent for walking past them without seeing them.

It was a skill, really. He'd refined it the way other people refined golf swings or sourdough starters — daily practice, small improvements, a quiet pride in the result. He could come home from the station at five in the morning, coffee-breath and dead-eyed, step over the box marked *WREN — KITCHEN*, hang his coat on the hook that still held her scarf because he hadn't moved it and wasn't going to move it, and sleep until noon with the particular, practiced unconsciousness of a man who has decided that grief is a room in the house he simply doesn't enter.

But today was Saturday, and today there was no show to prep, no engineer to call, no excuse left standing. The lawyer had emailed twice this week, polite as a dentist: *Whenever you're ready, August. No rush.* Which was a lie. There was always a rush. Probate didn't wait, the utility company didn't wait, and the house — Wren's house, now his house, the house they'd grown up in before their mother sold it and their grandmother's things went into storage like a family embarrassed of itself — the house definitely did not wait. Pipes groaned at night. Something in the attic shifted when the wind came off the ridge. And every morning the light through the east windows fell across the hallway floor in long pale bars, and August thought: *she used to lie in that light on Sunday mornings, reading, her feet against the wall,* and then he thought nothing else for a while.

He made coffee first. That was the ritual. Not because he wanted it — he'd stopped tasting coffee sometime around the funeral — but because the act of it took four minutes and those were four minutes in which he could stand at the counter and pretend he was a man doing an ordinary thing on an ordinary morning, instead of a thirty-four-year-old orphan twice over, sorting the earthly remains of the only person who'd ever called him Augie.

The kettle ticked. Rain had come in overnight off the mountains, a soft July rain that should have felt like mercy and just felt gray. He carried the mug down the hall, set it on the floor beside the first box, and sat down cross-legged on the hardwood like a kid about to open Christmas presents at a funeral.

"Okay," he said out loud, to no one, to her. "Okay, Wren. Let's see what you couldn't throw away either."

* * *

The kitchen box was mostly mundane, and the mundanity was the worst part. A garlic press still in its packaging — she'd owned it two years and never once pressed a clove, because Wren cooked like a jazz musician, all improvisation and no equipment. Takeout menus fanned like playing cards. A drawer's worth of pens, most of them dead, hoarded from banks and dentists' offices the way she hoarded everything, convinced that scarcity was always one bad week away. Their mother's illness had taught them both that. You kept the pens. You kept the foil. You kept the little soy sauce packets, because you never knew.

At the bottom of the box, wrapped in a dish towel, was the mug. The ugly one. Hand-painted at some church fair circa 1994, a lopsided sun with a smiley face, AUGIE scrawled across it in puffy fabric paint by a nine-year-old girl with more confidence than brush control. He'd drunk cocoa out of it every winter of his childhood. He'd assumed it had died in some move decades ago.

August held it in both hands for a long time. The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere down the street a dog barked twice and gave up.

"You absolute jerk," he said quietly, which was what he said instead of crying, and put the mug on the counter where he'd see it every morning, and kept going.

* * *

The second box was books, and the third was clothes, and he got through them the way you get through a long drive at night — mechanically, mile after mile, the radio off. Her paperbacks smelled like her apartment had smelled, that faint sweet dust of old pages and the vanilla candle she burned in winter. In the margin of her Rilke, in pencil, tiny: *no.* Just that. *No.* He laughed — an actual laugh, startled out of him, the first one in weeks — and wrote *fair* underneath it in pen, and felt briefly, absurdly, like she was in the room rolling her eyes at him.

That was the trick of it, he was learning. Grief didn't arrive like weather. It arrived like a pickpocket. One minute you were holding a pencil-marked book feeling almost okay, and the next minute your hand was shaking and your chest was full of wet cement and you were sitting on the floor of a dead woman's living room at eleven in the morning with a mug of cold coffee you didn't remember making.

He gave himself ten minutes. He'd gotten good at that too. Ten minutes on the floor, breathing, letting the thing pass through him like a storm front, and then up, and back to work, because the work was the only raft and everybody knew it.

* * *

It was the desk that broke him, and it broke him slowly, which he supposed was a mercy of a kind.

Wren's writing desk had come over from the old house last month, before — God, before, when "before" was still a word he could use without flinching. She'd kept it locked even then; he remembered teasing her about it once, standing in her kitchen with a beer, jiggling the little brass knob. *"State secrets?"* he'd asked. And she'd looked at him — he could see the look now, replaying it with the terrible new clarity grief lent to ordinary memories, the way death re-edited everything into foreshadowing — she'd looked at him with something that wasn't quite a smile and said, *"Just old stuff. Grandma Edith's stuff."*

And he'd let it go. Because that was August. He let things go. It was practically his brand — the easygoing host, the smooth voice on the late shift, the guy who never pushed, never pried, never noticed the frost on the window in July.

The key was in the third box. Taped under the lid, of all ridiculous places, inside an envelope with his name on it in her handwriting — her real handwriting, the slanted, impatient script she'd used for grocery lists and birthday cards, not the careful calligraphy she saved for things that mattered. But it mattered. He knew it mattered the way you know a door is locked: by the sudden, total change in the weight of the air.

*AUGIE.*

One word. His name. His chest did something complicated and painful.

Inside the envelope, folded into quarters, was a single sheet of legal-pad paper, soft at the creases like it had been folded and unfolded many times. Like she'd written it, read it, refolded it, taken it out again, read it again. Like a letter that had been argued with.

He unfolded it.

Three lines, in that same impatient slant:

*If anything happens to me —*
*Don't answer after three.*
*And don't sell the mirror.*

August read it twice. Then a third time, standing very still in the middle of the hallway with the rain coming down and the refrigerator humming its one dumb note in the kitchen.

"Don't answer after three."

He laughed. It came out wrong — too loud, too bright, bouncing off the walls of the empty house like a stranger's laugh. "Don't answer after three," he repeated to the ceiling. "What is this, a fortune cookie? What am I not answering, Wren? The phone? The door? Your ghost's collect calls from the great beyond?"

His voice cracked on *beyond*, and the laugh died in his throat, and for a moment the hallway was so quiet he could hear his own pulse in his ears, slow and heavy, like footsteps on stairs.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing he would spend the next weeks refusing to think about, turning over and burying and digging up again at 3 a.m. when the house creaked: Wren did not joke like this. Wren joked constantly, relentlessly, but she joked *sideways* — puns, impressions, devastating one-liners deployed at dinner parties. She did not write notes. She did not write ominous notes and tape them under box lids addressed to her brother in case of her own death. The last time she'd written him a note like this he'd been twelve and she'd been sixteen and it had said *MOM'S HOME EARLY HIDE THE CIGARETTES*, and it had been right, and hiding had saved them both a world of trouble.

Wren was practical. Wren was the responsible one, the one who'd handled the hospice paperwork at twenty-six while August hid in the hospital chapel pretending to pray. If Wren wrote *don't answer after three*, then somewhere in her careful, exhausted, loving head, there had been a reason.

He sat down on the bottom stair with the note in his hands and let that settle over him like cold water.

After three. After three in the morning. His show ran till four. He answered phones for a living — had done for a decade, the whole graveyard shift, the insomniacs and the lonely and the stoned teenagers calling in to request songs for girls who didn't know they existed. He talked to strangers in the dark every night of his life. It was the family business, if a family could be said to have such a thing: their grandmother Edith had hosted séances in this very house, their mother had claimed — half-jokingly, the way you claim things that are true — that Edith could *hear* people who weren't there. The Lockridges answered. It was what they did. Somebody had to pick up.

*Don't answer after three.*

"And don't sell the mirror." He frowned. "What mirror?"

There was no mirror in the house. He'd been through every room since the funeral — not cleaning, exactly, but moving through it, touching things, keeping the place inhabited the way you keep a heart beating with your fist. No mirror in the hall. Bathroom mirror, cheap, builder-grade. Bedroom mirror, full-length, hers, chipped at one corner. Nothing worth a clause in a death note.

Unless she meant the old one. The one from the photographs. He'd found the album two days ago in the hall closet — the good camera, the seventies prints gone amber at the edges. And there in the middle of the album, in a picture dated November 1971, stood their grandmother Edith on the porch of this house, young and severe in a wool coat, one hand resting on —

On a mirror. An enormous mirror, taller than she was, propped against the railing, wrapped in blankets except for one corner where the glass caught the gray sky. And the glass, even in the photograph, even at that size, even faded forty years — the glass didn't look like glass. It looked like frost. White ferns feathered across it corner to corner, beautiful and wrong, a blizzard sealed behind silver.

Edith was smiling in the photo. August had stared at that smile for a long time the night he found the album, because it wasn't a happy smile. It was the smile of a woman standing next to something she'd won. Or something she owed.

The rain thickened against the windows. August became aware, gradually, that he was cold — actually cold, in July, sitting on the stairs in a t-shirt, and the cold seemed to be coming up through the floorboards, or out of the walls, or out of the photograph still lying open on the hall table behind him. He got up, closed the album, and told himself firmly that old houses had drafts, and that grief made you stupid, and that his sister had been stressed and superstitious and raised on their grandmother's stories, and that people wrote strange things when they were scared, and that none of it meant anything.

He was very good at telling himself things. It was, professionally speaking, his entire skill set. He talked people through their nights; he talked himself through his.

*Don't answer after three.* Fine. Easy. He'd screen the board after three, route everything to voicemail, blame it on new FCC compliance whatever. Nobody would notice. Nobody ever noticed what happened in the last hour of a late-night show anyway; the audience thinned to ghosts by then.

*Don't sell the mirror.* Also fine, because there was no mirror to sell, and if he ever found the damn thing he'd take it straight to the dump, clause or no clause, and let Wren haunt him about it.

See? Handled. Both instructions processed, filed, dismissed. He was fine. Everything was fine. He was a grown man alone in his childhood home on a rainy Saturday, talking to a dead woman's handwriting, and he was *fine*.

Then he opened the envelope again, because there was one more thing in it.

* * *

The necklace lay in the fold of the envelope's bottom seam, coiled like something sleeping.

A thin chain, tarnished silver, and threaded on it — not a locket, not a charm — a key. Old brass, blackened with age, worn smooth at the bow where a thumb had worried it for years. He knew the shape of it instantly, the way you know your own front door in the dark. It was the key to this house. The original. The one that had hung on a nail by the back door his whole childhood, until the locks were changed sometime in the nineties and the key vanished into the drawer of forgotten things.

Except it hadn't vanished. Wren had found it. Wren had strung it on a chain and worn it against her skin — he tried to remember, desperately, whether he'd ever seen it, whether there'd been a glint of brass at her collarbone at Thanksgiving, at the hospital, at the funeral — and he couldn't remember, and that failure sat in him like a stone. How many other things had he not seen? How long had she been carrying the house around her neck, and why, and what had she known that she'd taped under a box lid instead of saying out loud?

She'd had time to say it out loud. They'd had dinners. Phone calls. Three weeks between the diagnosis-that-wasn't and the accident-that-was, three weeks of ordinary life in which she could have said, *Augie, sit down, I need to tell you something weird about Grandma Edith's mirror.* Instead: nothing. Instead a note. Instead a key on a chain, hidden in an envelope, waiting for a day she must have known was coming, even though the coroner called it an accident, even though everyone called it an accident, even though August himself had stood at the podium and called it an accident with a steady voice while inside him something screamed that accidents didn't leave notes.

He picked the necklace up. The chain slid cool across his palm, and the key turned slowly at the end of it, catching the gray window-light, and for one disorienting second the hallway seemed to hold its breath — the rain, the refrigerator, the ticking of the hall clock, all of it pausing, listening, the way a forest goes quiet when something moves through it.

Then the moment passed, or seemed to, and August was standing alone in a drafty house holding his sister's jewelry, and he did the only reasonable thing, the sane thing, the thing any rational adult would do.

He put it around his neck.

The key settled against his sternum, under his shirt, warm from the envelope, from her. Small and hard and present. A weight of maybe half an ounce that felt like fifty pounds.

"There," he said to the empty hallway, and his voice came out rougher than he intended. "Happy? I'm wearing the creepy heirloom. I'm following the creepy instructions. Next you'll want me to stop eating after midnight, and I want you to know right now, Wren, that's a bridge too far."

No answer. Of course no answer. The rain kept on, indifferent, and the clock in the hall ticked its way toward noon, and somewhere below the floorboards a pipe knocked once, softly, like a knuckle on wood.

August stood there a moment longer, one hand flat over the key, breathing.

Then he went to find the rest of the boxes, because there were six more, and because work was the raft, and because if he stopped moving — if he let himself feel the full size of what she'd been carrying, alone, all this time — he was afraid he might never get up off this floor.

In the last box, at the very bottom, beneath winter coats and a shoebox of photographs, he would find her notebook. Locked. But that was later. That was after dark.

For now, there was only the note, and the key, and the rain, and the small cold voice in the back of his skull that sounded, annoyingly, exactly like his sister:

*You're going to answer after three someday, Augie. You won't be able to help yourself. It's what we do.*

He checked his watch. 12:14 p.m.

Plenty of hours yet. Plenty of daylight.

Outside, the rain began, very softly, to freeze.
