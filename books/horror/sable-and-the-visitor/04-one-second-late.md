## 4. One Second Late

The drive home from the station took eleven minutes and felt like swimming through wet paper. August Lockridge kept the windows down even though it was July, because the night air moving across his face was the only thing convincing him he was still a man in a car on Route 9 and not a voice trapped inside a speaker grille somewhere, waiting for someone to answer.

Denny had called him dead. That was the fact of the morning, sitting in the passenger seat like a hitchhiker he couldn't make leave. Denny Okafor, thirty-four, of Cedar Hollow Road, who had died last Tuesday when his truck left the road at Miller's Bend — August had read the obituary aloud on air, his own voice going strange and thin in his headphones, reading the name of a man who was currently talking to him through the station's phone line with all the breathless urgency of someone running out of time. *You owe me forty dollars, Lockridge. You probably don't remember. I do.*

Then the line had gone dead. Then the studio clock had gone dead. Then every light in the building had flickered once, like something enormous turning over in its sleep beneath the floorboards, and August had sat alone in the dark with his hands flat on the console, listening to the hum of dead equipment, until he'd found the courage to flip the breakers back.

Now it was 5:40 in the morning, and the sky over Hollow Creek was doing that thing it did in summer — bleeding pink into gray, the hills soft as charcoal smudges, the cornfields steaming where the night's damp burned off. It was beautiful. It was aggressively, insultingly beautiful, the way mornings are when you haven't slept, the world showing off its freshness to someone still wearing yesterday.

He pulled into the drive of the house on Sable Hill Road. His house now. The house that had been Edith's, then his mother's, then empty for six years, then Wren's for one strange autumn before she moved into town, then his again by way of a lawyer's envelope and a clause nobody had explained properly. The porch needed paint. The gutters sagged like tired eyelids. In the early light it looked less haunted than simply old, which was somehow worse — haunted houses at least admitted what they were.

August sat in the driveway for a full minute with the engine ticking down, and made himself say it out loud, because saying things out loud had been his job for nine years and it usually worked.

"Denny Okafor is dead. I read his obituary. Somebody played a prank."

His voice sounded fine. Reasonable. A little hoarse from four hours of talking, but reasonable. Prank calls were the bread and butter of late-night radio; he'd gotten them all — the guy who claimed to be broadcasting from inside a whale, the woman who insisted her cat dictated stock tips, the teenager who called every Thursday to ask if August believed in Bigfoot and then giggled like a drain. People were bored and cruel and creative at 3 a.m. Someone had found the obituary, done a voice, gotten their laugh.

Except the voice hadn't sounded performed. Except the debt — forty dollars, borrowed outside the VFW hall three years ago for a cab ride home in an ice storm — was real, and private, and August had forgotten it so completely that hearing it spoken back to him had felt like being handed a photograph of himself he didn't remember posing for.

*It only gets one chance to ask you right.* No — that wasn't Denny. That was the other thing Denny had said, near the end, right before the line died. Something about asking right. About chances.

"Exhaustion," August told the windshield, firmly, the way you'd scold a dog. "That's all this is."

He went inside.

* * *

The house held its summer heat like a grudge. He'd left no air conditioning running — the electric bill on this place was a horror story of its own — and the hallway breathed warm, stale air against his face, air that smelled faintly of dust and old roses and, underneath, something mineral and cold, like the inside of a root cellar. He noticed the smell. He decided not to notice it. There was a difference, he was learning, between those two acts, and the gap between them was where a person could live for years if they were careful.

He dropped his keys in the ceramic bowl on the hall table — Wren's bowl, glazed a deep cobalt, chipped at the rim because she'd knocked it off a shelf in 2019 and refused to throw it away. *Everything broken can just be honest about it,* she used to say. Her key necklace was in his pocket. He'd taken it from her jewelry box two days ago, the small brass house key she'd worn on a leather cord since she was twelve, and he'd told himself it was to keep it safe, and he'd known even while telling himself that it was because he wasn't ready to let it be an object instead of a relic.

Upstairs, the bedroom was dim behind drawn curtains, the bed still rumpled from the afternoon nap he'd taken before his shift. He peeled off his shirt, dropped it on the chair that served as his closet, and stood a moment in the middle of the room with his arms hanging, feeling the particular weightlessness of a body that has been awake too long — that giddy, underwater lightness where your feet seem to be suggestions rather than instructions.

Bathroom, he thought. Teeth. Water. Sleep.

The bathroom was small and white-tiled, a room his mother had renovated in the nineties and never updated again: a pedestal sink, a medicine cabinet with a mirrored door, a window above the tub with frosted glass that turned the morning into a pale smear. The overhead bulb buzzed when he flipped the switch, that faint insectile whine of an old filament deciding whether to commit.

He turned on the tap and looked up into the mirror out of pure habit, the way everyone does, checking the face, confirming the self.

The face looked terrible. Gray around the eyes, stubble coming in patchy, hair flattened on one side where he'd leaned against the booth. Forty-one years old and looking fifty-eight, or looking like a man who'd been dead for a week and gotten up anyway because he had a show to do. He almost laughed at that. Almost.

He brushed his teeth. Spat. Ran water over his face, cold as the tap allowed, which wasn't very. And then, gripping the edge of the sink, dripping, he looked up again —

— and blinked.

And his reflection blinked a half-second later.

August went absolutely still.

Not the way a person goes still when they're startled — startled people flinch, breathe, make some small involuntary sound. This was the stillness of prey animals, of men in mine shafts hearing timber groan. Every muscle locked. The cold water ran off his chin and dripped from his nose onto the porcelain, each drop loud as a coin.

He blinked again. Deliberately. Slowly, the way you test a loose tooth.

His reflection blinked with him. Perfectly. Simultaneously. A man and his image, synchronized, ordinary, boring.

"Okay," August whispered. His breath fogged the glass faintly — which was odd, because the bathroom was warm, warmer than the hallway even, the little room holding heat like a cupped hand — but fogging happened, condensation happened, old mirrors did strange things with temperature differentials, there were articles about it, he'd read articles about stranger things at 4 a.m. waiting for callers. "Okay. See? Fine."

He straightened up. Toweled his face. Told himself the story, and the story went like this: he had been awake for twenty-three hours. Before that, four hours of sleep. Before that, a week of bad sleep, grief-sleep, the kind where you surface every ninety minutes with your sister's name in your mouth. Sleep deprivation caused micro-lapses in visual processing. There was a name for it — he'd interviewed a sleep researcher once, a cheerful insomniac named Dr. Patel who talked about how the brain, starved of rest, began to drop frames, edit reality on the fly, fill gaps with assumptions. Your perception of the world was always a second behind anyway, Dr. Patel had said, laughing. Consciousness was a highlight reel, not a live feed. Everyone lived in the past tense; some people just lived further in the past than others.

That was the story. It was a good story. It had a scientist in it. August clutched it to himself like a coat.

But here was the thing about stories, and he knew this better than most men alive: you could tell yourself any story you wanted, but your body kept its own account. And his body was telling him that what he'd seen hadn't been a dropped frame. A dropped frame was absence. What he'd seen was *sequence*. Blink, then blink. Cause, then effect. One thing happening after another thing, in order, with a delay between them — and the delay had a texture, a thickness, like the pause between lightning and thunder that tells you exactly how far away the storm is.

His reflection was one second behind him.

Or — and this thought arrived uninvited, sliding under the door of his mind the way cold air slides under a house — his reflection was exactly on time, and *he* was one second ahead.

He gripped the sink harder. The brass of the faucet was warm under his fingers. Warm. Everything in this house was warm, it was July, the whole world was a kiln, and yet when he exhaled toward the glass it bloomed white.

"Stop it," he said to himself, quietly. "Stop it right now."

In the mirror, his mouth said the words. His mouth. At the same time. Or — he watched for it, tested it, said *stop it stop it stop it*, a stuttering string, trying to catch the lag the way you'd catch a fish rising — and maybe, maybe, on the third repetition, the reflected lips trailed his by a fraction, a hair, a single frame of film. Maybe. Or maybe his eyes were lying, exhausted, dropping frames, filling gaps.

He shut off the light and left the bathroom without looking back, and not looking back cost him more than looking would have.

* * *

He didn't go to bed. That was the strange part, the part he'd examine later and find damning. He meant to. He walked into the bedroom, pulled back the covers, sat on the mattress edge — and then found himself standing in the hallway instead, fully dressed except for the shirt, holding the brass key on its cord, with no memory of deciding anything.

Grief did that, he told himself. Grief and exhaustion together made hours go missing. He'd lost whole evenings after the funeral, whole afternoons, found himself parked outside the cemetery with the radio off and no idea how long he'd been there.

The hallway ran the length of the house, from the stairs to the kitchen, and halfway along it hung the big mirror — the old one, the heavy walnut-framed thing that had come with Edith's furniture, that had hung in this same spot since before August was born, reflecting generations of Lockridges walking this exact corridor. As a boy he'd loved it. It made the hallway feel longer, richer, doubled — a secret corridor living parallel to the real one, where a boy could run his hand along the wall and know that somewhere, in silver, another boy was doing the same.

He passed it now without stopping. Then stopped. Backed up two steps.

The hallway mirror showed him the hallway. Himself, shirtless, hollow-eyed, the key hanging from his fist. Behind him, the stairwell, dim. Ahead of him, the bright rectangle of the kitchen doorway. All correct. All in order.

He raised his right hand.

The reflection raised its right hand.

He lowered it. Lowered. Raised it again, fast, a jerk — and the reflection matched, matched, matched, and he stood there doing this for what felt like a full minute, a man conducting a silent orchestra of one, testing, testing, finding nothing wrong, finding nothing right either, because the truth was he couldn't measure it. Not really. Not with his eyes alone. The lag, if it existed, lived below the threshold of certainty, in that miserable country between *I saw it* and *I can prove it*, which was, he realized with a dull sinking feeling, the same country Denny's phone call lived in. The same country Wren's note lived in. *Don't answer after three.* Written in her slanted hand, tucked in her desk drawer, dated eight days before she died.

Everyone in his life lately was leaving him evidence that couldn't survive daylight.

"You're tired," he said to the mirror. His voice came out cracked. "You're grieving and you're tired and you want the world to be strange because if the world is strange, then maybe strange things can be undone. Maybe death is just a door somebody locked. That's what you want. So your brain is serving it up. Mirrors that lag. Dead men calling. It's wishful thinking, Augie. It's the oldest trick there is."

The reflection said nothing, which was correct, and offered no comfort, which was also correct, and stood there wearing his face with a patience that suddenly seemed less like physics and more like *waiting*.

August turned away and walked to the kitchen, and if he hurried the last few steps, if he moved a little faster than a grown man strictly needed to move past a piece of furniture, well. Nobody saw. Nobody but the glass.

* * *

In the kitchen he made coffee he didn't want and toast he didn't eat and sat at the table with his laptop open to the station's email, scrolling through the overnight messages. Most were the usual: song requests, complaints about volume, one long screed about fluoride. But there, timestamped 3:11 a.m., was a message with no subject line, from an address that was just a string of numbers.

*you shouldnt have read his name out loud*

He stared at it. Read it again. Eleven words, lowercase, no punctuation except the final period-less nothing. He checked the headers — routed through a relay service, origin obscured, the kind of thing anyone could set up in ten minutes, the kind of thing teenagers did for fun.

*you shouldnt have read his name out loud*

Because names mattered? Because speaking a dead man's name on the air was like — what, knocking on a door? Opening one?

August closed the laptop. Sat with his hands wrapped around the cooling mug, watching steam rise off it in a thin ribbon, and let himself think the thought he'd been keeping in the cellar of his mind all morning, the one with the padlock on it:

*If Denny was really calling — if that was really him — then he knew he was dead. And the first thing a dead man does with a phone line isn't complain about forty dollars. The first thing he does is try to tell you something.*

*It only gets one chance to ask you right.*

Ask. Not tell. Ask. Like Denny was rehearsing. Like Denny was working up to a question, and something else — something that owned the line, maybe, something that owned the timing — kept cutting him off before he could get it right.

August looked up from his coffee, and his eye caught the window over the sink, and the window caught the light, and for one disorienting instant he saw the backyard doubled in the glass — the oak tree, the rusted swing set, the tall grass going gold — laid over the reflection of his own kitchen, his own face, so that he appeared to be standing in both places at once, inside and outside, living and —

He got up so fast the chair went over backwards. It crashed against the tile and the sound rang through the house like a shot, and August stood in the middle of his kitchen with his heart slamming, laughing at himself, actually laughing, a ragged awful sound, because he was a forty-one-year-old man frightened of a window, frightened of his own morning, and if Wren could see him she would put her hands on her hips and say *Augie, honestly* in that voice she had, that fond exasperated voice, and the laugh would crack open into the other thing, the crying thing, and he was too tired for the crying thing, he was too tired for everything.

He righted the chair. Washed the mug. Dried his hands on the towel Wren had bought him two Christmases ago — *ON AIR* printed across it in marquee letters, a joke gift, she'd been so pleased with herself — and walked back down the hallway toward the stairs, toward the bedroom, toward sleep, finally, desperately, sleep that would fix everything, sleep that would reset his brain and dry out whatever swamp his perception had become.

He passed the big walnut mirror without looking at it. He was proud of that. He kept his eyes forward, fixed on the banister, on the ordinary sunlit dust motes above the stairs, and he climbed, and he was three steps up when the thought arrived, casual as a caller on the line, smooth as his own voice:

*If the lag is real, it doesn't only happen when you're watching.*

He froze, one foot lifted.

Because that was the flaw in the story, wasn't it — the good story, the story with Dr. Patel in it. Dropped frames happened when you looked. Perception failed at the point of perception. But a mirror didn't need him watching. A mirror worked in the dark, in the empty house, all day, all night, faithfully recording a hallway no one stood in. If the delay was in the glass and not in his eyes, then right now, behind him, downstairs, the mirror was showing an empty hallway that was one second out of step with the real one — and in that one-second gap, in that sliver of stolen time, something could stand in the reflected corridor and not be in the actual one. Something could walk through the house a heartbeat behind him, matching him step for step, forever, and he would never see it, because seeing it required looking, and looking required turning around, and turning around —

August climbed the rest of the stairs without running, which took everything he had.

In the bedroom he shut the curtains, lay down on top of the covers in his jeans, and set no alarm, because his show wasn't until midnight and the day belonged to the dead hours now, the daylight dead hours, and he fell asleep the way falling objects fall — fast, graceless, terminal.

He dreamed of a hallway. In the dream he was walking down it, and the walls were lined with mirrors, dozens of them, and in every mirror his reflection was walking a half-step behind, and in the last mirror — the largest, the one framed in black ferns of frost — his reflection had stopped walking entirely, and was standing still, and smiling, and raising one finger to its lips.

*Shh,* said the reflection, though there was no sound. *She's almost ready to talk.*

August woke at 2:14 in the afternoon with the word *she* lodged in his chest like a splinter, and went downstairs for water, and it was only at the bottom of the stairs, passing the big walnut mirror, that he glanced at it — one glance, involuntary, the way you touch a bruise —

and saw that the hallway it reflected was colder than the hallway he stood in. Colder. Visibly, unmistakably colder, the light in the glass gone thin and wintry, the colors drained toward blue, and high in the upper corner of the mirror, spreading down from the frame like lace, like lungs, like something growing —

frost.

In July.
