## 6. Denny's Debt

Sleep did not come so much as it circled the house, looking for a door left open, and finding none, moved on.

August lay on top of the covers in his clothes, one arm flung over his eyes, listening to the particular silence of a house that had belonged to other people first. It was a furnished silence. A lived-in silence. The radiator ticked the way it had ticked under his grandmother's watch, under his mother's, under the whole long succession of Lockridges who had warmed themselves against these walls, and the sound should have been comforting and was instead the sound of a clock he couldn't read.

The bathroom mirror blinked after him. He knew it had. He'd stood there at four in the morning with the tap running and watched his own face hold its expression a beat too long — a half-second, maybe less, the gap between a lightning strike and its thunder — before the reflection remembered to be him again. He had blamed exhaustion. He had blamed the whiskey. He had blamed, briefly and with genuine irritation, the cheap contractor-grade glass the previous owner had installed in the upstairs bath, as though a mirror could be a knockoff of itself, a counterfeit reflection, a forgery of the only person he had left to be.

He was still blaming when the phone rang.

The landline. Not the cell. The old rotary-adjacent thing on Wren's nightstand, the one he'd been meaning to have disconnected since March, the one that rang with a bell-tone so antiquated it sounded like an alarm from a sunken ship. He had unplugged it twice. Both times, in the morning, the cord was in the wall again, coiled neatly, as if the house had tidied after itself.

August looked at it. The phone rang a third time, a fourth. In the receiver's plastic curve he could see a warped little reflection of the ceiling fan, turning.

He picked it up because not picking it up had become, somewhere between the first ring and the fourth, unthinkable.

"Night Line's off the air," he said. His voice came out gravelled, professional even in ruin. Old habit. "You've got August Lockridge's answering machine, and the machine is tired."

"Mr. Lockridge." The voice was familiar the way a bruise is familiar. Denny. Denny from the night before last, Denny with the debt, Denny whose obituary August had pulled up on the studio monitor with his own shaking hands — *Dennis Kubiak, 54, of Harlow Falls, died Tuesday* — while the man himself breathed static into his ear. "Mr. Lockridge, I have to ask you right this time."

"Denny." August sat up. The room was dark except for the streetlight leaking orange through the blinds, striping the bed like caution tape. "You're going to have to explain to me what game this is, because I've been very patient, and I'm — "

"It only gets one chance to ask you right."

The sentence landed strangely, grammatically crooked, like a bone set wrong. *It.* Not *I.* August felt the word settle into him with a cold weight, a coin dropped down the back of his collar.

"What's *it*, Denny?"

"It don't get many chances. It's been waiting a long time and it don't get many chances and I — " A breath, wet and labored, the breath of a man whose lungs had been busy being dead for six days. "I only got the one line out, Mr. Lockridge. I only got the one call, and I wasted it on the money. God forgive me, I wasted it on the *money*."

"The money you owed me." August was on his feet now, pacing the small orbit of Wren's bedroom, the phone cord stretching and snapping taut like a leash. "Two hundred dollars. You called a dead man's brother at three in the morning to settle a two-hundred-dollar debt, and now you're calling back to — what, apologize for the inconvenience?"

"To tell you where it lives."

August stopped pacing.

The house made a sound. Not a sound, exactly — a *shift*, the way a sleeping animal shifts, the radiator's tick faltering for one beat and resuming. He looked at the bedroom door. It was open onto the hall, and the hall was dark, and at the end of the hall, catching the streetlight's orange in a thin vertical gleam, hung the mirror.

His grandmother's mirror. The tall one with the walnut frame gone soft with a century of hands, the one his mother had never let anyone move, the one that had come with the house the way the house had come with the family, the way the family had come with the name. It had been in the hallway his entire life. He had walked past it ten thousand times. He had never once, until this week, thought of it as a *thing that was there*.

"The hallway mirror," Denny said, before August could speak, before August could even finish the thought, and the coincidence of it went through him like a nail through a board. "It's in the glass. It's been in the glass the whole time. I seen it when I — " The voice caught, and what came through the catch was not a cough but something worse, something like a man trying to remember how swallowing worked. "I seen it after. When I was on the other side of it. It's got my voice in a jar, Mr. Lockridge. It's got my voice and it's saving it, and I don't know what for, and I'm scared, and I been dead six days and I am *scared*."

August's mouth was dry. The streetlight stripe lay across his hand where it gripped the phone, and he watched it, fixed on it, as though the orange band could hold him to the world of ordinary things — bills, weather, the smell of coffee, the commute — where phone calls from the dead had explanations.

"Denny. Listen to me." He made his voice the voice he used on grieving callers, the soft one, the one that had won him the slot and the ratings and the small hollow reputation as the man who talked the night down off its ledges. "Whatever this is — a prank, a sick joke, some guy with a voice modulator — it's cruel, and I'm sorry for whatever's driving you to do it. But if you're in trouble, if you need — "

"I ain't in trouble. I'm in *the glass*."

The line hissed. And under the hiss, faintly, August heard something that made the hair on his forearms rise and stand at attention: a second voice, or the ghost of one, far down in the static, saying something over and over. He leaned toward the phone. He pressed it so hard to his ear the plastic ached.

It sounded like his name. Not *August*. The other one. The one only Wren had used, the childhood one, the one no one living had spoken in eleven years.

"Did you hear that?" he whispered.

"Hear what?" Denny said. And then, urgent, tumbling: "No — listen — I ain't got long, it don't let me hold the line, it's — Mr. Lockridge, the mirror in your hallway, the tall one, the walnut one. Don't look at it too long. Don't let it catch you counting. It likes to be counted. It's — it's almost done counting something, and when it's done — "

The line clicked. Not the click of a hung-up phone. The click of a connection being *taken*, the way a hand closes over a lit match.

The dial tone, when it came, was warm. August stood in the dark holding the receiver and could have sworn — could have sworn on his sister's grave, on his own — that the little electronic hum of it had a temperature, a faint animal warmth, like breath through a keyhole.

* * *

He did not sleep. He did not try.

He made coffee he did not drink and sat at the kitchen table with the phone's cord stretched to its limit and then, when the sky began to consider the question of dawn, he walked to the end of the hall and stood in front of the mirror.

The hallway was narrow, and the mirror made it narrower, a corridor of doubled wallpaper and doubled doorframes receding into a house that was almost his but not quite — the same, and one step behind, the way a shadow is the same and one step behind. He looked at himself. Gray-faced, unshaved, eyes rimmed red. The reflection looked at him. It wore his exhaustion faithfully, his stubble, the coffee stain on his cuff from the night he'd knocked the mug during the first call.

He raised his right hand.

The reflection raised its right hand.

He lowered it. It lowered. He did it again, faster, a child's test, a skeptic's test, and the glass kept pace, and he laughed — one short, ugly bark of a laugh that the hallway returned to him a half-beat late, or he imagined it did, he *imagined* it did — and then he stood very still and watched the stillness.

That was the mistake. Denny had said *don't let it catch you counting*, and August had thought he understood, and he had not understood at all, because it was not the mirror that counted. It was him. The moment he went still and looked, he was counting — the heartbeats, the seconds, the tiny lag he could no longer prove and could no longer dismiss, the way his reflection's eyes seemed to arrive at their focus a fraction after his own, the way the doubled hallway behind his doubled self seemed to hold a darkness at its end that the real hallway, he turned to check, did not.

There was nothing at the end of the real hallway. Just the bathroom door, shut. Just the morning, gray and ordinary, leaking under the window at the stairwell.

In the mirror, the hallway went back further.

He told himself it was angle. He told himself it was the glass, old glass, wavy glass, glass that had been poured by hand in some factory before the war and never sat perfectly flat, and wavy glass bends light, and bent light makes distance lie. All true. All reasonable. All the kind of thing he would have said on air to a caller, in the warm professional voice, with a smile you could hear.

He was still telling himself when he saw the frost.

It was July. It was ninety-one degrees by the thermometer on the porch, the kind of July that made the tar soften on the roofs and the radio station's ancient air conditioner weep. But along the bottom edge of the hallway mirror, in the walnut frame's shadow, a lace of white was creeping — fine as breath on a winter window, fern-patterned, delicate, *deliberate*, each frond branching with the mathematical patience of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

August crouched. He touched it. His fingertip came away wet and cold, and the cold stayed in the skin of the finger like the memory of a handshake.

The frost was on the *inside* of the glass.

* * *

He drove to the station with the windows down and the July heat pouring over him like a mercy, like absolution, because heat was the one thing the house had stopped having.

The streets of Harlow Falls at six in the morning were empty in the way small towns are empty — not deserted but *unattended*, the whole town off its leash and running loose somewhere out of sight. He passed the diner with its neon CLOSED sign buzzing, passed the church where Wren had been memorialized, passed the cemetery on the hill where he had not been able to make himself stop the car, and all the while the radio in the truck played the station's automated morning feed, his own pre-recorded sign-off from the night before looping in the rotation: *—and that's the Night Line, friends. Whatever's keeping you up, you don't keep it alone—*

He had said that sentence ten thousand times. He had never heard it before.

The station sat on the edge of town like a stubborn tooth — a cinderblock box with a transmitter tower behind it, red lights blinking at its crown against the paling sky. August parked crooked, let himself in with his key, and stood in the lobby breathing the familiar smell of burnt coffee and carpet glue and warm electronics, the smell of the only church he'd ever kept.

The night engineer, Sal, was just packing up, headphones around his neck, a man built entirely of rectangles and resignation.

"You look like hell, Lockridge."

"Feel like it. Sal — question. The automation feed. Who loads the overnight rotation?"

"Whoever's lazy. Usually me. Why?"

"The sign-off. Mine. It played twice on my drive over. Same hour mark, twice."

Sal frowned, a slow geological event. "Can't have. It's slotted once. Unless — " He shrugged. "Ghost in the logger. It happens. Old system." He said it easily, the way people say *old system*, meaning *not my problem, not a real thing, a word for the unexplained that lets us all keep walking*. August envied him the word the way a drowning man envies a swimmer.

After Sal left, August locked the lobby door behind him — a thing he had never done, the station being the one place he had never once felt the need to lock — and went into Studio B and sat down at the board and put on the headphones and let the silence of the dead hours pour into his ears.

He should go home. He should sleep. He should call a glazier, a priest, a psychiatrist, in whatever order his insurance would cover.

Instead he pulled the log book toward him and uncapped a pen, and in the column where callers were recorded — name, topic, time — he wrote, in his broadcast hand, the hand that didn't shake on air:

*Kubiak, D. — 2nd contact. Unfinished business: debt, $200. Location named: the hallway mirror. Line terminated externally.*

He looked at the word *externally* and understood that he had just, in ink, in a station log, admitted to himself what he was doing. He was not documenting a prank. He was *investigating*. He had driven through the gray morning to the one room in the world with a red ON AIR light, because the light was a wall, because behind glass — behind *any* glass — was the one place he still knew how to stand.

The dead had a radio host now. The thought should have been funny. It was not funny. It was the most serious thought of his life.

He flipped the board's monitors on, all of them, the phone hybrids, the remote lines, the unused channels, so that every input in the studio was live and listening, and then he sat back in the chair and looked at the ON AIR light, dark, waiting, and said out loud to the empty room, to the tower, to the town asleep under its July heat:

"Okay, Denny. You've got a station. Talk to me."

The phone hybrids were silent. The meters were flat. The morning came up slow and gold outside the studio's one window, and the transmitter tower's red lights blinked off one by one as the sun took the sky, and everything was ordinary, everything was fine, everything was —

The log book's page turned. By itself. One page, a small dry whisper of paper, and August watched it happen from three feet away, watched the breeze that wasn't there lift the corner and lay it flat on the next leaf, and on the next leaf, in the caller column, under his own handwriting, in a hand that was not his — a rounder hand, a leftward-slanting hand, a hand he had last seen filling the margins of library books and grocery lists and birthday cards signed *love, W* — three words had been written in pencil:

*Check my desk.*
