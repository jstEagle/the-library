## 7. Wren's Notebook

The desk had been Wren's since she was nine years old.

August knew this the way he knew the shape of his own hands — not from memory so much as from a lifetime of the desk simply being there, squat and dark in the corner of whatever room she'd claimed, its brass lock dented where their father had once lost his temper with a coat hanger and a locked drawer full of confiscated cigarettes. It had followed her through three apartments like a loyal dog. Now it sat in her bedroom here at the house, under the window with the warped blind, wearing a fine gray skin of dust that August had been careful not to disturb for eleven days.

He'd cleaned every other surface in that room. He'd washed her sheets, folded her sweaters, alphabetized her paperbacks even though he knew she'd have hated it — she kept books the way rivers kept stones, wherever they happened to settle. But he hadn't touched the desk. The desk was still hers in a way the rest of it wasn't. Opening it felt like going through her pockets while she stood there watching.

Tonight he opened it anyway.

It was nearly two in the afternoon, the hour when the house went amber and quiet, when the heat outside pressed against the windows like a palm laid flat against glass. He'd slept four hours after his shift — dreamless, thankfully, though he woke with the taste of copper in his mouth and the phrase *one second late* already forming before his eyes were open. The bathroom mirror had behaved itself all morning. He'd checked three times, standing there in his underwear making small movements — a raised hand, a turned head — and watching his reflection match him exactly, promptly, obediently. Exactly as a reflection should.

*Exhaustion,* he told himself again, and the word was starting to wear thin at the edges, like a coin rubbed too many times between thumb and forefinger.

The key to the desk was not on top of it. Of course it wasn't. Wren had never made anything easy; she'd once hidden the Christmas presents from herself because, she said, she didn't trust her own restraint. August knelt in front of the desk and ran his fingers along the underside of the drawers, feeling for tape, for magnets, for the little adhesive hooks she favored. Nothing. He tried the obvious birthdays, the obvious anniversaries. Their mother's birthday. His birthday. 1-2-3-4, which would have been an insult to them both.

Then he remembered the necklace.

He went downstairs to the kitchen, to the ceramic bowl by the door where he'd emptied his own pockets a hundred times, and dug past the loose change and the dead batteries until his fingers found the chain. Wren's house key, worn smooth, threaded on a length of fine silver chain she'd worn for the last two years of her life and never once taken off — not in the shower, not sleeping, not in the hospital during those last strange weeks when nobody could tell him what was wrong with her. He'd found it in her jacket pocket at the funeral home, coiled like something asleep, and he'd carried it around ever since without knowing why. It was too small to be a keepsake and too heavy to be nothing.

The key fit the desk's lock with a click so soft it sounded almost like recognition.

*Don't answer after three.*

Her note was still folded in his wallet. He took it out now and set it on the desk beside the lamp, smoothing it flat with one finger, the way you'd calm a nervous animal. Then he opened the top drawer.

Inside: pens, most of them dead. A hair tie. A receipt from the gas station on Delmore dated the day before she died. And beneath these ordinary things, filling the bottom of the drawer like sediment, paper. Dozens of pages. Notebooks, printouts, index cards in her slanted handwriting, photocopies yellowed with age. She'd been researching something, and she'd been researching it hard, and she'd done it all behind a lock.

August pulled out the oldest-looking item first: a composition notebook, cover soft as cloth from handling, the spine cracked into white seams. On the inside cover, in pencil, in handwriting he recognized from grocery lists and birthday cards but which looked older here, more deliberate:

*Property of E. Lockridge. If found, burn it.*

Their grandmother Edith had died when August was six. His memories of her were a handful of snapshots: a smell of camphor and coffee, hands like tree bark, a laugh that came out sideways. She'd lived in this house until the end, in the room that was now his studio, and she'd spent her widowhood, according to family legend, being "eccentric." That was the family word. *Eccentric.* It covered a multitude.

Edith's notebook did not read like eccentricity. It read like minutes.

There were dates running down the margins — January through March, 1971 — and beneath each date, entries in a hand that grew shakier as the pages turned. August read slowly at first, then faster, then not breathing much at all.

*Jan 9. Called the circle together. Seven of us. Told them what the medium in Corvallis said about the visitor. Marta wept. Ruthie said we were fools. Ruthie came anyway.*

Ruthie. He knew a Ruthie. Ruthie Calloway, two houses down, ninety if she was a day, who still brought over rhubarb pie every June and called him "that Lockridge boy" in a voice that suggested she remembered him at six years old and saw no reason to update the file.

*Jan 16. Mother worse. The doctors use the word hospice now and think we don't hear it. We hear it.*

*Jan 23. The circle sat for four hours. Nothing. Marta says we are doing it wrong, that you cannot call a thing, you can only leave a door open and be polite. I hate the way she says polite.*

*Feb 2. Snowed four feet. The roads are gone. Mother asked me today if I was frightened of her dying and I lied to her face and she knew it and held my hand anyway.*

And then, on a page whose date had been scratched out and rewritten twice:

*Feb 11. Something answered.*

August became aware, distantly, that the house had gone very quiet around him — quieter than a house should be at two in the afternoon with the refrigerator humming and the neighbor's dog losing its mind at squirrels. He couldn't hear the dog anymore. He couldn't hear anything except the paper under his fingers, dry as leaves.

He kept reading.

*It does not want blood. It does not want souls, whatever Marta's books say. It wants voices. It is lonely, or it is hungry, and I cannot tell the difference and I no longer believe there is one. It will heal her. It showed me — showed all seven of us — that it can. But it wants a pattern. One voice for each generation, it said, given freely, witnessed truly, named aloud. Seven families, seven winters, seven voices. It drew the shape in the frost on the window and none of us could look away until it finished.*

*Marta wants to refuse. Ruthie says refusing a thing like that is worse than dealing. I keep thinking about Mother's hands. How cold they've been. How warm they used to be.*

*We said yes. God forgive us, we said yes.*

The page after that was torn out. Not removed cleanly — torn, hurriedly, leaving a ragged stub, and pressed deep into the gutter of the binding as if whoever tore it had wanted it gone in a way that went beyond secrecy. August tilted the notebook toward the window light and caught, ghosted into the next page by the pressure of a heavy pen, the mirror-image shadow of words he couldn't reconstruct. Only one fragment survived legibly, pressed backward into the paper like a fossil:

*—not supposed to be a child—*

He sat back on the floor of his sister's bedroom with his grandmother's confession in his lap and listened to the silence of the house, and the silence seemed, for the first time, less like absence and more like attention.

* * *

He worked through the rest of the drawer methodically, because method was the only thing standing between him and the cold sweat gathering at the base of his skull. There were newspaper clippings, photocopied from microfilm by the look of the gray streaks: obituaries from February and March of 1971. Seven of them. He counted twice. An elderly man on Fenwick Road, found in his garden. A schoolteacher. A boy of nineteen, drowned in a creek that had frozen solid enough to walk on and then, apparently, hadn't been. A woman whose cause of death the coroner had listed, in language August had never seen in an obituary, as *"prolonged exposure, circumstances unclear."*

Seven deaths in five weeks, in a town small enough that five weeks usually passed without one.

Wren had circled each name in red ink. Above the seventh clipping — a man named Aldous Prewitt, whose photograph showed a gaunt face and eyes that looked directly into the camera with unsettling steadiness — she'd written a single word: *last?*

Beneath the clippings lay the photograph.

It was a real print, developed on old paper gone sepia at the edges, and August knew immediately that it was the reason for everything else in the drawer — the center of gravity around which all her research had organized itself. It showed this house, front view, in winter. Snow to the windowsills. The porch looked different — railings he didn't recognize, a door that had since been replaced — but the bones of it were unmistakable, the same stubborn gable, the same narrow windows squinting against the light.

Six people stood on the porch steps, bundled in coats and hats, their breath hanging in the air like exhaled secrets. Four men, two women. They were carrying something between them on a litter of quilted blankets — carrying it carefully, the way you carry something sleeping, or something that might wake badly.

They were carrying a mirror.

Even in the faded photograph, even at that size, the glass caught the light strangely. Every other reflective surface in the image — a window, a hubcap visible at the frame's edge, the ice-glazed walkway — threw back the flat white glare of a winter noon. The mirror alone held the light and gave back something dimmer, grayer, as though the sun had to negotiate with it. As though the photograph itself had caught the glass mid-blink.

On the back, in Edith's hand: *Feb. 12, 1971. It asked to come inside. We said yes.*

August turned the photograph over three times, as if the sentence might rearrange itself into something survivable. It asked to come inside. Not *we brought it home.* Not *we bought it at an estate sale.* It asked. We said yes. The grammar of invitation, preserved in ballpoint across fifty years.

His mother had recovered that spring. He'd grown up on the story — told at holidays, softened with each retelling — the miracle remission, the doctors baffled, the church friends who said someone upstairs was looking out for the Lockridges. Nobody had ever mentioned a séance circle. Nobody had ever mentioned seven funerals. Nobody had ever mentioned that the miracle had a price tag, or that the price had terms, or that terms, unlike miracles, do not expire.

*One voice for each generation.*

He thought of Denny on the radio, three nights ago, asking about a debt. *I just need to know it's settled. Can you check? Can somebody check?*

He thought of frost ferns in July.

He thought of Wren, twenty-nine years old, healthy as a summer morning, and the word *accident* that everyone had accepted because accepting it was easier than the alternative, and he thought — with a sensation like a floor joist giving way somewhere inside him — about the torn-out page. *Not supposed to be a child.* Whose handwriting had been meant for that line? What had Edith torn out and burned, or tried to burn, or failed to finish burning?

The desk had more to give. Beneath the photograph, a spiral notebook in Wren's own writing — recent, the ink bright, the pages crisp. Dates from this spring. She'd tracked something. Phone records, maybe, or utility bills, or — he squinted — weather data. Temperatures for specific nights going back months, annotated in her tight hand: *cold spot, hallway, 11:40 p.m., lasted 4 min.* *Mirror fogged, no steam source.* *Checked with thermal camera. See photo 14.*

Photo 14 was clipped to the back cover. A thermal image of the upstairs hallway, rendered in false color, blue and green and one long smear of violet leading from the bathroom door to the hall mirror. The mirror itself registered as a shape of deep, impossible indigo — colder than the walls, colder than the window glass, colder than anything else in the frame by twenty degrees.

Written across it, underlined twice: *it's not haunted. it's occupied.*

The last entry in the notebook was dated nine days before she died. August read it standing up, because sitting down had stopped feeling safe.

*Found Edith's other notebook — the one she hid in the attic joists. The bargain has a schedule. Six voices taken, one per generation, always in the anniversary week. Always at night. Always by phone or by glass. The seventh slot is open. The Guest has waited fifty years for it. I know who it's penciled in for. I need to think. I need to decide whether Augie can live with knowing, because I'm not sure I can live with it either.*

*Whatever happens, don't let him find out from the house.*

August stood in his sister's bedroom holding her handwriting, and outside the July sun poured down indifferent and golden, and somewhere far off the neighbor's dog had started up again, barking at nothing, at squirrels, at the ordinary invisible world.

*Penciled in for.*

Not *you*, the sentence said. Not *me*. *Him.* She had known. She had known what was coming for whom, and she had spent her last nine days deciding how to carry it alone, and then she had died in the anniversary week, in the taking season, and everyone had called it an accident, and August had called it an accident, because the alternative required believing in a world where his grandmother's desperation had armed something patient enough to wait half a century for dessert.

The lamp beside the desk flickered. Once, twice. The afternoon light through the blinds didn't change; only the lamp, as if the electricity in that one bulb had briefly remembered a different circuit.

August looked up at the bedroom mirror — Wren's mirror, a small oval thing on the back of the door — and made himself watch it. Watched his own face watch him back. He raised his left hand.

The reflection raised its right, promptly, perfectly, a half-second before he'd decided to raise his at all.

He stood very still for a long time. Then he gathered the notebook, the photograph, Edith's composition book, and the seven obituaries into a folder, tucked the folder under his arm, and pocketed Wren's key on its chain — not as a keepsake anymore, but as equipment.

He had a show tonight. And for the first time in fifteen years behind a microphone, August Lockridge knew exactly what he was afraid the callers would say.

At the top of the stairs he stopped. From the hallway below, faint and unmistakable, came the sound of the telephone ringing in the kitchen — a phone he'd disconnected weeks ago, a phone with no line, ringing the old-fashioned double-chime ring that hadn't been installed in this house since the seventies.

He counted the rings. It stopped at three.

*Don't answer after three,* Wren had written.

August stood on the landing with his sister's research under his arm and understood, finally and completely, that the past hadn't just left teeth in this house.

It had left something that was still using them.
