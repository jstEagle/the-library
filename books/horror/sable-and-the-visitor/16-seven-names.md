## 16. Seven Names

The county archive smelled of dust and cold paper and the particular loneliness of buildings that only opened on Tuesdays. August stood at the microfilm reader with his sleeves rolled despite the air conditioning, because the machine radiated heat like a small animal, and because he needed to feel something warm that wasn't coming from a mirror.

Ruthie's words had followed him home from her kitchen the night before. *Seven families lost someone that week.* She'd said it the way old people say terrible things — plainly, without decoration, as if grief were a weather report from fifty years ago. *Your grandma bought spring with other people's winters.*

He had not slept. Sleep was a country whose border he could no longer cross; every time he closed his eyes he saw frost ferns blooming in the dark behind his eyelids, patient and white and organized. So instead he had driven to the county seat at six in the morning and waited outside the records office until a woman named Dolores unlocked the door and asked him if he was all right, and he had said yes, which was becoming his most fluent lie.

The newspapers were on microfilm now, everything from 1971 reduced to gray ghosts scrolling past on a glowing screen. He started with the third week of January.

* * *

The first obituary took eleven minutes to find.

**HOLLIS, Gerald T., 54, of Millbrook Road. Beloved husband of Carol. Died suddenly at his home. Services Friday at First Methodist.**

Sudden. The word appeared in all of them, he would learn. Sudden, unexpected, taken too soon. Seven times over one winter week, seven people in a town of four thousand, and not one of them sick, not one of them old enough for it to make sense, and apparently no one had ever thought to add up the column.

August found the second by accident, tucked into the classifieds where the family couldn't afford a full notice: **PETERSON, infant daughter of Dale and Marie.** A baby. He sat back from the screen and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw orange light.

"Printer's two bits a page," Dolores called from the front desk, not unkindly. "Coffee's free."

He printed them anyway. He needed them in his hands, physical, weighty, so they couldn't scroll away like things on screens did, like voices on phone lines did, like sisters did.

By noon he had seven names, and he wrote them on the back of a gas station receipt in ballpoint, in letters that got smaller as the list went down, as if making them smaller might make them weigh less:

1. Gerald Hollis, 54
2. Peterson infant (no first name given)
3. Ada Kowalczyk, 71
4. Raymond Fessler, 33
5. June Marsh, 19
6. Thomas Ellery, 47
7. ————

The seventh entry wasn't an entry. It was a space. Because Ruthie had said seven families, but the papers only held six deaths that week, and August had gone through every page of every edition from December through March twice, and there was no seventh.

Which meant either Ruthie's memory had rounded up.

Or the seventh death hadn't happened yet.

* * *

He drove back through the July heat with the windows down and the receipt taped to his steering wheel, glancing at it at stop signs like a man checking directions to somewhere he already dreaded arriving. The town slid past him in its summer costume — sprinklers ticking across lawns, kids selling lemonade at the corner of Vine, the hardware store's thermometer reading ninety-four — and he found himself looking at each passerby with a new and horrible arithmetic running underneath: *which generation are you? Are you owed? Is someone in your house already listening?*

Because that was the pattern, once you saw it. That was the thing about patterns; they were patient teachers.

Edith's circle had summoned the Quiet Guest in the winter of 1971 to save a dying woman. The price: a pattern of voices, one per generation. And the deaths hadn't stopped in 1971 — they had simply learned to wait. Six names spread across fifty years like seeds planted in different soils. He'd spent the afternoon at the library pulling death notices from every decade, working backward from a hunch that had arrived fully formed sometime around dawn, and the hunch had been right, and being right had never felt so much like falling.

Gerald Hollis, 1971. The down payment.

Ada Kowalczyk, 1983 — twelve years later, a new generation grown. Her grandson had been in August's graduating class.

Raymond Fessler, 1994. June Marsh, 2005 — nineteen years old, Wren's age almost exactly, and August had to pull the car over when he made that connection, sitting on the shoulder of Route 9 with his hazards blinking, breathing through it. *One per generation.* The Guest didn't take the old and the finished. It took people mid-sentence. It took the ones whose voices still had things left to say.

Thomas Ellery, 2016. Nine years ago. Which meant the account had come due again, right on schedule, right around —

Right around the week Wren died.

He gripped the wheel. The heat came through the open window and lay against his face like a hand, and he let it, because it was honest heat, heat with no delay in it, no lag between cause and effect.

Wren. Wren was supposed to be the voice. He'd known it since the tapes, since Edith's circle begged their bargain into the attic air. But knowing it as a story and seeing it as arithmetic were different animals entirely. On paper, in dates, in columns — it stopped being tragedy and became bookkeeping. Someone had kept these books for fifty years. Someone had been keeping count while the town slept, while his mother recovered impossibly, while he grew up in a house with mirrors in every hallway, and no one had ever told him he was living inside a ledger.

* * *

Mara was waiting in his kitchen when he got home.

She had let herself in with the spare key he'd grudgingly given her after the night she'd spent monitoring his hallway with instruments that cost more than his car. She sat at the table with her laptop open and three legal pads fanned out in front of her, and she looked up at him with an expression he was beginning to recognize as her version of fear — which is to say, she looked slightly more awake than usual.

"You look like hell," she said.

"I've been doing math." He dropped the receipt on the table among her papers. "Six names. Six deaths, spread across five decades, roughly twelve years apart. One per generation, just like the tapes said. Except the last interval broke."

Mara pulled the receipt toward her and read it the way she read everything, fast and suspicious, as though the paper might be trying to hoax her. "Twelve, eleven, eleven, eleven. Then nine." She looked up. "It's accelerating."

"It's finishing." He sat down across from her. The kitchen window was above the sink, and he had covered it with cardboard three days ago, and the cardboard was already frosting at the edges no matter what temperature the room was. He didn't look at it. Looking at it felt like feeding it. "Six ferns on the mirror, Mara. Six blackened. One pale and waiting. I counted them this morning before I drove out. They're done growing except one."

"And the seventh name."

"There isn't one. Not in the archives. Six deaths, seven slots." He turned the receipt over, though there was nothing on the other side, nothing but blank paper, and somehow that was worse. "Ruthie said seven families. Either she miscounted after fifty years—"

"She didn't." Mara said it flatly, without hesitation, and he hated how much he trusted her certainty. "I interviewed her yesterday while you were sleeping, or whatever you do instead of sleeping. Her memory is a steel trap. Seven families. She helped cook for two of the funerals. She remembers the casseroles, August. You don't misremember casseroles."

"So the seventh slot is empty."

"The seventh slot is *open*." Mara flipped one of her legal pads around to face him. Her handwriting was small and ruthless, columns of dates and instrument readings, and at the bottom of the page, circled twice, a single line: **Pattern requires completion. Incomplete debts don't expire. They compound.**

"I've been thinking about what kind of entity runs on a schedule," she said. "Fifty years, six collections. It's not random predation — it's contractual. It takes exactly what it's owed, when it's owed, and not before. Which means the seventh voice isn't a question of *whether*. It's a question of *who*, and *when*, and—" She stopped. He watched her arrive at the same place he'd been sitting in all day, watched her get there faster than he had, because she was smarter than him and always had been, even when she was debunking him on live radio with that smile that made him want to throw his coffee.

"August," she said carefully. "Who inherited the house?"

The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere upstairs, in the hallway he refused to walk through anymore, the mirror hung on its nail with its ring of ferns, six black as burnt matches, one pale as breath on glass.

"Wren died in February," he said. His voice sounded far away to him, like it was coming through a bad connection, like it was coming through a phone line. "Nine months ago. The interval should have come due in—" He did the math again, the same math he'd done forty times since dawn, hoping each time it would come out differently. It never did. "February. Right when she went off the road."

"But you said the tapes — Wren was supposed to be the voice. Taken, not—"

"Maybe she was supposed to be." He heard his own heartbeat. "Or maybe the Guest doesn't care how it collects. Maybe an accidental death counts. Maybe it counted her, and the slot filled, and the pattern completed in February, and everything since then — Denny calling, the widow, the frost, the delay getting longer — maybe none of that is a debt being collected."

Mara went very still. "Then what is it?"

"Interest," August said. "On a debt that's already paid."

But even as he said it, he knew it was wrong. He knew it the way you know a note is flat, some frequency below hearing telling him the truth before his mind caught up. Because the pale fern was waiting. Waiting meant unfilled. Waiting meant *owed*. And there was one name that kept surfacing in his head all day, all week, all month, rising like a body in water no matter how many times he pushed it down, and it wasn't Wren's.

He picked up the pen. His hand was steady, which surprised him; he'd have expected shaking, expected drama, but instead there was just this terrible administrative calm, the calm of a man filling out a form he'd known was coming.

He wrote his own name in the seventh space.

* * *

They sat with it for a long time. The light moved across the kitchen floor, gold going to amber going to nothing, and neither of them turned on a lamp, because lamps threw reflections, and reflections had become a language he was afraid of understanding.

"One per generation," Mara finally said, quietly. "Your grandmother's generation paid. Your mother's generation — the bargain was *for* her, so maybe she was exempt, or maybe the Guest considered her covered. That leaves yours." She looked at him across the table, and for once there was no skepticism in her face, no professional armor, just a woman watching a man read his own obituary in pencil. "You're the generation, August. You were always the generation. Wren dying in February didn't fill the slot — it *cleared* it. Took the protector out of the way."

"Or she took my place and bought me nine months."

"Either way." Mara closed the legal pad. "The math ends at the same place. There's one fern left, and it has your name penciled beside it, and sunrise is—" She checked her watch, then stopped herself, and he saw her decide not to say the number out loud, and he loved her a little for that, and hated her a little too, because he needed the number. Numbers were the only thing holding him together. Numbers didn't lag. Numbers didn't blink a second late.

"How long?" he asked.

She exhaled through her nose. "If the intervals keep compressing — twelve, eleven, eleven, eleven, nine — the curve says the next collection isn't twelve years out. It isn't twelve months." She met his eyes. "It's days. Maybe hours. The frost finishes its ring, and the account comes due, and the Guest collects what's been waiting in that house for fifty years with its name already written down."

August looked at the receipt. Seven lines. Six names of strangers who had died suddenly, unexpectedly, taken too soon, in a town that had never added up the column. And his own name at the bottom in ballpoint, in letters that weren't small at all anymore, that seemed to have gotten larger since he'd written them, though he knew that was impossible, knew it the way he knew all the impossible things now, completely and uselessly.

"We need Edith's tapes again," he said. "All of them. Every reel in that attic. If there's a way out of a contract, the terms are in the original recording — and if there isn't a way out—"

"Then we find out what happens when the seventh voice refuses," Mara said.

Upstairs, faintly, through the ceiling, came a sound like a fingernail tracing a letter on glass. Once. Twice. Patient as winter.

It was writing something down.

And August, sitting in his own kitchen with his own name in the seventh space, understood with perfect clarity that it wasn't writing his name at all.

It was crossing one out — and writing a date beside it.
