## 39. One Last Call

The studio at 3 a.m. had learned a new kind of quiet.

It wasn't the old quiet — the dead-air quiet August had once feared, the silence that yawned open when a caller hung up and the board hissed like something breathing. That quiet was gone. In its place was a listening quiet, the hush of a room that had been through something and was still deciding whether it was over. The ON AIR sign glowed its steady red. The generator had been retired weeks ago, hauled back to the rental yard with a handshake and a story about storm damage, and the transmitter ran warm again, the way transmitters were supposed to run. Warm. Ordinary. August had learned to love ordinary things the way other men loved their mothers.

He sat at the board with his sleeves rolled and a mug of coffee gone cold beside him, and he waited for the hour to open the way a man waits at a dock for a boat he isn't sure will come.

*The Long Hour* had been on the air for nineteen nights.

The format was simple, because the truth was simple, and it had taken him forty years and one shattered mirror to learn that simple things carried the most weight. No music after midnight. No comedy bits, no prank calls, no manufactured outrage to fill the dark. Just August Lockridge, live until sunrise, asking the sleeping town one question: *Who do you miss, and what did you never get to say?*

The first week, the phones had been silent. He'd talked into that silence anyway, hour after hour, telling Wren's story in pieces — the way she hummed when she did the crossword, the way she'd once driven two hours in the rain because he mentioned offhand that he was out of cigarettes and too tired to move. He told the town about the mirror. About the frost. About the seven names and the six voices and the bargain his grandmother had made with something patient and starving in the winter of 1971. He told them about Ruthie, frozen in her kitchen with a note in her hand, and about Denny, who had owed a man forty dollars for eleven years and had finally, on the air, in front of everyone, been forgiven.

The town had listened. That was the miracle no one had predicted. Not the ratings — though the ratings had come, climbing like something startled into flight — but the listening. By the second week, the calls started. Living voices, mostly, shaky and ordinary, telling the dead things they'd hoarded for decades. *Mom, I should have come home that Christmas.* *Danny, I was the one who broke your guitar, and I'm sorry, I'm sorry.* Each confession went out over the airwaves and into the dark, and somewhere — August could not prove it, could only feel it, the way you feel weather coming in a healed bone — somewhere the words were being received.

Mara kept the promise. That was the term they'd written into the rewritten bargain, the guarantor's clause, her signature in her own blood on a page of Edith's ledger that August still kept locked in the desk that had been Wren's. One hour a night, the living speaking freely for the dead. Mara had signed it without hesitation, and August had hated her for it, and she had said, in that flat clinical voice she used when she was most afraid, *Someone has to hold the line, August. You're the voice. I'm the keeper. That's the arithmetic now.*

So the show ran. And the dead stayed quiet.

Mostly.

August glanced at the log book beside the board — a plain composition notebook, because some habits from the old show died hard — where he tracked every caller, every name, every story. He had started keeping a second column three nights ago, though he hadn't told Mara yet. It was a list of the callers whose stories had a certain texture to them. A hesitation before the name of the deceased. A detail that didn't fit. Nothing he could point to on a meter, nothing Mara's instruments would register. Just the instinct of a man who had spent a season learning what the dead sounded like on a phone line, and who now heard that sound in places it shouldn't be.

The clock read 3:04. Three minutes until the final segment.

He looked at the window.

The studio window was old glass, wavy with age, and in July it should have been clear as water. But along the lower left corner, small as a thumbnail, a frost fern bloomed. White. Not black — he checked it every night, the way a man checks a mole, the way a recovering drunk checks the clock — white, the color the ferns turned when the terms were being honored, when the words flowed freely and the Guest kept its side of the rewritten bargain. White, but *there*. It had been there since the first night of the new show, and it had not grown, and it had not faded, and August had decided — after long argument with himself, and one long argument with Mara — that this was not a haunting.

It was a signature. A witness mark. *Listening, perhaps,* he'd written in his own notebook, *but held to terms.* The Guest had been invited once, bound, and forgotten, and it had starved fifty years on the difference between a bargain and a betrayal. August was not going to make that mistake again. The fern stayed. He watched it. Every night, he watched it, and every night it watched him back with the patience of something that had learned, at last, how to wait without taking.

The clock ticked to 3:07.

August leaned into the microphone. The red light was steady. The transmitter hummed. Somewhere out in the dark, he knew, were the people who couldn't sleep — the grieving, the guilty, the curious, the ones who had lost someone and found, to their own surprise, that they wanted to talk about it at the hour when the walls between things wore thin.

"You're listening to *The Long Hour*," he said. "It's 3:07, and this is our last segment before sunrise. Lines are open. You know the question. Who do you miss, and what did you never get to say? Take your time. The dead aren't going anywhere — that's kind of the whole point."

He sat back. The board lit.

Line three.

He watched it ring for a moment — one ring, two — and felt the old familiar tightening in his chest, the one he'd never quite trained out of himself, the reflex of a man who had once answered a phone at 3 a.m. and heard his dead sister's voice say his childhood nickname before the static took her. He breathed. He let it ring a third time. Then he keyed the line.

"You're live. Go ahead."

"Hey. Hey, is this — is this the show? The one where you talk to the dead people?"

The voice was male, young-ish, thirties maybe. It had the loose warmth of a man who'd had a few beers and the careful diction of a man trying to hide it. It was, August thought, a *living* voice — he knew the difference now the way a jeweler knows paste from diamond, the way the dead sounded slightly delayed, slightly under glass, their words arriving a half-second after the breath that made them. This voice arrived on time.

"This is the show," August said. "Though I should say — we don't talk *to* the dead, exactly. We talk *for* them. The living call in, they say what needs saying, and it goes out on the air. Freely given. That's the whole engine. What's your name?"

"Tommy. Tommy Vane." A small laugh, nervous, the laugh of a man standing at the edge of a diving board. "Man, I can't believe I'm calling this. My sister listens to you every night. She made me promise — she said if I ever, you know. If I ever got the courage. She said call the show."

"Well, Tommy. Your sister's smarter than most of us. What did you never get to say?"

Silence on the line. Not dead-air silence — the living kind, the kind full of swallowed breath and the small sounds of a man rearranging his courage. August waited. Waiting was the job now. He had learned that from Wren, posthumously, from a letter he'd read forty times: *People don't need you to talk, Augie. They need somewhere the talking is allowed.*

"It's my dad," Tommy said. "He died in March. Heart. He was — we were working on his truck when it happened. Right there in the driveway. And the last thing I said to him was —" The breath caught. "The last thing I said was that he never listened to me. That he never *heard* me. Forty-one years old and I'm fighting with my dad in a driveway, and then he's on the ground, and those were — those were the words. That's what he got."

The studio was very quiet. The frost fern in the window seemed, in August's peripheral vision, to sharpen — each frond distinct, each edge bright, the way a listener leans forward.

"Tommy," August said gently. "What did you need him to hear?"

"That I was proud of him." The words came out fast now, the dam breaking the way it always broke, and August let them come. "Everybody thought he was just — a garage guy, you know? Fixed lawnmowers, fixed everybody's snowblower, never charged half of them. And I was embarrassed when I was a kid, I wanted him to be somebody, and it took me until I was thirty to understand that he *was* somebody, he was the guy the whole town called when it was broke, and I never told him that. I never once told him I was proud. And now —" A wet breath. "Now the truck's still in the driveway and I can't even look at it."

August looked at the window. The fern was white. Steady. He looked at the board. The signal was clean.

"Then say it now, Tommy. He can't hear it the way we'd want him to — I won't lie to you, I've stopped lying on this show, it's the one rule I've got left. But it goes out. It goes into the air, and it's witnessed, and that matters. I've seen what it does. Say it like he's in the driveway. Say it like the engine's running."

Tommy Vane said it. He said it badly and beautifully, in a voice that cracked and steadied and cracked again, and August sat with his hand flat on the board and let the whole town hear a grown man tell his dead father he was proud, and when it was done the silence on the line was the good kind, the rinsed kind, the kind that comes after crying that needed doing.

"Thank you," Tommy whispered.

"Thank you," August said. "That's why the show takes calls. Get some sleep, Tommy. And go start that truck. He'd like that."

The line clicked. August exhaled, logged the call — *Vane, T. Father, March. Pride, unpaid, paid* — and reached for his coffee out of habit, remembering too late it was cold, drinking it anyway.

The board lit again. Line one.

He keyed it. "You're live. Go ahead."

"Hi. Yes. Hello." The voice was male, older than Tommy's, maybe fifty, with the clipped pleasantness of a man used to being on the phone — a salesman's cadence, or a dentist's. "Quick question before I — before I say my piece. Does the show take requests?"

August smiled, tired and professional. "Requests? Like music?"

"No, no. Like —" A small chuckle. "Like topics. Things you'd want discussed. I've been listening every night, and I have something I think your audience would want to hear. Something that's been weighing on me. But I like to know the rules first. Does the show take requests, Mr. Lockridge?"

"We take anything freely given," August said. "That's the only rule. Who am I talking to?"

"Harlan," the man said. "Harlan Petty. Out past the reservoir."

August wrote the name in the log. *Petty, H.* And then, because the habit was three nights old and already load-bearing, he found himself pausing over the page. There was something in the voice — not the delayed, under-glass quality of the dead. This man was alive; August would have bet the station on it. His breath arrived on time. His words had the messy warmth of a pulse behind them. But there was something else, something he'd heard once before on this show, in a different register, in a voice he'd loved: the sound of a person calling from the near side of a threshold.

"Harlan Petty," August repeated, and reached under the board for the second notebook.

He had started it because of a dream — or what he'd told Mara was a dream, though they both knew better. In it, he'd been sitting at this same desk, and the log book had opened itself to a page he'd never written, and the page had been full of names and dates, and the dates had all been *after*. Not obituaries. *Pre*ituaries. A schedule. He'd woken with the shape of it in his mind the way you wake with the shape of a word in your mouth, and the next night, half-ashamed, he'd started a second column. Not a prediction. He wasn't stupid; he wasn't a psychic; Mara would have flayed him. Just a record. Just a place to note, privately, the calls that felt like they came from people standing close to the door.

He flipped to tonight's page. The lines were already there.

He had not written them.

The handwriting was his own — that was the worst of it, the detail his mind kept snagging on like a nail in a doorframe. His own slanted, left-leaning script, the script that had filled a thousand log books and two published novels. *Petty, H. Reservoir. Something weighing. Wants to be heard.* And beside it, in the second column, in the same hand, in ink that looked as fresh as the words he'd just written for Tommy Vane, a date.

Next Thursday.

Six days from now.

August did not move. The studio hummed around him — the transmitter, the ventilation, the refrigerator in the break room with its dying compressor — all the small warm sounds of a living building, and he sat inside them like a man inside a snow globe, looking at a date written in his own hand that he had never written.

"Mr. Lockridge?" The voice on the line was pleasant, patient. "Still there? I know it's late. I can call back tomorrow if —"

"No." August's voice came out level. He was almost proud of it. "No, Harlan, I'm here. Tell me what's weighing on you."

"Well —" A breath, and then the man laughed, soft and rueful. "You know what's funny? I practiced this in the truck. Had a whole — a whole speech. And now that I'm on, it's just — it's about my brother. My brother and a lake, and a summer in — God, it'd be 1989 now. And I've carried it thirty-six years, and my doctor says the carrying is —" He stopped. Started again, lighter, too light. "Anyway. It can keep another week. It's kept thirty-six years, it can keep another week. I just wanted to hear your voice say the lines were open. I just wanted to know there's a place, if I — you know. If I get up the nerve."

The clock read 3:19. The frost fern in the window was white, white, white — but August saw, now, at its outermost edge, a single frond that was not quite white. Not black either. The color of frost at the exact moment of thawing, or at the exact moment of forming. A color that hadn't decided.

"Harlan," August said. "Don't wait for the nerve. The nerve is a liar — it tells you there's time. There's a place here. There's a place here every night, and I'm here every night, and whatever it is — a lake, a summer, a brother — it goes out free, and it's witnessed, and it counts. I promise you it counts. I've seen what it does."

A long pause on the line. When Harlan Petty spoke again, his voice had changed — not colder, not stranger, just *closer*, as if he had leaned in from a great distance, as if the phone had become a window and his face was suddenly near the glass.

"You're a good man, August Lockridge," he said. "Your sister thought so. She told me so."

The studio went silent in a way it had not gone silent in nineteen nights. Not dead air. *Listening* air. The transmitter hummed. The refrigerator hummed. The fern in the window held its undecided color, and August sat very still, his hand flat on the log book, on the date written in his own hand, six days out, and heard his own heartbeat in his ears like a second, slower broadcast.

"Harlan," he said carefully. "How do you know my sister?"

The pause stretched. Somewhere on the line, faintly, under the man's breathing, August thought he heard water — the lapping kind, the reservoir kind, though Harlan had said he was calling from home.

"Oh," Harlan said lightly, "we go way back. Everybody out here goes way back with the Lockridges." A chuckle. "Thursday, then. I'll call Thursday. I'll have my nerve by then. You just keep the lines open, Mr. Lockridge. Keep the lines open, and the light on, and — well. You know how it is. Somebody's always listening."

The line went dead — the ordinary kind, the click of a receiver, the small mechanical death of a call ended by a living hand.

August sat for a long time. The clock ticked to 3:24. Outside, the sky was still hours from gray, and the fern on the window had not grown, and had not faded, and its outermost frond was still the color of a decision not yet made.

He looked down at the log book. At his own handwriting. At the date.

Then he picked up the phone and dialed the number he knew by heart now, the one Mara answered at any hour because she had promised to, and when she picked up — awake, of course, she was always awake at this hour, the keeper at her post — he said only:

"Mara. I need you to pull the archives. Everybody named Petty. And Mara — bring the ledger. I think the Guest is taking requests."
