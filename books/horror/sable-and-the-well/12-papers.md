## 12. Papers

The county archive closed at five. Edmund had learned this the way he learned everything, by showing up and being told no.

He came back at eight forty-five the next morning with a thermos of black coffee and a story about a property dispute he was half-inventing as he said it aloud to the woman at the reference desk. "Boundary issue," he said. "Old parcel. My client's grandfather bought a piece off a neighbor in the forties and nobody's been able to prove it since."

It wasn't even a good lie. It was just plausible enough, and plausibility had always been enough for Edmund Underhill. The world ran on paperwork, and paperwork ran on people not looking too closely.

"Name?" the archivist asked.

"Mott," he said. "Elias Mott. Around 1948."

She typed for a while. He drank his coffee and watched dust turn in the light from the high windows, and he did not think about handprints on ceilings arranged like a seating chart, because thinking about that was not why he was here. He was here because of records. Records were solid. Records were the opposite of a chair that moved between photographs.

"Probate file would be with vital records," she said finally. "Estate boxes are down in sub-basement B. You'll need to sign in."

"I'll sign anything," Edmund said, and heard how it sounded only after it was out of his mouth.

* * *

Sub-basement B smelled like cold paper and older cold. Fluorescent tubes buzzed at intervals down a corridor of gray shelving, each aisle labeled by decade, each decade holding its dead in acid-free folders. A clerk named Doris — sixty-ish, reading glasses on a chain, the unimpressed posture of someone who had spent forty years watching men discover their grandfathers' secrets — led him to a shelf near the back.

"Estate of Elias Mott, died 1948," she said. "One box. Nobody's pulled it since 1987, and before that, 1961." She set it on a table under the brightest light. "Gloves if you're touching photographs. Pencil only. And you sign out whatever you take into the reading room — nothing leaves the building."

"Understood."

"You look pale," Doris said, without sympathy. "People always do, down here. It's the air."

It was not the air. Edmund knew what pale meant. Pale meant you'd spent three nights photographing your own kitchen ceiling at two in the morning because there was a smudge above the stove that hadn't been there when you'd bought the apartment, a greasy arc, four fingers and a thumb, angled downward, like something reaching. Pale meant you'd caught yourself humming a tune with no name while you waited for the kettle, stopping one line short every time, the way you'd stop short of a stair that wasn't there.

Pale meant the invoice for the Sable job was still folded in his wallet, and last night he'd stood over his bathroom sink and held a lighter under it until the corner curled, and then couldn't finish, and put it out under the tap, and now it sat in his wallet water-stained and half-burned like a thing that refused either state.

"Take your time," Doris said, and left him alone with the box.

* * *

The box was shallow, cardboard gone soft at the corners, tied with cotton tape that crumbled when he touched it. Inside:

A funeral bill, unpaid. A church program for a service that read *memorial* rather than *funeral*, because there had been no body to bury — only what the coroner's note, clipped behind it, described as "recovery of personal effects from an open well shaft on the Sable property, remains not recovered, presumed deceased."

A pair of eyeglasses, wire-rimmed, wrapped in a 1948 newspaper whose headline was about rain. Rain all week, the paper said. The wells are full.

A leather folio, stiff as a board, stamped with nothing.

Edmund opened the folio last, the way you save the worst bite.

Inside was the counterpart deed. He knew it instantly, the way you know your own handwriting — because he'd spent Tuesday night in the county land office with photostats of the Sable transfer, staring at the blank signature line where the buyer's name should have been. *Sold this day to ______*, and the line empty, and the seller's side signed twice over in Agnes's late husband's careful hand, witnessed, notarized, sealed. A contract half-made. A door half-open.

This was the other half. Mott's copy. The same document, mirrored: *sold this day by Elias Mott to the estate of Harold Sable* — no, he corrected himself, reading again, the ink faded to brown — *from the estate of Harold Sable to Elias Mott*. Same transaction seen from the other shore.

And the signature line was not blank.

There was a name on it. Faint. Written in something darker than the rest of the ink, something that sat *on top of* the paper instead of in it, the way frost sits on glass. He bent close. The light buzzed. The name was **Elias Mott**, in a hand that trembled at the E and steadied by the final t, as though written by someone learning, letter by letter, how to be a person again.

Edmund took out his phone and photographed it. Then he did the thing he'd promised himself he wouldn't do, which was touch it.

The ink was wet.

Not damp. Not tacky with age. *Wet.* His fingertip came away dark, and the smell rose off it — iron and pond-bottom and something sweeter underneath, like flowers kept too long in standing water. He wiped his finger on his jeans and the stain didn't wipe. It spread slightly, the way a bruise spreads under skin.

"Okay," he said quietly, to the box, to the basement, to no one. "Okay."

Doris appeared at the end of the aisle as if summoned by the word. "Everything all right?"

"Fine." His voice came out level, which surprised him. "Just — this document. I'd like to see the probate notes. Who filed this? What happened to the estate?"

"No next of kin came forward," Doris said. She said it the way you recite a phone number. "Man drowned, no family, effects held in abeyance. Standard practice is destruction after thirty years, but—" she shrugged— "the box keeps getting misfiled. Every time we reorganize, it turns up somewhere it shouldn't. Top shelf. Behind the tax rolls. Once in with the birth certificates, which is frankly impossible, those are a different building." She looked at the open folio, at the deed, and her mouth thinned. "You'll want gloves for that."

"It's fine."

"It's never fine," Doris said. "That's why we have rules." And she went back to her desk, and Edmund understood that she knew more than she was saying, or less, and that either way she wanted him gone before five o'clock.

* * *

He worked through the rest of the box with pencil-nervous hands.

The newspaper clippings told the shape of it. *LOCAL MAN MISSING* — August 1948. *SEARCH AT SABLE WELL CONTINUES* — September. *WELL CASE CLOSED; FAMILY COOPERATES FULLY* — October, with a quote from Harold Sable expressing sorrow and noting the sale had "regrettably never been completed to Mr. Mott's satisfaction." Regrettably never completed. Harold had known. Harold had signed his side and watched the buyer walk toward the well with the unsigned papers in his coat pocket, and Harold had lived another forty years sleeping ten feet from the shaft, and Edmund wondered what kind of man that made him, and then stopped wondering, because he already knew: the kind who invoices for rodents.

The last item was a photograph.

Paper-clipped to the inside back cover of the folio, where the county inventory sheet said only *1 photo, misc.* It showed a farmhouse kitchen — the Sable kitchen, he recognized the window, the deep sink, the doorframe with the pencil marks measuring children's heights — and a wooden chair.

The chair stood against the far wall, facing the camera. Plain, ladder-backed, dark wood. Nothing remarkable about it except that the kitchen in the photograph was otherwise empty, and the date penciled on the back was June 1948, and Wren had shown him the same chair in Agnes's albums growing closer to the camera across seventy years, and here it was at the beginning, already waiting, already patient, photographed by a man who had six weeks left to live and had spent some part of them taking pictures of furniture.

On the back, beneath the date, in the same trembling-then-steadying hand as the signature:

*it wants the second name*

Edmund put the photograph face-down on the table. His heart was doing something he refused to call fear. Fear was for believers. This was — data. Evidence. A pattern. He was good at patterns. Patterns could be handled.

Except the pattern was handling him. Had been since the first day, since the handprints climbing the inside of the shaft like something ascending toward the light, hundreds of prints, all the same hand, descending — no, he'd thought descending then, but what if they'd been ascending? What if something had been climbing *out* for seventy years, one print at a time, and the well was not full but *emptying*, and everything he'd catalogued and dismissed was just the slow tide of it reaching the kitchen ceiling, reaching his kitchen ceiling, reaching—

His phone buzzed. Wren.

He stared at her name for four rings. Answered on the fifth.

"Where are you?" Her voice was low, fast. Agnes's voice murmured somewhere behind her, humming — he knew the tune, God help him, he knew it, and it stopped one line short even through a phone speaker, even through a dying woman's breath.

"The archive. I found Mott's copy of the deed."

Silence. Then: "And?"

"And the signature line isn't blank anymore." He looked at the folio. At the dark name sitting on top of the paper. "It says Elias Mott. In ink that won't dry."

Another silence, longer. When Wren spoke again her voice had changed — smaller, careful, the voice of someone standing at the edge of something. "Edmund. There's something else. Last night my grandmother woke up lucid, and she told me—" a breath— "she told me the papers can't be finished by the dead. Only the living. Both names have to be living hands. That's the whole trick of it. That's why it's waited."

"That's good," Edmund said. "That means we can close it. We get Agnes to sign her side, we countersign, we drown the deed in the well, done. Clean. Legal, even. I've been saying all along this is just a title dispute."

"Edmund." His name in her mouth did something to the air between them. "She also said the well doesn't want a signature. It wants a *resident*. Whoever holds the unfinished deed belongs to the house until the house lets go. She said—" and here Wren's voice did fracture, once, quickly repaired— "she said whoever finishes the papers has to be willing to live there first."

Down in sub-basement B, under the buzzing lights, Edmund looked at the photograph lying face-down on the table, and at the wet name, and at his own right hand resting beside the folio — and noticed, with the calm of a man watching a car crash in which he is the passenger, that there was a faint dark smudge on his index finger, four small marks and the ghost of a thumb, where the ink had soaked through the skin.

"I'm coming over," he said.

"Hurry," Wren said. "I don't think we have until the anniversary. I think it's started counting down."

He hung up. He gathered the deed, the photograph, the eyeglasses — Doris would want them logged, Doris could invoice him, everyone got paid eventually — and as he signed the exit form, the pen dragged, and he looked down and saw that the line where his own name should go was already darkening, the letters rising up through the paper like bruises surfacing, spelling themselves out in a hand that trembled at the E and steadied by the final l.

*Edmund.*

Half a name. Waiting for the rest.
