## 13. Wren's Bargain

The hospice nurse had a name for it — "transitioning" — as if Agnes were a caterpillar, as if dying were a change of costume rather than an exit. Wren had thanked her and closed the door behind her and stood in the hallway of the farmhouse listening to her grandmother's breathing, which had gone from a rhythm to a weather system, long wet draws with silences between them that lasted long enough for Wren to count to four, then five, then once, terrifyingly, nine.

That was three days ago. The silences were longer now. The breaths came like a man hauling buckets up from somewhere very deep.

Wren had moved into the back bedroom, the one that had been hers at fourteen, the one with the water stain on the ceiling shaped like a hand if you let yourself see it, so she made herself not see it. She slept in shifts against the silence. She kept the hymnal on the nightstand, the torn page pressed flat under a coffee mug, because she couldn't decide whether to throw it in the woodstove or keep it, and every morning she woke up and still hadn't decided, and the not-deciding was starting to feel like a decision itself.

On the fourth night, the house stopped pretending.

* * *

It began with the smell. Not rot — she'd braced herself for rot — but well-water. That cold mineral sweetness of deep stone, iron and limestone and something older underneath, the way the yard smelled in August when the water table came up high enough to touch the roots of things. It filled the kitchen while she washed the morphine syringes, and she stood at the sink with her hands in the rinse water and thought: *the cap is on. I checked the cap yesterday.*

She dried her hands. She went to the door.

The kitchen light was on but the kitchen was wrong. Not dramatically wrong — nothing levitated, nothing bled — just off by degrees, the way a photograph is off when someone has rearranged one object and your eye keeps snagging on it. The table had been pushed two feet toward the window. The chairs were arranged around it with a care no one in this family had ever shown furniture: four of them, evenly spaced, facing inward.

And the fifth chair — the wooden one, the extra one, the one that had been creeping toward cameras since 1951 — stood apart from the others, angled toward the hallway, toward Agnes's room, positioned like a listener outside a door.

Wren's first instinct was the old one, the Underhill-instinct she'd caught from him like a cold: name it, file it, make it small. *Sleep deprivation. Grief hallucination. The house settling.* But Edmund had burned his invoice, she'd heard, and there was no comfort left in the professional vocabulary. The chair was where it was. The smell was what it was. Her grandmother was dying upstairs and something in this house wanted to negotiate.

"I'm not singing," Wren said out loud, to the kitchen, to the well beneath the kitchen, to whatever leaned against the other side of the floorboards listening. Her voice sounded younger than she wanted it to. "So you can save us both the time."

The refrigerator hummed. The clock over the stove ticked. And then, from the direction of the hallway, softly, patiently, the sound of wood on linoleum — the chair dragging itself one foot closer to Agnes's door.

* * *

Wren did not sleep after that. She sat in the rocker beside her grandmother's bed with the lamp on low and watched the rise and fall of the blanket and listened to the house think.

Agnes looked smaller than she had even a week ago, folded down into herself like a letter being prepared for mailing. Her hands lay on top of the quilt, and Wren took one of them, the right one, the one that had signed everything her whole life — school forms, tax returns, the deed papers she kept talking about in her lucid hours, *find the papers, Wrennie, finish the papers* — and held it the way you hold something you're afraid will be taken.

At some point past two, Agnes opened her eyes.

Not the cloudy half-waking of the last weeks. Clear. Blue as a winter sky, focused, present, and for one absurd second Wren felt a spike of pure joy — *she's back, she's lucid, we have time* — before she saw what was underneath the clarity, which was fear.

"It talked to you," Agnes said. Not a question.

"In the kitchen." Wren kept her voice level. "It moved the chair."

"It moves the chair when it wants to be answered." Agnes turned her head on the pillow with great effort, and her eyes went to the window, though the curtains were drawn and there was nothing to see but dark. "Your mother heard it too, the year before she left. And my brother Daniel. It finds the one who loves the dying person best. That's how it chooses who to ask."

"Ask what?"

"You know what."

Wren did know. She'd known since the neighbor Mrs. Calloway had gripped her wrist at the mailbox and said the verse only lived in memory now, and whoever completed the song invited the singer down. She'd known since she found the hymnal with its final page torn away, since she'd lain awake reconstructing the shape of the missing lines without letting any of them reach her lips. Knowledge wasn't the danger. The danger was the mouth.

"It offered me a trade," Wren said. "It hasn't said it yet. But it's going to."

Agnes closed her eyes. When she opened them again they were wet. "Then listen to me, girl, because I won't get another hour like this one. Whatever it offers you — however it dresses it up — the price is never what it says the price is. It told my father it would spare the cattle. It told my mother it would end the drought. It always tells the truth about the thing it gives and lies about the thing it takes, because the giving is real. That's the trap. You can't catch it in the lie until afterward, and afterward doesn't come back up the ladder."

"What did it take from you?"

For a long moment Wren thought her grandmother wouldn't answer. Then Agnes said, "My brother sang one line. One line, humming, doing dishes, sixty years ago. He didn't even know he was doing it. He drowned in two inches of creek water the next spring, and they never found his boots." Her grip tightened on Wren's hand, surprisingly strong. "It collects by inches, Wrennie. A line. A boot. A chair in a photograph. Don't give it an inch tonight."

* * *

But the house had all night, and Wren only had the hours until dawn, and the thing in the well knew arithmetic better than anyone alive.

It came to her at three, the hour Edmund had named in one of their late phone calls — calls that had gotten longer and stranger, the two of them comparing notes like children trading evidence of a shared ghost, neither willing to say aloud what was happening between them beyond the haunting. Three a.m., he'd said. That's when the prints appeared fresh. That's when the house does its bookkeeping.

Wren was dozing in the rocker when the temperature dropped — not cold exactly, but *deep*, the specific chill of water that has never seen sun. The lamp dimmed to a brown ember. And the voice, when it finally came, did not come through the air. It came up through the floor, through the bones of the house, through the soles of her feet and the seat of the rocker, resonating in her chest like a note sung into a piano's strings.

It was not a horrible voice. That was the worst part. It was patient and reasonable and faintly sad, the voice of a clerk who has waited seventy years for a signature and bears no grudge about the wait.

*You love her,* it said.

"Yes," Wren whispered, because lying seemed unwise.

*She is in pain. You have seen the chart the nurses keep. Zero to ten. She circles eight most mornings. Nine at night. Morphine is a loan against pain, not a forgiveness of it.* The floorboards creaked, almost gently. *I can forgive it. All of it. She will sleep the way she slept as a girl in this house, before the well, before the debt, before any of it. She will go smiling. No more circling numbers. No more nights.*

Wren's throat worked. Because here was the obscene truth, the one she could never say at the bedside: she wanted it. She wanted her grandmother's suffering to end the way you want anything with your whole body — helplessly, without dignity. Every hour of the last week she had sat in that rocker doing the math of mercy, hating herself for doing the math, and now something had crawled up out of the dark offering to do the math for her.

"And the price," Wren said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she hated that too.

*You know the song. You have carried it carefully, written and not spoken, and I have watched you carry it, and I am — I will admit this — impressed.* Something that was almost warmth moved through the floor. *Sing it at the wellhead. Once. Whole. That is all. One verse, freely given, and her passing becomes a gift instead of a wound. Everyone gets what they want. Even me. Especially me.*

"That's not all," Wren said. "Mrs. Calloway told me what the verse does. Completing the song is an invitation. Whoever sings it—"

*Whoever sings it alone,* the presence corrected, smooth as silt. *You have misread the old woman's warning, as she misread her own mother's. The song is a door. A door needs someone on each side. Sing it and I open mine — and yours stays shut, and you walk away dry-shod, and I close the well forever, satisfied, paid in full. Why would I take you? What use is a singer when the song is finished?*

And the terrible thing — the thing Wren would carry for the rest of her life, the thing she would tell Edmund at dawn in a voice she didn't recognize — was that it almost worked. It almost worked because it was *reasonable*. Because grief makes lawyers of us all and she stood there in the brown lamplight constructing the argument herself: *one verse, one minute of singing, versus months of morphine and the wet rattle and the circling nines; what kind of granddaughter hoards her caution when her grandmother is drowning on dry land; what kind of coward—*

The chair scraped in the hallway. Closer now. Right outside Agnes's door.

"No," Wren said.

The word came out small. She made herself say it again, bigger.

"No. I don't believe you. My grandmother told me how you work — you tell the truth about what you give and lie about what you take, and the taking comes by inches. A line. A boot. If I sing your verse, the door opens both ways, and you'll call it a misunderstanding, and misunderstandings don't come back up the ladder either."

Silence. Long enough that she dared hope she'd won something.

Then, gently, terribly, the voice said: *Ask her.*

"What?"

*Ask your grandmother what she circled on the chart this morning. Ask her what she begged God for at four o'clock, when she thought you were asleep. You are keeping her alive for your sake, child, and calling it love. I am offering to end her pain for her sake, and you call it evil. One of us is lying. Go check which.*

The lamp came back up. The cold drained out through the floorboards like water through sand. In the hallway, the chair dragged itself back to its place by the window, unhurried, unbothered, a salesman closing his case for the night.

* * *

Wren sat in the rocker until the windows went gray, holding her grandmother's hand, watching her circle through the shallows of sleep. Twice Agnes moaned — a small, animal sound Wren had never heard her make, a sound with no dignity in it at all — and each time, the offer replayed itself in Wren's head with its reasonable voice and its clean arithmetic, and each time she hated herself a little more for how much she wanted to say yes.

Near six, Agnes surfaced. Her eyes found Wren immediately, and they were clear again, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"You said no," she rasped.

"How do you know?"

"The house got quiet. It gets quiet when it loses." Agnes's mouth bent into something that was almost a smile. "You did right, Wrennie. I need you to hear that before I can't say it anymore. You did right."

"But it's not done, is it." Wren leaned close. "It'll come back. Tonight. Tomorrow. It'll keep coming back as long as you're hurting, because it knows I'll break eventually. It can wait. It's good at waiting."

Agnes's eyes drifted to the window, to the gray light coming up over the yard, to the place beyond the curtain where the well cap sat on its shaft like a cork in a throat.

"Then we don't wait with it," she said. "Get the papers, girl. Get the boy with the flashlight and the invoices. We finish this while I've still got a hand that signs." She squeezed Wren's fingers, and her grip was fading even as she spoke, the lucidity draining like the tide going out. "And Wren — the verse. You wrote it down?"

"Written. Never spoken."

"Good." Agnes's eyes were closing. "Keep it that way. Because there's a way to use a song without singing it, and I think... I think your young man already knows..."

Her hand went slack. Her breathing settled back into its bucket-hauling rhythm, deeper now, slower, and Wren sat very still in the gray morning light, her heart going hard, turning the sentence over.

*A way to use a song without singing it.*

Outside, far off down the county road, an engine — Edmund's van, early, earlier than he'd ever come before, as if he hadn't slept either, as if the house had been talking to him too.

Wren wiped her face with the heel of her hand, stood, and went downstairs to let him in.
