## 16. The Chair Moves

The photographs came back from the one-hour lab on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were when Edmund Underhill did his accounting, cleaned his traps, and generally reminded himself that the world was a place with invoices in it.

He'd taken the pictures himself, which was the first stupid thing. He knew better. You didn't photograph your own apartment with the flash on at midnight unless you wanted to see something, and he hadn't wanted to see anything. He'd told himself it was documentation. He was good at telling himself things like that. It was practically his profession: walk into a house where something wrong is happening, name it something boring, write it down in a boring way, leave. Rats. Mice. Moisture damage. Vermin of one kind or another, always vermin, always explicable.

The roll had twenty-four exposures. He'd shot them over four nights, mostly of his kitchen ceiling, because that was where he'd heard the sound — a slow wooden drag, three feet maybe, then silence, then nothing for an hour, then again. He lived alone above a shuttered laundromat on Corben Street. There was no chair in his kitchen. He owned two folding chairs and a bar stool and that was the complete inventory of his seating, and none of them were wood, and none of them scraped.

He paid the kid at the counter, took the envelope, and walked home without opening it, because part of him — the part that had been growing since the Sable farmhouse, since the greasy handprints descending the inside of that shaft like something climbing down a throat — already knew what was in there. Knowing in advance didn't help. That was the thing nobody told you about dread. It wasn't suspense. Suspense was a door you hadn't opened yet. Dread was knowing the door had been open the whole time.

He sat at his kitchen table under the fluorescent tube he'd never gotten around to replacing with something warmer, and he opened the envelope, and he went through them one at a time, face down, flipping each like a bad card player.

Seventeen shots of his ceiling. Cracked plaster, water stain shaped like nothing, the fluorescent tube's ghost glare. Boring. Beautifully boring. He let out a breath he'd been holding since the lab.

Eighteen was the kitchen table from the other side, shot toward the window, and in the corner of the frame, half-cut by the edge, was a chair.

Wooden. Ladder-back. Seat worn pale in the middle.

He set it down. He picked it up again. He held it under the light and looked until his eyes watered, looking for the trick — a double exposure, a reflection, something from the neighbor's yard visible through glass. His building's windows faced a brick wall eight feet away. There was no yard. There was no light source in that corner of his kitchen after dark except the tube overhead, and the chair sat in shadow anyway, as if it declined to be lit properly, as if it had opinions about how it should be seen.

Nineteen through twenty-two: the same room, different angles, and the chair in every one, closer each time. Not moved by him — he'd have noticed, he'd have *screamed* — but closer between frames, the way things get closer in dreams, where distance is just a rumor the room tells itself. In nineteen it was against the wall. In twenty it had turned slightly, presenting its profile. In twenty-one it faced the table. In twenty-two it was pulled up to the table, tucked in, polite, waiting for someone to sit across from it or in it, and Edmund's hands had gone cold enough that he fumbled the print and it skated off the table and lay face-up on the floor, and he looked at it down there for a long time before he picked it up.

Twenty-three was the hallway. No chair.

Twenty-four was the bedroom doorway, shot from inside the bedroom, and the chair stood in the hall behind the camera's shoulder, and Edmund understood, with the flat calm of a man reading his own diagnosis, that the camera had been between him and the chair, and the chair had been behind him, and he had been alone in the apartment both nights he'd shot those frames, and he had not turned around.

He put all twenty-four prints in a row on the table, in order, like evidence. Like a case file. That was the reflex, and he watched himself do it with something close to pity. Twenty years of pest control. Twenty years of being the man who walked into other people's worst nights and said, *it's mice, ma'am, here's the invoice.* He had built a whole life on the principle that everything that frightened people had a Latin name and a price per unit. And now the Latin name was sitting in his kitchen in a photograph, and the price per unit was going to be something no invoice could hold.

*Resident-in-waiting.*

That phrase arrived in his head fully formed, wearing someone else's voice, and he hated it, and he recognized its logic immediately, because he'd been assembling that logic himself for weeks without admitting it. The Sable house collected signatures. It collected photographs. It collected people — Elias Mott first, drowned three weeks after buying a well he never got to sign for, and God knew who else in the decades since, the missing cousins, the uncle who "moved to Oregon" that nobody in the family could produce an address for. The house added a chair to every photograph it kept, and the chair got closer over time, and Wren had shown him the albums: 1951, chair by the stove. 1963, chair at the table's end. 1977, chair beside Agnes's mother. The chair was not a haunting. The chair was a *reservation*.

And now it had made one in his name.

* * *

He did what a rational man does. He went through the motions of a rational man, and he noted each motion as it failed, the way you'd log a bait station that keeps coming up empty.

He checked the negatives. The chair was on the negatives. Whatever was happening was happening before the printing, which meant it was happening in the room, or in the light, or in whatever word you wanted to use for the space between the two.

He photographed the photographs. The chair appeared in the new shots too, and in one of them — this was the detail that finally made him sit down on the floor, back against the cabinet, phone loose in his hand — in one of them the chair was angled differently than in the print it was photographing. Turned. Attentive. As if it had noticed him noticing.

He called his partner, Dez, and hung up before it rang, because what was he going to say? *Dez, the furniture in my photos is commuting?* Dez had already given him the look, two weeks ago, when he'd found Edmund on a job site photographing a client's ceiling for no reason he could articulate. *You've been weird since the Sable place,* Dez had said, not unkindly. *Weird like humming,* and Edmund had touched his own lips, because he had been humming, a tune with no name, five notes rising and one line that always stopped short, and he had no memory of learning it.

That was the part he circled back to, sitting on his kitchen floor at two in the morning with twenty-four photographs arranged above him like a timeline of his own annexation. The song. Chapter and verse, literally. Wren had found the hymnal with the last page torn out, and Agnes hummed the lullaby in her sleep and always stopped one line shy of the end, and the neighbor woman had said the verse existed only in living memory, passed mouth to mouth, and whoever completed the song invited the singer down. Down. Into the well. Into the water that reflected a kitchen that wasn't there.

Edmund had been humming it for weeks. Five notes rising. One line short.

He wasn't learning it. He told himself that. He was *catching* it, the way you catch a tune off a radio in a diner, and there was a difference, and the difference was everything, and the difference was also possibly nothing, because the thing in the well didn't need him to sing. It needed him to stay. The singing was for the ones who belonged by blood. For him — the outsider, the skeptic, the man whose entire identity was built on refusing to believe — it had a simpler mechanism. It just had to make the world stop making sense everywhere else, so that the Sable farmhouse became the only room in the universe where things still obeyed rules. Terrible rules. But rules. Order of a kind. A chair that arrives on schedule is still a schedule.

He looked at his kitchen — the tube light, the cracked plaster, the water stain shaped like nothing — and felt it going foreign on him. This was the collapse, and it wasn't loud. It was an audit. Line by line, the ledger of his certainties: *mice, moisture, coincidence, double exposure, sleep deprivation, stress, grief-adjacent fixation on a dying woman's house and her granddaughter's steady gray eyes.* Every entry he wrote, the chair in photograph twenty-two sat a little more comfortably at his table, as if the explanations were meals being set out for it.

Because that was the trap's true architecture, and he saw it now with terrible clarity: skepticism wasn't armor. Skepticism was a *vacancy*. A man who believed in nothing had nothing standing between himself and the thing that wanted believing. The believers had their wards, their prayers, their salt, their hymns with torn-out pages. Edmund had an invoice book and a firm handshake, and the well had looked at all that empty space inside him and thought: *furnished or unfurnished?*

Unfurnished. It would bring its own chair.

* * *

He slept, or something adjacent to sleep, in the bedroom with the door shut and a kitchen chair — his own folding one, dragged down the hall — jammed under the doorknob, and he woke at dawn feeling like a man who'd spent the night holding a conversation he couldn't remember. On his phone, in the notes app, in his own thumb's handwriting-speed, was a single line of text:

**it only needs the second signature. bring the papers.**

He stared at it for a long time. Then he did the most honest thing he'd done in twenty years of professional disbelief: he stopped disbelieving.

Not belief, exactly. Not kneeling, not praying, not any of the postures he'd privately pitied. Just — acceptance of the data. The data said: the chair was in his photographs. The data said: the chair got closer. The data said: the water in the Sable well had risen into the yard at dawn two days ago and reflected a kitchen with a table set for two, and Wren had called him with her voice level in the way voices get level when they're holding a lot of weight, and she'd said, *Edmund, it's setting places now. Plural.*

Plural. Him and her. The chair at his table, the chair at hers, the same chair probably, the way the same hand had made hundreds of prints descending the shaft. One household, distributed across two addresses, consolidating.

He showered, dressed, loaded the van out of pure muscle memory — flashlight, coveralls, the good camera, the invoice book he almost left and then brought anyway, because a man should die with his paperwork. Before he locked the apartment he stood in the kitchen doorway and looked at the corner where the chair lived in the photographs.

It wasn't there. Of course it wasn't there. It was never there when he looked directly; it existed only in the record, in the trace, in the evidence left behind — which was, he realized, exactly how he'd spent his career working. Reading droppings. Reading grease trails. Reading the mark the unseen thing leaves and inferring the shape of what made it. He had trained his whole life to perceive things exactly this way, and he'd thought he was doing it to *dismiss* them.

No. He'd been doing it to prepare.

"Okay," he said aloud, to the corner, to the well underneath the world, to whatever clerk maintained the files. "I read the sign. I'm coming."

His phone buzzed. Wren. He answered before the second ring.

"I found how the verse ends," she said. No hello. Her voice had that levelness again, but underneath it, something new — not fear. Resolve, packed tight as a charge. "Three sources. Half-memories. I've written it down. Edmund — I haven't said it out loud. Not one word. But Agnes signed yesterday, and the paper needs a second name, and the second name has to belong to the last resident."

"The deed makes the owner," he said. "And the owner closes the well."

"And the well decides who owns." A breath. "There's a chair in my photographs too. It's been at the head of the table since last night."

Edmund looked at the empty corner of his kitchen, at the space in the air where a ladder-back chair wore its pale seat like a face.

"I know," he said. "Mine came early."

Outside, far off toward the river flats, toward the old Sable acreage, something began — very faintly, carried on a wind that shouldn't have reached the city — to hum. Five notes, rising.

One line short.
