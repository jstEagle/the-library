## 6. What Grandfather Took

Ruthie Villalobos did not say *no* when Coral called on Thursday and asked, in the flattest voice she could manage, whether she could go through the attic boxes. What she said was: "Wear a mask. There's mouse." And then, after a silence long enough to hold a whole other conversation: "You'll want the light on the far side. The bulb near the stairs burns out if you look at it."

Coral wrote that down in her notebook without thinking, the way she logged callers. *The bulb near the stairs burns out if you look at it.* Her mother spoke in small superstitions the way other people spoke in weather. Coral had spent her whole childhood absorbing them without questioning where they came from, and it was only now—after the tapes, after Odile, after the concrete slab stamped with a mason's mark she'd known since she could hold a trowel—that the superstitions sounded less like quirks and more like instructions. Rules for living near something.

She drove out to Ruthie's on Saturday morning, the last Saturday of September, with the sleep tapes in a paper bag on the passenger seat. She hadn't listened to them in three days. She didn't need to. Odile's voice had moved into her head the way a song moves in—dry as paper, patient as paper, asking her polite terrible questions in the space between sleeping and waking. *Do you still have it?* Coral still didn't know what *it* was. But she knew where answers lived. They lived in attics. They lived in the parts of houses nobody went into on purpose.

The house on Fenwick Street was the house Coral had grown up in, and it smelled the way it always had—coffee, dust, the lemon oil Ruthie used on the banister like a religious observance. Her mother met her at the door in her gardening cardigan, kissed her cheek, and did not ask why Coral looked like she hadn't slept in a month, which was its own kind of answer.

"There's coffee," Ruthie said. "I'll be at the Hendersons' until four. Their daughter's home from Louisville."

"You could stay," Coral said. "We could go through it together."

Something crossed her mother's face—quick, gone, like a fish under ice. "I did my going-through," Ruthie said. "Thirty years ago. You do yours."

* * *

The attic ladder groaned the way it had always groaned, a complaint Coral's body knew by heart. She climbed into heat and dimness and the smell of forty years of stored air, and pulled the chain on the far bulb, and the light came on—steady, obedient, because she wasn't looking at it.

The attic was organized the way Ruthie organized everything: by category, by era, by an emotional logic Coral had never been able to reverse-engineer. Christmas in the corner by the vent. Coral's own childhood in labeled boxes—*schoolwork, 1984–89; toys, keep; toys, give away*. Her father's things, all six boxes of them, sealed since the funeral with tape gone amber. And along the north wall, under the sloped roof where you had to crouch, the boxes that had no labels at all.

That was where her grandfather lived, insofar as Emory Villalobos lived anywhere now. Dead since Coral was nine. A man who existed for her as a pair of hands—big, scarred, smelling of mortar—lifting her onto a workbench so she could watch him chip a design into wet concrete. A compass rose. He'd carved it into every slab he ever poured, he told her, so that even buried things would know which way was home.

She had thought, until last week, that this was a charming thing for a stonemason to say.

The unlabeled boxes were heavier than they should have been, or maybe that was the attic heat, or maybe it was the way her own pulse had picked up, a low drumming she felt in her ears like a caller holding on the line. She dragged the first one into the light and knelt over it, and the dust rose around her, and she thought, absurdly: *wear a mask, there's mouse,* and did not go back down for one.

The first box was tools. Trowels worn to silver crescents, a level, a chalk line in a rusted tin. Underneath the tools, wrapped in a feed-store calendar from 1973, a wooden mallet and a stamping die—the compass rose, cut into steel. She held it a moment. It was cold. The attic was ninety degrees and the die was cold, and she set it down with a care that felt like setting down something awake.

The second box was paper.

Newspaper clippings, mostly, in a rubber-banded brick. Coral untied the band the way you'd untie something that might be knotted for a reason, and the top clipping came free and unfolded in her hands, and the world went very quiet, the way it did on the board when a caller said something that stopped her pen.

**BELL CHILD STILL MISSING; SEARCH SUSPENDED, June 28, 1974.**

The photograph was grainy, half-toned into gray soup, but the girl in it was unmistakable. Thin face. Dark hair cut blunt at the jaw. Eyes that looked at the camera the way Odile's voice sounded on tape—like she was waiting for you to finish, because she already knew how it went.

*Odile Bell, 11, was last seen on the evening of June 9 near the family farm on Route 9. Sheriff's deputies have concluded the child likely wandered from the property. The search, which involved over 200 volunteers, was suspended Tuesday. The Bell family could not be reached for comment.*

Coral read it three times. The words didn't change. That was the horror of print; it didn't change, it didn't soften, it just lay there being true while you tried to find the seam in it.

*wandered from the property.* Odile's voice, dry as paper: *I didn't wander. I was put.*

Below the clipping, more clippings. Coral went through them one at a time, and the story they told was a story with holes in it, holes shaped exactly like a family. A piece from July 1974 about the Bells' farm equipment being auctioned. A piece from August, four sentences, about the family "relocating." Nothing after that. No follow-up. No anniversary piece. No *years later, questions remain.* In a county where a dairy fire got three articles and a bridge renaming got five, a missing child got one article and a silence, and the silence had Emory Villalobos's fingerprints all over it, because Coral was starting to understand that her grandfather had been the county's silencer. When you needed something buried—literally, structurally, permanently—you called the man who carved compass roses into the lids.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed it the way you notice rain starting, distantly, on someone else's roof.

Under the clippings was a folder, and in the folder, the ledger.

It was a hardware store ledger, the kind with the marbled cover and the columns, and the first twenty pages were ordinary—lumber, lime, a headstone for a Harkness, a headstone for a Pruitt, prices in a handwriting so neat it looked printed. Coral turned pages. The handwriting stayed neat. The entries stayed ordinary. And then, on a page dated June 11, 1974—two days after Odile Bell was last seen—her grandfather's hand changed. Not messier. Tighter. Smaller. The handwriting of a man writing carefully because he knew someone might someday read it, and wanted to be sure that what they read was nothing.

**June 11 — well work, R. Bell property — $400.**
**June 14 — well work, cap & seal — $250.**
**June 14 — gravel, extra — $60.**
**June 20 — well work, final — $400.**

And then, at the bottom of the page, in the margin, in the same tight hand, a single line that was not an entry at all:

**paid in full — do not reopen.**

$1,110. In 1974. Coral did the arithmetic twice because the first answer seemed impossible. Eleven hundred dollars was a roof. Was a year of her mother's grocery money. Was a fortune for a well cap, for a day and a half of masonry. Nobody paid that for concrete. You paid that for a job with conditions. You paid that for a man who could keep a secret and had already proven he could keep it in writing that looked like nothing.

*do not reopen.*

Coral sat back on her heels in the hot dark and heard her own breathing, and under her breathing, faint, from everywhere and nowhere—the attic vents, the wall cavities, maybe just the blood in her ears—a sound she had heard on forty-one nights of tape. Not a voice. A hum. Three notes, descending, patient. The melody from her dreams, the one she'd caught herself humming into the dead air between songs last Tuesday, live, on air, before she'd strangled it mid-phrase.

The attic hummed it back to her like a round.

* * *

She should have stopped there. She knew that the way you know a caller is about to say something you'll have to bleep. But there was one box left, smaller than the others, shoved into the deepest angle of the roof where the shingles came down low, and it was not a Villalobos box. It was a shoebox. A child's shoebox, the cardboard soft with age, with *BUSTER BROWN* faded on the side and a name written on the lid in pencil, in a child's careful cursive:

**odile**

Coral did not touch it for a long time. The attic held its breath with her. Somewhere below, the refrigerator cycled on, the ordinary sound of the living world going about its business, and it made the box on her lap more terrible, not less—the way a phone rings louder in a quiet house.

*Do you still have it?*

"No," she said out loud, to the attic, to the girl whose name was on the lid. "I don't have anything. I didn't even know—"

The pencil letters said otherwise. The pencil letters said the box had been in this family's keeping for fifty years, labeled in a dead child's handwriting, and nobody had ever written *return to sender.*

She opened it.

The christening gown came out first, folded around something, wrapped the way Ruthie wrapped the good dishes—cloth around cloth around the thing that mattered. It was fine work, cotton gone ivory with time, tiny pearl buttons down the front, a hem embroidered with leaves. It was a baby's gown, but it had been worn by someone bigger; the shoulders were stretched, the neck binding strained and restitched, hastily, by someone who needed the garment to fit a child of eleven and had only a gown for an infant. Someone had dressed a girl in a christening gown and sent her somewhere. Down, Coral's mind supplied, unasked. They sent her down.

Inside the gown, nested in the folds like a stone in a flower, was the bell.

It was a hand bell, brass gone dark, dark, darker than tarnish—dark like it had been underwater for fifty years, which, she understood now, it had. The handle was worn smooth in the shape of a grip. The mouth of it was rimmed with a design she recognized with a lurch that went all the way through her: a compass rose, stamped into the metal, the same mark Emory Villalobos had cut into every slab he ever poured.

*So even buried things would know which way was home.*

He'd stamped the bell. Or the bell had been stamped to match him, or he'd been chosen for the job because the mark was already his—Coral's mind skidded over the possibilities and refused all of them and refused, most of all, the one that was true: that her family's name was on this. That the mark she'd loved as a child, the compass rose, the *home* sign, was on the lid of a well a girl had been put into, and on the instrument they'd drowned with her, and that the whole county's silence had been mortared shut by Villalobos hands and paid for at eleven hundred dollars, *paid in full, do not reopen.*

The bell was cold in her palms. The attic was ninety degrees. The bell was cold as well water.

She didn't ring it. Some part of her, some animal part that had kept her alive through seven years of the dead calling in, understood with total clarity that ringing it here, in this house, over this town, would be like striking a match in a room she couldn't see the size of. She wrapped it back in the gown, fold for fold, the way it had been wrapped, and she put it in her bag—not the shoebox, not yet, but the bell and the gown together, because they went together, because someone had bound them into a single object fifty years ago and Coral was not going to be the one to undo that binding in a hot attic on a Saturday.

The ledger she took too. And the clippings. And the stamping die, after a moment's argument with herself, because if her grandfather's mark was on the well's cap, she wanted his mark in her hand when she went back to it.

* * *

She came down the ladder into the lemon-oil smell of the stairwell and her mother was standing at the bottom.

Ruthie was still in her gardening cardigan. The Hendersons' daughter wasn't due until four; it wasn't even one. Her mother had come home early, or had never really gone, and she was standing at the foot of the attic ladder with her hands folded the way she folded them in church, and she was looking at the bag on Coral's shoulder with an expression Coral had never seen on her face in thirty-one years of knowing her. It wasn't guilt. It was the thing that comes *before* guilt, the thing guilt is built on top of. It was recognition. Ruthie looked at the bag the way you look at a diagnosis you've been carrying for decades finally spoken out loud.

"You found the box," Ruthie said. Not a question.

"Mom—"

"Don't ring it." Her mother's voice came out level and quiet and absolutely certain, the voice she used for *wear a mask* and *the bulb burns out if you look at it.* "Whatever you do with all that, Coral. Whatever you decide you have to do." She stepped closer, and Coral saw that her hands, folded so neatly, were trembling. "Don't ring it in this house."

"Mom, whose box is that? Whose name is on the lid?"

Ruthie closed her eyes. When she opened them, she looked past Coral, up the ladder, into the dark at the top of the stairs, at the attic where she had done her own going-through thirty years ago, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing, a voice meant for a distance of inches, a voice from a church basement, from a kitchen at night, from 1974.

"Her mother made that gown for her christening," Ruthie said. "I held it once. Before." She swallowed. "You'll want to sit down for the rest. And Coral—" her mother's hand came up, and touched the bag, and did not open it, and Coral felt the cold of the bell through the canvas like a palm pressed flat against her side, "—there's a song that goes with it. And I need to know who taught it to you. Because it wasn't me. And it wasn't your grandfather. And if you already know it—" Ruthie's voice finally broke, fifty years late, "—then it's already too late to pretend we never heard it."

Outside, a car passed on Fenwick Street, ordinary, living, gone. In the bag, against Coral's hip, the bell waited, cold and patient, dry as paper.

*Do you still have it?*

"Yes," Coral said, to her mother, to the attic, to the girl under the water. "I have it."
