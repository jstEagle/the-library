## 8. The Second Voice Answers Back

Coral didn't sleep that night. She sat at her kitchen table with the recorder between two mugs of cold coffee, one for her and one for nobody, because seven years of hosting a show alone had taught her that a second cup made the silence feel like company.

The tape from last night was still in the machine. She hadn't listened to it yet. She'd told herself she would listen in the morning, with daylight on her side of the window, but morning had come and gone and now it was four p.m. and the light through the blinds was going amber and thin, and she was out of reasons to wait.

She pressed play.

First there was the sound of her own breathing, slow and even, the breathing of someone genuinely asleep. That was the part that always unsettled her most—not the other voice, not yet, but the proof of how completely absent she was from her own bed. She sounded like a stranger sleeping in her apartment. She sounded like a guest.

Then, faintly, the voice. Dry as paper dragged across paper. It came from somewhere near the foot of the bed, or under it, or—she'd stopped trying to map its location, because every time she tried, the mapping felt like an invitation.

*"Do you want to know something?"* the voice asked.

And Coral, asleep, answered: *"Yes."*

She remembered none of this. She had lain in that bed all night and remembered nothing, not a dream, not a flicker. But on the tape her own voice was clear and awake-sounding, patient, almost warm—the voice she used on callers at 2 a.m., the voice that said *I'm listening, go ahead.*

The voice said: *"Ask me."*

And Coral's sleeping mouth said—and this was the moment Coral's hand went flat against the table, steadying herself—"What do you want?"

But that wasn't what happened next. What happened next was this:

*"Why did you stop using your name?"*

Coral stopped the tape.

The kitchen was very quiet. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere down the block a dog barked twice and gave up. She sat with her thumb on the stop button and looked at the little spools through the plastic window, at the brown ribbon of her own nights wound tight around them, and she thought, with the strange calm that sometimes arrives just before panic: *I never asked that question out loud.*

She hadn't asked it anywhere. Not aloud, not written down, not whispered into a pillow. She had asked it only once, in the place where she kept everything she refused to say—the locked room behind her sternum, the one with no door, only weight. She had asked it in her head, lying awake at three in the morning two weeks ago, staring at the ceiling after the third call about funerals: *Why did I stop being her? Why did I bury that girl so deep even I can't find the grave?*

She had asked it only in her head.

She pressed play again.

On the tape, her sleeping self was silent for a long time. Long enough that Coral checked the counter—forty seconds of nothing but breath and the faint tick of the radiator. Then the dry voice spoke again, gently, the way you speak to someone standing on a ledge.

*"You didn't stop,"* it said. *"You were stopped. There's a difference. Would you like me to say it? Your name?"*

"No," Coral said aloud, to the empty kitchen, to the machine, to no one who could hear her.

On the tape, her sleeping self said: "Yes."

* * *

She made herself listen to the rest before she let herself do anything else—before she could pour a drink, before she could call her therapist's after-hours line and hang up before it rang, before she could drive to the station and burn the archive tapes in the parking lot like a woman in a movie. She made herself sit still and listen, because she had spent seven years practicing denial the way other people practice piano, and she knew every shortcut by heart, and she was tired of being fluent in it.

The voice said the name.

It said it slowly, with care, the way you handle something fragile that has been wrapped up a long time—unfolding cloth, unfolding more cloth, and then the small bright thing underneath.

It said: *"Marisol."*

Coral's whole body went cold, then hot, then cold again, like water running through pipes in an old house. Marisol Villalobos. Nineteen years old, a duffel bag, a bus ticket bought with tip money from the diner, a new name chosen off a list of names she'd liked the sound of—*Coral*, because it was pretty and blank and belonged to no one, because it sounded like a stone you could hide behind. She had left Marisol on the curb outside her mother's house like furniture too heavy to move, and she had never once, in fourteen years, said the name aloud. Not to Ruthie. Not to anyone. Some nights she couldn't even hold it in her head; it slipped away from her like a fish, and she'd let it go, relieved.

And now a dead girl's voice had lifted it out of her chest and set it on the tape like a specimen on a slide.

On the recording, her sleeping self made a sound. A small sound. Coral leaned closer to the speaker, hating herself for leaning, and identified it: it was a sob, but a muffled one, the kind you smother in a pillow so the house won't hear. Except the house had heard. Something had heard. Something had been sitting at the foot of her bed taking notes.

*"Thank you,"* the voice said, and it sounded—this was the worst part, this was the part Coral would carry around for days like a stone in her shoe—it sounded *grateful*. Satisfied. Like a lock turning. Like a debt recorded in a ledger.

Then her sleeping self spoke one more time, and her voice was different now. Thinner. Younger. It was, Coral realized with a lurch, the voice she'd had at nineteen, the voice from the bus station, the voice of the girl on the curb.

"Who are you?" that young voice asked.

"You know who I am," the dry voice said. "You've known since the first call. You logged me as a prank because logging is easier than answering."

"I don't know what you want."

"Yes, you do." A pause, long enough for the tape hiss to fill like water filling a boot. "I want what your family took. I want it back. And I want you to stop pretending you were never here, Marisol, because pretending is how they got away with it, and pretending is how it keeps working, and I have been under the water for fifty years while everyone above me practiced their explanations."

Coral stopped the tape again. Her hands were shaking badly enough that the button clicked twice before it caught.

*Under the water.* She turned the phrase over. In the first tapes, weeks ago, the voice had only said *thank you*, only asked polite terrible questions—*do you still have it, are they still keeping it, does anyone remember the tune.* Coral had built a theory out of those fragments, a theory she could live inside: a prankster, a historian, a woman whose family had some old grievance with Coral's grandfather's masonry work, someone doing research, someone cruel. The theory had walls and a roof and she had lived in it comfortably.

But *under the water* did not fit in the theory. *Under the water* put a crack right down the middle of it, and through the crack Coral could see the thing she'd been refusing to look at since the first funeral caller described the spray of carnations on his own casket: that the voice was not doing an impression of a dead person. The voice was a dead person. Or it was something wearing a dead person the way Coral wore her invented name—completely, patiently, with nowhere else to be.

She rewound. She played the name again. *Marisol.* She played it five times, telling herself she was checking for editing artifacts, for splice points, for any sign of trickery, when really she was doing what she always did at 2 a.m. with impossible callers: buying time with procedure.

There were no splice points. There was only her own sleeping voice saying yes to questions no one had asked out loud, and a dead girl's voice knowing the name Coral had buried deeper than the well.

* * *

She called in sick to the station. Dev took it without argument, which frightened her more than an argument would have—he'd been odd lately, distracted, carrying printouts he angled away from her when she passed his office. She'd ask him about it later. Later. Everything was later now; her whole life had become a corridor of doors marked *later*.

Instead she drove to the county library, because if the voice knew her name, then the voice knew everything, and Coral needed to know things too. She needed the ledger balanced. She needed to be holding at least as much as the thing under her bed was holding.

The local history room was on the basement level, carpeted in industrial green, lit by fluorescent tubes that buzzed at a frequency just below headache. The librarian, a retired schoolteacher named Mrs. Okafor who had listened to Coral's show for years, nodded at her without surprise. Everyone in town listened to the show; that was the joke Coral used to make. Now it didn't feel like a joke. Now it felt like a congregation.

The Bell file was thin. Thinner than thin—it was a single folder in a drawer of fat folders, and when Coral opened it, she understood why the librarian kept it face-down in the back. Inside: a June 1974 newspaper clipping, yellowed and brittle, headlined SEARCH SUSPENDED FOR MISSING BELL CHILD. A photograph of a girl, eleven or so, gap-toothed, squinting into summer sun. Dark hair cut blunt at the chin. A dress with a white collar.

Odile Bell. Missing since June 1974. The article ran eleven column inches and mentioned, in its final paragraph, that the family had "declined further comment" and that neighbors described the Bells as "private." Then nothing. No follow-up articles. No anniversary pieces. No missing-persons updates. Coral searched the microfilm for six hours, scrolling through July, August, September of 1974, and the town's newspaper simply—continued. Festivals. Zoning disputes. A new traffic light on Route 9. As if the girl had been subtracted rather than lost, divided out of the record entirely, and everyone had agreed to the arithmetic.

That was when she found the second strangeness, the one that made her sit back from the microfilm reader with her heart tapping at her ribs. Her grandfather's name appeared in the paper that August—not in connection with Odile, never in connection with Odile—but in a routine notice: R. VILLALOBOS MASONRY AWARDED COUNTY WELL-SEALING CONTRACT. Dated six weeks after the search was suspended. Six weeks after a child went missing on a farm with a well.

Her grandfather had sealed the well. Her grandfather had been paid to seal the well. And Coral had grown up eating dinners at that man's table, learning to point a trowel, hearing him say a hundred times, in his cheerful gravel voice: *A job done right is a job you never have to think about again.*

She had thought he meant stonework.

* * *

She got home after dark. She did not turn on the lights. She stood in her living room in the blue glow of the streetlamp through the blinds and looked at the recorder on the kitchen table, and she understood that she had a choice, and that she had already made it, and that the choice was to press play.

Not the sleep tape. A fresh one. If the voice could answer questions she asked only in her head, then she wanted to conduct the interview properly. On her terms. Awake.

She set the recorder running on the table. She pulled a chair up close to it, close enough that her knees touched the table leg, and she folded her hands like a host introducing a segment, because ritual was the only armor she owned.

"I'm awake," she said aloud. "I know you can hear me. You've been hearing me for weeks—more than that. Longer than the tapes." Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "So let's do this honestly. I'll think my questions. You answer them. And we'll see what the tape says in the morning."

Silence. The refrigerator hummed. The radiator ticked. She sat there for an hour, thinking questions into the air—*what do you want, who sealed the well, what was taken, why me*—and heard nothing, felt nothing, and finally went to bed feeling like a madwoman who had at least committed fully to the bit.

In the morning, the tape was full.

She played it over coffee she didn't drink, and the dry voice moved through the answers one by one, unhurried, thorough, like a witness giving a deposition it had rehearsed for fifty years.

Asked in her head—*are you real*—the tape said: *"As real as anything your family signed their name to."*

Asked—*what was taken*—the tape said: *"A bell. Cast for our church, blessed for our family, rung at every christening and every funeral on our land for sixty years. They said it was cursed. What they meant was it rang true, and they didn't like what it said about them. They drowned it in our well with me on top of it, and they paid a mason to make sure it stayed down, and the mason took the money, and the money fed his family, and his family became your family, and here we are."*

Coral's coffee went cold in her hand. She thought of the attic she hadn't searched yet, her mother's house, the boxes she'd been meaning to go through for years. She thought of her grandfather's hands, which had been gentle hands, hands that fixed things.

Asked—*why me*—the tape said: *"Because the debt runs to whoever holds the secret, and you hold it now. You've held it since you were born into that name. And because you, of all of them, know what it is to be erased. You did it to yourself, but you did it. So you understand. Don't you, Marisol? You understand better than anyone alive what it costs to be unwritten."*

And then, last, the question Coral had saved for the end, the one she'd held in her head like a coal, the one she'd almost not dared to think: *Is that really my name? Am I still her?*

The tape hissed. And then the voice, softer than she had ever heard it, almost kind, said:

*"Come to the well and find out."*

Coral sat in the gray morning light with the tape spinning past the end of the interview, hiss and silence, hiss and silence, and she reached for the phone to call Dev—because whatever was happening, it had gotten too big for one woman and a recorder, and Dev was the only person in her life who believed her without needing convincing—and the phone rang under her hand before she could lift it.

Dev's voice, when she picked up, was careful in a way she'd never heard from him. Careful the way people are careful around a fire.

"Coral," he said. "I need you to come in today. There's something wrong with your employment file, and I think—I think you're going to want to see it yourself."

Behind him, faintly, through the receiver, Coral heard the station's phone lines lighting up. Callers waiting. Dozens of them, maybe more, holding on the line before the show had even started.

All of them, Dev said quietly, asking about the girl under the water.
