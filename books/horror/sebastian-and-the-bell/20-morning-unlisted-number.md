## 20. Morning, Unlisted Number

Dev had never pulled an all-nighter that ended with him standing at the edge of a hole in the ground at five in the morning, holding a rope that went down into the dark like a vein.

The rope was his idea. The rope was the only thing he'd contributed to any of this, and he clung to that fact the way a drowning man clings to driftwood—because it was the only piece of the night he could be proud of. He'd found it in the bed of Ruthie's pickup, coiled under a tarp that smelled of fertilizer and forty years of farm dust, and he'd tested it against the bumper of his own car with his whole body weight before he'd let anyone tie it around anything.

"Sixty feet," Ruthie had said, watching him test it. Her voice was flat as a road map. "That's what they said in '74. Sixty feet and then water."

"And now?"

"Now I don't know."

Now, standing on the cracked slab with the sky going gray behind the tree line, Dev knew. The well was dry.

He could hear it being dry. All night the ground under Route 9 had made a sound he had no word for—a deep, swallowing gurgle, like a drain the size of a county—and then, somewhere around four-thirty, it had simply stopped. Not faded. Stopped, the way a held breath stops. And the wetness that had been seeping up through the concrete all evening, pooling around their boots in a thin black mirror, had begun to retreat. By first light the slab was dry except for one dark seam running corner to corner, clean as a fracture in bone.

Coral was down there. In the dry.

He hauled the rope hand over hand, and it came up lighter than it should have, and every foot of that lightness was a prayer he didn't know how to say.

* * *

He'd thrown the rope down at 4:51. He knew because he'd been checking his phone obsessively, not for the time but for signal—one bar, half a bar, no bars—the way you check a pulse on someone who's already flatlined. There was no calling anyone. There was no one to call. Who do you dial when the emergency is fifty years old? The sheriff's office would want names, reports, a reason why a radio host and her mother and her station manager were standing on private property at midnight prying up a slab stamped with a dead man's masonry mark. And Dev had seen enough impossible paperwork this week to know that whatever they wrote down would rot by morning anyway.

So it was just them. Ruthie on the slab, kneeling, both palms flat against the concrete like she was taking its temperature. Dev with the rope. Coral's flashlight beam cutting down into the shaft, catching nothing but stone and the wet gleam of old iron rungs bolted into the wall, descending past where the light wanted to go.

"I'm going," Coral had said. She said it the way she said everything on air—calm, level, pitched for a listener who might be fragile. But her hands were shaking so hard the flashlight chattered against the rocks.

"Coral." Ruthie grabbed her wrist. "Coral—" and then she said the other name. The real one. The one Dev had found on the yellowed application in the station's basement files three days ago, the name that had unraveled seven years of Coral Villalobos like a pulled thread. She said it soft, the way you say a thing you've kept locked in your mouth since childhood.

Coral looked at her mother for a long moment. Then she said, "If I don't come up, don't come down after me. Promise me. It doesn't work if more people go in."

"That's not how mothers work."

"It's how this one has to." And she'd smiled—not the radio smile, something smaller and truer—and stepped off the edge into the dark, ringing the bell as she went.

The sound of it going down was the worst thing Dev had ever heard. Not because it was frightening, though it was—it rang once, twice, a third time, each peal smaller than the last, swallowed by stone—but because it sounded *welcomed*. Like the well had been waiting by the phone.

Then: silence. Then: the rope paying out through his gloves, slow, steady, thirty feet, forty, fifty. Then it stopped moving entirely, and stayed stopped, and the night got very long.

Ruthie prayed. Dev hadn't known she was religious until he heard her doing it—not church prayers, not exactly, but a low running mutter of names, some of which he recognized from the parish directory and some of which he didn't, and underneath it all, over and over, *let her come back, let her come back, we paid enough, God, we paid enough.*

At 4:51 he'd felt the rope twitch.

Not a signal. A weight change. The line went slack in a slow, sliding way, like something at the bottom had let go—or been let go of—and Dev's whole body understood before his mind did. He threw himself backward onto the slab and hauled.

* * *

She came up over the lip of the well at dawn, gray-handed and gray-lipped, hair plastered to her skull, and Dev caught her under the arms and dragged her onto the concrete and thought, absurdly, *she weighs less.* Not dramatically, not impossibly—just less, the way a suitcase weighs less after you've taken out the books. As if she'd left something down there besides a bell and a gown and a ledger.

Her eyes were open. That was the first good sign. They were fixed on the brightening sky with an expression Dev had spent twenty years in radio learning to read across a studio glass, and what he saw on her face was not trauma, exactly. It was closer to the look of someone stepping outside after a long illness and finding the air still worked.

"Coral," he said. "Hey. Hey. You're up. You're out."

She turned her head and looked at him, and for a second he thought she wasn't going to recognize him, and he braced for it—for the horror-movie version, the blank stare, the stranger wearing his friend's face. Instead she said, "You threw the rope."

"You said six weeks. You gave yourself six weeks and then went down with one night to spare. Of course I threw the rope."

"Thank you." Her voice was wrecked—hoarse, water-scoured, like she'd been shouting underwater. "Dev. Thank you."

Ruthie reached them then and gathered her daughter in, and Dev turned away to give them the moment and found himself looking down into the well instead.

It was dry. Completely, impossibly dry. His phone flashlight showed sixty feet of stone going down to a floor of packed earth and river stones, and not one drop of water anywhere, and the walls were streaked with mineral lines marking every level the water had ever stood at over a century and a half, like the rings of a felled tree. At the bottom, arranged with a neatness that raised the hair on his neck, lay the christening gown, folded. Empty. No bell beside it. No ledger. Just the gown, folded small, the way you'd fold something you meant to keep.

And the slab itself—he turned and looked at it properly for the first time in full light. The crack ran corner to corner, straight through his grandfather-in-law's masonry stamp, splitting the old man's mark in two. Not broken by force. Cracked the way an egg cracks—from inside.

"Ruthie," he said quietly. "Come look at this."

But Ruthie was holding her daughter and crying in a way that sounded like it had been queued up since 1974, and Dev decided the geology could wait.

* * *

They sat in Ruthie's truck with the heater running while the sun came up proper, the three of them crammed into a cab built for two, steaming like laundry. Dev had a thermos of coffee that had gone cold hours ago; nobody drank it. Coral sat between them wrapped in a moving blanket from the truck bed, shivering in waves, and told them almost nothing.

"There was a girl," she said. "There was a girl, and she'd been waiting a long time, and now she isn't."

"Is she—" Ruthie started.

"Gone. Resting. Whatever word you want." Coral stared out the windshield at the ruined farmhouse, the collapsed barn, the sumac growing up through the porch. "She showed me what she would have been. If they hadn't. She was eleven and she was also seventy-something, both at once, and neither one was a lie." Her hands tightened on the blanket. "The town took fifty years of her life and put it in the well like coins in a fountain. She just wanted it back. Or—no. She wanted it *witnessed*. Those are different things. I understand that now. Nobody ever witnesses a drowned girl. They witness a missing girl, and then they stop."

Dev said nothing. He was thinking about call logs.

Because here was the thing he hadn't told her yet, the thing he'd found at three in the morning while waiting for dawn with his phone dying in his hand: he'd pulled the station's archived caller records—all seven years of them, or what remained of them after the great server migration of 2019 ate half the archive. And the numbers were dead. Not disconnected-dead, not moved-and-reassigned dead. *Dead* dead. He'd cross-referenced nineteen of them against obituaries, and nineteen callers had died before they ever called—some years before, one of them forty-one years before. A man named Hollis Greer had phoned in last March to describe his own funeral, and Hollis Greer's obituary ran in the *Courier* in November 1988.

Except.

He checked again at 3 a.m., sitting in his car outside the Bell property with the engine ticking, and this time the numbers weren't just dead. They weren't numbers anymore. He dialed Hollis Greer's number and got the three-tone message: *the number you have dialed is not in service.* Fine. Normal. But then he'd checked the station's own logs, the digital ones, the ones that auto-populate from the phone system—and the entries were blanking. Not deleted. *Blanking*, in real time, field by field, like frost melting off a window in reverse. Caller ID fields emptying themselves. Timestamps drifting. By the time he'd driven out here, the entire seven-year archive of dead callers had resolved to a single line item repeated across hundreds of nights:

**CALLER: UNKNOWN — NO NUMBER ON FILE**

As if the calls had never come from anywhere at all.

He should have been terrified. Some part of him noted, distantly, that he probably would be terrified later, once he'd slept. But mostly what he felt was relief—huge, embarrassing, physical relief—because if the numbers were unwriting themselves, then it was over. Whatever contract the town had signed in a church basement in 1974, whatever debt had been accruing interest through his friend's headphones for seven years, the account was closed. The dead had gotten what they came for. They were hanging up.

"Dev." Coral was looking at him. "You've got your manager face on. What did you find?"

He hesitated. Old habit—protect the talent, manage the story, keep the ugly machinery behind the glass. But he'd watched her climb out of a dry well at dawn. The glass was broken.

"The call log," he said. "All those callers. Seven years of them. Their numbers are gone—not disconnected, *gone*. The system doesn't even show them as calls anymore. It's like the phone company forgot they happened." He rubbed his eyes. "I checked one of them this morning. Dialed it. Not in service—which, fine, dead people's numbers get recycled. But I checked the same number Tuesday and it rang through to a voicemail box that was still accepting messages. Things are... closing. Tying themselves off." He looked at her. "Is that what it feels like from your side? Things tying off?"

Coral was quiet for a long moment. The heater ticked. Somewhere down the road a rooster was making its idiot morning announcement, and the sheer normalcy of it nearly made Dev laugh.

"My lease," she said finally. "I called the property manager from the car yesterday. To ask about breaking my lease early. And she said—" Coral laughed, a small stunned sound. "She said I didn't have a lease. Said there was no record of me at that address at all. Said maybe I was thinking of a different building. Dev, I've lived there four years. I have a *key*."

"Yeah." He'd heard about that. "Your employment file too. My copy. It's—thinning. Your start date keeps moving. Yesterday it said 2017, which was already wrong, and this morning it says—" he shrugged helplessly— "it says a date before the station existed. Before *I* existed, practically. The paperwork's rotting. Everything you built to hide inside is coming apart."

"Good," Coral said.

Both of them looked at her.

"I mean it." She pushed the blanket off her shoulders, sat up straighter, and Dev saw that under the gray exhaustion there was something new in her posture—not defiance, nothing so loud. Steadiness. "I spent seven years building a person out of paper. Coral Villalobos. Good surname, no history, no debts. A woman who could sit in a studio at 2 a.m. and take calls from the dead and tell herself it was pranks, because pranks couldn't touch the real her, because there *was* no real her, just a name on a lease and a name on a payroll file and a voice on the radio." She flexed her fingers, like testing them. "The paper's burning. Fine. Let it burn. I'm still here. Turns out I'm the part that isn't paper."

Ruthie made a sound—half laugh, half sob—and took her daughter's hand.

"What about the name?" Dev asked carefully. "Your—the other one. Does it feel different now? Down there, you gave it away. Do you still..."

Coral considered the question with the seriousness she brought to everything, the seriousness he'd mistaken for coldness for two years before he understood it was armor.

"I remember it," she said. "That's the strange part. I expected it to be gone—spent, paid out, whatever. But I remember all of it. The whole childhood. The name, the street, the school, the smell of my grandmother's kitchen. It's all still in there." She paused. "It just doesn't fit anymore. It's like—" she searched— "you know when you go back to try on a coat from high school? It's *your* coat. Every button works. And you stand there in front of the mirror and think, huh. I can't believe I was ever this small." She shook her head slowly. "I'm not going to use it again. Not because I lost it. Because I outgrew it. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. Losing a name is a wound. Outgrowing one is—" she exhaled— "weather. Seasons. You don't mourn summer for happening."

"So what do we call you?" Ruthie asked, and her voice cracked right down the middle on it, because Dev realized she was asking about more than a name. She was asking *what are you to me now*, and everyone in the truck heard it.

Coral reached over and took her mother's hand.

"Mama," she said—using the word like a key turning in a lock Ruthie had kept oiled for fifty years— "you can call me whatever you've got. I answer to it. That's the whole trick I learned down there. Names aren't cages unless you live in one alone."

* * *

Dev drove them back into town because Ruthie's hands hadn't stopped shaking and Coral wasn't insured to operate heavy machinery, emotionally speaking. The morning was aggressively ordinary—sprinklers ticking across lawns, the bakery on Main putting out its sandwich board, a kid dragging a backpack bigger than himself toward the elementary school—and Dev watched both women in his rearview mirror absorb it, the plain miracle of a town that didn't know yet what had almost come due on it.

His phone buzzed at a red light. Station group chat. The overnight automation report, which he normally skimmed and deleted.

He didn't delete it.

**AUTOMATED LOG SUMMARY — LAST NIGHT'S BROADCAST (REPLAY FEED):**
**TOTAL LISTENER CALLS: 0**
**NOTE: This is the first zero-call overnight in station history. Previous minimum: 3 calls (2/14/2021).**
**SYSTEM NOTE: Legacy caller archive integrity check complete. 100% of pre-2024 caller records unrecoverable. Recommend purge of corrupted files.**

Zero calls. The dead had called every single night for seven years, and last night—the night Coral went down the well and gave it all back—they'd all hung up at once, and the phone system had already forgotten they'd ever existed.

Dev lowered the phone. In the mirror, Coral was watching him. She'd seen his face change. Of course she had; she read faces for a living.

"Well?" she said.

"They're done," he said. "For good. Zero calls last night. First time ever." He managed a grin that only wobbled a little. "Congratulations. You're officially just a late-night radio host again."

Coral laughed—really laughed, a rusty, disbelieving sound—and Ruthie joined her, and Dev drove them down Main Street into the ordinary morning, and it was only later, much later, after sleep and food and a long hot shower, sitting alone in his office with the door shut, that he let himself feel the other thing.

Because there'd been one more line in the automated report. One he hadn't mentioned. One he'd told himself was a glitch, a rounding error, the system being tired.

**SYSTEM NOTE: Employee record update pending. Current file for C. VILLALOBOS cannot be verified against payroll history. Recommend re-issuance of employment documentation under corrected identity. Field "LEGAL NAME" is blank. Please advise.**

The field was blank. Not wrong. Not frayed. Blank—like a form waiting to be filled in, like a coat rack with nothing on it, like a microphone switched on in an empty studio at 2 a.m., waiting to find out what the next voice would be.

Dev stared at it for a long time. Then he picked up his phone and typed the only advice he had:

**Leave it blank. She'll tell us what goes there herself. On air, probably. She'll want the whole town to hear it.**

He hit send. Outside his office window, the sun climbed over the water tower, and somewhere across town Coral Villalobos was sleeping in daylight for the first time in seven years—dreamless, he hoped, or if not dreamless, then at least dreaming in her own voice.

Two weeks to the next broadcast. He had a feeling she'd want the whole hour.
