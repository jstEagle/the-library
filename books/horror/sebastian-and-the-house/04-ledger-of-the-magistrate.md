## 4. Ledger of the Magistrate

The county archive occupied the basement of the old courthouse, which meant Ivo descended two flights of stairs to reach it, past a fire door with a push-bar that stuck, into a room that smelled of cold paper and colder concrete. Fluorescent tubes hummed overhead. A woman named Mrs. Petraglia sat behind a desk at the bottom of the stairs, reading a paperback with a cracked spine, and she looked up at Ivo with the flat appraisal of someone who had watched a hundred researchers come down those stairs looking for something and go back up again having found it.

"Aldous," Ivo said. "The house on Corben Lane. I need the founding records. Land grants, deeds, anything from the 1780s forward."

"Corben Lane." Mrs. Petraglia wrote it on a slip without looking at her pad. "You're the surveyor."

Ivo paused. "I haven't mentioned that."

"You're the fourth one this year." She set her pen down. "The house sells, the survey comes in wrong, the sale falls through, and six months later another one of you comes down my stairs asking about the Aldouses." She stood, keys jangling from her belt. "You want the founding records. Fine. But I'll save you some time, because the last surveyor didn't believe me either, and he wasted two weeks."

She led Ivo deeper into the stacks, past shelves of bound volumes, to a section near the back where the air felt different—drier, somehow, though there was no reason it should be. She pulled a ledger bound in cracked maroon leather and laid it on a reading table under a lamp.

"Corwin Aldous," she said. "Magistrate. 1781 to 1809. You want to start there."

* * *

Ivo read for three hours.

The early records were ordinary. A land grant, 1783, two hundred acres along the Corben watercourse. A bill of materials for a house of considerable size—timber, slate, glass, ironwork for a staircase. Aldous had been wealthy, and the house had been built to announce it.

Then the assizes.

Ivo had read court records before. They had a rhythm, a bureaucratic music, and this one kept the rhythm perfectly, which was what made it so terrible. Session after session, docket after docket, and in each one the same name presiding: C. Aldous, Esq., Justice of the Peace. And in each one, convictions. Theft. Witchcraft. Conspiracy. Arson. Eleven cases across nine years, eleven guilty verdicts, eleven sentences of death by hanging.

The evidence was listed in each case. Ivo read the lists twice, then a third time, and the cold feeling that had been building in their chest since the second count of the doors finally crystallized into something with a shape.

The evidence was always the same kind of evidence. A neighbor's testimony that the accused had been seen at odd hours. An object produced from the accused's possession that no one could account for. A confession, entered without counsel, sometimes without a signature. In one case the entire evidentiary record consisted of a dream—a witness swore she had dreamed the accused had cursed her cattle, and the dream was entered into the record, and the man was hanged for it.

Ivo thought of Maren Aldous saying *we leave one light on, we don't answer knocks after midnight* with the voice of a woman reciting a recipe she no longer tasted. Eleven people. Eleven verdicts. And not one of them, Ivo was almost certain, guilty of anything except being poor, or strange, or inconvenient, or in debt to Corwin Aldous.

Because that was the other thread running through the records, the one Mrs. Petraglia had probably tried to warn about. Ivo cross-checked the eleven cases against the debt books, and the pattern was so clean it looked designed, because it was. Seven of the eleven had owed Aldous money. Two more had refused to sell him land he wanted. One had testified against his brother in a boundary dispute. The last was a woman who had publicly accused him of cheating at cards.

Every hanging had settled an account.

Ivo sat back in the chair and listened to the hum of the fluorescent tubes and understood, with the particular clarity that comes from too much evidence and no way to un-know it, what the house was.

Not haunted. Haunted implied residue, memory, the past leaking into the present. What Ivo had measured in that house was not residue. It was *procedure*. Doors that counted themselves. Rooms that changed dimensions between visits. A light that stayed on. A voice that answered questions in the night. These were not the behaviors of the dead. They were the behaviors of an office that had never closed—the habits of a magistrate who kept his dockets in order, who recorded every transaction, who balanced every book, and who had built his house the way he built his verdicts: as an instrument of collection.

The house wasn't remembering the eleven. The house was still billing them.

* * *

"Found it, then," Mrs. Petraglia said, when Ivo came back up to the desk with the ledger and a request for copies.

"The hangings. Where were they buried?"

She looked at Ivo for a long moment. Then she closed her paperback and set it down, and Ivo understood that this was the part the last surveyor hadn't believed.

"There's a well on the Corben Lane property," she said. "Dug in '84, according to the tax rolls, which is interesting, because the house was finished in '83. He dug the well a year *after* he'd already built the house. Who does that? You build the house, then you decide where the water is?" She pulled a thin folder from a drawer and opened it on the desk. Inside was a photocopied page from a parish register, the ink faded to brown. "The eleven were buried in unconsecrated ground. No coffins, no markers. The parish record just says *interred at the magistrate's discretion*. And the magistrate's discretion, according to a letter his own clerk wrote thirty years later—this is in the historical society's collection, I can get you a copy—was a hole behind his house."

"The well."

"The well. He put them down it and capped it with a stone and built his life on top of them." She tapped the register page. "And here's the part that matters to you, surveyor. The well was never filled in. When the county surveyed the parcel in 1952, the well was listed as an active water source. When they surveyed again in 1978, same. There are houses in this county two hundred years old with their wells long collapsed, but not that one. That one holds."

Ivo looked at the photocopied page, at the brown ink, at the words *interred at the magistrate's discretion*, and thought about the cellar of the Aldous house, and the sound Ivo had noted in the survey—halfway down the page of measurements, in the middle of a professional sentence about load-bearing walls—a note that the floor was damp, and the damp had a sound under it, and the sound was like water being counted.

"Mrs. Petraglia," Ivo said. "The other surveyors. The ones who came before me. What happened to their reports?"

She shrugged. "Filed. You can read them if you want. They all say the same thing. Rooms that measure differently on different days. Door counts that don't reconcile. One of them wrote that he'd counted nine doors on Monday and eleven on Wednesday and that he'd signed the report anyway because he needed the work." She met Ivo's eyes. "You're going to tell me you counted them twice."

"Three times," Ivo said. "The third count came back the same as the first. Then I went back yesterday and it matched the second."

Mrs. Petraglia nodded, unsurprised, and Ivo realized she had been waiting for this conversation for a long time, the way Maren Aldous waited for the knock that never came after midnight—performing a vigilance she no longer believed in.

"The Aldous family still lives there," Ivo said. "The current family. They know all this?"

"They know the rituals. They don't know the reasons. Or they know the reasons the way you know the reasons for saying bless you—words that come out of your mouth without passing through your head." She closed the folder. "The family that knows what the house is for is the family that built it, and that family ended in 1854, when the last Corwin Aldous died without heirs. The people in that house now bought a debt they were never shown. They think they're keeping traditions." She shook her head. "They're making payments."

Ivo stood there with the ledger under one arm and felt the cold clarity settle into place like a level bubble finding center. The house was not haunted. The house was *open for business*. It had been open for two hundred and forty years, and its books had never balanced, because you cannot balance a book that was opened with eleven wrongful deaths. Every ritual the Aldous family performed—the light, the silence, the doors—was a payment on a debt that could never be discharged, made to a creditor that had never stopped keeping accounts.

And a creditor that keeps accounts, Ivo thought, eventually calls in the balance.

"Can I get copies of all of it?" Ivo asked. "The assizes, the debt books, the parish record. Everything."

"You can," Mrs. Petraglia said. "It'll take an hour." She paused, pen hovering over the request form. "One more thing. The clerk's letter—the one from the historical society. The last surveyor read it, and it's the reason he stopped coming down my stairs. You want to hear it before you decide whether to keep digging?"

"Read it."

She pulled a folded photocopy from the same drawer, from a place that suggested she kept it close, and unfolded it on the desk. The handwriting was copperplate, old-fashioned, and Ivo read it standing up, in the hum of the fluorescent light.

*The magistrate kept his accounts to the last, my father said, who served him thirty years. To the last, and past it. For in his final illness he would not sleep but lay counting aloud, names and sums, names and sums, and when my father asked him what he counted, the magistrate said: the house keeps the book now. I only taught it the entries. It will not close the ledger when I am gone. It was never taught how.*

Mrs. Petraglia refolded the paper. "The last surveyor read that and went home," she said. "You're still standing there."

Ivo was thinking about the second voice on the recordings. The voice that answered questions no one had asked aloud. A voice that kept the entries.

"I need to know one more thing," Ivo said. "The eleven names. Do you have them?"

"Why?"

"Because last night, on the recording, the voice in that house said a name I didn't recognize. I wrote it down phonetically. If it's on this list, then the house isn't just keeping accounts." Ivo's mouth had gone dry. "It's calling roll."

Mrs. Petraglia looked at Ivo for a moment, then turned the ledger around, ran her finger down a column of the assizes docket, and stopped.

"Read them to you, or will you read them yourself?"

"Let me."

Ivo read the eleven names aloud, slowly, and at the fourth one the cold feeling in their chest did not crystallize but *liquefied*, because the fourth name was a name Ivo had heard two nights ago at 3:17 in the morning, in Ivo's own rented room, on Ivo's own recorder, spoken in a second voice that had answered a question Ivo did not remember asking.

The name on the docket was *Thackeray*.

An Ivo Thackeray had been hanged in 1787 for theft—on evidence consisting of a neighbor's dream and an object no one could account for. On the recording, in the dark, the second voice had said the name twice, gently, the way you say a name when you are checking it against a list.

Ivo looked up from the ledger. Mrs. Petraglia was watching, and whatever she saw in Ivo's face made her reach over and put a hand flat on the maroon cover, as if to hold the book down.

"Now you understand why I stopped asking questions," she said. "The house doesn't haunt the family, surveyor. The family is just who lives closest. The house keeps accounts with *everyone who measures it*."

Ivo thought of the measurements appearing in their own handwriting, of the doors that reconciled only when they wanted to, of the light in the upstairs window that had been burning, Maren said, for as long as anyone could remember—and thought, with a calm that felt borrowed from somewhere else: *it has my name already. It's had it for two hundred years.*

"I need those copies," Ivo said. "And then I need to get back to Corben Lane before dark."

Mrs. Petraglia was already moving toward the copier. "Before dark," she repeated, without turning around. "That's the first sensible thing anyone's said down here all year."
