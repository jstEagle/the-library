## 6. Invisible No Longer

Ivo decided, on the fourth morning, to treat the house like any other job.

That was the trick. That had always been the trick. A surveyor's authority came from indifference—the willingness to walk through a stranger's grief with a tape measure and call it data. Ivo had appraised houses where children had died, where marriages had ended in the kitchen, where the walls still smelled of the last argument. You measured. You wrote it down. You left.

So: finish the schedule of condition, photograph the cellar, price the roof, and go. Four days of work compressed into one long morning. Maren Aldous had stopped meeting their eyes at breakfast, which suited them fine. Tobias hadn't come down at all. The youngest—Wren, seven years old, all elbows and questions—had taken to standing at the top of the stairs whenever Ivo passed, watching them with the flat patience of a cat at a mousehole.

"Are you counting us?" Wren asked that morning, as Ivo laced their boots in the hall.

"I'm counting rooms."

"That's not what she said."

Ivo looked up. "Who?"

But Wren was already gone, back up the stairs, and the question sat in Ivo's chest like a swallowed stone for the rest of the day.

* * *

The measurements were wrong again.

Ivo had learned to expect this—the east bedroom giving 4.2 meters on Monday and 3.8 on Tuesday—but this morning the discrepancies had a new quality. They weren't random anymore. They were converging. Every room was shrinking toward some common figure, some number the house wanted to be, and when Ivo plotted the values in their notebook the trendline pointed at a single measurement: 1.7 meters.

The height of a man hanging from a beam.

They told themselves it was coincidence. They told themselves a lot of things that week.

By noon Ivo had finished the outbuildings and started on the final interior pass, working room by room toward the cellar, ticking items off the schedule with the mechanical satisfaction of a job nearly done. The house was quiet in the way of a held breath. No doors moved. No lights flickered. It was almost—almost—cooperative, and that cooperation frightened Ivo more than the knocking ever had. A house that fights you is telling you something. A house that lets you finish is waiting for you to leave so it can keep what you've given it.

Because that was the thing Ivo couldn't stop thinking about: everything they'd written down. Every dimension, every defect, every crack and sag and settlement, transcribed in their own careful hand into the survey book. Nine days of observation. The house knew exactly what they'd seen. And now—

Now the notebook was heavier than it should have been.

Ivo noticed it on the landing, actually felt the difference before understanding it, the way you notice someone has slipped something into your coat pocket. They stopped under the dim bulb—Maren kept only one light burning per floor, always had—and opened the book.

The pages were thicker. Not torn, not added; *thicker*, as if the paper itself had been fed. And between Tuesday's entry and Wednesday's there was a page Ivo had never written.

It was in their handwriting.

That was the first thing, and the worst thing—not the content but the hand, the particular forward slant of their numerals, the way they crossed their sevens. Ivo stared at it for a long time, the way you stare at your own face in a window at night, familiar and wrong. Then they read it.

*East range, second pass. Corridor measures 11.4 m. Eleven doors counted. Eleven doors counted again. Eleven doors counted again. The corridor does not end until the count is satisfied.*

Ivo had counted that corridor once. Seven doors. They remembered it clearly.

They flipped forward. Another page. Another entry, dated tomorrow.

*Cellar. Water at the third step. The well is taking an interest.*

Ivo closed the book. Stood very still on the landing. Told themselves, in the professional voice they used for clients and for panic, that they had written these entries and forgotten—that sleep deprivation did strange things, that the recordings had gotten into their head, that memory was a corrupt file and nothing more.

Then they heard, faintly, from the room behind them, the sound of a pencil moving across paper.

* * *

They didn't open the door. They want credit for that, later—they didn't open the door. They went downstairs instead, out the front, around to the yard, and sat on the low stone wall by the dead garden with the notebook open on their knees and the autumn sun doing its best and failing.

Read it properly, then. All of it. Know what the house knows.

The entries continued past tomorrow. Past next week. Dates Ivo recognized and dates Ivo didn't, marching forward through October into November, each one in their own hand, each one precise, each one describing a house that was slowly, methodically, becoming correct. Rooms settling to their true dimensions. Doors arriving at their proper number. Water rising by increments, noted in centimeters, dated like tide tables.

And threaded through the measurements, in the margins, small annotations Ivo had not made:

*He counts well.*
*He asks good questions.*
*Not invisible.*

The last one was underlined twice.

Ivo understood then what they had been refusing to understand since the first shifted wall. This was not haunting. Haunting was residue, was echo, was the past leaking through the plaster. This was attention. The house had been watching them the way an auditor watches a ledger, and somewhere in the last nine days—Ivo could not say when, could not name the moment, though they suspected the corridor, suspected the eleven doors—the house had decided they were not a visitor.

Visitors get measured. Residents get entered.

They turned to the back of the book, where the blank pages lived, the ones no surveyor ever fills. There was writing there too. Old writing, brown ink, a copperplate hand that belonged to another century—a list of names, columns of figures beside them, dates. An accounting. Ivo read the names without meaning to, without wanting to, and found Aldous at the top, Aldous repeated down the years like a refrain, each generation paying out its line item.

And at the bottom of the page, in fresh ink, in Ivo's own handwriting, dated today:

*Ivo Thackeray — entered.*

No figure beside it yet. No amount owing. Just the name, recorded, awaiting assessment.

The pencil sound upstairs had stopped. In its place, from deep below the floorboards—from beneath the house, from beneath the ground the house stood on—came a long, slow exhalation, like water finding a lower level, like something settling comfortably into a chair it intended to keep.

Ivo packed the notebook away with hands that almost worked, walked to the gate, and stood there looking at the lane beyond it. The lane ran downhill toward the village, toward the archive, toward anywhere. It was a quarter past three. There were six hours of daylight left. A bus passed the crossroads at four if you hurried.

They thought about the road. They thought about Maren's face at breakfast, the way she watched the windows during meals, the way she never once looked at the front door. They thought about the audio files on their laptop, the second voice answering questions Ivo didn't remember asking, and about the question it had answered most often, the one that recurred across nine nights of tape like a chorus:

*Which one goes in the well?*

They thought about Wren, standing at the top of the stairs. *Are you counting us?*

Ivo Thackeray turned around, walked back up the path, and let themselves into the house. The door closed behind them with the soft, satisfied click of a page being turned.

In the hall, the light was already burning for evening. On the newel post, in chalk, in a child's uneven hand, someone had written a number.

Eleven.

And beneath it, smaller, newer, added since morning:

Twelve.
