## 7. Duty Calls Collect

*Vesper*

The room did not have a clock, which was the first mercy and the first cruelty. Without a clock you couldn't count how long you'd been inside; without a clock the room could pretend it had always held you.

I had been in it — I estimated — two days. My phone showed no signal and no time, just a white screen that reflected my face back at me like a tiny, useless mirror. I'd stopped looking at it. The big mirrors were bad enough.

The room was exactly as I'd seen it through the glass: windowless, wallpapered in a pattern of faded pomegranates, lit from nowhere at all. The song lived in the walls here the way blood lives in a body — not coming from anywhere, simply present, circulating. Up close I could hear words in it now, or almost-words, syllables worn smooth as river stones by decades of repetition. A woman's voice led. Others followed, thinner, like breath drawn through keyholes.

And Ada Halvard stood near the wall with her hands folded, watching me the way you watch weather.

"You should sit," she said. Her voice was paper-dry and kind. "Standing makes the hours longer."

"I don't think there are hours."

"There aren't," she agreed. "That's why standing makes them longer."

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, but it came out, and several of the faded figures along the walls turned toward the sound with an eagerness that made my skin crawl. They hadn't heard laughter in a long time. I resolved to ration mine.

The others — there were nine, or eleven; they overlapped when I wasn't looking directly at them — kept their distance out of what I chose to interpret as courtesy. They were translucent the way old photographs are translucent, light passing through them and coming out the other side diminished. A man in shirtsleeves. Two children holding hands, which I refused to think about too hard because thinking about it too hard would end me. An elderly woman who hummed harmony to everything, including my breathing.

Ada was the only one who spoke.

"Your employer will send someone," she said on what I decided was the second day. She said it gently, the way you tell someone their flight has been delayed. "They always do. The house is on a schedule. Schedules have people behind them."

"Dana," I said. "Her name will be Dana Okafor." I'd read the email chain before the mirrors took me. My replacement. Sent because I'd missed three check-ins, because the firm's liability department had a policy about surveyors who go quiet inside condemned properties, because duty, like debt, always calls collect.

"You know her?"

"I've never met her. I know her invoice rate and her safety certification." I rubbed my face. "That's more than I knew about you, before."

Ada smiled at that, faintly. "You knew me better than most. You read my diary."

"I read four pages."

"Then you know we didn't want to do it." Her hands tightened on each other. "Knowing and wanting are different rooms in the same house. We sealed the door between them and called it peace."

Before I could answer — before I could ask the question I'd been carrying since chapter one of this disaster, *who exactly did you put in this room, Ada, say the name* — the mirror on the far wall shivered.

Not broke. Shivered, like water touched by a fingertip. And in it, gray and grainy and unmistakable: the hallway outside. The real hallway. And walking down it, clipboard first, high-vis vest catching the gray light —

"Oh no," I said.

* * *

*Dana*

The Okafor family rule was simple: never take a job where the previous guy disappeared. Dana's father had learned it on oil rigs, her mother had learned it in insurance adjusting, and Dana had learned it twice over in her own career, once in Lagos and once in Detroit, and both times the rule had saved her from something she still couldn't name in polite company.

So naturally, when Halvord & Finch offered her triple rate to finish an appraisal on a house where the last surveyor had gone silent for seventy-two hours, she took it.

"It's a paperwork problem," the office manager had said. "Eriksen probably dropped their phone in a toilet. Just walk the property, photograph the structural issues, file the report. In and out in an afternoon."

The house did not look like an afternoon. It looked like a dare.

Dana stood at the front gate for a full minute, doing the thing her mother called *taking attendance*: counting exits, noting sightlines, checking her phone signal. Two bars. Good enough. She let herself in.

Inside, the cold hit first — not draft-cold but *held* cold, the way a church holds cold, air that had been cold so long it had developed opinions. She photographed the entryway out of habit. Cracked plaster. Water staining shaped, if you squinted, like a hand.

"Hello?" she called, professional-bright. "Vesper Eriksen? Halvord & Finch sent me."

Nothing answered. But the house *listened*. She felt it the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder — a pressure on the back of the neck, attention without a body.

She worked anyway. That was the other rule: work until the fear gets bored. Hallway, parlor, staircase, each room documented with brisk efficiency. She talked to herself the whole time, narrating her shots, because silence in empty houses was how horror movies started.

It was in the upstairs corridor that she heard the singing.

It came from behind a wall that, according to the blueprints on her clipboard, contained nothing but joists and dead air. A woman's voice, low and patient, singing something that sounded like a hymn that had been left out in the rain — all the sharp edges washed away, only the longing left. Other voices underneath it. Children's voices.

Dana lowered her camera.

Every rule she owned lined up in formation. *Never follow the sound. Never open the door that isn't on the plans. Never take a job where the previous guy disappeared.*

But the previous guy might be *behind that wall.* Might be hurt, trapped, dehydrated, singing to keep themselves calm. Seventy-two hours was a long time without water. Dana Okafor had not become the kind of person who walked away from a voice behind a wall, and she wasn't going to start in some rich man's rotting house.

She found the seam of the door under forty years of wallpaper — a vertical line, a frame's ghost. No handle. She pressed her palm flat against it.

The singing stopped.

All of it, at once, mid-phrase, like a congregation turning to look at her through the wood.

"Hello?" she said again, quieter now. "Is someone in there? I'm with the surveying company. If you're hurt, I can get help."

From the other side of the wall, very close, very clear, a voice said:

"*Don't touch the door.*"

It was a man's voice. Hoarse. Familiar in the way voices from voicemails are familiar. Dana frowned.

"Eriksen?"

"Don't touch the door, Dana. Please. Step back from the wall."

"How — " She looked at the wall, then at her phone, then at the wall again. "Where are you? How do you know my name?"

A pause. When the voice came back it was strained, like someone talking while holding up something heavy.

"The door isn't a door. It's a mouth. And it's been waiting a very long time for someone to knock."

* * *

*Vesper*

Here is what nobody tells you about being trapped in a haunted room: the worst part isn't the dead. The dead are sad, and sadness is survivable. The worst part is the arithmetic.

Because the moment Dana's silhouette appeared in the mirror — clipboard, vest, the sensible boots of a woman who followed rules until a voice needed her — the room changed. The song swelled. The faded ones turned toward the glass with their faces opening like flowers, and I understood, with a sick drop in my stomach, that the room had been hungry for days and I was not enough. One voice wasn't enough. It wanted a *choice*, freely made, a living person reaching for the door with their whole heart.

Dana was giving it exactly that.

"No," I said aloud, to the room, to the mirror, to whatever ran the books in this place. "No. You don't get her."

"They come to us," Ada said softly, beside me. She had appeared at my shoulder without crossing any distance, the way she did. "We don't call them. We've never called them. That has to matter."

"It doesn't matter to her!"

"It matters to *you*." Ada looked at me then, and her eyes were the color of dust in sunlight, and there was something in them I recognized from my own mirror-self, from every reflection that had ever watched me make a decision I'd regret. "You could stay quiet, Vesper. Let her knock. Let her choose. The room would release you — it trades fairly, in its way. One keeper for another. You'd walk out into the morning and file your report and never think of us except at night."

"And she'd be in here for a hundred years."

"Yes."

I looked at the mirror. Dana had her palm flat against the door. Through the glass I could see her lips moving — calling my name, offering help, doing the decent thing with the whole stubborn weight of her decency. Somewhere behind her, faintly, the hallway mirror was lagging her movements by a second, then two, then five, drinking her in.

If I stayed quiet, I went home.

If I warned her off, the room would keep me. Not out of malice — out of bookkeeping. A warning was interference, and interference had a price, and the price was the current occupant staying on the ledger.

Love against duty, some novelist would write later. But it wasn't love, not really, not for Dana — I'd never met her. It was simpler and worse than love. It was the refusal to be the kind of person whose survival required someone else's door to open.

I put my hand against the inside of the mirror. It was warm, like skin.

"Dana," I said, and the glass carried my voice through, thin as a wire. "Listen carefully. The door is not a door. Back away. Get out of the house. Don't appraise anything. Tell them the property ate me — they'll believe it, they're accountants."

Through the glass, her eyes went wide. She looked directly at me — at *me*, not at a reflection, and that was its own small horror, being seen through instead of seen.

"Eriksen? Where — what is this? Are you behind this wall?"

"I'm behind every wall," I said, which was truer than I liked. "Dana, please. Walk out. Don't look at any mirrors on the way. Count the doors and use one of them."

Behind me, the song rose. The faded ones had drifted closer to the glass, their faces full of a hunger so old it had become indistinguishable from hope, and Ada stood apart from them, watching me with her folded hands, and I saw — God help me — that she was crying, silently, the way you cry at funerals for people you failed.

"You'll be kept," she whispered. "You understand that. If you save her, you're ours."

"Yeah," I said. "I did the math."

In the mirror, Dana hadn't moved. Her hand was still on the door, and the door — I could see it now, through the glass, from the inside — the door had begun, very slightly, to breathe.

* * *

*Dana*

The voice in the wall was Vesper Eriksen. Dana was sure of it the way you're sure of a fire alarm — not rationally, but in the body, in the spine.

Which meant the rational explanation was dead. There was no vent, no radio, no prank. There was a missing surveyor speaking to her through solid plaster, telling her to run, and behind his voice — around it, under it — the singing had started again, and it was closer now, and it was beautiful, and it was saying her name.

Not the song. The *room* was saying her name. She could feel it in the door under her palm, a warmth like a held breath, like a cheek leaning into a hand.

*Knock,* said the warmth. *Just knock.*

Dana Okafor had spent her whole life being the person who opened doors. It was in the job description. It was in the character. Her mother used to say she'd hold a door open for the devil himself and offer him a receipt.

Her hand curled into a fist.

And then, through the wall, ragged and cracking and deliberately, gloriously off-key, Vesper Eriksen began to sing — loud, terrible, drowning the room's song the way a shouted prayer drowns a whisper — and the warmth under her palm flinched.

"*Run,*" Eriksen sang-shouted. "*Run, you idiot, RUN—*"

Dana ran.

She took the stairs three at a time, past the parlor, past the entryway, and it was only at the front door, with her hand on the latch, that she made the mistake. She glanced back. Down the hall. At the tall mirror with the tarnished frame.

In the mirror, the hallway stretched away much farther than the hallway actually went, and at the end of it, small and gray and receding, a figure in a high-vis vest was running — but it was running the wrong way, deeper, and it was lagging behind her own body by a full second, then two, then ten, and its mouth was open in a scream she couldn't hear yet.

Dana burst through the front door into the cold clean daylight and did not stop until she reached the gate.

Then she turned around, chest heaving, and looked at the house — at the shuttered windows, the sagging porch, the door she'd left hanging open — and understood, with total clarity, that she was going back in.

Not today. Today she'd call the office, call the police, call anyone with a badge and a flashlight. But she'd seen a man trade himself for a stranger through a wall, and Dana Okafor had a rule about that too, one her father taught her on the rigs, the oldest one:

*Nobody gets left below.*
