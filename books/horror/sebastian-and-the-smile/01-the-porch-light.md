## 1. The Porch Light

The house had been waiting for him the way old houses wait—patiently, with all their dust arranged like an audience.

Theo Nakamura stood at the end of the gravel drive with his mother's ashes in a canvas tote bag slung over one shoulder and a rental car cooling behind him, and he let himself feel, for exactly ten seconds, the full weight of what he had come home to do. Thirty years of hospice work had taught him to ration grief in measured doses. You gave yourself a window. You breathed through it. Then you folded the feeling into a box and labeled it, and you got on with the work of the living.

He gave himself his ten seconds. He watched the house watch him back.

It was smaller than memory insisted. The clapboard siding had gone gray as driftwood, and the porch—the wide, sagging porch where his mother had shelled peas for forty summers—listed slightly to the left, like a man leaning to hear better. The screen door hung open at a tired angle. Someone had nailed it shut years ago; the nails were rusted now, bleeding orange streaks down the frame.

Behind the house, past the collapsed garden fence, Theo could just see the low stone ring of the old well, its cap of poured concrete furred with moss. He looked away from it the way you look away from a scar. Automatically. Without deciding to.

Inside, the air was cool and close and smelled of lavender and something underneath it, older—paper, plaster, time itself going soft. His mother had been a keeper. Every drawer held every receipt of her life: birthday cards from 1988, his father's war letters bundled in twine, Theo's own report cards, his Boy Scout badges, a photograph of a nine-year-old boy squinting into summer sun with a gap in his smile.

He worked through the afternoon the way he knew how, room by room, box by box, wrapping teacups in newspaper gone brittle as moth wings. The rhythm steadied him. Tear tape, fold flaps, label. There was comfort in the grammar of packing—a life reduced to inventory, everything accounted for, nothing left unexplained.

Which is why, when he carried the last box of the day out to the porch at dusk and found someone sitting in his mother's rocking chair, his first thought—not fear, not wonder, but *accounting*—was that he had miscounted. That somewhere in the dim rooms behind him there was one more box than he remembered, one more thing unexplained, and it had gotten up and walked outside.

"Hey, Theo."

Sebastian was exactly as he had left him.

That was the thought that arrived second, and it arrived with the slow, cold certainty of ice forming across still water. Because Sebastian had been left behind in 1974, in the summer Danny Voss drowned, in the season Theo turned nine and stopped being a boy who needed an imaginary friend. And imaginary friends did not have knees hooked over the arms of rocking chairs, or dirt on their sneakers, or a cowlick that stood up at the crown no matter how many times a mother licked her thumb and pressed it down.

But this Sebastian did. This Sebastian wore corduroys frayed at the cuffs and a striped t-shirt washed thin enough to read the ghost of another shirt beneath it. His hair was the same impossible brown. His hands were small—nine-year-old hands—and they gripped the arms of the chair the way they'd always gripped things, like the world might be taken away mid-sentence.

And he was smiling. That was the worst part. Not a cruel smile, not a mocking one. The same easy, gap-toothed grin he'd worn through three whole summers of games and secrets, the smile Theo had loved more than he'd ever admitted to anyone, because loving an imaginary friend too much at nine was how you got sent to Dr. Pellman twice a week to talk about your "feelings."

"You're not real," Theo said. His voice came out level. Thirty years of talking to the dying taught you level.

Sebastian rocked once, twice. The chair creaked a note Theo recognized, some specific pitch of wood and wear, and hearing it made his chest hurt in a way he refused to examine.

"Grief does funny things," Sebastian agreed pleasantly. "So does exhaustion. So does guilt, but we'll get to that." He tipped his head toward the tote bag resting against the doorframe. "How was the service? I listened from up here. Looked nice. Small."

"There were forty people."

"I said small. Forty people for a woman who fed half this town—" He shrugged. "Small."

Theo set the box down slowly, never taking his eyes off the boy in the chair. He catalogued himself the way he'd teach a new nurse to catalogue a patient: pulse steady, hands steady, mind—mind doing something it shouldn't, reaching backward across fifty years for the sound of that voice saying *race you to the creek*.

"You're a hallucination," Theo said. "I'm fifty-nine, I buried my mother yesterday, I haven't slept more than four hours a night since March. The brain produces—"

"Theo." Sebastian stood up. He was short; he had always been short; Theo had always been half a head taller even then. He walked to the edge of the porch and put both small hands on the railing and looked out at the darkening yard, at the shape of the well cap barely visible past the fence line. When he spoke again, the smile was gone, and what remained was worse. Patience. The terrible patience of someone who has waited a very long time and knows exactly how long.

"I'm not here because you're broken," he said. "I'm here because you took something. And I want it back."

The porch light buzzed on above them—old sodium bulb, amber, the same light his mother had left burning every night of Theo's childhood so he could find his way home from anywhere in town. It lit Sebastian from below, filled in the hollows of his face, made him briefly, horribly solid.

Theo's mouth had gone dry. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"Yeah," Sebastian said softly. "You do. But it's okay. It took me a while too, and I didn't even have to forget." He turned, and the smile came back—warm, awful, familiar as a scar. "Get some sleep, Theo. You look terrible. We've got until June fourteenth."

Then he stepped off the porch into the yard, and the dark took him between one stride and the next, the way dark always takes a boy running home before streetlight.

Theo stood a long time with his hand pressed flat against the doorframe, telling himself the only reasonable things: *hallucination, grief, exhaustion, the brain produces.* He told himself them in the calm clinical voice he used with families, and the words went through him like water through a net.

* * *

The night shift at Maplewood Hospice was quiet in the way Theo liked best—not silent, but hushed, full of the small honest sounds of people being cared for. He made his rounds at eleven. Mr. Aldous in 4B, congestive heart failure, sleeping with his mouth open. Mrs. Ferreira in 2A, morphine clockwork, her daughter asleep in the recliner beside her. And in 6C, Edgar Pruitt, ninety-one, lung cancer's final chapter, awake and lucid in the strange way the dying sometimes are at midnight, eyes fixed on the ceiling as though reading something written there.

Theo checked his drip, adjusted his blanket. "Comfortable, Ed?"

Edgar's lips moved. At first Theo thought it was prayer—it often was—but the cadence was wrong. Rhythmic. Descending.

"Nineteen," Edgar whispered. "Eighteen. Seventeen."

Theo had heard it before. Thirty years of deathbeds, and every few years, some patient near the end would count. Downward, always downward, never up. Doctors called it terminal lucidity, medication artifacts, hypoxia. Theo had long ago stopped needing a word for it. The dying counted down the way divers count down, the way children count down before bursting from hiding places. Toward something. Away from something. He had learned, over three decades, to stand quietly in the presence of it and not let it mean anything.

"Sixteen. Fifteen."

"Easy, Ed," Theo murmured, touching his wrist. Thin skin, paper pulse.

Edgar's eyes slid from the ceiling to Theo's face, and for a moment they focused with terrible clarity, the way eyes do in the last hours, seeing something past the nurse, past the wall.

"Fourteen," Edgar said.

And stopped.

Just—stopped. Lips closed. Breath evening out into sleep, or into whatever comes just before sleep at the end of a life. Theo stood there with two fingers on the man's wrist and waited for fifteen, for thirteen, for the count to resume, and it didn't. Fourteen sat in the dim room like a coin balanced on its edge.

June fourteenth.

Theo walked back down the corridor to the nurses' station, sat down under the fluorescent hum, and pulled the logbook toward him without seeing it. Outside the window, the town lay dark and ordinary, streetlights spaced like beads on a string, and somewhere among them the house on Corliss Road with its porch light burning amber over an empty rocking chair.

*We've got until June fourteenth.*

He wrote the date in the margin of his notes without meaning to. Then he crossed it out. Then he wrote it again, smaller, and stared at it until his shift ended, telling himself, in the voice he trusted most, that it meant nothing—that numbers were just numbers, that boys on porches were just grief wearing corduroys, that nothing in this town had ever been waiting for him.

He almost believed it. Right up until he drove past the house at dawn and saw, in the gray light, small footprints in the dew on the lawn—leading away from the porch, straight toward the well.
