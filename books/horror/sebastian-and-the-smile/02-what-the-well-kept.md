## 2. What the Well Kept

Theo woke on the couch with a blanket over him that he had not pulled up, and the smell of coffee in the kitchen that he had not made.

He lay still for a long time, listening to the house. Old houses talked—pipes ticking, joists settling, the refrigerator's small asthmatic hum—but this was different. This was the sound of someone moving through the rooms the way his mother used to move, unhurried, familiar, as though the house were a body she knew by touch.

He found Sebastian at the counter, sitting on one of the vinyl stools that had been there since 1971, swinging his legs because they didn't reach the rung. He wore the same yellow t-shirt, faded to the color of weak tea, the same corduroy shorts with the frayed hem. His hair was cut in the same blunt bowl shape. And he was smiling—the smile Theo remembered, wide and even, a smile like a door left open in winter.

"You look terrible," Sebastian said.

"I'm fifty-nine."

"I know." He said it gently, without mockery, and somehow that was worse. "You've been counting backwards for thirty years, Theo. I hear it every time you hold somebody's hand. You just do it inside now."

Theo's hands went cold around the mug of coffee he'd poured himself without deciding to. "You're not real. I buried my mother yesterday. Grief does—"

"Grief doesn't make coffee." Sebastian slid off the stool. "Grief doesn't wait fifty years on a porch. Come on. It's time to give it back."

"Give what back?"

But Sebastian was already walking toward the back door, and Theo's feet, traitorously, followed.

* * *

The well sat behind the collapsed garden shed where it had always sat, though Theo had half-forgotten it, the way you forget a scar until your fingers find it. A concrete cap poured over the mouth sometime in the eighties, cracked now, furred with moss. His father had sealed it after—after that summer—and told Theo never to play near it again, which was a strange thing to say about a well that had already taken everything it wanted.

Sebastian stood beside it, barefoot on the frost-burned grass, and held out both hands, palms up.

"It's still down there," he said. "You put it in a coffee can and you pushed it under the lip of the cap and you told yourself you'd throw it in the river, and then you didn't, and then you forgot, but you never forgot. You counted backwards so you wouldn't have to count forwards. To June fourteenth. Every year. You thought that was your own private madness." The smile flickered—for the first time it looked less like a door than a wound. "It wasn't private. I heard it too. All these years, I heard it."

"What did I take?" Theo asked, and his voice came out in the register he used with the dying, low and steady, a voice for holding people at the edge. "Tell me what I took, Sebastian."

And the memory came up out of him like water from a deep place.

* * *

Danny Voss's bike was red, with a banana seat and a sissy bar, and Danny rode it standing up, no hands, down Corliss Hill, screaming with joy, and all the kids screamed with him because Danny made everything into something you could scream about. That was June of 1974. Theo was nine. Danny was ten and would be ten forever.

They had been playing at the creek behind the Hollis property, because Sheriff Hollis let them—Sheriff Hollis let everybody do everything, Sheriff Hollis bought popsicles for the whole neighborhood from the ice cream truck when it came, Sheriff Hollis was the biggest man any of them knew and the kindest, and when Theo's father lost his job that spring it was Sheriff Hollis who quietly paid the electric bill, and when Theo's mother cried about money it was Sheriff Hollis who showed up with a paper sack of groceries and wouldn't take thanks for it.

That was the man. That was the man whose hands—

* * *

Theo came back to himself kneeling in the grass beside the well, palms pressed flat against the concrete cap, cold soaking through his trousers. Sebastian crouched across from him, watching with an expression no nine-year-old should be able to wear: patient, ancient, sorry.

"The photograph," Sebastian said. "You saw it fall out of his shirt pocket when he leaned over the well. You saw Danny grab his arm. You saw—" 

"Don't."

"—you saw him push. Both hands. You saw Danny go over, and you heard the sound, and you will hear that sound every night for the rest of your life, and you know it, Theo, you've been hearing it in other people's voices for thirty years, every dying patient who counts backwards is counting the same countdown you've been counting since you were nine—"

"He was good to us!" Theo's voice tore out of him, raw, shocking the birds up out of the hedge. "He paid our bills. He carried my mother's groceries. When he found me behind the shed that night—white as a sheet, shaking—he knelt down and he said *you don't ever have to carry this*, and I believed him. I was nine years old and he was the law, and I believed him."

"And Danny?" Sebastian said softly. "Who carried Danny?"

Theo couldn't answer. The concrete under his palms seemed to breathe.

* * *

Across town, in Room 4 at Meadowbrook Hospice, Mrs. Adele Kowalczyk, who had not spoken a full sentence in eleven days, opened her eyes at 3:14 in the morning and began to count.

"Thirty-one," she said clearly. "Thirty. Twenty-nine."

Her nurse, a young woman named Priya who had learned from Theo Nakamura how to sit with the dying, how to listen without flinching, felt the hair rise on her arms—not because of the counting. She'd heard counting before. Because of the direction. Dying people counted down; everyone in hospice knew that, some private arithmetic of letting go. But they stopped wherever they stopped. Random numbers. Personal numbers.

Mrs. Kowalczyk reached fourteen and stopped, and smiled, and said, "June," and slept.

In Room 9, Mr. Abernathy, sedated past speech, moved his lips against his pillow. Priya read them later in her notes and crossed them out and wrote them again: *fourteen, fourteen, fourteen.*

And in the staff lounge, she found the schedule Theo had left behind when he'd driven out to his mother's house, and on the back of it, in his handwriting, a note he must have written days ago and forgotten: *Ask T. why patients keep saying the 14th. Ask him why he goes pale.*

She stared at it a long time.

* * *

By afternoon the light had gone the color of old brass, and Theo was in the shed with a crowbar, prying at the edge of the concrete cap while Sebastian watched from the doorway, smiling that open-door smile, and Theo hated him for it and could not leave him.

Under the lip, wedged where a nine-year-old's arms could reach through the gap between cap and casing, there was a rusted coffee can. His hands shook so badly he dropped it twice.

Inside, wrapped in a bread bag gone brittle as autumn leaf, was a photograph.

He almost didn't look. Fifty years of not looking had become its own muscle, and the effort of looking now was like lifting something far beyond his strength. But he looked.

Sheriff Dale Hollis stood at the mouth of the old well in his tan uniform, hat tipped back, grinning at whoever held the camera—one of the kids, Danny probably, Danny with the camera always, documenting everything, that was Danny's summer project, *a history of Corliss Hill*. And in the corner of the frame, half-cropped, was the date scrawled in grease pencil on the film border: **6-14-74**. Taken that morning. Taken hours before. Proof that Danny had been here, at this well, on this day—with the sheriff, laughing, alive.

Not drowned in the creek two miles away, as the report said. Not an accident of slippery rocks. Here. With him.

"They said the creek," Theo whispered. "For fifty years they said the creek."

"Because you gave the sheriff the photograph," Sebastian said, "and he gave you back your family. Your father's job came back in August. Funny timing, wasn't it? And he told you if anyone ever found it, they'd say you pushed Danny. A nine-year-old boy, alone with his friend at a well. Who would they believe?" The smile finally fell away entirely, and underneath it Sebastian's face was thin and tired and terribly old. "You kept it anyway. Down here. Close enough to give back someday. That's the only reason I'm still here, Theo. That's the only reason Danny isn't at rest. Something of his is still in the ground, and something of yours is keeping it company."

Theo sat down hard on the broken cap, the photograph trembling in his hands, and somewhere across town, in a dozen darkened rooms, the dying turned their faces toward the walls and whispered the same number into their pillows, counting down, counting down, fourteen days and falling.

"There's a woman," Theo said at last, his voice barely there. "Ruth. Danny's sister. She's still alive. She lives in Cedar Falls." He looked up at Sebastian, and for the first time in fifty years, he did not look away. "If I give this to her—if I say it out loud—it won't just be him I bury. It'll be the man who fed my family. It'll be the lie I built my whole life on top of."

"Yes," Sebastian said. "That's exactly what it will be."

Outside, the wind moved through the hedge, and beneath the concrete, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of rising water.
