## 3. Fourteen Days in June

The county library still smelled the way it had in 1974—dust and old glue and the faint sweetness of paper slowly surrendering to time—but the microfilm machine was new, digital, its screen glowing a sterile blue that made everything it showed look like evidence. Theo sat down at it at nine in the morning, fourteen days before June 14th, and told himself he was only doing research. Only curiosity. Only an old man tying up loose ends before he sold the house.

He didn't believe himself anymore. That was the thing about being seventy-one: you ran out of lies that fit.

*The Harrow Creek Gazette*, June 21st, 1974. Page one. The headline sat there waiting for him, patient as a spider:

**LOCAL BOY DROWNS IN OLD WELL — SHERIFF HOLLIS LEADS RECOVERY**

There was Danny's photograph, the same one from the school yearbook, cropped smaller. Ten years old, gap-toothed, hair cut short for summer. *Daniel Voss, 10, of Mill Road.* Below it, the article unfolded in careful, official language: the boy had been playing near the abandoned well behind the Nakamura property, had climbed the rotted cover, had fallen. Sheriff Hollis had personally organized the recovery, working through the night, and had comforted the family. A quote from Hollis himself, printed in bold: *"It's a tragedy, but those old wells are a danger we've warned folks about for years. I'd ask parents to keep their children away from them."*

Theo read it four times. Each time, the same sentence snagged him like a nail in cloth: *had climbed the rotted cover.*

Danny hadn't climbed anything. Danny had been running—running from the porch toward the well with his arms out like wings, laughing, because they were all laughing, because Sheriff Hollis had said *race you* and then—

Then what? Fifty years of fog. Theo pressed his palms against his eyes until colors bloomed. He could see the sheriff's boots. He could see the photograph—the flash going off, Hollis crouched by the well with Danny's bike beside him, taking a picture of something, and Theo snatching it later from the sheriff's truck when the men were carrying the stretcher, hiding it inside his mother's sewing box because Sebastian had whispered *he can't ever find that, Theo, he'll hurt your mama*—

And then nothing. A hole where the rest should be.

"Mr. Nakamura?"

Theo startled so badly he knocked the mouse off the desk. A young librarian stood over him, all sympathy and concern. "Are you all right? You've been sitting here a long while."

"I'm fine," Theo heard himself say. "Just... old newspapers."

She glanced at the screen, at Danny's small smiling face, and her expression did something complicated. "That was before my time," she said quietly. "But my grandmother used to say the Voss family never got a fair shake. You want the truth about that summer, don't ask anyone whose last name is Hollis or married one." She hesitated, then lowered her voice. "Marion Hollis still lives on Kestrel Lane. White house, blue shutters. She's ninety now, but sharp as broken glass."

* * *

Marion Hollis answered the door herself, small and straight-backed, her white hair pinned with military precision. She looked at Theo for a long moment, and Theo watched recognition move across her face like weather—not surprise, not guilt. Recognition. As if she had been expecting this visit for fifty years.

"The Nakamura boy," she said. "You used to play with the Voss child."

"I did."

"You've come about the well."

"I've come about the well."

She stepped back and let him in. The house smelled of lemon polish and camphor, and every surface held photographs of a man in a tan uniform with a confident smile—Sheriff Dale Hollis, dead twenty years, beloved by half the county, his name on the new wing of the community hospital. Marion led Theo past all of them without looking at them, into a kitchen where two cups of tea were already set out.

Two cups. Theo stared at them. She had known.

"My husband was a good man," Marion said, sitting, folding her hands. "He served this town for thirty-one years. He pulled people out of floods. He carried old Mrs. Petrie's groceries every Tuesday until she died. Whatever you think you remember, Theodore, I'd ask you to weigh it against thirty-one years."

"I saw him push Danny into the well."

The words came out of Theo flat and strange, as if someone else had borrowed his voice. Marion didn't flinch. She lifted her teacup, drank, set it down. Her hands never trembled.

"Dale told me there was a witness," she said. "A child. He told me the night it happened, standing in this kitchen, soaked to the waist, and he said, *if the boy talks, we lose everything, and I'll do what has to be done.* And I told him—" her voice thinned to wire— "I told him a nine-year-old wouldn't talk. Children keep secrets when they're frightened enough. You kept yours, didn't you? Fifty years."

"I hid a photograph," Theo said. "From his truck. It shows—"

"I know what it shows." For the first time, something moved behind her eyes, deep down, like a fish turning under ice. "Dale searched your house twice for it. Did you know that? Officially, follow-up visits. Unofficially, he turned your mother's kitchen upside down while she made him coffee and thanked him for his service." She smiled, and it was the most terrible thing Theo had seen in thirty years of hospice work—a smile worn like armor over a wound that had never closed. "You hid it well, little boy. You hid it so well that you hid it from yourself."

The room tilted. Theo gripped the table. *Hid it from yourself.* The sewing box. His mother's sewing box, which he had opened a hundred times since coming home and found empty except for thread.

"It's not in the box anymore," he whispered.

"No," Marion agreed. "I imagine it isn't anywhere you'd think to look. Things taken to a well stay near a well. That's how it's always worked here." She stood, smoothed her skirt, and walked him to the door with terrible courtesy. At the threshold she stopped, her back to him.

"He wasn't sorry," she said. "Not once, not at the end, not on his deathbed when he counted backwards every night for a week before he died. Fourteen days' worth of counting. My son thought it was the morphine." She opened the door. "Whatever comes for him on the fourteenth, Theodore, tell it he already paid. Tell it I kept the name clean for fifty years and it bought me nothing but two cups of tea poured for ghosts."

* * *

Sebastian was waiting on the porch steps when Theo got home, sitting exactly where he always sat, legs dangling, and for the first time in fifty years Theo wished he weren't. Because Sebastian's smile was wrong tonight. Stretched too wide, too fixed, the smile of a mask rather than a face.

"You went to her house," Sebastian said.

"I went to the library. Then her house."

"You shouldn't have done that." Sebastian stood. In the failing light he cast no shadow at all, and Theo wondered how he had never noticed that before—or noticed and buried that too. "The well is refilling, Theo. Can't you hear it? Every night it gets louder. Fourteen days, and whatever you haven't given back, it takes different. It doesn't take things anymore." He tilted his head, and his smile widened another impossible degree. "It takes people."

"Then take me instead of the truth," Theo said. "Take the photograph, take my memory, take whatever you need. But Ruth Voss is eighty-three years old and she's spent her whole life believing her brother drowned climbing a cover he couldn't have reached. I'm done protecting dead men's names."

Something flickered across Sebastian's face—hurt, real hurt—and then the smile returned, and behind it Theo heard it clearly for the first time: a sound rising from the back of the property, low and rhythmic, water against stone, getting louder.

"You don't understand what you're saying," Sebastian said softly. "So let me show you. Let me show you what the well does with secrets, Theo. Let me show you where yours has been keeping warm."

He held out his hand. And Theo—who had spent thirty years holding the hands of the dying, who knew better than anyone what it meant when a grip was cold—took it.
