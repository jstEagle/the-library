## 2. An Extra Chair

The estate lawyer's name was Pemberton, and he had the kind of office that made you feel like you were already dead — dark wood, darker leather, a window that looked out onto the county courthouse as if to remind you where all roads ended. He slid two boxes across his desk with the reverence of a man handling other people's grief on an hourly rate.

"Photographs," he said. "Your grandmother's. The will specifies they're to be catalogued and divided among surviving family. I'd have my paralegal do it, but she took one look at the boxes and developed a sudden allergy to dust."

I looked at the boxes. They were ordinary banker's boxes, the kind that hold tax records and old Christmas cards and the sediment of a life nobody wants to sort through until it's legally necessary.

"An allergy," I said.

"Her words were 'that house gives me the creeps and now so does its mail.' She's very professional otherwise."

Five years of visiting that house had given me a tolerance most people never develop. You learn to work around a thing the way you work around a bad knee. You don't fix it; you just stop expecting it to behave.

"I'll take them home," I said. "Cataloguing's easier without an audience."

Pemberton nodded like this was the sensible answer, because it was, and because he wanted them out of his office by five o'clock. He handed me a form, a pen chained to nothing because he trusted me more than he trusted humanity in general, and said something about itemized descriptions and fair market division that I wrote down without hearing.

What I remember clearly is carrying those boxes to my car in the rain and feeling, for no reason I could name at the time, like the boxes were heavier than photographs should be. Like they'd absorbed something from the house they'd lived in for sixty years. Like paper could hold weight the way walls hold sound.

I want to be clear about what I believed at this point: I believed in damp wood settling. I believed in pipes, in raccoons, in the acoustic quirks of old balloon framing. I believed my grandmother had been a solitary woman who talked to herself, which explained the voices I'd half-heard through floorboards during appraisals of her furniture. I believed in everything except the one thing that would have required me to believe in nothing else ever again.

That lasted four days.

* * *

I set up at my kitchen table on a Tuesday evening, because my apartment has better light than the house and because I had no intention of spending more hours inside those walls than the appraisal contract strictly required. Two boxes, a legal pad, cotton gloves I'd bought for the occasion and felt ridiculous wearing, and a bottle of rye I told myself was for celebration when the task was done.

The first box was chronological, roughly. My grandmother had been organized in the way of people who organize things once and then defend the system against all evidence. Family portraits from the forties. Weddings. Christmases. A sequence of school photos featuring my mother growing progressively less willing to smile.

I catalogued. That was the job. *Portrait, family, c. 1954, silver gelatin print, good condition.* I wrote descriptions like a surveyor writes measurements — accurate, impersonal, safe.

It was somewhere around photo forty that I noticed the chair.

Here is what I can tell you about noticing: it doesn't happen all at once. It happens the way water finds a crack. First there's just a sense that something is off, some wrongness your eye registers before your mind consents to. I put down the photograph. Picked it up again. A family portrait, 1961 maybe — my grandmother young, my grandfather alive and awkward in his good suit, my mother aged six and furious about her dress. And behind them, against the wall, a wooden chair.

A high-backed chair, dark wood, spindles, a seat worn pale in the middle. The kind of chair that belongs in a church or a courtroom or a very old kitchen. It did not belong in this portrait. Not because chairs are unusual in portraits — people posed with furniture constantly back then — but because I knew that room. I had measured that room. It was the front parlor of my grandmother's house, and I had inventoried every stick of furniture in it for the estate appraisal, and there was no such chair. There was a settee, a rocking chair, a lamp table, a piano nobody had played since 1987. No high-backed chair with a worn seat.

I told myself it had been sold or discarded before I ever started working there. Obviously. Chairs leave houses. Chairs are the most migratory of furniture.

But then I opened the second box, and the chair was in 1972. And 1979. And in the Christmas photo from 1985 where my cousin Wren is missing two front teeth and I am a fat-cheeked toddler holding a toy truck like a weapon. The chair stood behind us in every single frame, patient as a relative who arrived early and intends to stay late.

I laid the photographs out on the kitchen table in rows. Forty-one prints spanning fifty-three years. Different rooms, different decades, different fashions, different degrees of everyone's happiness. One constant: the chair. Always unoccupied. Always positioned slightly behind the group, the way you'd position someone who hadn't yet been introduced. And always — this detail took me three passes to confirm, because my mind kept trying to file it under *coincidence* and I kept having to drag it back out — always facing the camera. Facing whoever held it. Facing me.

I sat back and drank some rye, not for celebration.

There's a specific vertigo that comes from realizing your record of the past might be unreliable. People talk about it with dementia, with false memories, with those studies where they implant entire childhoods in lab subjects. But those are failures of memory. This was different. This was the past itself misbehaving. These weren't my memories being edited; these were objects, physical artifacts, silver gelatin and Kodachrome, and they disagreed with each other and with me. Either the chair had been in every one of these rooms for fifty years and I had somehow never seen it in any visit, any album, any wall of framed photos at any holiday dinner —

Or it hadn't been, and now it was.

And the second option was not an option. The second option was a door I intended to keep firmly shut, thank you, and I am a man with experience keeping doors shut. Ask anyone. Ask the noises in my grandmother's house, which I have kept categorically shut for five years running.

I finished the rye and went to bed and dreamed, though I couldn't recall of what, of sitting down.

* * *

Wren answered on the third ring, which for Wren is enthusiastic.

"Let me guess," she said. "You found something weird."

My cousin Wren Kavanagh grew up in that house — her family rented the upper floor for three years while her parents got on their feet — and she has spent the twenty years since refusing to discuss it with a thoroughness I used to find dramatic and was beginning to reconsider. She lives in Portland now, does something with databases, owns two cats named after minor prophets.

"Why would you say that?"

"Because you called me. You call me twice a year, Orsen, and both times it's after you've been in the house." A pause, and then, quieter, the way people say things they've decided not to take back: "Did you find the chair?"

The kitchen went very still. So did I.

"What chair?" I said, and heard myself say it, and understood that I sounded exactly like a man who had spent forty-one photographs failing to see a chair.

"Right," Wren said. "Okay. Describe yours."

"High-backed. Dark wood. Spindles. Seat worn pale in the middle."

She didn't answer for long enough that I checked whether the call had dropped. When she came back her voice had changed registers entirely — flat, careful, the voice of someone walking on a floor they know has a weak board.

"Don't go back in the house alone."

"Wren—"

"I'm serious. Listen to me. Email me the scans. All of them. I have digital copies of a bunch of Grandma's albums — I scanned them years ago for Mom's anniversary thing. We'll compare."

"Compare what? What do you think we're comparing?"

"The past," she said, "with whatever is pretending to be the past," and hung up before I could point out that this sentence was insane, or before I could admit that it was the sanest sentence anyone had said to me all week.

* * *

The files arrived at midnight. Forty-six images, high resolution, dated eight years earlier by their metadata. I opened them side by side with my scans of the same prints and went through them one at a time, and I will describe the process honestly, because honesty is all I have left of that week.

Photo one: identical. Photo two: identical. Photo three: identical, right down to a scratch on the emulsion and my grandfather's thumb blurring the corner of the frame.

Photo seven: the 1961 portrait. In Wren's copy, the space behind my grandmother's young shoulder is empty wall. Wallpaper, a picture rail, a small framed sampler. No chair.

In mine, the chair.

I checked the metadata twice. I checked the filenames. I put the physical print next to the screen and looked from one to the other until my eyes watered, and they were the same photograph in every respect except the one that mattered. Same grain. Same light. Same six-year-old fury on my mother's face. One had a chair in it. One did not.

I went through all forty-six. The pattern held without exception. Every image Wren had digitized eight years ago showed rooms I recognized, rooms without the chair. Every print from my grandmother's boxes showed the chair, seated in the background of my family's entire history like a guest who had always been invited and had only now bothered to arrive.

Except — and here is the detail I circled at three in the morning with a red pen, because if I could reduce it to data then it stayed data — except the last three photos in the second box. Photos taken within the last decade, ordinary snapshots, my grandmother on her porch, my grandmother at Wren's graduation, my grandmother in the kitchen making the lemon cake she made every Christmas until her hands stopped cooperating. In Wren's copies: no chair. In my prints: no chair either.

The chair wasn't in the new photographs.

It was only in the old ones. Only in the past. As if it had gone back and inserted itself retroactively, patiently, into every year it could reach — and had stopped at some line, some boundary, sometime in the last ten years. As if it were still working forward. As if it were filling in the record year by year, and the present simply hadn't come up yet.

I sat in my kitchen with forty-one photographs of a chair that had never existed, in rooms I had measured with a laser level, and I felt something shift in me that I've never fully shifted back. It wasn't fear, or not only fear. It was the vertigo Wren must have known her whole life — the ground-level realization that memory itself can be edited, that the past is not a fixed country you can emigrate from but a document, and documents get revised, and the question of *who holds the pen* is the only question that matters.

Every childhood Christmas I remembered from that house — was that memory mine, or was it the current draft?

I thought about the noises. Five years of noises. Pipes, I'd said. Settling. Raccoons. And underneath the rationalizations, a truth I'd been sitting on like a man sitting on a chair:

Something in that house had been waiting for me to look at the record. And now that I had, it knew I'd seen it.

My phone buzzed. Wren, a text, sent at 12:47 a.m.:

**Checked my files again. There's one more thing. The 1891 photo — the one of the house when it was built? In my scan there are four people on the porch. In the one you sent me there are four people AND the chair, sitting on the porch, in the doorway.**

**Orsen, the Aldouses built that house. Look up the Aldouses.**

I stared at the name. I had read it myself, weeks ago, in the deed abstracts — *Aldous, Aldous & Aldous, builders* — and filed it under trivia, the way you file everything about that house under trivia.

Outside my apartment window, the city went on being the city. Traffic. Rain. A siren trailing off toward the river. None of it knew. None of it had a chair in the background of its photographs.

I picked up the oldest print in the box — the 1891 image, sepia gone tea-brown, the house new and raw and proud of itself. Four figures on the porch, three men and a woman, dressed in the black formal wear of people who treat architecture as a sacrament. And behind them, in the open doorway, exactly where a threshold should stand empty:

The chair. Waiting to be brought inside.

I turned the print over. On the back, in pencil, in handwriting I recognized from thirty years of birthday cards — my grandmother's handwriting, but younger, shakier, pressed hard enough to groove the paper — were four words:

*Never let it seat you.*

I booked a research appointment at the county archive for Monday morning, first slot, and I did not sleep between then and now, and if you ask me why, I'll tell you it was the coffee.

It was not the coffee.
