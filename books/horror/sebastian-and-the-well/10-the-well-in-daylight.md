## 10. The Well in Daylight

Noon was a strategy. I want to be honest about that, because everything else I did that week I can pretend was instinct or grief or accident, but the well I visited on purpose, at the brightest hour the sky would give me, with a flashlight in my coat pocket like a talisman and my phone set to record before I'd even left the kitchen.

Daylight is a kind of armor. That's what I told myself, standing at the back door with my hand on the latch, looking out at the field where the grass had gone gold and brittle at the tips. Whatever lived in the dark under our land — whatever knocked pipes and answered questions in my dead brother's voice — it kept office hours. It worked nights. At noon even the church bells sounded secular.

I said it out loud, because saying things out loud had become a habit I couldn't break: "It's just a hole in the ground."

The house didn't answer. The house had stopped answering anything, which was somehow worse than when it used to sigh and settle around me like something turning over in its sleep.

* * *

The path to the well wasn't a path anymore. Fifteen years will do that — the sheep-track my father used to walk twice a day had been swallowed by nettles and couch grass, and I had to push through waist-high growth that caught at my sleeves and left thin red welts along my forearms. The field itself hadn't changed. That was the unsettling part. Every other thing in my life had been renovated or demolished or quietly replaced, but this field was exactly the field of my childhood: same slope, same hawthorn at the corner leaning east like a listener, same low hum of bees in the clover. I could have walked it blindfolded at seven. Walking it now, at thirty-seven, with my eyes open and the sun high, felt like trespassing on a memory that hadn't finished being lived.

The well sat in its shallow dip near the field's center, where the ground softened toward the old spring line. From thirty yards I could see the cap — the flat slab of grey stone my father had laid over it the summer after the drowning, mortared down, ringed with a low kerb of smaller stones. He'd done it himself, alone, over two days, refusing help. I remembered watching him from the kitchen window, mixing mortar with his sleeves rolled up, his face doing nothing at all. I'd thought then that he was being strong. Now I understood he was building a lid on something and did not trust anyone else to press it shut properly.

I stopped ten feet away.

There were flowers on the stones.

Fresh ones. A small bundle of white yarrow and a few stems of meadowsweet, tied with green garden twine, laid neatly at the base of the cap as if on an altar. The petals were unblemished. No rain damage, no browning at the edges, no drift of fallen blossom scattered by wind. Someone had put them there recently — this morning, maybe. Possibly within the hour.

I stood very still and let the field make its ordinary noises around me. Bees. A crow somewhere behind the hawthorn. My own blood in my ears.

Because here is what I knew about Ravensworth Farm: nobody came here. Mrs. Callow next door was the only neighbor who still spoke to me, and she was eighty-one and hadn't crossed the boundary wall since the funeral. The village below had made its position clear at the graveside — polite nods, quick departures, a vicar who couldn't meet my eye. Nobody from that village was walking up through the nettles to lay yarrow on a capped well that officially contained nothing but fifteen years of dark water and a stone slab.

Someone tended this place. Someone had been tending it for a while, probably — I crouched and looked closer and saw, pressed into the mud at the kerb's edge, older stems gone brown and papery, the ghosts of previous offerings. Weeks of them. Months, maybe. Laid with the same neat care, the same twine.

I took out my phone and photographed them. The flash wasn't needed; the light was hard and white and total. In the image the flowers looked almost aggressive against the grey stone, a bright wound on a closed mouth.

"Who does this?" I asked the field. My voice came out smaller than I intended.

The hawthorn leaned. The bees went on with their arithmetic. And underneath all of it, so faint I might have invented it, I thought I heard water moving — not the trickle of the spring line, which ran shallow and audible near the ditch, but something deeper, slower, a sound like swallowing.

I got up. I walked to the cap.

* * *

Up close, the slab was worse than I remembered, or better, depending on how you count. My father had been a careful man and it showed: the mortar lines were true, the kerb stones fitted snug, the whole thing built to outlast weather and doubt. But time gets into everything. Hairline cracks webbed the mortar. One kerb stone had shifted, tilted inward like a tooth going bad. And moss had colonized the north side of the cap in a soft green pelt, except —

Except for a patch in the middle, roughly the size of a kneeling person's knees, where the moss was worn away. Worn smooth. As if someone knelt here often. As if someone knelt here regularly, season after season, pressing their weight against the stone above the water, and whatever they whispered down through the cap had polished the moss off the rock the way prayer polishes the steps of old churches.

I put my palm flat on the slab. Cold, even at noon. Colder than stone should be in July sunlight, cold like the memory of water, and I snatched my hand back before I could decide whether I'd felt anything move beneath it.

Then I saw the iron ring.

Set flush into the cap, near one edge: a lifting ring, rusted to a nub, the kind you'd hook a chain to if you ever needed the slab moved. Which nobody would need. Which nobody should need. But it meant the cap was designed to open, and someone had designed it that way on purpose, and my father — my quiet, mortar-mixing, grief-stricken father — had built a door where he'd sworn to me there was only a grave.

I circled it slowly, photographing everything. The kerb. The cracks. The worn knees of moss. On the south side, half-hidden under a curl of lichen, I found scratches in the stone — deliberate marks, not weathering. Tally marks? Letters? I brushed the lichen away with my sleeve and my breath went strange in my chest.

They were letters. Small, deep-cut, the work of a knife or a nail point, in a hand I recognized because I'd seen it on every label of every jam jar in the pantry and inside the cover of every book in Sebastian's room.

My mother's handwriting.

I couldn't read what it said. The lichen had eaten most of it, and what remained was fragments — a word ending in *-ember*, the tail of another that might have been *forgive* or *forget*, and a single numeral that could have been a seven or a one. She had carved something into the cap of the well and then, apparently, let the stone grow over it. Or someone had helped it grow over. Or she'd written it for the well itself, not for eyes, the way you leave notes under doors for people you know are home but won't answer.

My mother who sang counting-songs. My mother who vanished. Her handwriting, on the lid of the thing that took her son.

I sat down on the grass — not touching the stones, just near them — and opened the flashlight I'd carried like a charm, and felt immediately ridiculous, and did it anyway. There was no gap to shine it into. The cap was sealed. The light made a weak coin of glow on the grey slab and told me nothing, and I held it there anyway for a long moment, a child with a torch defending against the cellar dark, before I clicked it off.

That was when I noticed the second set of marks. Not carved. Cut. Deeper, cruder, recent enough that the exposed stone inside them was still pale, unweathered. They ran along the top edge of the cap, where the slab met the kerb, and at first I took them for tool marks from the original construction.

Then I saw they were words too. Two words. And they were not my mother's hand.

They were mine.

* * *

I know how that sounds. I've had three days to sit with it and it still sounds like madness, so let me describe it precisely, because precision is the last thing I own.

The letters were cut into the stone in block capitals, uneven, gouged rather than carved — the work of someone using a screwdriver or a strong knife and no skill. I have no skill. I have never cut letters into stone. But the letterforms were unmistakably mine: the way I cross my sevens, the flat-topped A I've made since primary school, the slightly forward lean of everything I write, as if the words are always trying to hurry somewhere.

Two names.

*TAMSIN.*

And beneath it, in the same hand, the same gouged urgency:

*SEBASTIAN.*

Both names. Both cut by the same hand. My hand, or a perfect copy of it, chiseled into the cap of the well sometime — I touched the edges, carefully, and the stone dust in the grooves was still loose, unfixed by rain — sometime within the last few weeks. Since I came home. Since the funeral. Since the recordings started.

I sat back on the grass and the noon light seemed to tilt, the field swinging gently around me like a boat coming loose from its mooring. Because there were explanations, weren't there? There were always explanations. Sleepwalking. Some fugue state, some stress response, me wandering out at night with a tool and carving my own name and my dead brother's name into a stone I had no reason to touch. People did stranger things under grief. The internet was full of them.

But I didn't believe it. I want to be honest: I checked myself for mud on my shoes that morning, I checked my hands for splinters and stone dust, and my hands were clean, and my shoes held nothing but kitchen crumbs, and I did not believe the explanation, because the explanation required me to forget an act of violence done to granite, and forgetting had never been my problem.

Remembering had always been my problem.

Both names. Not one. Not *Sebastian*, the drowned boy, memorialized by some secret mourner. Both. As if the stone couldn't tell which of us belonged to it. As if someone — something — had marked us both, the way you tag two envelopes when you're not sure which address is right.

Or the way you mark a debt that can be paid by either twin.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. The shadows moved; the bees thinned; the crow left the hawthorn and did not come back. At some point I realized I was humming, low, without deciding to — the counting-song, the first verses, the ones the tape had given me, *one for the water and two for the stone* — and I clamped my mouth shut so fast I bit my tongue.

The silence afterward had texture. It had attention in it.

"Stop it," I whispered. To the well, to myself, to whichever of us was humming. "He's dead. He drowned. It's over."

The cap said nothing. The cap was very good at saying nothing; my father had built it that way. But under the slab, deep down where the daylight has never reached and never will, I heard — clearly this time, no room for invention — the sound of water shifting. Not moving. Shifting. The way a sleeper shifts when they hear their name spoken in another room.

And then, fainter than the water, thinner than a thread drawn up through fifteen years of stone:

Humming.

The same song. The same tune. Coming up from underneath, matching me note for note, patient as tide, waiting for me to go on to the verse I didn't know.

I stood up so fast the field spun. I backed away — three steps, five, nettles whipping my calves — and I did not take my eyes off the cap, off the two names cut in my own handwriting, off the fresh yarrow lying in its neat bundle like an answer to a question nobody had asked aloud yet.

Someone tends this place. Someone kneels here until the moss wears smooth. Someone carves both our names and lays white flowers and keeps the door my father built, and I was suddenly certain — with the terrible certainty that had been dismantling my life one plank at a time since the night I first played those recordings — that it wasn't me.

Mrs. Callow's kitchen window faced this field. I'd noticed it my first morning home and thought nothing of it: an old woman's window, aimed at nothing, at grass and sky.

Aimed at the well.

I turned and walked back through the nettles, faster than dignity allowed, and the whole way across the field I could feel the slab at my back like an eye, and once — only once, at the gate, with the farmhouse rising ahead of me safe and sunlit and lying — I heard it again, that voice under the world, and this time it wasn't humming.

This time it said my name. Not *Tamsin*.

The other one. The childhood one. The nickname only two people alive had ever used, and one of them was in the ground.

I went to find Mrs. Callow.
