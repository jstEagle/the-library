## 17. Mrs. Callow's Confession

Edith Callow had spent sixty years learning which silences were kind and which were cruel, and she had come to believe that most of the cruel ones were kept out of love. Love was a terrible accountant. It kept the worst books. It wrote debts in the margins of other people's lives and then died before it could balance them, leaving the arithmetic to the children.

She had not meant to be the one left holding the ledger.

She heard Tamsin Ravensworth coming up her garden path before she heard the gate, because the gate didn't squeak anymore—she'd oiled it herself, years ago, so that no one could sneak up on her, so that no one could sneak up on *it*, though she'd never said that part aloud—and so the first sound was footsteps on gravel, hesitant, the way a person walks when they have rehearsed a conversation all the way to a stranger's door and forgotten the first line.

Edith put down her tea. She had been expecting this visit for eleven days, since the funeral, since the boy—no, not a boy, a grown person with tired eyes and their mother's jaw—had stood at the graveside looking not at the coffin but at the well beyond the churchyard wall, the way a person looks at a door they're not sure they want to open.

She opened the door before the knock came.

"Come in," she said. "Before you lose your nerve."

Tamsin stood on the step holding a cassette tape in a plastic bag, like evidence, like something that might bite. Rain was coming. Edith could smell it in the hedges, that green iron smell the fields gave off before a storm, and underneath it, fainter, the smell of the well water that had soaked into the whole valley's bones a long time ago.

"You know why I'm here," Tamsin said.

"I've known why you'd come for thirty years." Edith stepped back to let them in. "That's the trouble with being the last one who remembers. You spend decades waiting for the knock."

* * *

Her front room smelled of lavender and coal smoke and, if you knew to notice it, of nothing else—she had been careful, all these years, to keep nothing in the house that the well might take an interest in. No photographs of that summer. No christening gowns. She had burned what needed burning and buried what needed burying and kept what needed keeping in a place no one would ever look, because no one would ever think to look in the one room of a lonely old woman's house that was always locked.

She unlocked it now.

The little room off the kitchen had been a pantry once, before her husband died and she had no one to feed but herself. Now it held a chair, a lamp, and a biscuit tin that had not held biscuits since 1987.

Tamsin stood in the doorway and did not come in. Edith understood that too. Some rooms you had to be invited into twice.

"Your mother sat in that chair," Edith said, "the night you were born. Both of you. She was in labor for nineteen hours and she never once cried out, and when the midwife said *twins*, your mother laughed. Not happy laughing. The other kind. The kind you do when a bill arrives that you'd been pretending wasn't coming."

"What bill?" Tamsin's voice was very steady, the way voices go steady when the person behind them is gripping something hard. "What was she paying?"

Edith lowered herself into the chair. It was the chair. She had never sat in it before. It seemed important that the first weight it held after Maren Ravensworth should be the weight of the truth, even if the truth was thirty years late.

"Sit down," she said. "And I'll tell it the way it happened, not the way it's been told. There's a difference. There's always been a difference in this village, child. Half of what we call history is just a debt we've agreed to stop reading."

* * *

"You'll have heard the story," Edith said, "the one we tell at the harvest supper, when the young ones ask why the first song at the feast is always the counting-song. You'll have heard that the well on Ravensworth land is old, older than the parish, older than the road, and that it's *hungry*, and that the Ravensworths made a bargain with it before there was a Ravensworth, back when the family was only a name scratched on a boundary stone. That's all true, as far as it goes. It doesn't go far enough.

"The bargain wasn't made with the well. You can't bargain with a hole in the ground. The bargain was made with what the well *holds*. And what it holds is older than hunger. It's older than wanting. It's a thing that was put down there before anyone alive could say why, and it sleeps, and the singing keeps it asleep, and the debt keeps the singing honest.

"Here's the part that isn't in any hymnal. The debt was never meant to be a child."

Tamsin made a small sound. Edith didn't look up. Some things you could only say to the middle distance, to the lamp, to the wall.

"The original terms—your grandmother's grandmother knew them, and hers before—the terms were *a voice*. A Ravensworth voice, singing at the well's mouth, once a generation, to renew the sleep. That's all. A song, sung whole. But songs wear out, the way ropes wear out, and somewhere along the way—your great-grandfather's time, maybe—the family started singing it incomplete. Leaving off the last verse. And an incomplete song is a broken promise, and a broken promise has to be paid for in something heavier than music.

"So it started taking. Not every generation. Only when it woke hungry. And it learned, over the years, to want the sweetest thing it could hear. A voice that had just learned to sing. Do you understand what I'm telling you? It didn't ask for blood. It asked for *first songs*. Children who had only just found their voices. It wanted the newest music there was."

Tamsin was very pale. "The counting-song. Mum's song. It was a lullaby—"

"It was a leash," Edith said. "Maren wrote it. Or half of it. She took the old renewal verse and she hid it inside a lullaby, note by note, the way you hide a key in a cake, and she sang it to you both every night of your lives so that the debt would hear it and stay lulled. Two children singing it back to the dark every evening—that was the payment. That was supposed to be enough. Two small voices, keeping the thing dreaming, until you were grown and could sing the full song yourselves and end it properly.

"But the well got impatient. Or the thing in it did. And in the autumn of '94, it stopped being patient."

* * *

Edith got up, because her knees had stiffened and because standing made the next part easier to say, and went to the biscuit tin, and opened it, and took out what was inside: a christening bracelet, very small, tarnished black; a photograph gone soft at the corners; and a page torn from a hymnal, the last verse cut out with scissors so cleanly you'd never know a verse had ever lived there.

She laid them on the table between them like a tarot spread.

"This is Maren's," she said, touching the bracelet. "She left it with me the night before. And this—" the photograph: a young woman with dark hair standing at a garden gate, laughing at someone out of frame— "is the only picture of her I kept. I gave the rest to the vicar for the memorial book, and the vicar's grandfather put most of them in the fire, because a woman who's about to do what she did shouldn't be remembered doing it. That was the thinking. That was always the thinking. Spare the living, starve the dead. It's not cruelty, child, I want you to know that. It never was. It's cowardice wearing mercy's coat, and I wore it too, and I'm too old now to take it off."

"What did she do?" Tamsin asked. And then, softer, in a voice that already knew: "The night she vanished. She didn't vanish."

"No." Edith sat back down. "She didn't."

Outside, the rain arrived, first in single drops that hit the window like fingers testing the glass, then all at once, a long gray hiss. Edith was glad of it. It was easier to say terrible things when the weather was doing some of the work.

* * *

"The well woke in October of '94," she said. "You'd have been seven. You wouldn't have known—children never know, that's the whole cruelty of it—but the signs were there. The cattle wouldn't graze the near field. The water in every tap in the parish tasted of stone. And the song stopped working. Maren told me she'd sing the lullaby at the well's mouth at dusk and she could feel it *listening through* it, the way you'd listen through a wall. The debt had grown ears. It had heard seven years of two small voices and it had decided it liked the taste, and it wanted one of them whole. Not the song. A singer.

"She came to me on the last night. I'd known her since we were girls—we picked stones out of the same turnip field, we shared a bicycle until we broke it—and I'd never seen her afraid, not in childbirth, not when your father died, not ever. She was afraid that night. She sat where you're sitting and she said, *Edie, it's asked for one of them, and if I don't give it one, it'll come take one, and it won't be gentle about the taking.*

"And I said the stupid thing. I said, *then give it the song, sing the last verse yourself.* And she looked at me the way you look at someone who has offered you a rope made of paper, and she said, *the verse has to be sung by a willing Ravensworth, Edie, and I'm not willing, I'm a mother, and a mother who's willing to die isn't willing, she's only tired.* And then she said the thing I've kept locked in this room for thirty years.

"She said: *But I can be a bargain.*

"Because that was the loophole. That was the door she found. The debt wanted a Ravensworth voice, newly given, freely offered, to seal the sleep for another generation. It didn't say the voice had to be a child's. Maren was a Ravensworth by marriage, but the well was never particular about the fine print—it was particular about the *singing*. And a mother's voice, a voice that had sung those children to sleep every night of their lives, a voice the debt had been listening to for seven years through the walls of the world—that voice was worth more than a child's. She knew it. She'd been singing to that thing in the dark her whole motherhood. She'd been *feeding* it, one verse at a time, so it would never be hungry enough to come for them. She'd known, all along, that she was fattening it and starving it at once, and on the last night she went to collect what she'd promised it without ever saying so."

Tamsin's hands were flat on the table, pressing down, as if the table might otherwise tip. "She went down the well."

"She walked out to the fields at dusk, singing," Edith said. "The whole village saw her, or thought they saw her—saw a woman walking into the twilight singing the counting-song, and by morning she was gone, and we let them think what they thought. Run off. Grief-mad. Taken. It was easier. A woman who abandons her children is a tragedy you can gossip about. A woman who climbs down a well in the dark to trade her voice for her children's lives—that's a saint, and saints make demands, and demands make the debt restless. So we buried the truth with her. We let her be a runaway. It was the cruelest kindness this village ever did, and we did it to protect you, and I have regretted it every single day, and I would do it again, and that is the whole of what it is to love someone in this valley."

* * *

"But it didn't work," Tamsin said. "She bought time, and then—" Their voice cracked on the second word and they pushed through it. "Three years later. Sebastian."

Edith nodded slowly. "Three years. She bought three years, and she knew it was only three—she told me, that last night, *three years, Edie, maybe five, and by then they'll be ten and their voices will be too old to be sweet, and maybe it will have forgotten the taste.* She thought growing up would save you. That's what she bought with her life—not your lives, your *childhoods*. She thought if the debt could just be held off until you were grown, the hunger would pass you over like measles.

"But children remember songs. And the debt remembers voices. And one of you kept singing. Not at the well—never at the well, we made sure of that—but in the bath, in the garden, walking home from school, that tune, her tune, carrying her voice in it like a letter carries a scent. And the thing below heard her in the child's mouth, and it remembered what it had been promised and what it had been given, and it understood it had been *shortchanged*. A mother's voice for three years. It wanted the original terms restored. It wanted a child."

Edith stopped. The rain filled the silence for her, and she let it, because the next part had a name in it and she had not said the name aloud in thirty years and her mouth had gone stiff around it like a joint that's never used.

"And on the night it came," she said finally, "Maren's bargain held just long enough to matter. Just long enough for one child to be hidden and one to be—"

She couldn't finish. She looked at Tamsin, who was looking at the cut page from the hymnal, the verse excised so cleanly, and Edith saw the exact moment the arithmetic finished itself behind those tired eyes—the moment they understood that the story they had carried for thirty years, the story of which twin, the story of who drowned and who survived, was a story that had been *arranged*—and that Edith had been one of the hands that arranged it.

"You hid one of us," Tamsin said. "That night. You were there."

"I was there." Her voice came out small. "Maren asked me to be. She said, *if it ever comes to it, Edie, take the one who's sleeping and don't tell me which, and don't tell the other, and don't tell anyone, ever, because a secret that only one person holds is a secret the well can't read.* So I took a sleeping child out of that house in a blanket, and I carried them across three fields to the vicarage, and I never let myself know which one I carried, because knowing was a door and I had promised to keep it shut. And the next morning there was one child in the farmhouse and the well had taken the other, and the village said *drowned, the poor mite, drowned*, and we let it say that too, because drowned is a grief and *taken* is a debt, and grief you can bury but a debt you have to keep paying.

"And the child I'd hidden—" Edith's throat closed. She pressed her knuckles to her mouth for a moment. "The child I'd hidden woke up in the vicarage and didn't cry. Seven years old, in a strange bed, and didn't cry. Just looked at me and said, *you have to tell everyone I'm the other one now, or it will come back for the wrong name.* Seven years old. Your mother's eyes. And I said yes. And that was the second lie, and it was bigger than the first, and it's the one I've kept longest, and it's the one I'm most sorry for, because it made you live a whole life under a name that wasn't—"

She stopped. Because Tamsin had gone absolutely still, the stillness of a person standing on ice and hearing it begin to speak.

"Wasn't theirs," Tamsin finished.

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke. The rain eased. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"I don't know which I carried," Edith said at last, gently, because the gentlest thing left was the truth itself, laid down like a blanket. "I've never known. Maren didn't know. The vicar didn't know. That was the design—your mother's design. She said the well could only take what was named to it, and if no one living knew which name belonged to which child, then the debt would stay confused, and confusion was mercy. She built the lie to be unresolvable. She built it out of love, child, every stone of it. And you have spent thirty years turning it over in the dark, and I am sorry, I am so sorry, because there is no answer down at the bottom of it. There's only the song. And the song is the only thing in this valley that knows the truth, and it's kept it longer than any of us."

Tamsin stood. They looked at the tin, at the bracelet, at the photograph of Maren laughing at someone out of frame—and then at the cut hymnal page, and their hand went out toward it and stopped, hovering, the way you hover over a door you've been told all your life never to open.

"Mrs. Callow," they said. "The last verse. The one she cut out. The one she hid in the lullaby. I need it. All of it."

Edith looked at the door to the little room, at the chair where Maren had sat, and felt thirty years of careful silence come to the end of itself like a rope paying out to its final knot.

"I can't give it to you," she said. "Not because I won't. Because I never learned it. Maren cut it out of every book in the parish and she only ever sang the last verse once, whole, in one place, where it could be heard properly—and that place was the well's mouth, at night, the night before she went down. She made me stand at the top and listen, so that someone would know it existed. But the verse was never meant for ears, child. It was meant for the water. And the water keeps it. And if you want it now—if it's come to that—then there's only one way left to learn it, and you already know what it is, because your mother knew it, and I think you've known it since the night you set that recorder by your bed."

She reached out and closed Tamsin's fingers, old hands around young ones, around the hands of a child she had once carried across three fields without letting herself know whose they were.

"The well is singing again," she said. "I've heard it. Three nights now, under the rain. It's singing the verse *to* you, Tamsin. It's been waiting thirty years for a Ravensworth to come down and listen."
