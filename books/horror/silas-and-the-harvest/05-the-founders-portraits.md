## 5. The Founders' Portraits

The library at Hollowbrook Academy was the kind of place that pretended to be open.

It had tall windows and generous light in the afternoons, green-shaded lamps on long oak tables, a card catalog no one had bothered to digitize because, the librarians said, *the old system still works fine*. It smelled of paper dust and lemon polish and, beneath that, something older—stone, maybe, or the memory of stone. Students came here to study. That was its costume.

Silas had learned in her first month that the library kept its real self in the back, past the periodicals, where the shelving went from alphabetical to merely chronological, where books slumped against each other like old men on a bench, spines cracked, titles worn to ghosts of gold leaf. Nobody dusted back there. Nobody, as far as she could tell, had wanted anything back there for decades.

Which was exactly why she'd been coming every evening for two weeks.

It started with Wren. It always circled back to Wren—the empty desk third from the window, the name that teachers' tongues slid over like a stone in a shoe. Silas had gone looking for records because grief needs somewhere to put its hands, and what she'd found instead was a school with too many locked drawers and a history that had been edited by someone with very steady hands.

Tonight the stacks were hers alone. Rain worked at the high windows, a soft persistent fingernail-tapping, and the radiators ticked like patient clocks. Silas pulled her cardigan tight and knelt in front of the bottom shelf of the local history section, where the books leaned at drunk angles, and drew out a volume she'd been circling for days: *Hollowbrook: A Founding History, 1847–1902*, bound in leather gone the color of weak tea.

She carried it to the far table, under the lamp with the green shade, and opened it with the care you give something that might be sleeping.

The first hundred pages were exactly what they appeared to be—benefactors, building dates, a photograph of the original chapel with its foundation stones being laid by men in dark coats and stiff collars. She skimmed, taking notes in the margins of her notebook, letting the prose wash over her. And then, near the middle, a chapter heading in small caps:

*THE FOUR WHO BUILT.*

Silas sat up straighter.

There were four portraits reproduced in grainy halftone, arranged in a square. The captions named them: **Edmund Hollow**, whose gift of land had given the school its name; **Abigail Voss**, sharp-eyed even in miniature, her mouth set like a closed door; **Thomas Reed**, young and bearded, one hand resting on a stack of books; and **Margaret Marsh**.

Silas's breath caught.

Marsh. Her own surname, printed in a book older than her grandmother, attached to a woman with pale eyes and hair pinned in a style Silas recognized from photographs on her grandmother's mantel—a style that had passed down through generations like an heirloom nobody talked about.

She looked closer at Margaret Marsh's portrait and felt the thrill rise in her chest, bright and cold, the feeling she lived for—the scholar's vertigo, the sense of standing at the edge of something true. This was her family. Her blood had helped build this school. The scholarship that had carried her out of her grandmother's cramped house and into these halls wasn't charity at all. It was, perhaps, a return.

Then she looked at the fourth portrait.

The caption read **Margaret Marsh**, but the image above it had been ruined. Someone had taken a blade—or a pin, or a fingernail, patiently, over years—to the face, scratching it away down to the paper's tooth, leaving a pale oval blur where eyes and mouth should have been. The scratch marks radiated outward like cracks in ice. Whoever had done it hadn't been careless. They had been thorough. Almost tender, in the way that destruction done repeatedly becomes tender.

Silas touched the page with one fingertip. The paper there felt different—slightly roughened, slightly warm, though everything under the lamp should have been the same temperature.

"Founding families," said a voice behind her, and she nearly knocked the lamp over.

Mr. Abernathy, the head librarian, stood at the end of the table holding a reshelving cart. He was seventy if he was a day, all elbows and cardigan, with reading glasses pushed up into white hair. He was also, Silas had noticed, one of the few adults at Hollowbrook who ever looked directly at her.

"For your essay?" he asked.

"Sort of." She turned the book slightly, instinctively hiding the scratched portrait, then made herself stop hiding it. "I didn't know my family was connected to the school."

Abernathy came closer. His eyes went to the page, to the ruined face, and something moved behind his glasses—not surprise. Recognition. The look of a man greeting an old acquaintance he'd hoped was dead.

"Four families built this place," he said. "Hollow, Voss, Reed, Marsh." He said the names evenly, but Silas heard the pause before *Marsh*, a half-beat, like a stair with one step missing. "You'll find the school is very proud of three of them."

"And the fourth?"

"The fourth," Abernathy said, "is why we keep the lights on."

He said it mildly, the way you'd say *that's why we lock the gates at night*, and then he pushed his cart onward into the stacks, wheels whispering, leaving Silas staring at the space where he'd been.

*Why we keep the lights on.* She thought of Ivo Voss walking the corridors at dusk, checking fixtures, counting doors. Voss. Abigail Voss's line, presumably—he'd have grown up inside this history the way she'd grown up outside it. Maybe that explained him. Maybe it didn't explain the lights.

She wrote the phrase in her notebook anyway, underlined twice.

* * *

The ledger found her two nights later, and afterward Silas would never quite believe it was accident.

She'd come back for the founding history, meaning to cross-reference names against the alumni rolls, but the volume wasn't where she'd left it. In its place on the shelf, wedged spine-inward as if someone had shoved it back in haste or shame, was a slim book with no title at all—just a leather cover, cracked and dark, tied shut with a ribbon that had once been red.

She should have taken it to Abernathy. She knew that even as her fingers worked the bow loose. But the bow fell apart at her touch, the ribbon crumbling like a dead thing, and the book opened itself across her palms, and there was no longer any version of the evening in which she walked it to the front desk.

It was a ledger. Financial, at first glance—columns of dates, columns of entries, a clerk's careful hand that changed style every few decades as one keeper died and another took up the pen. The early pages were mundane: roof repairs, coal deliveries, tuition remitted for the children of donors. Silas flipped forward, skimming, almost disappointed.

Then, in 1866, the format changed.

A new hand, smaller and more pressed-down, began each entry with a single word: ***Tithe.***

*Tithe — 1866. Paid in full. The ground is quiet.*

*Tithe — 1885. Paid in full. The ground is quiet.*

*Tithe — 1904. Paid in full. The ground is quiet.*

Nineteen years apart. Every entry. Nineteen years, nineteen years, nineteen years, marching down the page like footprints toward her. Silas did the arithmetic without wanting to, the way you count stairs in the dark. 1923. 1942. 1961. 1980. 1999.

Her pencil stopped moving.

2018. The last entry was dated this year, in ink so fresh it still shone faintly under the lamp—but the line beside it was blank. Not *paid in full*. Not *the ground is quiet*. Just the date, waiting, and after it a dash, and after the dash nothing at all except the indentations of a pen pressed hard enough to tear slightly at the next page, as though whoever had written the date had been gripping the pen the way you grip a railing.

*This year falls due.*

Silas became aware, gradually, of sounds she'd been ignoring: the rain, the radiators, her own heartbeat loud in her ears. She became aware of the light overhead—one of the library's ceiling fixtures, the kind Ivo Voss checked every evening—flickering once, twice, in a rhythm that felt less like faulty wiring than like attention.

She looked around the empty reading room. Green lamps. Long shadows. The windows black with rain. Somewhere in the stacks, a book shifted on a shelf—just settling, just old wood and gravity—and she told herself that firmly, twice.

The later entries weren't all identical. As she turned pages with hands that insisted on trembling, she saw that some years the clerk had written more. In 1942, beneath *Paid in full*, in a different, shakier script: *a girl from the Reed line, fourteen years old, taken from the east dormitory. The family was compensated. The record expunged.* In 1961: *the Whitlock boy. No one will speak his name. This is the arrangement.* In 1980, only four words, the handwriting crushed small as if trying to take up no space at all: *God forgive us. Quiet again.*

And in 1999—the most recent harvest, nineteen years ago, the year Silas was born—there was no entry at all. Just a page torn out. Ragged at the stub. Someone had removed it, violently, recently enough that the fibers were still pale.

Silas sat very still.

Every nineteen years, something was paid. Children, sometimes, if the shaky marginalia could be believed—children taken from dormitories, their names scrubbed from records, their families bought off or frightened silent. Expunged. *Expunged* was a word she knew now in her bones; she'd watched it happen to Wren in real time, watched a whole school close around an absence like water closing over a stone.

Wren had disappeared in October. Nineteen years since 1999. The math sat in Silas's chest like swallowed ice.

"It takes whoever remembers you least," she whispered—the words from Wren's locker note, which she had not yet shown anyone, which she carried folded in her wallet like a splinter. Except now the note meant something worse. Now it sounded like a rule. A mechanism. A clause in a contract written before either of them was born.

She copied the ledger entries into her notebook until her pencil wore blunt—dates, tithes, the marginal confessions—because some part of her understood, with a scholar's cold clarity, that the original might not survive her possession of it. Books that revealed things had a way of disappearing at Hollowbrook. People did too.

When she finally looked up, the reading room clock said nearly midnight. The rain had stopped. The silence it left behind was enormous, and in that silence she heard, distinctly, from somewhere deep in the stacks, a door close.

Not slam. Close. Gently. The way you close a door when you don't want to wake someone—or when you've finished counting.

Silas packed the ledger inside her coat, against her ribs, where its warmth seemed to pulse faintly, though she told herself it was only her own body heat. On her way out she passed the shelf where the founding history should have been.

It was back. Exactly where she'd left it, squared neatly between its neighbors.

She hadn't reshelved it.

She stood in the aisle a long moment, breathing the paper-dust air, looking at the book that had returned itself. Then, deliberately, she reached out and opened it to the portraits, to the four faces in their square, to the caption that read *Margaret Marsh* above a ruin of scratches.

Under the lamp's green-white light, she noticed something she'd missed before. The scratches weren't random. Radiating from the ruined face, cut deep and deliberate into the page, was a pattern—lines crossing lines, a shape she almost recognized, like a star drawn wrong, or a window seen from underneath.

Or a chair. Seen from above. Four legs, a seat, a high straight back.

The same shape, she realized with a lurch, that was carved—small, almost invisible unless you knew to look—into the stone lintel above the chapel door. The same shape her grandmother's hands had flinched away from tracing when Silas had asked about it last holiday, before turning the conversation, as she always did, to safer things.

Silas closed the book gently, the way you close a door when you don't want to wake someone.

Outside, the campus lay silvered and still under a sky already clearing toward a moon that was, she now understood, beginning its slow climb toward fullness. Along the humanities corridor, every single light burned in its fixture—every one, on a night when the cleaning staff should have gone home hours ago.

And at the far end of the corridor, a tall figure in a dark coat stood at a door with a notebook in his hand, lips moving silently, counting.

Ivo Voss looked up. Across forty yards of polished floor, his eyes found hers, and Silas saw something in his face that was not anger and not surprise but a grim, weary recognition—the look of a man watching a debt come due and knowing, finally, who the collector would ask for next.

He raised one finger to his lips.

Then he pointed, slowly and deliberately, at the ledger-shaped bulge beneath her coat, and mouthed a single word she couldn't hear across the distance but understood perfectly anyway:

*Tonight.*
