## 17. Three Lines Gathered

Ivo Voss had spent thirty-one years learning that consent was the rarest currency in Hollowbrook. Everything else could be bought, bullied, or inherited—land, silence, the careful arrangement of a town's memory—but consent had to be carried across a threshold like a candle, and it went out at the first breath of fear.

He carried the ledger with him that morning, wrapped in oilcloth beneath his coat, because the covenant demanded the blood of five lines and the ledger demanded proof that each line had given it *willingly*. That was the cruelty stitched into the founders' arithmetic: the ritual could not be completed by force. It could only be completed by persuasion, and persuasion, in Hollowbrook, had always been a polite word for pressure.

Three lines remained. He went to gather them the way a man goes to collect a debt he never agreed to hold.

* * *

The Calloway house sat on the rise above the orchards, all white clapboard and black shutters, the kind of house that had been renovated so many times it had forgotten its own age. Ivo had taught Tobias Calloway's older brother, and his father before that, and his father's father. The Calloways were the third founder line—merchants, originally, men who had sold the town its first lumber and its first lies in the same transaction.

Tobias was seventeen, broad-shouldered, captain of the crew team, with his grandfather's heavy jaw and his mother's quick, calculating eyes. He met Ivo at the door already dressed for the day, though it was barely seven, as though he had been waiting.

"Mr. Voss." He stepped aside to let him in. "I wondered when you'd come."

Ivo paused on the threshold. "Did you."

"The lights." Tobias closed the door behind them. "My grandfather kept a bulb burning in the hall his whole life. Never let anyone change it. When it burned out last month, my mother cried. I thought she was losing her mind." He turned, and there it was in his face—not fear, not confusion, but appetite. "Then the first-years started going missing, and I did the math. You're here about the ritual. You're here because I'm one of the five."

The boy said *ritual* the way other boys said *scholarship* or *inheritance*. Ivo set his case on the kitchen table and did not unwrap the ledger. "Tobias. Before you say another word, I need you to understand what this costs. Not what it buys. What it *costs*."

"I've read what I could find." Tobias pulled out a chair, sat, folded his hands like a man closing a deal. "My grandfather's papers. There's a box in the attic I wasn't supposed to open. Tithes. Nineteen-year intervals. Names." He smiled, and the smile was the worst part of it, because it was entirely without malice. It was the smile of a boy who believed the world had finally offered him terms he could win on. "I'm not afraid. I want to do it. I want my name in that book. When this is over—when the school is safe, when the town remembers what my family *did* for it—there will be people who owe the Calloways. There always have been. My grandfather understood that. The founders understood it. You give blood, you get standing."

"Your grandfather," Ivo said quietly, "gave a name to the harvest in 1967. A girl named Ada Finch. She was fifteen. He wrote her name on a slip of paper and left it in the chapel, and she went, and the town slept well, and he got his standing. There's a photograph of him at the harvest dinner that November, and he is *smiling*, Tobias. That is what your family's standing is built on."

Something flickered behind the boy's eyes—brief, quickly banked, like a match struck in a draft.

"I'm not my grandfather."

"No. You're not. That's why I'm asking instead of taking." Ivo unwrapped the ledger at last and laid it open on the table, and watched the boy's breath change when he saw the founder's marks, the columns of dates, the dried rust-colored stains that no archivist had ever been permitted to call what they were. "The ritual needs your blood given freely. Not coerced. Not traded. The covenant is specific, and the covenant is *old*, and it has teeth about this that neither of us fully understands. So I will ask you once, plainly, and whatever you say, I will believe you meant it."

Tobias stared down at the ledger for a long moment. His jaw worked. When he looked up, the appetite was still there, but underneath it now was something rawer and more honest: the simple, human wish to matter.

"Will it work? Will it hold?"

"It was never finished. That's why it's failing. We finish it, it holds—*something* holds. I can't promise you what."

"And my family gets their due."

Ivo felt the old weariness settle over him like a coat. "The covenant doesn't trade in dues. It trades in blood. What you make of it afterward is between you and your conscience."

Tobias rolled up his sleeve. He took the lancet from Ivo's hand without trembling, and pressed the pad of his thumb to the fifth founder's mark—no, the third, Ivo corrected himself, the third line—and said, in a clear voice, the words Ivo dictated: *I give this freely, of my own will, for the holding of the bound.*

The blood beaded dark against the stone-dust rubbed into the carved line, and the ledger's page seemed to draw it in, and Tobias Calloway sat back looking, for one unguarded second, like a boy who had just signed something he could not read.

"Thank you," Ivo said, and hated how much it sounded like an apology.

* * *

The second line lived at the poor edge of town, in a narrow house on Bellwether Lane where the paint had given up years ago and a garden gone to seed testified to someone who had stopped tending things. Maren Oduya answered the door herself. She was sixty-one, a retired nurse, the last of the fourth founder line, and she had been expecting Ivo too—though her expectation looked nothing like Tobias's.

She looked, Ivo thought, like a woman who had already died a little and was simply waiting for the rest to catch up.

"You'd better come in," she said. "I've got the kettle on. My mother said you'd come before the moon turned. She said it like a sentence being read out."

The kitchen smelled of chamomile and dust. Photographs covered one wall—generations of Oduyas, and in every one of the older frames, Ivo noted with a chill he had never quite learned to suppress, an empty chair stood beside the family group, angled toward the camera as though it were posing.

"You've seen them," Maren said, following his gaze. "Everyone sees them eventually. My mother spent her whole life trying to figure out which ancestor the chair belonged to. She died not knowing. I think the not knowing is what killed her, in the end." She poured two cups without asking whether he wanted one. "Sit down, Mr. Voss. Tell me what my blood is for."

He told her. All of it—the binding, the unfinished ritual, the tithes, the nineteen years, the students who had gone, the entity beneath the chapel learning Silas Marsh's name. He told her because she had the right to it, and because he had learned that the broken ones broke worse when you lied to them gently.

Maren listened with her hands wrapped around her cup, and when he finished she was quiet for a long time.

"Nineteen years ago," she said. "That was Danny Pruitt. I was his school nurse. He came in with a scraped knee the week before he went, and I bandaged it, and I remember thinking he had his whole life in front of him." Her voice did not waver, which was somehow worse than if it had. "And the town forgot him in a fortnight. I didn't. I *couldn't*. I wrote his name in my diary every year on the anniversary so somebody would remember. Forty-one pages, Mr. Voss. Forty-one entries for a boy the whole world agreed to lose."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be honest with me." She set down the cup. "If I give my blood, and the ritual holds, does it happen again? In nineteen years? Do I get to look forward to another Danny Pruitt?"

Ivo looked at his hands. "The ritual as the founders wrote it—yes. It would hold until the next debt came due. That's what binding means. It doesn't end the hunger. It schedules it."

"Then it's not a cure. It's a prescription." She laughed, a dry, terrible sound. "I was a nurse for thirty years. I know what we do with prescriptions. We hand them out and we don't look too hard at the pain underneath."

"Maren—"

"But you're not offering me the founders' ritual, are you." Her eyes came up, and they were sharp, sharper than Tobias's had ever been, because they had been sharpened by grief instead of ambition. "There's a girl. The Marsh girl. I've seen her at the market, asking her questions, getting those lovely blank smiles. She's after something different. I can smell it. My mother smelled it in her grandmother too, God rest the cowards." She held out her hand, palm up, steady. "So ask me the real question, Mr. Voss. Not the ledger's question. Hers."

And Ivo, who had rehearsed a dozen careful formulations on the walk over, found that none of them survived her eyes. He said only: "There may be another way. Blood given, not taken. Freely, without bargain, without standing, without anything in return. It's in the covenant's oldest clause—the one the founders buried because it offered them nothing. If it works, it doesn't schedule the hunger. It ends the bargain entirely."

"Then that's the one I'll give my blood to." She said it simply, the way she must once have said *this will hurt but it will heal*. "Not to the binding. Not to the founders and their ledgers and their empty chairs. To the ending. Write my name against that."

He gave her the lancet. Her hand did not shake. When her blood touched the fourth line, she closed her eyes and let out a breath she might have been holding since Danny Pruitt's scraped knee.

"There," she said. "Forty-two entries. The last one says *paid in full*."

* * *

The headmaster received him at four o'clock in an office that smelled of lemon polish and old privilege. Aldous Crane sat behind a desk that had belonged to every headmaster since the school's founding, and behind him hung the four portraits—loaned, Ivo had always suspected, less as tribute than as collateral.

"You've been collecting," Crane said, without preamble. He turned a heavy signet ring on his finger—the Crane mark, the second founder line. "The Calloway boy. Old Maren Oduya. My sources are better than you think, Ivo. I taught half this town."

"You know what's coming, then."

"I know what's *always* come." Crane rose, crossed to the window, looked out over the quad where students moved in the long gold light of the failing afternoon, unaware, unmarked, uncounted. "Nineteen years ago I was a young master here. I watched Whitlock choose the Pruitt boy, and I told myself it was necessary, and I slept, Ivo. I *slept*. Do you know what that's worth? The ability to sleep?" He turned back. "I'll not pretend I'm innocent. But I am the headmaster of this school, and I will tell you the terms on which my blood enters that book."

"There are no terms. The covenant—"

"The covenant is a piece of paper written by frightened rich men two centuries ago, and you know it." Crane's voice stayed level, which made it worse. "Here are my terms. The Marsh girl. She's the last line, and she's marked, and she's *willing*—I've watched her walking toward it for weeks like a lamb counting its own steps to the slaughter. Give me her name for the tithe. One girl, freely offered—she'd offer, I'd make certain of it—and the ritual completes, the school is safe, the town forgets again, and everyone lives. Everyone who matters."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere down the corridor a radiator ticked, and Ivo found himself counting the sounds the way he counted doors—out of habit, out of the warden's reflex that had kept him alive this long.

"You're asking me to do what Whitlock did," Ivo said.

"I'm asking you to do what *works*."

"It didn't work. It never worked. It bought nineteen years and a boy's whole life, and the debt came back with interest, and now it's learning the name of a child who never asked to be a founder. That's the arithmetic, Aldous. That's always been the arithmetic. Every tithe is a loan against the future, and the future has come to collect."

Crane's face hardened. "Then you'll fail. And when the moon rises and the undercroft opens and that *thing* comes up through the floor looking for its fifth line, you'll come crawling to my terms, and my price will have gone up." He sat back down behind his desk, the portrait of his ancestor glowering over his shoulder, and picked up a pen as though Ivo were merely the day's third appointment. "Get out, Ivo. And take your ledger with you. I don't sign things I intend to survive."

Ivo stood in the corridor outside for a long moment, the oilcloth bundle heavy under his arm. Three lines gathered. Two freely, in their different currencies—ambition and grief. One withheld, priced in blood that was not the headmaster's own.

And through the tall window at the corridor's end, he could see the sky over the chapel, where the moon was already swelling, and even in the daylight it had begun, faintly, obscenely, to blush.

That night, the first bulb over the chapel doors burst, and the door beneath it stood open onto a darkness that had not been there when he counted at dusk.

*Four*, Ivo thought, and reached for his coat. *It's learning to count too.*
