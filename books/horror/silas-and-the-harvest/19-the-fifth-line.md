## 19. The Fifth Line

The undercroft smelled of cold stone and old iron, and beneath both—faint as a memory of a memory—the sweetness of ripe apples rotting in an orchard no one had tended for a century.

Silas stood at the edge of the stone table, the covenant pressed flat against her ribs inside her coat, and watched the four founder marks catch the lantern light. Three of them were dark. The fourth, at the table's northern edge, had begun to weep.

Not the stone. The mark itself. A thin thread of red seeped from the carved channels as though the rock beneath had a pulse, and with each pulse the air in the undercroft grew warmer, sweeter, more patient.

"Three lines gathered," Ivo said quietly. He stood opposite her, sleeves rolled, his hands stained to the wrist with other people's blood—Marguerite Hale's given in trembling consent, Tobias Finch's given with the desperate eagerness of a boy who believed the school would finally love him for it. "Three lines freely given, as the covenant requires. The fourth is the headmaster's."

"And the fifth is mine."

"And the fifth is yours." He would not dress it up. She was grateful for that, even now. Especially now. "Which is why we have perhaps an hour, and why he is coming down those stairs."

She heard them then—footsteps on the chapel floor above, measured and unhurried, the tread of a man who had never once in his life needed to hurry because the world had always arranged itself around him. The trapdoor opened. Lamplight spilled down the stair, and Headmaster Aldous Crane descended into the dark with the calm of a landlord inspecting property.

He was not alone. Two proctors flanked him, and between them, held gently by the arm as though she might be escorted somewhere pleasant, came a first-year girl. Silas knew her face from the corridor, from the dining hall, from the endless small traffic of a school. She had been missing for six days. Her eyes were open and empty as windows in an unlit house.

"No," Silas said.

Crane smiled the smile he used at assemblies, warm and paternal and utterly without content. "Miss Marsh. Mr. Voss. You've been busy. I confess I expected nothing less from a Marsh."

"Her name is Polly Vane," Silas said. "She's fourteen."

"She is collateral," Crane said, "and she will be returned the moment this is concluded. You misunderstand me if you think I take any pleasure in—"

"Then let her go."

"I will let *everyone* go." Crane's voice softened, and that was worse. He came to the table and laid one hand flat upon the stone, beside the fourth mark, the way a man touches a thing he believes he owns. "That is the entire point, Silas. May I call you Silas? We are family, after all. Distant, perhaps, but the blood remembers. The binding completes tonight, the harvest is paid, the children come home, and Hollowbrook goes on being Hollowbrook for another nineteen years. All that is required is that you stop sentimentalizing and give what you were born to give."

*What you were born to give.* Silas felt the words settle on her like a hand on the back of her neck. All her life she had been the scholarship girl, the outsider, the one who counted the cost of everything because nothing had ever been given to her freely—and here, at the end, the town had finally found a use for her. Belonging, it turned out, had always been a bill.

"You tried to buy me first," she said. "Protection. A place. You offered to spend Polly Vane's life for my comfort, and when I refused, you went looking for another way to spend me."

"I went looking for the *only* way," Crane said, and for a moment the paternal warmth dropped away and something older looked out of his face—something that had been fed for generations on other people's arithmetic. "Do you imagine the founders were monsters? They were tired. They were frightened. They made the best bargain available to frightened people, and every generation since has kept the books. That is what a civilization *is*, Miss Marsh. Someone pays so that the rest may sleep."

"Then pay yourself," Silas said.

The words dropped into the undercroft and lay there.

Crane's hand lifted from the stone. When he turned to Ivo, his voice was almost gentle. "Voss. Step aside. You have served this institution your whole life—your father before you, his mother before him. You have counted every door and changed every bulb and told yourself it was duty. One more duty. Hold the girl's arm. That's all. Hold her arm, and in nineteen years you'll be an old man in a quiet school, and no one will ever know what it cost."

Silas looked at Ivo.

Ivo looked at the table—at the four marks, three dark and one weeping—and at Polly Vane swaying between the proctors like a coat on a hook, and at the stair behind him, up which he had climbed and descended a thousand times in faithful, exhausted service to a lie he had told himself was stewardship.

"I counted the doors," he said.

Crane frowned. "What?"

"Every door in this school. Every threshold. For thirty years I counted them, because my grandmother taught me that a counted threshold is a kept threshold, and I believed her. I believed that keeping was the same as saving." He rolled his sleeves down, slowly, and buttoned the cuffs. "It isn't. Keeping is just a slower way of feeding. And I am done feeding."

"Aldous," Ivo said, and used the headmaster's Christian name for the first time in Silas's hearing, "I am going to ask the proctors to take that child up those stairs and out into the open air. And then I am going to ask you to open your own vein, freely, or stand aside while a Marsh does what your family never had the courage to do—which is finish the work honestly."

The headmaster looked at him for a long moment.

"You would turn against everything," Crane said, "for a scholarship girl."

"I would turn against everything," Ivo said, "for the truth. The institution was supposed to serve that. Somewhere along the way it started serving the harvest instead, and men like you and me kept the lights on so we wouldn't have to see."

* * *

It happened fast, after that. It always does, Silas thought later; the horror of the world is mostly a matter of bookkeeping, and bookkeeping is done quickly, in the dark, by men who do not wish to watch themselves.

Crane did not argue. That was the thing she had not anticipated—the absence of argument. He simply nodded to his proctors, and they moved.

Ivo moved faster.

He had spent thirty years learning the undercroft the way a blind man learns a room, and the lantern went over in his first stride and the dark came down like water, and in the dark Silas heard the flat crack of a body meeting stone and the wet sound of breath knocked out of a man, and then Ivo's voice, low and terrible in its calm: "Up the stairs. Both of you. Now."

She heard them go—scrambling, one dragging the other—and she heard Polly Vane's feet, light and unresisting, being half-carried, and then the trapdoor boomed and the lantern light guttered back up from the floor where Ivo had righted it, and the two of them were alone with the table and the fourth mark, which had begun to bleed faster.

Crane was on his knees by the table's southern corner, one hand pressed to his temple, blood threading between his fingers. He laughed. It was the worst sound Silas had ever heard—not because it was cruel, but because it was *relieved*.

"You see," he said. "You see what it does. Nineteen years I kept the books, and the moment I refuse to pay, it takes its interest out of my skull." He looked up at them, and his eyes were bright and wet. "I was going to let you do it, you know. The girl. I told myself she'd be returned, and perhaps she would have been, and in nineteen years some other headmaster would have stood where I stand and told himself the same story. That's the inheritance, Voss. Not the school. The *story*."

"Then end the story," Silas said.

"I can't." And the honesty in it was absolute, and it broke her heart a little, because she understood it—understood the cowardice not as wickedness but as weight, generations of it, pressing down until a man could no longer lift his own arm. "I gave my consent to the old covenant when I was nineteen years old and my father was dying. Consent given to *that* text. The blood it takes from me will be taken, not given. It will feed it. You must see that. You must—"

The floor moved.

Not shook—*moved*, the way a great animal shifts its weight beneath a blanket. The stone flags of the undercroft bowed upward in the center of the room, a slow swell, and from the seams between them came a smell so rich and sweet that Silas's mouth flooded and her stomach turned over together. Apples. Hay. The warm animal smell of a field at the end of summer, at the end of its usefulness.

"It knows," Ivo said. "It knows the books are open and the debt is due. Silas—" He was at the table now, pulling the covenant from her coat, spreading it across the stone beside the weeping mark, his finger moving down the columns of founder script. "The fifth line. It has to be yours, and it has to be *given*, and it has to be soon, because when the moon crests—"

The lantern flared and went out.

In the dark, the seams in the floor began to glow a faint, rotten gold, and through them Silas could hear it—not a voice, nothing so kind as a voice—a rhythm, a patient peristaltic pulse, the sound of something that had been swallowing for four hundred years and was very, very good at it.

"Silas." Ivo's hand found hers in the dark. His palm was slick with blood—his own, from the scuffle, or Crane's, or the table's. "Listen to me. The covenant's hidden clause. Your grandmother's book. *Blood given, not taken.* If you cut yourself and give it in fear, if you give it because the thing below is squeezing it out of you, it counts as a tithe and the binding completes and you become what the founders were—a keeper, a feeder, a chair that waits. It has to be free. Do you understand me? It has to cost you nothing you didn't choose."

"How," she said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her, "do I choose a thing while it's rising through the floor?"

"You tell the truth," Ivo said. "That's all the ritual ever was. The founders made it a machine because they couldn't stomach making it a confession."

The gold light in the seams brightened. The stone table shivered. From the southern corner came the sound of Aldous Crane weeping—not loudly, not dramatically, just the small exhausted crying of a man whose story had finally run out—and above them, through the chapel floor, through the whole skeleton of Hollowbrook Academy, Silas heard doors opening. Every door Ivo had counted, every threshold he had kept, swinging wide at once, and through the open doors came footsteps, hundreds of them, the hollow-eyed missing walking home across the harvest fields with the red moon rising at their backs.

The fifth mark carved itself into the table while she watched—no blade, no hand, just the stone splitting in a long slow line, fresh and waiting, at the place where a founder's blood was meant to fall.

Silas Marsh rolled up her sleeve.

"Ivo," she said. "Hold the light. Whatever happens—whatever I become—hold the light."

He struck the match, and the lantern caught, and by its small stubborn glow she laid her forearm across the fifth line, and the thing below went utterly, hunarily still—

—and waited, as it had waited for four hundred years, to see what she would give.
