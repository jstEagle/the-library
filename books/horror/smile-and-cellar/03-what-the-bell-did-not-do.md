## 3. What the Bell Did Not Do

The parish archive of Hollowmere occupied a single room above the vestry, reached by a staircase so narrow Edmund had to turn his recorder case sideways to bring it up. The room smelled of dust and mouse and the particular sweetness of paper that had been dying slowly for a century. One window, its glass warped into ripples, let in a grey morning light that seemed to fall already tired.

The key had been left for him under the third flowerpot from the left — Marta Hale's instruction, delivered without looking up from the glasses she was polishing. *"Reverend Ainsley kept the records tidy enough. Nobody's been up there since him. Mind the step on the landing; it's rotten."* That was all. No offer to show him round, no curiosity about what he wanted. In two days of canvassing Hollowmere he had collected eleven polite refusals, one slammed door, and a single sentence of lodging. He was beginning to feel like a man knocking on the outside of an egg.

He set his case down, blew dust from the first shelf, and got to work.

The registers were, as promised, tidy. Ainsley had been one of those country clergymen who believed God lived in the margins as much as the text, and his hand was small, upright, and patient. Edmund worked through baptisms first out of habit — names were the skeleton of any community, and you learned a village fastest by learning who it bothered to name. Hollowmere's font had run dry early. Births thinned through the twenties, thinned again through the forties, until by 1970 the entries came singly, like survivors.

But it was the churchwardens' accounts he wanted. And there, wedged between repairs to the boiler and a dispute over six shillings owed for lime-washing, he found it.

*October 1911. Received of Messrs. Crabbe & Sons, bellfounders, Leeds, the sum of £14 10s., being payment in full for the great bell of this parish, sold by order of the Parochial Church Council, the said bell to be broken up and the metal taken away.*

£14 10s. He read it twice. Somewhere in Leeds, in the autumn of 1911, a parish bell that had hung over Hollowmere for — he checked the earlier accounts — at least two hundred years had been fed into a furnace and poured out as scrap. There was even a note in Ainsley's margin, the only marginal note in the whole ledger that wasn't arithmetic: *Council voted 9–2. The expense of recasting could not be borne. It is better so.*

*It is better so.* Four words doing the work of a paragraph. Edmund copied them out carefully, along with the date, the sum, the foundry's name. His pen moved with the small pleasure of a man whose profession still functioned. Here was the whole mystery, solved before lunch: the village had sold its bell sixty years ago, the tower stood empty, and whatever Marta Hale thought he might ask about the church, the answer was a receipt. Villages sold their bells in hard times. Nothing haunted about it. Nothing at all.

Except, of course, that he had heard it.

He put the thought down the way you'd put down something hot. He had heard *a* sound, on his first evening, walking up from the crossroads — a single deep stroke rolling across the valley, felt more than heard, arriving through the soles of his boots before his ears. He had assumed a farm gate, a falling branch, a lorry on some distant road. The tower had been roofless. He'd seen it himself: four walls of honey-coloured stone open to the sky, the empty frame visible from below like the ribcage of something long dead. Bells do not ring themselves, and bells that have been melted in Leeds certainly do not.

He worked until the light began to fail. The archive had no lamp that worked, or perhaps no bulb at all, and by half past three in October the window gave out. He spent the last usable minutes on a box marked *Miscellaneous — Ainsley* and found sermons, harvest festival programmes, and a photograph of the bell itself, taken sometime around the turn of the century: a great dark shape hanging in gloom, its shoulder catching one stripe of light. Someone had written on the back in pencil, in a different, older hand:

*She has a good voice yet.*

Edmund turned the photograph over twice, as though the rest of the sentence might be hiding on the other side. It wasn't. He filed it carefully in his notebook, under a heading he wrote and then, feeling foolish, did not cross out: *The Bell — Voice.*

* * *

Marta Hale served him his supper in the taproom of the Lamb at six o'clock sharp, because that was when supper was served and not a minute after. The room held four men who looked up when he entered and looked down again when he sat, which was, he was learning, the local dialect of welcome.

"Found what you wanted?" Marta asked, setting down a plate of mutton and root vegetables that steamed like a small weather system.

"The sale of the bell. 1911."

"Mm." She wiped the table where nothing had been spilled.

"There's a receipt. Fourteen pounds ten shillings. The metal went to a foundry in Leeds." He said it lightly, offering her the rational explanation the way you'd offer a nervous animal your open hand. "So the tower's been empty for sixty years."

"Mm," Marta said again, and then, because she was the only person in Hollowmere who spoke to him in full sentences, she paused with the cloth in her hands and looked at him with an expression he couldn't file anywhere. Not warning, exactly. Something wearier than warning. "You'll be wanting to know why we never replaced it."

"It crossed my mind."

"Bells cost money." She said it flatly, a door closing rather than opening. "Eat while it's hot."

He ate while it was hot. The four men drank their pints in silence. Outside, the light went from grey to blue to the particular bruised violet that October does best, and the fire settled, and Edmund found himself listening — not meaning to, but listening — the way you find yourself watching a clock.

At some point he realised the men weren't silent so much as *held*. Their conversation, such as it was, had a shape to it: they talked about drainage and the price of feed and whether young Telfer would get his potatoes in before the frost, and they talked in the manner of people filling a room with ordinary sound on purpose, the way you'd fill a house with lamps against the dark. One of them, the oldest, kept glancing at the window. Not nervously. Glancing the way a man checks a watch.

"What time does it get properly dark out here?" Edmund asked, mostly to break the surface tension.

"Quarter past five," said the oldest man, instantly, precisely, and then looked surprised at himself, as if the answer had come from somewhere deeper than courtesy.

"And what happens at quarter past five?"

Nothing happened at quarter past five. The men finished their drinks and went home to their suppers, each pausing at the door to look up at the sky, and Marta began stacking glasses, and Edmund carried his notebook to the corner table by the window where the lamplight was good, and told himself he was transcribing the day's finds while the information was fresh.

At seventeen minutes past five, the bell rang.

One stroke. Deep, broad, unhurried — a sound with weight and width to it, rolling up the valley from the direction of the church, passing through the walls of the Lamb as though walls were a rumour, entering the room through the floor and the soles of Edmund's feet and the bones of his chair. The windowpane buzzed faintly in sympathy. The lamp flame leaned, very slightly, toward the sound.

Edmund was on his feet before he knew he'd stood. His chair scraped. His heart was going like a trapped bird, and he was ashamed of it, and he was also, in some cellar of himself he didn't visit often, *delighted* — the scholar's awful delight, the part of every folklorist that hears a ghost story and thinks *primary source.*

"That's not—" he began. "The tower's empty. I've seen it. The bell was melted in 1911, there's a receipt—"

"I know," Marta said.

She hadn't looked up. That was the thing that stopped him mid-sentence, that made the delight curdle and go cold in his chest. She stood with her cloth in her hand, wiping a glass that was already clean, and she had not looked up, had not flinched, had not gone to the window. Neither had anyone else — but everyone else had gone home. It was only the two of them, and the fire, and the second toll beginning to fade somewhere out over the fields like a stone sinking in slow water.

"You heard it too," he said.

"I hear it every year, this time." She set the glass down with a small precise click. "Every evening from now till harvest's done. Once a night. Just the once."

"From an empty tower."

"From an empty tower." She finally raised her eyes to his, and he saw that what he'd taken for weariness was something else entirely: the look of a woman standing in a river she'd stopped fighting years ago, letting it push against her because pushing back took strength she needed for other things. "Mr. Voss. You're a collector of songs. So collect this one, if you like: there are things in this valley that don't care what's written in ledgers. Paper says the bell's gone. Paper's right. The bell's gone." A pause, exactly long enough. "That doesn't mean it's finished ringing."

"That's not possible."

"No," Marta agreed. "It isn't." And she picked up his plate and carried it out to the kitchen, and that was the end of the conversation, because in Hollowmere it appeared that all conversations ended that way — not concluded, but *closed*, like doors on rooms with lights still burning inside.

* * *

He did not sleep well, which was unusual for him; he was a man who slept the way he worked, thoroughly and without sentiment. But the toll had gotten into him somehow, lodged under the sternum like a swallowed coin, and every time he drifted toward sleep it sounded again — not out loud, he understood that much, but in the body, in the ribs, one deep stroke rolling through the dark of his own chest. At some hour past midnight he gave up, lit the lamp, and opened his notebook to make sense of the day the way you'd set a broken bone: firmly, and quickly, before the swelling started.

*Facts,* he wrote.

*1. The great bell of Hollowmere parish was sold 4 Oct. 1911 to Crabbe & Sons, Leeds, for £14 10s., and melted down. Receipt extant. Council vote 9–2. Clergyman's note: "It is better so."*

*2. The tower stands roofless and empty. Verified by direct observation.*

*3. On the evening of [date], at approximately 17:17, a single bell-stroke was heard throughout the valley, originating from the direction of the church. Witnessed by E.V. Corroborated (tacitly) by M. Hale, who states the phenomenon recurs nightly from early autumn until harvest and is known to the village.*

*4. No villager will discuss the church.*

He sat back and looked at the list. It was a good list. It was the kind of list that won grants. Any rational man would draw the obvious conclusions: acoustic phenomenon — wind through the empty frame, resonance in the tower stonework, a trick of the valley's peculiar geography carrying some distant sound. He had recorded stranger things. He had a tape in his collection of a Cornish mine that groaned in F-sharp when the tide turned. The world was full of hollow places that made noises, and people were full of stories to explain them, and both facts were interesting and neither required belief.

And yet.

And yet the men in the taproom had known the time to the minute. And yet Marta had not looked up. And yet — and this was the fact he could not get to sit straight on the page, the splinter under the skin — nobody in Hollowmere had looked up. He had walked the village for two days. He knew now how these people watched strangers: sideways, patiently, missing nothing. If a bell rang from a ruined tower every autumn evening, the rational responses were fear, wonder, superstition, pride, annoyance — any response at all. What the villagers offered instead was the response of people for whom the event had been absorbed so completely into the furniture of life that it no longer qualified as an event. You do not look up when the floor creaks. You do not look up when the house settles.

Unless, said a voice in him that he did not like, you have learned that looking up is how it knows you've noticed.

He wrote that down too, then struck it through, then un-struck it, because a folklorist's first duty was to record, and his second duty — the one he'd chosen the profession to escape — was to notice what the recording meant about himself. He had come to Hollowmere because old songs die with old people and someone ought to write them down. That was true. It was also true that he had no one waiting anywhere, that his London rooms contained a camp bed and four hundred tapes and letters from two colleagues and a landlady, and that a village which refused to speak to him had nonetheless given him more attention in two days than the world had in ten years. He was not going to leave. They both knew it — he and the valley, whatever the valley was.

Before bed he checked the window latch, which was absurd, and listened, which was worse. The night was enormous and quiet. Somewhere out in the black fields a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

In the morning he would go back up to the archive. There was a box he hadn't finished — *Miscellaneous, pre-Ainsley* — and a photograph of a bell with *she has a good voice yet* pencilled on the back, and a foundry called Crabbe & Sons whose own records might say what became of fourteen pounds ten shillings' worth of Hollowmere bronze. There was always another document. That was the faith he'd built his life on: that everything leaves a paper trail, that the dead are talkative if you learn their filing systems, that the world is ultimately legible.

He fell asleep at last near dawn, and dreamed of a rope hanging in an empty tower, swaying gently, with no hand upon it.

When he woke, the first frost had come in the night, silvering the fields all the way down to the churchyard wall — and on the sill outside his window, where no one could have climbed without leaving prints in the rime, someone had laid a single line of white chalk, straight and deliberate, the length of the stone.

He was still staring at it when Marta knocked, and called through the door, in a voice carefully level:

"Breakfast's ready, Mr. Voss. And you'll be wanting to see Ivy Thorne. She's been asking after you."
