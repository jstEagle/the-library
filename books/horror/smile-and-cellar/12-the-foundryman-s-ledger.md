## 12. The Foundryman's Ledger

The foundry had been a foundry once. That was the first thing Edmund understood about the place, standing in the yard off the Ashcombe road with his collar up against a wind that smelled of iron and wet leaves: whatever Hollowmere was now—a village of closed mouths and half-sung songs—this place had been an engine of making. The long shed still stood, its corrugated roof furred with moss, its brick chimney a broken tooth against a sky the color of pewter. Nettles had taken the yard where coal trucks had once backed up to the casting floor. A rusted sign on the gatepost read HOLLAND & SON, BELLFOUNDERS — EST. 1841, the ampersand nearly eaten away.

The son. Edmund turned the phrase over as he let himself through the gate. Ivy Thorne had given him the name two nights ago, in her kitchen, in that voice like paper being unfolded after seventy years of folding: *Holland. Josiah Holland. He cast your bell, and he wrote his own boy's name in the mold instead of mine.* She had said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. *He thought he was saving me. He didn't know what he was spending.*

Marta had driven him out in the estate's ancient Land Rover, silent the whole way except to say, at the crossroads, "Nobody comes here. Not even the council knows why they don't." Then she'd waited by the car, arms folded, watching the shed as if it might watch back.

The door was padlocked, but the hasp had rusted to lace; Edmund worked it loose with a flathead screwdriver from his field kit and stepped into air that had not moved since before the war. Dust hung in the shafts of light from the clerestory windows like something suspended rather than settled—like the room itself was holding its breath.

Inside, the casting pit yawned in the center of the floor, a rectangular depression lined with old firebrick, big enough for a bell of six or seven hundredweight. Loam crusted the edges. Along one wall stood a bank of wooden cabinets, their drawers labeled in faded copperplate: *MOULDS. CORES. PATTERNS. PAPERS.* And above them, nailed to the beam, a child's horseshoe and a sprig of rowan, wired there so long ago the wire had grown into the wood.

Edmund put on cotton gloves—he was a folklorist, not an archaeologist, but he knew what archives deserved—and opened the drawer marked PAPERS.

* * *

They came out in bundles tied with waxed string: order books going back to Victoria's reign, wage ledgers, correspondence with parish councils across three counties, invoices for rope and bell-metal and beer for casting-day celebrations. Edmund worked methodically, the way he'd worked a hundred archives, laying each bundle aside in sequence, photographing what mattered. Outside, Marta's silhouette crossed the windows once, twice, then went still.

He found 1909 within the hour.

The order book entry was dated the fourteenth of June, 1909, in Josiah Holland's own hand—a strong, slanted script, economical, the writing of a man who valued ink:

*Bell for St. Brannoc's, Hollowmere. Tenor cwt. 6-2-14. To be cast with inscription per instruction of Rev. T. Mowbray. Names of the six children to be included in the founder's line, as agreed. Payment on delivery.*

Below it, in different ink, added later, the words that made Edmund's gloved fingers go cold against the page:

*Seven names required. Six supplied.*

He sat down on an upturned loam tub and read it again. *Seven names required. Six supplied.* No explanation. No elaboration. Just the arithmetic of it, sitting there in the ledger like a debt entered in advance.

He photographed it, then kept digging. The correspondence bundle for 1909–1911 held letters between Holland and the Reverend Mowbray, and it was in these that the shape of the thing began to assemble itself—not all at once, not cleanly, but the way a figure assembles out of fog when you stare too long.

Mowbray's letters were those of a grieving man grasping at ritual. The six children—Edmund already knew their names from the vestry register, had copied them out in his notebook in his careful folklorist's hand: *Alice Merridew, Thomas and Sarah Cutler, John Pike, Edith Sorrell, Walter Fenn*—had drowned in the millpond in the autumn of 1909, four of them together, two more pulled out over the following week like the pond finishing a sentence. Mowbray wanted them remembered in bronze. Wanted their names rung out over the valley every harvest, so that no one could ever again say the village had forgotten.

Holland's replies were those of a craftsman humoring a client, at first. *Bronze holds names well, Reverend, but it does not hold grief, and grief is what you are asking me to cast.* Then, in October of 1909, a change. A letter that began differently, the handwriting suddenly cramped, pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

*Reverend—I have consulted my father's papers regarding the composition of the metal and the rites observed at pouring. I must be plain with you, though you will think me mad or worse. My father held—and his father before him, who learned the trade in Belgium, where such things are not spoken of above a whisper—that a bell is not made but born, and that what is born must be given a full complement or it will go seeking. Seven voices, Reverend. Every bell we have ever poured has carried seven names, whether the client knew it or not. Six saints and one sinner, my father called it, though I never learned which was which. Your six children make six. I cannot pour a bell of six. Name me a seventh, or I must find one myself, and neither of us will like how it is found.*

Edmund read the letter three times. Around him the shed ticked and settled in the wind. Somewhere overhead a pigeon shifted its weight on a rafter.

*A bell must hold seven voices or take them.*

There it was. The mechanism, laid out in a dead founder's anxious hand like an engineer's diagram of a trap. Not malice. Not witchcraft, even—or not only. Arithmetic. A container built to a specification, and the specification unmet, and the container doing what containers do: filling itself.

But with what? With whom? He turned the pages faster now, hunting for Mowbray's answer, and found it—a single sheet, undated, in a hand gone shaky:

*I cannot choose among my flock, Mr. Holland. It would be a judgment upon one family, and God knows they have suffered enough alike. Cast the six and trust to Providence for the seventh.*

And beneath it, clipped to it, Holland's final note—the last word in the exchange, written on the back of a working drawing of the bell's profile, the kind of note a man writes when he has stopped expecting an answer and started simply recording what he intends to do:

*Providence does not pour bronze. I will supply the seventh myself. Forgive me, whoever you turn out to be.*

* * *

Edmund came out into the grey afternoon because his hands were shaking and he needed the sky. Marta looked up from the wall she'd been leaning against, saw his face, and said nothing—which, he was learning, was her way of saying everything.

"There's more," he managed. "There has to be. The letter says he'd supply the seventh himself. Ivy said he wrote his own son's name."

"Then keep looking," Marta said. Her voice was flat, but her eyes went to the shed door and stayed there, the way a dog's eyes go to a door behind which something waits. "And Edmund—" she stopped. Started again. "My father read these papers. Once. He came out here the year before he stopped speaking, and he read for a whole day, and when he came home he burned his notes in the yard and never said a word about it. Whatever you find, it doesn't unfind you. Remember that."

She turned her back to the shed and lit a cigarette with hands that weren't quite steady, and Edmund went back inside.

The later bundles were thinner. Business had fallen away through the twenties and thirties—car bells, motor horns, the slow strangulation of the trade—and the correspondence dried to invoices and circulars. But the drawer had a false bottom. He found it the way he'd been taught to find things: by the sound. His knuckles rapped the drawer's base and one corner answered hollow, and when he pried up the board there was a shallow space beneath, lined with oilcloth, holding a tin document box and, wrapped in flannel, a small brass foundryman's stamp—the punch used to mark a bell's crown—with the initials J.H.

The box held three things.

The first was a photograph: a young man of perhaps nineteen, standing in this very shed, one hand resting on a finished bell the size of a bathtub. He had his father's strong jaw and none of his economy; the smile was wide, unguarded, a boy delighted with the world and unaware it might require anything of him. On the reverse, in pencil: *Alfred, casting day, St. Brannoc's bell, Sept. 1910.*

Alfred. Edmund said the name aloud just to hear it exist in the room, and the room did not object, which felt like permission and also like a trap closing.

The second item was the letter. One page, both sides, dated the second of September, 1910—eleven days before the photograph, or eleven days after; Edmund couldn't tell if the boy had smiled before writing it or after receiving whatever answer it earned. The handwriting was rounder than his father's, quicker, a young man's hand:

*Father,

I know what you've written in the mold book. I've seen the seventh line. You think I don't check your work, but I do, I always have, since I was small enough to stand on the casting pit edge and watch you ram up the loam.

I won't have it. Whatever grandfather believed, whatever the Belgian taught him, I'm not a name to be poured into anything. I'm nineteen years old and I'm going to Bristol in the spring—Mr. Callard has offered me the apprenticeship, the letter came Tuesday—and I'll not spend my life under this roof waiting for you to decide I'm the missing piece of some reverend's monument.

Strike my name. Take mine out and leave the line empty, or take the commission back, or pour the bell short and let it crack, I don't care. But you will not write me into that metal. Swear it. Write back and swear it, or I'll come to the foundry on pouring day and say so in front of everyone.

Your son,
Alf*

Edmund set the letter down on the casting pit's edge, very carefully, as though it might bruise.

Because he could see it now—the whole terrible machine of it, every gear turning. The father, bound by his trade's inherited law: *seven voices or it takes them.* The son, refusing with the magnificent, doomed confidence of the young: *you will not write me into that metal.* And between them the mold, the loam, the inscription cut in reverse into the cope, waiting for its seventh line.

Had Josiah obeyed? Had he struck the name? Edmund lifted the third item from the box with fingers that no longer felt entirely his own.

It was a single card, stiff, the kind used for mold-book entries, and on it, in Josiah Holland's slanted, economical hand, was the founder's line for the Hollowmere bell exactly as it had been cut into the bronze—the same line Edmund had transcribed from the bell's surviving rubbing in the vestry, the line naming the six drowned children. And beneath the sixth name, where a seventh should have been, a line had been scored through so deeply the card was nearly cut in two. Under the scratch, pressed hard enough to read through the damage, a name:

*Alfred Josiah Holland.*

And beside it, smaller, in ink gone brown, the father's last note—the note Edmund would carry out of that shed and into every sleepless night that followed:

*Struck from the mold as he demanded. Sworn to him on his mother's grave. But the law is older than either of us, lad, and a bell must always hold seven voices or take them. If I will not give it a name, it will learn to ask. God forgive me—I have taught it whose voice to want.*

* * *

He sat in the ruined foundry until the light went from grey to blue, and he understood, finally and completely, what he had walked into—and understood equally that understanding was not escape.

That was the cruelty of it, he thought. Knowledge was supposed to be power; that was the founding superstition of his entire profession. Go into the field, collect the song, trace the custom to its root, and the root would explain the flower. But the root of Hollowmere's horror was not an explanation. It was a promise. A bell built incomplete by design, denied its seventh by a father's love, and left—as any denied thing is left—to seek. Seventy years of seeking. Six verses sung and a seventh withheld, an invitation forever extended and forever unanswered, and every few decades some fool with a notebook—some surveyor, some collector, some man who loved songs the way Edmund loved them—came to the village and mouthed the verse alone in the dark, and the bell heard its name at last.

Not chosen. *Wanted.* That was the mechanism's true teeth. The bell did not take strangers. It took the ones who answered.

Edmund packed the papers back into the tin, returned the tin to its hiding place—or almost; he kept the son's letter and the mold card in his inner pocket, against his chest, where they lay like two halves of a wound—and stepped out into the dark.

Marta was still by the wall. The cigarette had long gone cold between her fingers. When she saw him she straightened, and in the light spilling from the shed doorway her face was pale and set.

"You found it," she said. Not a question.

"I found it." He looked back at the shed—at the casting pit, the cabinets, the rowan sprig wired above the papers, planted there by someone who had known exactly what was made in this room and had tried, too late, to hedge it with the old protections. "Marta, the bell isn't cursed. It's *incomplete*. It's been incomplete since 1911, and it's been looking for its seventh voice ever since, and it doesn't care whose. It cares whose voice *answers*."

"Then don't answer it."

"It's not a question of answering." He heard his own voice go thin and strange. "Marta—the frost is coming. Ivy told me the tolling starts with the first frost. And this morning, copying out the sixth verse, I caught myself humming it. Humming it, Marta. I hadn't decided to. My throat decided for me."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she crushed the dead cigarette under her heel, got into the Land Rover, and started the engine, and as they pulled out onto the Ashcombe road with the foundry shrinking black in the mirror, Edmund Voss watched the first frost of autumn silver the hedgerows ahead of them, field by field, mile by mile, all the way home to Hollowmere—

—and somewhere behind them, faint as memory, patient as bronze, the empty tower gave its first toll of the season.
