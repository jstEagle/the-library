## 16. Ivy Decides

Ivy Thorne had spent seventy years learning how to be quiet.

She had learned it as a girl, when the foundryman's cart came through Hollowmere and every door along the lane closed like a held breath. She had learned it as a young woman, when her mother's chair sat empty and no one—not her father, not the vicar, not the women who'd sung beside her mother at harvest—could say what had become of her. She had learned it in her marriage, in her widowhood, in the long grey decades of being the only one left who remembered that there was anything to remember. Quiet was a craft, like lace-making or hedging. You got good at it or it got good at you.

So when she stood up in Marta Hale's back kitchen on the morning after the tape had been played to the village, and said, in a voice cracked but perfectly clear, "I'll sing it," the silence that followed was the loudest thing Ivy had heard since 1911.

Edmund Voss was sitting at the far end of the table with his notebook open and his hands flat on either side of it, as if he were pinning the pages down against a wind. He looked up at her. His face did the thing faces did in this village—it went carefully blank—but she was ninety-one years old and she had watched people hide things for longer than he had been alive, and underneath the blankness she saw the flinch.

"You'll not," he said.

"I will." She kept her hands folded on the table where everyone could see them, steady as stones. "You've done your sums, lad. I can see you doing them, up all hours with that lamp burning. The bell wants a seventh voice. It's wanted one since before either of us drew breath. And here's the truth of it—" she looked around the kitchen, at Marta by the stove, at the two council men who hadn't gone home since the meeting, at old Tam Weller who'd wept like a child last night when the answering voice came out of the recorder's little speaker, "—it doesn't want just any voice. It wants one that knows the verse. Whole. Every word. There's three of us in this room who know it whole. Me, him, and you, Edmund Voss. And of the three, I'm the one whose account is longest overdue."

"Ivy." Marta's voice came out low, warning.

"Don't you *Ivy* me, girl. I buried your father's silence. I sat with him thirty years while he sat with his, and neither of us ever said the plain thing out loud: that the debt could be paid any year, by anyone willing, and none of us ever would." She turned back to Edmund. "In 1911 I was meant to go into that bronze. My name was written in the ledger, seventh of seven, because my mother owed the pond a life and the pond collects in bronze now instead of water. The foundryman crossed me out and wrote his own boy in, and his boy refused, and the bell has been hungry ever since. Seventy years I've eaten bread bought with another child's name. Do you understand? Every harvest since, when the bell rang and took someone—a mother, a surveyor, a carter's boy in '62—I ate my breakfast the next morning and thanked God it wasn't me. That's not survival, lad. That's interest. Compounding."

Edmund had gone very still. Not the stillness of fear—she knew that stillness too, had seen it on the faces of the taken in their last unremembered days. This was the stillness of a man building an argument, brick by brick, against something he already felt in his chest.

"It isn't yours to pay," he said.

"Whose is it, then?"

"No one's. That's the point. That's the whole—" He stopped, pressed his palms harder into the table. "Ivy, listen to me. What happened in 1911 wasn't justice. A mother loses six children to a millpond and the village decides the answer is to put her seventh into a bell? That isn't law. That isn't even custom. That's grief wearing arithmetic as a coat. And everything since—the foundryman swapping names, the bell hunting, the village forgetting its own dead rather than mourn them—it's all grown out of that first rotten trade. One life to balance six lives, as if lives were coins." He shook his head slowly. "If you sing that verse alone tomorrow night, you're not settling the debt. You're paying it again. You're telling the bell it was right. You're telling it the trade stands, and always stood, and all it ever has to do is wait for someone lawful enough to step forward."

The kitchen was quiet. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere across the valley, faint as a memory, the tower stood empty against the grey morning sky, and Ivy felt it the way she'd felt it all week—a warmth in the air over Hollowmere, patient as a hand waiting to be shaken.

"And what's your answer, then?" she asked. "Your hundred voices. Your choir." She didn't sneer; she was too old for sneering and too fond of him for it anyway. "You heard the council last night. Half of them wouldn't look at you. They'll stand in their kitchens with their doors shut and let it take you, same as they let it take my mother, same as they've let it take everyone, because forgetting is easier than singing and singing together means grieving together and grieving together means—" her voice caught, and she hated it, hated the crack after seventy years of masonry, "—means admitting what we let happen. To my mother. To all of them."

"Then I'll teach them anyway," Edmund said. "Starting with you."

"Me?"

"You're going to help me rehearse the children. You said last night you'd do it. I'm holding you to it." Something moved behind his eyes then, tired and fierce and very young. "And while we're teaching them, Ivy, you're going to stay alive. Because if you spend yourself buying my safety, then I'm just the surveyor with better notes. Another name in another ledger. And I didn't walk past a bus driver too frightened to finish his route so that the pattern could close neatly around me with everyone agreeing it was tidy."

Ivy looked at him for a long moment. Ninety-one years, and she could count on one hand the times someone had told her no and meant it kindly.

"You're a stubborn man, Edmund Voss."

"I'm a folklorist," he said. "We collect songs. We don't spend singers."

* * *

He walked home through the rain without hurrying, which was itself a kind of defiance. For eleven days he had moved through Hollowmere like a man wading upstream—shopkeepers mishearing his name, the child asking why the stranger's chair was empty, the bell tolling nightly now from a tower that held no bell, each toll closer, each toll warmer, until he woke with the sixth verse sitting on his tongue like a stone he'd swallowed in his sleep. *Come down, come down, the water's sweet,* it went, and then the line that waited for an answer: *and name yourself among the deep.* He had learned to wake with his mouth clamped shut, learned to sleep with a scarf knotted between his teeth until Marta had nailed the window sash down against himself. The enemy lived in his body now. He knew that. Knowing it was the only weapon he had.

But Ivy's offer had shown him the shape of the trap more clearly than anything the bell had done.

It wanted a volunteer. Of course it did. Six drowned children had not volunteered; they had been taken, and the taking had curdled into bronze, and everything wrong with the bell came from that. But a volunteer—a lawful sacrifice, freely given, grieved and honored and agreed upon by all—that would be the cleanest payment imaginable. The bell would take Ivy's voice and the village would call it mercy, and they would be right to call it mercy, and that was precisely what made it poison. Because the next time—and there would be a next time, autumn after autumn, forever, the mechanism he'd read in the foundryman's ledger humming under everything—there would be another volunteer, another lawful soul stepping up to spare another stranger, and the bell would have taught Hollowmere the worst lesson of all: that the drowning of one could be made noble, and therefore repeated.

Justice outside law, he thought, turning the phrase over as his boots sucked at the lane's mud. He'd carried the idea half-formed for days without knowing its name. The bell operated outside any human law—no court had sentenced those children, no statute authorized the taking—but it had dressed itself in the grammar of law: debts, payments, accounts, a name owed and a name rendered. And the only way to honor real justice in the face of a false legality was refusal. Refusal even of the sacrifice offered in your own defense. Especially then. Because the moment you accepted that someone else's voice could be lawfully spent to buy yours, you had conceded the bell's entire jurisprudence, and concessions were how towers stayed hungry for a century.

His cottage was cold. He lit the lamp, set his recorder on the table, and looked at the notebook open to the transcription he'd made in Ivy's kitchen a week ago, her voice trembling through five verses and her lips moving silent around the sixth. Below the words, in his own handwriting, he had copied the foundryman's note from the ledger:

*A bell must hold seven voices or take them.*

He stared at it. Then, slowly, he turned to a fresh page and wrote, pressing hard enough to emboss the paper:

*A bell must hold seven voices—or be given them.*

That was the flaw. That was the seam in the bronze. The bell needed consent—not full consent, perhaps, not informed and joyful consent, but the shape of it: one throat opening willingly, one name offered into the dark. Six children taken by water had never consented, and so the bell rang hollow and hungry and had to hunt. But give it one true volunteer and the hunger would be fed, sanctified, made permanent. Ivy understood sacrifice. She had been raised on it, named for it, nearly cast into it. She thought offering herself was the only honest act left to her.

She was magnificent. And she was wrong.

Edmund closed the notebook and went out into the rain again, up the lane toward the schoolhouse, where Ivy had promised to meet the children at four o'clock and teach them the melody they'd hummed all their lives without knowing why their elders' faces changed at the final bar.

She was already there when he arrived, seated on the low chair the teachers used, her hands in her lap, twelve children arranged on the floor before her like kindling. Through the fogged windows the village lay below in the wet dusk, chimneys smoking, lamps coming on one by one, and beyond it the tower, black against a sky the color of pewter.

"Late, folklorist," Ivy said, without turning.

"Had some thinking to do."

"And?" Now she turned, and her eyes were sharp and dry and terribly brave. "Have you thought your way out of it? Have you found the clever sentence that keeps an old woman from doing the one useful thing left in her?"

"No," Edmund said honestly. "I've found something better than clever." He set his recorder on the teacher's desk and faced the children, and when he spoke again his voice carried to the back of the small room, steady, pitched to carry further still—to the lane outside, where Marta Hale stood beneath the eaves pretending to shelter from the rain, and past her, if sound could travel the way grief traveled, to every closed kitchen in Hollowmere.

"Ivy Thorne is not going to sing alone," he said. "None of us is. Tomorrow night, every voice in this village learns the sixth verse—including theirs." He nodded at the children. "Because the bell's whole law rests on one throat opening. So we'll give it a hundred. Let it try to choose."

Ivy looked at him for a long moment, and then—seventy years of quiet cracking all at once, like ice going out in spring—she began, softly, to laugh.

"Well," she said, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Then we'd best begin. Children—hands on your knees. We start with the fifth verse, and work backward, and by midnight every one of you will know the words that brought your great-grandmothers home." Her voice dropped, gentled, became the voice that had once sung lullabies to mothers who were later forgotten. "*Come down, come down, the water's sweet—*"

Outside, across the darkening valley, the tower answered.

Not a toll this time. A hum—low, resonant, almost melodic, rising from the empty frame like heat off a forge. The children froze. The lamp guttered. And in the ringing quiet that followed, every person in the schoolhouse understood, with perfect clarity, that the bell had heard them planning—and was practicing too.
