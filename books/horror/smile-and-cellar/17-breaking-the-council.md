## 17. Breaking the Council

Marta Hale had poured beer through three funerals, two weddings, and one flood in her years behind the bar of the Lamb and Flag, and she had learned that a village meeting was none of these things. A funeral let people weep because grief had been given permission. A wedding let them hope. A flood gave them something to bail. But a village meeting asked them to decide, and Hollowmere had spent a hundred years perfecting the art of deciding nothing at all.

She watched them file in that October evening — the same faces she'd seen every council night since her father stopped speaking: Aldous Crane with his chair angled toward the door as always; the Petch brothers sitting shoulder to shoulder like bookends holding up nothing; Widow Fenner with her knitting she never finished; young Tam Osgood, who'd inherited his grandfather's seat and his grandfather's silence along with it. Eleven souls on the council, out of a village of ninety-three, because the rest had long ago decided that whatever Hollowmere kept hidden was better kept by fewer hands.

Edmund Voss stood at the end of the room with his tape machine under his arm like a man carrying an injured bird.

Marta had argued with him for two days. She had stood in her own kitchen, flour still on her hands, and told him plainly: *You'll break them, and broken things here don't mend, they only bleed where no one can see.* And he had looked at her with those tired, stubborn eyes and said the thing she hadn't been able to answer.

"You already are bleeding, Marta. You've just agreed not to notice the wound."

Now she called the meeting to order, and her voice came out steadier than she felt.

* * *

The business of the evening was meant to be drainage ditches and the price of feed meal. Aldous Crane saw to it that it stayed that way for forty minutes, droning through the ditch schedule with the devotion of a man reading scripture in a language he didn't believe. Marta let him run. Every minute he ran was a minute Edmund waited, patient as water finding a crack.

But she had seen what was happening outside these walls, these last weeks. The forgetting had come early this year, and it had come hungry. Old Mrs. Tregowan had asked her daughter-in-law, twice in one afternoon, when her son was coming home from sea — a boy drowned off Whitby in 1961, a death the village had mourned properly, or thought it had. The schoolmistress had found herself unable to recall the face of the girl who used to sit third row, window seat, until shown a photograph, and even then it was like remembering a stranger's dream. And little Susie Petch had come into the pub the week before, tugging her mother's apron, asking why there was a chair laid at their table every supper for someone nobody could name.

The wall was cracking on its own this autumn. The bell had grown impatient, and impatience made it careless.

When Aldous finally sat down, Marta did not open the floor. She nodded once across the room.

Edmund stepped forward and set the tape machine on the trestle table, and the sound of it — the small hard click of the case, the whir as he threaded the reel — went through the room like a dropped glass.

"I know why you're all looking at me like that," he said. His voice was quiet. He'd learned, Marta had noticed, that loudness died in Hollowmere; it bounced off the closed faces and came back as nothing. Quiet got in under the doors. "You're wondering how long I'll be. Whether you can wait me out. You've done it before." He looked down the row of them. "There was a man in 1954 who stood where I'm standing. A surveyor. He also had questions."

"We don't know any surveyor," said Aldous Crane, automatically, the way a man says the Lord's Prayer.

"No," Edmund agreed. "That's rather the point."

He pressed the button.

* * *

The tape hissed first — that long breath of empty air, fifty years old, air that had been breathed by a dead man into a microphone in a church that had no roof. Marta's hands found each other on the bar top and held on.

Then a voice. Younger than Edmund's, brisker, a man used to measuring things and having them hold still. *"Testing. The twenty-third of September, 1954. Harold Vane, land surveyor, recording conditions at the tower site."* A pause, the shuffle of feet on stone. Then, softer, almost embarrassed: *"Conditions being that there is no bell, and yet at dusk yesterday it rang. I am recording this so that tomorrow, when I have doubtless talked myself out of believing it, I will have proof."*

Someone near the door shifted their weight. Widow Fenner's needles had gone still.

*"I've been asking about the song,"* Vane's voice went on. *"The harvest one. Everyone hums it. No one finishes it. Last night Mrs. Thorne's mother—"*

The voice stopped. On the tape, there was a long silence, and then a different quality of breathing, the breathing of a man deciding something.

*"I found the sixth verse,"* Harold Vane said. *"In the vestry records, written in a hand from 1911. It isn't a warning. That's what nobody understands. It isn't a warning at all. It's an invitation. The bell sings it, and something has to answer with a name. One name. One throat. And I have been alone in this village for six weeks, and I begin to understand that being alone is the whole mechanism. So I'm going to sing it now, into this machine, so that if anything happens, whoever finds this will know exactly what happened, and to whom, and—"* a laugh, thin and terrible — *"and so that someone, somewhere, remembers me. My landlady already swears I've never lodged there. My office thinks I'm in Norfolk. If I go, I go out of the world entirely. Except this. Except this reel."*

He cleared his throat. And then, in a voice that wavered only twice, Harold Vane sang the sixth verse of the harvest song of Hollowmere.

Marta knew the words. She had known them since childhood, mouthed but never voiced, the way you know the shape of a scar. *Come down, come down, the water's sweet, / the bronze remembers what the earth forgets, / bring your name, bring it warm, / seven voices make the chorus whole.*

And then the tape did the thing that Marta would hear in her sleep for the rest of her life.

It answered.

A second voice rose out of the hiss — small, high, a child's voice, or six children's voices braided into one, singing the answering line back: *bring your name, bring it warm,* and underneath it, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, a single toll of a bell that had been melted down in 1911.

Harold Vane's recording caught one more sound after that. A breath drawn sharply inward. And then nothing. The reel ran on, empty, for another full minute before Edmund stopped it.

* * *

The silence afterward was not the silence of a room. It was the silence of a dam.

Marta watched it happen the way you watch weather come over a ridge. Aldous Crane opened his mouth — she could see the refusal forming, the practiced *we don't know any surveyor*, the great grey wall of Hollowmere rising on schedule — and then, halfway up the aisle, Widow Fenner began to cry.

Not quietly. Not the tidy weeping of funerals. She put down her knitting and she wept like a girl, huge and ugly and helpless, and between the sobs she said a name. *"Tommy. Tommy Fenner. He played the fiddle at every harvest do until 1949, and then he was gone, and I stood in the churchyard and couldn't remember his face, and everyone told me I'd never had a brother, and I believed them, God help me, I believed them because it was easier—"*

"Sit down, Edith," said Aldous Crane. "That's enough now."

"He played the fiddle!" she cried at him. "You danced with him, Aldous! You danced with my brother at the harvest do in '48 and then you helped us all forget him!"

And the wall cracked.

It went down the rows like fire through dry stubble. Tam Osgood stood up abruptly, knocking his chair back, and said, in a voice cracked straight down the middle, "My grandfather. There was a grandfather. I've known that the way you know a hole in your mouth where a tooth was — everyone said I was imagining it, and I *let* them say it—" He couldn't finish. He sat back down and put his face in his hands and his shoulders shook.

The Petch brothers, who had not spoken in a council meeting in eleven years, turned to each other simultaneously, and Marta saw the moment they remembered the same person at the same instant — she saw it pass between their faces like a struck match — and both of them said, at once, "*Annie*," and then neither could say anything else.

Half the room was weeping. Names were falling into the air like leaves — names of the vanished, dredged up out of fifty years of sanctioned darkness, spoken aloud for the first time since they'd been taken. A schoolteacher from 1938. A peddler who'd stayed a winter. A girl whose name Marta herself had lost, and felt return now like a bone setting — *Bess Underhill, Bess who sang alto, Bess who was going to leave for London in the spring of 1972 and never left at all* — and Marta found that she was crying too, standing behind her own bar, gripping the tap handle, weeping without shame in front of the whole village for a woman she had been persuaded out of knowing.

This was the thing, she understood suddenly, this was the filthy genius of it: the bell didn't just take the singers. It took the mourners. It stole the grief itself, and a village that cannot grieve is a village that cannot refuse anything. They had not been keeping a secret all these years. They had been keeping a wound, uncleaned, untended, because cleaning it hurt more than the rot.

"Enough." Aldous Crane was on his feet now, white-faced, shouting over the weeping. "Shut it off. Shut that thing off. This is how it starts — this is exactly how it starts, you give it names, you give it *grief*, you call its attention—" 

"It already has our attention, Aldous," Marta said. Her voice carried. She was surprised by it. "It's ringing every night now. Did you think that was politeness?"

"The arrangement holds if we keep it!" His voice cracked on *holds*. "We keep quiet, we keep half a song, we lose one every few generations and the rest of us live — that was the bargain, that's what our fathers—"

"Your father rang the warning bell every autumn," Marta said quietly. "Until he went silent. Until he sat in that chair—" she pointed at Aldous's own seat, angled toward the door, the seat her father had taught him to sit in — "and never spoke again. Was that the bargain holding? Or was that the bargain eating him?"

Aldous stared at her. Around the room, the weeping was settling into something harder and quieter — tears drying into resolve, the way salt dries into crust. Widow Fenner had taken out a handkerchief and was folding and unfolding it, and her eyes, when they lifted, were the driest eyes in the room.

Edmund spoke into the quiet. "The man on that tape thought he had to sing alone because he was alone. That was the trap — loneliness wasn't his misfortune, it was the mechanism. One throat, one name. But a hundred throats—" He stopped himself. Marta understood why. It wasn't time yet. Let them grieve first. Grief first. Refusal after.

Tam Osgood raised his head. "What do we do?" he asked, and it was the first question anyone on the council had asked in Marta's memory that wanted an answer rather than an ending.

"You learn the verse," Marta said. "All of you. All of Hollowmere. Before harvest night."

"And if we refuse?" Aldous whispered. "If we refuse together? What does it do then?"

No one answered him. No one could. Outside the dark windows, far up the valley, the church that had no bell gave a single toll — soft, almost gentle, the way a creditor knocks.

Nobody flinched this time. That was new. That was everything.

Widow Fenner stood, gathered her unfinished knitting, and said clearly, in the voice of a woman setting out on a long road: "Teach me the words, then. Tonight. I've spent thirty years not knowing my own brother, and I'll not spend one more night of it silent."

One by one, the others rose and followed her out — not scattering home as they always did, but clustering, walking together, talking in low urgent voices, the first congregation Hollowmere had formed in living memory around something other than fear.

Only Aldous remained, sitting in his door-angled chair, staring at the tape machine as if it were a snake that might yet be charmed back into its basket.

"Aldous," Marta said gently. "Come and learn it with us."

He shook his head slowly. "You don't understand what you're doing," he said. "My father tried to warn them, the year they nearly sang it together, 1938. He told me what happens when a whole village raises its voice to that thing." He looked up at her, and Marta saw that behind his fear there was something worse — knowledge. "It doesn't fall silent, Marta. When it can't choose one throat—"

The bell tolled again. Closer. Twice.

"—it comes down to choose for itself."
