## 18. The Night Before Harvest

The rehearsal began in Marta Hale's back room, which had never in its history held so many people and had certainly never held them singing.

Edmund stood by the hearth with his notebook closed. He had learned, over these last weeks, that some things were better kept off the page — that ink could be a kind of invitation, the way a name spoken into bronze was an invitation, and he did not intend to write down the sixth verse anywhere it might be read aloud by accident. It lived now in twenty-two heads, taught mouth to ear over three nights of whispered instruction, and tomorrow night it would live in a hundred more or it would not live at all.

"Again," Ivy said.

She sat in the best chair, wrapped in two shawls despite the fire, her hands folded on her cane like a woman at church. The children sat cross-legged on the floorboards in front of her — six of them, because someone had counted and someone else had gone pale and someone else had said *don't be bloody stupid, it's not numerology, it's a song* — and they sang the verse in voices like small cracked bells of their own.

*Come down, come down, the water's warm—*

The melody wobbled. Tam Weller, who was nine and tone-deaf in the way only nine-year-olds can be magnificent at, dragged half a beat behind everyone else. The girl beside him, Alice Priddy, sang a third too high and corrected herself mid-word, and the correction was worse than the error. The line came apart at its seams.

*—the water's warm and the stone is kind—*

Ivy lifted one finger. They stopped.

"No," she said gently. "You're apologizing when you sing it. Every one of you. You slide off the note because you don't want to land on it properly." She looked around at their small anxious faces. "This verse is not an apology. It is an answer. When the bell calls, somebody has to say *we hear you*. That's all the verse is. Six drowned children calling across a hundred years, and us saying: we hear you, we know your names, you are not singing alone anymore."

Tam Weller frowned. "But if we sing it wrong—"

"You cannot sing it wrong." Ivy's voice went quiet and iron-hard. "That is the whole point, child. The bell needs one voice. One clean throat, alone in the dark, offering itself up. A hundred voices out of tune? It doesn't know what to do with that. Sing it flat. Sing it early. Sing it like frogs arguing. Just sing it *together*."

They sang it again, and it was still terrible, and Edmund felt something loosen in his chest that had been clenched since the frost.

* * *

Marta Hale carried the last tray of cups through from the kitchen and stopped in the doorway to watch.

Her father had rung the warning bell every autumn for thirty years — walked up to the empty tower with his coat collar turned up and pulled the rope of a bell that wasn't there, because the ringing of nothing was still a warning, still a *no*, still a man standing between his village and the dark and saying *not this year*. And then one October he had come down from the tower and never spoken above a whisper again, and the following October he hadn't gone up at all, and Marta had understood that the bell had finally taken the only thing left to take: his voice, while he still lived to miss it.

She had spent her whole life believing that silence was the price of survival. Pay it yearly. Keep your head down. Forget on purpose, because remembering was how the bell found the loose thread and pulled.

Now she watched old Ivy Thorne teach six children a verse that had killed every soul who ever sang it alone, and she watched those children laugh when they got it wrong, and she thought: *we have been paying protection money to a ghost for a hundred years, and tonight we stop.*

"Mrs. Hale?" Alice Priddy had appeared at her elbow, holding out an empty cup. "Can I have more of the honey milk? Tam says there isn't any more but Tam lies."

"There's plenty more." Marta refilled the cup. Her hands were steady. She was surprised by that. "Alice. Tomorrow night, when we go to the churchyard — whatever you hear from that tower, however close it sounds, however much it sounds like it's calling *you* specifically — you stay in the middle of the crowd. Do you understand? The edges are where people get picked off."

Alice nodded solemnly, the way children nod at things they've decided not to be frightened of yet. "Mr. Voss says the bell will try to make it feel like a conversation. Like it's just me and it."

"Mr. Voss is right."

"He looks tired," Alice said, with the merciless observation of the young. "Is he going to be all right?"

Marta looked across the room at Edmund Voss — the folklorist who had arrived with a recorder and notebooks and a loneliness he wore like a coat he'd forgotten he owned, and who would leave, if he left at all, as the man who refused to let an old woman spend her last name buying his. She thought about lying to the child. She decided against it; the whole village had lied enough for a century.

"We're all going to be all right," Marta said, "because tomorrow none of us will be alone. Now take your milk back before Tam drinks it out of spite."

* * *

In the corner by the window, Tom Bracken sat with his cap in his hands and did not sing.

He knew the verse. Ivy had taught him with the others, patiently, twice through, and he had mouthed along well enough. But his mouth wouldn't open on the actual words, not here, not with people watching, because the last time Tom Bracken had heard the sixth verse sung whole he'd been seven years old and standing in this same back room — no, not this room, the old room, the house had burned since — listening to his aunt Enid harmonize the answering line while she kneaded bread, and the next morning Aunt Enid's bed was made and her apron was on its hook and nobody could tell him who the apron belonged to, and his mother had said *what apron, love,* in a voice so genuinely puzzled that Tom had doubted his own memory for fifty years.

He doubted it no longer. The tape in the church wall had settled that. But knowing a thing and opening your mouth were different animals.

His wife nudged him. "Tom."

"I'm resting my voice."

"You haven't used your voice since 1971 except to complain about the football."

"That's strategic conservation."

She laughed, and the laugh caught him off guard, and before he could stop himself he hummed the first bar under his breath — just the melody, no words — and old Ivy's head came round like a hawk's and she smiled at him across the room, and Tom Bracken discovered that his eyes were wet and that nobody was looking at him like he was mad.

"Tomorrow," he said to his wife, gruffly. "I'll do it tomorrow. With everyone."

"I know," she said. "I'm holding you to it. I'll pinch you if you mouth it."

* * *

Edmund stepped outside for air and found the village waiting for him in the dark.

Not literally — though after these weeks he no longer entirely discounted the possibility. But the street was full of people who should have been indoors: the Wexfords' boy practicing the verse against the pump handle, getting it wrong, starting again; Mrs. Priddy and the postman comparing notes on where the high notes sat; two farmers whose families hadn't spoken since a fence dispute in 1988 standing shoulder to shoulder by the wall, humming the same broken harmony. The whole of Hollowmere was out under a sky scoured clean by frost, rehearsing grief in whispers, and the sound of it — imperfect, off-key, unashamed — rose off the stones like heat.

"Mr. Voss." The postman broke off and touched his cap. "We was wondering. The bit where it goes up—" he demonstrated, badly, "—does it go up like that, or like *that*?"

"Like whichever way you sing it," Edmund said. "Truly. I've listened to the 1954 recording forty times. The surveyor sings it slightly sharp the whole way through. It didn't matter. What matters is that no one is singing it *by themselves*."

The postman nodded slowly. "My gran," he said, abruptly, to the frost rather than to Edmund. "I remembered her yesterday. First time since I was six. Ellen. Ellen Postlethwaite. She used to give me barley sugar." His jaw worked. "Fifty years I didn't know I'd lost anyone. And now I can't stop seeing her kitchen."

Around them, quietly, other voices joined in. *My uncle.* *The Cooper girl.* *There was a woman who lodged with us one winter — I couldn't have told you her name this time last week.* Each memory landed like a stone dropped in a well, and the well of Hollowmere gave back its echoes one by one, and Edmund stood in the middle of it and understood that this — not the cracking of the bronze, not whatever happened at midnight tomorrow — this was already the victory. The bell had ruled them by making each loss private, unwitnessed, deniable. Tonight the losses were being witnessed. Fifty years of solitary mourning was becoming a chorus.

"The names," Mrs. Priddy said suddenly. "Tomorrow. After. We should read the six names out loud. Off the tower wall. Like Ivy said."

"Yes," Edmund said. "All six. And the seventh."

Nobody asked which seventh. Everyone knew. The foundryman's son, whose refusal was scratched into a ledger in a dead man's hand — *I will not go into the metal, Father* — and whose name had hung unpaid on the bell for a hundred years like a debt accruing interest in drowned children's voices.

"Get some sleep, all of you," Edmund said. "Tomorrow we'll need our throats."

They drifted home in twos and threes, still humming, and the humming followed him down the street like a blessing he hadn't earned.

* * *

He did sleep, eventually, in his narrow bed above the taproom, and dreamed of nothing — a mercy — and woke once in the small hours to a sound he had trained himself not to fear and failed: a single toll, far off, patient, almost polite. *One more day,* the bell seemed to say. *One more night. Then we settle accounts.*

Edmund lay in the dark and did the thing he had done every night since the frost: he ran through the sixth verse silently, word by word, checking it was still there, checking it was still *his* — a tool in his hand and not a hook in his throat. It was there. His mouth stayed shut. Below him, faintly, he heard Marta's floorboards creak as she passed his door on her nightly check, and paused, and moved on satisfied.

* * *

Morning came grey and windless, the kind of morning that feels like held breath.

By ten o'clock the whole village knew the plan by heart, because they had made it themselves and repeated it like a catechism: at dusk, everyone into the churchyard, children in the middle, lanterns lit but hooded until the tolling started. No one alone. No one at the edges. And when the bell called — and it would call, it had been calling closer and louder every night, and last night Edmund had sworn the six small voices inside the peal had sounded less like echoes and more like impatience — when it called, they would answer. All of them. Together. Badly, if necessary.

Ivy Thorne spent the afternoon on Marta's bench in the sun, wrapped in her shawls, and the children took turns sitting with her, and she told them stories that had nothing to do with bells: about the millpond frozen solid in 1947, about a pig that got into the churchyard and ate the vicar's sermon notes, about her mother's hands in flour to the wrist. She was storing warmth in them, Edmund thought, watching from the doorway. Banking it against tomorrow.

Late in the afternoon, she beckoned him over.

"They'll hold," she said, without preamble. "The village. Fear makes people run, but shame holds them — and I've shamed every last one of them properly, don't you worry." A dry pause. "But you. You'll be standing nearest the tower. It knows you. It's been tuning itself to your throat for weeks." Her eyes, milky and ancient, fixed on him. "When it calls your name, Edmund Voss — and it will, it always offers the bargain first — you keep your mouth shut and you look at the people behind you. Not at the tower. At us. Can you do that?"

Edmund thought of the surveyor's tape, the second voice rising underneath the first like water under ice. He thought of the chalk marks in the tower, counting down to this year. He thought of Ivy, seventy years carrying a debt that was never hers.

"I can do that," he said.

"Good lad." She patted the bench beside her. "Now sit down and listen to these children murder my mother's song one more time. If I have to hear Tam Weller come in late on the third line again, I may die contented after all."

And they sang — six children and an old woman and a folklorist who had come to collect songs and stayed to become one — the sixth verse rising off the bench into the windless evening air, flat and early and cracked and utterly, magnificently together, while down in the valley the light failed and the roofless tower waited, and somewhere inside its empty frame, something bronze and patient drew one long breath.

At midnight, it would exhale.

At midnight, so would Hollowmere.
