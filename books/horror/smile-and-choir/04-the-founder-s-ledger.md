## 4. The Founder's Ledger

The basement of Hollowmere smelled like a stopped clock.

Wren had worked at the hospice for six years and had never once been given a reason to go below the ground floor. The stairwell behind the laundry room was marked AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY in paint so old the letters had gone the color of weak tea. The bulb at the top of the stairs buzzed but did not illuminate much; the one at the bottom was dead, and no one had ever replaced it, which told Wren everything she needed to know about how often anyone came down here.

She stood at the top of the stairs for a long moment, doing what she always did before entering a new space: she counted.

Fourteen steps. She could see fourteen. She would count them as she descended anyway, because seeing and counting were different things, and only counting made a thing true.

*One. Two. Three.*

The air changed on the fifth step—cooler, damper, with a mineral smell underneath the dust, like water that had once run through stone and remembered it. *Six. Seven.* Her hand found the rail. *Eight. Nine.* The dark thickened around her like something settling into a chair. *Ten. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen.*

Floor. She let out a breath.

The corridor ran thirty feet ahead of her, ending at a door. She counted the paces as she walked them—one through thirty, her lips moving silently, her flashlight beam jittering across walls furred with gray dust that had never been disturbed by so much as a cobweb's ambition. Nothing down here had been touched in decades. Possibly longer.

The door at the end was iron, painted over so many times that its panels had gone soft-edged, melted-looking, like wax left near heat. There was no lock. There didn't need to be. The door was swollen shut with age, and Wren understood, standing before it, that its real lock was the simple fact that nobody wanted to open it.

She wanted to open it.

Three nights ago she had watched her own reflection blink a full second after she did. Two nights ago she had dropped a paper cup in the staff bathroom and watched it hit the floor in the mirror after it hit the floor in her hand—a small white lie of physics, a delay so brief most people would have doubted their own eyes. Wren did not doubt her eyes. Her eyes had been trained since childhood to catch exactly this kind of thing: the flicker at the edge of a room, the name called from a hallway where no one stood, the way certain silences had weight and texture, like fur brushed the wrong way.

Her grandmother had taught her that attention was a door, and doors opened both ways. Wren had spent her whole life keeping hers shut.

But the mirrors had noticed her anyway. Or something behind the mirrors had. And Mr. Aldous had counted backwards from thirty in his sleep and reached zero with a smile on his face, and in the morning he'd been cheerful in a way that sat wrong against her skin, like a shirt sewn by someone who had never seen shoulders.

So: the archive. Because if the building was lying to her, the building's paperwork might not be.

She set her shoulder against the iron door and pushed. It groaned—an actual groan, low and vocal, the sound of a thing being woken—and gave all at once, spilling her into darkness that swallowed her flashlight beam whole.

* * *

The archive was smaller than she expected. One room, maybe twenty feet square, shelved floor to ceiling on three walls. Boxes of records, ledgers bound in cracked leather, file cabinets with drawers rusted half-open like mouths caught mid-word. A desk under the far window—boarded over from the outside, she noted, which meant someone had once worried about light getting *in*—and on the desk, centered with a precision that felt deliberate after sixty years of neglect, a single ledger.

Not shoved aside. Not buried. Placed.

Wren's skin prickled. She checked the room first—counted the corners, swept the beam along the ceiling, confirmed there was no second door, no grate, no gap in the boards over the window. Four walls, three shelves, one desk, one ledger. She counted herself: one.

Then she picked it up.

The cover was black leather gone greenish at the edges, stamped in faded gilt: *HOLLOWMERE — FOUNDED 1911 — E. MARLOWE, KEEPER OF RECORDS.* Keeper of records. Not matron, not administrator, not director. Keeper. The word landed strangely, the way a key lands in a lock you didn't know you had.

She opened it.

The first pages were mundane enough to disappoint her: supply inventories, staff rosters, a complaint about the well water tasting of iron. Edith Marlowe's handwriting was beautiful—small, upright, unhurried, the hand of a woman who had been educated to write letters worth keeping. Wren turned pages. Coal deliveries. Linen counts. A note about a piano tuner.

Then, a third of the way in, the format changed.

A new section began, headed by a single line in Marlowe's careful script:

*Of the Counting.*

Wren sat down on the desk's accompanying stool—it held, creaking—and read.

*It has been asked why our patients count backwards in their final days, and why we do not discourage it. I record here the truth of the matter, for whoever keeps these records after me, though I pray no one ever needs it.*

*The dying do not count because they are confused. They count because they are answering.*

Wren looked up from the page. The dead bulb in the corridor beyond the open door seemed, somehow, to make the room darker rather than lighter—the doorway a rectangle of deeper black, like a mouth. She made herself look back down.

*When my sister Margaret lay dying in the winter of 1909, she began each evening at thirty and counted down to zero, and at zero she smiled, and slept, and woke diminished—not weaker, for she was dying already, but *less*. As though something had been skimmed off her. I asked her once what she was counting toward. She said: "Toward is the wrong direction."*

*I did not understand until the night the mirrors in this house lagged.*

Wren's hands went cold around the ledger's edges. She read the sentence again. Then again. Then she read it aloud, quietly, just to hear it in a human voice, because hearing it in Marlowe's silent voice had been unbearable.

*"I did not understand until the night the mirrors in this house lagged."*

*One second,* Marlowe wrote. *Everything in the glass arrives one second late. My reflection blinks after me. Drops things after me. And in that second—in the gap between the world and its image—something waits. Something patient. It does not enter. It cannot enter. But it can *keep*, the way a cold cellar keeps meat. It keeps what the dying give up when they reach zero. Their last second. Their remainder. And it feeds on the keeping.*

*This is not cruelty. This is architecture.*

Wren turned the page with fingers that did not feel entirely like her own.

*My husband Aldous—you will know the ward by his name, for he paid for this building with the last of our money and the first of our grief—believed we could turn the feeding into a wall. That if the dying gave willingly, in order, on schedule, their surrendered seconds could be woven into a seal. A closure. So that what waits in the lag would wait forever, fed just enough never to hunger past the glass.*

*We built Hollowmere on top of the seam. The house is the stitch. The patients are the thread.*

*And the keeper walks the pattern.*

Wren's breath had gone shallow. She was aware, distantly, of the building above her—the shuffling feet of the night shift, the hum of the medication fridge, forty-one patients sleeping or failing to sleep—and it sounded different now. Not like a hospice. Like a machine running. Like a choir holding a note.

She kept reading.

*The ritual was laid out in seven stations. We completed four before Aldous died in the spring of 1912. I completed the fifth alone. The sixth nearly took me—I will not describe it; the description would do harm—but I finished it, and I believed the seventh would follow within the year.*

*I was wrong. The seventh station requires more than the keeper's courage. It requires her surrender of what holds her to the world. And I discovered, standing at the threshold with the words in my mouth, that I could not say them. I had a daughter. I had her face in my mind and her name in my heart, and I found that I loved her more than I loved every soul this house would ever protect, and I chose her.*

*The seal holds, but imperfectly, the way a dam holds with a finger in the crack. Every keeper since has come to the threshold and turned back. I do not condemn them. I was the first.*

*But I record this, so that no future keeper may claim ignorance: the lag grows. Each year the mirror falls a fraction further behind. In my time it was a blink. In your time, reader, it may be a breath. When the lag reaches the length of a heartbeat—when the glass runs a full second late—the seam thins, and the counting of the dying converges, and on that convergence night the choice comes due again.*

*Someone must finish it. Or everyone must pay for the finishing.*

Wren closed the ledger.

She sat in the dark for a while, listening to her own pulse, which sounded too loud, too fast, like knocking.

*A full second late.*

She had measured it. Dropped cup, delayed fall. She had stood in the bathroom with her wristwatch and counted the gap between her blink and her reflection's, and it had been—she had told herself *almost* a second, *nearly* a second, because almost and nearly were words that kept the world shaped correctly.

The lag wasn't coming. The lag was here.

* * *

She should have stopped reading. Some part of her, the part her grandmother had built brick by brick—*stay small, stay quiet, stay unremarkable, so they can't pick you*—was screaming at her to put the ledger back on the desk exactly where she'd found it, close the iron door, climb the fourteen steps, and spend the rest of her employment perfecting the art of having never gone downstairs.

But her grandmother had also vanished, hadn't she. Had gone out one October morning for bread and become a story, then a silence, then a wound Wren learned to dress daily without looking at it. Staying unseen had not saved anyone Wren loved. It had only guaranteed that whatever took them took them unwitnessed.

Wren opened the ledger again and turned to the patient registers.

Marlowe had recorded deaths the way other matrons recorded fevers—with dates, names, and clinical brevity. But beside each entry, in a narrow column ruled into the page for the purpose, she had written numbers.

Backwards counts.

*Harriet Bellamy, d. 14 March 1911. Counted from thirty-one. Reached zero 11 November.*

*Thomas Greer, d. 2 June 1911. Counted from twenty-nine. Reached zero 11 November.*

*Constance Airey, d. 19 September 1911. Counted from thirty-three. Reached zero 11 November.*

Wren's eye snagged. She went back. Read the column again, top to bottom, her lips moving now without shame.

Eleventh of November. Eleventh of November. Eleventh of November.

Page after page. Year after year. Patients who died in spring, in summer, in the grey teeth of February—all of them beginning their backward counts in their final weeks, all of them reaching zero on the same calendar night. Not the same date of death. The same date of *zero*. As if their individual dyings were irrelevant, incidental—as if each patient was simply a clock wound to run down at the appointed hour, and the appointed hour was always, always the eleventh of November.

She flipped forward through the decades. Marlowe's hand gave way to other hands—keepers she'd never find names for, administrators, someone with a furious slanted scrawl, someone who pressed so hard the nib tore the paper. But the column persisted. The column survived every change of handwriting like a spine survives changes of flesh.

*Eleanor Voss, d. 1954. Zero: 11 November.*

*Martin Oduya, d. 1961. Zero: 11 November.*

*Ines Calloway, d. 1987. Zero: 11 November.*

And then—her stomach dropped through the floor of the archive and kept going—she found names she knew. Names from the current census, from the med pass sheets she initialed every shift.

*Ruth Ellery. Admitted March. Counting began Tuesday. Zero projected: 11 November.*

*Samuel Okafor. Counting began last week. Zero: 11 November.*

*Gerald Aldous. Counting began in his sleep, unbidden, from thirty. Zero: 11 November.*

Mr. Aldous, smiling at nothing in bed 12. Ruth Ellery, who had told Wren just yesterday, in a voice bright as a bell, that she was "getting her sums done." Samuel Okafor, who hummed his numbers like a lullaby.

They weren't dying on the eleventh of November.

They were *arriving* on the eleventh of November. All of them. Delivered.

Wren did the arithmetic with a steadiness that frightened her. Today was the fourth of November. Seven days. Seven days until every clock in the building ran down to zero at once, until the column of numbers in a hundred-year-old ledger converged on the present tense, until whatever waited in the one-second gap between the world and its reflection got the largest meal it had been offered since 1911—or whatever the unfinished seal demanded instead.

Seven days. And somewhere in this ledger, in some hand she hadn't read yet, was the description of seven stations and the price of walking them.

She thought about her sister. She thought about her grandmother's voice saying *so they can't pick you*, and how Wren had obeyed that voice for thirty years, and how obedience had bought her nothing but a front-row seat to other people's vanishings.

She thought about her reflection, blinking a beat behind her, patient as debt.

Wren stood. The stool scraped. In the silence afterward, she heard it—faint, rising through the floors, through the foundations, through whatever seam this building had been stitched across:

From somewhere above her, thin and sweet and horribly synchronized, a voice began to count. *Nine. Eight. Seven.* Then a second voice joined it, a third, a small chorus of the dying keeping their appointment early, rehearsing, and Wren stood alone in the founder's archive with a dead man's wife's confession in her hands and understood, finally, what kind of house she had been invisible inside of.

She tucked the ledger under her arm.

Whatever the eleventh of November required, she intended to read all of it first.
