## 9. Zero Night Approaching

It started with the minute.

Wren noticed it the way she noticed everything—sideways, without looking directly, because direct attention was an invitation and she had spent her whole life declining invitations. But even sideways, the pattern was impossible to miss once you'd seen it. Every night, at 11:47, the ward began to count.

Not all at once. Not loudly. Mr. Aldous first, his voice a dry rustle through the door of Room 4: *twenty-nine, twenty-eight.* Then Mrs. Okonkwo in Room 7, her accent softening the numbers into something almost like prayer. Then the others, one by one, until the whole east wing was murmuring backwards in the dark, a dozen dying voices descending toward zero like water finding its level.

They never finished together. Aldous would stop at eleven some nights, fourteen others, and fall back into breath. Okonkwo rarely made it past twenty. The new man in Room 9—the one whose name Wren still hadn't learned because learning names was how you got attached and attachment was how you got *noticed*—counted fastest, as if he were racing something. But they all started at the same moment. 11:47 exactly. Wren had checked her watch against the hallway clock against the administrator's office clock, and it was always the same minute, to the second.

And every night, the lag in the mirrors grew by a fraction. She could feel it now without testing, the way you feel weather coming in your teeth.

On the fourth night, she stood in the corridor at 11:46 with her back against the wall and her arms folded tight, counting her own breathing—four in, seven hold, four out, the rhythm her grandmother had taught her, the rhythm that kept you small and safe—and when the minute turned, the sound that rose out of the rooms was not twelve separate voices. It was one voice wearing twelve throats.

*Thirty. Twenty-nine. Twenty-eight.*

The cadence was identical in every room. The same pauses. The same faint smile she could hear in it, though smiles weren't supposed to be audible. She pressed her palm flat against the wall and felt, or imagined she felt, a low vibration running through the plaster, like the building itself was humming along.

She did not sleep that night. She sat at her desk in the staff room with the Founder's Ledger open under a pool of lamplight, and she began to make a map.

* * *

The ledger's columns were Edith Marlowe's handwriting, brown ink gone the color of old tea: patient names, admission dates, death dates. And beside each death date, in a narrower hand, a number. Wren had assumed at first these were ages, or bed numbers, or doses. Now, cross-referencing, she understood what they were.

Counts. The number each patient had counted down from on their final night. Marlowe had recorded them the way other administrators recorded temperatures.

Wren ruled a grid in pencil on the back of a supply requisition form. Down the left side, the names of the current ward—Aldous, Okonkwo, the man in Room 9, nine more. Across the top, dates. In each cell, the lowest number that patient had reached the night before. She'd started collecting them three nights ago, slipping down the corridor at 11:50 after the voices faded, noting figures in her small cramped hand.

The numbers were falling. Nightly. Predictably.

Aldous: stopped at fourteen two nights ago, eleven last night. Okonkwo: nineteen, then seventeen. The fast counter in Room 9: six, then four. She plotted them and drew lines through the points, and the lines converged—not approximately, not roughly, but with the clean terrible precision of something designed. All thirteen lines met the zero axis on the same date.

She checked the arithmetic twice. Three times. She held the paper up to the lamp as if the light might reveal an error.

Six days.

In six days, on the night of the winter solstice—the longest night, of course it would be the longest night—every patient on the ward would reach zero at the same moment. Thirteen counts ending together. And the ledger told her what happened when counts ended at zero on the convergence night. Marlowe's entries for those dates were different from all the others. Shorter. The death dates clustered there like birds on a wire, and beside them, instead of counts, Marlowe had written only a single word, over and over across the decades:

*Held.*

Held. As in contained. As in kept shut. As in, this year, we held it again.

Except 1938. Beside the 1938 cluster, the word was different. Smaller, pressed so hard into the paper the nib had nearly torn through:

*Fled.*

Wren sat very still in the empty staff room, listening to the building settle around her, and thought about what a seal does when it is not renewed.

* * *

In the morning she took the map to Administrator Voss—no relation, she'd checked, or none she could find, though the surname sat wrong in her mouth ever since the ledger—and laid it on the desk between the coffee mug and the stack of budget approvals.

"The patients are counting in unison," Wren said. "Every night. Same minute. The counts are decreasing at a fixed rate, and they converge on zero six days from now."

Marion Voss looked at the paper for a long moment. She was a practical woman, gray-blond, competent, the kind of administrator who could get a replacement oxygen concentrator approved in an afternoon and who ran Hollowmere the way you run any old building—by refusing to acknowledge the parts that creak.

"Terminal patients often count," she said. "It's well documented. Countdowns give them a sense of control. Order. It's actually quite touching."

"They start at the same second. They've never met each other. Two of them are non-verbal."

Voss's coffee mug paused halfway to her mouth.

"Non-verbal patients vocalizing near end of life isn't unknown," she said, but her voice had lost its footing. "Wren. You're an excellent nurse. One of the best I've had. But you've been odd lately. Distracted. I've had two families mention you standing in corridors at night." She set the mug down. "If you need leave—if it's grief, or burnout, or whatever it is you carry around like luggage—you only have to ask."

"I don't need leave. I need you to look at the dates in this ledger."

"I don't know what that ledger is."

"It was in your basement."

"It's an archive," Voss said, with the finality of a woman closing a drawer. "Historical documents. Nothing more." She slid the map back across the desk. "I'll have Dr. Prasad assess the counting behavior. Sedation review if necessary. And Wren—" something flickered behind her practical eyes, something that might have been fear wearing professionalism as a coat, "—on the solstice night, I'd prefer you took the evening off. It's standard rotation. You've earned it."

Standard rotation. Wren had worked at Hollowmere four years and had never once been offered solstice night off. She took the map back, and as she reached the door, Voss spoke again, quieter, not quite looking at her.

"My predecessor said the same things you're saying. In—" a pause, arithmetic being done and discarded, "—a long time ago. She also made charts." A thin smile. "We gave her leave too."

"Did she take it?"

"She retired," Voss said. "Somewhere coastal, I believe. People do that. They go where they can't hear the building."

Wren walked back up the corridor, past Room 4, past Room 7, past Room 9, and did not let herself look through any of the windows, because looking was attention and attention was how things found you. But she heard, faintly, from behind the closed doors, a sound she couldn't place until she was nearly at the nurses' station.

Humming. All of them, humming the same low note, holding it, the way you hold a note when someone has asked you to wait.

* * *

That evening Callum found her at the supply cupboard, and she didn't hear him coming, which frightened her more than anything else that week—she always heard people coming; hearing people coming was half of staying invisible.

"You've got the look," he said. He leaned against the opposite shelf, close enough that she could see the tired creases at his eyes, far enough that she could pretend she wasn't cataloguing them. "The one where you've figured something out and hate it."

"The counts converge," she said. It came out before she decided to say it. That was happening around him—words leaving without clearance. "Six days. Solstice night. Every patient reaches zero at once, and according to a ledger written by a woman dead eighty years, that's the night the building either holds or doesn't. And the administrator wants me to take the evening off."

"What did you tell her?"

"That I'd think about it."

"And?"

Wren pulled a box of gauze off the shelf and held it, needing her hands occupied. "My grandmother used to say that when you hear counting in the dark, you don't join in and you don't run. Running makes you interesting." She turned the box over. "I used to think it was just something old women said."

"And now?"

"Now I think my grandmother knew exactly what happens when the count ends." She looked at him, finally, straight on, which cost her something. "Six days, Callum. And I'm the only one here who's read the ledger. The only one who hears it the way I hear it."

Something moved through his face—she would remember later that it looked like recognition, and wonder why she hadn't asked about it then.

"Then you've got six days," he said, "to figure out what you're going to do about it."

"I already know what I'm going to do about it," Wren said. "I'm going to spend them reading. Every page. Including the ones that are sewn into the binding." She had found the stitches that morning—uneven threads at the ledger's spine, a section hidden the way her grandmother hid things, the way her family hid things, sewn shut and meant to stay that way. "There's a confession in there somewhere. Marlowe wrote everything else down. She wouldn't have left the important part blank."

That night at 11:47, the ward counted, and Wren stood in the dark corridor with the ledger open on her knees, and listened to thirteen voices descend toward zero like a tide going out—like something being drained, or something being fed—and beneath the counting, so faint she might have invented it, she heard the mirrors in the hallway ticking softly to themselves, keeping a time of their own.

One second late. Two seconds now. Falling further behind every night, like a clock winding down toward a moment it already knew was coming.

Six days.

She picked up her pencil and began unpicking the first stitch.
