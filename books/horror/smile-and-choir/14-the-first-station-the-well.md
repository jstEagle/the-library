## 14. The First Station: The Well

The ledger said the ritual could not be rehearsed.

*No station may be walked in advance. No payment may be tested. The seal knows a rehearsal from a vow, and it will take the coin and give nothing back.*

Wren read that line four times, sitting on the floor of the archive with her back against the cold stone, and then she closed the book and decided she was going to do it anyway.

Because there was a difference, she reasoned, between walking a station and standing near one. Between paying and pricing. She wasn't going to speak the words. She wasn't going to make the vow. She was only going to go down to the well—the first of the seven stations—and look at it. Learn its shape. Learn what it asked. A soldier doesn't fire his rifle before battle; he cleans it. He learns the weight of it in his hands so that when the moment comes, the weight is already familiar.

That was what she told herself, anyway, as she waited for the hospice to settle into its night rhythms. That was what she told herself while she checked the lights on the east corridor—twelve of them, all burning—and counted the doors—fourteen, locked—and confirmed that Callum was asleep in the on-call room with his door ajar, the way he'd started leaving it lately, as if he wanted to be findable. As if he knew she checked.

She told herself a lot of things at half past eleven, standing in the basement stairwell with Edith Marlowe's ledger under her arm and a flashlight in her hand, listening to the building breathe around her.

Hollowmere breathed differently at night. She had known this for years without admitting it. In the daytime the hospice was pipes and radiators and the settling groans of old timber. But after midnight the exhalations came slower, deeper, timed to nothing mechanical. And tonight—two nights before Zero Night, when the lag would reach its deepest point and every mirror in the building would become a held breath—the breathing had a rhythm she almost recognized. Almost like counting.

She went down.

* * *

The well was older than the hospice.

Edith Marlowe's ledger was matter-of-fact about this: *the house was raised over the well because the well was here first, and what is first must be honored or fed.* The founders hadn't dug it; they'd found it, a dry stone throat sunk into the hillside, and they'd built their seal around it the way you'd build a lock around a keyhole. The chapel above it. The bell above that. Seven stations arranged like vertebrae down through the building's spine, and the well was the first vertebra, the one everything else hung from.

Wren found it behind a door she had counted a hundred times without ever opening—the fourth door past the boiler room, which her inventory listed as *storage, flooded, do not enter.* It wasn't flooded. It was a short stone corridor, sloping gently downward, and at the end of it the floor simply stopped being floor and became a mouth: a circular shaft perhaps eight feet across, ringed with an iron lip worn smooth by generations of hands. Darkness below. Cold air rising, carrying a smell like wet flint and something sweeter underneath, something organic, like the inside of a shell.

Her flashlight beam went down maybe twenty feet before the dark ate it.

She stood at the lip for a long time, doing what she always did: cataloguing. Exits—one, behind her. Handholds—the iron ring set into the wall just below the lip, plus footholds chiseled into the stone in a spiral pattern, deliberate, ancient, made for climbing. Sound—the drip of water somewhere far below, though the ledger insisted the well was dry. Except it wasn't quite a drip. It was too regular. She timed it against her pulse and found it slower. Against her breath and found it patient.

And then she looked down again, and her stomach dropped out from under her.

There was a face at the bottom of the well.

Pale, small, tilted upward, catching her flashlight beam from below—impossible, because the light was pointing down and the face was looking up and the geometry didn't work, the light should have been hitting the top of a head, not illuminating a full face turned toward her like a flower toward sun. It took her three full seconds to understand what she was seeing, and understanding didn't help.

It was her own face.

Her reflection was at the bottom of the well. Waiting for her.

Wren did not scream. Screaming makes noise, and noise draws attention, and attention is how things find you—that catechism had been drilled into her so deep it operated below thought. What she did instead was step back from the lip, slowly, one silent foot at a time, until her spine met the corridor wall, and she stood there shaking while the flashlight trembled in her grip and threw her shadow in wild shapes against the stone.

The reflection didn't climb. That was the thing. It stayed at the bottom, face upturned, and even from thirty feet away Wren could see that it was smiling. Not the way she smiled. She smiled with her lips pressed flat, a closed-door smile, a nothing-to-see-here smile. This one was open. Warm. Delighted. The smile of someone who has been waiting a long time for a friend to arrive and has finally heard footsteps.

*Come down,* the smile said, without any mouth moving at all. Or maybe her own mind supplied the words. Maybe that was worse.

She thought about her grandmother's voice: *they can't pick you if they can't see you.* She thought about her sister, gone thirty years now, taken because someone noticed her, because someone looked too long. She thought about the thing behind the glass, the thing that remembered people, the thing that wore faces. And she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the reflection at the bottom of the well was not a trap she had stumbled into.

It was a courtesy. An usher showing her to her seat.

The stations weren't defenses against the thing behind the glass. They were doors between her and it, and every one of them opened from both sides.

* * *

She nearly left. She wants that recorded, wherever such things get recorded—she climbed back up the basement stairs, sat in the staff kitchen under the buzzing fluorescent light, drank a cup of tea she didn't taste, and told herself very firmly that the sensible move was to wait two more nights, walk the stations on Zero Night in the proper order with Callum holding the doors, and never once stand alone over open dark.

But the sensible move required believing she had two nights of ordinary time left, and she no longer believed that. The lag was spreading. That afternoon she had watched a patient's water glass sit on his bedside table for a full second before his reflection picked it up. The ward mirrors had begun to fog from the edges inward, like breath on cold glass, like something pressing its face close from the other side. Time itself was thinning around Hollowmere, wearing through, and every hour she spent not-knowing was an hour the thing behind the glass spent knowing her better.

Knowledge was armor. Her whole life had been built on that principle. So she finished the tea, and she went back down.

This time she brought the ledger, open to Edith Marlowe's hand-written page on the first station, and she read it aloud at the lip of the well, because some instructions apparently needed a voice:

*"The first station asks the least and teaches the most. It takes a small joy, freely given, and shows the keeper what giving feels like. Let her learn on something she can spare."*

*A small joy, freely given.*

Wren knelt at the iron lip and took inventory of her small joys, and the inventory was short, because she had spent forty years keeping her possessions of the heart minimal, portable, easy to abandon. There was Callum—no. Not that. Never that; she'd need that for later stations, or rather she'd lose it at later stations, and she wasn't spending it early, not on a test. There was the smell of rain on hot pavement. There was the first sip of coffee in silence. There was—

Her tenth birthday.

It surfaced unbidden, whole and bright: the kitchen in her grandmother's house, flour dust hanging in the yellow light, her grandmother's hands shaping a lopsided cake while her sister sat on the counter swinging her legs, and the whole day smelling of vanilla and woodsmoke, and Wren—ten years old, gap-toothed, safe—knowing with a child's absolute certainty that she was loved. That was all. Nothing happened. Nothing bad. It was just a day when she was happy without guarding herself, one of maybe five such days in her entire careful life.

A small joy. Freely given.

Her eyes were stinging. "I don't want to lose it," she said out loud, to the well, to the dark, to whatever listened. Her voice sounded very small in the stone throat. "That's the point of it. That's why it's mine."

The drip from below paused. Resumed. Patient.

And she understood suddenly what Edith Marlowe had meant by *teaches the most.* The well wasn't asking for the memory. The well was asking whether she could hold a thing she loved in her open hands and let it go—not have it torn away, not have it stolen in the night like her sister, but choose the loss, say the word herself, keep her eyes open while it left. Because that was the whole ritual, wasn't it? Seven stations, each one a larger letting-go, ending at the threshold where she would give up love itself. If she couldn't release a birthday, she'd shatter at the threshold. If she couldn't release a birthday, she deserved to shatter at the threshold.

The dying counted backwards because they were practicing the same descent. Giving it back piece by piece. Mr. Aldous, smiling at zero.

"Okay," Wren whispered.

She spoke the words from the ledger. Her voice cracked on the second one and steadied on the third. The old language tasted like iron filings and church dust, and when she finished, the silence below changed texture—the way a room changes when someone in it decides to pay attention.

Then she gave it.

She didn't know how else to describe it. She reached for the birthday the way you'd reach for a bird perched on your finger, and she opened her hand, and it flew. There was no pain. That was the horror of it—there was no pain at all, only a lightness, a subtracting, a page lifting cleanly out of a book. One moment the day existed, warm and vanilla-scented and safe. The next moment there was a Wren-shaped hole where it had been, and she was kneeling at the lip of a dry well knowing only that something had just been taken and that she had handed it over herself, willingly, and that the grief she felt had no object. It was grief with the photograph removed from the frame. Mourning a person whose name she'd never learned.

Below her, in the dark, her reflection laughed.

Not cruelly. That was the worst part. It laughed the way you laugh when a child finally manages a somersault—warm, proud, delighted—and the sound rose up the stone throat and wrapped around her like a coat, and for one terrible half-second it felt like being loved, and she understood exactly how the thing behind the glass had survived this long. It didn't hunt. It welcomed.

Then the laugh faded, and the well was just a well, and Wren Lockridge knelt alone on cold stone with wet cheeks and a hole in her heart shaped like a day she could no longer remember.

* * *

Climbing out took longer than climbing down, though the distance was the same. Her arms shook. At the top of the spiral footholds she paused, gripping the iron ring, and looked back down one last time.

The reflection was still there. Still face-up. Still smiling—but the smile had changed. Smaller now. Satisfied. The smile of a teller who has counted the drawer and found it correct.

*Six more,* it seemed to say. *And each one heavier than the last.*

She climbed out, shut the door, and stood in the boiler-room corridor doing her rituals twice through because once didn't calm her: twelve lights, fourteen doors, twelve lights, fourteen doors. Her hands wouldn't fully stop trembling. Somewhere overhead, faintly, through floors of old hospice, a patient was counting backwards in his sleep. She let the numbers wash over her without listening to them. Listening was how things got in.

Back in her room, she sat on her bed and tried to reconstruct the memory she'd paid, the way you probe a tooth socket with your tongue. She knew the facts, because facts were written elsewhere: her tenth birthday had happened at her grandmother's house. There had been a cake. Her sister had been alive. But the day itself was gone—gone so completely that she couldn't even feel bad about losing it properly, because feeling bad required remembering what was lost, and all she had was the shape of the absence. A silhouette where a scene used to be. She kept reaching for it and finding smooth walls.

Except for one thing. One fragment had survived, wedged somewhere the well's appetite couldn't reach, or maybe deliberately left behind, the way you leave a lamp on for someone coming home late:

She was afraid of the dark. Had been, all her life—she knew this the way she knew her own name, a fact without a story attached. Flashlight under her pillow. Doors checked twice. Forty years of counting lights because darkness had done something to her once, or been something to her once, and the fear predated every memory she could access.

She sat with that fear now, turning it over, looking for its root.

Nothing. Smooth walls all the way down.

And that was when the true cost of the evening arrived—not the loss of the birthday, but the loss of the *reason*. Whatever had made a ten-year-old girl afraid of the dark had lived inside that day, or beside it, braided into it, and the well had swallowed the whole braid. She was afraid of the dark the way she was afraid of the number thirteen: structurally, superstitiously, with no experience underneath. A fear amputated from its history. She would carry it for the rest of her life and never know why, and no one could ever return it to her, because she had signed for its delivery herself.

She thought of Ada Voss, fleeing in 1938, choosing survival. She had judged her, quietly, from the safety of eighty years' distance. Now she understood. It wasn't cowardice. It was this. It was the discovery that the price was real, that the well smiled at you while it ate, that every station forward was another piece of yourself handed into open dark—and Ada Voss had stood where Wren knelt and calculated, and fled, and lived a long life with holes where her days used to be.

Wren lay down without undressing. She was tired in a way sleep wouldn't fix.

Through the wall, muffled, the counting continued. And beneath it, so faint she might have invented it, another sound: the drip from the dry well, keeping time, patient as ever—except now, she noticed, it had changed. It wasn't counting down anymore.

It was counting up.

One. Two. Three. Four.

Like something at the bottom, learning her pace. Like something beginning, at last, to climb.
