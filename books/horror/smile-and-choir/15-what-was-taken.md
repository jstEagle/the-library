## 15. What Was Taken

The county archive smelled like dust and old decisions.

Callum had been there since eight in the morning, hunched over a table in the records room with a lamp pulled close and a pencil he kept chewing on when he forgot himself. The archivist—a woman named Mrs. Okafor with reading glasses on a chain and the patience of someone who had spent forty years watching people find out things they wished they hadn't—had already brought him two boxes he hadn't asked for.

"You're the third person to come looking at Hollowmere," she'd said, setting them down. "In my time. The other two didn't stay long."

"Did they find what they wanted?"

"No one ever finds what they want," she said. "They find what's there."

He understood what she meant by noon.

The 1938 file was thin. That was the first wrong thing about it—every other year had folders fat with requisitions, inspection reports, correspondence between the hospice board and the county health office. But 1938 was a single folder, and inside it were only four documents: an inventory of linens, a letter from Edith Marlowe's estate lawyer confirming the transfer of property to the Hollowmere Trust, a newspaper clipping about a flood upstate that had nothing to do with anything, and a resignation letter.

Callum read the resignation letter three times.

It was written in a hand so small and controlled it looked typeset. *To the Board of Trustees of Hollowmere Hospice: I hereby resign my position as Keeper of the Count, effective immediately. I am sorry for what I have failed to do. Do not look for me. Do not finish it in my name. Some doors are better left half-shut than held open by a frightened woman.*

It was signed: *Ada Voss.*

Keeper of the Count. He sat back in his chair and let the words settle. Wren had described the ritual to him two nights ago, standing in the linen room with the ledger open between them like a patient on a table—the seven stations, the payments, the willing keeper who must walk it through on the night of deepest lag. She hadn't used the phrase *Keeper of the Count*, but she hadn't needed to. It was the same word wearing different clothes.

Ada Voss had been the keeper. And Ada Voss had run.

*Some doors are better left half-shut.* Callum thought of the mirror in the east hallway, the one that showed him and Wren standing there a full second before their bodies caught up. Half-shut wasn't how he would describe what was happening behind the glass anymore. Half-shut was generous. Half-shut was what you called a door that still pretended to be a door.

He turned to the last document in the folder and felt the room tilt.

It was a county register—not a Hollowmere document at all, just misfiled, the way old paper drifts into the wrong boxes over decades like sediment settling. A birth record. Dated March 1911, the same year Edith Marlowe had opened the hospice. The child's name was Ada Voss. The mother's name was listed as *Voss, née Marlowe.*

Ada Voss had been Edith Marlowe's daughter.

Callum stared at the entry until the ink seemed to swim. The founder's own blood had been the one to flee. The seal had been left unfinished not by some hired caretaker, not by some stranger who got scared—but by the woman Edith Marlowe had raised, presumably trained, presumably trusted with everything. Which meant the debt wasn't institutional. It wasn't a job posting that had gone unfilled since 1938.

It was a family inheritance.

And if it ran in Ada Voss's blood—

His pencil stopped against his teeth.

He thought about Wren saying, in her flat careful voice, *the ledger says the keeper must be one who hears.* He thought about the way she counted doors without noticing she was doing it, the way she checked lights in threes, the way she moved through the ward like a rumor of herself. He thought about her grandmother teaching her to stay unseen. *So they can't pick you,* she'd said. He'd assumed it was superstition, the kind of folk wisdom that gets handed down like a recipe.

But what if it was curriculum?

What if the Lockridges had been keeping something all along—not the seal itself, but the readiness for it? What if every generation of Wren's family had been taught the same lessons, told the same stories, shaped into exactly the kind of person who could hear the counting and walk the stations—and every generation had hoped, quietly, desperately, that they'd never be asked?

He pulled the box toward him again and started going through it properly this time, page by page, because if Ada Voss was real then there might be more of her somewhere in the paper trail. People leave traces. Even people who don't want to be found—especially people who don't want to be found. They change their names but keep their handwriting. They move towns but marry within them. They think distance is anonymity, and it never is.

He found her in the 1940 census. *Vera Adell Moss*, living in a boarding house in Corning, occupation listed as *seamstress*. Same small, controlled handwriting in the enumerator's notes—no, wait, that wouldn't be hers. But the age matched. The birthplace matched. And in the margin, in a different hand, someone had written *see also: Voss, A.* as though even the census taker had sensed the name was a coat worn over another coat.

From Corning he tracked her to Elmira, then Binghamton, each move smaller, each town further from Hollowmere, as if she were walking away from it at the pace of a slow bleed. In 1952 there was a marriage license—*Vera Moss to Henry Delacroix.*

Delacroix.

Callum put the page down flat on the table and pressed both palms against it, as if it might get up and leave.

Delacroix was not Wren's surname. Wren was a Lockridge—her mother's line, she'd said once, offhand, the way people say things they think don't matter. Her father had been a Delacroix. Her father, who died when she was six. Her father, whose side of the family she never talked about except to say there wasn't much to talk about.

Henry Delacroix. If the dates lined up—and Callum did the arithmetic twice, then a third time because he wanted to be wrong—they lined up perfectly. Henry Delacroix would have been Ada Voss's son. Which made Ada Voss Wren's paternal grandmother.

Not great-aunt. Grandmother.

The debt didn't just run in Wren's blood. It ran in the most direct line blood allowed.

*

He didn't go straight back to Hollowmere.

He told himself it was because he wanted to check one more source—the county historical society had a photograph collection, and photographs were harder to lie with than documents. But the truth was uglier and simpler: he sat in his car in the archive parking lot for twenty minutes with the engine off, holding the steering wheel, trying to figure out how to say it.

*Hey, Wren. Good news and bad news. Bad news is the woman who doomed your family has been dooming your family specifically, by name, for eighty-six years. Good news is—*

There was no good news. That was the problem. Every angle he turned it, the information only did damage. If he told her, he took away whatever small comfort she'd built from believing her grandmother's paranoia was just paranoia—that the counting rituals and the invisibility training were folk fear, inherited noise, nothing with a signature on it. Instead she'd learn that her whole childhood had been preparation. That the lessons weren't warnings about a vague *they* but about a specific thing behind specific glass, and that her grandmother had known its name.

Worse—if he told her, he'd be handing her a reason to doubt herself at the exact moment she couldn't afford one. Because here was the thought he couldn't stop having, the one that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: *what if the ledger chose her not because she hears, but because her family broke it?* What if the keeper requirement wasn't a gift but a restitution? The debt runs in the blood; the blood pays the debt. Edith Marlowe's daughter fled, and now Edith Marlowe's great-great-granddaughter would be the one to finish what Ada abandoned—or die trying, or worse than die, or give up every memory of everyone she loved, which after knowing Wren for six weeks Callum already suspected was worse.

And if he didn't tell her?

Then he became the thing he hated: another person deciding what Wren Lockridge could handle. Another person managing her. He thought about how carefully she watched everyone at Hollowmere, how she catalogued every shift in tone, every glance held too long. She would know. Not today, maybe not tomorrow, but eventually—and she'd know he'd carried it alone, and she'd understand that he'd decided her grief for her, the way everyone always had, the way her grandmother had decided it before anyone else got the chance.

Mrs. Okafor found him still sitting in the lot at four o'clock. She tapped on the window, and he rolled it down, embarrassed.

"The historical society closes at five," she said. "If you're going."

"How did you know I was going?"

"Because you have the face." She peered at him over her glasses. "Everyone who finds the Voss connection has the face. You sit in your car and you try to decide whether knowing someone is the same as loving them."

"That's not—" He stopped. "Is that what happened to the others? The ones who came looking before?"

"The first one was a journalist, 1979. He wrote a piece nobody printed and drank himself quiet. The second was a young man in 2003." She paused. "He had the same face you have. Dark hair. Kind eyes. He came asking about a patient who'd died here counting backwards."

Callum's mouth went dry. "Your mother."

"He didn't tell me that. But he asked all the questions a son asks." Mrs. Okafor folded her hands. "He found the Voss connection too. And then he went back to Hollowmere, and I never heard from him again. So." She shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "Go to your society. Or go home. Either way, you'll end up telling her or you won't, and either way the count keeps counting. That's the one thing I've learned in forty years, Mr. Reyes. The paper waits. People don't."

She walked back inside. Callum started the car.

*

The historical society was a converted house with creaking floors and a volunteer named Gus who let him into the photograph room and promptly fell asleep in a wingback chair. Callum went through the Hollowmere materials with white cotton gloves and hands that wouldn't quite steady.

He found what he was looking for at quarter to five: a group photograph from the hospice's dedication day, June 1911. Staff arranged on the front steps in two rows, stiff and sun-squinted. Edith Marlowe stood at the center, tall, severe, her hands folded like a woman posing for a verdict. And beside her, maybe nineteen years old, with dark hair pinned up and eyes that even in silver gelatin looked like they were listening to something no one else could hear—

A girl. Young. Familiar in a way that made the hair rise on Callum's arms, because he knew that face. He'd seen it softened by sleep across a linen room, sharpened by resolve in a mirror-lagged corridor, lit by lamplight while she traced dead women's names in a ledger older than both of them.

The caption beneath the photograph read: *Miss E. Marlowe and staff, dedication of Hollowmere Home for the Dying. At right: Miss M. Voss, assistant keeper.*

M. Voss. Not Ada. He leaned closer. The girl beside Edith Marlowe was labeled M. Voss—but the face was Wren's face, or near enough that a stranger would ask if they were sisters. And Ada had been born in March 1911, which made her an infant at the dedication. This was someone else. An M. Voss. A sister, perhaps. A mother.

Which meant the Vosses hadn't just worked at Hollowmere. They had *staffed* it. Generation after generation, keeper after keeper, the same family standing on those steps in different clothes, holding the same door shut with their bodies until one of them finally let go.

He photographed the picture with his phone, then sat with it a long moment while Gus snored. In the glass of the photograph's frame—he noticed it only as he was leaving, and told himself afterward that he'd imagined it, though he knew he hadn't—his own reflection arrived a beat late, raising the phone a half-second after his hand had already lowered it.

*

He drove back through hills going purple with dusk, and the whole way he argued with himself in the silence of the car, both sides losing.

Tell her. Don't tell her. Tell her tonight. Wait until after Zero Night. Never tell her. Tell her everything except the part about restitution. Tell her the part about restitution and nothing else, let her draw the rest.

By the time the hospice lights came up out of the dark—those warm yellow windows he'd come to think of as a face he recognized—he had settled on nothing, which was its own kind of answer, and the worst one.

Wren was in the staff kitchen when he came in, standing at the counter with tea going cold beside her and the ledger open under the little lamp they'd started leaving there for her. She looked up, and for once she didn't do the thing she usually did, the flicker of assessment, the quick inventory of his face for threats. For once she just looked glad. Tired and hollowed-out and glad, in the small specific way that was hers.

"You're back late," she said. "Find anything?"

And there it was. The question, plain as a held-out hand.

He thought of Mrs. Okafor: *the paper waits; people don't.* He thought of the girl on the steps with Wren's face. He thought of his mother, counting backwards in a room like this one, dying toward a zero no one explained to him, and how he had sworn—sworn on her actual grave, in the actual dirt—that he would never again let someone he loved carry a secret that was eating them, even if the secret-eater was him.

"Yeah," Callum said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "I found Ada Voss."

Wren went very still. Outside, down the hall, faint as breath, the night shift of patients had begun their minute—the soft rising chorus of numbers falling backward, thirty, twenty-nine, twenty-eight, the sound he'd stopped being able to hear as anything but a lullaby sung in reverse.

"And Callum?" Wren said quietly, watching his face the way she watched mirrors. "You're doing the thing where you decide what I can handle. Don't."

He set the photograph on the counter between them, face up, and turned the ledger toward the page where the names ran in Edith Marlowe's hand, and began at the beginning—which was also, he now knew, the ending, waiting sixty feet below them in the dark of the well.

"It's not just that she fled," he said. "Wren. She's your grandmother. The debt isn't near your family. It *is* your family."

The counting down the hall reached ten. Nine. Eight.

Wren looked at the photograph, and then at him, and he watched her arrive at the same place he'd been sitting in the parking lot all afternoon—the place where knowing someone and loving them stop being the same thing, and you have to choose which one you're going to act on.

Seven, said the ward. Six. Five.

"Start from the birth record," Wren said. "Don't skip anything."

Four. Three. Two.

He told her everything.
