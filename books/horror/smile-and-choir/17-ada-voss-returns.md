## 17. Ada Voss Returns

The admission came through on a Tuesday, which felt wrong. I had come to think of Hollowmere's bad days as arriving without calendar courtesy—zero nights, mirror lags, the ward counting in its sleep—but this one had paperwork. A transfer from St. Brendan's, sixty miles east. Female, eighty-nine, terminal cardiac decline, no family listed.

Her name was Ada Voss.

I read it three times at the nurses' station, the fax paper trembling slightly under my thumb, and told myself it was a coincidence. There were thousands of Vosses in the world. There were probably dozens of Ada Vosses. The ledger was nearly a century old; names got reused, recycled, handed down like coats.

But my hands knew better than my logic did. My hands went cold the way they used to when Grandmother pulled me behind the coat rack at church and whispered *don't look up, they're choosing.*

"Lockridge." Administrator Pell stood in his doorway with his tie loosened and his expression set to administrative concern, the only setting he had left since the ward started counting in unison and he'd decided, with magnificent discipline, not to notice. "You're taking the new admit. Room nine. She asked for you specifically."

"She asked for me by name?"

"By name." He frowned at the chart. "Said she'd been waiting for you to be old enough. Which I assume is confusion. She's quite far along."

*Waiting for you to be old enough.* I took the chart because refusing would have required explaining, and explaining was a door I couldn't afford to open in front of Pell. Room nine was at the end of the east corridor, past the mirror hall—the long corridor of framed and silvered glass that now lagged three full seconds and where, two nights ago, I had watched my missing sister press her palms flat against the inside of the world and mouth my name.

I did not look into the mirrors as I passed. I had gotten very good at not looking. It was, after all, the one skill I'd been raised in.

Room nine smelled of lavender and something older underneath, something mineral, like the air in the basement archive where Edith Marlowe's ledger lived. The woman in the bed was small and folded, a letter creased too many times. Her hair was white as salt and cut short, practical, a survivor's haircut from some decade that hadn't been practical in fifty years. Her eyes were open. They found me before I finished crossing the threshold, and they were not confused at all.

"You have her chin," Ada Voss said. "Your grandmother's. I'd know it anywhere. I helped hold her when she was born."

My hand found the bed rail and gripped it.

"You're Ada Voss," I said.

"I'm Ada Voss." She smiled, and there was nothing sweet in it—only a kind of exhausted accuracy, the smile of someone who has done arithmetic for eighty years and finally gotten the answer. "And you're the girl who's going to finish what I ran from. Sit down, child. We don't have the time your face says we have."

*

I sat. Of course I sat. Every instinct I owned said run—run the way she had run, run the way every keeper before her had run—and I sat down anyway, because Callum had taught me over these weeks that some doors you walk through even when every alarm in your body is ringing, especially then.

"I read about you," I said. "In the archive. The last keeper. 1938. You were supposed to walk the seven stations on the night of deepest lag, and you didn't."

"No." She said it simply, no flinch, no excuse. "I was twenty-two years old and I was supposed to give up everything I loved, and I looked at the well and I thought: I am young. Someone else will do it. Someone always has." Her laugh was dry as ash. "Someone always had, until me. Eighty-seven years of one-second delay, growing longer. Growing hungry. Because I chose my mother's face over the world's."

"The lag is because of you."

"The lag is because of all of us who chose. I was just the last." She turned her head on the pillow with effort, and I saw how thin her neck was, how the tendons stood out like cables. "Edith Marlowe built the seal in 1911 with six keepers walking the stations together, each paying what held them. It held for twenty-seven years. Then the flu took two of them, age took the rest, and by 1938 there was only me, and the ritual needs a willing keeper, alone, on the night of deepest lag, or it frays. And I—" She closed her eyes. "I heard my mother calling from the well. Do you understand? The thing behind the glass had already learned what I loved. It called me in her voice, and I believed it, and I ran. I ran so fast and so far that I convinced myself for sixty years that there had never been anything to run from. That's the cruelest part of surviving a cowardice. You get to forget you were brave enough to know the truth."

Outside the window, October light lay flat on the grounds. Somewhere down the hall a patient began counting backwards—*twenty-six, twenty-five*—the way they all did now, every night, the same minute, a choir of the dying answering a call I could hear but not yet name.

"Why come back?" I asked. "Why now?"

"Because I'm dying, and dying people get honest." Her eyes opened again, and they were terrible and kind at once. "Because the convergence is four days away, and you've found the ledger, and you've started the stations—I can smell the well water on you, child, the ritual marks what it takes from—and because if you walk into zero night knowing only what the ledger told you, you will fail. Not because you're weak. Because the ledger doesn't tell you the true cost."

"The ledger says I give up what holds me to the world. My memories. Everyone I've ever loved."

"Yes. That part's accurate." She said it gently, the way you tell someone a bone is broken. "But here's what the ledger leaves out. It isn't a payment made once. The stations take memories, yes—but the final station takes the love itself. Not the memory of loving. The capacity. You'll wake hollow and calm and you'll know you loved someone the way you know a room was once painted a different color—you'll see the outline, the shadow on the wall where the picture hung, and you will never, ever find the picture. And the thing behind the glass knows this. It has watched eighty-seven years of keepers refuse, and it has gotten very good at the moment of refusal. So listen to me now, girl, and listen with whatever part of you still believes in danger."

She reached out and caught my wrist. Her grip was astonishing, iron wrapped in paper.

"It will offer you exactly what you most want. Not what you say you want. What you *most* want, down in the cellar of yourself, the want you don't say out loud. For me it was my mother, alive, in the kitchen, asking why I'd stayed away so long. I was twenty-two and I nearly stepped through the glass to her. If I had—" She shook her head. "There'd be no hospice here. There'd be no town. There'd be a hole in the world shaped like everything, and it would be smiling."

"My sister," I said. My voice came out small. "It already showed me my sister. In the mirror hall. She was mouthing my name."

Ada Voss's grip tightened.

"And did you go to her?"

"No. Callum stopped me. Or I stopped myself. I don't know which."

"Then you're stronger than I was at twenty-two, and weaker than you'll need to be in four days." She released my wrist and lay back, breathing hard from the effort of the grip, and for a moment I saw the whole shape of her: a girl of twenty-two stretched across ninety years, still running, arrived at last at the place she'd fled. "Here is what I know that the ledger doesn't. Three things. Write them on the inside of your skull."

I waited.

"One: the offer will be perfect. That's how you'll know it's false. Everything real is flawed, child. Your sister's face in the glass will be more beautiful than your sister ever was, her voice kinder, her forgiveness cleaner. Real love comes with damage attached. The thing behind the glass can copy the face but it cannot copy the damage—it doesn't understand damage, it's never been hurt, it's only ever been *outside*. So when the offer comes, look for the flaw. Look for the thing that's too good. That's the seam. That's where you tear it open."

"Two?"

"Two: it will use what you've already paid. The memories you gave the stations are *in there* now. It has your tenth birthday. It has whatever else you've surrendered. It will wear them, offer them back to you, dangle them like keys. When it offers you something you already paid away, that is not generosity. That is a counterfeit showing you the mold it was cast from." Her mouth twisted. "And three—"

She stopped. Her breath caught, and for a moment I thought the end had come early, right there in the middle of her confession, and I was reaching for the call button when she caught my hand again.

"Three," she said, quieter. "When you stand at the final station, and it offers you the last thing, the biggest thing—the love itself, the person, alive, warm, forgiven, *returned*—you will not be able to remember why you should refuse. That's the trap inside the trap. The ritual will already have taken the memories that would arm you against the offer. You'll be standing there unarmed, offered everything, and the only thing left in you that knows the truth will be—" she tapped my sternum with one finger, light as a moth landing, "—this. Whatever's under the memory. Love isn't stored in the recollection of it. Recollection is just the receipt. The love itself lives somewhere the ritual can't reach until the very last second, and it will know its own. Trust the ache, not the story. The ache is yours. The story will be theirs."

The ward counted. Down the corridor, faintly, in the voice of Mr. Aldous and the others: *fourteen, thirteen, twelve.* Ada Voss listened to it with her eyes closed, and something in her face eased, the way a soldier eases at the sound of a familiar bombardment.

"They're feeding it," I said. "The dying. Aldous told me—they're not fading, they're answering a call. Keeping it fed so it stays behind the glass."

"Yes. Edith's design. The dying pay their last breaths backward, and the thing drinks, and stays sated, and stays *out*. But the seal is fraying, and the feed isn't enough anymore, and that's why the lag grows. One second became three. By zero night it will be five, maybe more. Long enough for it to reach through." She opened her eyes. "Which brings us to the question you haven't asked me yet, though you've been carrying it since you walked in."

"What question?"

"Why I came back to die here instead of anywhere else in the world." She smiled again, and this time there was mercy in it, actual mercy, worn smooth by decades of regret. "Because I owe the seal a keeper's death. I refused to pay in life, so I'll pay in death—my last breath goes backward into the count like the others, and it buys you minutes. Maybe an hour. On zero night, when you're standing at the sixth station and the building is screaming, you'll feel the seal strengthen for a little while, and that will be me. An old coward's last act of courage. It won't be much. But it will be something, and I have spent eighty-seven years owing something."

I didn't trust my voice, so I said nothing. Outside, the light was failing. Four days.

"There's one more thing," she said. "And it's the hardest, so I'll say it while I still have the breath for hard things." She pulled herself up against the pillows, and her eyes fixed on mine with a ferocity that belonged to the twenty-two-year-old still living inside her. "Callum Reyes. The boy who requested this posting because of his mother. You love him."

It wasn't a question, and I didn't insult us both by denying it.

"When he asks to walk the ritual instead of you—and he will ask, he's already asked, hasn't he—"

"He has."

"—refuse him. Not because the ledger says the keeper must be one who hears, though it does, and not because he can't pay the price, though he can't, none of us can, that's rather the point. Refuse him because if he walks it, he becomes what I am. He survives. The ritual refuses the unwilling, spits them back out, and leaves them hollow in a different way—hollow with *survival*, which is the worst hollowness there is. I know. I am its expert." Her voice dropped. "And worse—if he walks it and fails, the seal breaks with no keeper at all, and everything ends tonight instead of in four days. You are the only door, child. That's not a burden anyone chose for you fairly. But it's the truth, and I am too old and too close to the dark to lie to you about it."

*Eleven, ten, nine,* said the ward.

"Rest," I said, and meant it as mercy, and she took it as surrender, which perhaps it was.

"I'll rest when I've paid," she said. "Now go. Check your mirrors. Sleep if you can. And child—" I paused at the door, and she was watching me with those terrible kind eyes, and the evening light made her look briefly, unbearably young. "Whatever it shows you in four days, however real it looks, however much it hurts to refuse—remember that the real ones, the dead ones, the true loves, they wouldn't ask you to open the door. Only the false ones ask. The true ones wait."

I walked back down the east corridor past the mirror hall, and I kept my eyes forward, and behind the glass, in the corner of my vision, something turned to watch me pass—something wearing a face I had spent eleven years trying not to remember, smiling with a perfection no living sister had ever managed.

Too good, Ada had said. Look for the flaw.

I found it. The flaw was that it wanted me to look.

That night, for the first time since the lag began, every mirror in Hollowmere went dark at once—all of them, in every corridor, in every room, a building closing its eyes—and in the silence afterward, from room nine, thin and clear and steady, an old woman's voice joined the count:

*Nine. Eight. Seven.*

Four days. And now the seal had its coward back, and the dark knew we were coming.
