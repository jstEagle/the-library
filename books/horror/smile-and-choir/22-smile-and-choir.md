## 22. Smile and Choir

### Wren

The dying at Hollowmere counted forward now.

Wren had learned to listen for it in the first weeks after—the difference between a number climbing and a number falling. Climbing sounded like effort. *Forty-one, forty-two.* A patient hauling herself up a staircase made of her own last strength. Falling had always sounded like surrender, like something being pulled down and down toward a door. But now the counts rose gently, the way breath rises before sleep, and when they reached whatever number was last for them—sixty-two, or thirty, or nine—they arrived there smiling, and they stopped, and they were done.

She still worked nights. She still checked the lights in their careful order, still touched each doorframe once as she passed, but the rituals had changed their meaning somewhere along the way, the way a scar changes from wound to weather. She did them because they were hers. Not because anything was watching.

Mostly not because anything was watching.

The mirrors on the east corridor had been replaced in the spring—new glass, thick as paving stones, ordered special by an administrator who never asked why the old ones had all cracked inward on the same night in November, shattering toward the wall instead of away from it, as if the building had swallowed. The new mirrors showed only what was in front of them. Wren checked anyway, most nights. She stood before the long glass in the staff bathroom and blinked, and her reflection blinked with her, instantly, obediently, and she felt nothing but the small flat disappointment of a woman testing a lock on a door that no longer exists.

Her memories came back in pieces, the way ice comes back to a lake—not all at once, and never in the shape it left.

She remembered her grandmother's kitchen. She remembered the smell of iron keys and the sound of counting through a wall. She remembered that she had loved someone—that was the strangest part, the part that survived everything else, lodged in her like a splinter too deep to work loose. She knew the shape of loving. She could feel the groove where it had run, worn smooth by years of passage. She simply couldn't find what had traveled that groove. Someone's laugh. Someone's way of standing in a doorway. Someone who had asked her to remember for both of them, though even that instruction came to her without a face attached, like a letter with the signature torn away.

Dr. Okafor called it grief without an object. Wren called it what it was: the price, paid in full, receipt lost.

She didn't mind. That was the thing she never told anyone, the thing that would have worried them more than the hollow itself. She didn't mind, because the hollow was clean. Before, her fear had been a crowded room—her sister's absence, her grandmother's warnings, the ledger's arithmetic of souls. Now the room was empty and swept, and in the emptiness she could do the only work that had ever mattered. She could sit with the dying and be unseen, and being unseen, she could be present. Invisible by choice. It turned out those were different things entirely, invisibility as armor and invisibility as gift. One hid you from the world. The other let you stand inside it, at the bedside, holding a hand, asking nothing back.

On the last night of September, the ward smelled of apples—someone's family had brought a bag of them from an orchard outside town—and Wren walked her rounds with the particular lightness she'd come to trust, the feeling that the building was breathing easy around her.

Room 9 was new. Admitted that afternoon, an old man named Merrick, transferred from a hospital two counties over, no family listed. The chart said end-stage heart failure, weeks perhaps, days possibly. The chart said nothing about his eyes, which were open when Wren looked in, fixed on the ceiling, and full of an expression she hadn't seen at Hollowmere in nearly a year.

He was smiling.

Not the peaceful smile of the ones who finished their counts. Something else. Something anticipatory. The smile of a man hearing music through a wall.

"Comfortable?" Wren asked, because she asked everyone, and because the question was a kind of knock—a polite announcement of herself, since he seemed otherwise inclined to look straight through her, which was her own trick, borrowed, and she found she didn't mind.

"Oh, I'm comfortable," Mr. Merrick said. His voice was dry and pleasant, like paper turning. "They told me this was the place. They told me the singing starts here."

"We don't have much singing," Wren said. "There's a choir from the village church some Sundays."

"Not that kind." He turned his head and looked at her then, fully, and she felt the old prickle walk up her spine—the feeling of being *noticed*, which she had spent a lifetime avoiding and had almost forgotten the texture of. "The other kind. Underneath." He tapped his sternum with one knuckle. "I've heard it my whole life. Faint. Like a choir practicing in a basement. And lately—" he smiled wider, and his teeth were very even, "—lately they've been tuning up."

Wren stood very still. Down the hall, a light buzzed in its fixture, steady, in its proper order. Everything was where she had counted it. Nothing lagged.

"I'll bring you some water," she said.

"You do that," said Mr. Merrick, and closed his eyes, still smiling, like a man saving a good song for later.

### Callum

Callum Reyes had stayed at Hollowmere through the whole year after, and if anyone in administration wondered why a nurse with his credentials remained at a small county hospice, they kept it to themselves. He wondered sometimes too. He had his answer ready—he liked the work, liked the quiet, liked the hills—but the true answer lived in a room at the end of the east corridor, in a woman who could not remember his name meant anything, and he had decided, with a patience that surprised him, that the answer could stay there as long as it needed to.

She had asked him to remember for both of them. Those were nearly her last words before the mirrors went, before the seal closed with a sound like a held breath finally released, before she woke up calm and empty-eyed and thanked him politely, the way you thank a stranger who has helped you find your car in a parking lot.

So he remembered. He remembered everything: the hallway mirror showing them both a second before they arrived; her hand shaking over Edith Marlowe's ledger; the kiss in the counting ward while forty voices fell backward through the numbers; the way she'd looked at him at the threshold door—not afraid, not anymore, but *choosing*, deliberately, the way you choose to step off a ledge into water you've decided to trust. He remembered her sister's face in the glass, mouthing her name, and how Wren had known, somehow, even then, that hope arriving too easily is the oldest trap there is.

He kept it all behind his teeth. Not bitterly. It was easier than he'd expected, actually—loving someone who doesn't know, when you've chosen it. It was like tending a fire in a room no one visited. The room stayed warm whether anyone came in or not.

Some nights he read near her in the nurses' station while she did her rounds, and she would pause in the doorway on her way past, and look at him with that faint crease between her brows—the crease that said *something about this man is familiar the way a song is familiar, the way a smell from childhood is familiar*—and then she'd nod and move on, and he'd turn the page, and that was enough. That was the arrangement. He honored it the way you honor any promise made at the end of the world.

On the last night of September, he was restocking the med cart when Wren appeared in the corridor outside Room 9, standing very still under the night-light, and every instinct he had—the ones from before, the ones that had brought him to Hollowmere chasing his mother's backwards count—stood up at attention.

"What is it?" he asked.

"A new admission," she said. "Merrick. End-stage cardiac." She paused. "He says he hears singing underneath things. He says it's tuning up."

Callum set down the ampoule he was holding. Slowly. The way you move when a floor might be listening.

"That's not in his chart."

"No," Wren agreed. Her eyes came up to his, and he saw it there—the thing that memory couldn't name but that her body remembered perfectly, the old cold knowledge rising like groundwater. "Callum." She used his name easily; names were safe; names had never cost her anything. "Do you ever feel like we're waiting for something?"

"Every day," he said. "But mostly I figured that was just Tuesdays."

It earned him half a smile, and he banked it like coal, and they walked the rounds together, and everything was counted, and everything was in order, and the mirrors showed them only themselves, walking, in real time, blink for blink.

### Wren

Three nights later, Wren woke in the on-call room with her heart going like a struck bell.

She lay in the dark and listened. The building was quiet. The vents breathed. Somewhere below, the old boiler ticked through its cycles, faithful as a pulse. Nothing was wrong. Nothing was wrong, and yet her body had hauled her out of sleep the way you'd drag someone from a burning house, and she sat up, and pressed her palms to her eyes, and waited for the reason to surface.

It surfaced as a feeling before it surfaced as a thought: the feeling of a word on the tip of the tongue. There was something she was supposed to be remembering. There was a date, or a season, or a number, sitting just behind her eyes, wearing a little veil, refusing to show its face.

She got up. She checked the lights—east wing first, then west, then the stairwell, in order, touching each switch twice. She counted the doors: fourteen between the on-call room and the nurses' station, same as always. The order of things held. The order of things always held now. But her hands wouldn't stop moving through the rituals, so she let them, and she ended up, without quite deciding to, standing outside Room 9.

Through the gap in the door, the night-light laid a thin amber stripe across the floor. Mr. Merrick lay exactly as she'd left him, hands folded on the blanket, eyes closed.

Smiling.

And then, without opening his eyes, without stirring at all, in a voice as clear and unhurried as a man reading a train timetable, he began to count.

"Ten," said Mr. Merrick.

Wren's whole body went cold, and colder than cold—cold the way it had been that November night, cold the way it had been in the mirror hall, cold in a way her mind had no record of but her blood remembered like a mother tongue.

"Nine."

*No,* she thought, and the thought had no logic attached, only terror, only the absolute animal certainty that this number was falling in the wrong direction, that ten was supposed to be a beginning—

"Eight."

—and then something stirred in her that memory could not name. It wasn't a memory. Memories have pictures, dates, faces. This was older than any of that. This was the groove itself, the worn channel where love had run for years, and something was moving along it now, something vast and gentle and grieving, like water returning to a dry riverbed, and she put her hand against the doorframe because the floor had gone soft.

"Seven."

Down the corridor, a door opened. Footsteps, quick and quiet, growing closer. She knew the rhythm of those footsteps. She had never known she knew the rhythm of those footsteps until this moment, and knowing it cracked something open in her chest that was not pain and was not joy but was some third thing, some thing that had waited eleven months in an empty room to be felt.

"Six."

Callum reached her. He didn't ask what was happening. He had gone pale in the night-light's amber, and his eyes were fixed on the gap in the door, and his hand found hers in the dark and closed around it, warm, certain, practiced—as if his hand had done this before, as if his hand had been waiting months for permission.

"Five," said Mr. Merrick, and smiled wider, and began, very softly, to hum.

It was not a tune Wren recognized. And it was not a tune Wren failed to recognize. It hung in the space between those two conditions, in the exact place where she lived now, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the choir underneath the world had never stopped singing. It had only changed songs. The seal held. The seal would hold. Whatever came through the crack tonight was not coming *through*—it was coming *home*, the way a voice comes home to a chorus, finding its part in the harmony.

"Four," said Merrick.

"Wren." Callum's voice was low and rough. He was looking at her now, not the door, and his thumb moved across her knuckles, once, twice, a gesture so specific, so worn with use, that her breath caught. "Whatever happens—it's okay. Whatever you're feeling right now, it's allowed."

"How do you know what I'm feeling?"

"Because I know you," he said simply. "Even the parts you don't."

"Three."

The humming swelled. From Room 7, Mrs. Okonkwo's voice joined it, thin and sweet, asleep, counting forward—*one, two, three*—weaving into the descending line like a second melody, and from the far end of the ward another voice rose, and another, the dying and the sleeping and the dreaming all finding their places, and the sound filled the corridor the way water fills a vessel, and it was terrible and it was beautiful and it was, Wren realized, the first time in her life she had ever heard a choir and wanted to be in it.

"Two," said Mr. Merrick.

Wren's hand tightened around Callum's. The groove in her was blazing now, riverbed running full, and at the bottom of it, at the very deepest point where the water pooled, something glinted. Not a memory. Not a face. Just a fact, small and indestructible as an iron key:

*I loved someone. I love them still. The record is gone. The love remains.*

"One," said Mr. Merrick, and opened his eyes.

They were bright. They were clear. They were looking directly at Wren—at her, specifically, past every defense she had ever built, seeing her the way she had never once in her life consented to be seen—and he smiled at her with an expression of such perfect, terrible recognition that her knees nearly went.

"There you are," said Mr. Merrick. "Keeper."

And beside her, Callum's hand went rigid around hers, and in the amber dark of the corridor, every new mirror on the east wing fogged at once, gently, like breath on winter glass—and then cleared.

Mr. Merrick folded his hands. Closed his eyes. Went on smiling.

"Zero," he said, contentedly, like a man arriving home. "Right on time. Now—we have so much to catch up on, you and I. All of us." His eyes stayed closed. His smile deepened. "You didn't think the song was over, did you? Sweet child. The founders only ever wrote the first verse."

Outside, far off across the hills, the chapel bell began to ring—though Wren had counted that bell a thousand times, and knew for a fact that no one was in the tower, and knew for a fact that it was not midnight, and knew, in the place deeper than memory where her love still lived, that it was ringing seven times.

One for each station.

The count had begun again.
