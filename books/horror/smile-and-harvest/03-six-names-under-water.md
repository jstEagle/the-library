## 3. Six Names Under Water

Wren found the shoe on a Tuesday, in the hour her father called the gray time—the dead hour between the last call and the generator's dawn cough, when the reservoir went the color of an old television screen and nothing moved but the mist.

She hadn't meant to be down at the waterline. She'd meant to sleep. But she'd counted the doors again—eleven of them, same as always, though the station had only ever had six—and after counting she couldn't stay inside. The doors didn't rattle or breathe or open. They just *were*, eleven of them, patient as teeth, and some nights being near them felt like standing too close to a wasp nest you weren't sure was empty.

So she'd walked down through the birches with her father's torch and her boots unlaced, to the mud flats where the water had pulled back another few feet this autumn, exposing more of the drowned town's bones. Everyone in Hollowmere did this now, in a casual way—reservoir tourism, Councilman Pike called it, putting it on leaflets. People came to photograph the church steeple that broke the surface like a shark's fin, to buy postcards of OLD HOLLOWMERE, LOST BUT NOT FORGOTTEN.

Lost but not forgotten. Wren thought about that slogan as she picked along the flats. It was such a comfortable lie. The town wasn't forgotten; it was *merchandise*.

The mud took her boot to the ankle with a sound like a mouth closing. She yanked it free, stumbled, put out a hand—and there it was, half-buried where the water had been for fifty years: a child's shoe, black leather, small enough to cup in both hands like something newborn.

It should have rotted. It should have been nothing but a stain and a buckle. But the leather was whole, supple even, and when she turned it over, wiped the silt from the sole with her thumb, she saw the name stamped into the inner heel in careful, old-fashioned lettering:

**ELLERY MARSH**

Wren knew every name in Hollowmere. She'd grown up reading the war memorial on rainy afternoons, tracing the brass letters the way other kids traced gravestones. There were Brightwaters and Pikes and Fenwicks and Corrigans, and there had been a Marsh family once—old Mrs. Marsh who ran the tea room until she died in—

No. That was the thing. Old Mrs. Marsh had been the *last* Marsh. Everyone said so. The family had died out, moved away, thinned into nothing. And Ellery was not a name anyone used anymore. It sounded like a boy's name from a book, or a girl's name from before books.

The shoe fit in her coat pocket exactly, which felt like a fact she should not have noticed.

* * *

Her father was asleep when she got back, slumped in the broadcast chair with his headphones still on, one hand resting on the mixing desk like a man holding a sleeping animal. The ON AIR light glowed its soft red above him. He never fully turned it off anymore. He said the desk liked to be warm.

"Dad." She touched his shoulder. "Dad, wake up."

He came up out of sleep fast, the way he always did since the calls started—no grogginess, just instant attention, his eyes going first to the phone bank. All dark. All quiet. He exhaled and looked at her properly. "What is it? What's wrong?"

She set the shoe on the desk between them.

He stared at it for a long moment. Long enough that she watched three separate expressions cross his face and get put away somewhere behind his eyes, locked in a cabinet she wasn't allowed to open. His grandfather's cabinet, maybe. She didn't know yet why she thought of it that way.

"Where," he said quietly, "did you find that?"

"Flats. North side, past the second fence line." She pointed at the heel. "There's a name."

"I see it." He didn't touch it. That was the strangest part—her father, who handled everything, who fixed generators and spliced cables and cradled the skulls of dead robins he found by the door because he couldn't stand to leave them—he would not pick up the shoe. His hands stayed flat on the desk on either side of it, framing it, like it was evidence and he was already guilty of something.

"Ellery Marsh," Wren said. "Who's Ellery Marsh?"

"A child." He said it flatly, and then seemed to hear how much he'd said, and stopped.

"You know the name."

"I know the name's not on anything." He stood, pushed past her, went to the shelf where he kept the parish records he'd started collecting after the bell rang—that single toll at 3 a.m., weeks ago now, a bronze voice from a bell that had been melted down in 1911, and the phone lines lighting up all at once with voices decades dead saying *return what was taken* until they stopped saying it and just waited. He pulled the big ledger bound in cracked green cloth and set it down harder than he needed to. "Look for yourself. Baptisms, burials, relocations. Everything the council kept when they built the dam."

She flipped pages. 1972. 1973. Then 1974, and the paper changed—newer stock, whiter, and a section headed RELOCATION OF AFFECTED HOUSEHOLDS, and beneath it a list of six family names, typed:

**MARSH. FENN. OAKDEN. PRYCE. HOLLIS. GREY.**

Six families. Relocated. Compensation paid. Signatures below, including one that made her father turn his face toward the window: **A. BRIGHTWATER**.

"Relocated where?" Wren asked.

"That's what I've been trying to find out for two months."

"And?"

"And nowhere." He sat down heavily. "I've written to the county archive twice. No forwarding addresses. No school transfers for any Marsh children. No new GP registrations. Six families, twenty-some people, and they walked out of Hollowmere in March of 1974 and left no footprints anywhere in England." He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "Your gran used to say the low town people were strange. Kept to themselves. Wouldn't come up to the new village after the water rose. Wouldn't come to church." He rubbed his eyes. "I used to think that was just how she talked about people poorer than us."

Wren looked at the list. Six names. She said them under her breath like a spell read backward, and the studio lights flickered—not the generator stuttering, because the generator hummed steady underneath, but the lights themselves, dipping once, politely, like a nod.

Neither of them mentioned it. They were getting good at not mentioning things.

* * *

That night's show began the way it always did now. Midnight. The red light. Her father's voice dropping into its radio register, warm and low, made for the dark: *"You're listening to Smile and Harvest, broadcasting from the top of Hollowmere hill, where the signal carries further than it has any right to. Lines are open. If someone you lost hasn't finished talking, let them finish."*

Wren ran the board. It was her job officially since October, unofficially since forever. She screened the calls, though screening had become mostly ceremonial—the dead didn't need dial tones. They arrived the way weather arrives.

The first caller was a man who'd died Thursday, a tractor accident up at Corrigan's farm, wanting his wife to check the third fence post on the east field because he'd hollowed it out and there was £400 inside and she'd need it more than the tax man would. Wren wrote it down. In the morning she'd leave the note in the farm gate like always, no explanation, and Mrs. Corrigan would find it and go pale and go get the money and never ask.

The second caller was a woman who'd died in her bath, wanting her sister to know the cat needed the vet, not the neighbor's tablets, the *vet*.

And then, at 3:04 a.m.—four minutes past the hour the bell had rung, four minutes past the hour the phones had all lit at once that first night—the third call came in, and Wren knew before she answered it, from the weight of the silence on the line, that this caller was not newly dead.

Newly dead sounded like people. This sounded like a room.

"Go ahead, caller," she heard herself say.

"Good evening, dear." An old woman's voice, papery, unhurried, with the flattened vowels of the low town—the accent nobody under seventy in Hollowmere had anymore. "Is this the Voss girl? The little one? You have your great-aunt's way of answering a telephone. Brisk. Kind underneath it."

"I—" Wren's hand hovered over the fader. "Who am I speaking to?"

"Hollis. Edith Hollis." A pause, and then, gently, almost apologetically: "Though I expect you'll want the date of death as well. You lot do love your dates. The eleventh of March, 1974. Drowned. All of us, dear. All six households. We didn't relocate. There was nowhere to relocate *to*—the compensation was fiction, the papers were signed by men who never met us, and the water came up faster than they told us it would. They opened the sluice gates early. Did you know that? They moved the date up a fortnight and only told the families who'd already signed. We were the ones who hadn't."

Wren's mouth was very dry. On the far side of the glass, her father had gone absolutely still, his hand frozen over the master fader, watching her through the window with an expression she'd never seen on him before. Not fear. Something worse. Recognition.

"You're saying you're dead," Wren managed. "You're saying you've been dead fifty years."

"Fifty-one this March, if anyone's counting." The old woman chuckled, a soft rustling sound. "Nobody's counted, dear. That's rather the whole trouble. Do you know what we actually lost? Everyone assumes it's the houses, the land, the lives—and yes, those. But what they took that can't be replaced is smaller and crueler. Our names off the memorial. Our graves—there are no graves, we're down there under two hundred feet of cold water with our christening spoons and our wedding photographs. My own funeral, dear. Would you like to hear about my funeral?"

Wren's throat worked. "...Yes."

"There wasn't one." Simply said. Terribly said. "No service, no stone, no mourner. My daughter was eleven. She was up at the new village with her aunt that week—thank God, thank God—and she was told her mother had moved to Coventry. For years that child wrote letters to Coventry. They came back unopened, and the aunt burned them so the girl would stop crying, and when the girl grew up she hated her mother for abandoning her, and she died hating her, and *that*, Miss Voss, is what your family bought with their signature. Not land. A lie that eats forward through generations like rot through floorboards."

The line hissed softly. Somewhere in the station, faint and impossible, eleven doors shifted on their hinges—not opening, just settling, like a house making itself comfortable.

"Why are you telling me this?" Wren whispered. "Why now? Why us?"

"Because the bell's ringing again, dear. One ring, then two, then however many it takes. And because you found the shoe." A pause, and when Edith Hollis spoke again her voice had gentled into something almost tender. "You have it in your pocket. Leather holds a name longer than stone does, did you know that? Ellery's mum stamped every shoe herself. She was proud of that little name. Say it for me, dear. Say it out loud, over the air. Names travel on the wireless. That's the one road out of the water that's still open."

Wren looked through the glass at her father. He shook his head—slow, small, pleading—but his hand had drifted to the master fader without him seeming to notice, and the red ON AIR light burned steady, and the whole sleeping valley below them held its breath under two hundred feet of water.

"Ellery Marsh," Wren said.

The lights dipped. Every phone on the board lit at once—all eight lines, then the two dead extensions that weren't connected to anything, glowing like eyes in the dark—and through the speakers, beneath the hiss, came a sound none of them had heard on the show before.

Singing. A woman's voice, far away and underwater, singing a lullaby in the flattened vowels of the low town.

"Thank you, dear," said Edith Hollis, and hung up.

Wren sat with her hand pressed flat against the shoe in her pocket, feeling its small weight, and understood with total clarity that the grief she'd been carrying all these months—for the callers, for the lonely dead—had just curdled into something else. Something with a body count. Something with six names and a signature her own blood had written.

Her father leaned to the talkback mic. His face was gray.

"Wren," he said. "We need to talk about what's under this floor."
