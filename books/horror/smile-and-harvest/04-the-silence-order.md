## 4. The Silence Order

The parish office smelled of dust and old glue, the way all buildings smell when they have outlived their purpose but not their custodians. Dominic Voss stood in the archive room with a flashlight cupped in his palm, because the overhead fluorescents had died sometime during the last decade and nobody had mourned them. The records of St. Aldhelm's lay in gray boxes along one wall, ordered by year, each box labeled in a hand that grew shakier as the decades advanced.

He had told himself he was here for Wren. That was the story he'd been telling since the bell rang—*I'm doing this for her, I'm protecting her, I'm finding out what's haunting my daughter's sleep so I can stop it.* It was a good story. It had the shape of love. But standing there among the boxes, flashlight trembling just slightly against his ribs, he understood that he wasn't looking for what happened to Hollowmere.

He was looking for his family in it.

The 1974 box was thinner than the others. He noticed that before he opened it—a wrongness in its weight, the way you notice a coffin is too small for the name on the lid. Inside: minutes of parish council meetings through the spring, agendas, a water-rate schedule, and then nothing. The record simply stopped in June, mid-year, mid-sentence almost, and resumed in October with a note about carol concert planning, as if the summer between had been surgically removed.

Except whoever did the surgery had been hurried.

Dominic lifted the June minutes to the light and tilted the page. Beneath the final entry—the last ordinary sentence about hedgerow maintenance—he could see the ghost of pressed-in writing from a page that had once sat beneath it. Carbon transfer, faint as breath on glass. He read it sideways, letter by letter, his heart going slow and strange in his chest:

*...the undersigned parties agree that the matter of the low town relocation shall be considered closed, no party hereto or their heirs shall disclose...*

*Their heirs.*

He set the page down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might be listening.

* * *

He found the rest at the county records office, forty minutes' drive through rain that came down like static. The clerk, a young man with earbuds in, waved him toward the microfiche without looking up, and Dominic spent two hours scrolling through documents that had never been digitized because nobody wanted them remembered.

The settlement agreement was filed under *Hollowmere Parish Council v. Inhabitants of Low Town (Discontinued)*. Discontinued. As if the six families had chosen to stop existing.

He read it twice. The first time as a stranger, horrified. The second time as a grandson, and that was the reading that broke him.

His grandfather, Edmund Voss, had signed it. Not as a witness. As a *party*. His signature sat third from the top, that confident looping hand Dominic knew from childhood birthday cards, from the flyleaf of the Bible that still lived in the studio's back room. Below the signature, in smaller type, the clause:

*The parties hereto bind themselves, their heirs, executors, and assigns, to perpetual silence regarding the circumstances of the removal of the Low Town residents, it being agreed by all signatories that said residents accepted relocation terms freely and departed the district of their own volition.*

Freely. Of their own volition.

Dominic sat in the humming dark of the records room and thought about the word *removal*. You removed a stain. You removed a tumor. Nobody used that word for people who got on a bus with their suitcases and waved goodbye.

There were attachments. Relocation papers—six sets, six families, each certifying that the household in question had accepted compensation and resettled in towns far away: Ashford, Swindon, a place near Coventry. Each document bore a signature. Dominic pulled up the electoral rolls for those towns in the years following, cross-referenced the names, and found nothing. No Marshes in Ashford. No Callows in Swindon. No Hollises anywhere near Coventry.

The signatures were beautiful. Practiced. All six written in the same confident slant, the same distinctive way of crossing the t's—one hand, signing six lives away.

One hand that looked, when he held the photocopy beside his grandfather's birthday card inscription, exactly like Edmund Voss's.

* * *

He drove home through the rain with the windows fogged and the wipers keeping time like a metronome counting down to something. Wren was awake when he got to the station, of course she was awake, she was always awake now, sitting cross-legged on the mixing desk with the generator's rhythm thudding through the floorboards into her spine.

"Did you find it?" she asked. Not *did you find anything*. She'd known there was an *it*. Children always knew; that was the obscenity of children, they perceived the shape of a lie long before they learned the words for it.

"I found some things."

"About us?"

He didn't pretend not to understand. "Maybe. Why?"

Wren looked down at her hands. "Because last night, after the phone calls stopped, I counted the doors like always. And there was a new one." Her voice stayed level, which frightened him more than tears would have. "A door in the studio floor. Just—there. Under the carpet where the dead spot is, where the signal always drops. And it wasn't nailed shut, Dad. It was *latched*. Like someone meant to come back for it."

"The floor doesn't have doors, love."

"It does now," she said. "It's been waiting for us to be ready."

* * *

He waited until she finally slept—three hours past midnight, curled in the chair beneath the ON AIR light, which he'd stopped turning off because the red glow seemed to keep the worst of the quiet at bay—and then he went to the dead spot in the floor.

Every studio has one. A patch where the signal dies, where cables hum wrong, where engineers shrug and say *old building, old wiring*. At Smile and Harvest it was a rectangle maybe a meter square behind the transmitter rack, and for eleven years Dominic had walked over it every night without a second thought, the way you walk over a grave when you don't know it's a grave.

He moved the rack. He rolled back the carpet. The floorboards looked ordinary until he knelt and put his palm flat against them, and then he felt it—not sound exactly, but pressure, a deep subsonic patience rising through the wood like cold off a lake.

The boards came up easier than they should have. Nails that slid free without screaming, wood that lifted like a lid rather than resisted like a floor. Beneath: a cavity lined with tar paper, dry as a tomb despite eleven years of English weather seeping through the walls, and sitting in the center of it, a lead box the size of a bread bin.

Lead. He registered the choice of material even as his hands closed on the cold metal. Lead stops radiation. Lead stops signals. Whatever was inside had been put there by people who wanted it *silent*—unbroadcast, undetectable, buried under the one building in Hollowmere built to fill the air with voices. He almost laughed. Almost. The cruelty of it was so precise it circled back around into elegance: they hadn't hidden the truth in the dark. They'd hidden it directly beneath the microphone.

The latch was stiff but honest. It opened.

On top lay an envelope, yellowed, sealed, addressed in that same looping hand: *For whoever inherits.* Dominic's own name was not on it, but the word *inherits* landed on him like a hand on the shoulder.

Inside the envelope, a single sheet. He read it kneeling on the cold floorboards with the red ON AIR light washing over him, and by the end he was reading it aloud, though there was no one awake to hear, because some things cannot be read silently:

*I, Edmund Voss, being of sound mind and unsound conscience, do set down what was done in the summer of 1974.*

*The dam was to flood the low town. Six families refused the offered terms, and rightly, for the terms were theft dressed as generosity. There was a meeting at the Bell Inn on the ninth of June. I was there. I signed the order that night—I signed it believing we were buying peace, and by morning I understood we had bought silence instead. What was done to the Marshes, the Callows, the Hollises, the Prynnes, the Oakes, and the Ferriers was not relocation. It was drowning with paperwork. The waters rose faster than any natural filling should rise. I heard the church bell of the low town ring under the water, three times, and then stop, and I have heard it every night since in the space where my hearing should be quiet.*

*The deeds to their lands are in this box. I could not destroy them. Destruction would have made me certain, and certainty is the one thing I could not afford. I built this station over them instead, thinking perhaps that if I spent my life putting voices into the air above their silenced names, the arithmetic might balance.*

*It does not balance. Nothing balances. To whoever finds this: they will come asking. Return what was taken. Do not sign anything. Do not let your children sign anything.*

*God forgive us. We smiled, and we harvested.*

The pen had pressed hard at the end, tearing slightly through the paper. Below the signature, a date: March 1975. Eleven months after the flooding. Edmund Voss had lived another twenty-two years, and had never sent this, never spoken it, had carried it under his feet every day of his life like a stone swallowed whole.

Dominic knelt there a long time. The generator thudded. The red light breathed.

Beneath the envelope, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the deeds. Six of them. He unfolded the topmost and read the name at its head—*Ellery Marsh, minor, by her guardian*—and the description of land that now lay at the bottom of the reservoir, meadow and orchard and a cottage with a blue door, drowned and erased and legally, eternally, owned by a nine-year-old girl who had never come up for air.

As his fingers closed on the papers, every light in the studio dimmed at once—not out, but down, the way a room dims when something large passes between it and the sun. From somewhere beneath the floorboards, beneath the tar paper, beneath the lead itself, came a sound he recognized from his grandfather's confession, a sound Edmund had carried in his ears for fifty years.

The bell. Ringing under the water.

Once. Patient. Counting.

And in the dead air of the studio, the phone began to ring.
