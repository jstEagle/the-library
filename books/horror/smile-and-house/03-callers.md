## 3. Callers

The dead man called back on Thursday.

I want to be clear about the sequence of events here, because later—when people ask, and people do ask—I want the record to show that I did not invite this. He called *me*. I was simply the only person in the county still awake at two in the morning with a transmitter and nothing better to say.

"Back on the line we've got Gerald from Route 9," I said, because that's what you do. You keep the show moving. You keep your voice level. You do not mention that the last time this man called, he described his own funeral with the fond specificity of a man reviewing a restaurant.

"Cyrus." His voice was better this week. Clearer. Like a signal finding its tower. "You sound tired."

"It's two a.m., Gerald. Tired is the brand."

"You should sleep more."

"You should be alive more," I said, and immediately regretted it, because it came out sharp, and because somewhere in my chest there was a small, humiliating relief at hearing a voice—*any* voice—that wasn't mine bouncing off the studio walls.

He laughed. It was a good laugh, warm, the kind of laugh that belongs to a man who coached Little League and fixed his neighbors' snowblowers and died last Tuesday of a heart attack in his driveway while carrying groceries. I know all of this because he told me. In detail. Including what the casket lining looked like, which he did not care for.

"Keep talking, Cyrus," he said, when the song ended and I came back on mic.

"I'm talking, Gerald."

"No. I mean—" and here his voice did something strange, dropped down under the music like a stone under water, "—keep talking. Even when we're not on. Even when the show's over. It matters."

"Is this a bit? Are you a bit?"

"I was a mailman for thirty-one years," he said, wounded. "I don't do bits."

I put him on hold and took a call from a woman named Ruthie who wanted to request "Blue Bayou," except Ruthie, according to our logs, had requested "Blue Bayou" every night for the past three weeks, and Ruthie, according to the county obituary page I now checked with the grim diligence of a man reading stock prices, had passed away eleven days ago.

I played "Blue Bayou."

I'm not proud of a lot of things, but I am proud of that.

**CALL TRANSCRIPT — WQRT-AM 1340, 2:47 A.M., THURSDAY**

**CALLER:** Is this the girl? The Almeida girl?

**HOST:** This is the host. Who's asking?

**CALLER:** You know the house on Kestrel Lane. The one with the porch light.

**HOST:** I know a house on Kestrel Lane.

**CALLER:** Keep the lights on. All of them. And if something knocks—[unintelligible—approx. 9 seconds]

**HOST:** You're breaking up. Say that again?

**CALLER:** Don't answer the knocks. It counts how long you wait. [line disconnects]

* * *

Here is the thing about working the graveyard shift at a station with a signal that couldn't reach the next county on a good day: you develop a relationship with your callers that is less "radio host and audience" and more "night watchman and the things the night watchman watches." Marcus calls them the Insomniac Caucus. Marcus has a joke for everything, which is one of the three reasons I keep him employed. The other two are that he fixes the transmitter with electrical tape and prayer, and that he once bought me a desk fan for my birthday because I'd mentioned being cold in June, and I think about that fan more than I think about most people.

Marcus did not believe me about Gerald.

I want to be fair to Marcus. If one of my hosts came to me and said, hey, the dead guy from Tuesday called back and asked me to keep talking, I would have said, "Did you check if he was pranking you, or are we just skipping to the intervention?" Marcus said approximately that, except with more sighing.

"Cyrus." He set down his coffee—the gas station kind, the kind that tastes like a tire fire's apology—and looked at me with the expression of a man who has already composed the email to HR in his head. "The obituary thing is a coincidence. Old people listen to AM radio. Old people die. The overlap is enormous."

"He knew what the casket lining looked like."

"Velvet. They're all velvet. Congratulations, you've independently discovered the funeral industry."

"He knew the *color*."

"Taupe," Marcus said. "It's always taupe. My grandmother's was taupe. This is not evidence of the afterlife, this is evidence of a limited product line."

I wanted to argue. The problem was that Marcus was right in the way that reasonable people are right, which is to say completely, uselessly, and at the exact moment when being wrong would have been a mercy. Because the calls kept coming. And the calls kept being wrong in ways coincidences can't cover.

There was the man who asked me to tell his wife the spare key was in the rain gutter, third one from the downspout, and I checked—don't ask me why, don't ask me what I told myself, I drove to the address at four a.m. like a lunatic and it was there, third gutter from the downspout, a key furred with moss. There was the woman who sang along with every song I played in a thin, cracked voice and thanked me at the end of each one like I'd performed it just for her, and whose name, when I finally asked, matched a headstone in the cemetery two miles from the tower. There was the little boy—I'm not going to do the little boy, actually. Not yet. Maybe not ever. Some things you keep in the vault.

But they all said the same thing. Every one of them, in different words, with different voices, some scared and some calm and the little boy—

The little boy just wanted to know if his mom was still leaving the hall light on.

They all said: *keep talking.*

And four of them, four separate callers, on four separate nights, said: *don't answer the knocks.*

**CALL TRANSCRIPT — WQRT-AM 1340, 3:12 A.M., SATURDAY**

**CALLER:** He's getting closer to your house.

**HOST:** Who is? Gerald, is this you?

**CALLER:** Not Gerald. Gerald's resting. We rest, between. You sound tired, Cyrus.

**HOST:** How do you know my name? I didn't say my name.

**CALLER:** You say it every night. Sign-on. 'This is Cyrus Almeida, keeping you company till the sun does.' We listen. We all listen. It's the only channel that reaches where we are.

**HOST:** Where are you?

**CALLER:** [long pause—approx. 22 seconds] Closer to your house than I was.

**HOST:** Okay, we're going to go to the weather.

**CALLER:** Don't answer the knocks. Please. It's not that we don't want you to open the door. It's that we can't warn you twice.

* * *

The house on Kestrel Lane has a porch light I've never turned off. This predates everything. My grandmother kept it on for sixty years—Marcus's fan, her porch light; we are a family that expresses love through small electrical gestures—and when I moved in after the funeral, leaving it on felt less like superstition and more like housekeeping. Like watering a plant she'd left behind.

But I'll admit that after the calls started, I added lights. The hallway. The kitchen. The one over the stove that buzzes like it's gossiping. I told myself it was because the wiring was old and dark corners were a trip hazard, and I believed this with the full, desperate sincerity of a woman who had recently driven to a stranger's rain gutter at four in the morning to verify the existence of a spare key.

The first knock came on Sunday.

Three knocks. Evenly spaced. Polite, almost. The kind of knock you'd give a neighbor you liked but didn't know well. It was 3:33 in the morning, which I noticed because I was staring at the clock, because I'd been staring at the clock for an hour, because sleep and I were in a bad place.

I did not answer it.

I want that on the record too. I sat on the floor of my kitchen with my back against the cabinets and my grandmother's cast-iron skillet in my lap like a medieval peasant, and I did not answer the door, and the knocking did not come again, and at some point the light through the window went from black to the gray that means the night has decided to release you on parole.

In the morning I called Marcus.

"Marcus. Hypothetically. If someone knocked on your door at three-thirty in the morning—"

"I don't have a door, I have an apartment, and if someone knocked at three-thirty I'd assume it was a drug thing and lie on the floor."

"Four callers told me not to answer knocks."

"Four callers told you—" I heard him put his coffee down. The gas station cup has a specific percussion. "Cyrus. You know what I do when a caller says something weird? I say 'thanks for calling WQRT' and I hit the button. That's the whole job. That's the sacred trust between producer and host. You hit the button."

"They're dead, Marcus. All of them. I've checked. I've checked eleven of them."

A silence on the line. Then, gently, in the voice you'd use on a spooked horse: "Eleven."

"Eleven callers whose names match obituaries."

"Or," Marcus said, "eleven callers whose names are common enough that a woman running on four hours of sleep is pattern-matching her way into a ghost story. Cyrus, you inherited a house, you lost your grandmother, you work nights alone in a building that smells like burnt dust. I'm not saying you're crazy. I'm saying you're *lonely*, and lonely is a chemical, and chemicals lie."

"That's very poetic for a man who communicates primarily through sighing."

"I contain multitudes. Take a night off. Let the automation run. Sleep with the lights off like a normal person."

"I sleep with the lights *on*."

"See, that's what I mean."

He hung up before I could argue, which is his signature move, and I sat in my car in the station lot watching the tower light blink its slow red heartbeat against the sky, and I thought: he's wrong, he's wrong, he's wrong.

And then I thought, in a smaller voice, from a lower floor of myself: *what if he's right, and the dead are the only ones who call, and the living have all hit the button.*

**CALL TRANSCRIPT — WQRT-AM 1340, 2:19 A.M., MONDAY**

**CALLER:** Cyrus. It's Gerald.

**HOST:** Hey, Gerald. You okay? You sound far away.

**CALLER:** We're not supposed to call this much. It costs us. But I wanted to say—your grandmother used to do what you're doing.

**HOST:** My grandmother hosted the night shift in the seventies. Before your time.

**CALLER:** I know. I called her too.

**HOST:** [pause—approx. 14 seconds] What?

**CALLER:** Same show. Same chair. She kept talking, and we kept listening, and it kept everything quiet. You're doing it right, Cyrus. Don't stop. And when the knocking starts—

**HOST:** It already started.

**CALLER:** [long silence—approx. 40 seconds] How many doors does your house have?

**HOST:** Eleven. Why?

**CALLER:** Count again. Count them tonight. And Cyrus—count them out loud. It likes to be counted. It settles when it's counted. Like a child.

**HOST:** Gerald, what did my grandmother—

**CALLER:** Count the doors. [line disconnects]

* * *

I called Marcus back at noon and told him everything. The knocks. The transcript. The part about my grandmother, which I delivered with the careful flatness of someone reading someone else's mail.

Marcus was quiet for a long time. When he finally spoke, it wasn't a sigh. It was worse. It was the voice he uses for pledge drives, the one that means he's managing me.

"Cyrus. I'm coming by the station tonight."

"You don't have to—"

"I know I don't have to. That's what makes it a favor." A pause. "And Cyrus? I'm going to sit in on the show. And when a caller says something about doors, or knocks, or your grandmother, I'm going to hit the button, and then we're going to go get pancakes, and you're going to tell me about literally anything else. Deal?"

"Deal," I said, because it was easier than saying *please believe me*, which was the sentence sitting in my throat like a bone.

He hung up. I sat in the studio with the ON AIR sign glowing its red confession over my head and I looked at the phone—the old board phone, the one with the buttons that light up—and I noticed something I should have noticed days ago, something so obvious it had been hiding in plain sight like a spider in a lampshade.

The board was lit. Four lines. All of them held.

I hadn't screened any calls tonight. I hadn't taken any callers at all. I'd been playing music into the dark for two hours, alone, talking to no one.

Four lines, glowing steady, patient, like eyes in the dark of a room you thought was empty.

And underneath the hum of the transmitter, underneath the song fading out, so faint I could almost believe I imagined it—almost, if not for the fact that I have never once in my life imagined anything that polite—

Someone was knocking. Not on the studio door. Not on any door I could see.

On the inside of the glass of the soundproof window, three soft taps, evenly spaced, and no one there.
