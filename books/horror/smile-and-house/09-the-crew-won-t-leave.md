## 9. The Crew Won't Leave

I've been running excavation crews for nineteen years, and in that time I have learned exactly three things about fog.

One: it comes off the water or up out of low ground when the temperature drops, and it behaves like weather, which is to say it doesn't care about you. Two: it burns off by mid-morning, and if it doesn't, you send the men home and bill the delay honestly. Three: it does not, under any circumstances I have ever witnessed, sit still.

The fog on the Almeida lot sat still.

It came in around six-forty in the morning, while we were still arguing about the door — because yes, we were still arguing about the door, forty feet down in the bottom of the pit, iron-banded oak with its hinges on the wrong side and bolts thrown from within like somebody had locked themselves in a coffin on purpose. OSHA had been called twice already that week, once by me and once by Reyes, and both times the inspector had come out, looked down into the hole, looked at his clipboard, and left without climbing down the ladder. That should have been my first clue. Inspectors climb down holes. It's their whole personality.

The fog didn't roll. That's the thing I keep coming back to, the thing I've told maybe four people and will probably never tell again. Fog rolls. It drifts, it curls, it moves the way smoke moves, lazy and directionless. This came up out of the ground around the edges of the site — not drifting in from the creek, but *up*, seeping through the spoil piles and the graded earth like the lot itself was exhaling — and then it stopped. Just stopped. A wall of gray standing maybe eight feet high all the way around our perimeter fence, thick as wet wool, and inside it we had clear air and flat gray light, like we were working at the bottom of a glass of milk.

"Barometric," said Vasquez, who reads weather apps the way other men read scripture. "Pressure inversion. Happens."

"Inversions move," I said.

"So do we. Take lunch."

But nobody took lunch. That was the second clue, and I missed it too, because I was busy being a foreman instead of being smart. The crew stood around the edge of the pit looking down at the door, and they weren't talking, and men who aren't talking on a job site are either planning to quit or planning to do something stupid, and I couldn't tell which yet.

* * *

The door itself deserves a minute, because I've had two weeks to think about it and I still don't have a better explanation than "no."

We hit wood at thirty-eight feet. Oak, black as tar, preserved by whatever the soil down there is doing, set in a frame of stone that isn't any stone I know and I grew up quarry country. The bands are iron, hand-forged, no rust. Forty feet down in wet ground for — the county archaeologist guessed conservatively, then stopped guessing — and the iron looks like it was hung last Tuesday.

And the hinges are on the inside.

I want you to sit with that the way I had to sit with it, kneeling on a mud shelf with my headlamp on it, running my thumb along the barrel of a hinge that opens inward into solid earth. Doors open outward so people can get out. That's the whole point of a door. Somebody built this one backwards. Somebody built a door that could only be opened from within, and then — here's the part that made Reyes go sit in his truck for an hour — threw the bolts. From the inside. There are four of them, each one the size of my forearm, and the throw-marks in the wood are clean. No hesitation. Whoever bolted that door knew exactly what they were doing and exactly why.

Then there are the scratches. At first we figured them for claw marks, animal, something trapped. But I got down close with a light, and they're not random. They're lines. Rows of them, tally marks, hundreds upon hundreds, cut into the inner face of the door in neat columns like somebody counting. And under the tallies, in a script none of us read, words cut deep enough to bleed sap even now, black beads of it along the grooves like the tree is still trying to say something.

Reyes photographed everything. His phone died before he got back up the ladder. So did his backup battery, which was in his truck, which was fifty feet away. He doesn't talk about it.

The county told us to shore the pit, leave the door undisturbed, and await further instruction. Further instruction has been "await further instruction" for nine days. Meanwhile the Almeida woman keeps calling my office asking whether we've found anything else down there, papers, a ledger, personal effects, and she asks in this voice like she already knows the answer and is checking whether we know it too. I told her once: lady, we found a door sealed from the inside, what else could there possibly be. She was quiet for a long moment and then she said, "That's what I was afraid of," and hung up, and I sat there holding the phone thinking, well, that's a strange way to react to your own family's property.

Anyway. Fog.

* * *

By seven-thirty the light had gone wrong. Not dark — the sun was technically up somewhere above that gray ceiling — but flat, sourceless, shadowless. Your eyes don't like that. Men work worse in shadowless light; there's nothing for the brain to anchor depth against, and everybody misjudges distances, stumbling over ruts they'd normally step over. I sent two men up to surface the pump hoses and told the rest to knock off early, we'd cut losses, county could bill me.

That's when Gutierrez said, "Boss. You hear that?"

I didn't hear anything, which was the problem. A job site has a sound — pumps, generators, the idle of trucks, men talking trash. We'd shut most of it down, but the generator was still running and the generator makes noise, and suddenly it didn't. Not muffled. *Gone.* Like the fog had eaten the sound on its way past, or like we were working inside a recording of a job site with the volume pulled down to zero.

And in that silence, thin as wire, a voice.

I'm going to describe this carefully because later, when Marcus — the radio guy, we'll get to him — asked me what it sounded like, I said "a voice," and he said, "Whose?" and I said, "Everybody's." Because that's the truth and it makes no sense. It wasn't one voice. It was every voice I knew layered into one, my mother's cadence under my old sergeant's rasp under my daughter saying *Dad* the way she did when she was four. It wasn't loud. It didn't need to be. It came from everywhere the fog was, which was everywhere, and it said one word.

"Almeida."

Just that. Once. The way you'd say a name you'd finally tracked down after a long search.

Vasquez crossed himself. Gutierrez said, "That's the lady's name. The property lady." And I said, "Yeah," and started walking the line telling everyone to pack up, tools down, we're done for the day, done for the week, maybe done forever, I don't care what the county says, I'll eat the penalty clause myself.

Two men didn't stop working.

Ortega and Brandt were down in the pit. They'd been down there since six, cleaning the mud shelf around the door frame per the county's precious instructions, and when the voice came they just — kept going. I hollered down. Ortega looked up at me. I want to be precise: he *looked* at me, turned his face up toward the rim, and his headlamp caught him full in the face, and his expression was fine. Calm. Pleasant, even. Like a man who'd been asked a question and had decided on his answer. Then he turned back to the door and kept scraping mud away from the frame with his trowel, gentle, almost tender, the way you'd clear debris off a grave marker.

Brandt was doing the same on the other side.

"Ortega!" I went down the ladder. I don't know why I went down instead of sending someone; pride, probably, the dumb foreman instinct that says you handle your own problems. The pit was cold. Not morning-cold. Cold like the far side of a lake. My breath didn't fog down there, which I noticed and filed away and wish to God I hadn't, because breath always fogs, that's physics, and where I come from when physics takes a day off you leave.

"Pack it up," I told Ortega, putting a hand on his shoulder.

He shrugged me off without malice. "Almost done," he said, and his voice was his voice but the rhythm was wrong, evenly spaced words like a metronome, no music in it. "She wants it clean."

"She? Who wants it clean?"

He didn't answer. He brushed mud from the base of the door frame, and underneath the mud the stone was carved — more of that script, and around the frame, worn nearly smooth, a border of small shapes I didn't look at long enough to identify because some part of me understood that identifying them would cost me something.

"Brandt," I said. "Both of you, topside, now. That's an order."

Brandt stood up. He smiled at me. Nineteen years, and I'd never seen Brandt smile, the man complained professionally, smiling was against his religion, and now here he was grinning down at a door buried since before his grandfather's grandfather with pure contentment on his face, and he said, "It's almost time," and stepped toward the door.

Not toward the ladder. Toward the door.

"Hey." I grabbed his arm. His sleeve was warm — no. His sleeve was warm and his arm underneath was not, and the difference between those two temperatures is the single worst physical sensation of my life, like shaking hands with a mannequin someone just brought indoors. He turned and looked at me and his eyes were fine, normal brown eyes, and behind them absolutely nothing was home.

"The door's not open," I said, and my voice cracked like a teenager's. "There's forty feet of dirt behind it. Whatever you think is happening—"

"It's open on her side," Brandt said pleasantly.

Then the fog moved.

After hours of sitting still, it moved — not rolled, not drifted, but *leaned*, the whole gray wall tilting inward over the pit like a wave deciding, and the voice came again, all the voices, and this time it wasn't one word. It was a hum. Low, patient, almost sweet, the kind of sound a mother makes over a crib, and Ortega straightened up from the door frame and walked into it.

I don't mean he climbed. I mean he walked toward the door and the space around the door — I can't explain this any better than that — the space around the door opened. The air in front of the oak went soft, went deep, went the particular black that has distance in it, and Ortega walked four steps across a mud shelf and into a doorway that led nowhere we had dug, and he was gone. No fall. No sound. The hum swallowed the sound of his boots and then swallowed the boots.

Brandt followed. He patted my hand off his arm the way you'd pat off a child, kind, dismissive, and he said, "Tell her we kept it clean," and walked through, and the black closed behind him like water.

I stood in the pit alone for — I don't know how long. Long enough that when I climbed the ladder my arms shook and Reyes had to haul me over the rim. The fog was still leaning over the hole. The hum was fading, winding down like a music box, and the last thread of it, the very last thing before the ordinary horrible silence of a job site with two men missing, was that word again, spoken gently, almost apologetic:

"Almeida."

* * *

Here's what pride would have done. Pride would have called the sheriff, filed a report, waited for the county, stood at the rim of that pit all day being a responsible adult while the fog leaned closer and the voice got friendlier. Pride would have gotten the rest of my crew killed, because I could see it in their faces — Vasquez praying out loud, Gutierrez staring at the pit like it might stare back, young Danny Okafor already turning his head toward the fog whenever the hum breathed — they were listening the way you listen to a lullaby. Half asleep. Wanting to follow.

So I did the cowardly, sensible, unforgivable-by-the-county thing. I called an emergency stand-down, ordered everyone into vehicles, drove the perimeter gate shut myself, and ran a chain through it with a padlock I keep for exactly this kind of liability nightmare, except the nightmare was supposed to be OSHA fines. Then I put Reyes in the lead truck and told him: radio station. KVLN, 1340 AM, the graveyard shift lady, Almeida. Because the voice said her name, and because she'd been asking about the dig like a woman waiting for bad news, and because when the dead start saying one word over and over, you go find the person the word belongs to.

"Why the radio station?" Reyes asked on the drive, white-knuckled on the wheel. "Why not the sheriff?"

"Because the sheriff is going to ask me questions I can't answer without ending up in a psych hold," I said, "and because that woman already knows. You heard how she talks. She's not asking what we found. She's asking how much time we bought."

The fog followed us. I checked the mirror maybe forty times between the site and town, and the wall of gray stayed with us, hanging back a quarter mile, keeping pace, polite as a hearse. It didn't touch the road behind us. It didn't need to. It knew where we were going. Everything on that road seemed to know where we were going; the traffic lights cycled green ahead of us the whole way, which sounds like luck until you remember that luck doesn't usually feel like being escorted.

The station sits on a low hill at the edge of town, a squat concrete block with a lattice tower on top, red lights blinking against a sky that was starting to go the color of the fog. Through the front window I could see a figure moving inside — a man, heavyset, moving fast, the agitated pace of someone whose morning has already gone wrong. Reyes parked crooked. I was out of the truck before it fully stopped.

The man met us at the door. Marcus, according to the laminated badge swinging from his jacket, though I'd have known him for station staff anyway; he had the hollowed-out look of a man who'd spent the night listening to things he couldn't explain.

"You're the crew," he said. Not a question. "From the Almeida lot."

"We need to talk to Cyrus," I said. "Almeida. Now."

Something went through his face — relief and dread fighting for the same real estate. "She's not here. She hasn't been here since—" He stopped. Started over. "Come inside. Don't stand in the fog."

I looked over my shoulder. The gray wall had stopped at the bottom of the hill, patient, filling the whole horizon behind us, and standing at its edge, half-dissolved, were two figures. Ortega. Brandt. Standing in the fog like men waiting at a bus stop, faces calm, mouths moving in perfect silence, and even at a hundred yards I could read the shape their lips were making. One syllable. Over and over.

Almeida. Almeida. Almeida.

Marcus saw where I was looking. He went pale in a way I recognized, because I'd been that color since seven-forty in the morning.

"How long," I said, "has she been gone?"

"Since three-thirty," he said. "And there's something you need to hear first. On the tape. Because I think—" His voice dropped, and he glanced at the fog, and he said the sentence that made the bottom drop out of my already ruined day:

"I think she went home to count her doors."
