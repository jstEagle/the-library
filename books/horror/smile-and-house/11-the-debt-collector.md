## 11. The Debt Collector

The knock came at 3:33 a.m., which by then I understood was less a time than a signature.

I had been sitting at my grandmother's kitchen table with her ledger open in front of me, drinking coffee that had gone cold an hour ago because I'd stopped trusting the stove. The ledger lay flat under the overhead light, and I'd been staring at the last entry—my name, in her handwriting, in that careful schoolteacher's cursive she'd used for report cards and recipes and, apparently, contracts—when the knock sounded at the front door.

Three knocks. Polite. Spaced evenly. The knock of someone who has all the time in the world and wants you to know it.

I didn't answer. The dead callers had been unanimous on that point, and dead people have a credibility on the subject of not answering doors that the living simply cannot match. I sat very still and did what I'd been doing for a week now, which was narrate my own life under my breath, because silence was the one thing I could not afford.

"Someone's at the door," I whispered. "I'm not answering. It's three thirty-three in the morning and I'm not answering."

The knock came again. Same rhythm. Patient.

Then my grandmother's voice, muffled through the wood, warm as a Sunday: "Cyrus, sweetheart. It's Nana. Let me in."

My whole body went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the kitchen. Because my grandmother had been dead for eleven months, and I had stood at her graveside in a dress I hated, and I had watched them lower a box into the ground while a man in a bad suit said words about rest eternal. Whatever was on the other side of that door, it wasn't her. I knew that the way you know a thing in your bones, the way you know the difference between a photograph of a fire and the fire itself.

But God, the voice. It was her voice. The exact grain of it, the little rasp at the end of sentences, the way she said my name with the emphasis on the *rus*, like a small joke she'd never gotten tired of. Somebody had studied that voice. Somebody had studied it for a very long time.

"I know you're not her," I said, and my own voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"I know you know," the voice said, and there was something almost fond in it. "That's why I knocked instead of coming in. Manners matter to me, Cyrus. They always have. Ask your grandmother—oh. You can't. Well. I'm the next best thing."

I looked down at the ledger. The last page. My name sitting there in blank space like an empty chair pulled up to a table.

"Come to the door," the voice said. "You don't have to open it. I'd just rather talk face to door. It's how your grandmother and I did business."

Every instinct I had said stay at the table. I went to the door anyway. Here's the thing nobody tells you about terror: it doesn't always freeze you. Sometimes it walks you forward, because the alternative—not knowing—is its own kind of unbearable. I crossed the kitchen, down the hall, past the doors I'd counted every night for a week. Eleven of them. I counted them on the way, out loud, a rosary of carpentry. Eleven. Good. Eleven was the number of doors a house was supposed to have when it was pretending to be a house.

I stopped three feet from the front door. The porch light was on—I kept every light on, all of them, the electric bill looked like a ransom note—and through the frosted glass I could see a shape standing there. Small. Slight. The silhouette of a woman in a cardigan, hands folded, the way Nana used to stand on the porch waiting for the paper.

"You look like her," I said.

"I look like everyone I've ever been given," it said. "She gave me this one. Consider it a courtesy. Consider all of this a courtesy, actually. I've come to talk terms, and I find people listen better when the voice is familiar. It's not a trick, Cyrus. It's a courtesy. There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm telling you."

"What terms?"

"Ah. Good. Direct. She was direct too, at the end." The silhouette shifted, and I would have sworn the porch light dimmed slightly, the way it does when a cloud passes—but there were no clouds. There hadn't been clouds in days. "May I itemize? I've been keeping accounts for seventy years. I'm rather proud of the bookkeeping."

I leaned against the wall. My legs were making executive decisions without me. "Itemize."

"Thank you." And it cleared its throat—a small, dry, elderly sound, her sound—and began.

"Item one. In the winter of 1954, a child in this house was dying. Pneumonia, the doctors said. The child's name was Ruth Almeida, aged six. Her mother—you would have called her Great-Grandmother Vera, though you never met her—came to the door of the cellar and asked for help. Not me specifically. I was simply what answered. I want to be fair about that. She didn't know what she was calling. She knew only that something under the ground had been listening to this house for a very long time, and that it had never once asked for anything, and she was desperate enough to mistake that for generosity."

The voice paused. Let it sit. A good broadcaster's instinct, I thought hysterically. Know when to leave dead air.

"Item one, continued. The child lived. Ruth Almeida recovered in three days, to the astonishment of the doctor, who billed them anyway. The price was not collected at the time. The price was deferred. Seventy years of borrowed time, Cyrus, on one child's life. And time, I'm afraid, compounds."

"Compounds how?"

"Nightly. Every night that this house went quiet, the interest accrued. Every night that no voice filled it, the balance grew. Your grandmother understood this before I ever explained it—she was clever. She noticed that on the nights the radio played, the house slept easy. She noticed the nights the station went off the air, the way the walls seemed to lean in. So she made a career of it. Forty-one years on the graveyard shift at a station nobody listened to. You thought she did it because she was lonely." The voice did something that was almost a laugh. "She did it because she was *paying*. Every hour of airtime was a payment. Every voice in the speaker, every word into the dark, a coin laid on the table. She kept the account current for forty-one years. Remarkable woman. I mean that sincerely. I've eaten a great many people and she is in my top ten."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be honest, which is rarer." The silhouette's head tilted. "Item two. When Ruth grew ill the second time—her heart, at seventy-three—the debt was called. She had been living on borrowed time for sixty-seven years. The interest alone was considerable. Your grandmother renegotiated. Do you know what she offered?"

I knew. I'd read the ledger. But I made myself say it. "Airtime. The rest of her life on the radio."

"She offered *attention*. The one currency I cannot take, only receive. Do you understand the difference? I can strip a voice from a throat—I've done it hundreds of times, there's a whole choir of them behind the door, lovely singers, some of them—but a voice given freely, broadcast into the dark, offered up night after night by someone who knows exactly what they're feeding?" The porch light flickered. "That's not prey. That's a patron. I honored it. She died in her sleep at ninety-one, which, given her outstanding balance, was a mercy I extended personally. And the debt—Cyrus, I'm so sorry to be the one to tell you this—the debt did not die with her."

"It passed down."

"It *inherited*. Like the house. Like the chin you have, the one from Vera's side. Debts run in blood in this family. It's terribly old-fashioned of us both." The voice softened, and the softness was the worst part, because it was Nana's softness, the one she'd used when I was small and feverish, the one that meant *I have you now*. "Which brings me to item three. The current balance."

"How much?"

"Everything," it said pleasantly. "But that's the good news."

I laughed. I couldn't help it—a short, cracked sound, the laugh of a woman three days past her last full night of sleep. "That's the *good news*?"

"The good news is that I don't want everything. I want what I've always wanted. I want the arrangement continued. Your grandmother is—was—my finest patron in seventy years. Do you know how rare it is, to be fed *willingly*? The others, the ones I take, they go quiet so fast. Terror is a thin broth, Cyrus. But devotion—devotion is a feast. Every night she sat at that microphone knowing what she was and who she was feeding and she did it anyway, for the house, for the name, for you." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "She did it for you. The last eleven months, the account has been running on her reserves. Those are nearly spent. The excavation—your friends with the machine, digging up my door—has accelerated matters considerably. The balance is due, Cyrus. Tonight, or very nearly tonight."

"And your offer."

"My offer." The silhouette raised one hand, and I saw the fingers—her fingers, spotted, knuckles swollen with age—press against the frosted glass, and I stepped back before I could stop myself. "The ledger is on your table. The last entry is blank except for your name. Sign it. Take her chair at the station. Broadcast every night, the way she did, the way her mother's bargain requires. Keep talking into the dark, and I will keep the account current. You will live long. You will live *well*—I have no interest in a suffering patron; suffering sours the milk. You will have your health, your house, your little show. You'll even have your callers." The voice smiled; I heard it smile; there is no describing how a voice smiles, but it did. "You already love them, don't you? The dead ones. You'd rather talk to them than to no one. I've heard you. I hear everything on this frequency."

My throat was tight. "And the cost."

"Loneliness," it said simply. "That's all. That's the whole price. You'll be alone—truly alone, the living will drift, they always do; your Marcus is already halfway gone, isn't he, telling himself you're unwell—but you'll never be *lonely* in the way that matters, because you'll have the microphone, and the dark, and me. I'm excellent company. I've had seventy years to practice being her."

I stood in my hallway, in a house I'd lived in for three weeks, and I will not lie to you, whoever ends up reading this or hearing this: for a moment—maybe ten seconds, maybe twenty—I wanted it.

That's the part I keep circling back to. Not the horror of the voice, not the itemized debt, not the fingers on the glass. The wanting. Because the offer was tailored to me the way a good tailor tailors, and the thing on the porch had been listening to me my whole life through that house's walls and my grandmother's stories, and it knew that the loneliest woman in the county was being offered a lifetime of being heard, and it knew that some part of me had been waiting my entire adult life for exactly that offer, from anyone, at any price.

"You're crying," my grandmother's voice observed, gently. "That's all right. She cried too, the first time we talked terms. Then she signed, and she had forty-one good years. Forty-one years of mornings where she woke up and the house was hers and the child was alive and the debt was paid down. That can be yours. That *is* yours. It's already written. All that's missing is your signature."

"The dead callers," I said. My voice was wrecked. "The ones behind the door. What happens to them if I sign?"

"They stay where they are. They're not part of the arrangement. They're—" the voice searched, and found a word with Nana's precision, "—*inventory*. You needn't think about them. Your grandmother didn't."

"She did, though." I wiped my face with the heel of my hand. "That's the whole point of her ledger. She logged their death dates. She logged their airtime. She was keeping track of them, not just the debt. She named every single one."

A silence on the porch. Longer than any silence yet. When the voice came back, the warmth had thinned by a degree, the way milk does when it's starting to turn.

"You've been reading."

"I've been reading."

"That was not in the terms of your inheritance."

"Neither was a twelfth door," I said, "but here we are."

The shape in the glass went very still. And when it spoke again, the patience was still there, but underneath it, for the first time, I could hear the thing the patience was wrapped around—the way you can hear the ocean inside a shell, the vast pressure behind the pretty curve of it.

"Cyrus. Sweetheart. I have been courteous. I have knocked. I have itemized. I have offered you the same mercy I offered her, which is more than I have offered anyone in this county's history. But the account is due, and I am not a bank. I am what the bank would be afraid of. So I will ask once more, and then I will stop asking, and you will discover the difference between a debt collector and a debt *collecting*."

"The answer's no."

"Think—"

"No. And here's my counteroffer, since we're doing business." I pressed my hand flat against the door, on the inside, opposite where her fingers pressed on the outside, and I felt the cold of the wood like the cold of deep water. "You want a voice given freely? You want a patron? Then hear this: I'm going to broadcast. Not for you. *About* you. Every night, on every watt that transmitter's got, I'm going to say your name and her name and the child's name and the year, and I'm going to tell this whole county exactly what's buried under my floor and what it costs and who's been paying. You feed on one listener at a time in the dark. Fine. I'm going to turn on every light in the county and put you on the *air*."

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. The porch light didn't flicker this time—it *bowed*, dimming like a head inclining.

"You would starve yourself to spite me," it said, and there was something new in the voice now, and I took a moment to identify it, and it was *interest*. "Your grandmother never thought of that. Seventy years, and none of them thought to simply—tell."

"Yeah, well." My heart was slamming. "She didn't have a producer with a portable transmitter and a grudge."

"She has a what?"

And that was when the headlights swept across the frosted glass—two bright bars swinging up the driveway, an engine I knew, a horn giving one short angry honk, and a familiar voice yelling my name from somewhere inside the fog, getting closer, getting louder, getting *answered* by something in the walls that was not me.

The shape on the porch turned its head toward the sound.

"Marcus," it said, in my grandmother's voice, tasting the name. "How thoughtful. I do so hate for anyone to miss the signing."

* * *

I was moving before it finished the sentence—through the kitchen, past eleven doors I counted at a dead run, grabbing the ledger off the table because I was not leaving it in this house, not with my name in it, not blank. Behind me the front door didn't open. It didn't need to. The doorknob simply turned from the inside, slow and courteous, the way a host lets himself into his own home.

Outside, the fog had swallowed the driveway whole, and Marcus's headlights were two dim coins sinking in it, and he was still honking, still shouting my name—and then, from the fog, in a voice I knew even better than my grandmother's, something else started shouting it too.

In my voice. Perfectly.

"Marcus!" it called, warm and scared and *mine*. "Marcus, in here! Hurry!"

I burst out the back door with the ledger under my arm and the porch light dying behind me, and I ran into the fog toward the headlights, toward my producer, toward the one living person in the county who might still believe me—and I did the only thing I could think of, the thing I'd been trained for, the thing my grandmother had done for forty-one years.

I started talking.

"Marcus! It's me, it's really me, and I need you to listen to the voice, not the words—*listen to it*—because I'm about to tell you what's in this house, and you are not going to believe a single word, and you have to believe every one."
