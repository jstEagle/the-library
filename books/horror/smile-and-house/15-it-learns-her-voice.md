## 15. It Learns Her Voice

The first call came at 4:17 in the morning, which is the hour when even the phone company gives up pretending to be on your side.

I was asleep in the good chair—the one facing the door, because I'd stopped sleeping in my bed after the night the hallway grew a thirteenth door—and the ringing pulled me up out of a dream I was grateful to leave. In the dream, my grandmother had been doing her makeup at the kitchen table, powdering her face with ash, telling me she'd be right back.

The house phone. Not my cell. The old rotary in the front hall that hadn't had service since before I inherited the place, the one Marcus kept threatening to sell as "vintage ambiance" for the station. It should not have been able to ring. It rang anyway, patient and polite, like a guest who knows you're home.

I picked it up because that's what I do. I answer. It's the whole architecture of my life: someone speaks into the dark, and I'm there.

"Cyrus Almeida, you're live."

There was a pause. Then my own voice said, "Please let me in. It's cold out here."

I want to tell you I dropped the receiver. That I screamed, or threw it across the hall, or did any of the dramatic things people do in stories about haunted telephones. What I actually did was worse. I listened. Because it *was* my voice—every rasp and flat vowel, the little hitch I get on words ending in -ing, the specific tiredness of a woman who has been awake too long. And underneath it, faintly, like a second singer coming in late on a harmony, something else. Something that didn't know how the throat worked yet. My voice played by an instrument that had only ever heard me through a wall.

"You're not me," I said.

"I'm exactly you," my voice said kindly. "You're just on the wrong side of the conversation now. Open the door, sweetheart. You sound so tired."

It called me sweetheart. My grandmother used to call me that. I hung up the phone with both hands, the way you'd put down something venomous, and then I stood in the front hall for a long time watching the eleven doors—I counted twice; still eleven, thank God—and thinking about how the thing behind them had spent seventy years listening to this family talk. It knew our cadences. It had been taking notes the whole time, patient as interest accruing.

And now it was doing impressions.

* * *

By dawn I'd told Marcus everything, mostly because he showed up at eight with coffee and a bag of gas-station donuts and refused to leave until I stopped looking like a woman composing her own obituary. Dario arrived an hour later, smelling of diesel and cold dirt, his knuckles still scabbed from the night he'd held a door against a tide of fog. We'd become, in the space of two weeks, the kind of people who have meetings. Our agenda item was always the same: how do we survive until sunrise, and what does it cost.

"It's testing us," Marcus said. He'd spread the ledger open on my kitchen table, my grandmother's spidery columns of names and airtime glowing under the overhead light we never turned off anymore. "That's what this is. It's not trying to get in through the phone. It's trying to get in through *us*. Through what we believe."

"Then it's going to be disappointed," Dario said. "I don't believe anything anymore except load ratings and my own hands."

"That's the problem," I said. "It doesn't need you to believe. It needs one of you to doubt *me* for ten seconds. Ten seconds is enough to turn a knob or lift a latch."

Marcus looked at me then, and I watched him do the arithmetic every skeptic does eventually: if the thing can sound like Cyrus, then Cyrus can't prove she's Cyrus. There is no authentication protocol for a soul. His face did something complicated and settled on loyalty, but I saw the math land, and I loved him for being honest enough to let me see it.

"For the record," he said, "if a version of you calls me tonight and asks me to open something, I'm going to ask it a question only you'd flunk."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"You'll know." He almost smiled. "It's the same trick my mother used on telemarketers pretending to be me."

* * *

It learned fast. That's the thing nobody tells you about these creatures—they're not mindless hunger. They're *students*.

The second call came that evening while Marcus was in the kitchen making the world's saddest spaghetti. The rotary rang again, and this time I made him answer it, because I needed to hear what it did with an audience.

"Marcus," said my voice, warm and low, the register I use when I'm trying to soothe a caller who's crying. "It's me. I locked myself out on the porch. Can you get the side door?"

He covered the mouthpiece with his palm, which did nothing, because the house was listening with more than phones now. "Cyrus is standing right next to me," he said carefully.

"No, she's not," my voice said gently, so gently. "Look again, Marcus. Look at where she's standing. Count the doors. She always counts the doors—you know that about her, you've known her six years. Ask her what she whispered at her grandmother's funeral when nobody else could hear. Ask her why she really took the graveyard shift. She'll know the answers. But ask yourself why she's never told *you*."

I watched Marcus's face go through it. The flicker. Just a flicker, half a second of *what if*, there and gone like a match struck in wind. But the thing heard it. I felt the line hum with satisfaction, the way a wire hums before a storm takes it down.

He hung up and looked at me and said, "I didn't—" and I said, "I know," and we stood in my kitchen not touching, two people suddenly aware of the width of the room between them, while the spaghetti boiled over unattended and the smoke alarm stayed politely silent, because even the alarms in this house had learned discretion.

"She knew things," Marcus said finally. "Things I told you at three a.m. over the board. Things I've never written down."

"Seventy years of calls," I said. "Every secret this county ever whispered into a microphone at 2 a.m. went into that house, Marcus. Into *her*. My grandmother fed it a county's worth of confessions, and it memorized all of them, and now it's reading from the archive." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I've been running a feeding program for decades and calling it community radio."

* * *

Dario nearly opened a door on Thursday.

I want to be fair to him here, because fairness matters when you're writing down what people did in the dark. He didn't open it because he was stupid. He opened it because the thing had gotten good, and because Dario was running on four hours of sleep and a grief he hadn't mentioned—that crew of his, the two men who walked into the fog toward the open frame and never came back. Their names were Tomás and Reyes. He said them out loud exactly once, and then never again, which is how you can tell a name is load-bearing.

It called the house phone at 3:33 in the morning—it liked that number now, it was showing off—and asked for Dario by name. In *Tomás's* voice.

I was downstairs when I heard him moving. He was in the front hall, hand on the knob of the fourth door, the one that led to the coat closet until last week, when it started leading somewhere with colder air. He wasn't turning it. He was just standing there with his forehead against the wood, and through the door, muffled and wet, Tomás was saying, "*Jefe. Jefe, it's dark in here. Why'd you leave us? We walked where you pointed.*"

"Dario." I kept my voice level, the way you talk someone off a ledge. "That's not him."

"He sounds scared." Dario's hand was shaking on the knob. "Cyrus, he sounds *scared*. Twenty-two years I ran crews. Nobody dies on my sites. Nobody."

"He died on yours. And the thing that took him is wearing his fear like a coat, because fear is the fastest way through a man's hands." I moved closer, slow, palms out. "Tomás isn't behind a door, Dario. Tomás is behind *the* door, forty feet down, standing in rows with his mouth moving. You've seen them. You told me. Mouths moving in silence."

"The silence," Dario whispered. And that was the hinge, God bless him. He stepped back from the knob like it had bitten him. "They were all silent. Even when they screamed, they were—" He turned around and looked at me with an expression I'll never forget, a man understanding that his guilt had been weaponized against him by something that had never met either of them. "It used the exact thing that would make me turn the handle."

"Yeah," I said. "It's been studying us. We're its syllabus."

We sat on the stairs after that, shoulder to shoulder, not talking, until the sky went gray at the edges. Around five, the rotary rang once—a single ring, a courtesy, a *still here*—and we all three looked at each other and nobody moved, and the ringing stopped on its own, satisfied.

* * *

The worst of it came Friday, and it came for Marcus, and it didn't use the phone at all.

He'd fallen asleep in the armchair in my living room—we'd stopped driving home after sunset, all of us, a quiet agreement nobody signed—and around 4 a.m. I woke to hear him talking. Low, conversational, the tone of a man catching up with an old friend. I came down the hall and found him sitting up, facing the dark window, and reflected in the glass—not outside it, *in* it, like a figure pressed between panes—was my grandmother.

She looked good. That's the horror I keep circling and can't land. She looked better than she had in years, cardigan buttoned, hair pinned, smiling at Marcus with such warmth that my chest hurt before my brain caught up.

"—told her about your father," the reflection was saying, in my grandmother's voice, soft as flour. "How he used to sing to you in the truck. How you found the tape after. How you play it sometimes in the booth when you think no one's logging the hours. She keeps your secrets safe, Marcus. She's kept mine for seventy years. All you have to do, when the time comes, is trust the voice that sounds most like love."

"Marcus." I said his name the way you say it to a sleepwalker, flat and firm, and I crossed the room and put my hand over his eyes, and he shuddered like a man surfacing from deep water.

"It knew about the tape," he said. His voice was wrecked. "Nobody knows about the tape, Cyrus. I never told anyone about the tape."

"I know." I sat on the arm of the chair and kept my hand on his shoulder, grounding him, grounding myself. "It knows everything we've ever said in the dark. That's the whole design. This house was built as a confessional, and confessionals are just archives with kneelers."

"So how do we tell?" he asked, and it was the realest question anyone had asked all week. "If it can be you, and it can be her, and it can be Tomás—if it can be anybody we love—how do we ever know which voice is real?"

I thought about it. I thought about all the dead callers who'd phoned in over the past weeks, begging me to keep talking, warning me about knocks, and how none of them had ever once asked me for anything except attention. And I thought about the thing behind the doors, which never stopped asking. Asking to be let in. Asking me to sign. Asking Dario to turn a knob. Every request, every single one, ended in *open something*.

"Here's the test," I said slowly, feeling it solidify as I said it. "The dead want nothing from us but to be heard. They say *keep talking, keep listening, stay on the line*. The thing always wants access. A door, a bolt, a signature, a threshold. So we listen to anything that asks for nothing, and we refuse anything that asks us to open."

Marcus nodded slowly. From the hallway, Dario's voice, gravel-rough: "And if it figures that out? If it starts asking for nothing?"

I didn't have an answer for that. I opened my mouth to say so—

—and the rotary phone in the front hall began to ring.

Not the usual ring. Not the patient double-bell. This was my show's cue line, the exact three-note chime Marcus had built for the top of every hour, playing out of a 1950s telephone in an empty hall at 4:44 in the morning.

I picked it up, because answering is what I do, and I said, "You're live."

My own voice came through, but different this time. Calm. Resigned. Almost tender. It had stopped asking to be let in.

"I figured it out," it said. "You're right, you know. The dead only want to be heard. So here's what I want, Cyrus: nothing. Nothing at all. I just wanted to tell you that tomorrow night, when you go down into the pit with your transmitter and your dead congregation, I'll already be inside the plan. I've been listening to you rehearse. I know the frequencies. I know the cues. I know which voice goes first."

A pause, and then, in a perfect imitation of Marcus's gentlest on-air manner:

*"Keep talking. I'm listening."*

The line went dead, and upstairs, in the chamber beneath my floorboards, something began—very softly, very sweetly—to hum along with the station ID.
