## 18. Bolt the Door

**DARIO**

There's a thing they don't put in the safety orientation, right after the module on trench collapse and the one about silica dust. It's this: sometimes a door pushes back.

I'd been standing at the top of the pit for two hours, watching the fog do its slow, wrong drift — not moving with the wind because there wasn't any wind, just rolling in place like water deciding whether to boil. Below me, forty feet down in the chamber we'd cut around that iron-banded door, Cyrus Almeida sat behind a folding table with a broadcast microphone and a portable transmitter older than my marriage, talking to dead people like she was taking requests at a wedding.

And it was working. That was the worst part. You could hear it working. The speakers we'd rigged along the pit walls were carrying her voice up and out, and underneath her voice there was this other sound — a congregation of voices rising like heat shimmer, hundreds of them, singing something with no melody I could name. The fog had stopped drifting. It was pulling. Streaming down into the pit like the whole gray ocean of it had found a drain.

"Flow rate's good," said Reyes beside me, which was a stupid thing to say about fog, but we're contractors, we measure things, it keeps us calm.

Then the lights died.

Not flickered. Died. Every work lamp on the site went dark at once, all four generators coughing in unison like they'd been slapped, and the only light left was the sick amber glow bleeding up out of the chamber below, where the transmitter was still running on battery backup.

The pressure hit a second later.

I want to be precise about this, because nobody would believe me otherwise, and honestly some days I don't believe myself. The door — the buried one, the one sealed from the inside by a woman who'd been dead for years — began to open. Not swing open. *Push.* The way a door pushes when there's a crowd behind it. When there's a flood behind it. The hinges groaned a note so low I felt it in my fillings, and the gap between the iron-banded edge and the frame widened by an inch, then two, and through it came air that smelled like a cellar full of old coats and something sweeter underneath, something like flowers rotting in a vase.

"It's coming through," I heard myself say, in the flat foreman voice I use when the news is bad and the crew needs calm. "Reyes — get to the truck, radio the station, tell Marcus we need power *now*."

He ran. Good man. Didn't ask questions, which is how you know a crew trusts you, or how you know they've seen enough tonight that questions feel pointless.

I did the math the way you do on a job site: what's failing, what can I hold, how long. The transmitter needed mains power; the batteries gave it maybe twenty minutes. The door needed weight; the counterweight rig we'd built — a chain hoist, steel plate, sandbags, the kind of jury-rig you pray OSHA never sees — was rated for maybe eight hundred pounds of pull.

The thing behind the door pulled like a tide.

I got my shoulder against the plate bracing the frame and threw everything I had into it. My boots skidded on wet clay. The chain hoist screamed. And the door slowed — didn't stop, but slowed — grinding against whatever I could give it, inching open a quarter-inch at a time instead of all at once.

Through the gap, I could hear it breathing. Not lungs. Tides. In and out, vast and patient, and threaded through the breath were voices — dozens, hundreds, all of them hers, all of them saying *let me in* in Cyrus Almeida's voice, sweet as pie, reasonable as a neighbor asking to borrow a cup of sugar.

"Nope," I told it. Out loud. Because you talk to heavy loads, everybody knows that, you talk to them so they know somebody's paying attention. "You're not on the schedule."

It pushed harder. My shoulder blade cracked — I felt it, a hot wire of pain from neck to elbow — and I held anyway, because here's the thing about ordinary men: we don't get superpowers. We get stubbornness, and a bad back, and a reason. My reason was a skinny woman with a microphone forty feet below me who'd walked into a hole in the ground on purpose so the rest of us wouldn't have to.

"Hold," I grunted, to the door, to the rig, to God if He was taking shift calls at three in the morning. "Just — hold."

The chain hoist gave a shriek like a dying bird. A link stretched. Somewhere above, thunder rolled across a cloudless sky.

Twenty minutes, I thought. Hold for twenty minutes, and pray the electrician's hands are steadier than mine.

**MARCUS**

Here's what they don't tell you about being a skeptic: it's easy right up until it isn't. Right up until you're sprinting through fog that erases the road behind your truck like a chalkboard getting wiped, toward a radio station where every clock reads 3:33 and the transmitter is playing the sound of your dead father breathing.

I'd heard it on the drive over. AM 1240, the frequency I've engineered for six years, carrying a recording I made — no, I didn't make it, I *found* it, on a tape labeled with my own handwriting I don't remember writing — and on that tape, under the static, my father. Not a memory of his voice. His voice. The specific wheeze he got laughing too hard at his own jokes, the one I'd have sworn I'd forgotten until it crawled out of the speaker and made me pull over on Route 9 with both hands shaking on the wheel.

So yeah. Skepticism: retired. Effective immediately.

The station was dark when I arrived except for the ON AIR sign, burning red like a cigarette in the black. Inside, the board was live, faders up, and the transmitter meter was pinned in the red — drawing more power than the building's feed should allow, pulling it from somewhere, from everywhere, from the grid itself bending toward this one little building like iron filings toward a magnet.

Cyrus wasn't there. She was underground, in the pit, doing the part of the plan that required a brave fool with a good voice. Which meant the part that required a coward with steady hands was mine.

The plan, as she'd laid it out over cold coffee two nights ago, was simple the way surgery is simple: keep the transmitter running. Her voice, amplified through the pit speakers, was the lure. The dead voices rising around her were the bait. If the power failed even for a minute, the song stopped, the thing noticed the silence, and silence — per chapter seven of this nightmare — wakes it up hungry.

The power failed.

I knew it before Reyes's frantic call crackled through the base unit, because the studio monitors went dead and the emergency fluorescents kicked on with their sad hospital hum. Grid feed: gone. Both legs. Not tripped — *gone*, the way a road goes when the fog eats it.

Backup generator: in the shed, sixty feet away, which might as well have been sixty miles, because outside the windows the fog had gone vertical, a standing wall of gray churning like something enormous was stirring it with a spoon.

Okay. Okay okay okay. Think like an engineer. The grid feed comes in at the pole, runs to the main panel in the back hall. If the breakers are intact, the problem's upstream — transformer, line, something the utility company fixes while you complain on hold. But the meters inside weren't reading zero. They were reading *negative*. Power was flowing backward, out of the building, into the dark, like the night had plugged itself into us.

Which meant the line was alive. Which meant if I could throw the transfer switch and isolate the building from the grid, the generator could carry the load.

The generator was off. Of course it was. Nobody starts the backup for a ghost hunt.

I went out the back door into fog so thick it felt like walking into wet wool, flashlight beam bouncing straight back at me like the air had opinions. Sixty feet. I counted steps. At thirty, the shed door appeared out of nothing like a stage set rolling in. I hauled the cord on the genset — once, twice, nothing — and on the third pull it caught, roared, settled into its diesel heartbeat, and I stood there for one stupid second feeling proud of myself.

Then I saw the main line.

It ran overhead from the pole to the building, and where it passed through the fog, it was glowing. Faint blue corona, crawling along the insulation like veins under skin. The line wasn't dead. It was *charged*, harder than it should ever be, humming a note I could feel in my teeth, and the transfer switch I needed to throw was on the other end of it, mounted on the exterior wall, right beneath the junction box where the feed terminated.

Throw the switch with the line hot, and I'd be touching a system that was pulling power from somewhere that wasn't the utility company. Touch it wrong, and I'd be a headline. *Local Man Electrocuted By Weather.*

From the pit, faint through the ground itself, I could hear the voices. Hundreds of them, singing. And under them, thinner, fraying at the edges: Cyrus, still broadcasting, still talking, her voice going hoarse, saying *keep talking, keep talking,* to people who couldn't answer and answering them herself.

My father used to fix lines. That's how he died, eventually — forty feet up a pole in an ice storm, because somebody had to keep the phones working and he was the somebody. I spent my whole life choosing the indoor version of him: wires, yes, but grounded ones, insulated ones, ones that obeyed physics.

The fog pressed against my back like a hand.

"Sorry, Dad," I said. "Guess I'm the somebody."

I killed the building's main breaker first — cut ourselves loose from the poisoned feed — and then I climbed the ladder to the transfer switch with rubber gloves from the shed that were rated for half this voltage and my heart going like a drum solo. The switch handle was ice-cold. The air around it tasted like pennies and ozone.

You don't grab a live switch. You brace, you look away, you throw it fast and clean, and you pray the arc finds the contacts and not you.

I braced. I looked away. I threw.

The world went white and loud. Something hit me like a swung door — not the current itself, I think, but the discharge, the arc flash, a fist of superheated air that picked me up and set me down hard on the gravel six feet back. My ears rang. My eyebrows, I'd find out later, were a fond memory. My hands were burned in two neat stripes where the gloves had done exactly enough.

But the switch was thrown. The building was isolated. And when I limped back inside, coughing, half-blind, the transmitter meter swung off the red pin, the ON AIR light steadied, and through the monitor speakers came Cyrus's voice — ragged, cracking, *alive* — riding clean over the congregation of the dead.

Down in the pit, the singing swelled.

**DARIO**

The link broke at minute fourteen.

I heard it go — a snap like a rifle shot, and the counterweight rig dropped six inches, and the door surged open a foot, and the smell that came out of it was the smell of the inside of a head, warm and close and endless. Voices poured through the gap like water through a cracked dam, all of them Cyrus, all of them begging, and I swear on my mother's grave one of them used my daughter's nickname, the one nobody on this earth knows but me and Elena.

I got my back against the steel plate and my boots against the frame and I became furniture. That's the job, sometimes. You're not a hero, you're a load-bearing element. The door pushed and I pushed and the clay floor gave under my heels, carving furrows, and my shoulder screamed, and I thought about the most boring things I could think of — concrete cure times, my truck's oil change, the exact price of rebar in March — because boring things hold a man together.

Then the lights came back.

Every work lamp on the site blazed at once, and the pit speakers surged with volume, and the singing coming up out of the chamber got *loud* — joyful, almost, a thousand throats finding their note at the same time — and the pressure against the door changed. Eased. Like a tide reaching high water and hesitating.

I pushed. For the first time all night, the door moved the direction I wanted it to, grinding closed an inch, two inches, the gap narrowing to a slit of darkness that breathed.

"Don't you quit on me," I told it, and shoved again, and the plate groaned, and the slit narrowed to a seam, and through the seam I heard — clear as a phone call — a woman's voice, old and dry and familiar from the photographs in the ledger, saying words I didn't understand but understood completely:

*"Hold, child. Almost dawn."*

The grandmother. Helping me shut her own door. Sealed from the inside, always sealed from the inside — that was the family trade, and apparently it ran both directions.

The seam closed. The bolts — those great iron throws, thrown by a dead woman decades ago — slid home one by one with sounds like knuckles cracking, and I fell backward into the clay, gasping, my shoulder hanging wrong, laughing the ugly laugh of a man who has been very frightened for a very long time.

Above me, the eastern sky had gone the color of a bruise healing. Gray, then pearl, then the first thin gold.

The fog collapsed. All of it, everywhere, at once — not lifting, *draining*, pouring down into the pit and through the sealed door and gone, like a tide going out and taking the sea with it. The work lamps stood in clean, empty, ordinary morning air.

Reyes helped me up. My arm didn't want to cooperate. Neither did my legs, much.

"The door," he said. "Did we—"

"It's bolted." I looked at the pit, at the amber glow fading below, at the folding table and the microphone and the small figure sitting at it, still talking, still on the air, though the sun was coming up and the show was supposed to be over. "Somebody tell her it's sunrise. Somebody tell her she can stop."

Reyes didn't move. He was staring down into the chamber with an expression I'd never seen on a grown man's face, and when I followed his eyes, I understood why.

Cyrus Almeida was standing up. Walking toward the door. Toward the bolts.

And she was smiling — and mouthing something to someone on the other side, someone only she could see, three words over and over, and even from forty feet up, even without hearing a sound, I knew exactly what they were:

*Not you yet.*
