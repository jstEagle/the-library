## 19. Sunrise Clause

The bolt was on my side of the door.

I want you to appreciate the irony of that, because it took me a while to. Dario had held that door against a tide of fog and pressure while Marcus fried his hands bringing power back to the transmitter, and between them they'd bought me exactly what I'd asked for: I was inside, the door was shut, and the thing that had eaten seventy years of my family's voices was standing in front of me wearing my grandmother's face.

The plan had been simple in the way plans are simple right up until they aren't. Lure it home with the voices of its victims, seal the door from within, pay the price. The price being me. I had done the arithmetic on the drive down into the pit chamber — or rather, I hadn't, because arithmetic requires believing you have options, and I'd spent three weeks learning that I didn't. Grandmother's ledger said so. Seventy years of borrowed time, compounded nightly, and the final entry blank except for my name.

So there I was, inside the chamber, transmitter humming at my back, the dead singing around me like a congregation that had finally found its hymnal, and my grandmother's face smiling at me with an expression she had never once worn in life.

In life, Edith Almeida's smiles were rationed like wartime sugar. This one was generous. That was how I knew it wasn't her.

"Cyrus." Her voice came out warm, gravelly, familiar enough to make my chest ache. "You're shivering. Sit down. Let's talk terms."

"I know the terms," I said. "I've read the ledger."

"You've read *a* ledger." She tilted her head, and the gesture was wrong — too smooth, like a bird deciding whether you're food. "Ledgers get amended. Ledgers get renegotiated. You're a broadcaster, sweetheart. You understand contracts better than most."

Behind her, past her shoulder, the chamber stretched back into dark, and in that dark stood the rows. Every voice the thing had ever taken, standing shoulder to shoulder in silence, mouths moving with nothing coming out. My grandmother was among them — the real one, the small stooped woman who'd taught me to count doors before I could count money. Earlier, when I'd first seen them, she'd mouthed three words at me: *Not you yet.*

I was counting on those three words now. It was a thin thing to count on. Most of my assets had thinned out over the past month.

"Here's my offer," the thing wearing her face said. "Renewal. You sign the ledger, you take the show, you broadcast until sunrise every night for the rest of your very long life. The house stays standing. The doors stay counted. Nobody else gets hurt — not your Marcus, not your foreman friend. You'll never be lonely again. Think about that. You, Cyrus Almeida, who would rather talk to ghosts than no one at all. You'll always have callers."

And God help me, for about four seconds, I thought about it.

That's the thing about temptation dressed as mercy — it knows your measurements. It had watched me take calls from dead men because ratings are ratings, and ratings are just loneliness with a spreadsheet. It knew that the worst thing it could offer me wasn't terror. It was company.

"Forever alone," I said. "Just me and the microphone."

"Just you and everyone who needs you," it corrected gently. "There's a difference."

"There is," I agreed. "It's the difference between a lighthouse and a prison with good acoustics."

The smile flickered. Just at the corner. Like a bulb deciding whether to go out.

* * *

Here's what I'd found, twenty minutes earlier, crouched behind the transmitter with the ledger open on my knees while the dead sang and the temperature dropped and something vast turned its attention homeward through forty feet of earth.

I'd been looking for a way out. There wasn't one; I'd known that going in. So instead, being a professional, I did what I do when I'm stuck with a script that doesn't work. I read it again, closely, looking for the error.

Grandmother's handwriting filled page after page. Names. Dates of death. Airtime logged in hours and minutes, neat as a bank statement. *March 4th, 1961 — 6 hrs 12 min. April 19th, 1963 — 5 hrs 40 min.* Decades of it. A debt serviced nightly, punctually, by a woman who never missed a shift, not once, not even the night my mother was born — there was an entry for that, too, logged from a hospital payphone, 3 hours 51 minutes, and I sat with that one for a while because it rearranged my whole childhood.

And then, near the end, in fresher ink, the clause. The renewal terms. The same offer it was making me now, made to her, refused:

*The debt binds the voice that speaks the name.*

I'd read it four times before it landed. The debt binds **the voice**. Not the bloodline. Not the house. Not the body standing in the pit chamber holding the book. The voice. The specific voice that spoke the bargain aloud, that kept the secret, that carried it like a tune you can't stop humming.

My grandmother hadn't died of natural causes. She'd died of silence — she'd stopped broadcasting, three days before the end, and whatever she'd fed all those years had come to collect the arrears. But her death hadn't transferred the debt to me because of inheritance. It had transferred because she'd told me. Because I was the only one she'd ever spoken the truth to, the night before she died, in a kitchen that smelled of coffee and camphor, when she'd gripped my wrist and said, *If the phones start ringing at two in the morning, girl, you answer them.* The debt passed to the last voice that knew. Mine.

Which meant the debt was exclusive. Proprietary. One voice per contract.

And I was sitting in a room full of transmitters with a confession in my throat.

* * *

"You're stalling," my grandmother's face said.

"I'm pacing myself," I said. "It's live radio. You learn to stretch."

"It won't work, whatever you're thinking." It drifted closer, and the face stayed fixed but the space around it didn't quite agree to move with it, the way a reflection lags. "You think you've found a trick in the book. The book is mine. I wrote more of it than she did."

"Then you know what it says," I said. "The debt binds the voice that speaks the name. Singular. Exclusive. One keeper at a time." I turned toward the transmitter. "So here's my counteroffer."

"Don't."

"I'm going to tell everyone."

The temperature, which had already dropped low enough that my breath showed, dropped further — dropped like a trapdoor. The rows of silent figures in the dark shifted, a rustle like dry leaves, and for the first time their mouths stopped moving in unison.

"You'll tell no one," it said, and the warmth was gone from grandmother's voice, and underneath it was a sound like a dial tone stretched over miles. "No one is listening. It's four in the morning. Your little station reaches eleven counties of sleeping farmers and one insomniac gas station attendant. And even if they heard—" the face leaned in, and up close the eyes were wrong, the irises moving like oil, "—even if they heard, they wouldn't understand. They never understand. That's why this works, Cyrus. Secrets need keepers. Truth needs only one witness willing to carry it alone. You'd be carrying it to people who will change the channel."

"Maybe," I said.

I put on the headphones. My hands were shaking. I let them. Shaking hands still flip switches.

"But here's the thing about secrets," I said, keying the mic. "They're only powerful while they're exclusive. The moment you say one out loud, it stops being leverage and starts being information. You've spent seventy years as the only game in town. The only explanation. The thing in the dark that gets named in whispers by one terrified woman per generation, alone, at night, where nobody can corroborate." I looked at the ON AIR light, unlit, waiting. "I'm about to ruin your exclusivity."

"If you speak," it said, and now the whole chamber spoke with it, the walls vibrating, the rows of taken voices swaying like wheat, "I will take everything. Your voice first. Then Marcus's. Then the foreman's. Then every soul in this county, one by one, starting with—"

"With what?" I said. "Starting with what? You're bound by the ledger too. That's the part you never liked about contracts, isn't it? They bind both parties. You can't touch anyone whose voice isn't signed to you. And mine—" I tapped the open page, the fresh ink, my own name in my grandmother's careful hand, "—mine is the only signature on file. Which means the moment I stop being the sole keeper, the moment this stops being a secret and becomes a story, your collateral evaporates."

The thing stood very still.

And in the stillness, from the rows in the dark, my grandmother — the real one, the small one, the rationed-smile one — raised her head. Her mouth moved. No sound, of course. Sound was the first thing they'd taken from her. But I'd grown up reading her lips across dinner tables, across hospital rooms, across the last conversation we ever had.

Three words again. The same three words. But this time I understood them differently.

Not *not you yet.*

*Not. You. Yet.* As in: not yet yours. As in: hurry.

* * *

I hit the switch.

The ON AIR light bloomed red, and the transmitter caught its stride beneath me, sixty watts of stubborn AM signal pushing up through forty feet of earth and out into the dark above, into the fog, into the sleeping counties, into whatever was left of the night.

"This is Cyrus Almeida," I said. "It's four-seventeen in the morning, and I'm broadcasting from somewhere I shouldn't be, and I need you to listen, because I'm only going to say this once, and then I'm probably going to be very busy."

Beside me, the thing wearing my grandmother's face went rigid.

"For the past month, callers have been phoning my show who died days before they dialed. I thought it was a prank. It wasn't. They were warning me. And the reason they were warning me is a bargain my grandmother made before I was born, and I'm going to tell you the whole thing, every detail, because that's the only weapon I've got left and it turns out it's a good one."

The chamber began to shake. Fine at first, dust sifting from the old iron bands overhead, then harder, a tremor running up through the floorboards like the ground clearing its throat.

"In 1954, my grandmother had a daughter who was dying. Fever. The doctors were out of ideas. And something answered when she asked the dark for help — something old, something hungry, something that eats voices the way I eat vending machine crackers, which is to say completely and without dignity. It saved my mother's life. In exchange, it wanted a keeper. One voice, one generation at a time, to stay awake through the nights and talk to it, to feed it sound, to keep it dormant behind a door sealed from the inside by someone braver than any of us. My grandmother paid that bill for fifty years. Then she passed it to me without asking, which was wrong, and I forgive her, and I'm ending it anyway."

The shaking got worse. The rows of the taken were pressing forward now, and their mouths were moving faster, and though no sound came out, the transmitter picked something up anyway — a hum under my signal, a harmonic, dozens of voices trying to harmonize through a wall of silence.

"Because here's what it doesn't want you to know," I said, and my voice steadied, the way it does when the red light's on and there's nothing left to hide behind. "It can only feed on secrets kept by one. That's the diet. That's the whole menu. Attention, concentrated — one frightened person alone at a microphone, holding the truth like a lit match in a room full of gas. Dilute the attention and it starves. So I'm diluting it. Right now. Every one of you hearing this owns a piece of this secret now. It's yours. It can't be collected. Consider it a gift from the Almeida family, no returns."

The thing moved.

It didn't walk; the distance between us simply disagreed with itself, and then it was close, close enough that grandmother's breath — cold, mineral, like well water — washed over my face, and its hand closed around my wrist, and the grip was exactly, horribly, the grip I remembered from that last night in the kitchen.

"Stop," it said, in her voice, in my voice, in a chorus of every voice it had ever swallowed, the dead man from my first call, the woman who'd described her own funeral, all of them layered and screaming and *begging*, and I understood suddenly that some of those voices weren't performing hunger. Some of them were prisoners using its mouth. "Stop talking. Stop *talking*—"

"No," I said. "This is the part where I don't."

And I kept going. I gave it dates. I gave it names. I described the door under my floorboards and the twelve doors that became thirteen and the ledger with its columns of hours like a prayer log run backward. I told the county everything, in broadcast order, in a clear voice, at four in the morning, to farmers and insomniacs and gas station attendants and whoever else was awake and listening, and with every sentence I felt something shift in the chamber — felt the pressure ease by degrees, felt the grip on my wrist weaken from crushing to clinging to merely desperate, felt the vast attention that had turned homeward begin to *fracture*, pulled apart among thousands of waking minds, none of them alone, none of them exclusive, each one holding a piece too small to feed on.

Across the county, I would learn later, thousands woke at dawn to hear a dead woman's broadcast. But that hadn't happened yet. What happened next was simpler and closer: the thing let go of my wrist, staggered back into the dark among its rows, and began — in my grandmother's voice, in a whisper meant only for me, the last exclusive secret it would ever try to keep —

"You have no idea what you've just started."

"Yeah," I said, watching the first gray suggestion of dawn bleed down through the seams in the earth overhead. "Actually, I've got a pretty good idea. Sunrise clause, sweetheart. Read your own contract."

The light came.
