## 7. Miriam

Ada fell asleep at her kitchen table with the predecessor's notes spread out in front of her like a confession she'd been reading over someone's shoulder.

She hadn't meant to. She'd meant to drive home, meant to eat something that wasn't vending machine biscuits, meant to call her mother and ask—casually, so casually—what Gran had been like at eleven. But the notes had pulled her down page by page, dates and names and tally marks in a hand that grew shakier as it went, and somewhere between the fourth cup of tea gone cold and the last line—*it asked me too*—her head had come to rest on her folded arms, and Hollowmere had let her sleep.

The dream did not begin with darkness. It began with chalk dust.

She was standing in a classroom that was almost hers—the same tall windows, the same radiator ticking in the corner—but the light coming through the glass was the color of candle wax, and outside there was no playground, no chapel wing, no town. Just a pale, sourceless glow, like the sky had been replaced by the inside of an eggshell.

The desks were arranged in rows of eleven.

"Sit down, Miss Montrose," said a voice behind her. "You'll want to be comfortable."

Ada turned.

The woman standing by the blackboard could have been thirty. Could have been fifty. Her face had the terrible smoothness of a photograph kept too long in a drawer—features intact, everything else worn away. She wore a cardigan the color of oatmeal, sleeves pushed up, chalk on her fingers. A name badge pinned to her chest read MRS. VOSS in careful capitals, though the V had been scratched over and rewritten so many times the card had gone soft.

"You're Miriam," Ada said.

"I'm what they made of me." Miriam smiled, and it was a teacher's smile, warm and practiced and aimed slightly past you. "Sit. We haven't much time before you wake, and I've waited fifty years for this lesson."

Ada sat. The desk accepted her the way water accepts a stone.

* * *

"There's a version of this conversation you're expecting," Miriam said, writing nothing on the clean blackboard, chalk hovering. "You've read the notes. You've seen the door. You think I'm going to tell you it hungers, that it takes children, that it must be fed or fed upon. That's the story the town tells itself, because the town needs the monster more than it needs the truth."

"And the truth?"

"The truth is that it's a landlord." Miriam set the chalk down with a small click. "It owns the ground. It has always owned the ground. Hollowmere wasn't built over it by accident—your founders came here because the land was cheap, and the land was cheap because everyone who'd lived here before had the sense to leave. And when your founders realized what they'd built on, they didn't leave. They negotiated."

"Rent," Ada said.

"Rent." Miriam nodded approvingly, as if Ada had raised her hand with the right answer. "Paid in presence. Not blood—it doesn't want blood, blood is just meat, and meat spoils. It wants *keeping*. Someone below who tends the thing, who keeps its accounts, who answers when it stirs. In exchange: prosperity. Good harvests, good funding, good luck. Your new wing. Your bypass. The fact that no child at this school has died of anything worse than a broken arm in fifty years."

"That's not what the missing students say."

"The missing students say nothing. They're not missing." Miriam's voice didn't change, but the room got colder anyway. "They're *kept*. Down here, where nothing ages, nothing sickens, nothing is taken from them. They are the most protected children in England, Miss Montrose. They're simply not available."

Ada thought of the register. Nine names present, two absent, and then the fresh ink appearing overnight like frost forming on a window. "Then why do the teachers vanish? You said it asked them too. Asked them what?"

Miriam looked at her for a long moment, and behind the smoothness of her face something moved—not an expression but a weather system, grief passing through at depth.

"It asks every one of them. Every headmaster, every supply teacher, every poor soul who signs the register. When the term of keeping runs short—when the current keeper begins to fray—it offers the position. Willingly given, freely stayed. That's all it requires. Not sacrifice. *Succession.*" She folded her hands on the desk, teacherly, patient. "And every single one of them refused. Your predecessor. The one before her. The deputy head in 1989 who left his car running in the staff lot and walked into the trees. All of them were offered the choice, and all of them chose themselves."

"So it took students instead."

"It invoiced them instead." Mirium's smile flickered. "Refusal isn't free. Refuse, and the shortfall comes out of the rolls. Two children here. One there. Never enough to cause a scandal—Hollowmere has always been very good at absorbing loss. The town calls it bad luck. Bad luck with excellent bookkeeping."

"And you?" Ada heard her own voice go quiet. "What did you refuse?"

The classroom changed. It was subtle—a shift in pressure, like ears popping—and suddenly the desks were older, the wood darker, the eleven chairs occupied by children in 1970s school uniforms, faces turned toward the front, patient as furniture. On the blackboard, in a child's handwriting: *OUR CLASS PROMISE.*

"I didn't refuse," Miriam said. "I was eleven. The class made the bargain for me—they drew lots, they signed a promise, they told me it would only be for a little while, until the town found a proper arrangement. My grandmother held my hand while they carried me down." She paused. "Your grandmother, Miss Montrose. She held my hand. She said *we'll come back for you.*"

Ada's throat closed.

"They never came back. None of them ever came back. But they sent their children, and their children's children, generation after generation, walking over my ceiling every day, learning their times tables above my head." Miriam's voice remained level, which somehow made it worse. "So yes—I keep the accounts now. Yes, I take the refusals and convert them into invoices. Because if I don't, the thing below stops being a landlord and becomes something with no interest in contracts at all. I am the only reason your school has a roof, Miss Montrose. I am the thin, tired woman standing between Hollowmere and the alternative. For fifty years. At eleven years old."

"Pity and terror braided together"—that was the phrase that surfaced in Ada's mind, unbidden, and she hated herself for thinking in her predecessor's vocabulary even now.

"What happens," Ada said slowly, "if someone doesn't refuse?"

Miriam went very still.

For the first time, the agelessness cracked—not into age, but into hope, which was far more frightening. Her eyes filled without spilling, the way eyes do in dreams.

"Then the ledger closes," she whispered. "The debt transfers. The children go home—all of them, every name under that door, fifty years of kept things returned to the daylight, remembering nothing but a long nap. The town keeps its luck. And I—" her voice caught on the word like a nail catching cloth— "I get to stop."

"Just like that."

"Nothing about it is *just like that*." Miriam straightened, and the teacher returned, armor going back on plate by plate. "It will show you everything first. What the watch costs. What you'll become. It wants consent, real consent, informed and awake—that's the old law of it, older than the town, older than the chapel. It cannot take. It can only be *given* to. Which is why it has spent half a century collecting refusals and billing them against the innocent." She leaned forward across the desk, and up close her eyes were the exact gray of the iron door. "It will offer you the choice within three days. It always does, once it knows a name. And I need you to understand, before it does, exactly what refusing means—because refusal feels like virtue, Miss Montrose. Refusal feels like keeping your hands clean. And the invoice goes to the children. It always goes to the children."

* * *

The classroom began to dissolve around the edges, chalk dust rising off the floorboards like breath in cold air. The eleven desks emptied one by one, and in the last moment before the light took them, Ada saw the names scratched into each desktop—dozens of names, the same names from the door, and beneath the newest one, still bright:

*M. VOSS — KEEPER.*

"One more thing," Miriam said, her voice already distant, already coming from everywhere. "Ask yourself why it's you. Why the granddaughter of the girl who held my hand. Ask yourself what your grandmother promised, all those years ago, with her fingers laced through mine." A pause, and in it, almost gently: "She promised we'd come back for you. I've been counting the pronouns, Miss Montrose. So has it."

Ada woke with her cheek printed by the seam of her sleeve, the cold tea untouched, dawn graying the window.

On the table, on top of the predecessor's notes, lay a sheet of paper that had not been there before. Old paper. Soft-edged. In a child's careful hand, dated June 1974:

*Class promise, renewed. We will send someone willing. Signed, Class 4B, Hollowmere Primary.*

Below the signatures, in ink so fresh it shone, a twelfth line had been added:

*A. Montrose — ?*

Ada sat very still, listening to the house settle, to the town beyond it beginning its morning noises—kettles, engines, the distant bell of the chapel that had no rope.

Three days, Miriam had said.

Outside, the excavation crew's van turned into the school car park, and Callum stepped out of it looking up at the chapel wing, and Ada pressed her palm flat against the promise note as if she could hold the whole arrangement down by weight alone.

She had until Friday to decide what kind of Montrose she was.
