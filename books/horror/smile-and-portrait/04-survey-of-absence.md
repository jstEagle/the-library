## 4. Survey of Absence

The county archive had nothing. That was the first thing I loved about Hollowmere.

I'd spent three days in the records office in Ashcombe before coming back, pulling drainage surveys, ordnance sheets, a 1968 geological assessment commissioned when someone had briefly dreamed of building a reservoir up the valley. The village appeared on maps the way a stain appears on a ceiling — present, acknowledged, never explained. The church was marked. The green was marked. Eleven houses were marked, though only nine had ever been listed on any electoral roll I could find.

No tunnels. No culverts. No mine workings, no chalk adits, no Victorian drainage gallery. According to every document the county owned, the ground beneath Hollowmere was as solid and uninteresting as ground could be.

Which meant that either the county was lazy, or the ground was lying.

I'd built a career on the gap between those two possibilities. Twelve years of urban exploration, of crawling through spaces that existed officially nowhere — storm drains under cities that insisted they had no storm drains, service tunnels beneath hospitals, the cold war infrastructure every government swears it never built. My rule was simple: paper is a story people tell about a place. The place itself keeps its own accounts. You just have to go down and read them.

So on my fourth morning at Hollowmere, I went down.

* * *

There were three ways into the undersides of a village like this, and I'd already found two. The first was the well shaft behind the derelict forge — capped with a concrete slab some time in the eighties, but the slab had cracked along one edge, and the crack was wide enough for me and my kit bag if I exhaled going through. The second was a sinkhole in the wood north of the green, half-swallowed by bramble, breathing cool air upward at a steady four degrees. Air moving means air has somewhere to be. Somewhere to be means passage.

The third way I hadn't found yet, and I suspected it was the important one, because both of my entrances seemed to want to take me to the same place.

I dropped into the sinkhole first, rigged a static line off an oak root that had grown over the lip like a hand gripping a ledge. Eight meters of free descent, then my boots found sloping floor — packed earth and rubble, dry, which surprised me. Chalk country holds water like a sponge. A dry tunnel under Hollowmere meant either excellent drainage or no connection to the water table, and both of those options interested me more than they should have.

I ran my survey line from the drop point. Laser measure, compass clinometer, the little waterproof notebook I've kept since I was nineteen, its pages soft as cloth from years of damp hands. I work old-school for the mapping even though everything gets digitized later. There's something about numbers written by hand in the dark that feels like testimony.

The passage ran south-southeast, dead straight for forty meters. Not roughly straight. Dead straight, with walls that had been faced once — you could see the ghost of pick marks under a century of silt, regular as corduroy. Somebody had cut this. Somebody had wanted it straight.

"Okay," I said, to nobody, because I always narrate when I'm alone underground. It's not superstition. It's just that silence down there starts to feel like a room with someone standing in it, and my voice takes the room back.

Forty meters, then a junction. Left branch trending west toward the village center. Right branch continuing southeast, downslope. I shot both. Then I stood in the junction for a long moment, headlamp beam swinging, doing the thing I do without meaning to: reading the space like a sentence.

And the sentence was wrong.

Tunnels have grammar. Drainage runs downhill and branches outward, like roots. Mine workings follow seams and stop where the seam stops. Service tunnels connect points of function — pump house to main, kitchen to cellar. Every legitimate underground network I've ever mapped obeys a logic of distribution. Things spread out. Things reach away from their source, because the whole point of a tunnel is to carry something somewhere else.

This didn't do that.

Both branches curved. Gently, almost politely, over distances long enough that you wouldn't notice walking them — you'd notice only in the data, the way I noticed now, plotting my bearings against the sketch map forming in my notebook. The left branch bent as it ran west. The right branch bent as it ran southeast. And when I projected the curves forward with a pencil and a ruler, both lines converged on the same coordinate.

Under the church.

I sat down on a rubble pile and looked at the two penciled arcs meeting at a single point and felt the specific pleasure that I live for — discovery, the clean click of pattern — and underneath the pleasure, faint as a note held too long, something else.

Because the curves weren't accidental. Accidental convergence happens; geology is full of coincidence. But these weren't following strata or fault lines. They were smooth. Deliberate. The kind of curve you get when the person cutting the tunnel knows where they're going and wants the approach to take its time.

Not drainage, then. Drainage doesn't saunter.

* * *

I took the left branch, toward the village.

It widened as it went, which was the second wrong note. Tunnels under old settlements are usually cramped, pragmatic things — just big enough for the purpose. This one rose until I could stand fully upright, shoulders easy, and the floor had been metalled at some point with crushed flint, compacted into something like a road. A road, underground, running toward the church, wide enough for two people abreast.

At sixty meters I found the first stake.

It was a surveyor's marker, galvanized steel, driven into the flint floor at the wall's base, and it took my brain a moment to process what my eyes were seeing because it was so aggressively ordinary. A yellow plastic cap, sun-bleached almost white, with a number stamped into it: **HM-04**. Below that, smaller: **1974**.

I crouched over it for a long time.

Nineteen seventy-four. The year the parish was dissolved — I'd pulled the dissolution order from the diocesan archive, one page, bureaucratic and final. The year, according to the electoral register, the population of Hollowmere went from thirty-one to one. Maud Ferris. Everyone else gone inside eighteen months, scattered to Ashcombe and beyond, deaths registered elsewhere or not at all. Villages die all the time; that's not strange. What's strange is a village whose death involved someone driving steel stakes into a secret tunnel and numbering them.

HM-04. Which implied HM-01 through HM-03, and presumably onward.

I found HM-05 forty meters further on, and HM-06 after that, each one exactly where my projected centerline said the tunnel's axis ran. Someone in 1974 had surveyed this passage with professional care. Someone had come down here with instruments and a crew and a numbering scheme, in the same year the village emptied out, and none of it — none of it — appeared in any record anywhere. I know because I'd looked. I'd looked twice.

You don't secretly survey your own village's drainage. Drainage isn't secret. Whatever these people had been measuring, they hadn't wanted it measured by anyone else.

My camera came out. I photographed each stake with a scale bar, logged positions, shot the facing walls, the flint road, the ceiling where the pick marks gave way — around HM-06 — to something smoother. Tooled stone. Dressed blocks, fitted without visible mortar, older than anything else down here by centuries if I knew my masonry, and I do. The 1974 crew hadn't built this passage. They'd followed it. They'd surveyed something that was already here, something old enough that whoever made it dressed stone by hand and hid it under a village that wouldn't exist for another thousand years.

Except villages don't hide things under themselves before they exist. So the hiding had gone the other direction. The village had been built *over* this. On top of it. Like a lid.

I wrote that word in my notebook and immediately crossed it out. Lids imply contents. Contents imply intent. I don't deal in intent; I deal in dimensions. I turned the page and moved on.

* * *

The passage ended at the archway.

I smelled it before I saw it — a change in the air, the mineral flatness of wet lime, and under that something organic and old, the smell of a place that has been shut for a very long time and knows it. My headlamp picked out the wall first: dressed stone again, but finer here, blocks the size of bread loaves fitted so tightly I couldn't slide a knife blade between them. Then the arch itself, centered in the wall, maybe two and a half meters high, semicircular, its voussoirs carved with a running pattern that my light flattened into shadow until I got close.

A vine. Or a rope. Or a chain — the carving was ambiguous in the way old carving is ambiguous, worn down to suggestion. Links descending into links, looping down the arch's curve on both sides, meeting at the keystone. At the keystone there had been something else. A boss, maybe, or a face. It had been chiseled off. Not broken — chiseled, carefully, the scar smoothed after. Someone had stood here with tools and removed one specific thing from the middle of the pattern and left everything else intact.

And the archway was bricked.

Not recently. The bricks were hand-molded, soft-edged, laid in a lime mortar gone the color of old bone. Nineteenth century at the latest, possibly eighteenth. Whoever closed this door had done it properly, from this side, with a craftsman's care — no gaps, no shortcuts, courses level as a spirit line. You don't brick up a doorway that carefully unless you're sure. And you're only ever that sure about one thing.

That whatever is behind the door should stay behind it.

I put my palm flat against the bricks. Cold, but not grave-cold. There was air movement — barely, a pressure I felt more than felt, the way you sense a door open somewhere else in a house. The tunnel system breathed. Everything I'd walked that morning fed into this point, and this point fed into whatever lay beyond, and beyond, according to my projections and the churchyard's position on the surface map, was the crypt.

Saint Aldhelm's, the parish church of Hollowmere, deconsecrated 1974, roofless since a fire in the fifties, tower standing empty above a nave full of nettles. Under its floor, behind a bricked archway at the end of a road that shouldn't exist, surveyed in secret by a village that was busy emptying itself: the crypt.

I did the math three times because I didn't like it the first two times. The left branch curved to meet it. The right branch curved to meet it. The well shaft behind the forge — I plotted its bottom coordinates from memory — fell within six meters of the same convergence. Three approaches, all converging, all funneling inward with the gentle patience of a throat.

Tunnels distribute. These collected.

I stood in front of the bricked arch with my notebook open and my pencil making the small fast marks of someone writing faster than they can think, and I let myself say it plainly, in words, in my own voice, because saying it out loud makes it data instead of dread:

"This wasn't built to lead away from anything. It was built to lead here."

Every tunnel I have ever mapped was infrastructure. Infrastructure serves the living — water, coal, cables, escape. You can read a civilization off its tunnels the way you read it off its kitchens. And by every standard I own, Hollowmere's underside failed the test. No function. No distribution. No record. Just a road, going down and in, maintained across centuries by people who bricked the door and then, a hundred years later, snuck back down with survey stakes to check on it.

Checking on it. That was the phrase my brain offered, unasked, and I hated it, because checking implies the thing might change. Doors don't change. Walls don't change.

Whatever they were checking on, they thought it might.

* * *

I spent two hours shooting the archway — photogrammetry, measurements, mortar samples scraped into labeled bags — and I'll admit the work steadied me. Work always does. There is no anomaly that survives a good dataset; that's been my religion since I was a teenager sleeping in drain outfalls while my foster placements rotated around me. Numbers don't leave you. Numbers don't ask you to belong to anything.

But the numbers kept agreeing with the dread, and that was new.

Total surveyed length that morning: 214 meters. Bearing error from perfect convergence: under two degrees across all three approaches. Probability of natural coincidence: I ran it that evening in my tent, properly, with the software I use for karst work, and the answer came back so small it printed in scientific notation.

Hollowmere's tunnels had been dug to gather. Everything flowed to the crypt the way water flows to a plughole, and I was the first person in fifty years to stand at the bottom of the basin and look up at the closed door.

One more thing, and I've saved it for last because I'm writing this account in order and I want it on the record in order.

When I packed up to leave, I swept my headlamp back across the archway one final time, and I noticed the bricks. The lower courses. All day I'd assumed the sealing was complete, and structurally it was — but the bottom row, the course nearest the floor, was different. Same age, same mortar. Different wear. The bottom bricks were polished. Worn smooth along their inner edges, in a band about forty centimeters wide, right at the base of the door.

The kind of wear you get on a threshold. The kind of wear made by feet, or by things passing over it, many times, for years — after the door was supposedly sealed.

Or the kind of wear made from the other side. By something pressing against the bricks, patiently, along the bottom edge, the way water tests a dam.

I photographed it. I logged it as **unexplained surface modification, archway threshold, consistent with repeated contact, origin indeterminate**. That's the language I use when the honest entry would be *I don't want to know*. It was nearly dusk. Above me, through sixty feet of chalk and nettle-grown ruin, the empty tower of Saint Aldhelm's stood against the reddening sky, and I climbed out of the sinkhole into the last of the light telling myself the day had been a triumph.

It had been. That's the worst part. It was the best survey of my career.

And as I coiled my rope in the blue hour, with the wood going quiet around me in that particular way woods go quiet — not gradually, but all at once, like a held breath — the bell rang.

One note. From a tower with no bell.

Then a second note, closer together than the first.

I stood in the brambles with my notebook against my chest and counted, the way I count everything, because counting is the only prayer I know.

Three. Four. The intervals were shrinking.

Five.
