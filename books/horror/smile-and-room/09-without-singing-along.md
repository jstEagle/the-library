## 9. Without Singing Along

Marguerite had spent the night before the lesson doing the one thing she had sworn she would never do again: memorizing.

She sat at her kitchen table with the lyric sheet from the piano bench spread flat beneath a lamp, and she did not hum it, did not tap its rhythm with her finger, did not let her lips so much as shape the syllables in silence. She only read. She read it the way you might read a confession written by someone you loved—slowly, without judgment, taking each line into yourself and setting it down again like something hot. The final verse was forty-one words long. She counted them twice, because counting was what Hollowmere taught you to trust. Forty-one words that had been sung once, by thirty-two children in a warm June assembly hall, and had swallowed twenty-six of them whole.

The paper wanted to be sung. That was the horror of it, the thing no archive note or Ansel's gray-faced warnings could have prepared her for. The words were built like a lullaby, all falling cadences and open vowels, and when she read them her throat ached with the shape of a melody that wasn't printed anywhere on the page. It was simply *there*, waiting in the white space between the lines, patient as water. Fifty years of patience. She could feel it leaning up out of the ink toward her mouth.

She turned the lamp off before dawn and sat in the dark until her hands stopped trembling, and then she wrote the lesson plan.

Not *music*. Not *song*. She wrote it the way Ansel had once demonstrated the door counts—ritual stripped of everything but procedure. **Recitation exercise. Words only. No melody. No humming. Hands flat on desks.** At the bottom, after a long moment of staring at the ceiling, she added a line she knew would look strange to any inspector who ever read it: *If a child begins to sing, stop them gently. Do not shout. Shouting is almost singing.*

* * *

She gathered them in her own classroom rather than the assembly hall, because the assembly hall had mirrors along its east wall and she was not yet ready to walk back through the auditorium glass, not with what waited on the other side of it watching her through every reflective surface in the building. Sixteen children. Her sixteen—the six who had always attended, plus the ones who had drifted in over the autumn as if summoned by something patient, plus Tamsin, who sat in the third row with her hands folded and her reflection sitting perfectly still beside her in the window glass, too still, the way a dog lies down when it has done something wrong.

Marguerite had covered the classroom mirror with butcher paper on Sunday. She could feel it behind the paper anyway, the way you can feel a bruise.

"Today," she said, "we are going to learn the last verse of the school song."

The room went quiet in a way rooms never go quiet for ordinary lessons. Even the smallest ones, the five-year-olds who fidgeted through arithmetic, went still. They had grown up inside these rituals. They knew what lived past the second verse the way farm children know which fields hold bulls.

"But we are not going to sing it," Marguerite said. "We are going to say it. Like a poem. Like a shopping list." A few nervous smiles flickered. "Words have power when you give them music. Music is a door, and voices are the key, and I am asking you—all of you—to keep your keys in your pockets today. Can you do that?"

Tamsin raised her hand. When Marguerite nodded, the girl said, "Miss Oyelaran, my reflection already knows it."

"I know," Marguerite said, and her voice held steady, which she would remember afterward as the single hardest thing she did that day. "That's why we're practicing."

* * *

She taught it in fragments, the way you'd defuse something.

First line only: *Hollowmere, Hollowmere, hold us fast.* Said flat. Said like a list of spelling words. She made them say it while pressing their palms flat against their desks, an anchor, something physical to remind their bodies that they were speaking and not singing. She walked the rows while they recited, listening—not for mistakes but for the terrible opposite of mistakes: for any child whose voice began, unconsciously, to find the tune. Because the tune was there. She could hear it trying to happen, rising out of sixteen flat little voices like heat shimmer off a road, the melody assembling itself out of their unison the way a face assembles out of fog if you stare long enough.

When it started to assemble, she clapped once and said, "Again. Flatter this time. Bored. Say it like you're reading a cereal box."

They laughed, and the laughter broke the melody apart, and she breathed again.

By mid-morning they had four lines. The windows had begun to behave strangely around the second hour—light coming through at the wrong angle, shadows pooling where no objects stood—and behind the butcher paper something shifted with a sound like wet cloth. Marguerite did not look at it. Looking was acknowledgment, and acknowledgment, in this school, was invitation.

At the fifth line, the radiators began to hum.

It was a low, sweet hum, three notes cycling, and it took her a full ten seconds to understand what she was hearing, because it was *tuned*. It was the verse's melody, played by pipes that had carried nothing but cold water for fifty years, and it was offering itself to the children like a hand offered across a table. Two boys near the window swayed slightly in their seats, mouths opening.

"Hands flat," Marguerite said, sharp and calm at once. "Palms down. Feel your desk. You are in Room 4, it is Tuesday, and we are reading a list."

The humming climbed. Somewhere down the corridor, another radiator joined it, then another, the whole building finding the harmony, and beneath the butcher paper the classroom mirror gave a long shivering groan like ice on a pond beginning to give way.

* * *

They reached the ninth line just after lunch.

Marguerite had chosen the afternoon deliberately—full daylight, the brightest hours the room would get—but the light had other plans. Clouds had come in off the moor without warning, thick and low, and the classroom dimmed to the color of old photographs. She switched on every light. All of them worked. She noted, distantly, that this was the first time since her arrival that every bulb in Room 4 had functioned simultaneously, and understood that the school was cooperating with her, or pretending to, the way a spider's web cooperates with the fly.

"Last lines now," she said. "These are the ones that matter. These are the ones Odile's class sang with music, and we will not make their mistake. Flat voices. Bored voices. We are reading a list of things to buy at the shop: bread, milk, eggs—" 

"*and carry us home where the glass grows thin,*" sixteen voices recited, dutifully toneless, and the mirror behind the butcher paper screamed.

There was no other word for it. Not a sound so much as a pressure, a shriek felt in the teeth, and the paper tore down the middle as if sliced, and the mirror stood revealed: not showing the classroom at all, but the 1974 assembly hall, sunlit and June-warm, twenty-six children standing in neat rows with their mouths open, leaning toward the surface of the glass from the far side like swimmers pressing against the underside of ice. Their leader stood at the front. Odile. Her eyes found Marguerite's with terrible, patient recognition, and her mouth moved, shaping the words along with the living class, and around her the dead children's lips began to curve into the beginnings of the melody—

"—stop." Marguerite's voice cracked across the room. "Everyone stop. Look at me. Not the mirror. Me."

They looked at her. Good children. Obedient, frightened, wonderful children.

And Tamsin did not.

Tamsin was looking at the mirror, and her mouth was open, and from the girl's throat came a sound that was almost the first note of the song—rising, sweetening, the melody seizing her small voice like a current seizing a leaf—and her reflection in the window glass beside her was smiling, was conducting her with two small raised fingers, was *her* but more awake than she was—

Marguerite crossed the room in three strides and covered the child's mouth with her own hand.

The note died against her palm. She felt it go—felt the vibration of the half-born melody buzz against her skin and then gutter out, felt Tamsin's chest heaving under her other arm, the little body rigid with the effort of a song she hadn't chosen to sing. Marguerite pulled her close, the girl's back against her chest, one hand sealed over her mouth, and faced the mirror with the child shielded against her like something carried out of a fire.

"No," she told the glass. Quietly. The way you speak to something that understands every word. "Not this one. Not any of mine. You want a voice? You want someone to finish it? Then you'll have to take the offer I'm bringing you instead, and you know exactly what it is, because you've been waiting fifty years for someone foolish enough to make it."

Odile's head tilted. Around her, the frozen assembly stilled, attentive, hungry, hopeful—a terrible thing, hope, on the faces of the drowned.

Behind the glass, faintly, the radiators' hum faded to silence. The dead children waited. And in the window, Tamsin's reflection slowly lowered its conducting fingers, folded its hands, and—for the first time since September—closed its eyes, as if surrendering, as if stepping aside.

Marguerite looked at the clock. Twenty minutes to dawn's opposite: the longest night of the year was three days off, but the glass didn't keep the calendar anymore. It kept hers.

"Class dismissed," she said softly, to the living children. "Go straight home. Don't look at anything shiny."

Then, alone with Tamsin's trembling hand gripped in hers and twenty-six pairs of eyes pressed against the inside of the mirror, Marguerite Oyelaran began, silently, mouthing the words without breath or sound, to rehearse the ending she would sing for them at dawn—

*alone, inside the glass.*
