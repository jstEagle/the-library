## 3. The Song They Won't Sing Twice

The third morning at Hollowmere arrived the way a cat arrives on your chest — all at once, and with an expression of mild judgment. Frost had climbed the inside of my bedroom window overnight, feathering up from the sill in patterns I would later have cause to study far more closely than I wished to. At the time, I merely noted them as pretty, breathed a hole in them with my thumb, and went down to breakfast smelling of woodsmoke and borrowed soap.

Edith Vane ran her kitchen the way a lighthouse keeper runs a lamp: with total authority and the assumption that failure meant death. She had eggs frying before I'd fully committed to being awake, and she counted things as she worked — spoons, chairs, the doors out of the kitchen, which was two, though she said "three" under her breath in a way that suggested she was including something I couldn't see.

"Mr. Ishikawa," she said, sliding a plate at me with the efficiency of a curling stone, "you'll want Bram this morning. He's in one of his moods, but he's always in one of his moods, so it's as good a day as any."

"I don't want to impose—"

"You're imposing either way. Better to impose usefully." She wiped her hands on an apron that had clearly been through several wars. "He'll sing for you if you let him think it was his idea. Don't ask him directly. Ask him what the song *means*, and he'll sing it just to show off."

"That's devious."

"That's family." She paused at the doorway, hand on the frame, and looked back at me with something I couldn't quite read. "You do listen properly, don't you? Mirren says you do. Most folk hear and call it listening. There's a difference."

"I'd like to believe there is."

"Mm." And she was gone, off to count something else.

I found Bram Vane in the barn, which was less a barn than a museum of things that had once been useful. Harnesses hung like shed skins; a plow stood against the wall with the patient dignity of a retired soldier. Bram sat on an upturned crate sharpening a blade that did not need sharpening, an activity I recognized immediately as the universal posture of men who wish to be interrupted.

"Morning," I said. "That's a fine edge you're putting on nothing."

"It's a scythe stone. Keeps the wrist honest." He didn't look up. "Edith sent you."

"She may have mentioned you knew some songs."

"I know some songs." The stone whispered along the steel. "Everyone knows some songs. Question is whether they're worth knowing."

"Mirren says yours are worth crossing a county for."

That got him. A grunt, half suspicion, half pleasure, the exact sound of a man deciding whether to be flattered. He set down the stone and squinted at me with eyes the color of weathered slate.

"Mirren says too much lately." But there was no sting in it. He patted the crate beside him. "Sit, then, if you're going to sit. What is it you actually want?"

I told him the truth, or the professional portion of it: I was a collector of songs, the kind that lived in only one throat, the kind that died when their singer did. I'd spent eleven years chasing variants across three countries, and somewhere in an archive in Edinburgh I'd found a fragmentary reference to a Hollowmere harvest song — a lullaby, really, sung at the turning of the year — that appeared nowhere else in the entire corpus.

Bram's hands went very still.

"Huh," he said.

"'Huh' isn't a no."

"It isn't a yes, either." He looked out the barn door at the white fields, at the frost lines running between the furrows like veins under skin. "It's an old thing. Older than me, and I'm older than I look, which is a trick I recommend to nobody." He rubbed his jaw. "You'd want it proper? Word for word, note for note?"

"As proper as it lives in you."

Another long silence. Somewhere overhead, a pigeon rearranged itself with the sound of a small argument. Then Bram stood, rolled his shoulders like a man preparing to lift something heavier than a song, and said:

"Well. It was my mother's, and her father's before her, and I'll be damned if it dies in a barn with me. Fetch your machine, then. But we do it here, not in the house. Sound carries wrong indoors."

* * *

By early afternoon I had the recorder set up on a feed sack, its reels turning with a soft mechanical purr that Bram regarded with deep suspicion, as though it might be taking something other than sound.

"It's just a copy," I assured him. "The original stays with you."

"That's what everyone says about copies." But he settled himself on the crate, clasped his big knuckled hands between his knees, and closed his eyes.

What happened next, I can only describe the way I experienced it, which is badly, because I am a scholar and scholars are terrible witnesses to beauty. We expect songs to be objects. This was not an object. It was a weather system.

It began low, almost spoken, a melody that moved the way smoke moves — never quite where you expected, always arriving where it meant to. The words were dialect, old dialect, the kind that predates the standardization of vowels, and half of them I'd need a week with a dictionary to parse. But the shape of it needed no translation. It was a harvest lullaby, Edith had said, and that's exactly what it was: a song for singing the land to sleep after the reaping, telling the fields rest now, rest now, the work is done, the grain is gathered, sleep until the thaw.

Except — and this is the detail I wrote in my notebook with a hand that was not yet shaking — except the song wasn't entirely reassuring. Woven into the tenderness were lines of instruction. *Keep the door counted. Keep the glass turned. Whatever walks the white rows, walk not beside it.* A lullaby, yes. A lullaby with a lock built into it.

And underneath Bram's voice — so faint I told myself I imagined it, so faint that even later, with the tape, I would spend hours convincing myself otherwise — there was something answering him. Call and response. His line, and then a pause shaped exactly like another line, a silence with syllables in it.

I said nothing. The reel turned. Bram sang the whole thing through, seven verses, and by the end his voice had gone rough and his eyes were wet, and when the last note settled into the rafters like a bird coming home to roost, neither of us moved for a full minute.

Then I made the mistake.

It was, I want to be clear, a completely reasonable mistake. Any archivist in the world would have made it. I smiled, and I said, warmly, honestly:

"Bram, that was extraordinary. Could we do a second take? Just for safety — tape is temperamental, and I'd hate to lose anything."

The change in the room was instantaneous and total, like a door closing in a house you thought was open throughout. The pigeon stopped moving. The wind outside seemed to hold its breath mid-gust. Even the light through the barn boards went flat and gray, as though the sun had stepped behind a cloud specifically to avoid witnessing whatever came next.

Bram opened his eyes. He looked at me, and what was in his face wasn't anger. Anger I could have handled. It was fear — old fear, worn smooth like a river stone, fear that had been carried so long it had become furniture.

"No," he said quietly.

"Of course. Forgive me, once is—"

"Once is how it's done." He stood, and his knees cracked like green branches. "Once is how it's *always* been done. You don't sing it twice. Not for safety, not for practice, not for God Himself asking nicely." He picked up his scythe stone and put it in his pocket with great care, the way a man handles something when his hands want to shake and he won't permit it. "You listen to it once, Mr. Ishikawa, and you thank your lucky stars you were allowed the once."

"Bram, I'm sorry, I didn't mean any disrespect, it's just standard practice to—"

"I know what it is." He was already walking toward the door. He stopped there, silhouetted against the white yard, and for a moment I thought he might turn around and explain everything — the song, the silence, the shape of that pause between verses. Instead he said, without looking back:

"Don't play it after dark."

And then he was gone, boots crunching over frozen mud, leaving me alone in a barn that felt suddenly much larger and much colder than it had an hour ago.

I sat on the crate for a while, doing what scholars do when frightened, which is take notes. *Subject refused second performance. Refusal absolute, ritualistic, unaccompanied by explanation. Prohibition phrased as temporal restriction: 'after dark.' Note: prohibition implies the recording itself retains some property of the performance.*

Underneath that, in smaller handwriting, because my pen was less steady than I'd have liked: *Why would a song need to be protected from repetition? What happens the second time?*

* * *

Dinner that evening was a study in strategic silence. Bram ate heartily and spoke of drainage ditches. Tam, the youngest Vane, kept his back to the window and his eyes on his plate, and when the lamplight flickered once — just once, a draft, surely a draft — his fork stopped halfway to his mouth until Edith said "eat, love" in a voice gentle as snowfall. Mirren caught my eye across the table and raised one eyebrow, a question: *Well?*

I shook my head slightly. *Later.*

Later came at half past midnight, when the house had settled into its creaking rhythms and every door had been counted — I heard Edith doing it, her footsteps moving through the dark rooms, her voice a soft murmur: "...four... five..." and then a pause at the top of the stairs, and then, very quietly, "six," as though confirming a number she'd hoped wouldn't change.

I sat at the little desk in my room with a single candle — the electric light in Hollowmere had opinions, and most evenings it declined to share them — and I threaded the tape onto the playback machine. My room was cold enough that my breath showed. I wrapped the blanket around my shoulders like a disreputable bishop and pressed play.

Bram's voice filled the little room, warm and rough and alive, and it was strange how much warmer the room felt with him in it, even secondhand, even magnetic. I followed along with my transcription, verse by verse, filling in the gaps the afternoon's awe had left in my notation. Rest now, rest now, the work is done. Keep the door counted. Keep the glass turned.

Verse four. The pause between lines — the one I'd noticed in the barn, the silence with syllables in it.

I leaned closer to the speaker.

There. Faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall. Underneath Bram's voice, buried in the noise floor of the tape, something was answering him. Line for line. Verse for verse. Where he sang *rest now, the fields are gathered in,* the other voice — thin, sibilant, pitched strangely, almost like wind through a crack but *shaped*, undeniably shaped like speech — sang something back. I couldn't make out the words. But I could make out the structure, and the structure was response. Every line Bram offered, the second voice accepted. Every verse he closed, the second voice sealed.

A duet. The song was a duet, and Bram had been singing both halves — or rather, Bram had been singing his half while something else, present in the barn that afternoon, present perhaps in every performance for two hundred years, sang the other.

My scholarly heart, I confess, leapt. Here it was — the thing every folklorist dreams of and none admits to dreaming of: a documented dialogue with the unnamed. An actual recording of the *answering*. I fumbled for my notebook, scribbling: *Second voice present on tape, absent (audibly) from live performance. Either artifact of recording process OR voice below threshold of casual hearing OR—*

I stopped writing.

Because I had done something, in my excitement, that I probably should have thought about first. I had reached over and pressed the button to rewind the tape, intending to play the fourth verse again, to isolate the second voice, to hear it properly.

And as the reels spun backward, in the quiet of that frozen room, with the candle guttering and the frost climbing the glass behind me, I became aware of a sound in the bedroom that was not coming from the machine.

Humming.

Very softly. From the direction of the wardrobe mirror — the one uncovered mirror in the house, the one Edith had never explained, the one that faced my bed. Someone humming the harvest lullaby. The melody slightly wrong. The pauses filled.

I did not turn around. I will not pretend this was courage; it was simply that my body had made a decision my mind hadn't authorized, and the decision was to sit absolutely still. The humming continued for perhaps ten seconds — long enough for me to notice that it was humming the *response* lines, the second voice's part, the half of the duet that belonged to whatever answered Bram.

Then it stopped. And in the silence that followed, the frost on my window — I saw this in the candlelight, reflected, which is somehow worse — shifted. Not melted. *Shifted.* The way a sleeper shifts. Settling into a new pattern, one line at a time, patient as handwriting.

I played the tape until dawn. Not because I needed to. Because as long as Bram's voice was singing in the room, whatever else was in the room stayed politely quiet, like a guest waiting for the music to finish.

At first light, exhausted, chilled to the marrow, and in possession of a transcription that would make my career and possibly unmake everything else, I wrote one final line in my notebook:

*The song is not a solo. It has never been a solo. The question — the only question that matters — is: who has been singing the second part all these years, and why does its voice, on the tape, sound so terribly like mine?*
