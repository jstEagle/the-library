## 6. What Edith Knows

Edith Vane had buried two husbands, one son, and a great many secrets, and she had learned that the order in which you did the burying mattered enormously. Husbands first, because they expected it. The son because there was no choice. Secrets last, and carefully, and never all in one place — a secret kept whole was a burden, but a secret broken into pieces and scattered through the years, through different silences, through the careful way she said *we don't look after dark* instead of *the thing in the yard will count you home* — well. A secret in pieces could be carried.

She had been carrying hers for sixty-one years, since the winter her grandmother took her small hands in her own cracked ones and said, *Edith, love, when the frost starts writing, you stop reading.*

And now there was a folklorist sleeping under her roof with a machine that caught voices like flies in amber, and he had asked Bram to sing twice, and Edith had felt the floor of the world shift half an inch beneath her slippers.

So on the third morning, while Mirren walked Tam down to the schoolhouse and Bram pretended to mend a fence that had needed no mending since 1987, Edith put on her good cardigan — the maroon one, the one she wore for funerals and difficult conversations, which in Hollowmere were frequently the same event — and knocked on the door of the blue room.

"Gideon, dear. Might I borrow you? There's tea."

It was not a question. Edith Vane had not asked a genuine question since the Falklands War, and even then she'd been fairly sure of her position.

He came down looking rumpled and earnest, that terrible open face of his, the face of a man who had spent his life believing that if only you listened hard enough to people, nothing bad could happen to anyone. She led him into the front room — the one with the covered mirror, the sheet over it tucked at all four corners, because a loose corner was an invitation and Edith Vane did not extend invitations — and poured the tea herself, because if you let someone else pour, you couldn't control how much time the pouring bought you.

"Sit," she said. "Drink. Don't talk until I've finished, because I'm eighty-one years old and I don't have the stamina for interruptions."

He sat. He drank. Bless him, he actually obeyed, which made this harder rather than easier. It was always harder when they were polite.

"I'm going to tell you some things," she began, "and I want to be clear from the outset that I am telling you almost none of them. What I give you today is fragments. Deliberate fragments. You're a scholar, so you'll be tempted to assemble them into a whole, and I want you to resist that temptation, because the whole is worse than any of its parts, and parts can be set down and forgotten, whereas wholes follow you about."

Gideon set down his cup. "Mrs. Vane—"

"Edith." She raised a finger. "I said no talking. That includes talking back, which is what you were doing just then, technically, by starting a sentence."

He closed his mouth. Good lad. She allowed herself a small internal sigh, because it would have been so much easier if he'd been rude. Rude men she could have thrown out. This one she was going to have to save, and saving people was exhausting work, particularly people who didn't know they needed it.

"The founding Vanes," she said, "made a bargain. Before the church came, before the county mapped us, before there was a Hollowmere with a name on it. My family has lived in this house for two hundred years and we have paid for the privilege every single day, in small installments, the way you pay rent. We do not uncover mirrors. We do not sing certain songs twice. We do not finish patterns. And once a generation or so, something comes to collect, and we make sure it collects *correctly*, and that is all I will say about that."

His eyes had gone bright — that scholar's brightness, the hunger she'd seen in him the moment he stepped over her threshold with his recording equipment and his letter and his terrible, wonderful curiosity. She had known within four minutes of meeting him that he was the sort of man who would take a fragment and worry at it like a dog at a bone until either the bone broke or his teeth did.

"You understand," she continued, "that I am telling you this against my own judgment. Bram wanted to handle it himself, but Bram's idea of handling things is to feel guilty loudly in the barn where nobody can hear him, and Mirren would simply glare at you, which works on Tam but won't work on a grown academic. So it falls to me, as it always does."

"Edith," he said quietly, "I haven't understood anything yet, but I promise you I'm listening."

"Oh, I know you are. That's the problem." She looked at him over the rim of her cup. "Do you know what happened the day you arrived?"

"The fog was thick on the road—"

"The count began."

The words came out flat and cold, and she watched them land. Watched the brightness in his eyes flicker, just slightly, like a candle in a draft.

"There is a counting," she said. "There has always been a counting. It is slow, and it is patient, and it does not care about Thursdays or harvests or the feelings of old women. It counts, and when it reaches its end, someone is taken. And Gideon Ishikawa, folklorist, collector of songs nobody will sing twice — it began the morning your car crossed the frost line at the edge of our land."

"That's—" He stopped himself. She appreciated the effort. "You're saying I caused it."

"I'm saying you *coincided* with it, which is a kinder word and means nearly the same thing in Hollowmere." She set her cup down with a click. "Though I'll note that my grandson signed a letter inviting you here, so between the two of you, the blame is a shared meal. But blame isn't why I'm talking to you. Protection is why I'm talking to you."

She rose and went to the window. The frost had come up the glass overnight in long silver filaments, branching, deliberate — and she positioned herself, quite casually, directly in front of the section she did not want him examining. Sixty-one years of practice. You learned to stand in front of things.

"When you look out windows," she said, "and you will, because you're the looking kind, I can see it in you — I want you to notice the frost. Really notice it. And then I want you to forget it."

"Forget it?"

"Frost is weather, dear. Weather writes whatever it likes and melts by nine o'clock. There's nothing in it worth a scholar's attention, and certainly nothing worth standing at a window at dusk to trace with your finger, which is what your kind always ends up doing, and which I am asking you, pleading with you, not to do." She turned. "Because the frost writes patterns here. And the patterns must never be finished. Do you understand me? Whatever shape you see forming in that glass — a curve, a line, a little figure, anything — you do not complete it. You do not help it along. You breathe near it wrong and it grows, and a finished pattern is a door, and we have spent two hundred years keeping every door in this house shut."

He was pale now. Good. Pale was progress. Pale meant the fragments were assembling into something with edges, and edges frightened people properly, unlike wholes, which merely fascinated them.

"Thursday," she said.

"What about Thursday?"

"Leave by Thursday. Take the early bus out of Marrow's Cross — Bram will drive you, he owes you that much and considerably more, though he hasn't worked up the courage to say so. Pack tonight. Tell Mirren whatever soft nonsense you like about deadlines and conferences; she'll see through it, she sees through everything, she gets it from me. But be gone by Thursday, and don't stop anywhere with reflective surfaces between here and the motorway, and when you get home, write your book about some other village. There are hundreds of them. Pick one with a church and a pub and nothing underneath."

"Edith." He stood now, and his voice had that gentle stubbornness she was coming to dread. "If there's something here — if your family is in danger because of me — I can't just walk away from that."

"Yes," she said, "you can. It's remarkably easy. You put one foot in front of the other in a generally outward direction. I've watched people do it. Some of them even managed it at speed."

"And you? And Mirren, and Tam, and Bram?"

"We are the Vanes." She said it the way other women said *we are the Church of England* — with weariness, with pride, with the absolute absence of any expectation of rescue. "We were here before the bargain and we'll be here after it, one way or another. This house knows us. That is precisely why you must go, dear. The house doesn't know you. Nothing here knows you. And the things that don't know you are the things most curious to learn."

She saw him turn that over — she could practically hear the gears, the scholarly machinery grinding away, fragment clicking against fragment. He opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"The song," he said finally. "Bram's lullaby. When I played the recording back, there was a second voice answering him. Underneath. Line for line."

The room went very still. Outside, wind moved along the eaves, and somewhere in the walls the house settled with a sound like a held breath being released slowly through teeth.

Edith picked up the teapot and refilled his cup, though he hadn't touched it, because her hands required occupation and the alternative was wringing them, and Vane women did not wring their hands. They folded them. They kept them busy. They did not shake, not in front of company.

"Then you heard more than I did at your age," she said, "and I heard enough to last me."

"It sounded like—" He hesitated, and she watched him decide whether to say it, and say it anyway, because of course he did, because he was constitutionally incapable of leaving a true thing unsaid. "It sounded a bit like me."

*There it is,* thought Edith, and something behind her ribs went tight and cold as wire.

She crossed the room and took both his hands in hers. His fingers were cold — scholars' hands, always cold, always holding pens in unheated archives — and she warmed them the way she'd warmed Tam's, the way she'd warmed Mirren's, the way she'd once warmed a husband's, back when warming hands was all a bargain ever asked of her.

"Listen to me, Gideon Ishikawa," she said. "I am going to tell you the only whole truth I have left, and it is this: whatever answers you, whatever follows you, whatever smiles at you out of the corner of a mirror — it is not your friend, and it is not your enemy, and those are the only two categories you have, and neither one fits, and that is exactly what makes it dangerous. You cannot fight it and you cannot study it. You can only be elsewhere when it finishes counting. So be elsewhere. Be elsewhere by Thursday, and live a long life, and grow old in some warm southern town where the frost never writes anything, and think kindly of an old woman who told you almost nothing, because almost nothing was everything she could afford."

His hands tightened around hers. His eyes were wet, damn him, and furious, and still — infuriatingly, unforgivably — bright.

"Edith," he said, "what happens Thursday?"

She let go of his hands. She gathered the cups. She straightened the sheet on the covered mirror, tucking the corner that didn't need tucking, because her hands still required occupation.

"Thursday," she said, "the frost finishes writing."

* * *

That night Edith sat up past midnight in the kitchen with the lamp turned low, doing sums she already knew the answer to. Three days until Thursday. One bus out of Marrow's Cross, if the roads behaved, and the roads had been behaving strangely since the fog came in — Bram mentioned the mile markers were counting wrong, which was a sentence she'd made him repeat three times and then regret repeating.

Through the kitchen window, the yard lay white and silent under a fat indifferent moon. The frost on the glass had grown again. She did not look at it directly. She looked at it the way you look at a sleeping animal — sideways, with respect, counting its breaths without meaning to.

And there, low in the corner where the panes met the sill, a new line had appeared since supper. A single stroke, clean and deliberate, curving gently inward.

Not weather. Weather did not practice. Weather did not, stroke by patient stroke, night after night, work toward a shape.

Edith pulled her shawl tighter and told herself the old lie, the family lie, the load-bearing lie: *it isn't finished yet.* And then, because she was honest in the privacy of midnight the way she was nowhere else, she admitted the truth underneath it — that the pattern hadn't been growing toward completion these three nights at all.

It had been growing around a space. A blankness in the center, roughly the size and shape of a standing man.

And tomorrow, she thought, watching the dark yard where the snow lay unbroken and innocent, tomorrow I'll burn the letter, hide his boots, and put laxative in his tea if that's what it takes — because that blank space in the frost fit Gideon Ishikawa perfectly, and she had invited him into her house, and Edith Vane did not lose guests.

From the hall behind her, faint and unmistakable, came the sound of the uncovered mirror in the blue room — the one mirror, the forbidden one, the one they kept facing the wall — creaking softly against its frame.

As if something on the other side had leaned closer to listen.
