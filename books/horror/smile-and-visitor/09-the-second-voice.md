## 9. The Second Voice

Gideon had a system for analyzing field recordings, and like all of his systems it was elaborate, slightly superstitious, and entirely his own invention.

First: the tape had to rest overnight before he touched it again. "Let the song settle," he'd tell his graduate students — the two of them, back when he'd still had students, back when he'd still had a flat in Cambridge with a radiator that clanked like a ghost with opinions. "A recording is like a stew. You don't taste it straight off the fire."

He'd never told them the real reason, which was that first listens were always contaminated by excitement. The ear heard what it hoped to hear. The second listen, cold and morning-sober, heard what was actually there.

So on the morning after Bram Vane's lullaby had gone into the machine, Gideon sat at the little table in his room with headphones on, tea going cold beside him, and listened to what was actually there.

The room was quiet. Downstairs, Edith was counting doors — he could hear her faintly through the floorboards, her voice steady as a rosary: *eleven, twelve, thirteen, all present.* Tam's footsteps scampered past his door on some errand, quick as a mouse avoiding open ground. And outside, the frost had come down again in the night, thick and white and patterned, though he hadn't yet looked closely at the windows. He would. He always would. But first:

First, the voice.

He'd noticed it last night, just barely, on the edge of sleep — a shadow under Bram's melody, something answering each line a half-beat late. He'd assumed then that it was bleed-through, an echo in the stone kitchen, maybe old Bram humming harmony without knowing it, the way old singers often did. Folk singers were full of ghosts like that; half the tradition was one voice talking to another across a hundred years.

But now, with the gain pushed up and the tape slowed to three-quarters speed, Gideon could hear that this was no echo.

It was a duet.

Bram sang the lullaby — the harvest verses, the ones about the barley bowing and the reaper resting — and underneath him, low and patient, a second voice answered every line. Not repeating. *Answering.* Where Bram sang *the fields lie down in silver*, the other voice sang something back, words Gideon couldn't yet resolve into language, syllables that slid against the ear like wet stones. Where Bram's melody rose, the answer fell. It was call and response. It was a conversation.

And Bram, Gideon realized with a slow crawl of cold up his spine, had been singing *to* it. The pauses between lines weren't breaths. They were listening gaps. Old Bram Vane hadn't recorded a lullaby. He'd held up his end of a dialogue and prayed the other party kept its side brief.

"Right," Gideon said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "Right. Echo. Stone kitchen. Very resonant, these old houses."

His voice sounded thin even to himself.

He spent two hours on isolation work, and it was the kind of work he loved best in the world — fiddly, absorbing, the scholar's equivalent of whittling. He filtered out Bram's fundamental frequencies. He notched the harmonics. He cleaned and cleaned until the answering voice stood alone in the headphones, naked and unmistakable.

Then he sat very still for a long time.

Because here was the thing, and the thing was this: he knew every voice in Hollowmere now. Three days of fieldwork, a village of forty-odd souls, and he had recordings of nearly all of them — Edith's crisp churchwarden vowels, Mirren's low warm alto, Bram's gravel-and-honey baritone, Tam's bright chirp, the blacksmith's wheeze, the postmistress's laugh like a gate hinge. Voices were his trade the way faces were a portrait painter's. He could pick Mirren out of a crowd blindfolded. He'd have sworn to it.

The answering voice matched none of them.

Worse than none of them — and here Gideon set down his tea because his hand had developed a tremor he didn't care for — worse than matching nobody, it matched somebody. There was a timbre there, under the wrongness, a grain and a pitch center that tugged at his memory like a name on the tip of the tongue. A familiar resonance. The kind of vocal tract you'd expect from a man of medium build, thirty-some years, who'd grown up speaking English with —

With his accent.

"No," Gideon said to the empty room.

The empty room did not disagree, which he felt was unhelpful of it.

He played the isolated voice again. Listened like a scientist. Made himself take notes, because notes were solid, notes were the scaffolding of sanity: *Timbre: light baritone. Resonance: forward placement, slight nasal brightness consistent with — * and there his pen stopped, because writing *consistent with my own* felt like signing a confession to something.

He wrote it anyway. Scholars wrote true things. That was the whole job.

*Consistent with my own.*

* * *

He needed air. He needed the ordinary world: snow, chickens, the smell of Bram's pipe. He needed to walk through Hollowmere and look at actual human faces attached to actual human throats and confirm to himself that the world was still populated by people whose voices came out of their mouths in the normal direction, at the normal time.

He found Mirren in the yard, breaking ice on the water trough with the heel of her boot, breath steaming, cheeks pink as apples. She looked up when the door banged open and something in her face — relief? wariness? both? — flickered and settled into a smile.

"You've got your listening face on," she said.

"My what?"

"That face. Like a dog hearing a whistle nobody else can hear." She nodded at the headphones hanging around his neck. "You've been at the tape all morning. I could see you through the window, hunched over that table like a gull over a mussel bed."

"It's a very good mussel," Gideon said. Then, because she was Mirren and lying to her felt like writing with his left hand: "There's a second voice on Bram's recording."

The ice cracked under her boot. She didn't look up right away.

"Aye," she said finally. "There usually is."

"*Usually is?*" He came down the steps two at a time. "Mirren. You knew?"

"I knew there's never just one voice in this house after dark." She straightened, wiping her hands on her apron, and gave him a look that was equal parts fondness and warning — a look he was beginning to suspect was simply her face. "I told you the first night. We don't sing twice. Now you know why. First time is a question. Second time is an invitation."

"But the voice — whose is it? Have you heard it? Can you identify—"

"Gideon." She said his name the way you'd set down something fragile. "Listen to yourself. *Identify.* You want to pin it in a book with a Latin name. Some things you don't identify. Some things identifying is how they notice you noticing."

"I'm a folklorist. Noticing is the entire profession."

"And how's the profession treating you?" She tipped her head toward the house, toward his window, toward — he realized with a small cold jolt — the mirror inside it. "You slept badly. You've got shadows under your eyes like thumbprints."

"I had a productive night."

"Mm." She picked up the water pail. "Come walk the river with me if you're going to interrogate me. I think better moving, and you'll worry less with your legs busy."

They walked the frozen river path, and she talked about everything except the voice — the ice patterns, the year the willow split, the proper way to make sloe gin (which involved, apparently, pricking each sloe individually with a thorn, "because shortcuts make bitter gin and bitter people"). Her chatter was a kindness, he understood that. She was giving him somewhere ordinary to stand while the extraordinary drained out of his ears.

But at the bend where the river deepened under black ice, she stopped, and her voice dropped into its lower register, the one she used for true things.

"Bram's father answered it once," she said. "The singing. My great-grandfather. Sang the response back, line for line, thinking it was a game or a test or a prayer — nobody remembers which, and he never said. After that, the mirrors in this house started keeping time wrong." She looked at the ice beneath their feet, at their own reflections floating pale and doubled down in the dark water. "Whatever sings back isn't learning the song, Gideon. It already knows it. It's learning *you*."

The reflections in the ice looked up at him. His own face, white against dark water, and beside it Mirren's, and both of them perfectly still, perfectly synchronized, the way reflections are supposed to be.

He looked away first.

* * *

That evening he ran the comparison tests, because he was who he was, and fear had never once in his life stopped him from making a chart.

He lined up the isolated voice against his own field announcements — those little spoken intros he recorded at the start of every session: *"Gideon Ishikawa, Hollowmere, November the fourteenth, interviewing Bram Vane regarding harvest lullabies."* Forty-one such clips. He cut the answering voice into similar-length phrases, normalized the levels, and played them side by side.

The accent was his. The pitch center was his. The little upward flick at the end of phrases — the one his mother used to tease him about, *you sound like you're asking questions even when you know the answers* — was his.

The differences were worse than the similarities. The answering voice never breathed. Across every phrase, however long, there was no intake of air, no catch, no moisture. It spoke the way a gramophone speaks: fully formed, tireless, coming from somewhere just behind itself.

And it was *slightly* too good. That was the detail that finally made him close the notebook. Human voices wavered; this one wavered only where a wavering would be expected, like an actor remembering to add imperfection. It was his voice performed by something that had studied his voice. A portrait so faithful it revealed the painter had never seen a living face.

Gideon took off the headphones and sat in the lamplight, and the silence of the house pressed in around him, and he thought: *Thursday. Edith said leave by Thursday. Perhaps scholars also need to know when a text is reading them back.*

Then he caught sight of the mirror.

It hung above the washstand, the one uncovered mirror in the house — Edith's single concession to necessity, turned to face the wall most days but swung out for guests, because even haunted families had manners. In it, Gideon saw himself sitting at the table: rumpled, hollow-eyed, headphones around his neck like a yoke.

He smiled at himself, experimentally. The way you check a lock.

His reflection smiled back a half-second too long.

Not lagging — he'd have almost preferred lagging, because lagging had a mechanical explanation, some trick of lamplight and tired eyes. This was different. He had stopped smiling, and the reflection went on smiling, easily, warmly, holding the expression the way a host holds a handshake a beat past comfort, before letting it fall away into neutrality just as Gideon's own face arrived at neutral.

Gideon sat absolutely still. So did the reflection. Two men in a lamp-lit room, one of them breathing.

"All right," he said quietly. "Test."

He raised his right hand. The reflection raised its right hand — promptly, correctly, no lag at all now, which was somehow more alarming than delay. He lowered it. Lowered. Raised his left eyebrow. Raised. He made a series of random faces — grimace, pout, wide-eyed stare — and the reflection tracked him faithfully, a perfect puppet, obedient, exact.

Then, when he'd finished and let his face go slack with exhaustion, the reflection smiled again. Small. Private. The smile of a man alone in a room who has just thought of something pleasing.

Gideon had not thought of anything pleasing.

He stood up so fast the chair skidded. He crossed to the mirror and put his palm flat against the cold glass, and his reflection met it, palm to palm, and for one absurd moment he simply stood there, a scholar touching hands with his own portrait, waiting to feel something — warmth, movement, a pulse of wrongness through the glass.

Nothing. Just cold glass and his own worried eyes, doing exactly what his eyes did.

Except that when he stepped back, the reflection stepped back a fraction *after* him. One second. Maybe less. A heartbeat of delay, there and gone, like a stutter in time itself.

"One second," Gideon whispered. "What happens in one second?"

Downstairs, faintly, Edith began counting doors. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. All present.

And beneath her voice — so faint he might have imagined it, except he had spent the entire day teaching his ears to hear exactly this — something else in the house hummed the harvest lullaby, softly, getting one note deliberately wrong, over and over, practicing.

Gideon turned back to the mirror. His reflection was looking at him with an expression of perfect, patient sympathy.

It raised one finger to where its lips were, and mouthed, soundlessly, in perfect synchrony with nothing at all:

*Soon.*
