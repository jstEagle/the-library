## 1. The Wrong Sister

The bus from the mainland let Elodie off at the top of the hill, where the road to Well bent like a question mark around the old salt marsh. She stood a moment with her suitcase and the smell of diesel on her coat, looking down at the town she had sworn never to see again: the grey stone terraces stacked against the seawall, the church spire leaning east as if listening for something out past the breakwater, the well itself—capped with iron, ringed in lichen—squatting in the square like a patient animal.

Seven years away, and Well had not changed so much as settled. The way a body settles.

She walked down into it because there was no other direction to walk. Her sister was dead, and her sister was here, and whatever else was true, that much was true.

* * *

They had laid Odile out in the front parlour of the house on Chapel Lane, the same parlour where their grandmother had been waked, and their grandmother's mother before her, and Elodie supposed every Halloran back to the founders, all of them arranged on the same trestle boards with the same white cloth folded under their chins. The room smelled of beeswax and cold salt water, though the windows were shut. It always smelled that way in Well, she remembered now. As if the sea were in the walls, waiting politely to be invited further in.

Her mother met her at the door. Maren Halloran looked smaller than Elodie remembered, or perhaps just more compact, the way things get when they are braced. She took Elodie's face in both hands and studied it for a long moment—not the way a mother looks at a grieving daughter, Elodie thought later, but the way a woman checks a coin against another coin, edge to edge, weight to weight.

"You came," Maren said.

"Of course I came."

"She'd have wanted—" Her mother stopped. Started again. "Come and see her."

And Elodie went, because refusing would have meant saying aloud what was already rising in her throat like floodwater, and she wasn't ready. She crossed the parlour. She looked down into the coffin.

A woman lay there, drowned and then dried out by the undertaker's art, her hair combed flat and dark against the pillow, her hands folded over a sprig of sea thrift. Her face was calm in the way of faces that have been arranged. The funeral home had done its work well. Too well. Every feature smoothed toward an average, toward a kindness, toward the idea of a face rather than the fact of one.

"That isn't Odile," Elodie said.

It came out plainly, without drama, the way you say *it's raining*. Behind her, the murmur of arriving mourners went quiet—not all at once, but in a ripple, the way birdsong stops along a hedgerow.

Maren did not gasp. She did not weep or argue. She simply came to stand beside her daughter, took her hand, and said, gently, the way you speak to someone standing too close to a cliff edge:

"It is, love. Look again."

Elodie looked again. And this was the thing she could not explain afterward, the thing that made her doubt herself from that first hour onward: the longer she looked, the more the face agreed to be Odile. The jaw was right. The mole below the left ear was right—or nearly right, half a centimetre off, but grief blurs measurements. The hands were long and pale and bitten-nail familiar.

But they weren't. They weren't, and some animal part of her knew it the way you know a room has been searched even when everything sits exactly where you left it.

"That's not her," Elodie said again, quieter.

"Come and sit down," her mother said. "You've had a long journey."

* * *

The wake filled the house the way tide fills a pool: gradually, then completely. Neighbors Elodie hadn't seen in seven years pressed her hands and kissed her cheeks and told her how sorry they were, and every single one of them held her gaze a beat too long before letting go. Not hostile. Nothing so useful as hostility. Just a fraction of a second extra, as if each of them were confirming something against a list only they could see.

Mrs. Tregowan from the bakery, who had once given the twins free iced buns every Saturday, squeezed Elodie's fingers and said, "You're so like her, you know. Always were." A beat. "So like."

Old Callum Voss, who kept the archive and had taught them both to read Latin from the founders' gravestones, nodded at her across the room and did not come over at all, which somehow felt worse than coming over.

The vicar, a soft-spoken man named Reverend Pike who had arrived in Well only three years ago and therefore should have known nothing, caught Elodie by the fireplace and said, "Your sister spoke of you often. Toward the end."

"Toward the end of what?" Elodie asked.

He smiled. It was a kind smile, professionally kind, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Of her life," he said, and moved on.

By nine o'clock the parlour was full and warm and loud with low voices, and Elodie stood in the corner holding a glass of sherry she hadn't sipped, watching people file past the coffin. They didn't look at the body. That was what she noticed, finally, what snagged in her like a fishhook: they looked at the coffin, they touched the wood, they bowed their heads—but their eyes slid to Elodie. One after another after another, dozens of them through the evening, each glance flicking from the dead woman to the living one and back, quick as counting.

As if checking. As if verifying.

As if making sure the right sister was in the right place.

* * *

She escaped to the kitchen near midnight. The house had emptied to a core of aunties and elders who would keep the vigil till dawn, per custom, and Elodie needed air that didn't taste of other people's certainty. In the kitchen she found her cousin Sennen, twenty now, whom she'd last seen as a gangly boy, sitting alone at the table eating cold ham straight off the carving board.

"They won't stop looking at me," Elodie said, because she was tired and it was midnight and he was family, technically.

Sennen chewed. Swallowed. Wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, and for a moment he looked like the boy she remembered, the one who used to dare the twins to swim past the second buoy.

"You look at them too," he said.

"What?"

"When you think no one's watching. You look at everyone like you're counting them as well." He shrugged. "Maybe it runs in the family."

"Sennen. Look at me. Do you believe that woman in there is Odile?"

He considered the question with a seriousness that frightened her more than any answer could have. He looked at the ceiling, at the window where the night pressed black against the glass, and finally at her.

"I believe," he said slowly, "that Well needs what Well needs. And I believe your mum's had the worst year of her life, and I believe you should eat something." He pushed the plate of ham toward her. "Grief does funny things, Elle. Makes people see wrong. My gran saw my grandad walking the seawall for a month after he went. Doesn't mean he was walking."

"Doesn't mean he wasn't," Elodie said.

Sennen laughed—a short, startled bark—and then didn't laugh anymore, and the silence between them got very wide and very deep, and Elodie understood that she had just heard the town speaking through him, patient and reasonable and absolutely immovable, wearing her cousin's face the way the undertaker had fitted a borrowed calm onto the drowned woman's features.

* * *

At two in the morning, unable to bear the vigil any longer, she went back into the parlour alone. The candles had burned down to stubs; the last of the watchers dozed in chairs by the door, an auntie snoring softly, a neighbour with his hat over his eyes. Elodie knelt at the coffin rail.

Up close, in the guttering light, she could be honest with herself. She catalogued the face like evidence. The mole, misplaced. The earlobes—Odile's were attached; these were free. The fingernails, unbroken, when Odile had bitten hers to the quick since they were six years old. The hair, dark brown, when Odile had streaked it with lemon juice every summer since they were fourteen and it had never fully gone back.

Drowned women don't dye their hair, she thought wildly. Drowned women don't get to choose.

"I know you're not her," she whispered to the dead stranger. "Whoever you are—I'm sorry. But you're not her."

The candle flames leaned, all together, though the windows were shut.

"Elodie."

Her mother's voice, behind her, very quiet. Maren stood in the doorway wrapped in a shawl, and her face in the candlelight was terrible in its gentleness—the gentleness of someone who has decided, long ago, what must be believed, and will carry that decision to the grave and past it.

"Mum. Tell me the truth. Please. Whatever it costs. Just tell me."

Maren came forward and knelt beside her, and took her daughter's cold hands in her warm ones, and looked at her with such naked, bottomless love that Elodie felt herself start to cry before she knew why.

"My darling girl," Maren said. "That is your sister. And tomorrow we bury her. And then everything can go back to normal."

She kissed Elodie's forehead, rose, and banked the candles, leaving the room in a red dusk.

"One thing," Maren added from the doorway, not turning around. "When you go up to bed—she kept your old room just as it was. But I've put you in hers. You'll sleep better there. She'd have wanted you surrounded by her things."

Elodie sat alone in the dark beside the stranger in the coffin, listening to her mother's footsteps climb the stairs, and felt the first cold thread of understanding slide through her, thin as water finding a crack in a wall.

Her old room, kept unchanged. And her, moved into Odile's.

As if something had already been decided about which sister slept where. As if the house—and the town, and the tide, and the capped iron well in the square—had all of them, patiently, for seventy years, been waiting to see which twin came home.

On the bedside table upstairs, she would find it in the morning: a dictaphone, and a shoebox of cassettes, labelled in handwriting she had known since before she could read her own name.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight there was only the candle-smoke, the sleeping watchers, and the drowned stranger smiling her borrowed smile up at Elodie from inside her sister's coffin—as if, somewhere beneath the undertaker's art, something was pleased.
