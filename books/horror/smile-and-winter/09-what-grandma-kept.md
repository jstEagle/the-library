## 9. What Grandma Kept

The attic stairs were the kind that folded out of the ceiling like a tongue, and they had always terrified Orsen, even as a child. Grandma used to stand at the bottom and say, *Go on up, birdie. The house won't let anything happen to you in its own attic.* Orsen had believed her then, the way children believe things—completely, without evidence, because believing was easier than the alternative.

Now she stood at the bottom of those stairs with a flashlight in one hand and her phone in the other, phone set to record, because Marisol had made her promise.

"If you find anything weird," Marisol had said, "you narrate it. Out loud. So I know you're okay."

"Define weird."

"Ines Ravensworth weird. Not normal-people weird."

Orsen pulled the cord. The stairs unfolded with a groan that sounded almost like complaint, like the house resented being opened in a place it kept private. Cold air came down and settled on her shoulders. It smelled up there the way attics always smell—dust and cedar and old paper—but underneath it there was something else, something she'd been smelling all week without naming it: the particular sweetness of a room where someone has sat for a very long time.

She climbed.

The flashlight found boxes first. Rows of them, labeled in grandma's handwriting—*Winter Coats*, *Christmas*, *Orsen's School Things*—and then labels that stopped making sense: *Calls 1961–63*, *Calls 1964–70*, *Calls: Unclassifiable*. A whole shelf of boxes devoted to calls. Grandma hadn't owned a radio show. Grandma had lived alone on a quiet street where the most exciting thing to happen in forty years was a mailbox knocked over by a delivery truck.

Orsen narrated anyway. "Found some boxes," she said to her phone. "Labeled weird. Still okay."

Her voice sounded small under the rafters.

She knelt and opened the nearest box. Inside were cassette tapes, hundreds of them, each one dated in pencil. And beneath the tapes, wrapped in a dish towel, journals. Dozens of journals, cloth-bound, their spines cracked into soft white seams. She lifted one out and the towel fell open around it like something being unwrapped after a long sleep.

The first page said: *For whichever Fairweather girl comes next.*

Orsen sat down on the floorboards. The house creaked once above her—a rafter settling, probably—and then went quiet in a way that felt less like silence and more like attention.

She read.

---

*March 4th, 1958. The telephone rang at 2 a.m. and I answered it because Mama taught me we always answer. A man asked me to play "Blue Moon" for his wife, except we don't have a radio station in this town and never have. He described his own funeral to me while I held the receiver. He sounded happy about the flowers. I did not sleep again until Sunday.*

*Mama says this is the gift. She says Fairweather women hear the recently dead, and that the hearing fades if we refuse it, and that refusing is the only mercy available to us. She refused for thirty years. She says her mother refused too. She says the chair comes for the ones who listen too long.*

*I asked what chair. She went pale and left the room.*

---

Orsen's hands had gone cold. She turned pages faster now, the flashlight trembling slightly, reading decades of entries that read like weather reports from a country that shouldn't exist.

*June 1971. The chair appeared in the kitchen photograph again. Third decade running. Harold says I'm imagining it. Harold has started sleeping in the spare room. He says it's his back.*

*August 1971. Harold moved out today. Before he left he stood in the kitchen doorway and looked at the corner by the stove, at nothing, for a long time, and then he said, "It watches me eat, Ruthie." His name wasn't Harold. I don't know why I wrote Harold. I can't make myself write his real name anymore. When I try, my hand stops.*

Orsen stopped breathing. *Ruthie.* Her grandmother's name had been Eleanor. She flipped back to the cover of the journal, checking, as if the name might be a mistake, a different woman entirely. But no—the handwriting was grandma's, the loopy, patient script Orsen knew from birthday cards and recipe cards. And yet every few entries, the names went wrong. *Harold* instead of whoever he really was. *Ruthie* instead of Eleanor. As if writing the truth about certain things cost something, and the hand protected itself by lying.

She kept reading.

*November 1973. The calls come more often now. They ask for songs. There is no station to play songs on, so I sing to the telephone. I have a voice like a screen door, but they don't seem to mind. Last night a woman asked me to sing the lullaby, both verses, and when I finished she said, "There's a third one, dear. You know there's a third one." I hung up. I have not hung up on a caller in fifteen years. My hands shook until morning.*

*December 1973. I found the sheet music in Mama's piano bench after the funeral. Two verses written out. The third marked do not sing along. I burned it. It came back. I buried it in the garden. It came back. I finally understood that it isn't a piece of paper. It's a door, and doors don't burn.*

*January 1974. I put it in the bench and locked the bench and gave away the key. If a Fairweather girl is meant to find it, she'll find it. That's how these things work. That's how everything in this family works. We are given exactly what we need exactly when we need it, and it is never what we want.*

---

Orsen lowered the journal. The attic was darker than it had been. Not dimmer—darker, as though the flashlight's circle had shrunk to accommodate what she was learning.

*A Fairweather girl is meant to find it.* She thought about the piano bench downstairs, unlocked for weeks now, sitting open like a mouth. She thought about humming two verses last Tuesday and watching the lights flicker in time, and telling herself it was the wiring.

She picked up another journal. Later years. The handwriting had begun to deteriorate, letters leaning like tired people.

*1986. Everyone is gone now. Every single one. I told myself for years that it was coincidence, that families scatter, that people die of ordinary things. But I kept a list, and the list keeps agreeing with itself: everyone who stayed near me while I listened to the dead has gone. Moved away. Stopped calling. Died early. The dead don't take us, birdie. They don't have to. They just wait, and the living get tired of competing with a telephone that rings for ghosts.*

*So I made a decision. I would be invisible. I would be nobody's warm room. I pushed everyone away before the waiting could push them. It worked. I am alone, and I am alive, and I hate that those are the same sentence.*

*But here is what I learned, and learn it fast, girl: the loneliness is not the price. The loneliness is the training. You cannot protect people from a thing that wants you unless you are already standing apart from them. Distance is armor. Coldness is armor. I built mine out of silence, and it held for fifty years.*

*It also cost me everything. Write that down twice.*

Orsen wrote it down twice. She didn't decide to; her hand simply found the pen in her jacket pocket and copied the sentence onto the back of a receipt, twice, in the dark, by feel. When she looked at what she'd written, the words seemed to belong to someone else—someone older, someone who had already spent years practicing distance without knowing why.

*You keep people at arm's length,* she thought. *You've done it your whole life. Dev invites you to things and you say maybe and never go. Marisol asks about your weekend and you give her the weather. Even Ruth—*

Even Ruth, who had hired her, who covered for her, who looked at her sometimes with an expression Orsen had never been able to name. Like recognition. Like grief wearing a manager's badge.

*Ruthie.*

The name from the journal surfaced again and Orsen's stomach dropped through the floorboards of herself. No. No, that was a coincidence. Lots of women named Ruth. Lots of grandmothers named Eleanor who wrote like—

*1998. A young woman came to town asking about overnight radio work. She had the look. The listening look. I told her about the old AM station on the edge of town, the one that can't quite die, and I told her my sister's name might still carry weight there. I have not been inside that building since 1979. I will never go inside that building. But the station needs a keeper, and the girl needed a place, and Fairweather women leave lighthouses behind them wherever we go.*

Orsen set the journal down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be sleeping.

Her grandmother had sent her to the station. Had known—had *known*—that the girl with the listening look would end up on the graveyard shift taking calls from the dead, because that was the inheritance, that was the whole shape of it, passed down like eye color. And Ruth—Ruth, whose sister had hosted overnights in 1979 and vanished, Ruth who had one rule and delivered it like scripture—Ruth had been grandma's sister. Was grandma's sister. Was possibly, impossibly, still alive and still keeping the lighthouse lit.

Orsen fumbled for her phone with shaking hands. The recording was still going. She stopped it, stared at the file, and didn't send it to Marisol. Some things you had to sit with first. Some things changed shape if you said them out loud too soon.

She read until the flashlight died.

By then she had learned the rest: that the chair appeared in photographs three decades before it appeared in kitchens. That the dead called the ones who listened, always politely, always with song requests, always patient, because patience was the one advantage the dead had in unlimited supply. That the last verse could not be destroyed, only declined, and that declining it was a full-time job lasting an entire life. That grandma had spent sixty years saying no, beautifully, exhaustively, alone.

And on the last written page, undated, in handwriting gone thin as thread:

*If you're reading this, I'm sorry. Not for leaving you the gift—you couldn't have escaped it, it's in the blood like the brown eyes. I'm sorry for the training. For teaching you coldness when I meant to teach you armor. For every birthday I kept short and every hug I ended first. I was keeping you at a distance from the thing that waits, and I mistook the distance for love, or love for distance, and now I don't remember which.*

*One more thing, and then I'll let you read in peace. The verse will come to you. It always comes. When it does, remember: a song sung alone is a summons. A song shared is a conversation. I never figured out what that means. Maybe you're smarter than me, birdie. God knows somebody in this family has to be.*

*Leave the lights on for someone.*

*—Grandma*

Orsen sat in the dark for a long time. Somewhere below her, the house ticked and settled, counting its rooms, keeping its lights on. Somewhere across town, a studio generator hummed through the winter night, holding a frequency open like a held breath.

And somewhere between those two places—in the space a phone signal occupies, in the static between stations—something waited, patient as winter, for her to finish learning a song.

She gathered the journals into her arms, all of them, decades of refusals, and carried them down the folding stairs. At the bottom she paused, looking back up into the dark attic, and heard—or imagined she heard, though she was long past pretending that distinction mattered—the faintest sound of a wooden chair being drawn back from a table.

Her phone buzzed. Marisol.

"You okay? You never sent the update."

"I'm fine," Orsen said. "I found grandma's journals."

A pause. "And?"

"And I need to talk to Ruth," Orsen said. "Tonight. Before my shift."

Another pause, longer this time, and when Marisol spoke again her voice had gone careful, the way voices go when someone has been waiting a long time for a question they hoped would never come.

"Orsen... Ruth already called here looking for *you*. Twenty minutes ago. She said—" Marisol's breath caught. "She said the tapes from 1979 just started playing in the archive room. By themselves. And Orsen—they're playing *your name*."
