## 19. Aftermath

The calls stopped on a Tuesday, which Ruth found fitting. Nothing about grief ever stopped on a Friday, when you had the weekend to absorb it. It stopped on Tuesdays, mid-shift, while you were doing something ordinary—restocking the coffee filters, arguing with the transmitter log—and then you had to sit there in the ordinary and realize the extraordinary had packed its bags without leaving a note.

She was in her office at 4:40 in the morning, three weeks after the broadcast, when she noticed the board. Orsen was on air doing her sign-off patter, the gentle voice she'd developed for the overnight crowd, and the phone lines—Ruth had them patched to her office the way she'd patched them for thirty years, a habit from the bad old nights—were dark. All four of them. Not quiet the way lines went quiet when nobody was calling. Quiet the way a room goes quiet after a party, when you can still feel the shape of everyone who'd been standing in it.

She waited through the news break. She waited through Orsen's first request, a living woman in Cedar Falls who wanted something by Fleetwood Mac for her husband's birthday. She waited through the second hour, the third.

Nothing.

She walked out to the studio and stood in the hallway where Orsen could see her through the glass, and Orsen gave her a look over the board—a look Ruth had learned to read the way sailors read weather. *You feel it too.*

Ruth nodded once and went back to her office, and that was when she let herself cry, quietly, into a coffee filter she'd pulled out for no reason she could name. Not because she was sad. That was the strange part. She cried the way you cry when a fever breaks—relief and exhaustion and the sudden, disorienting absence of a thing that had organized your whole life around it.

The dead were gone. Her sister was gone. And the station, this creaking lighthouse she'd kept lit for forty-six years, had finally, finally done its job.

* * *

The tapes lived in a closet off the archive room that nobody opened unless the furnace needed servicing. Ruth had labeled them herself, decades ago, in her own handwriting, back when her handwriting was still confident: *1979 — OVERNIGHT — DO NOT ERASE.* Below that, in smaller letters she'd added later, after she'd listened to all of them twice and then never again: *CALLERS UNVERIFIED.*

There were ninety-one tapes. She knew the number the way you know your own birthday.

She carried them out in two banker's boxes, and it took her most of the morning because she stopped at tape forty-seven and sat down on the archive room floor with her back against the shelf and listened to eleven seconds of her sister's voice.

"—and that's a request from a listener who says she's calling from the kitchen, and honey, I believe you, I believe every word—" A laugh, warm and unguarded, the laugh Ruth hadn't heard since 1979 except on this tape and its ninety brothers. "You're listening to the night watch with Delia Marsh, and it is 3:14 in the a.m., and the sky over this town is doing something I don't have words for."

Delia. Ruth said the name out loud, to the empty archive room, and the room did not answer, and that was the whole miracle.

For years she'd kept the tapes because erasing them felt like killing her sister twice. Then, after the calls started coming to Orsen's show—after she'd stood in the hallway listening to Orsen talk to people who had no business having phone service—she'd kept them as evidence. Proof she wasn't crazy. Proof the thing that took Delia was real, and patterned, and might be bargained with.

Now they were neither relic nor evidence. They were just recordings of a dead woman doing the best work of her life, and Ruth found that she could finally hear them as that. As radio. As her sister being good at the thing they'd both loved.

Marisol found her on the floor an hour later, tape forty-seven still spinning its silent reel.

"Ruth?" The kid crouched down, all elbows and worry. "You okay?"

"I'm retiring these," Ruth said. Her voice came out steadier than she'd expected. "The overnight archive. Pre-1980. I want them digitized first—all of them, every tape—and then I want the originals buried."

Marisol blinked. "Buried?"

"With Delia. It's where they belong. She made them; they should go where she went." Ruth stood up, knees cracking, forty-six years of overnight shifts announcing themselves in her joints. "You got a problem with that?"

"No," Marisol said, and then, because she was Marisol: "I can do the digitizing. I've been meaning to learn the archive deck anyway."

Ruth looked at her for a long moment. The kid had held the studio doors during the gathering. Had stood in the hallway with a fire extinguisher she had no plan for, because Dev had one and Orsen needed the hallway covered, and Marisol hadn't asked a single question about what the doors were for. She'd just stood there.

"You're going to be trouble someday," Ruth said. "Good trouble."

"I try."

"Digitize them. All ninety-one. And Marisol—" Ruth paused at the door. "When you get to tape ninety-one, you can skip it. It's just her singing."

Marisol's face did something careful. "The last verse?"

"The last verse." Ruth nodded. "She sang it live. That's how it works, that's how it worked. The song needs a voice that's living, and it needs to be given, not taken. She gave it." She looked at the boxes. "Orsen figured out the other half of it. That it can be given *back*. That they can give it to each other." She shook her head, half a laugh. "Forty-six years I ran this place and it took a twenty-six-year-old with insomnia to solve it."

"Orsen says it took all of us."

"Orsen is generous to a fault." Ruth picked up her coffee, cold now, and drank it anyway. "Digitize the tapes. And keep the copies somewhere that isn't this building. I don't ever want to lose her voice again, but I don't need it living in my walls, either."

* * *

They drove out to Fairhaven Cemetery on a Saturday in late October, in Ruth's Buick, with the heater rattling and Orsen in the passenger seat holding a paper sack of gas station coffee like it was a peace offering.

"You didn't have to come," Ruth said.

"You didn't have to invite me."

"I didn't. I said I was going, and you invited yourself."

Orsen smiled at the windshield. "That's how family works, I thought."

Ruth let that sit. The fields along Route 9 were the color of old rope, corn stubble and frost, and the sky was doing that big flat Midwestern thing where it went on forever in every direction. She'd driven this road ten thousand times. She'd driven it to this cemetery forty-six times—once a year, every October, on the anniversary—and every single time she'd sat at the grave and talked to a headstone and gotten nothing back, and every single time she'd driven home with the same splinter in her chest: *is she at peace, is she at peace, is she at peace, or is she still standing in that studio, singing, forever, because of me.*

Because Ruth had been the one who told her to take the overnight shift. Ruth had been the one who said, *you've got the voice for it, Dee, the night people need somebody.* Ruth had been the one who wasn't there the night the calls came from houses with no phones, the night her sister sang a lullaby she'd only half-learned to a congregation of the dead, and was gathered like wheat.

"Pull over up here," Orsen said suddenly. "There's something in the road."

Ruth slowed. A dog, maybe. But the road was empty, and Orsen was looking not at the road but at the field, and her face had gone the particular shade of still it went when the other world brushed up against this one.

"Orsen."

"I thought—" Orsen exhaled. "No. It's nothing. It's just—it's a good field. Somebody's standing in it. Or was." She shook her head. "I don't hear anything. I don't *hear* anything, Ruth. That's the thing. Three weeks and it's just been quiet, and I keep waiting for the quiet to be a trick."

Ruth pulled back onto the road. "And if it is a trick?"

"Then it's a kind one." Orsen looked out at the frost-bright fields. "Grandma's journal said the gift doesn't leave. It just goes dormant when there's nothing nearby that needs it. So either everyone's crossed over, or..." She didn't finish.

"Or the town's about to get interesting again," Ruth said. "Well. We've got a station. We'll keep the light on."

Fairhaven Cemetery sat on a low rise with a view of the water tower and, on clear days, the red blinking eye of the station's tower, eight miles east. Ruth had chosen the plot herself in 1980, against the family's wishes—they'd wanted Delia in the church cemetery, close to their mother—but Ruth had wanted her sister where she could see the tower. Where she could, in whatever way the dead see, keep listening.

The headstone said DELIA RUTH MARSH, 1951–1979, and beneath it, in letters Ruth had picked out at twenty-three years old and never once regretted: *SHE KEPT THE NIGHT COMPANY.*

Ruth stood at the grave with her coffee going cold in her hand and did what she always did, which was talk.

"Hey, Dee." Her voice cracked on the second word and she let it. "It's me. I brought company. This is Orsen—you've heard her, probably, if the dead get the AM band, which I have reason to believe they do." Orsen made a small sound beside her that was either a laugh or a sob. "She fixed it. What you did—I know you meant it kindly, I know you were trying to give them the song, and I've had forty-six years to be angry about it and I'm done, I'm done being angry, I just needed you to know it wasn't your fault. It was mine. I gave you the shift."

The wind moved through the cemetery grass with a sound like a long exhale.

"They're gone, Dee. All of them. The calls stopped three weeks ago. Orsen taught them the verse back—made *them* sing it to each other, can you believe that, the little genius—and one by one they said goodbye and crossed over. And you're gone too. I can feel it." Ruth pressed her palm flat against the headstone, cold granite, October cold, nothing else. "You're verifiably, boringly, wonderfully gone. There's no extra chair anywhere. Dev checked. He checks weekly. I think it's how he prays."

Orsen knelt and set something at the base of the stone. Ruth looked down: a cassette tape, in a clear case. Tape ninety-one.

"You said bury them with her," Orsen said. "I thought she should get the first one. I thought—" She stopped, started again. "I digitized it. I listened to the first two verses. I skipped the last one. I figured it was hers to sing, and nobody else's to hear."

Ruth looked at the kid kneeling at her sister's grave, holding a tape like an offering, and felt something in her chest that had been clenched since 1979 finally, fully, let go.

"Dig," she said, and handed Orsen the small trowel she'd brought for exactly this, because Ruth Marsh planned for everything, and always had.

They buried the tape together, six inches down, in the frozen ground, and Ruth said the words her mother used to say over graves—short, plain, no poetry, the Marsh family didn't hold with poetry at funerals—and when she finished, the wind stopped. Not faded. Stopped, all at once, the way a held breath releases.

Then it started again, ordinary, from a different direction.

"There," Orsen said quietly. "Did you feel that?"

"I felt it."

"That was her." Orsen stood, brushing dirt off her knees. "I can't hear her. But I felt her the way you feel a door close in another room. She's not here anymore. She's *through*." Her eyes were shining. "Ruth, she's through."

Ruth nodded and didn't trust herself to speak for a moment. Then: "Well. Let's go get warm. I'm eighty-one years old and I'm standing in a cemetery in October talking about doors."

"You're seventy-four."

"I'm eighty-one in spirit and my knees are ninety."

* * *

She gave Orsen the microphone in the studio, because it seemed right. Not in her office, with the door closed. On air, or near enough—during the last break of Orsen's shift, with the ON AIR light dark and the board humming its eternal hum.

The microphone had lived in a padded case in the bottom drawer of Ruth's desk since 1980. An RCA 77-DX, ribbon mic, the kind they didn't make anymore, the kind with a voice like warm honey poured over gravel. Delia had bought it secondhand with her first paycheck from the station. She'd named it, God help her. She'd called it *the Preacher.*

"Open it," Ruth said.

Orsen lifted the lid. She knew what it was—she was a radio kid, she'd know the shape of it blind—but she went still anyway, the way you go still in a church.

"Ruth."

"She'd have hated it in a drawer." Ruth kept her voice level, manager-voice, because the alternative was not level. "It's yours. The overnight shift comes with it. That's the succession, if you want it. Officially. In writing, if you want that too, though God knows why, the paperwork at this station is mostly fiction."

Orsen lifted the microphone out of the case with both hands, careful, the way you hold something sleeping.

"I can't—Ruth, this is her mic. This is the most important thing in this building."

"It's a microphone, honey. The important thing was the woman, and the woman is through." Ruth tapped the case. "She'd want it working. She'd want it in front of somebody who talks to the night people. And I'm retiring in the spring, and this station needs an overnight host who understands that the graveyard shift isn't a metaphor." She paused. "It's a lighthouse."

Orsen looked at her for a long time. Then she set the microphone on the boom arm, fitted it into the clip, and swung it into position in front of the chair—the chair, the one at the center of the board, the one Ruth had never let anyone else sit in, not in forty-six years.

"Can I?" Orsen asked.

"It's your chair," Ruth said. "It's been your chair since the night you talked down that man on the bridge and didn't even know you'd done it."

Orsen sat. She pulled the microphone close, and Ruth watched her do the thing Delia used to do—tap it twice, listen to the thump through the headphones, adjust the angle a quarter inch. The same gesture. It shouldn't have meant anything. It meant everything.

"Test, test," Orsen said, soft, just into the dead air of an empty studio. "This is the night watch. We're on the air, and we're staying on the air, and the light is on."

The board hummed. The tower light blinked red out in the dark, eight miles of nothing between here and the water tower, and Ruth stood in the doorway of her sister's old studio and watched her sister's microphone catch her successor's voice, and thought: *there. That's the whole job. That's all it ever was.*

"One more thing," she said, because she was Ruth Marsh and there was always one more thing. "House rule. New one. I'm adding it to the list under Ruth's rules, right after *never put a dead caller on air live.*"

Orsen turned in the chair. "What's the new rule?"

Ruth looked at the board, at the four phone lines, dark and patient. At the ON AIR light waiting to wake up.

"Keep one chair empty," she said. "Always. Not for the dead—they've got where they're going now. For whoever comes next. Because somebody always comes next, Orsen. Somebody always calls. And this station—" she tapped the doorframe, the old wood, the forty-six years soaked into it, "—this station answers."

Orsen smiled, and it was Delia's smile, and Ruth had to look away for a second at the window where the frost was climbing the glass.

That was when line one lit up.

They both went still. Three weeks of silence. Three weeks of dark lines. The light glowed a soft, patient red, and the board hissed with the sound of an open line and nobody speaking—yet.

Orsen's hand moved toward the fader and stopped. She looked at Ruth. Ruth looked at the light.

It blinked twice, like a tower in the distance. Like something signaling across eight miles of dark.

"Ruth," Orsen said quietly, "it's not ringing. It's just... open."

Ruth leaned closer, and through the hiss, faint as frost forming, came a sound. Not a voice. Not yet.

Someone, on the other end of the night, was humming.
