## 21. Staying Visible

The morning after the burial, Ansel woke in Nystrom House and the frost was gone.

Not melted — gone. The windows were clear as if they'd never known winter, and when he pressed his palm to the glass it was cool but ordinary, the temperature of any window on a mild October morning. He stood there for a long time in his grandmother's old dressing gown, waiting for the ferns to come back, for the letters to crawl up from the sills, for the house to remember what it wanted from him.

Nothing came.

Downstairs, the kettle behaved like a kettle. The doors he'd stopped bothering to close stayed shut overnight. The cellar door — which had stood ajar for weeks no matter how many times he latched it — was closed, and when he opened it out of something between duty and curiosity, the stairs went down into plain darkness that smelled of coal dust and old potatoes and nothing else at all.

"Right," he said to the dark. "Thank you."

The dark did not answer. It occurred to him, standing on the landing with his hand on the rail, that this was the first time in his life silence had felt like a courtesy rather than an ambush.

He made tea. He drank it standing at the back door, looking out at a garden gone wild with late bindweed, and he thought: I could leave. The bus still ran on Thursdays. His flat in the city was sublet to a woman who watered his one plant and asked no questions; he could have it back by November. There was nothing keeping him now except a grave with the right name on it and a house that had finally, after twenty years of rehearsal noises and frost-written demands, gone quiet.

Except that wasn't quite true either, and he knew it before the tea was half cold.

What kept him was a list, and the list looked like this: Idris Vale's garden, where the last of the runner beans hung like small green pendulums. Wren's archive, which was technically a shed and spiritually a cathedral. Maud's kitchen, where the toast always came out somehow both burnt and underdone and nobody minded. Sam, who had promised to teach him how to check ground before you trusted it, "a skill," Sam said, "that transfers."

Ansel rinsed his mug. He got dressed. He walked down the lane to Idris's gate, and Idris looked up from the bean row with dirt on her knuckles and an expression she tried very hard to keep neutral and failed at completely.

"Oh good," she said. "You're still here."

"I'm still here."

"For now." She snapped a bean off its stem and handed it to him like a test. "People say that the morning after a funeral. Then Thursday comes, and the bus comes, and so do they."

Ansel ate the bean raw, because it seemed important not to waste time on ceremony. "I don't have anywhere to be," he said. "That's not the same as having nowhere I want to be. I want to be here. I just — haven't worked out how to say it without it sounding like a eulogy for my old life."

Idris studied him for a moment. Then she pointed at the compost heap, then at a fork leaning against the fence, then at him.

"Turn that," she said. "And while you're turning it, say it however it comes out. Compost doesn't judge."

So Ansel turned the compost and told Idris Vale, in fits and starts, over the smell of rotting apples and the industrious outrage of disturbed worms, that he would like to stay in Hollowmere. Not hide in it. Not serve a sentence in it. Stay. He said it badly, with pauses, and once he had to stop entirely because he turned up a forkful containing a hedgehog and had to negotiate its relocation with great care while Idris watched with barely concealed delight.

"You know," she said, when the hedgehog had been escorted to the woodpile, "most people propose with less effort than that."

Ansel straightened up, aching pleasantly across his whole back. "Was that a proposal?"

"It was a compost heap. But I'll take it."

* * *

Wren took the news the way Wren took everything: as an archival matter.

"You'll need to sign things," she said, in the shed, surrounded by boxes labeled in her small fierce handwriting — ST. BRANNOC'S (MELTDOWN), NYSTROM (DEBT), BELLS: GHOSTS OF, MISCELLANEOUS HAUNTINGS, LOCAL. "Well. You won't need to. But you should. If you're going to live here, you should be in the record properly. That's sort of the whole point of what we did, isn't it? Names in their right places."

She pulled out a fresh folder and wrote ANSEL NYSTROM — RESIDENT on the tab, and then, after a moment, crossed out RESIDENT and wrote KEEPER, and then crossed that out too, embarrassed, and left it blank.

"What do you want it to say?" she asked.

Ansel looked at the blank space. Twenty years ago — or rather, twenty years ago on paper, in a coffin under a name that wasn't being used yet — he couldn't have answered that question. He hadn't been able to answer most questions about himself. He'd been a man shaped like a placeholder.

"Friend," he said. "Of the archive. And consultant. On bells."

Wren lit up like a struck match. "Consultant. Yes. Because you hear them. You'll always hear them." She said it without pity, which was why he could bear it. "Do you hear one now?"

He listened. This was becoming a habit, the way other people checked the weather. Somewhere far off — farther than Hollowmere, farther than anywhere — a bell was ringing, slow and kind, the way a lighthouse is kind. It had rung all through Tamsin's burial, and he was fairly sure nobody else had heard it, and he was fairly sure it had been saying what it always said now, which was: found, found, found.

"One," he said. "Far away. A good one."

Wren wrote CONSULTANT, RE: AUDIBLE PHENOMENA in the blank space, and shut the folder with the satisfaction of a woman closing a door on a well-organized room.

* * *

Maud's kitchen smelled of bread that had given up on rising and decided to be a scone instead. Maud herself sat at the table shelling peas with the speed and violence of someone who had been doing it since before the war and resented every pea individually.

"So," she said, when Ansel had been seated and supplied with tea. "You're staying."

"How does everyone already know? I decided this morning."

"Idris told Wren, Wren told Sam, Sam told me while digging Mrs. Pettifer's border for her bulbs, and Mrs. Pettifer told the postman, who tells everyone, including people who've moved away." Maud shrugged. "In Hollowmere, news travels faster than sound. I'd know. I can't hear sound."

Ansel laughed, and Maud allowed herself the ghost of a smile, and then put down the peas, which meant business.

"Fifty years I kept quiet about that clapper," she said. "Fifty years I read lips through floorboards and kept my mouth shut, because that's what peace cost, and I paid it, and I hated paying it, and I'd pay it again if it were those children on the line instead of mine." She tapped the table. "But here's what I learned, boy, and you learn it too: keeping a secret isn't the same as keeping a family. I kept the secret and lost fifty years of Sundays. Don't you do the same. Whatever you're carrying now — the hearing, the remembering, all of it — you bring it to this table. We'll listen. Well. They'll listen. I'll watch."

"Maud—"

"And another thing." She pushed the bread-scone-thing toward him. "Thursdays you eat here. That's not negotiable. Grief keeps better with regular meals, and so do you, you look like a coat hanger wearing a cardigan."

Ansel ate the bread. It was terrible. He had two more pieces.

* * *

Sam found him at the churchyard a few days later, sitting on the low wall beside Tamsin's grave, reading aloud from her diaries. He'd taken to doing this in the evenings — finishing the sentences she'd left unfinished, filling in the endings she'd never got to write. It wasn't healthy, probably. Wren had opinions. But the grave was warm in the late sun and the headstone said TAM SIN ASHLEY NYSTROM, BELOVED TWIN, and every time he read it he felt the rightness of it click somewhere in his chest, like a bone setting.

"Ground's settled nicely," Sam said, arriving with the particular gait of a man whose knees had opinions about churchyards. "Good earth over her. Clean. No frost in it at all." He sat down on the wall with a grunt. "First grave I've dug in thirty years where I didn't have to check twice."

"Thank you for checking anyway."

"That's the job." Sam picked up a clod of earth and crumbled it absently. "You know what I like about you, Nystrom? Nothing personal. Just — you're the first person in this village who ever thanked me for the checking."

They sat in silence for a while, the comfortable kind, the kind Ansel had spent forty years not knowing existed.

"The house," Sam said eventually. "It's different. I walk past your gate now and it's just a house. Ugly, mind. Your family built like they were angry at angles. But just a house." He glanced sideways. "You feel that too?"

"I feel like it's not watching me anymore." Ansel turned his mug-less hands over in his lap. "Or — no. That's not right. It's still paying attention. It's just not hungry."

"Houses are like dogs," Sam said. "They can tell the difference between a guest and a keeper. Yours spent twenty years thinking it had a corpse in it. Now it knows it hasn't." He stood, knees cracking like green wood. "Come by the yard sometime. I'll show you how to read soil. It transfers."

"You said that about the ground-checking."

"It transfers to everything. Soil, people, soup. Come by."

* * *

October deepened. Ansel learned the shape of his days, and the shape surprised him by being good.

Mornings: the house, which he had begun, tentatively, to call home without flinching. He fixed the loose stair. He oiled hinges that had shrieked for decades. In the cellar, which was only a cellar now, he found his grandmother's old seed packets and planted them in the garden in defiance of the season, because Idris said autumn planting was for optimists and he had decided, provisionally, to be one.

Midday: the village. Wren's shed on Tuesdays, cataloguing. Maud's kitchen on Thursdays, eating crimes against bread. Idris's garden whenever the light was good, which was also to say: whenever Idris was in it, which Ansel noted with the quiet, astonished happiness of a man who had spent his whole life believing he was the sort of person things happened near, not to.

Evenings: the churchyard wall, the diaries, the far-off bell. Found, found, found.

The hearing never left him. On some nights it was a comfort and on some nights it was a weight — a bell tolling in a town three counties away when a miner's son came home from the sea; a bell in a city he'd never visited, ringing for a stranger's wedding; once, unbearably, a school bell, and under it a child crying, and Ansel had lain awake until dawn hoping, with his whole chest, that someone had heard the child too. He was, he understood now, permanently tuned. The magic had kept its receipt, and the receipt read: you will hear, always, everywhere.

But receipts, he was learning, could be lived with. Maud had lived with deafness fifty years and made it a country with its own customs. He could make a home in the hearing. He was making one.

* * *

The first hard frost of the year came in early November, and Ansel woke to find the windows white again.

His heart did something complicated — a full somersault of old terror, followed by a slower, stranger feeling underneath it, almost like greeting. He got up barefoot and crossed the cold floor to the bedroom window, breath fogging, and looked at what the frost had written.

One word. Small, neat, positioned dead center in the pane like a note left on a kitchen table:

STAY

Not ANSEL. Not TAM SIN crossed out. No countdown, no demand, no hour struck off toward some deadline. Just the word, and around it the ordinary ferns of ordinary frost, decorative, harmless, almost cheerful.

Ansel stood there a long time. Then he laughed — a short, wet, surprised laugh — and pressed his finger to the glass and traced beneath the word, slowly, so the frost could see it done properly:

STAYING.

The frost didn't answer. Houses don't. But when he went downstairs, every door in the house stood open — every single one, top to bottom, the way a dog leaves every door open behind it when it wants you to follow — and the cellar door, for the first time since he'd arrived, stood open too, showing nothing below but clean dark steps and, on the landing, his grandmother's seed packets arranged in a tidy row that he was nearly certain he hadn't left that way.

"All right," Ansel told the house, pulling on his coat. "All right. I'm coming back. I always come back now. You can stop leaving the lights on."

There were no lights to leave on. But he felt, distinctly, the house settle — a long exhale through sixty years of joists — and outside, far beyond Hollowmere, farther than anywhere, a bell rang once, kindly, like a nod.

* * *

On Sunday, Idris came to Nystrom House with a trowel in one pocket and a bottle of elderberry wine in each hand, and announced that the east bed was a disgrace and she intended to disgrace it further, and did he have gloves, and if not she'd brought spares, and also she was staying for dinner, and also — here she paused, in the doorway, backlit by grey-gold light, dirt already on her despite having touched nothing — "also, Wren found something in the founder's ledger. From 1911. She says it's about the bell's second name. The one it kept even after the melting." Her eyes were bright and slightly worried. "She says it's not finished naming things. She says there's a page torn out, and the tear is recent."

Ansel looked past her, down the hall, to the cellar door standing open at the end of it, patient as a held breath.

"Gloves are by the back door," he said. "Tell Wren we'll want the bad tea. It's going to be a long evening."
