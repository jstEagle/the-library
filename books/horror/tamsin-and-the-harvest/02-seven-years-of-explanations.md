## 2. Seven Years of Explanations

The taxonomy lives in a green Moleskine with a cracked spine, and I have been faithful to it the way other people are faithful to churches.

*Category One: Hydraulic.* Water moving through pipe joints, air displaced ahead of it, the wet percussion of a drip finding its rhythm. This category has absorbed roughly forty percent of everything I've ever heard down there, and I trust it the way you trust a dog that's never once bitten you.

*Category Two: Structural.* Settlement, thermal expansion, rebar singing under load shifts, the town above shrugging in its sleep. Another thirty percent.

*Category Three: Faunal.* Rats, raccoons, a truly ambitious opossum I once met face to face near outfall seven and will defend to my grave as having been more frightened than me, regardless of what the audio suggests.

*Category Four: Mechanical.* Pumps at the treatment plant two miles east. Old elevator machinery from buildings that no longer have elevators. Ghost signals in the municipal grid.

*Category Five:* — and here the pen always hesitates, hovering over the page like a man on a diving board who has suddenly remembered he can't swim —

*Category Five: Unresolved.*

There are eleven entries under Category Five. I have never once written the word *voice*. I write *tonal anomaly*, *patterned acoustic event*, *non-random sequence*. I am a cartographer of things I refuse to name, and I tell myself this is rigor. I tell myself this is science. Mostly, at three in the morning, with my headphones on and my heart going like a trapped bird, I tell myself whatever works.

Tonight the notebook is open on my kitchen table next to a bowl of cereal I poured at midnight and forgot to eat until it achieved the consistency of pond silt. The recorder rig hums on the counter where it always hums, charging, patient, doing its job better than I ever asked it to. And because tonight is a bad night — one of the nights when the taxonomy feels less like armor and more like a joke I've been telling for so long I've forgotten the punchline — I do what I always do on bad nights.

I go back to the beginning.

* * *

I was twenty-three, and I want to be clear that twenty-three is not an age at which a person should be trusted alone with a headlamp and a problem.

It was October. I'd been in Harrow's Bend four months, subletting a drafty apartment above a nail salon from a woman named Deb who left passive-aggressive notes about my boots tracking quarry dust up her stairs. I'd come for the water, if you can believe that. There was a hydrology angle to my thesis — how abandoned extraction sites reshape regional drainage over decades — and Harrow's Bend was a textbook case: a town that had spent sixty years pretending the quarry wasn't there while every storm drain in the county quietly drained into it.

The first night I went down, I mapped two hundred meters of storm tunnel and heard nothing but my own breathing amplified into something embarrassing. The second night, same. It was the third night that I found the junction where the 1950s concrete gave way to brickwork that predated any municipal record, and the brickwork ran off the edge of my map like a sentence someone stopped writing mid-word.

And in that brick throat, faintly, there was a sound.

I have replayed it so many times since that I no longer know if I'm remembering the sound or the recording of the sound or the seventeen analyses of the recording, the way you can wear a photograph out by looking at it until all that's left is the looking. But what I wrote in my field notes that night, in handwriting already too fast, was: *low tonal event, approx. 40–60 Hz, duration irregular, source indeterminate. Possibly pump resonance.*

Possibly pump resonance. At one in the morning. In a sealed tunnel. In a town whose pumps shut off at eleven.

I stood in that brick corridor for forty minutes telling myself *possibly pump resonance*, and then I climbed out into the cold and drove home and did not sleep, and somewhere around dawn I understood — with the specific clarity that only arrives alongside total exhaustion — that I would be going back tomorrow night.

That's the part nobody warns you about. Not the fear. Fear is easy; fear is just your body voting no. What nobody warns you about is the pull. That some sounds get into you the way water gets into limestone, patiently, along the seams, until one day the whole structure is mostly cavity and you're calling it character.

* * *

For the first year, it was almost healthy. I want that on the record.

I built systems. Spreadsheets. A naming convention for audio files so rigorous it could have survived an audit. I cross-referenced my recordings against the municipal pumping schedule, against weather data, against train timetables from a line that hadn't run freight since 1987. Every anomalous sound got logged, timestamped, spectrographed, and assigned a provisional rational cause with a confidence percentage. I was very proud of those confidence percentages. They were little numbers standing guard between me and the dark, and they said things like *87% probable: settling earth* and I believed them the way you believe a nightlight.

My sister called on Sundays back then. Priya, three years older, living in Columbus, working a job she described as "corporate adjacent" in a tone that meant she hated it. She'd say, "So what did the tunnels say this week?" — teasing, because early on I'd made the mistake of being honest with her, of telling her about the tonal event, and she'd laughed and said, "Ros, you're describing a haunted drain," and I'd laughed too, because it *was* funny, because we were siblings and laughing at each other was our native language.

"Eighty-seven percent pump resonance," I'd say.

"And the other thirteen percent?"

"The other thirteen percent is margin of error."

"Uh-huh." I could hear her smiling through the phone. "You know Dad did this. Not the tunnels. The — finding one thing and making it the whole room." A pause, the kind she used instead of saying *after Mom*. "Just. Come visit sometime. Bring the notebook. We'll put it in a drawer and watch terrible movies."

"I'll come after the survey," I said.

That was the trick of it, see. There was always an *after the survey*. The survey was never done because the tunnels were never done, because every answer opened two more passages on the map, literally and otherwise. I told myself I was dedicated. Dedicated people miss calls sometimes. Dedicated people reschedule. The word *dedicated* is a coat with very deep pockets, and I kept putting things in them — birthdays, holidays, a funeral in Columbus I attended by sending flowers and a card that said *Wish I could be there, deep in a deadline* — and the coat never got heavy because I never had to carry it. The obsession carried itself. All I had to do was keep walking.

Year two, Priya started leaving voicemails instead of expecting me to pick up. Year three, the voicemails got shorter. Year four, there was one that I still have saved, though I've only played it twice, both times drunk, both times regretting it:

*"Hey. It's Sunday. Obviously. Look — Marco and I are doing the thing in June, the backyard thing, and I know you'll say survey, so I'm not even asking, I'm just — Ros, I looked at the calendar and realized I couldn't remember the last time I actually talked to you. Like, talked. Not texted a map emoji. And that scared me more than your haunted drain stuff ever did, honestly. So. Call me. Or don't, and I'll call again, because that's what sisters do, apparently, even the ones who are tired. Bye."*

She didn't call again after June. Or rather — she called twice more, and I let both go to voicemail because I was underground when they came in, because I was *always* underground when they came in, because the thing below makes its best sounds between ten p.m. and four a.m. and I had long ago organized my entire life around being available for it.

Available for it. That's a phrase I only wrote down last month, and when I did, I sat very still for a while.

Because here's what I've started to wonder, in the small hours, in the space between Category Four and Category Five: seven years is a long time to spend listening to something. Seven years is a lot of attention. And attention is a resource, isn't it? It's the only thing we actually spend. You can give it to a sister on Sundays, or you can give it to a hole in the ground, and the hole doesn't thank you, but it also doesn't stop wanting it.

I'm aware of how that sounds. I'm aware that a rational person reads that paragraph and says, *Rosalind, you're describing guilt, not geology.* I know. I built the taxonomy precisely so I wouldn't have to have this conversation with myself. But the conversation keeps happening anyway, lately, in a register I don't quite recognize, and I keep catching myself answering questions I don't remember being asked.

More on that later. Or hopefully never.

* * *

Present tense again. Kitchen table. Soggy cereal. The green notebook open to Category Five, entry eleven, which I transcribed last week and have not been able to close the book on since.

Entry eleven is different from the others. Entries one through ten were sounds — tones, patterns, sequences that could be argued with. Entry eleven, recorded on the night of the fourteenth at 3:12 a.m., captured by the new sleep rig I installed specifically to monitor whether I talk in my sleep (a diagnostic project, I told myself; insomnia research; totally normal), contains the following:

Me, asleep, audibly asleep, breath slow, voice slack: *"What's under the bend?"*

And then — and I have listened to these four seconds maybe two hundred times, and each time my hands do the same cold thing — a second voice answers. Faint. Distant. Coming from somewhere in the apartment that the microphone shouldn't reach. And the second voice is mine. Not similar. Mine. My exact timbre, my exact cadence, the way I swallow before I say something I care about.

It says: *"You already know."*

I have run the analysis six ways. I have checked for bleed-through, for echo, for the recorder picking up my own playback. The waveform doesn't match anything in the room. The second voice comes from nowhere and everywhere and it uses my mouth's whole biography.

Here is the part I haven't written down anywhere until now, the part I've been circling for days like something dead in the water: I don't remember asking the question. But I've wanted to ask it. For seven years, *what's under the bend?* has been the question sitting at the bottom of every survey, every spreadsheet, every confidence percentage — the question the whole taxonomy exists to avoid asking out loud.

Something answered it anyway. Something answered it in my own voice, which means either my sleeping mind invented the reply — plausible, insomnia does strange things, I have a whole sub-category for this, *Category Five, subsection: artifacts of the instrument* — or something down there has been listening to me listen for seven years, cataloguing me the way I catalogued it, learning my voice the way I learned its frequencies.

Seven years of explanations. That's what I called this chapter of my life, back when chapters were things that happened to other people. Seven years of hydraulic, structural, faunal, mechanical. Seven years of *possibly pump resonance* at one in the morning.

And now I sit at my kitchen table with the notebook open and I understand, finally, with the clarity of the completely exhausted, that the taxonomy was never a map of the sounds.

It was a map of what I was willing to lose. And I have lost nearly all of it. Sister, friends, Deb's tolerance, the ability to sit through a movie without wondering what the pipes are saying. I gave it all to the listening, pocket by pocket, and I told myself it was dedication, and the coat never got heavy because I never once set it down.

The recorder light blinks green on the counter. Charging. Patient.

Tomorrow night I'm going back down. Not for the thesis. Not for the survey. There's nothing left to be dedicated to except the question itself, and I am done pretending I don't know where it lives.

There's a tunnel on my hand-drawn map that matches nothing in any archive. Brickwork older than the town, running off the edge of the page like a sentence someone stopped writing mid-word.

I think I'm finally ready to find out who stopped writing it — and what they were trying not to say.
