## 5. The Founders' Ledger

The Harrow County Records Annex occupied the ground floor of a building that had been a bank, then a feed store, then a bank again, and had finally given up on commerce altogether in favor of climate-controlled indifference. It smelled of toner and old paper and the particular despair of microfiche machines that had outlived their manufacturer's warranty by three decades.

I'd been there before, plenty of times. Seven years of mapping tunnels will send you to an archive eventually, if only to confirm that the tunnels aren't supposed to exist. I had a system for it. Folder one: municipal infrastructure, 1911 to present. Folder two: quarry operations, which had drowned along with half the town when the river was dammed in '38. Folder three: everything else, which is where hope went to die quietly.

I knew the drill. Sign the log — *R. Eriksen, purpose of visit: personal research* — take the cotton gloves from the box at the desk like a surgeon preparing for a very boring operation, and settle in among the boxes nobody had requested since the Nixon administration.

What I didn't expect was the archivist.

"Looking for something specific?" she asked. She was maybe sixty, cardigan the color of oatmeal, reading glasses on a chain around her neck like she expected them to bolt.

"Founding families," I said. "Harrow's Bend. Land grants, incorporation papers, anything pre-dam."

Her eyebrows did something complicated. "You're the second person this month."

I should have paid attention to that sentence. I want it on the record that I should have paid attention to that sentence. Instead I said, "Oh? Who was the first?"

"Girl." She said it the way you'd say *weather event*. "Barefoot. Came in wearing a coat that looked like it lost a fight with a woodchipper. Asked for the same thing, then got angry when I told her we don't let people handle documents without gloves." She shook her head. "Left before I finished the sentence."

Tamsin. It had to be. The girl from the drainage cut, the one who moved through the dark like it owed her money, who spoke in half-threats and half-scripture. She'd been here. Asking about founders. Which meant whatever she was guarding down there had a paper trail, and paper trails were my native habitat.

"Did she find what she wanted?"

"She found nothing. Everything's indexed wrong." The archivist sighed with the weariness of a woman whose life was other people's bad filing. "The founding records were donated in 1962 by the Harrow family estate. Whoever catalogued them organized by *donor family*, not chronology or subject. So if you want the town charter, you need to know which family's box it ended up in. Nobody knows. It's a nightmare."

"I love a nightmare," I said, and meant it in a way that would have concerned a therapist.

She gave me the finding aid — four pages, typed on a machine that had clearly been having a bad decade — and pointed me toward the back room where the founder collections lived in gray archival boxes on metal shelving.

Four families. That was the thing I hadn't known, or hadn't cared to know: Harrow's Bend wasn't founded by a crowd. It was founded by four names, and every land deed, every water right, every grave plot in the original survey traced back to one of them. Harrow, obviously. Vane. Ostrander.

And Eriksen.

I stood there holding the finding aid for longer than a person should stand anywhere. Because Eriksen was my name. My father's name, his father's, a line I'd always assumed ran back through Minnesota wheat farms and before that across some cold sea to people who named their children like they were naming boats. I'd never once thought of it as a *local* name. The Bend was ninety minutes from the city, a place I'd discovered by accident at twenty-three, chasing a storm drain that turned out to be a throat. Coincidence. The world was full of coincidences. I had built an entire taxonomy of them.

But my hands were doing something without consulting me. They were pulling the ERIKSEN box off the shelf.

* * *

The box was thin. That was my first note, written later, because I took notes even then — especially then, because notes were how I kept the floor under my feet. Thin box, thin history. The Harrow box next to it held forty folders; mine held six.

Folder one: a land grant, 1849, two hundred acres along the river bend, signed by an Aldous Eriksen. His signature was confident, looping, the signature of a man who expected to be alive a long time.

Folder two: a bill of sale for quarry equipment, 1853.

Folder three: a letter, 1856, from Aldous Eriksen to Josiah Harrow, discussing drainage. I read it twice because the word *drainage* is basically my love language, and because something in the phrasing snagged. *"We are agreed the water must be given somewhere to go, and that what lies beneath must be given no cause to rise with it."* What lies beneath. Nineteenth-century men wrote like that about everything — geology, God, their neighbors' daughters — so I filed it under *florid* and moved on.

Folder four: a church record. Baptisms. Eleven Eriksen children across three generations, and here was the thing that made me sit down on the stool provided for exactly this purpose: every single entry had a marginal notation in a different hand, older ink, cramped and careful. Next to each name, the same phrase.

*Spoken for.*

Not *baptized*. Not *received*. Spoken for. Like the children were claims staked before they could walk.

Folder five: a deed transfer, 1901, the entire Eriksen acreage signed over to the town of Harrow's Bend for the sum of one dollar, "and consideration otherwise rendered, the nature of which is recorded elsewhere." Recorded elsewhere. In a ledger, as it happened. But I'm getting ahead of myself, the way I got ahead of myself that afternoon, because folder five rattled me enough that I almost stopped. Almost. You know me better than that by now.

Folder six was the last one, and it was nearly empty. One page, loose, not filed in any order. A single sheet of heavy rag paper, folded once, and when I opened it with gloved hands that were not entirely steady, I understood why the archivist had said the girl left angry. This was what she'd come for. Maybe she'd never found it. Maybe she already knew it by heart.

It was a ledger page. Just one — torn cleanly from a bound volume, the stub of it still visible at the top edge like a pulled tooth. And at the head of the page, in that same confident looping hand I'd seen on the 1849 land grant, in ink gone brown as old blood:

**The Arrangement.**

Below it, entries. Dated. Generations of dated entries, each in a different hand, each following the same formula. I copied them into my notebook exactly, because exactness felt like armor:

*Renewed this day by Aldous Eriksen, keeper, who gives his name and his line's keeping, that the town may keep its harvest. Witnessed: J. Harrow, T. Vane, M. Ostrander.*

Then, 1874: *Renewed by Prudence Eriksen, keeper, daughter of Aldous...*

1889: *Renewed by Silas Eriksen, keeper...*

1901 — the year of the dollar deed: *Renewed by Miriam Eriksen, keeper, who renders the land and keeps the name...*

And so on, down through the century, the handwriting changing, the formula never changing, until the final entry in a hand so recent it could have been yesterday:

*Renewed by Edith Eriksen, keeper, who gives her name and her line's keeping.*

Edith. My grandmother's name. My grandmother, who died when I was nine, who I remembered mostly as a smell of lavender and woodsmoke and a habit of going quiet whenever anyone mentioned the old towns upriver. My grandmother, who had grown up — I now realized, with the particular vertigo of a floor giving way in slow motion — ninety minutes from where I sat.

Keeper. The word sat on the page like a stone on a chest.

* * *

Here's what I did, because I am who I am: I photographed the page. Front, back, raking light at an angle to catch indentations from the sheet above it. Then I asked the archivist, very casually, whether there was a companion volume — ledgers usually came in sets, and this page had been torn from something.

"There's a Harrow family ledger in that box," she said. "Complete. Why?"

Because I needed to see if the Harrows' book matched. Because if the Eriksen page was unique, it might be a forgery, a joke, a mad ancestor's fantasy. But if the Harrow ledger said the same thing —

The Harrow ledger was a fat volume bound in cracked leather, and it opened to a ribbon bookmark someone had placed decades ago, and the entry under the ribbon read:

*Renewed this day by Abigail Harrow, keeper, who gives her name and her line's keeping, that the town may keep its harvest. Witnessed: E. Eriksen, R. Vane, S. Ostrander.*

Four families. Four keepers. Or — no. Reading closer, cross-referencing the dates against the torn page, the pattern emerged ugly and clear: four names entered every generation, but only one name per generation held the title. The others witnessed. The rotation moved — Harrow, then Vane, then Ostrander, then Eriksen — but it always came around. And the Eriksen entries, when I tracked them through the Harrow volume and the Vane papers and the Ostrander correspondence, were always the fullest. Always the longest entries. Always, after 1901, written by the keeper alone, because the Eriksens had sold the land and moved away and yet somehow, impossibly, the entries continued. Dates in cities I recognized. Dates in the hospital where my father was born. A renewal entered in 1987 in handwriting I would have known anywhere, because I'd seen it on birthday cards: Edith Eriksen, keeper.

My grandmother had been renewing *the arrangement* from four hundred miles away. Whatever the arrangement was, distance hadn't dissolved it. Distance hadn't even inconvenienced it.

And the last entry in the Harrow ledger, the one after Edith's, was blank. Waiting. A ruled line with nothing on it, and beneath the line, in pencil, faint, someone — Edith? — had written a single word:

*Unrenewed.*

Underneath that, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the paper: *the listener will hear it themselves.*

I closed the ledger. I sat with my hands flat on the table and breathed the way the internet says you're supposed to breathe, four counts in, seven counts hold, eight counts out, and none of it helped, because the problem wasn't my nervous system. The problem was arithmetic.

Seven years ago, at twenty-three, I had wandered into Harrow's Bend on a whim and a wrong turn, and I had heard the first sound in the drains — the sound I'd spent seven years refusing to name — and I had come back the next night. And the next. I had built maps and taxonomies and checklists. I had listened. Every night, listening, the way you'd keep a vigil, the way you'd answer a call.

*The listener will hear it themselves.*

I wasn't a visitor who'd stumbled onto something. I was the great-grandchild of a keeper, returning to a post my family had held for a hundred and fifty years, walking straight down into the dark with a flashlight and a notebook like the world's most overqualified new hire. The unmapped tunnel on my map — the one that matched no archive — matched *this* archive. It matched a ledger. It matched a marginal note beside eleven baptized children: spoken for.

The girl in the drainage cut had looked at me like she knew me. I'd filed that under *hostile local*, another rational category, another box. But she hadn't looked at me like a stranger. She'd looked at me like a replacement. Like relief and dread arriving in the same face.

I returned the boxes. I signed the log out — *R. Eriksen, purpose of visit: personal research*, and for the first time in seven years the phrase wasn't a polite fiction. It was the truest thing I'd written all year.

Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of old paper. I sat in my car in the annex lot and called my sister, because apparently horror makes me sentimental, and because there was a question I suddenly needed answered more than sleep.

Voicemail. Of course voicemail. Seven years of unreturned calls had earned me voicemail.

"Mara," I said. "Weird question. Did Grandma Edith ever — I don't know. Say anything about Harrow's Bend? About keeping something? About—" I heard myself and stopped, because the sentence sounded insane even to me, and I was the one who talked to drains. "Just call me back. Please. It's about the family."

I hung up. And in the silence of the car, in the space between the engine ticking cool and my own breath, I heard it — faint, patient, coming from nowhere and everywhere, from the vent seams and the storm grate by my front tire and possibly from inside my own skull, where it had every legal right to be:

A voice. My voice. Gentle as a librarian.

*"Now you understand,"* it said. *"Welcome home, keeper."*

I didn't answer. Chapter eleven rules, though I wouldn't learn them for another week: never answer it. I gripped the wheel and drove toward the Bend with the windows down and the radio loud, and behind me the archive settled into its climate-controlled indifference, holding one torn page and one blank line, waiting for a name.
