## 13. The Room of Names

Gran always said the chamber was the one place in the dark that belonged to us, and she said it the way you'd say *this house is your home* to a child while nailing the windows shut.

I'd been doing rounds since I was six. Feed at the low junction, recite the names at the brick door, walk the perimeter where the water sings, come back before the thing below finishes deciding what it wants to be tonight. Thirty-one years my grandmother walked those rounds before her knees gave out, and thirty-one years before that, her mother, and so on backward into the ledger paper until the ink gives up pretending it was ever about anything but keeping.

The listener — Rosalind — followed me through the flooded cut with their lamp held high like a man walking into church he didn't believe in. They'd been down here three nights running now, since the collapse sealed the mouth, and they'd stopped flinching at the voices, which worried me more than the screaming had. A person who flinches still believes the dark is wrong. A person who stops flinching is starting to negotiate.

"Where are we going?" they asked. Their voice echoed wrong down here. Everything echoes wrong down here; the tunnels take your words and hand them back a half-beat late, like a child repeating what you said to be annoying.

"You wanted to know what a keeper keeps," I said. "So."

"I didn't ask that."

"You've been asking it for seven years." I didn't look back at them. "You just asked it with instruments instead of words. Same question. Worse manners."

They shut up after that, which I'll admit surprised me. Most people, when you tell them the truth about themselves, get loud about it.

* * *

The keeper's chamber sits past the third brick door, down a passage that narrows until you have to turn sideways, which is deliberate. The founders built everything down here twice: once for function and once for meaning, and the meaning was always *you don't get to leave easily*. The passage squeezes you the way a fist tests a fruit.

I went through first because it's mine, and then stood aside while Rosalind came through sideways, lamp first, breathing hard, and raised the light.

I've seen the wall every night of my life and it has never once gotten easier.

Niches. Hundreds of them, cut into the living rock in rows that climb up past where the lamplight reaches, each one the size of two cupped hands. And in each one, something. A hairbrush with silver bristles gone black. A pair of spectacles folded neat as a sleeping moth. A child's shoe. A pipe, a prayer book, a doll with one eye, a wedding veil folded into a square no bigger than a fist. Things that were loved by someone once, set into stone like relics, except nobody prays to these and nothing here is holy.

"What is this," Rosalind said, and it wasn't a question, it was the sound a person makes when the floor of their understanding gives way and they're falling toward a new one.

"The room of names," I said. "That's what Gran calls it. Each niche is a keeper. What's in the niche is theirs. Something they couldn't put down."

Rosalind lifted the lamp higher. The light climbed the wall and the wall kept going, niches on niches on niches, older ones crumbled at the edges, newer ones sharp-cut, and somewhere near the top — I knew without looking, I always know — the newest row, only three niches filled.

"How many?" Rosalind whispered.

"I stopped counting at two hundred. Gran made me stop. She said counting them was a way of asking how long, and asking how long is a way of telling it we're tired."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

I watched them move along the bottom row, reading the objects like a man reads headstones, and I let them, because there was something they needed to find and I'd known it since the night I first saw them in the drainage cut, moving through my dark with their little lights and their careful maps. I'd known it the way you know weather coming.

Rosalind stopped at a niche about chest height. Went very still.

Inside, on a bed of dust finer than flour, sat a ring. Gold band, worn thin on one side from years of turning, with a small dark stone that might have been garnet once and was now just the color of old blood.

"Eriksen," Rosalind read, though there was nothing to read. No plaque, no date. But they said it anyway, the way you say a name you've carried your whole life without knowing why. "That's — my family name. That's on the founding ledger. That's—"

"Yours," I said. "Or close enough. Founding families all kept keepers. Eriksen line ran the listening. Your people heard it first, that's what the ledger says. Heard it and answered."

Rosalind reached toward the ring and I caught their wrist, faster than I meant to, my fingers digging in harder than I meant to.

"Don't touch what's in a niche."

"Why? What happens if—"

"It remembers being held." I let go of them slowly. "Everything down here remembers. You want to put your hand on a hundred and fifty years of somebody's kept grief, go ahead, but do it knowing it'll put its hand on you back."

Rosalind looked at me like I'd spoken a language they almost knew. Then they pulled their lamp close to the niche and studied the ring without touching it, and I watched them do the arithmetic I'd already done — the dates, the ledger, the blood-name, seven years of listening in their own veins — and arrive somewhere terrible.

"Keepers don't die," Rosalind said quietly. It wasn't a question either. Nobody asks real questions down here; the dark answers them first. "Do they."

I could have lied. Gran lied to me for twenty years and called it love, and maybe it was, the way a cage is a kind of shelter.

"No," I said. "They don't die. They're kept."

* * *

I told them standing up, because if I sat down in this room I wasn't sure I'd stand again.

"A keeper serves until the thing below chooses the next one. That's the arrangement. That's what the ledger means by *renewed*. Somebody walks the rounds, feeds it, speaks the names, holds the invitation closed from the inside. And when it's time — when it picks — the old keeper doesn't get buried above ground. There's no grave. There's a niche."

"And the object—"

"Is what's left of them that the thing doesn't want." My voice came out flat and I was glad of it. Flat was armor. "It takes the voice. That's the payment, that's always been the payment — the founders paid in voices and it's never renegotiated. But it leaves the rest. The hands that brushed the hair, the eyes behind the spectacles, the feet in the shoe. It leaves those in the wall like change from a purchase."

Rosalind's lamp hand was shaking. I pretended not to see, because seeing would have been crueler than pretending.

"My grandmother," I said, "is dying right now, up in the house, in the bed by the window where she can watch the quarry fill with morning. Her heart's going. She's known for a year. And do you know what she told me last month?" I laughed and it came out ugly. "She told me she was proud. She said she'd held the line her whole term and now it was my turn and she'd finally get to rest."

"Rest," Rosalind repeated.

"There's no rest in that wall." I turned and looked up at the niches climbing into the black, all those small beloved things, all those hands and eyes and feet, kept like pressed flowers by something that eats voices. "There's no rest anywhere down here. She's been preparing me my whole life for a promotion that's a burial. She taught me the names, the rounds, the rules — never answer it, never give your true name, never accept what it offers — and she never once, not one single time, told me what happens at the end of a keeper's service. Because if she had, I'd have run at twelve. At eight. I'd have run before I could walk."

"Tamsin—"

"She loves me." I said it fast, before they could say anything soft. "That's the worst part, you understand? She loves me the way the wall loves things. Carefully. Forever. In a niche." My throat hurt. I made my voice flat again. "And I was going to do it. I was going to walk the rounds and feed it and speak the names and end up two cupped hands of dust and a hairbrush, because that's duty, that's what I was raised on, duty like a bone you're handed to chew."

Rosalind hadn't moved. In the lamplight their face had the look I'd started to recognize on them — the look of a person whose whole taxonomy of explanations has burned down and who's standing in the ash deciding whether to rebuild or to finally, finally believe their own eyes.

"You said the thing chose a replacement," they said. "For me. For my role on the surface."

"It did. That's how it works now, since your seven years of listening thinned the membrane. It needs a keeper below—" I touched my own collarbone, where the mark sat under my shirt, cold as a coin left out overnight, "—and a listener above. Two halves of the same hinge. It picked someone with your blood, Rosalind. Someone who shares your name and half your face and hasn't spoken to you in four years."

I watched that land. I watched it land like the collapse had landed, sealing the mouth.

"No," Rosalind said.

"It wears her voice already. You've heard it."

"No." Louder, and the tunnels took the word and handed it back a half-beat late, mocking, and I saw Rosalind understand for the first time what I'd understood at six years old: that down here, even your denials belong to it eventually.

"So here's what I decided," I said, and my voice cracked and I let it, because some things should crack, "standing in this room tonight, looking at two hundred years of hairbrushes. I decided my grandmother's death is not going to be honored by obedience. She thought duty was love. Fine. Then I'll love her the other way. I'm going to unmake the invitation. All of it. Every niche. Every name. I'd rather the thing below starve angry than eat polite."

"You can't unmake it alone. You told me — it takes someone marked, spoken aloud, at sunrise—"

"I know what it takes."

"Then you're planning to pay it yourself." Rosalind's voice went strange. "You're planning to trade one keeper for none. That's not unmaking, Tamsin, that's just a smaller cage."

"Then find me a better plan by sunrise," I said, "or hold your tongue and hold the lamp."

And that's when the lamps — both of them, mine and theirs — guttered at once, though there was no draft, and from far down the passage behind us, warm and reasonable and wearing a voice I'd have known through stone, through earth, through the end of the world, my grandmother called:

*Tamsin, love. Come say goodnight.*

Gran was three miles away and dying.

The thing below had started spending her early.
