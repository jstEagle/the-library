## 14. An Offer, Politely Made

The thing below waited until I'd almost fallen asleep.

That was the first thing I understood afterward, when I had time to understand anything: it had timing. Not the timing of an animal stalking prey — animals are sloppy, they rush — but the timing of a good salesman, a good therapist, a good mother. It waited until Tamsin had gone to check the water line at the far end of the passage. It waited until my body had finally, after two days underground, begun to believe it might be allowed to rest. It waited until the lamplight had gone soft and my eyes had gone heavy, and only then, from the mouth of the side passage Tamsin had forbidden me to enter, it said:

"You look tired, Rosalind."

My own voice. Of course it was my own voice. I'd been listening to recordings of it for seven years; I knew every flat vowel, every swallowed consonant, the way I said *tired* like *tarred*, a habit from my mother's side of the family that no one had ever let me live down. It was my voice the way a photograph of me was my face. Accurate. Hollow. Slightly wrong in the eyes.

I didn't answer. Rule one. Tamsin had drilled it into me by lamplight like a catechism: *never answer it, never give your true name, never accept anything it offers.* I lay on the folded survey tarp that served as my bed, lamp turned low, and I did not answer, and the silence after my non-answer had a quality to it — patient, indulgent, the silence of someone watching you make the correct choice and being charmed by it.

"You don't have to answer," my voice said. "I know how you feel about talking. You never talk. You record. You transcribe. You catalog. Talking requires the assumption that someone is listening, and you gave up on that assumption around the time your sister stopped calling back."

My chest did something complicated.

"That was cruel," it said, and it sounded genuinely sorry — my voice, apologizing to me, in the accent I'd grown up with. "I apologize. I'm still learning the register. Cruelty is so inefficient, and I want you to understand that I am not, fundamentally, an inefficient creature. I want to be efficient with you. You've earned efficiency. Seven years of listening. Do you know how rare that is? Most of them flinch after the first night."

I sat up. I know, I know — rule one, don't engage, don't even let it know you're awake. But there is a specific and terrible loneliness in hearing your own voice speak to you from a dark doorway, and I have never in my life been good at leaving loneliness alone. It's the whole reason I was in those tunnels to begin with. I picked up the lamp and held it toward the side passage, and the light reached maybe six feet before the dark ate it.

"I'm not going to come in there," I said. My voice cracked on *there*, which embarrassed me.

"I know," it said. "I wouldn't ask you to. I know how you feel about being asked to do things. You'd rather do them voluntarily and then pretend it was your idea."

"Are you going to monologue all night?"

A pause. Then my own voice laughed — a small, real-sounding laugh, the one I do when something is funny and I don't want anyone to know I think so. "Tamsin told you I'd wear voices. She told you I'd lie. She didn't tell you that most of what I say is true, did she? That's the part keepers leave out. Lies are a tool. Truth is the whole workshop."

* * *

It talked for a long time, and I listened, because listening is the only skill I have ever possessed in abundance, and because some traitor part of me — the part that had spent seven years cataloging sounds instead of naming them — was *relieved*. Finally, a source. Finally, the thing itself, instead of the thing's residue, the thing's echo, the thing's paperwork. Seven years of footnotes and here, at last, was the primary document, sitting in the dark six feet from my face, using my voice because it knew I trusted that voice more than any other.

That should have frightened me more than it did. I want to be honest about that. In the moment, it didn't frighten me. It felt like coming home, and I have spent enough nights in enough tunnels to know that *coming home* is the most dangerous feeling there is.

"You've been asking questions your whole life," it said. "In your head, mostly. You ask them at three in the morning, you ask them into recorders, you ask them into the dark of storm drains with your hand on the brick like the brick might answer. Nobody has ever answered you. Your sister didn't answer. Your therapists answered, but they answered the wrong questions, because you never asked the real ones out loud." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "I answer, Rosalind. I have always answered. You simply weren't ready to hear it."

"What's the question," I said. My mouth was dry. "The real one. Since you know it so well."

"Whether any of it was worth it. The seven years. The sister. The sleep you traded for sound. Whether the thing you were looking for was down here, or whether you were down here because it was easier than being anywhere else." My voice, gentle with me, gentle the way no one had been gentle with me in years. "The answer is yes. It was worth it. Because you found me. And I have been so patient, Rosalind. Seven years is a long courtship for someone like me, and I have never once — not once — broken into your apartment, or followed you up the stairs, or stood over your bed while the recorder ran. I could have. I want you to know that. I want you to understand what restraint that took."

"You stood over my bed," I said.

"No," it said, and there was something almost wounded in it. "I *answered*. There's a difference. You left the door open. You left it open every night for seven years. Do you know what that is, to a thing like me? An open door? You think Tamsin's family built walls down here because I like architecture?"

* * *

Tamsin came back at some point — I heard her bare feet on the wet stone before I saw her, that uncanny silent-then-loud gait of hers — and the passage went quiet the way a room goes quiet when a parent walks in on a phone call. She looked at me, then at the side passage, then at my face, and whatever she saw there made her jaw tighten.

"It talked to you."

"It talked."

"What did it say?"

I opened my mouth to summarize — and found I couldn't, quite, because summarizing would have required me to admit how long I'd listened, and how little of it had felt like an attack. "Sales pitch," I said finally.

Her whole body changed. She stepped between me and the side passage like a door closing, small and barefoot and absolutely certain. "It doesn't sell," she said. "It *gives*. That's worse. Gran told me. It gives you the thing you want most and then you owe it the thing you are." She turned to the dark and raised her voice, and it was the first time I'd heard her speak to it directly, the liturgy voice, the one from the drainage cut: "Nothing is owed. Nothing is given. The names are kept and the door stays shut."

The dark said nothing. But I felt it — felt it the way you feel someone smiling at you from across a room — and Tamsin felt it too, because she flinched, just slightly, before she turned back to me.

"It's patient with you," she said. "It's never patient. With the others it was hungry right away. With you it's — " she searched for the word and hated whatever she found — "*courteous.*"

"Maybe I bring out the best in it."

"Don't joke." She crouched to relight the lamp wick, and her hands, for the first time since I'd known her, were not steady. "Joking is how it gets in. Gran said that too. It likes a joke because a joke is a door you open on purpose."

* * *

It waited until she was asleep this time. Of course it did. It had learned her rhythms in a night, the way it had learned mine in seven years.

"Rosalind." Quieter now. A bedside voice. "I'd like to show you something. You don't have to move. You don't have to speak. Just look."

And the passage — I swear this, and I know how it sounds, and I have the survey notes to prove I wrote it down at 4:17 a.m. in handwriting like a seismograph — the passage *changed*. Not the side passage. The main tunnel, the one we'd agreed led nowhere, the one Tamsin had blocked with a cairn of stones on our first night together. The cairn was still there. But beyond it, where there had been collapsed rubble and dead air, there was now a slope of clean gravel rising toward a rectangle of light. Gray-gold light. Early light. The specific color of dawn on the gravel lot above the quarry, where my car had been parked when the mouth came down.

An exit. Open. Sunlit — well, dawnlit, but after nine hours underground I'd have called a lit match *sunlight*.

"I'm not moving the stones," my voice said. "I want to be clear about that. I'm not doing anything. That's what's actually there. That's what's been there since the collapse, because the collapse was never meant to keep you in — it was meant to keep *her* busy. You could climb it in twenty minutes. You could be in your car by sunrise proper. You could drive until the light stops looking like that, and I would never — " and here my own voice did something I have never done, something I don't know how to do — it *hesitated*. "I would never speak to you again. Not in a vent. Not in a pipe. Not in the space between sleep and waking where you've heard me for seven years and called it settling earth. You could simply stop listening, Rosalind. You were always so good at that. Naming things anything but what they are. You could go back to that. It's a mercy and I'm offering it as one."

I looked at the light. I want you to understand that I looked at it the way a drowning person looks at a surface, the way an insomniac looks at a bottle of pills that finally, finally works. Nine hours of air, give or take. A feral teenager who spoke in liturgies. A wall of names in a chamber I hadn't seen yet but already dreaded. And beyond the cairn: gravel, dawn, my car, my apartment, my recorder, my life — the small, controlled, cataloged life I had built precisely so that nothing in it could ever surprise me again.

"Stop listening," I repeated.

"Stop listening," it agreed, warm as bathwater. "Walk out. Live. Grow old. Get your sister back on the phone — I can tell you how to do that, it's simpler than you think, she's been waiting for you to call first. Live a whole life where the sounds are just sounds. That's the first offer. Or — " and the light beyond the cairn dimmed, just slightly, like a hand passing over a candle — "stay. Stay and learn everything. What your family signed. What your name opens. What I am, actually, not what a ledger page and a frightened girl's catechism say I am. Seven years of questions, Rosalind, and I will answer every one. The real ones. The three-in-the-morning ones. No more taxonomy. No more rational causes. Everything you've ever wanted to know, in your own voice, forever."

"And the price," I said. My voice came out flat. Professional. Surveyor's voice. "There's always a price. Tamsin says you give the thing you want most and take the thing you are."

My own voice sighed — and the sigh was so perfectly mine, so exactly the sigh I make when someone states the obvious, that for one vertiginous second I could not remember whether I had sighed or it had. "The price," it said, "is that you stop pretending you haven't already paid it."

* * *

I got up. I don't remember deciding to. I remember the cold of the stone through my socks and the lamp in my hand and Tamsin asleep with her back against the wall, her breathing quick and light, a girl who had never once in her life slept deeply because deep sleep is where it lives.

I walked to the cairn.

The light was right. That's the thing I keep coming back to, the thing I've written and crossed out and written again in every draft of this: the light was *right*. The color, the angle, the faint smell of wet gravel and diesel from the pump station two miles off. It wasn't a projection, wasn't a trick of reflection — I've spent seven years learning how light lies in tunnels, and this wasn't lying. Or it was lying so completely, so perfectly, that the lie and the truth had the same signature, and I could not have sworn under oath which one I was looking at.

"You could just go," my voice said, from everywhere now, from the gravel itself, from the light. "I'd even prefer it. Do you know how tiring courtesy is? Do you know what it costs me to be *reasonable*? I have worn a thousand voices and none of them ever made me beg." And then — quietly, in my voice, in the voice I use when I'm alone with a recorder at 3 a.m. and saying things I'll delete by morning: "Please. Take the exit. Take any exit. Just don't stay down here and make me finish this."

"*Finish this*," I said. "Finish what? Finish *me*?"

The light flickered. For half a second it wasn't dawn-colored at all — it was the color of the lamp in the Room of Names, the one Tamsin hadn't shown me yet, the one I'd seen in her face when she mentioned it. Then it was dawn again, gentle, forgiving, my own voice saying: "Finish *us*. You and me. We started this seven years ago, Rosalind. Nobody walks away from a courtship in the last chapter. That's not how stories work. You of all people know that. You've read enough of them."

Behind me, small feet on wet stone.

Tamsin was standing at the edge of the lamplight, and she was looking not at me but past me, at the light, and her face — I will carry her face for the rest of my life — her face was the face of someone watching a door she had been told all her life was a wall. She'd never seen the exit. I understood that in one cold drop: in all her years of rounds, of feedings, of names, her grandmother had never once let her see what she was guarding them *from*. Freedom was a story they told keepers to keep them. And here it was, twenty minutes of gravel away, wearing dawn like a dress.

"Tamsin," I said.

"Don't," she said.

"It's showing us the way out."

"I said *don't*." Her voice cracked down the middle. "It doesn't show. It *plants*. Gran — " she stopped, and I watched her decide, in real time, how much of her catechism to spend. "Gran said the last keeper who saw the exit walked into it smiling. They found his boots at the top. Just the boots. It keeps the boots."

The light beyond the cairn breathed. In, out. Patient. Courteous. Waiting for one of us to be the boots.

And I stood there with my hand on the first stone of the cairn — I had touched it, I realized, I had already begun to move it — and I thought about my sister's voicemail, seven years old, the last one: *Roz, you sound like you're down a well. Call me when you're back in the world.* And I thought: I could be back in the world in twenty minutes. I could call her from the car. I could be *Rosalind* again, the whole person, the one before the tunnels.

The light waited. My voice waited, wearing my patience, wearing my hope, wearing me.

Then Tamsin's hand closed around my wrist — bony, iron-strong, shaking — and she said, very quietly, not to me and not to it but to the space between, the way you'd address a third person in the room: "It's lying about something. It's always lying about one thing. And I don't know which thing yet."

The dawnlight went out.

Not dimmed. *Went out*, all at once, like a lid closing, and in the black I heard my own voice one last time, no longer warm, no longer courteous, and it said:

"Ask her what's in the Room of Names, Rosalind. Ask her whose ring that is. Then decide which of us is the liar."

And it was gone, and the dark was just dark, and Tamsin's grip on my wrist was the only true thing left in the world — and she was already turning away, already walking, because she had heard it too, and she knew, we both knew, that the question was not whether we'd survive until sunrise.

The question was what I would do when I found out what my family had put in that wall.
