## 15. What Was Invited In

The lamp between us had burned down to a coin of light, and I'd been putting off the telling for two hours, which is a personal record for cowardice.

Rosalind sat with their back against the brick and their knees drawn up, and they kept looking at me the way people look at a doctor when they already know the diagnosis but want to hear it said out loud anyway. Like saying it makes it real, and real means you can finally do something about it.

"Tell me," they said. "All of it. Not the keeper's version. The version you tell yourself when you can't sleep."

I almost laughed. "I don't sleep down here. Sleeping's how it gets in."

"Then the version you don't tell anyone."

I turned my grandmother's ring around on its cord at my throat. It was warm. Everything of hers stayed warm, like she hadn't quite finished leaving it.

"All right," I said. "But you're not going to like it."

"I haven't liked anything since chapter one," Rosalind said, which didn't make sense, but that was Rosalind all over — jokes with no context, like they were keeping themselves company. I was starting to understand why. You keep yourself company or the dark does it for you.

* * *

Here's what the ledger doesn't say, because ledgers are written by people who want their grandchildren to be proud of them.

In 1847, Harrow's Bend was dying. Not dramatically — not fire, not flood — just slowly, the way a body dies when it can't keep food down. The quarry had flooded three years running. The wheat came up gray. The mill pond went to rot and the fish floated belly-up in rows like little silver spoons somebody had dropped. Children died first, then old people, then everybody in between who wasn't stubborn enough to hold on out of spite.

My great-great-great-something — the first Tamsin, whose name I wear like a hand-me-down coat that never fit anybody right — was eleven years old that winter. She watched her baby brother eat candle wax because his stomach thought anything was better than nothing.

And her mother — my ancestor, a woman named Mercy Harrow, whose name is on the town sign if you squint past the rust — did what desperate people do. She didn't pray harder. She didn't wait for God, who had apparently stepped out. She went looking for something closer to home. Something that answered.

There were stories even then. Older than the town. The Lenape had names for the hollow places under the ridge, and the Dutch farmers before them had refused to dig certain hillsides, and there was a spring that ran sweet in summer and tasted of iron in autumn, and nobody drank from it after sundown. The land under Harrow's Bend was never empty. That's the thing people get wrong about empty places. They're not empty. They're *vacant*, which is different. Vacant means something left and the door was never locked behind it.

Mercy found the door.

Not with a ritual, not with blood and candles — that's the other lie, the gothic lie, the lie where wicked people do wicked ceremonies and everyone else gets to wash their hands. She found it by listening. Seven nights at the mouth of the old spring cave, listening, the way you'd listen at a wall when you think someone might be alive on the other side. And on the seventh night, something listened back.

It asked her what she wanted.

She told it the truth: bread. Rain that fell clean. Her children alive in the spring.

And it said — and this part I know word for word, because we say it every night on rounds, and I have hated every syllable since I was four years old — it said: *What will you give?*

Mercy looked at her starving children in her head, and she said the worst sentence ever spoken in this valley: *"Whatever you ask."*

* * *

"It didn't take her voice that night," I told Rosalind. The lamp guttered and steadied. "That's important. People think it's a monster that grabs. It doesn't grab. It *waits*. It gave them the harvest. First frost came late, the quarry drained itself overnight — men swore the water level dropped six feet while they slept — and the wheat came in gold, so heavy the stalks bent double. The town ate. The town sang about it. There's still a festival, did you know that? Harvest Days, third weekend of September. Pie contest."

Rosalind made a sound that was half laugh, half something worse.

"The price came due slow. A year later, Mercy woke up and couldn't remember her own mother's face. Two years later, she lost the sound of her sister's laugh — knew she'd had one, couldn't hear it anymore, like a room in her head with the furniture gone. By the time Tamsin-the-first was grown, Mercy could remember everything about her life except how any of it felt. And then one morning she opened her mouth to call the girl in from the fields and nothing came out. No voice. Just air moving over a shape that used to make words."

"And the town noticed?"

"The town *benefited*. That's different." I picked at the mortar between the bricks, because looking at Rosalind while I said this felt impossible. "Rich soil, full bellies, and one woman gone quiet. Easy math. The founders did the math. Then they wrote it down — not the truth, the arrangement. Renewed each generation by a named keeper. Which sounds official until you realize what renewal meant. Each generation, the thing below got hungrier for voices, and each generation, the family that invited it had to feed it. Not throats. Voices. The sound of you. Your name in your own mouth. Your mother calling you home at dusk. It takes those first, and it keeps them, and it plays them back down here in the dark whenever it wants to feel full."

"That's what I've been hearing," Rosalind said quietly. "Seven years. All those voices in the drains."

"You weren't hearing echoes. Echoes don't know your name." I let that sit. "You were hearing the pantry."

* * *

Here's the part I've only ever said to my grandmother, and she was too far gone by then to argue, which is probably why I could say it.

We didn't trap it. We never trapped it. Every story the town tells itself — the sealed tunnel, the founders' brickwork, the doors carved inward to keep something *in* — it's all backwards. The doors open inward because inward is where it lives, and the brickwork isn't a cage. It's a *threshold marker*. Like chalk around a summoning circle, except the circle is a whole town and the chalk is our history and every generation we redraw it.

You don't build a prison for something you invited to dinner. You build a dining room.

"It saved us," I said, and my voice came out flat as slate. "That's the rotten heart of it. It worked. Famine ended. Town survived. My family got rich on the harvest and then spent the money building walls and writing rules, because once you've eaten at that table you can't leave it, you can only decide who sits next to you. And the rules — never answer it, never give your true name, never accept anything it offers — those aren't protections. They're *etiquette*. Ways to stay polite at dinner so it doesn't ask for seconds."

"But it marked me," Rosalind said. "Years ago. Just by listening."

"You listened *back*." I looked at them then, because they needed to hear this looking at them. "Do you understand what that means to it? Everyone else runs. Everyone else hears a voice in a drain and calls it water or wind or their own tired brain and walks away fast. You built spreadsheets. You bought recording equipment. You came back *every night for seven years* and paid attention. Attention is the oldest currency there is. You've been courting it without knowing. It knows your voice better than your sister does. It's been practicing you."

Rosalind's face did something complicated. I let them have the moment, because the next part was mine to carry and I needed both hands free.

* * *

"So it can be unmade," they said, when they could talk again. "You said unmade. How."

I took a breath. Down here the air tastes of wet stone and old pennies, and I swear sometimes it tastes of bread, fresh bread, which is the cruelest trick in its whole repertoire.

"The invitation was spoken. Spoken things can be unsaid — but not erased, not forgotten. Unmade means *answered*. The invitation was an offer accepted, so the unmaking has to be an offer refused, out loud, by someone with standing. Someone it has marked. Someone whose voice it holds a piece of already, so the refusal lands somewhere real instead of bouncing off the brick." I pulled the ring cord tight against my throat. "Spoken aloud. At sunrise. At the threshold, in the heart chamber, when the light comes down the old ventilation shaft and touches the floor. Dawn is the one hour it's weakest, because dawn is when Mercy first heard it answer, and beginnings can be closed like doors."

"What happens to the person who speaks it?"

"There's the price tag." I smiled, and I knew it was a bad smile, the kind my grandmother called my *keeper's face*. "Nobody's ever done it. So nobody knows for sure. Best case: the marked one loses whatever piece of their voice it holds, and maybe some they didn't know they'd given, and they walk out into the sun missing pieces and spend the rest of their life wondering which thoughts are theirs. Worst case—" I shrugged. "Worst case, unmaking an invitation means the guest leaves and takes the table setting with it. The voice goes with the voice-taker."

"So you'd lose—"

"Everything it's collected of mine. Which is a lot. I've been doing rounds since I could walk. Fourteen years of talking to it, feeding it, reciting the names. It has more of me than I have of myself some nights." I said it lightly, the way you carry something heavy by convincing your arms it's nothing. "But here's the thing, listener. Here's the thing I figured out sitting with my grandmother while she forgot my name for the fourth time in an hour."

I leaned forward into the lamplight.

"It has to be someone it's marked. And it's marked two of us now. But only one of us it trusts. Only one of us it's been *practicing*. If you speak the unmaking, it'll fight you with your sister's voice and your mother's voice and seven years of your own recorded self, and it'll win, because you love those voices and it knows exactly how much. But me?" I tapped my own chest. "It has nothing left to tempt me with. It already took the only voice I ever loved. My grandmother's going to die upstairs whether I'm there or not. It can't threaten me with losing her. She's already being taken, one memory at a time, and the thing below is holding the door."

"Tamsin." Rosalind said my name carefully, like it was load-bearing. "You're volunteering."

"I was born for the job. Might as well quit it spectacularly."

* * *

They argued. Of course they argued. Rosalind argued the way water argues with stone — patiently, endlessly, in a direction. They said we'd find another way. They said two marked people could speak it together. They said a lot of things, and underneath all of them was the same sentence, the one nobody says out loud because saying it makes it true:

*I don't want to lose you.*

I let them run out. Then I said, "How many hours till sunrise?"

Rosalind checked their watch, and their face changed, and I knew before they said it.

"Four."

"Then we stop arguing and start walking," I said, and stood, and picked up the lamp. "Heart chamber's forty minutes through the flooded section if the water's low. Longer if it's feeling clever. And it's going to be feeling very clever, because it's been listening to us plan, same as it listens to everything."

I held out my hand. Rosalind looked at it for a second — at me, barefoot, ring-cord, keeper's face — and then took it, and their hand was cold and shaking and human, and I held on.

"One rule before we go," I said. "Whatever you hear down there in the next four hours — however much it sounds like me, however much it begs — you check my hands. It can do my voice perfect. It cannot do my hands. I always move them when I talk. Grandmother said I talked with my fingers before I talked with my mouth."

Rosalind nodded. And then, quiet, in the dark, they asked the question I'd been dreading, the one that had been sitting between us since the collapse like a third person:

"And if it offers you me? If it offers you my life, my freedom, me walking out of here safe — what then?"

I started walking toward the flooded section, toward the heart, toward the dawn that was coming whether we earned it or not.

"Then it'll learn," I said, "what my grandmother learned too late. Never accept anything it offers."

Behind us, far down the passage, something began to hum — soft and sweet and terribly familiar, a lullaby in a dead woman's voice, sung perfectly, in the dark.

I didn't look back. I counted my footsteps instead, and kept my hands where Rosalind could see them.
