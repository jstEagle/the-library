## 1. Dead Air

The thing nobody tells you about hosting a radio show from midnight to four a.m. is that the hours are not actually silent. They're just quiet in the way an empty church is quiet — full of things waiting to be named.

Paloma Nakamura had learned this over eleven years behind the microphone at KRSP, 1340 AM, "The Voice of Cold Spring," a station so small its transmitter sat on a hill behind the feed store and its entire news department was a corkboard. Her show, *The Cold Spring Line*, ran on a simple contract with the town: you call, I listen, we both pretend the night is shorter than it is.

She had rules, laminated and taped to the console like scripture. Rule one: never dead-air longer than ten seconds — silence on the radio is a door left open. Rule two: no politics after two a.m., because everyone becomes a philosopher after two a.m. and philosophers are boring. Rule three: if a caller sounds like they're crying, let them finish before you ask anything.

It was 1:47 on a Tuesday morning in October when Ruth called.

"Line one, you're on *The Cold Spring Line*. Who's this?"

"Ruth Ellery." The voice was dry and pleasant, an old woman's voice, the kind that had spent decades being patient with children and telephone solicitors alike. "I've listened to your show since you started, dear. You were terrible at first."

"I was," Paloma agreed, leaning into the mic. "I once spent forty minutes discussing whether raccoons hold grudges."

"They do," said Ruth. "The one in my garage has never forgiven me for the broom incident." A small laugh, then a pause that went on a beat too long. "I'm calling about my funeral."

"Your—" Paloma smiled for the benefit of no one. Late-night radio attracted a certain kind of caller, the way porch lights attract moths and worse. "We doing a hypothetical? Planning ahead? Because honestly, Ruth, more people should. My aunt pre-planned her whole service and all she requested was 'no hymns, play something with a beat,' and it was the best funeral I ever attended."

"Last Tuesday," Ruth said, ignoring her entirely. "At the First Methodist. There were maybe forty people. Marjorie Kettleman cried harder than my own daughter, which did not surprise me. The flowers were wrong — chrysanthemums, as if I'd asked for them, which I didn't, I wanted lilies, but nobody listens. Pastor Boone said I was 'a fixture of this community,' which is a polite way of saying old furniture." She sighed, a soft crackle through the line. "The eulogy was fine. The casket was too much money. My son overspends when he's guilty."

Paloma's hand hovered over the fader. She'd been doing this long enough to know grief could take strange shapes — people sometimes called in to rehearse their own deaths, to say goodbye while they still had a voice to do it with. It was sad, and she took those calls seriously, but something about the tense here was off, like a picture hung one degree crooked.

"Ruth," she said gently, "are you ill? Is that why—"

"I'm not anything anymore, dear. That's rather the point." A pause. "You'll want to check. You're a fact-checker. I can hear it in you, always wanting the number before the story. Go look me up. *Cold Spring Courier-Dispatch*, last Wednesday's edition. Page six, below the grocery ads, where they put us old ones."

And then, before Paloma could answer, Ruth said: "Give back what was taken."

The line clicked dead.

Paloma sat back in her chair. The booth hummed around her — the console's idle buzz, the tick of the transmitter meter, the little refrigerator where she kept creamer that expired sometime during the previous administration. Outside the glass, the hallway lights of the station were off except the one over the bathroom, which flickered on a dying ballast and had been doing so since roughly the invention of electricity.

"Huh," she said out loud, because saying things out loud kept the night honest.

She pulled up the *Courier-Dispatch* archive on the ancient computer by the door, the one that took four minutes to boot and made a sound like a man clearing his throat before every command. Last Wednesday's edition loaded in grainy gray. Page six. Below the grocery ads.

**ELLERY, RUTH MARIE**, 84, of Cold Spring, passed peacefully October 3rd. Services held Tuesday at First Methodist. Preceded in death by husband Walter; survived by son Dale...

Paloma read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you reread a sentence in a contract that changes everything after it.

Funeral Tuesday. Forty-ish attendees, plausible. Chrysanthemums — she couldn't verify that, but who would invent chrysanthemums? And *Marjorie Kettleman* — she knew that name, vaguely, from some pledge drive or other. It was a real name attached to a real town, not the invented furniture of a prankster.

Pranksters, in her experience, wanted attention. They wanted to be funny, or cruel, or famous for eleven seconds. They did not want to be *verifiable*. This caller had handed her a paper trail and hung up before enjoying the payoff.

Rule four, which wasn't laminated yet but lived in her chest: don't chase weirdness down dark alleys at three a.m. Weirdness keeps office hours. Call it back in daylight.

She put on a block of music — the station's overnight automation was basically a shoebox of CDs, heavy on Linda Ronstadt — and gave herself until the top of the hour.

*

At 2:00 she came back on mic, lighter than she felt. "That was Ms. Ronstadt, and this is *The Cold Spring Line*, still awake so you don't have to be. Lines are open. Let's talk about anything that isn't raccoons, I've said enough about raccoons to last a lifetime."

Line one lit up immediately.

"Caller, you're live."

"Give back what was taken."

A man's voice, flat as a table. Not angry. Not threatening. Just *stated*, the way you'd state your address.

"Okay," Paloma said, keeping her voice warm, professional, the voice she used on drunks and insomniacs and the man who called monthly to report UFOs over the water tower. "Taken by whom? We fixing something tonight? Because I love a fix-it call. My sink has been dripping since June, so if you've got a plumber on retainer—"

"You have to give it back." A breath that sounded wrong — too long, too even, like wind moving through a pipe instead of lungs. "They buried it where she drowned. It's cold down there. It's been cold so long."

"Sir, what's your name?"

Silence. Then, softly, almost tenderly: "Harold Finch. 411 Alameda Street. You can check."

Click.

Paloma stared at the phone. Harold Finch. She knew that name too, and she hated knowing it, because Harold Finch had been in the obituaries two weeks ago — a retired county surveyor, died in his sleep, services private. She'd probably read it aloud to herself over coffee the way she read the obits every morning, a habit she'd inherited from her grandmother along with her hands and her stubbornness.

Automation, she thought. Pranksters with soundboards. Spoofed numbers. There was a whole economy of cruelty available to anyone with a laptop and a grudge against late-night radio hosts.

But the obituaries matched. Both of them. Exactly. Down to the details no hoaxer would bother getting right — the chrysanthemums, the overspending son, the private services.

Line two lit. Line three. Line four. All at once, which never happened, because Cold Spring had maybe nine hundred souls awake at 2 a.m. and most of them were watching television with the sound off.

She took line two.

"Go ahead, caller."

"Give back what was taken." A woman's voice, young, with an accent Paloma couldn't place — older than the town, older than Spanish, older than English. "In 1974. In the riverbed. She was singing when they—"

Paloma hit the dump button. The caller vanished mid-word.

Her heart was doing something unprofessional. She keyed the mic anyway, because rule one, never dead-air, never leave the door open. "Folks, we're having some technical difficulties with the lines tonight, so let's take a break and—" she queued a song without looking at what it was, "—hear some music."

It was Linda Ronstadt again. Of course it was.

*

For twenty minutes she sat in the booth and did not move.

The calls kept coming. She watched them stack on the board — line one, line three, line five, blinking red like a Christmas tree in a house where nobody lived anymore. She didn't answer them. She told herself she was screening. That was the professional word for it. *Screening.*

What she was actually doing was reading obituaries.

She had the *Courier-Dispatch* spread across the console now, three weeks' worth, and she'd begun making a list on the pad she normally used for song requests. Names, dates, causes of death. Ruth Ellery, heart failure. Harold Finch, died in sleep. Dolores Aguilar, stroke. Martin Beck, fall on the stairs. Seven names in three weeks, which was a lot for a town this size, though not unheard-of — October was hard on the old, her grandmother used to say, the year turning takes them with it.

Every single one of the callers so far had been on her list.

She checked the numbers again. Spoofing, she reminded herself. Anyone could spoof a number. But the voices — she'd grown up here, she'd hosted this show for eleven years, she knew the town's voices the way a mother knows footsteps on stairs. Ruth Ellery had sounded exactly like Ruth Ellery, whose granddaughter had been in Paloma's fourth-grade class. Harold Finch had that surveyor's precision, measuring out his street address like a property line.

And the woman on line two — the young one, the one with the old-old accent — she hadn't sounded like anyone Paloma knew. She'd sounded like someone from *before* the town knew itself. Like a voice preserved under ice.

"Give back what was taken," Paloma murmured to the empty booth. "Taken *what*? Taken by *who*?" She wrote it on the pad, underlined it twice, then crossed it out, then wrote it again. "Nineteen seventy-four. What happened in 1974?"

Nothing. That was the answer that came back, and it came back smooth and blank as fresh snow. Nothing happened in 1974. She'd been born in '81; 1974 was the year her parents got married, the year of the big flood scare, the year the mission downtown closed for renovations. Just history. Just the ordinary sediment every town sits on.

Except the woman's voice had cracked on the word *singing*, and Paloma had heard a lot of people lie on the radio, and that had not been a lie. That had been a memory with the skin torn off it.

Line one blinked again. She looked at it for a long time.

Then she reached over and flipped the master switch to automation, letting the shoebox of CDs carry the show home, and pulled off her headphones, and the booth went suddenly, enormously quiet — the kind of quiet that isn't empty but *attentive*, the quiet of a room listening back.

"Get a grip, Nakamura," she said to herself. Her voice sounded fine. Steady. Radio-ready. She was proud of it, and ashamed of how hard it was working.

She gathered her keys, her list, the folded newspapers. On her way out she paused at the front desk, where the overnight log sat open, and — because she was a professional, because documentation was armor — she wrote in careful print:

*2:00–2:40 a.m.: Multiple calls re: 1974. Callers identified as deceased persons (see attached). Recommend line maintenance.*

Underneath, smaller, in handwriting she barely recognized as her own:

*Or recommend nothing. Please recommend nothing.*

Outside, the night was cold and clear, stars sharp as tacks, the hills black around the town like cupped hands. Her car started on the second try. As she pulled out of the lot, the transmitter light on the hill blinked its slow green blink, sending Linda Ronstadt out over the rooftops of Cold Spring, over the sleeping houses and the dry riverbed beyond them, and Paloma drove home with the windows up and the heater on and the feeling — irrational, unshakable, growing with every block — that she was not driving away from something.

She was driving toward it.

Because there, on the porch of her little house on Mesquite Lane, pale in the yard light, something was waiting. Small. Still. Patient the way only children and the dead are patient.

She turned off the engine and sat with her hands on the wheel, and forty years fell away from her all at once.

"No," she whispered. "No, that's not possible."

But he was there. He was *still there.*
