## 3. A Bell That Wasn't There

The dusk came early that October, the way it always did in Hollowmere, sliding down off the moors like something poured rather than fallen. Rosalind Eastwick stood at her kitchen window with a cup of tea going cold in her hands, watching the light drain out of the sky over the eastern fields, and she was thinking about Mr. Tallow's hands.

They had been still when he died. That was the part that stayed with her, more than the counting itself — she had heard it so many times now that the numbers had worn smooth as river stones, thirty, twenty-nine, twenty-eight, each one softer than the last, until the final *one* arrived not like a word but like a door closing gently somewhere inside him. But his hands had been folded on the coverlet, and they had gone still all at once, both of them, like birds settling. She had checked her watch against the moment. She always did. 4:47 in the afternoon. The dying of Hollowmere kept better time than the church clock, and she had long ago stopped pretending to herself that this was coincidence.

She drank the cold tea anyway. Waste nothing; that was one of her rules, though not one of the important ones. The important ones lived in a small notebook in her apron pocket and in the architecture of her evenings: every bulb burning before full dark, doors counted twice, salt along the thresholds renewed on the first of each month whether it needed renewing or not. People in the village thought her eccentric. Eccentric was a kind word for what she was, and she had learned to accept kind words the way one accepts small change — gratefully, without examining where they came from.

She was rinsing the cup when the bell rang.

* * *

It did not come from anywhere. That was the first wrongness, though it took her some minutes to understand it, the way it takes a moment to notice a room has gone quiet. The sound simply arrived, whole, in the air of her kitchen and in the air of the lane outside and, she would learn later, in the air of every kitchen and lane and field in Hollowmere at once. A single toll, deep enough that she felt it in her back teeth, in the bones of her wrists, in the soles of her feet through the flagstones. Bronze, she thought. Old bronze, thick-walled, cast by someone who knew their trade and loved it.

The second toll came eleven seconds later. She counted without meaning to. Counting was what she did; it had been done to her often enough that she'd become fluent.

She stood with the wet cup in her hand and water running over her fingers and listened, and the listening was the worst part, because there was a quality to the sound that she recognized from somewhere she could not name — a warmth under the weight of it, almost tender, the way a voice goes tender when it is calling someone home for supper. Bells are not supposed to be tender. Bells announce. They warn, they celebrate, they mourn. This one did none of those things. It *invited.*

Three tolls. Four. On the fifth she set down the cup, dried her hands with the care of someone performing a ritual — which she was, though she didn't know yet what kind — and went to the front door.

The lane was empty. Dusk had thickened to true dark between the hedges, and the lamps at the corner burned their usual orange, steady, unremarkable. Down toward the green she could see Mrs. Petchy's windows lit, and beyond them the black bulk of the yew trees by the churchyard wall. No bell tower stood tall enough to carry sound across the whole village. St. Brannoc's steeple held only a small modern peal, light-voiced, cheerful, incapable of the sound that was still rolling through her chest like a tide going out.

And yet.

Sixth toll. Seventh. Between them, silence so complete she could hear her own blood moving.

Rosalind did the thing she always did when the world misbehaved: she made tea she didn't want, turned on every light in the house — the lamp in the parlor, the bare bulb in the pantry, the little night-light in the hall that she told visitors was for reading — and then she took down the parish records.

* * *

St. Brannoc's records lived in a locked cabinet in the vestry, but Reverend Hollis was a man who believed locks were for strangers, and Rosalind had copied the relevant pages years ago into her own notebook, because a nurse learns early that the past is mostly a list of things people failed to prepare for. She sat at her kitchen table under the glare of two lamps and read by lamplight what she already knew, needing to see it in ink again, needing the paper to hold its shape against the sound still faintly trembling in the floorboards.

*The great bell of St. Brannoc's, cast 1743, weight 31 cwt, cracked in the frost of 1910–11. Taken down March 1911 and conveyed to the Whitechapel foundry to be broken up and recast. The metal sold; the proceeds applied to the roof fund.* There followed a list of signatures, and beneath them, in a different hand, a note: *No replacement hung. Congregation resolved that the old voice could not be improved upon, and would rather have silence.*

Silence, then, for a hundred and thirteen years. Silence was what Hollowmere's evenings were built on, the deep rural quiet that visitors remarked upon and locals stopped hearing, the silence Rosalind had arranged her whole life around like furniture.

The bell had been melted down before her grandmother was born. Its bronze had been sold, scattered, quite possibly beaten into church roofs or ship fittings or garden ornaments in towns that had never heard of Hollowmere. There was no bell. There was no tower for a bell. And yet the eighth toll came while she sat there, and the ninth, and she felt them not in her ears primarily but in her sternum, in the hollow behind it where she had spent thirty years keeping other people's deaths company.

She noticed, around the twelfth toll, that the intervals were lengthening. Eleven seconds, then fourteen, then nineteen. Not fading — the sound stayed full-bodied, undiminished, as if distance meant nothing to it — but slowing. Like a heart deciding, deliberately, to beat less often. Like something settling in.

On the seventeenth toll, she got up and locked her front door, and counted the lock turning twice, and hated herself a little for it, and did it anyway.

* * *

In the morning she went to the village, because the alternative was staying home and listening to the silence for evidence of what had filled it, and Rosalind Eastwick had survived thirty years of the dying by never once letting herself listen too hard.

The bakery smelled of cinnamon and warm flour, and Edith Tallow — no relation to Mr. Tallow that anyone had traced, though Rosalind privately suspected the village of being related to itself in ways genealogy couldn't capture — was serving loaves to a Saturday queue. Rosalind bought a cob loaf she didn't need and asked, lightly, the way you ask about weather:

"Did you hear anything odd last night? After dark?"

Edith frowned, wiping her hands on her apron. "Odd how?"

"A sound. Deep. Like a bell."

Edith's frown deepened, and then changed — softened, actually, into something puzzled and pleasant, and that softening frightened Rosalind more than any alarm could have. "You know," she said slowly, "I did have a funny feeling, round about dusk. Like — you know when you're away somewhere, and you smell bread baking, and you're homesick all at once, out of nowhere?" She laughed. "That's it exactly. Homesick. Standing in my own kitchen, homesick. Silly." She handed over the loaf. "Why, did you hear something?"

"Thought I did. Probably the wind in the chimney."

"Mm." But Edith was looking past Rosalind's shoulder, out the window, eastward, where the fields ran down toward the river and the low ground beyond, and her expression had that pleasant puzzled softness still, like a woman trying to remember the words to a song.

Outside, Rosalind conducted her own small survey, casual, patient, the way she took pulses. Mrs. Petchy, watering her geraniums despite the season: yes, a funny feeling at dusk, a sort of longing, couldn't say for what. Young Tam Weller fixing a fence: he'd felt like walking somewhere and hadn't known where, had gotten as far as his gate and turned back, embarrassed. Old Mrs. Quill said she'd dreamed of her mother's kitchen and woken with tears on her face. Every account the same in structure if not in words: a pull, gentle and directionless-seeming, arriving at dusk.

Except that it wasn't directionless. Rosalind asked each of them, lightly, which way they'd felt drawn, and every one of them — after a pause, after a small frown, as if consulting something just behind their own eyes — pointed or nodded or tilted their heads *eastward.* Toward the river. Toward the low ground. Toward Old Chapel Road, though not one of them named it, and when Rosalind named it herself, testing, they looked at her blankly, pleasantly, as if she'd spoken a word from a language they'd been happy to forget.

Only she had heard the bell itself. Only she could tell you its intervals, its weight, the tenderness underneath the bronze. Everyone else had received the feeling and discarded the cause, the way a body discards a splinter — pushed it up and out and left only the ache of entry.

She walked home the long way, along the lane above the river, and the whole way she felt it too — she would have been lying to deny it — a faint tug below the breastbone, eastward, gentle as a hand on the small of the back. *Homesickness,* Edith had called it. Rosalind knew homesickness. This was not it. This was the feeling of being missed. Of being wanted somewhere, specifically, by name, patiently, by something that had waited a hundred and thirteen years and could wait longer.

At her gate she stopped. The dusk was coming down again, earlier than yesterday, it seemed, or perhaps she was only noticing it sooner now. Behind her, westward, the village lights were coming on one by one, ordinary and orange and safe. Ahead, eastward, the fields went blue-black toward the low ground, and somewhere out there, under the grass and the history, lay the foundation stones of a chapel whose bell had been melted down before the war that everyone's grandfathers talked about.

Her hand found the latch. Her feet stayed planted. For three full breaths she stood balanced between the lit windows and the dark fields, feeling the pull like a tide against her ankles, and she understood — with the flat, clinical clarity she brought to deathbeds — that whatever had begun last night at dusk had begun *for* her, or at least *through* her, and that her rituals of light and salt and counted doors had been built, all these years, against smaller weather than this.

Then the bell tolled again — once, close, unmistakable, though there was nothing out there but grass and dark — and every dog in Hollowmere began to bark, and every light in Rosalind's house, every bulb she had left burning since morning, went out at once.

Not flickered. Went out. All together. Like eyes closing.

She stood in the dark at her own gate, loaf of bread under her arm, heart hammering the way it did in sickrooms when the end came fast, and far off to the east — so far it might have been memory, so near it might have been her own pulse — the bell began, softly, patiently, to count.
