## 4. Old Chapel Road

The bell rang again at dusk, and this time Rosalind did not stay behind her counted doors.

She told herself it was duty. She was a nurse; she had spent eleven years walking toward the things other people walked away from — the stopped breath, the wet rattle, the small blue hour of a body becoming a memory. It was in her nature, or it had been trained into her so deeply it made no difference. When something called, she went to it. That was all this was. That was all she would let it be.

She dressed in her heaviest coat, wound a wool scarf to her chin, and stood a long moment in the doorway of her cottage with her hand flat against the frame, feeling the wood. The door was solid oak, older than her tenancy, and she had counted its hinges twice every night for eleven years — two hinges, three bolts, one lock — the way other people said their prayers. Tonight the wood felt warm. Not sun-warm; there had been no sun since noon, only a low bruised light the color of an old bruise. Warm like skin. Warm like something that had been holding its breath and had just, gently, begun to exhale.

The bell rang again, and she felt it in her back teeth.

She had spent the whole of the afternoon trying to make it stop being true. She had telephoned the vicarage and listened to Reverend Pike's apologetic confusion — *a bell? No, Mrs. Eastwick, there's been no bell in Hollowmere since before the war, the old one was melted down, 1911 or thereabouts, it's all in the parish record* — and she had thanked him and hung up and stood in her kitchen with the receiver still warm against her ear, watching the light die through the window. She had walked down to the Green, where Mrs. Penhale was locking up the post office, and asked, casually, terribly casually, whether she'd heard anything odd at dusk.

Mrs. Penhale had frowned, and then her frown had softened into something Rosalind had no word for — a softening, an unfurling, like a flower turning toward a window.

"Odd," Mrs. Penhale repeated. "No. Only — do you know, I've had the strangest feeling all day. Like homesickness. Only I'm home." She laughed at herself, a small embarrassed sound, and pressed her hand briefly over her sternum. "Like there's somewhere I've forgotten I'm meant to be. Silly. East, isn't it. Isn't that funny? I keep wanting to walk east."

East. Toward the chapel. Toward Old Chapel Road.

Rosalind had watched her say it and had felt the cold go through her like water through a net. Mrs. Penhale did not hear the bell. None of them heard the bell. But all of them, it seemed, were leaning toward it — the whole village tilted a degree or two off true, like furniture in a house with a sloping floor, and only Rosalind stood upright, and only Rosalind could hear what they were all leaning toward.

She had gone home. She had lit every lamp. She had salted the threshold. She had counted the doors.

And then the bell had rung, and she had picked up her torch and her walking stick, and she had gone out into the dark toward the thing that was calling her village home.

* * *

Old Chapel Road began at the lych-gate of the new church and ran eastward through the fields like a sentence no one had finished. It was a lane of packed earth and old stone, hedged with blackthorn, and the villagers walked it on fine Sundays to show their children the ruins — the roofless nave, the ivy-eaten arches, the stump of a tower that had stood empty for over a hundred years. Children dared each other to touch the stones. Lovers carved their initials into the soft limestone and came back decades later to find them gone, the stone healing itself the way stone does, slowly, indifferently.

Rosalind had walked it a hundred times and never once felt anything but the ordinary melancholy of ruins, that gentle human ache for the hands that built them. Tonight the lane felt like a throat.

She knew that was a mad thing to think. She thought it anyway, and kept walking, because the thought fit the evidence. The hedgerows rose high on either side and curved inward at the top, and the lane narrowed as it went, and the air moved through it in one direction only — eastward, toward the chapel, a slow warm current like breath drawn through parted lips. Her torch beam caught motes drifting past her, all of them traveling the same way, and she tried to tell herself it was wind. There was no wind. The thorn branches stood perfectly still, black against the last gray light, and the motes drifted east, and the bell rang again, and this time she felt it pass through her chest and out her back, between the shoulder blades, the way a hand passes through a curtain.

She stopped. She put her hand against her sternum, where Mrs. Penhale had put hers, and she hated herself for it, and she kept walking.

The tolls were not regular. That was one of the things she had not said to anyone, because there was no one to say it to who would not have reached for the telephone to the county doctor, and she could not be the madwoman of Hollowmere; she had patients. But the tolls were not regular, and they were not random either. They came the way a dying man's breath comes in the last hours — not on any clock, but with a terrible internal logic, each one a little different, each one *listening*. She had sat with enough deaths to know the sound of something paying attention. A bell that rang at dusk was weather. A bell that paused, and then rang again as if in answer to something — that was a voice.

And beneath the bell, so faint she could not have sworn to it under oath, there was the singing.

It was not words. It was the *shape* of words, the rise and fall of a hymn heard through a wall, through water, through the years. She had caught it first at Mr. Tallow's bedside two nights ago, in the silence after his counting ended — a thread of melody rising out of the stillness like heat-shimmer off a summer road, gone when she turned her head. She had caught it again last night, and again tonight, and tonight it was louder, or she was closer, and it had a quality she recognized from her work: it sounded like many people trying very hard to be gentle.

The dying sang like that, sometimes. Not the ones in pain — the ones past pain, the ones drifting on the last warm tide, who would open their eyes at the ceiling and move their lips and let fall a few notes of something old, something their mothers had sung, tuneless and tender and utterly certain. Rosalind had always found it the most bearable part of her work. The singing meant the fear was gone.

The singing under the bell sounded like that. And that was the worst thing she had learned yet: whatever waited at the end of Old Chapel Road, it was not frightening the way wolves are frightening. It was frightening the way a warm bath is frightening to a freezing man who knows the water is not water.

* * *

The lane opened, and the chapel rose out of the dark.

It had no roof. It had had no roof for longer than anyone living could remember — the slate taken for barns, the beams for firewood, the lead stripped and sold in some harder century — and so the nave stood open to the sky like a ribcage, the arches climbing and stopping midair, holding up nothing, holding up the weather. In daylight it was a gentle ruin, softened by ivy and wildflowers, a place for picnics and photographs. Tonight the arches were black cuts against a sky the color of iron, and the tower stump stood at the east end like a broken tooth, and Rosalind stood at the edge of the lane with her torch off and her breath smoking and understood that she had walked into the bell.

There was no bell. She could see the whole of the ruin from where she stood; the tower was open to the elements, a hollow shell no higher than a man and a half, and she could see the stars through its broken crown. There was no bell in it. There was no bell anywhere. There was no rope, no wheel, no beam to hang from, no floor to stand on. And yet the air above the tower *shuddered* — she could see it, actually see it, a trembling in the starlight like heat off a road, like the air over a struck drumhead — and with each shudder the toll came, deep and vast and impossibly close, a sound with weight, a sound with a body, rolling through the stones and through her boots and up into the roots of her teeth.

The bell was not in the tower. The bell was in the *air*. Or under the ground. Or in the stone itself, in the old limestone, sleeping in the walls the way heat sleeps in bricks long after the fire is out.

"Where are you," she whispered, and her voice came out very small, and the toll answered it — not in words, nothing so kind as words — but the next toll came sooner, and deeper, as if her voice had been a knock and something inside had turned over in its sleep and said *yes, I'm here, come in.*

She did not go in. She was proud of that, later, in the way one is proud of small mercies in a catastrophe. She stood at the boundary where the lane became the chapelyard — and there was a boundary; she could feel it, a line drawn in the dark like the edge of deep water, the place where the warm eastward current of the air stopped and pooled — and she swept her torch beam slowly along the ground.

The grass of the chapelyard was long, unmown for years, silvered with the first frost of the season. And through it, pressed flat, ran a path.

Footprints. Dozens of them. Hundreds. All pointing in.

They came from every direction — from the lane where she stood, from the fields to the north, from the collapsed wall on the south side — converging on the ruin like the spokes of a wheel, and every single one of them pointed east, pointed in, pointed toward the tower stump. The frost had not filled them. That was the detail her mind circled and circled, unable to set down. It was cold enough that her own breath smoked; the frost had been down since midnight, thick enough to silver the thorns; and yet the footprints lay crisp and fresh-edged in the grass, as if the people who made them had passed through minutes ago, or were passing through still, or had never stopped passing through at all — a hundred years of arrivals compressed into a single unweathered moment, all of them coming, all of them recent, all of them *now*.

She walked the edge of the yard with the torch held low, following the boundary line, and she looked for the prints going the other way.

She looked for a long time. She was thorough; it was her nature, and her training, and her curse. She walked the whole southern wall. She walked the lane on both sides. She knelt in the frost at the mouth of the path her own boots had just made and she examined the converging tracks the way she had once examined Mr. Tallow's chart, line by line, looking for the thing that would make the story make sense.

There were no footprints leading out.

None. Not one. Not a single heel-drag, not a single print turned the wrong way, not a single set of tracks that doubled back or wandered or hesitated at the boundary the way hers had. Everyone who had walked up Old Chapel Road had walked in. And no one — not in all the frosts, not in all the years the grass had grown unmown over a hundred years of arrivals — no one had ever walked out.

The torch beam trembled. She steadied it against her knee and made herself breathe, the way she did in the ward, four counts in, six counts out, because panic was a luxury the dying could not afford her. In the silence between tolls she could hear the singing again, clearer now, and she could hear something else beneath it — a rustling, a settling, like a congregation taking their seats, like coats brushed and hymnals opened, the small courteous sounds of people arranging themselves in the dark. The tower stump stood empty against the stars. The nave stood empty under the arches. And the air was full.

"Rosalind."

She did not hear it with her ears. That was the only way she could ever describe it afterward, and she never did describe it, not to anyone, not for years. It came from the bell's direction but not through the air — it arrived inside her the way her mother's voice had once arrived inside her dreams, with the intimacy of blood, with the terrible familiarity of a name spoken by someone who has known you since before you could speak. It did not say anything else. It only said her name, once, in a voice like bronze and like an old woman's, in a voice that was almost — she would spend the rest of her life not saying this either — almost like her own.

The frost crept up her boots. The bell tolled, and the air shuddered, and the empty arches held their silence, and somewhere in the walls, in the stone, in the ground beneath a hundred years of grass, something waited with the patience of bronze for her to come the rest of the way in.

Rosalind Eastwick backed away. She backed away the whole length of the chapelyard, never turning her back on the ruin, the way you do not turn your back on the dying, because it is rude, and because they are always watching. At the lych-gate she turned at last, and walked, and then — she would not remember deciding this — she ran, down the lane, through the blackthorn, against the warm eastward current of the air, all the way to her own door.

She lit every lamp. She salted the threshold. She counted the doors twice, and then a third time, which broke her own rule, because the rules were all she had and the rules had not followed her home. The bell tolled behind her, softer now, farther, and beneath it the singing went on, gentle and patient and many-voiced, and it did not sound like a warning at all.

It sounded like a welcome, held open, for as long as it took.

She sat up all night with the notebook of names open on her knee and did not write in it. And it was not until the gray beginning of morning, when the tolls had stopped and her hands had stopped shaking, that she understood the thing that would not let her sleep again for a week: the footprints had been fresh. All of them. Every one. Which meant that somewhere in Hollowmere, tonight and every night, people were getting up out of their beds and walking east — and no one had noticed, because no one was left behind to notice, because whatever walked up Old Chapel Road did not come back to be missed.

Except that was not quite the worst of it. The worst of it came an hour later, in the pale light, when she finally took off her boots — and found the chapelyard's long grass and the silver frost still clinging to the laces, and the hem of her coat stiff with frost to the knee, and her stockings soaked, and no memory at all of kneeling down.

She had stood at the boundary. She had been so sure she had stood at the boundary, and only at the boundary, the whole time.

The bell had not rung again since dawn. But her feet were cold, and her hem was wet, and on the inside of her left wrist, faint as an old scar, the skin was pink and tender, as if something had been written there and had only just finished fading.
