## 14. One Hundred and Eleven Days

The surgery at Hollowmere kept its records the way old men kept teeth — reluctantly, and with gaps. Elias Marsh had inherited the ledgers from Dr. Prewitt, who had inherited them from a Dr. Salter whose handwriting looked like a spider drowned in ink. Forty years of names, complaints, doses, deaths. He sat at his desk on the first Tuesday of November with the lamps lit at four in the afternoon because the dusk had begun arriving like a creditor, and he turned page after page, and he did not find what he was looking for until he stopped looking for it.

He had been hunting for the pattern in the wrong place. He had thought, at first, that the disappearances would follow the bell's tolling — that the taken would go the night the sound was loudest, the way moths went to lamplight. But the verger, Mr. Pettibone, had gone on a Thursday of no particular note. Edith Tallow's sister had been seen last on a market morning, haggling over onions. The dates refused to rhyme with the tolls.

It was only when he laid the parish burial register alongside the surgery ledger — two columns of names in two different inks, forty years deep — that the thing surfaced, the way a body surfaces in a flooded quarry: slowly, then all at once.

Every disappearance, every death at the count of one, every patient who had hummed that wretched verse in his consulting room with their eyes gone soft and elsewhere — they clustered. Not around the tolls. Around the moon.

He sat back. The lamp hissed. Outside, the lane was going the colour of pewter, and somewhere east of the village — always east — the air carried that faint pressure he had stopped denying, a sound felt in the sternum rather than the ear, like a door being closed very slowly inside his own chest.

He took a fresh sheet and began, properly this time, to work.

* * *

There were eleven names now. He wrote them down the way he wrote down symptoms, because that was the only honest way he knew to hold a thing: verger Pettibone, the Ashcombes' lodger, the two farmhands from Nether Fen, the widow Crane, the deputy — no. Not yet the deputy. That was coming. He could feel it coming the way one felt a fever in a household, moving bed to bed. He wrote the nine confirmed and left two lines blank, which he hated himself for, and which proved, within the week, to be a kind of prophecy.

Beside each name, the date. Beside each date, the moon.

The verger had gone four nights before the full. The lodger, six. The farmhands together, on the dark of the moon — and Elias remembered that night, because he had been called out to Nether Fen for a calving and had walked home under a sky with no moon in it at all, and the whole way the silence had seemed to lean against his shoulders, listening.

He turned to the liturgical calendar that hung, yellowed and curling, behind the surgery door. Prewitt had been a churchwarden before he was a doctor, and the old feasts were marked in faded red: Michaelmas, All Saints, the Ember Days, Advent. Elias laid the calendar flat beside his columns and ran his finger down the dates of the taken.

All Saints' Eve. One of the Ember Days. The feast of a minor saint whose name he had to look up — a bell-founder, of all things, a man called Eligius, patron of metalworkers and their furnaces.

He sat very still for a long moment, and the lamp hissed, and he thought: *it is keeping a calendar. It is keeping ours.*

That was the thought that undid the last of his comfort. He could have borne a beast. Beasts were fed by appetite and appetite was stupid; beasts could be starved, poisoned, shot. But this — this was a thing that read the church calendar and the moon's ledger together, that took its tithe on holy days the way the parish once took its tithes on Lady Day, that had learned, over a hundred and eleven years of being melted and silent and forgotten, to keep the old offices. It was not hungry. It was *observant*. And observant things could not be starved, only answered.

* * *

He went to Rosalind that evening, because he always went to Rosalind in the end, and because the arithmetic in his pocket had grown too heavy to carry alone.

She opened the door before he knocked — she had taken to doing that, and he had taken to wondering whether she heard his footsteps or something older in them — and her house was lit like a chapel at midnight, every lamp burning, every bulb, the hall and the parlour and even the cupboard under the stairs, where a single candle stood in a saucer of salt. She looked as though she had not slept in a week, which was accurate, and she looked at his face and said, "You've found something," before he had said a word.

"How do you know?"

"Because you have your ledger face." She stepped back to let him in. "The one you make when the news is bad but the news is at least *organised*."

He spread his papers on her kitchen table, under the brightest of her lights, and walked her through it: the columns, the moons, the feasts. She listened the way she did everything — completely, without flinching, her hands folded on the table as though holding it down against a wind he could not feel. Only once did her hands tighten, and that was when he laid his finger on the last date.

"The winter solstice," he said. "The twenty-first of December. It's not a church feast, not exactly — the old calendar had it as the feast of Saint Thomas, but the older calendar than that had it as nothing at all, the longest night, the hinge of the year. Every pattern in the ledger points to it. The intervals are shortening — the bell is taking its tithe more often as it approaches, the way a fever climbs before the crisis. And the moon that night—" He stopped. He made himself say it. "The moon that night is new. The darkest night of the darkest season. If the ledger holds, that is the night the feeding completes. That is the night it either gets its last verse from a free voice, or it takes the town entire."

"How long?" Rosalind asked. Her voice was level. He had learned that her levelness was not calm; it was a wall she built and manned, and he had come to think of it as one of the bravest things he had ever watched a person do, night after night, without relief.

"Forty-two days," Elias said. "Six weeks. A hundred and eleven days since the first toll — I checked that too, and I wish I hadn't. A hundred and eleven years since the bell was cast. It's counting, Rosalind. It's been counting in its own currency the whole time, and the solstice is the coin it's saving up for."

She was quiet. The candle in the cupboard under the stairs guttered, righted itself, guttered again, as though something on the far side of the house's walls were breathing against the flame. Rosalind rose without a word and went and cupped her hand around the candle, and it steadied, and she came back and sat down, and neither of them remarked on it, because they had passed beyond remarking.

"Forty-two days," she said at last. "And you came here first. Not to Dyer, not to the vicar."

"Dyer would raise a posse. The vicar would raise a prayer. Both would be useful and neither would be sufficient." He looked at his own hands on the table — a doctor's hands, kept clean, kept steady, and lately kept from shaking only by an act of will he was spending like a man spending his last banknotes. "I came to you because you're the only person in this village who has known from the first night that the world was not what it pretends to be. You built your whole life against a thing like this. I need to learn how you did it, because in forty-two days I suspect we'll all be living in your house."

She almost smiled. It was a small thing, worn at the edges, but it was real, and he held onto it the way a drowning man holds a spar — not because it will carry him, but because letting go is unthinkable.

* * *

The days that followed had the quality of a held breath.

Elias began keeping a second ledger — not the surgery's, but his own, a cheap exercise book from the post office with a marbled cover, in which he wrote everything: the tolls and their hours, the hummings reported by whom, the moon's phase copied from the almanac, the temperature of the air at dusk (colder east of the churchyard, always colder, as though the road to the ruin ran through a doorway left open in winter). He measured. It was the only prayer he knew. If the thing was keeping a calendar, then he would keep one too, and perhaps the two calendars could be set against each other like opposing armies, and perhaps — this was the hope, thin as a winter sun — knowing the hour of the assault would let him stand at the wall when it came, rather than asleep behind it.

The village, meanwhile, went on pretending, and the pretending had begun to curdle. He saw it in the surgery. Mrs. Hobb came in for her drops and hummed four bars of the verse while he took her pulse, and when he asked her where she'd learned it she looked at him with mild, untroubled eyes and said, "Everyone knows it, Doctor. Don't you?" And the terrible thing, the thing he wrote in the marbled book that night with a hand that was not quite steady, was that when she hummed it he had felt his own lips want to follow. Not his mind — his lips. The melody lived in the body now, below thought, the way a heartbeat lived, and no ledger of his could reach that far down.

He interviewed them, one by one, the hummers, with the pretext of a survey on seasonal sleep. Their memories were intact in every particular except one: the song. Ask them their mother's maiden name, their wedding day, the price of coal in the hard winter of '62, and they answered promptly. Ask them where they first heard the verse, and they smiled — that was the word, smiled, all of them, the same mild untroubled smile — and said they had always known it. The melody had not been added to their minds. It had been *revealed* as having always been there, which was a different and far worse operation, because a thing added could be removed, but a thing revealed had roots.

Except in Rosalind. He tested her, gently, without telling her he was testing her, the way one tested for a condition one dreaded naming. She could not carry the tune. When she tried to hum what the others hummed, the melody slid off her like water off oiled cloth; she got three notes and lost the thread, and her face when she lost it was not relieved but frightened, as though the slipping were itself a symptom. He did not tell her what he suspected — that she was not immune so much as *reserved*, that the bell's song parted around her the way a crowd parts around someone expected. He had no proof. He had only the shape of the absence, and the shape frightened him more than any presence would have.

* * *

On the ninth of November he did the arithmetic again, because he did it every night now, the way Rosalind counted her doors.

Forty-two days. He wrote it at the head of a fresh page and then, because his mind would not leave it alone, he began to break it down the way he broke down a prognosis. Forty-two days was six Sundays. Was one full moon and one waning half and one last thin crescent dying into the solstice dark. Was — he counted the surgery book — an estimated eleven deaths at the count of one, the ordinary deaths, the ones that came to every village every winter, except that now each of them would be a small rehearsal, each dying patient counting backwards toward a bell that would be listening. Was an unknown number of taken, if the intervals kept shortening. He plotted them on graph paper, the disappearances against the calendar, and drew the curve, and the curve bent upward, gently, patiently, like the neck of a swan, like a scythe being lifted.

And yet — and this was the hope, and he clung to it with both hands and knew it was thin — the curve could be read the other way. The bell was on a schedule. A scheduled thing could be met. Dyer could have his watchers at the ruin on the solstice night not as a mob but as a garrison. The vicar could have his prayers said at the right hour instead of the wrong one. And Rosalind — Rosalind could be kept from Old Chapel Road on the longest night, locked in her lit house with her salt and her candles, guarded if need be by him and Dyer and every sober man in the parish, because the ledger said the feeding completed at the solstice, and a feeding needed feeding *from*, and if the free voice it wanted was not there—

He stopped writing. The lamp hissed. And in the silence after the lamp's hiss he heard, very faintly, from the east, from the direction of the ruin, the pressure against his sternum that was not quite a sound, and it seemed to him — and he wrote this down too, because he had promised himself he would write down even the shameful things — it seemed to him that the pressure *paused*. The way a listener pauses. The way a thing that has been reading your calendar reads, for one instant, yours.

He looked at the page. At the top, in his own neat doctor's hand, he had written: *42 days.*

And beneath it, without his having decided to write it, in the same hand, because his hand had been resting on the pen and his mind had been elsewhere: *she will walk up the road. she always was going to. ask her not to and watch her not promise.*

He sat for a long time looking at those words. He did not cross them out. Crossing them out would have been a lie with a line through it, and he had promised Rosalind once — or had he only promised himself? — that between the two of them there would be no more of those.

* * *

He found her at the hospice two evenings later, doing her rounds with the lamp turned high, and he told her everything — the curve, the solstice, the new moon, the forty-two days — and she took it the way she took everything, standing very straight with her hands quiet, and then she said the thing he had been afraid of, only she said it about herself, which was somehow worse and somehow better.

"Then we have six weeks to learn how to answer it," she said. "Not to run. Not to hide. To *answer*." And she looked east, toward the darkening road, and her face in the lamplight was the face of a woman reading a letter she had known was coming all her life.

That night, in the marbled book, under the words he had written without deciding to, Elias added one more line, and this one he did decide, and his hand was steady when he wrote it:

*111 days since the first toll. 42 remain. The bell keeps its calendar; now I keep mine. And on the longest night, whatever walks up Old Chapel Road, I will be walking beside it.*
