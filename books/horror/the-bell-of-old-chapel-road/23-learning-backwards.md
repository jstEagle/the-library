## 23. Learning Backwards

The kitchen at Foxglove Cottage had become a rehearsal room, though neither of them would have dared call it that aloud. Words like *rehearsal* implied a performance to come, and the performance they were preparing for was not one either of them wished to imagine too precisely. So they called it *practice*, the way one calls a funeral a *service*, and they kept the lamps burning low and the curtains drawn against the dusk that pressed at the glass like a face.

Rosalind sat at the scrubbed pine table with a sheet of paper before her, and on the paper, in ink gone brownish where her hand had paused too long, the verse ran backwards — right to left, end to beginning, each word turned inside out. She had written it in mirror-script because Elias had insisted, and Elias had insisted because of what had happened in the small hours three nights past, when she had caught herself humming the melody forward in her sleep and stopped only at the penultimate note, tasting bronze on her tongue like an old coin.

"You write it forward," he'd said, "and your eyes read it forward, and your mouth wants to follow. The eyes are traitors, Rosalind. We starve them."

So she wrote backwards now, letters reversed, lines running from the right margin toward the left, and to read them she had to hold the page up to the lamp's reflection or tilt her head until her neck ached. It was awkward. It was ugly. It was, she thought, exactly as it should be. Nothing about this work should come easily. Ease was how the bell got in.

She mouthed the first line without sound. Her lips shaped the syllables the way a deaf woman might shape a prayer — carefully, completely, giving each consonant its full architecture while letting no breath escape. *Nrub si eht niard.* The rain is burnt. Backwards, the verse was nonsense; forwards, she did not let herself know what it said. That was the discipline. She had spent eleven days building a wall in her own mind between knowing and saying, and every morning she walked its length and checked the mortar.

Elias stood behind her, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him through her shawl but not touching, because touch loosened things, and looseness was dangerous now. He held a tuning fork — absurd, she had told him, there was nothing left in Hollowmere that wanted tuning — and he tapped it gently against his knuckle and listened to the thin silver note with his eyes closed, calibrating himself to a sound that belonged to the ordinary world.

"Again," he said softly. "From the top. And this time, don't let the third syllable go hollow. You're swallowing it."

"I'm not swallowing anything," she murmured. "I'm keeping it."

"Same thing, from where I'm standing." He came around the table and knelt so that his eyes were level with her mouth, which should have been intimate and was instead clinical, and somehow both. "Show me."

She showed him. Lips parting, tongue rising, the shape of the word made whole and silent as a photograph of speech. He watched with the fierce attention he gave to heartbeats, to pupils, to the small betrayals of the body, and when she finished the line he nodded once.

"Better. But you rushed the ending. You always rush the ending. You want it over with."

"Of course I want it over with."

"I know." He reached out and turned her paper ninety degrees, so the mirror-script ran sideways, unreadable even in reflection. "But rushing is how mistakes are born. On the night, you'll have frost climbing your arms and thirteen voices pulling at you and God knows what else, and if the ending isn't in your bones by then — truly in your bones, past the reach of fear — then fear will finish the sentence for you. We don't give it that chance. Again."

She began again. Outside, the light failed by degrees, going from pewter to ash to the deep blue-black of early December, and somewhere eastward, faint as a pulse felt through a wall, the bell tolled the hour it had been tolling since 1911. Neither of them looked up. Looking up had become a luxury they rationed.

* * *

Elias Marsh had delivered babies, set bones, sat with the dying through more long midnights than he could count, and he had thought himself a man who understood patience. Then he had undertaken to teach a woman to sing a song backwards without ever singing it forwards, and he had learned that everything he knew about patience was the patience of a child waiting for Christmas.

The problem was this: the voice was not an instrument one could lock away. It lived in the breath, and the breath lived in the body, and the body had spent forty years learning to answer music with music. When Rosalind heard a melody — any melody, a hymn, a lullaby, the milk cart's jingling harness — something in her throat leaned toward it like a plant toward light. The bell's verse was worse than a melody. It was a hunger wearing a melody's clothing, and it had been feeding on her bloodline for a hundred years.

So he coached her the way he imagined a singing master might coach a soprano whose instrument was made of gunpowder.

"Breathe here," he would say, tapping his own sternum. "Not the chest. Here. If the breath sits high, the syllables get light, and light syllables run away with you."

"Stop the breath entirely," she'd answered once, dry as salt. "That's the safest coaching I've had all week."

"That's the coaching of a coward, and I refuse it." But he had taken her hand then, briefly, and pressed it flat below her ribs, and said, "Here. Low and slow. A breath that heavy can't be hurried."

They worked in the evenings after his rounds, by lamplight, with the doors counted twice and the salt renewed along the thresholds — though both of them knew the salt was ceremony now rather than protection, a rosary of habit. They worked in snatches through the day: in the dispensary, mouthing shapes across the counter while a patient dozed; in the lane between their houses, walking slowly, her lips moving, his hand raised like a conductor's to slow her down. Once, in the churchyard, he had stopped her mid-line and made her start over four times because the fourth syllable kept drifting toward its forward twin, the way a compass needle drifts toward north no matter how you hold the case.

"The danger isn't the big mistakes," he told her that evening, stamping feeling back into his feet in the frozen grass. "You won't suddenly sing the whole verse forward. The danger is the small ones. One note leaning the wrong way. One syllable that comes out rounded instead of broken. The bell doesn't need much. It needs a door left open the width of a note."

"And if I leave one open?"

He hadn't answered. He had looked at her for a long moment with an expression she had never seen on him before — not the doctor, not the friend, but something older and more frightened — and then he had said, "Then we fix it. Whatever happens, we fix it together. That's not a plan, Rosalind. It's a promise. Plans fail. Promises don't know how."

She had wanted to kiss him then. She had wanted it the way the drowning want air, sudden and total and humiliating. Instead she had mouthed the fourth syllable again, correctly this time, broken and backward, and watched his shoulders come down half an inch, and understood that this was what love looked like when it was being forged under weight: not grand declarations but the endless, unglamorous labor of keeping each other precise.

At night he made her rest, properly rest, because a tired voice makes tired errors. He brewed her tea with honey and sat across the table while she drank it, and he had begun, without either of them remarking on it, to hum — low, tuneless, deliberately graceless little sounds, nothing resembling any melody at all, filling the silence so that the silence couldn't fill itself with the verse. He called it *keeping the well stirred*. She called it, privately, the kindest noise she had ever heard.

* * *

On the ninth day of practice, Rosalind dreamed of water.

Not the bell, for once. Not the chapel, not the choir, not her great-grandmother's voice coming up through the floorboards of sleep. Just water — dark, still, the flooded foundry pit from the ledger's margins, perhaps, though she had never seen it — and herself standing at its edge holding a sheet of mirror-script that dissolved whenever she tried to lift it from the surface.

She woke with the taste of bronze again, fainter than before, and lay listening to the house. Every bulb burned. The doors were locked. Downstairs, very softly, Elias — who had fallen asleep in the armchair he now kept by her hearth most nights, sleeping badly, guarding nothing and everything — was making his graceless humming sounds even in dreams.

She rose and went to the table and wrote, in mirror-script, the line she had gotten wrong in the dream, whatever it had been; she couldn't remember, and that frightened her more than remembering would have. Then she practiced it forty times, lips moving in the dark kitchen, silent as a fish, until the shape of it lived in her mouth the way her own name lived there.

When she looked up, Elias was awake, watching her from the chair with his hair flattened on one side and his eyes full of something he was too disciplined to say at two in the morning.

"How long?" he asked.

"Forty times."

"Good." He rubbed his face. "Do it ten more. Then sleep. Frost on the arms costs the body something, even in practice. Especially in practice."

"You sound like my old piano mistress."

"She sounds like a woman who understood instruments." He stood, joints cracking, and crossed to stand beside her at the table. For a moment they both looked down at the paper, at the backwards words crawling right to left like footprints leaving a scene. "Say the last line for me. The true last line. The one your great-grandmother taught you."

Rosalind hesitated. This line was different from the others. It had come to her in the dream of the prisoner, in her great-grandmother's cracked and ringing voice, and it was the line the whole reversed verse bent toward — the keystone, the unringing made flesh. She had never practiced it in front of him. Some animal part of her had hoarded it, as if speaking its shape aloud even silently, witnessed, might spend something irreplaceable.

But she looked at his face — at the exhaustion worn openly now, at the love he had stopped disguising as concern — and she gave it to him. Mouthed it, whole and silent, every syllable given its full architecture, her lips shaping the words that would either save Hollowmere or bury her in bronze.

When she finished, Elias did not nod. He did not correct her. He took her hand, the one still resting on the paper, and pressed it between both of his, and bowed his head over it as if it were something consecrated.

"There," he said quietly. "Now it's in two bodies instead of one. If yours falters, mine remembers the shape of it. That's all I wanted. That's all I've wanted since the ledger."

Outside, far off to the east, the bell tolled — and for the first time since it had begun, the toll sounded different. Thinner. Strained. As if something inside the bronze had heard them practicing, and understood, and was afraid.

Rosalind met Elias's eyes across the dark kitchen, and saw that he had heard it too.

"It knows," she whispered — the only words she permitted herself forward, and even those cost her. "It knows we're coming."

Elias drew her against his chest, above the humming of his graceless, stubborn, loving heart, and said into her hair: "Good. Let it count the days. We've already learned to count backwards."
