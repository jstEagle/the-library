## 27. The Keeper's Confession

The dream did not come like a dream.

Rosalind knew the difference the way a nurse knows the difference between sleep and the shallow pretense of it — by the weight of the air. Dreams had that cotton-soft give to them, that forgiving blur at the edges. This had edges like cut bronze. The floor beneath her bare feet was flagstone, cold enough to ache, and the smell was the smell of Old Chapel Road after rain: wet stone, old lichen, and underneath it something mineral and faintly sweet, like a coin held too long in the mouth.

She stood inside the chapel ruin, but not as it was now. The walls rose whole around her, limewashed and candlelit, and the roof arched overhead in dark oak beams gone amber with age. It was 1911 or some year pressed flat against it. The candles did not flicker. The flames stood straight as needles, as if even fire here had been told to hold still and listen.

And there was a bell.

It hung where no bell had hung for over a century — in the tower arch above her, vast and dark, its bronze surface moving like the surface of deep water. Not swinging. *Breathing.* With each slow swell of its metal, the candlelight bent toward it, and Rosalind felt her own heartbeat lean in sympathy, pulled off its rhythm the way iron filings lean toward a passing magnet.

"You came," said a voice behind her.

Rosalind turned.

The woman standing between two pews was small — smaller than Rosalind had imagined from the ledger's single line of ink, *M. Eastwick, foundry hand*. She wore a foundry apron scorched at the hem and a shawl the color of wet ash, and her hair was pinned up in a style forty years out of fashion even for 1911, because she had been dead longer than she had ever been alive and her ghost had stopped keeping up with the calendar. Her face —

Rosalind's breath caught. Her face was Rosalind's face, aged hard: the same narrow jaw, the same deep-set grey eyes, the same crease between the brows that Rosalind saw every morning in the bathroom mirror and had always privately called *the worry line* and never, until this moment, *the inheritance*.

"Great-grandmother," Rosalind said. Her voice in the dream sounded strange to her — too loud, too alive, ringing against the stone the way a dropped spoon rings against a hospital tray.

"Marian." The woman said it simply, offering it like a hand. "They wrote me into the ledger as M. Eastwick, as if I were a sack of nails. Marian is what my mother called me when I skinned my knees. You may use it. We are past formality, you and I."

"You're the one who's been calling me."

"I've been *ringing* you," Marian corrected gently. "There's a difference, and before this night ends you will understand it better than any living soul should have to." She looked up at the breathing bell, and something moved across her face that Rosalind recognized with a sick lurch — it was the expression Mr. Tallow had worn on his last evening, counting backwards, weary and unafraid and utterly unable to stop. "Come away from the tower arch. She listens better when we stand apart from her."

"She?"

"The bell has a name. They gave it one at the casting, thinking a name would tame it. *Concordia.* Harmony. The foundry master believed if you christened a thing sweetly, it would grow into the name." Marian led her down the nave, her footsteps making no sound at all while Rosalind's rang and echoed. "He was wrong about that. He was wrong about most of it. But he kept good books, and so do I. Sit."

There was a pew, and Rosalind sat, and the wood was solid and real and cold through her nightdress, and she clung to that solidity the way she clung to the click of a counted door.

* * *

"I need to know what you were going to tell me," Rosalind said. "In the dream before this. There was a verse —"

"There is always a verse." Marian sat beside her, hands folded on her knees, and up close Rosalind could see that the woman's throat bore a thin pale scar, circling, like a collar worn so long it had become part of the skin. "But first you must understand what happened in 1912, because everyone who might have told you is either dead or *in* the metal, and the ones in the metal only sing forward. Forward is all they can do now. That is what being taken means, child. It isn't death. Death lets go. This—" she touched her scarred throat "—this holds."

The bell swelled above them. The candle flames all leaned east, toward Old Chapel Road, toward the ruin as it stood in the waking world — roofless, empty, innocent-looking in the way of wounds healed over but not healed.

"In 1911 they poured thirteen of us into her," Marian said. "I was the fourteenth voice at the pour — the free voice, the one that sings the last line to finish the feeding. That's how it works, how it has always worked. A bell like Concordia is cast hungry. She drinks the verse, all but the end of it, and the end must be given willingly by someone whose blood is in the bronze and whose feet are still on the earth. A tithe of voices and a gift of one. My father thought he was making a church bell. What he made was a mouth."

"And you sang the last line." Rosalind's own voice had gone very quiet. "That's why the village didn't vanish in 1911."

"I sang it." Marian's eyes closed. "And I have regretted the shape of every syllable since. Because here is what the foundry master's book does not say, what I learned too late: finishing the feeding does not end anything. It only sets the table. Concordia ate our thirteen and was satisfied for a season — a long season, sixty years, then less, then less again. Hunger comes back, Rosalind. Hunger *always* comes back, and each time it comes back larger. And each time, she needs a new last line from a new free voice of the line. Your grandmother was asked. She paid. Your mother was asked. She paid. And now the hunger has come round to you."

"But the taken villagers — Edith Tallow, the verger — if the feeding finished in 1911, why are they—"

"Because I tried to stop it." Marian opened her eyes, and in them Rosalind saw something that frightened her more than the bell did: not guilt exactly, but the terrible patience of someone who has been rehearsing an apology for a hundred years. "Listen carefully now, because this is the part that matters, and I can only say it in dreams, and dreams are thin ice."

She took Rosalind's hands. Her grip was cold and dry and strong, a working woman's grip, foundry-strong, and Rosalind did not pull away.

"In 1912, when I understood what I had done — when the first of the tithed began appearing in the bell's sound, their voices woven into her toll — I went back to the ruin with my father's book and I found the page he had torn out. He tore it because he could not bear it, not because he wanted it hidden. On that page was the way to *unring* a bell. To unwind the casting. To give back everything she swallowed."

"And?" Rosalind whispered, though she already knew. She had known since she'd read the ledger, since the dying woman had gripped her wrist. You always knew the endings of these stories; you just hoped you were wrong.

"And it half-worked." Marian smiled, and it was the saddest thing Rosalind had ever seen. "Half-worked is worse than failed, child. Remember that. Half-worked is how you become part of the machine. I sang the verse backwards — the true reversal, syllable by syllable, note by note, the whole architecture of the hymn inverted — and Concordia began to crack. I felt the thirteen loosen in her. I felt them *almost breathe*. And then, on the final line, I faltered. One note. I sang it forward instead of back — a slip no bigger than a miscounted door — and she took me. Not into the bronze. Into the *ringing*. I am not in the bell, Rosalind. I am in the sound. Every toll she has rung since 1912, my voice is inside it, going out over your fields, your rooftops, your hospice ward. When the dying count backwards and hear something in the silence beneath the numbers — that is me. That has always been me. Trying to finish what I started, one toll at a time, for a hundred years, and getting nowhere alone."

The candles guttered — the first movement they had made — and steadied.

"So the plan in Tallow's book," Rosalind said slowly, "the unringing — it's been done. Attempted."

"It's been *half*-done. Which means the door is open a crack, and doors open a crack are easier to open wide than doors shut fast. That is your advantage, and it is a thin one, and you must not mistake it for safety." Marian leaned closer. "But there is more. There is the reason I let you find the ledger, the reason I have been pulling you down Old Chapel Road in your sleep like a fish on a line, and I will tell it plainly because you have no time left for riddles."

* * *

"The verse you've been learning," Marian said, "the one humming through the village, the one Wren Ashcombe carries like a stone in her pocket — it is incomplete. Concordia keeps it incomplete on purpose. The version the town hums, the version the choir walks to, ends on a suspended note — an unresolved chord, a question mark set to music. That is the hunger. That is the wanting. Every soul that sings it forward is answering her question wrongly, and every wrong answer feeds her a little more."

"And the true final line?"

"Is not in the town's version at all. It was never sung aloud after 1911. It exists in three places only: in my memory, which is now part of her ringing; in the torn page, which you have; and—" Marian's cold fingers tightened "—in the blood. In you. You have heard it your whole life, Rosalind, in the moments you thought were your curse. When the dying count backwards and reach one, and there is a half-second of silence before the end — you have heard the first notes of the true line in that silence. You thought the counting was the curse. The counting is only the bell's shadow falling across you. The curse, if we must call it that, is that you were born hearing what everyone else has forgotten."

Rosalind sat very still. Somewhere far above, in the tower of the dream, the bell breathed in, and the whole chapel leaned toward it like grass toward wind.

"Teach me," she said.

"I am going to teach you nothing," Marian said. "You are going to remember. There is a difference, and the difference is everything. If I teach it to you, it passes hand to hand, and Concordia can take what is handed. But if you *remember* it — if it rises out of your own blood, your own nights at the bedside, your own hundred counted deaths — then it is yours, grown from your own root, and she cannot pull it up without pulling up all of you, and she cannot have all of you unless you give it." Her grey eyes held Rosalind's. "Do you understand? Willingness is the lock on every door in this house. She needs your consent shaped correctly. Starve her of it."

"How do I remember it?"

"Go back to the deaths." Marian's voice softened, and for a moment she sounded less like a hundred-year-old ghost and more like what she had been: a tired woman at a foundry bench, doing the work in front of her. "Every patient you have sat with. Every backwards count. Thirty, twenty-nine, twenty-eight — you heard the numbers fall away, and beneath them, under the last number, under the *one*, there was a sound you filed away as silence because silence was safer. It was not silence. Go down into those silences, Rosalind. All of them. Tallow's room. Mrs. Petch. The young man from the quarry, years ago, who counted so fast. The line lives in the space after *one*, where the living cannot follow and the dying have not yet arrived. That gap is hers and it is mine and it is yours, and the true final verse is stitched through every one of those gaps like thread through cloth."

Rosalind felt tears on her face and did not remember starting them. "And if I get it wrong. If I slip, like you slipped."

"Then you join me in the ringing," Marian said simply. "And the village follows, and Wren Ashcombe grows up inside a bell, and your doctor stands at the edge of Old Chapel Road until his knees give out, listening for a voice that will never again say his name. So don't slip." She almost smiled. "Or rather — slip, and be caught. But we will come to that."

"What do you mean, caught?"

Above them, the bell's breathing changed. Quicker now. Shallower. The candle flames all bent east at once, and the flagstones under Rosalind's feet began, very faintly, to hum.

"She's waking," Marian said, rising. "Dawn is coming in your world, and dawn makes her drowsy, but she stirs before she sleeps, like a dog turning twice before lying down. Listen to me now, quickly, because I can only hold this shape while the dream holds."

She gripped Rosalind's shoulders, and her cold seeped through the linen of the nightdress like water through paper.

"The true final line — the one that completes the unringing — begins with the word your patients hear at the end of every count. Not *one*. The word under *one*. Say it with me, in your mind only, never aloud, never aloud —"

And Rosalind opened her mouth, and no sound came, and yet she *knew* it — felt it rise in her chest like water finding a level, a syllable she had heard a thousand times in a thousand sickrooms without ever knowing she was hearing it, the word that lived in the gap after the counting stopped, warm and small and terribly familiar—

The bell tolled.

Once. The dream shattered like a struck window, candles snuffing, walls dissolving, Marian's grip tearing away — and in the last instant before waking, Rosalind saw her great-grandmother's face one final time, and it was not afraid. It was fierce. It was the face of a woman handing over a hammer.

"*Remember the gap,*" Marian said, receding into the dark like a figure into fog. "*And Rosalind — when you climb the road tomorrow night, whatever she offers you, whatever shape the offer wears — look at her hands. Look at her hands and you will know—*"

* * *

Rosalind woke with frost on the inside of the window and her own name dying on her lips.

The bedroom was dark except for the streetlight's orange seep through the curtains, and she lay still for a long moment, cataloguing herself the way she catalogued a ward at the start of a shift: heart rate elevated, hands cold, cheeks wet, jaw aching from clenching. Ordinary human inventory. She was almost relieved by it.

Then she realized she was mouthing something. Had been mouthing it, perhaps, all night — her lips moving silently in the dark, shaping a single syllable over and over, the word from the gap, the word under *one*.

She reached for the notebook on the nightstand — the notebook of names, the ledger of the counted — and by streetlight she wrote, in careful mirror-script that would mean nothing to anyone who glanced over her shoulder, not the word itself but its description, the only safe way to keep it:

*The last word is the first word. It is what I say to them anyway. It has been in my mouth for years.*

She stared at it. Her breath fogged the page.

Because it was true. She knew it the way she knew the weight of a sleeping hand. All those vigils, all those counts ending at one, all those times she had leaned close and given the dying the only gift she had — and the word she had whispered was the word. It had always been the word. The bell hadn't needed to teach her the final line.

It had only needed her to notice she already carried it.

Outside, far to the east, so faint it might have been the house settling, so real it made her teeth ache — the bell rang once, low and questioning, like a mother calling a child home before dark.

Tomorrow was solstice eve.

Rosalind got up, checked every light, counted both doors twice, and began to practice remembering.
