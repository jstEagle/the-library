## 35. Aftermath in Hollowmere

The frost came early that year and stayed like a guest who would not be persuaded to leave.

It silvered the hedgerows along Old Chapel Road, thickened the puddles into small grey mirrors, and lay on the garden wall — the new one, the low one — in a crust that glittered at dawn as though the stones had been sugared by some enormous, careful hand. The villagers of Hollowmere had built that wall themselves, stone by stone, through the worst weeks of January, when the ground was hard enough to bruise a spade and no one suggested waiting for spring. Waiting was not something Hollowmere did anymore. Waiting was what the bell had taught them, and they were done being taught.

The chapel stones came down quietly. There was no vote, no argument, no notice in the parish newsletter. Constable Dyer simply arrived one grey Tuesday morning with a cart, two crowbars, and a thermos of tea, and began prying at the west wall where it still stood hip-high against the sky. By noon there were eleven of them. By dusk, thirty. No one had called them. They had woken that morning, looked east toward the ruin, and understood without discussion that today was the day.

They worked in silence for the most part. Not the heavy silence of grief — they had had enough of silences that listened back — but the working silence of people who have agreed on something so completely that speech would only slow it down. The stones came away from each other with a sound like teeth unclenching. Beneath the moss and the birdlime they found the old foundations, blackened mortar, a coin from 1908 pressed flat into the earth as though someone had dropped it a century ago and the ground had decided to keep it. They did not dig deeper than they needed to. There were things under old chapels that Hollowmere had learned, at terrible cost, it did not want to meet.

Edith Tallow carried the first stone to the wall herself.

She was sixty-one years old and she had walked into the dusk choir smiling, waving goodbye to her brother's empty house, and she remembered all of it — the warmth of the road under her feet, the sweetness of the verse in her mouth, the way her own name had seemed suddenly like a coat she could take off. She remembered standing inside the resonance, suspended, singing, watching Rosalind Eastwick walk up the road alone with her chin set like a woman walking into weather. She remembered wanting, in some deep and shameful chamber of herself, to keep singing.

She set the stone on the new foundation, kneeled it level with her bare hands despite the cold, and patted it twice, the way you'd pat a child's shoulder.

"That'll do," she said, to no one, to everyone. "That'll do now."

* * *

The returned were thirteen. Everyone knew the number; no one said it aloud more than necessary. Thirteen figures who had collapsed onto the frost that solstice night, breathing, weeping, alive but marked — marked with a faint bronze shimmer along their collarbones that no doctor could explain and that faded, over the winter, to something like an old scar, or a birthmark, or the memory of a birthmark. Elias Marsh examined each of them twice, then a third time out of pure stubbornness, and found nothing wrong with any of them except what could not be measured: they slept with lamps burning. They flinched at church bells on the wireless. And they remembered everything.

That was the cruelty of it, Elias thought, sitting in his surgery in February with the radiator ticking and four patient files open before him that he was not really reading. If they had forgotten, they could have been told a kind story. But the bell had been pulled out of them root and branch, and its roots had grown through everything. Verger Pettibone remembered three months of standing in the ruin at night, mouth moving out of sync with his own voice, watching himself sing from somewhere just behind his own eyes. The Ashcombes' eldest boy remembered leading his little sister Wren by the hand halfway up Old Chapel Road before their mother caught them at the gate. He was nine. He did not sleep properly until March.

And Edith Tallow remembered waving goodbye.

Elias had expected denial. He had prepared, in his methodical way, a whole architecture of gentle fictions — the cold, the fever, the walking in sleep — that he might offer the returned as scaffolding for a livable version of events. He never used it. On his second visit to Edith Tallow's cottage she had poured him tea, sat him down across the table, and said, "I remember wanting to stay, Doctor. That's the truth of it, and I'll thank you not to soften it." She had wrapped her hands around her cup. "A person ought to know what's in them. Even when it's put there. *Especially* then."

He had written that down later, verbatim, in the notebook he kept now beside the one full of clinical notes — a second notebook, private, in which he recorded things medicine had no column for. *A person ought to know what's in them.* Underneath it he wrote: *Mostly, they forgive themselves. Not because it wasn't real. Because holding it would be letting the bell win twice.*

Mostly. That was the honest word, and Hollowmere had earned the right to honest words. Mostly they forgave themselves. Old Sam Petch, whose wife had gone into the choir in November and come back in January, did not forgive himself at all — or rather, he forgave her instantly and completely and could not extend the same mercy inward, though there was nothing in him requiring forgiveness. He took to walking the eastern fields at dawn, alone, checking fences that did not need checking, and his wife let him do it, understanding that some men mend by moving and some wounds close from the outside in. By April he was walking those fields with her again, and neither of them ever spoke of the night she'd left, and this too was a kind of healing — imperfect, unsentimental, held together like the garden wall: stone by stone, by hands that shook a little.

* * *

The wall went up along the line where the chapel's north wall had stood, and nowhere else. It was waist-high, dry-laid without mortar, so that it could be taken apart again if it ever needed to be — a promise and a provision both. Someone — no one admitted to it, but everyone suspected Mrs. Hollis the postmistress, who had strong opinions and stronger wrists — had insisted the stones be laid with their carved faces turned inward, buried, so that no fragment of the old consecration showed to the sky. What grew against it would grow against plain stone.

And things did grow. That was the surprise of the spring. In March, while the wall was barely knee-high, Wren Ashcombe appeared at the site with a jam jar of snowdrops she had dug up from the verge, roots and all, and planted them in the gap between two stones with the absolute authority of a seven-year-old who has decided something is finished. No one moved them. By May the wall wore a ragged green hem, and by midsummer it was crowned with wildflowers — campion and yarrow and a straggle of vetch — as though the ground itself had decided to forgive the place faster than the people had, and meant to lead by example.

They planted a garden behind it. This had been Rosalind's suggestion, offered in writing, in her careful upright hand, on the back of a prescription pad: *A wall alone looks like fear. A wall with a garden looks like a choice.* The village read it, nodded, and by the last week of April there were bean rows going in and a rhubarb crown that Edith Tallow donated from her own plot with the words, "It'll thrive on neglect, which is more than can be said for the rest of us."

They worked the garden together on Saturdays, the whole shifting population of Hollowmere, and it became — without anyone deciding it — the place where the remembering happened safely. Hands in the soil, backs bent, eyes on the beans: it was easier to say the true things sideways, to the earth, than face to face. Verger Pettibone, weeding, told Dyer about the three months of nights in the ruin, and Dyer, also weeding, told Pettibone about the morning he'd found the boots facing the empty tower and how he had known, even then, even denying it with his whole rational soul, that the boots were right about where their owner had gone. Neither man looked up. Both men felt lighter after. The beans didn't mind.

Children played in the garden, because children play anywhere, and the adults watched them with a vigilance that was learning, slowly, to relax its grip. Wren Ashcombe ruled the bean rows like a small general. She had come through the winter strangely whole — she had hummed the verse flawlessly once and lost her mother's face for a terrible hour, and then Rosalind had shielded her, and then the bell had cracked and given everything back — and the village treated her with a tenderness that bordered on superstition, as though she were a candle that had guttered and steadied and must not be jostled. Wren bore it with the patience of the very young, who know better than anyone that being protected is its own peculiar burden.

* * *

Rosalind Eastwick came to the wall on the first Saturday of every month.

She did not work the garden. Her hands, since the solstice, had a tremor that came and went, and besides, the village had developed a collective, unspoken agreement that Rosalind's labor had been spent in another currency entirely and she owed the soil nothing. So she walked up from the village at a slow pace, sat on the low wall where the north transept had been, and kept the company of whoever was weeding nearest, speaking little, listening much. Her voice, when she used it, was a whisper — a thread of sound, frayed at the edges, enough for a sentence, never enough for a song. She wrote longer things. She always carried the little notebook.

In February she wrote to Edith Tallow, who was turning compost: *You were the bravest of us. You walked in smiling and you walked out weeping and you built the first course of this wall with your own hands. I saw what it cost.*

Edith read it, wiped her hands on her apron, and said aloud, "You saw what it cost, did you? Then write this next: it cost less than not doing it." And she went back to her compost, and Rosalind smiled — a real smile, the first of the new year — and wrote it down exactly as spoken, because Edith Tallow's sentences deserved to survive intact.

With Elias she was different, and the village noticed, and was tactful about noticing, which is to say they noticed enormously and discussed it only in the oblique grammar of small places — a raised eyebrow here, a knowing pause over a fence there. He walked with her on those first Saturdays, matching her slow pace without appearing to slow, and when she tired he offered his arm as naturally as offering salt, and she took it as naturally, and the two of them covered perhaps half a mile in an hour and considered it time thoroughly well spent. He had learned to read her lips over the winter — was learning still, patiently, getting it wrong and laughing at himself — and she had learned the particular quality of silence he made when he was thinking, which was a kind of voice too. They had said the plain words to each other on solstice eve, with the longest night coming down like a lid, and having said things plainly once, they found they could go on saying them. It was not a courtship, quite. It was two people rebuilding on cleared ground, deliberately, with load-bearing honesty.

"You're staring at the wall," he said to her in March, as she sat with her notebook closed on her knees.

She turned. *Counting the courses,* she mouthed.

"How many?"

*Forty-one. One for each day the bell rang before I got up the road.*

"And now?"

She looked at the wall a long moment — the sugared frost long melted off it, the snowdrops gone over, the vetch beginning its purple riot. Then she opened the notebook and wrote, and turned it toward him:

*Now it's just a wall. That's the miracle nobody preaches about. Horrors don't get destroyed. They get outlived, and repurposed, and planted over. The bell isn't gone. But look — beans.*

Elias read it twice. Then he said, quietly, in the voice he saved for difficult diagnoses and for her, "You know that's the wisest thing anyone's said in this village in fifty years," and Rosalind wrote beneath her own words, in smaller script: *Don't tell Mrs. Hollis. She'll want it for the newsletter.*

* * *

Spring came on, indifferent and generous. The lambs arrived in the eastern fields and Sam Petch stopped walking his grief-fences. The returned thirteen shed the last of their bronze shimmer, all but Edith Tallow, who kept a faint gleam at her collarbone like a pendant worn under the skin, and who declared she intended to keep it, as a receipt. The parish council met in April and voted, twelve to none with one abstention (Mrs. Hollis, who abstained on principle whenever she approved of something, believing approval should never be allowed to become complacent), to remove Old Chapel Road from the list of named roads and fold it into the ordinary lane network — not to erase it, for erasure was beyond them and they knew it, but to demote it, to make it merely a road again, a thing you walked to reach somewhere else.

On May Day the children danced the ring around the maypole on the green, and half the village stood watching with their hearts in their mouths, waiting — all of them, though none would say it — for the humming to start. It did not. The fiddler played the same tune he'd played for forty years, badly, and the children sang the same nonsense words, and the bell stayed silent in whatever deep place bells go when they are unmade, and gradually, note by note, the watching hearts came down out of their throats and back to their ordinary stations.

Dyer stood at the edge of the green with his deputy — his new deputy, the old one returned and scarred and re-sworn in a small ceremony in March — and watched the dancing, and said, "Do you hear that?"

The deputy tensed. "Hear what?"

"Nothing." Dyer's face, weathered and unsentimental, almost smiled. "Best sound there is."

But it was not entirely nothing, and Dyer knew it, and by June the whole village knew it, in the way villages know things: wordlessly, comprehensively, overnight. There was a sound sometimes at dusk, very far off, or very near — impossible to place — a single soft toll, smaller than any bell Hollowmere had ever owned, warmer than lamplight. It did not pull. It did not call. It rang, if that was the word, only rarely, and only — the returned thirteen noticed it first, and then everyone — at moments that felt less like summons and more like accompaniment. The night old Mr. Fenwick died in his bed at the age of ninety-four, his daughter swore she heard it, one gentle stroke, as his breathing eased. The night the Petches' mare foaled, Edith Tallow heard it, faint as a pulse, and stood in her doorway with her hand at her collarbone, listening.

The village did not discuss it at the parish council. Some things, Hollowmere had learned, are not improved by committee.

* * *

On the last evening of June, with the light lying gold and long across the garden, Rosalind sat on the wall as she always did, and Wren Ashcombe climbed up beside her as she increasingly always did, planting her small boots on the stones between the vetch.

They sat in the easy silence of two people who have survived the same thing at different scales. Below them, Elias and Edith argued amiably about runner beans. Beyond the wall, the ground where the chapel had stood was all green tangle and bee-drone, and the evening smelled of warm earth and crushed grass, and somewhere a wood pigeon said its one dull sentence over and over, contentedly.

Wren swung her feet. Then she went still, in the sudden total stillness of children approaching a question they have been carrying a long way.

"Rosalind." Her voice was small and very clear. "Is the bell really gone?"

Rosalind turned to look at her. The question hung in the gold light. Down in the garden, the argument about beans went on, unaware. Far off — or very near, impossible to say — something in Rosalind's chest, in the silence the solstice had left there, shifted like a sleeper turning over.

She took out her notebook. She uncapped her pen. And she wrote, slowly, in her careful upright hand, the answer she had been composing all spring without knowing it — the answer she would show the child, and the answer that would follow her home through the dusk, and sit with her through the summer, and wait.

Wren leaned close to read it.
