## 4. The Staff of Bell House

The kitchen at Bell House was the only room that behaved as though the house were loved.

Edmund noticed it his second morning, coming down the back stairs at half past seven with the particular hollow ache of a man who had slept four hours in a strange bed. The corridors up here were cold and smelled of dust and old varnish, of rooms shut for decades, but the kitchen door stood ajar at the end of the hall and poured out warmth like a lit window in a winter painting. Bread. Woodsmoke. Something with cinnamon in it. He followed the smell the way a cold man follows a lamp.

Marisol Vidal was small and round and moved through her kitchen with the economy of someone who had cooked in a hundred places and owned none of them. She was perhaps fifty, with grey shot through her black hair and flour to the elbows, and when she saw him she did not ask if he wanted breakfast. She simply pointed him to a chair at the scrubbed pine table and put a plate in front of him: eggs, toast dark as he liked it, a ramekin of stewed plums.

"I didn't—"

"You did," she said. "Sit. Nurses who skip breakfast faint in hallways and then I have to feed them lying down, which is undignified for everyone."

He sat. The tea arrived before he'd finished the eggs, strong and sweet, and he understood that this was not hospitality so much as a system, long established, into which he had been slotted without consultation. It should have irritated him. Instead he found his shoulders dropping a half inch from his ears, and he distrusted the feeling even as he let it happen.

"Ruth says you don't talk much," Marisol observed, kneading at the far counter without looking at him.

"Ruth is observant."

"Ruth is nosy and calls it observation." Marisol turned, wiping her hands. Her face was open, lined, the kind of face that had decided long ago what it thought of people and revised it rarely. "You slept badly."

"The house creaks."

"The house does more than creak." She said it lightly, the way one says *the weather does more than drizzle*, and went back to her dough, and Edmund, who had a professional gift for letting strange sentences pass unexamined, let that one pass too.

The kitchen filled slowly through the morning. Ivo Petrov came in first, a long grey man with a stoop like a drawn bow and hands that looked carved from the same wood as the garden fences he mended. He nodded at Edmund once, sat, and ate an entire loaf's worth of toast in silence, and the silence was not unfriendly; it was the silence of a man who had used up his words for the day among the roses and had none left for people. Then Tomas.

Edmund had seen the boy—no, young man, he corrected himself; nineteen, maybe twenty—only in glimpses, hauling logs, sweeping the front step, always moving, always with that quick, watchful stillness underneath the motion, like a bird that feeds and feeds and never stops listening. Tomas slid into the seat across from Edmund, poured himself tea, and looked at him with frank, unembarrassed curiosity.

"You're the new nurse."

"I'm the temporary nurse."

"That's what the last one said." Tomas grinned, showing a chipped front tooth. "Temporary. He stayed two years."

"And then?"

The grin flickered—just at the edges, like a candle in a draught. "Then he left." Tomas drank his tea. "They mostly leave."

Edmund let that settle. Around the table, Marisol's kneading slowed, and Ivo looked at his hands, and for a moment the warm kitchen held a cold little pocket of air, there and gone, like walking past a cellar door.

* * *

He learned their histories the way one learns anything at Bell House: sideways, in pieces, over days.

Marisol had come eleven years ago, fleeing a kitchen in a city hotel where, as she put it, "the head chef made my life a recipe for misery," after a divorce that had cost her everything but her knives. She'd answered an advertisement she admitted she never should have—too far, too isolated, too little pay—and had meant to stay a season.

"The season had a funny way of stretching," she said, rolling pastry. "You'd think about going and then—well. Then it's winter. And then it's planting. And then it's Mrs. Marsh's birthday and who'll make the almond cake if I go? And then it's been ten years and you've stopped thinking of it as staying."

Ivo had been a groundskeeper at a big estate in the next county for thirty years until the estate was sold and the new owners wanted, in his words, "a garden like a picture, not a garden like a garden." He'd been sixty-one and unemployable when a letter arrived from Bell House, offering him the walled garden and a cottage and wages that made him wince with embarrassment to accept.

"I wrote back to say I could not in conscience take so little for so much work," he told Edmund, deadpan, pruning shears moving through the rose bed with surgical calm. "Mrs. Marsh wrote back to say the work was not so much as I thought and the wages were exactly as much as I deserved, and that if I argued further she would assume I was too stupid to prune roses, which the references said I was not." A pause. The shears snicked. "That was nine years ago. I have never won an argument with a Marsh and I do not expect to start now."

And Tomas—Tomas was the village's, or rather the village's castoff. Orphaned at nine when his mother's lungs gave out, shuffled between an aunt who hadn't wanted him and a series of placements that hadn't kept him, he had shown up at Bell House's gate at fourteen, half-starved, looking for work, any work.

"Mrs. Marsh looked at me for a long time," Tomas said. They were splitting logs in the yard, or rather Tomas was splitting and Edmund was holding things, an arrangement that suited them both. "Everyone else looked at me and saw a problem. She looked at me and saw a pair of hands and a roof that had room in it." He spat on his palms and gripped the axe. "Ruth says we're strays. Marisol hates the word. But it's true, isn't it. She collected us."

"Collected you."

"Like—" Tomas set the axe head against a log, considering. "Like a woman who can't have children collects other people's. Or like a lighthouse collects ships that are wrecking." He swung. The log fell open, pale and wet-smelling. "She found us all when we were going under. That's not nothing."

No, Edmund thought. It was not nothing. He had spent twenty years in rooms where people were dying and had learned to recognize the shape of a life being held together at the seams, and Bell House was that: a household of people who should, by every economic and social law, have scattered, held in orbit by a dying old woman and a wage structure that made no sense and an atmosphere of—

He didn't finish the thought. He'd learned not to finish thoughts like that. Finish thoughts like that and you started believing them.

* * *

It was Ruth who named it, on his fourth evening, when he found her in the staff sitting room mending a curtain with fierce, tiny stitches.

"Marisol tells me you've been asking questions."

"I asked what people's jobs were. It seemed relevant to a household."

"Mm." Ruth bit her thread. She was a broad, calm woman with iron-grey hair cropped short and eyes that assessed everything twice—once as a nurse, once as something older and warier. "We're a family of strays, Mr. Villalobos. That's the whole of it. The pay is poor, the house is falling down, the village thinks we're odd, and nobody leaves. If you find that suspicious, I'd ask you to consider what your alternatives looked like when you took this post."

He considered them. A hospice in Leeds with a manager who wept in the supply closet. A private client with a son who resented every penny of his father's care. A gap in his CV he couldn't explain. Bell House had offered a room, a salary that was almost fair, and a dying woman who needed him.

"It's not suspicious," he said. "It's just unusual."

"Unusual things get called suspicious by people who've never needed unusual." Ruth tied off the thread and looked up at him, and for a moment the assessment in her eyes was naked, and not entirely kind. "You'll do fine here, Mr. Villalobos. You're competent and you keep your own counsel and you don't flinch in front of the dying, which is rarer than you'd think. But I'll say one thing, and I'll say it once, and then we needn't speak of it again."

"Go on."

"If you ever hear the bell at night—" She stopped. Started again, more carefully, as though choosing her footing on ice. "The tower's empty. You know that. The plaque in the church says the bell was melted down in 1911. Whatever you hear at night, whatever you think you hear, you stay inside. You don't go out to look. You don't go out to help. You don't go out to *see who's ringing it*." Her needle hovered. "There is never anyone ringing it, and going out to find no one is how people get found."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard ticked, the house settling its old bones.

"That's a strange thing to say," Edmund said at last.

"It is." Ruth resumed her stitching, calm as Sunday. "Tea's in the blue tin if you want it. Marisol leaves the lamp lit in the hall for you. She does that for everyone."

* * *

Tomas's turn.

Tomas had heard the bell since he was small. That was the thing nobody told you: it wasn't the house that chose you, exactly. It was the hearing.

His mother had heard it too, near the end, when her chest was full of water and the doctors had stopped pretending. She'd held his hand in the hospital room and said, *Listen, love, they're ringing for someone*, and he'd listened and heard nothing, and she'd smiled at him with that terrible kind patience the dying have and said, *You will.*

He was nine. Four months later she was gone and he could hear it.

Not always. That was the other thing nobody told you. It came and went like weather, like tinnitus, a far bronze shimmer at the edge of sleep, and for years he'd assumed it was grief making noises in his head, and then he'd come to Bell House and heard Marisol say to Ivo, one night in the kitchen, *the bell's early tonight*, and watched them both go still as rabbits, and understood with a lurch that went through him like cold water that he was not mad, or rather that if he was mad, he had company.

They'd taken him in that same week. He'd never worked out whether that was coincidence.

Now the new nurse was here, and Tomas watched him the way you watch weather coming in over the hills—because the new ones were always weather, always something either passing through or settling in for a season that changed the ground. And the nurse was hard to read. He had a face like a locked drawer. He was kind to Mrs. Marsh—genuinely kind, not the performed kindness of the ones who thought dying people couldn't tell—and he was polite to everyone and believed nothing, or said he didn't, and Tomas had learned that the ones who said they believed nothing were the ones the bell liked best to argue with.

Tonight the bell rang early. Not midnight, not three—half past nine, a soft bronze pulse rolling down from the empty tower while the dishes were still being washed, and Marisol's hands stopped in the water, and Ivo set down his cards, and Ruth, in the corner with her mending, said without looking up: "Inside, all of you. Doors checked."

It was ritual. Tomas did the back door, Ivo the shutters, Marisol banked the fire, and the new nurse stood in the middle of the kitchen holding his tea and watching them with an expression Tomas recognized from the mirror of his own fourteen-year-old face: the expression of a man doing arithmetic and getting an answer he doesn't want.

"You all do this," Edmund said. "Regularly."

"We do this," Marisol said.

"The tower's empty. There is no bell. There's a plaque."

"There is," Ruth agreed. "And yet."

The bell rolled again, closer-feeling now, though the tower was a quarter mile off across the churchyard, and Tomas saw Edmund's hand tighten on his cup—not much, a fractional whitening of the knuckles, but Tomas had spent six years learning to read people the way Ivo read soil, and he saw it.

"You hear it," Tomas said. It wasn't a question.

Edmund looked at him. The locked-drawer face, and behind it, faintly, something moving.

"I hear the wind," Edmund said. "In a chimney, perhaps. Old houses make noises."

"Yeah," Tomas said. "They do." He carried his cup to the sink, and at the sink, quietly, under the sound of the water, he said the thing he'd been turning over for three days, the thing Marisol had told him not to say, the thing Ruth would have boxed his ears for. "Mine started when I was nine. My mum was dying. She told me I'd hear it one day, and I told her she was confused, and she said, *no, love, I'm just early.*"

The water ran. The bell rolled again, and this time Edmund didn't say anything at all.

"Third night from now it'll be loudest," Tomas said, drying his hands. "It always builds. So. You know. Stay in. Lock your door. And if you hear your name in it—" he made himself look up, made himself meet the nurse's eyes, because this part mattered, this part was the whole of it— "don't answer. Not out loud, not in your head. It's like the fairies in the old stories. Answering's how it gets your address."

Edmund stood there with his tea going cold, and for a long moment the kitchen held all of them in its warmth like a cupped hand, and Tomas watched the locked drawer of the man's face and saw, for the first time, a hairline crack of light around its edges.

"That," Edmund said finally, "is not a medical instruction I can write in a chart."

"No," Tomas agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

That night Edmund lay in his narrow bed in the east room with the lamp still lit, because Marisol left it lit for everyone and he had decided, without deciding, to leave it lit too.

He ran the day back through his head the way he ran all his days, cataloguing, filing, keeping the professional ledger balanced. Four staff members, each with a history of desperation, each recruited at a moment of maximum vulnerability, each paid poorly, each devoted. A household that performed nocturnal rituals against a bell that did not exist. A boy who spoke of hearing his name in bronze the way other people spoke of allergies.

Every line of it, written in a case note, would read as folie à famille. Shared delusion, culturally reinforced, sustained by isolation and economic dependence. He'd seen it before, milder versions: households that grew strange together, that built little theologies out of grief and habit. It was not sinister. It was human. People needed their rituals, especially the ones living in the shadow of a death.

And yet.

And yet Marisol left the lamp lit. And yet Ruth's voice had gone flat and careful as a person walking on ice, saying *going out to find no one is how people get found*. And yet the boy had looked at him across the sink with those too-old eyes and said *my mum said she was just early*, and something in Edmund, some sealed and dusty room deep in the house of himself, had answered before he could stop it: *I know. I've heard it too.*

He had not heard it. He had heard wind. He had heard the settling of an old house on a cold night, and he had heard, possibly, the memory of a bell-shaped sound from a dream he wouldn't remember by morning.

He was a professional. He did not believe the patients. That was the whole of his craft, the load-bearing wall of twenty years: the dying said extraordinary things, and you held their hands and you did not believe them, because believing them was how you drowned, how you went under with them, how you ended up like poor mad Callum in Leeds who'd started sitting vigils with the families and whispering to them about doors.

The lamp flickered. The house ticked and settled. And far off—far off, at the very threshold of hearing, so faint it might have been his pulse in his ears, might have been wind in a chimney, might have been nothing at all—

—a single bronze note, soft as a fingertip laid on a singing-glass.

Edmund lay still and did not answer it.

He was almost asleep when it came again, and this time it was not one note. It was two. Then three. Patient, spaced, unhurried, like footsteps coming down a long corridor, like someone counting.

Like someone counting down.

He pulled the blanket higher and told himself, in the flat competent voice he used on frightened families, that it was wind, that it was nothing, that in the morning there would be tea in the blue tin and Marisol's cinnamon and Ivo's silence and the boy's quick grin, and that these things—these small warm human things, this accidental family he had not asked to join and was beginning, against all his training and all his rules, to be glad of—were worth any amount of strange noises in the dark.

He was nearly asleep when he understood, with the clarity of the half-dreaming, what was actually frightening him.

It was not the bell.

It was that for the first time in twenty years, he had something he would mind losing—and the house knew it, and whatever counted in the tower had begun, very softly, to count him too.
