## 10. The First Toll Taken

The bell rang on a Thursday, and Ivo went out to fix the gate.

Edmund learned the shape of these nights by then—the way the house changed its breathing when the ringing started, how Marisol would set down whatever she held and stand very still, how Tomas would press his palms flat against his ears as if he could hold his skull together through sheer will. Ruth lit candles. Nobody said much. It had become, in three weeks, a kind of liturgy without words: the bell spoke, and the household of Bell House endured it.

But that night there was wind. That night the east gate—the old iron one at the end of the lime walk, the one that groaned on its hinges even in fair weather—had been banging against its post since dusk, and Ivo had muttered about it all through supper, and when the bell began its low bronze counting he was already pulling on his oilskin coat.

"Ivo." Ruth's voice came from the kitchen doorway, flat and hard as a slammed drawer. "Sit down."

"It'll tear off the post," Ivo said. He was a big man, slow-moving, with hands like root balls and a face weathered into something older than his fifty-odd years. He'd been at Bell House eleven years. He fixed things. Edmund had watched him mend a gutter in the rain rather than let the drip keep Marisol awake. "It's just the gate. The bell's not for me."

"The bell doesn't care who it's for," Ruth said.

And here was the thing Edmund noticed, standing in the corridor with his tea going cold in his hand: nobody moved to stop him. Not really. Ruth said sit down, and Ivo said it's just the gate, and then everyone looked at each other in that way they had—guilty, complicit, tired—and Ivo went out into the dark because the gate needed fixing, and because some part of him, Edmund thought later, needed to prove that the rule didn't apply to him. Eleven years of loyalty. Eleven years of hearing your name woven into bronze and never once answering.

Maybe a man gets proud of that. Maybe pride is the crack the bell waits for.

* * *

The ringing lasted four hours.

Edmund sat up with Agnes, as he did most nights now, though she slept more and spoke less. Her counting had grown fainter, a thread of numbers under her breath like water under ice. *Forty-one. Forty. Thirty-nine.* Never zero. Never close enough to frighten, never far enough to forget.

At some point past three, Tomas came to the door of her room, white-faced, still in his boots and coat.

"He's not come back," Tomas said. "The gate's quiet. I can hear it from the window—it's shut. But he's not come back."

Ruth appeared behind him, candle in hand, and Edmund saw something in her face he hadn't seen before. Not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman watching an old debt arrive at the door with interest accrued.

"Nobody goes out," Ruth said. "Nobody. We wait for light."

"He's been out there two hours," Tomas said. "In October. In the wet."

"We *wait*," Ruth said again, and her voice cracked on the second word, and Edmund understood that this was not callousness. This was a woman who had learned what going out cost, and who would rather grieve a living man than bury whatever came back instead of him.

They waited. Edmund sat with them in the kitchen because he could not have said which of them needed it more. Marisol made coffee nobody drank. Tomas kept getting up to look out the window at the black shape of the lime walk, and each time Ruth pulled him back by the sleeve like a mother with a child at a busy road. The bell had stopped at half past three, mid-count, the way it always did—as if interrupted, as if it would pick up the number where it left off tomorrow.

Dawn came gray and reluctant, the way it does in late October, and the moment the light was strong enough to argue with, Ruth took the lantern herself and led them out.

Ivo was sitting at the base of the gatepost.

He was sitting the way a man sits when he means to get up in a moment—coat on, tools scattered beside him, one hand still resting on the hinge he'd evidently oiled or tightened or simply laid his palm against. The gate itself hung true and silent, freshly greased, latched neat as you please. Whatever Ivo had gone out to do, he had done it.

He had not gotten up.

"Ivo," Ruth said, and knelt, and touched his shoulder, and Ivo turned his head toward her with a slowness that made Edmund's stomach drop before he understood why.

It was the face.

Edmund had spent twenty years around dying people. He knew every speed at which a face could fail—the days-long fade, the hours-long collapse, the gray bloom of the final morning. What he did not know, what no hospice textbook had ever covered, was a man aging forty years between midnight and dawn while remaining alive. Ivo's skin hung in folds at his jaw. His hair, thick and iron-gray yesterday, was white as frost and thin as cobweb over a scalp gone shiny and brown-spotted. His hands, those great root-ball hands, were knotted with joints swollen like walnuts, spotted dark, trembling with a palsy that had not been there twelve hours ago.

His eyes were the worst part. They were clear. Perfectly, horribly clear—young eyes looking out of a ruin, and behind them nothing. Not pain, not fear. Just a vast, polite absence, like a house with all the furniture carried out but the lights left on.

"His mouth," Tomas whispered.

Ivo's lips were parted. Between them, packed into his cheeks, visible when his head tipped back, was earth. Rich, dark, finely crumbled grave-dirt, filling his mouth the way a child fills its pockets with sweets. As they watched, working with terrible patience, Ivo opened his jaw wider and let a little of it fall into his lap, unhurried, unbothered, the way a man spits out tobacco.

"Oh God," Marisol said. "Oh God, oh God—"

"He's alive," Ruth said, and her voice was the nurse's voice, clipped and functional, and Edmund loved her for it and hated the necessity of it. "Ivo. Can you hear me? Squeeze my hand."

Ivo squeezed her hand. Gently. Like a man remembering how.

They got him inside. It took all of them; he weighed less than he should have, as though the years had taken marrow along with everything else. They put him in the ground-floor room that had once been a servant's parlor, and Ruth examined him with the detached competence Edmund recognized from mirrors, and the findings were nonsense and they both knew it: heart steady, lungs clear, reflexes present but sluggish, cognition intact when he chose to use it. A body of ninety wearing a man of fifty-three like an ill-fitting borrowed coat.

"What happened out there?" Edmund asked him, quietly, when the others had stepped away.

Ivo looked at him for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was gravel poured slow.

"Heard my name," he said. "In the bell. First time in eleven years it ever said it plain. Not woven in. Said it. Like—" his throat worked, earth-flecked spit on his lip— "like someone calling you in from the fields for supper. And I thought, well. Eleven years I've been good. Eleven years I've mended what's broken and kept my mouth shut and never once answered. Surely I've earned one small errand. One gate."

He smiled. It was the smile that broke Edmund's heart, because it was Ivo's own smile, warm and rueful, rising out of that ruined face like a flower through rubble.

"And it let me fix the gate," Ivo said. "Let me do the whole job. Latched it nice. And then it said, *there's a good lad*, and it shook my hand." He held up one spotted, trembling claw of a hand. "Cold hand. Long fingers. Smelled of bronze and turned earth. And it said, *you've paid eleven years of interest, Ivo. Now we'll start on the principal.*"

The kitchen was very quiet when Edmund told them—or rather, when Ivo insisted on telling them himself, propped in his chair by the stove, wrapped in two blankets, refusing bed. Marisol cried into a dish towel. Tomas sat rigid, staring at the table, and Edmund watched the boy's hands and saw that they were shaking.

"So it takes your years," Edmund said. "If you answer it."

"It takes what you've given it reason to collect," Ruth said. She stood at the window with her arms crossed, not looking at any of them. "That's what nobody understands about it. It's not cruel. Cruelty would be simpler. It keeps accounts. Ivo gave it eleven years of service and silence, and tonight it decided the account had matured." She turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and afraid. "Do you see? It waited until he felt *safe*. Until he felt owed. That's when it collects. When you think you've earned a favor."

"That's why you don't go out," Tomas said dully. "Not because it's forbidden. Because—" he swallowed— "because going out is answering. Even if you're only fixing a gate."

"Especially then," Ruth said.

Marisol wiped her face. "We should take him to a hospital. To a doctor in the city—"

"And tell them what?" Ruth asked, not unkindly. "That the church bell aged him overnight? The bell that's been melted down for a hundred years?" She shook her head. "There's no doctor for this, Mari. There's no hospital. There's only us. That's what Bell House *is*. It's the only place on earth where what happens to us can be tended."

And that, Edmund thought, watching them gather around Ivo's chair—Marisol already talking softly about soup, Tomas fetching the old man's slippers from his room, Ruth arranging blankets with brisk tenderness—was the most terrible thing he had seen yet. Worse than the fragment in the crypt with his own name cut fresh into it. Worse than the ringing.

Because they were grieving him *and keeping him*. Because the horror had not driven them apart but pressed them closer, wove them tighter, bound them to the house and to each other with bands of shared dread. Fear was doing what love alone might not have managed: making them stay. Making staying feel like devotion. Making any thought of leaving feel like abandonment—not of the house, but of Ivo, of each other, of the family that only existed here, inside the walls, inside the bargain.

Grief, weaponized. Loyalty, invoiced.

He caught Ruth watching him across the kitchen, and he knew she saw it too—saw him see it. Her expression didn't change. But she gave the smallest shake of her head, almost imperceptible, and Edmund understood the message perfectly:

*Don't say it. Don't say it out loud. If you say it, they'll have to decide, and deciding is worse than enduring.*

* * *

Agnes was awake that evening, and sharper than she'd been in days, as if the house's grief had lent her something.

"Ivo went out," she said. It wasn't a question. News traveled strangely in Bell House; Edmund suspected the walls themselves were gossiping.

"Yes. Last night. During the ringing."

"Mm." She lay looking at the ceiling, her count running faint beneath her breath—*nineteen, eighteen*—and then she stopped it, which she almost never did, and turned her head to look at him. Her eyes were bright and terrible. "My sister went out once. 1911. The winter after the bell came across. She heard our mother's name in it—our mother had been dead two years—and she ran out into the snow to answer, because she was seven and she missed her." Agnes's voice didn't waver. That was the awful part. It was dry as ledger paper. "She came back the next morning. Old. Hollowed. Like your Ivo. And she lived thirty more years like that, sitting in that chair by the east window, and do you know what the village said?"

"No."

"They said the Marshes were blessed. That the bell had taken her up as a maiden sacrifice and given her back sanctified. They brought flowers." Agnes smiled, and it was the coldest thing Edmund had ever seen a human face do. "They brought *flowers*, Mr. Villalobos. For a seven-year-old girl with dust in her mouth. Because the village cannot afford to see what actually happened. If it's a blessing, they owe nothing. If it's a toll, they owe everything."

She reached out and took his wrist. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be.

"Now they'll bring flowers for Ivo too," she said. "You watch. And every person who lays one down will sleep a little sounder, and believe a little harder, and hold the door shut a little tighter against anyone who tries to end it. That's how it feeds, Edmund. Not on blood. On *loyalty*. On people choosing, every day, to stay and serve and call it love." Her fingers tightened. "So I ask you, and I want an honest answer. After last night. After seeing what it does. Are you still planning to dig?"

Edmund thought of Ivo by the stove, wrapped in blankets, asking Marisol to leave the lamp burning because he didn't like how the dark sounded now. Thought of Tomas's shaking hands. Of Ruth's small, desperate shake of the head.

"Yes," he said.

Agnes studied him for a long moment. Then she released his wrist and resumed her counting, soft as breath.

"Nineteen," she said. "Eighteen. Seventeen."

Outside, far off, in the empty tower where no rope hung and no bell existed, something turned over in the dark—patient, unhurried, a creditor checking its calendar—and Edmund could have sworn that in the silence after the last ring, very faintly, very distinctly, it had begun trying out a new name.
