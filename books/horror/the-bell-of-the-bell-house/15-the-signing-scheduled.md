## 15. The Signing Scheduled

Fenwick came back on a Thursday, and he did not come alone.

Edmund was in the kitchen when Marisol looked out the window over the sink and went still, her wooden spoon suspended above a pot of barley soup as though the steam itself had told her to stop. He followed her gaze down the drive and saw them: three cars moving slowly up the gravel, tires crunching with the deliberate unhurried sound of people who knew they were expected. Fenwick's grey Rover first. Then a boxy estate car with two men in it, one of whom Edmund recognized from the village shop—the butcher, perhaps, or the man who sold him paraffin. The third car carried Reverend Hale, who sat in the passenger seat with his hands folded in his lap like a man attending his own funeral and finding the eulogy satisfactory.

"They've never come up the drive before," Marisol said quietly. "Not once. Not for any of them." She meant the deaths, Edmund understood. Forty years of Marshes dying in this house, and the village had always waited at its edge for word, like gulls waiting for a boat to land.

"Ruth," Edmund called up the stairs, and heard her door open before he finished the name.

By the time Fenwick stepped into the hall, shaking rain from his umbrella with the crisp economy of a man who had rehearsed the gesture, Ruth was already on the bottom stair, arms crossed, wearing the expression she reserved for doctors who asked too many questions at handover.

"Mr. Fenwick."

"Miss Okafor." He smiled at her, and Edmund watched the smile fail to find anything in her face to hold onto. "I've brought witnesses. And good news." He said *good news* the way other men said *unavoidable necessity*, with the same brisk cheer. "Mrs. Marsh has been given every courtesy, every consideration. But the trust cannot wait indefinitely, and neither—forgive me—can she. We've set a date. Saturday morning. Ten o'clock. The signing will take place here, in the drawing room, with the whole village in attendance. It will be an occasion. A celebration of continuity. Agnes Marsh giving Bell House back to the people who have loved it for four hundred years."

"The people who melted down its bell, you mean," Tomas said from the doorway of the morning room. He was nineteen and usually careful about being invisible; today his voice cracked across the hall like a whip.

"Tomas." Ruth's voice, low.

But Fenwick only turned to the boy with something almost gentle in his face, which was somehow worse than if he'd been angry. "The bell was a sacrifice, young man. Everything given to that house was given gladly. You'll see on Saturday." He turned back to Ruth. "The vicar will say a few words. There'll be refreshments—my wife is making the seed cake Agnes liked as a girl. We want it to be kind. I want you to understand that. Whatever you may think of me, I want it to be kind."

*Whatever you may think of me.* As if what Edmund thought of him were a settled question, a known quantity, like the weather. Edmund stood by the coat rack and felt the house listening through him—all around him, in the walls, in the damp plaster, in the bones of the staircase—and wondered, not for the first time, whether a house could dread.

"We'll discuss it with Mrs. Marsh," Ruth said, "and let you know."

"There's nothing to discuss, Miss Okafor. That's rather the point of a date." Fenwick checked his watch—a heavy gold thing that caught the grey light—and then, as if remembering himself, softened. "How is she today?"

"Dying," Edmund said.

It came out flatter than he intended, and everyone looked at him. Fenwick studied him for a moment with those pale, patient eyes, the eyes of a man who read documents for a living and found people merely another kind of fine print. "Yes," Fenwick said. "Well. That is why we're here."

They left the way they'd come, slow and ceremonial, and Hale paused at the threshold to look back—not at Ruth, not at the house, but at Edmund specifically, a long level look that contained no threat and no warmth, only assessment, the look of a man confirming a figure in a ledger. Then the cars ground away down the drive and the rain took them, and Bell House exhaled.

Edmund hadn't known a house could exhale until he'd lived in one that breathed.

* * *

Agnes received the news sitting up, propped against pillows that Ruth had plumped with the fierce tenderness of a woman who expressed love exclusively through linen. She was smaller than when Edmund had arrived—there was no honest way around it. Six weeks ago she had been a thin old woman; now she was a collection of angles wrapped in a shawl, her hands like folded paper, her eyes enormous and clear in a face the rest of which seemed to be leaving ahead of schedule.

"A date," she repeated, when Ruth finished. "Saturday. Ten o'clock." Her mouth twitched. "He's kept the seed cake, then. Doris Fenwick's mother used to make it. I haven't tasted seed cake since I was nine years old." She looked out the window at the wet garden, where Ivo's empty wheelbarrow still stood where he'd left it, three weeks ago, before the gate and the ringing and the dawn. Nobody had moved it. Nobody would. "They'll fill the drive, won't they. All of them. Standing in their good coats, waiting for me to sign my name so the luck keeps running."

"You don't have to decide anything tonight," Ruth said.

"I decided forty years ago, Ruthie. I'm just slow about announcing it." Agnes turned her head on the pillow with effort and found Edmund. "Nurse Villalobos. Come here where I can see you properly. My eyes are going faster than the rest of me, which I consider rude."

He came and sat in the chair by the window, the one that had become his without anyone assigning it. Outside, the rain moved through the yew trees in long silver curtains.

"Fenwick likes you," Agnes said. "Did you know that? He told the vicar so, after your walk into town. Said you were a sensible man. Said sensible men don't make trouble for dying women." She smiled, and there was iron in it. "He thinks sense is the same thing as agreement. It's a common mistake among people who've never had to be brave."

"What do you need from me, Mrs. Marsh?"

"Agnes." She said it the way she always did, correcting him with the patience of someone who had corrected many people about many things and expected to go on doing it past the point of death. "What I need is small, and it isn't for Saturday. It's for Friday night." Her hands worked at the edge of the blanket, pleating and unpleating it. "The night before a signing, the house gets loud. It did before my mother signed. It did before Uncle Charles. The bell starts early, and it doesn't count the hours—it counts us. And in the morning, when they bring the papers, a person is tired. Frightened-tired. The kind of tired where your own hand feels like it belongs to somebody else." Her eyes held his, steady and terrible in their clarity. "That's how they get the signature, Edmund. Not with force. With exhaustion. They wear the yes out of you."

"That's coercion," Edmund said. "Legally speaking, capacity requires—"

"Oh, legally." She laughed, a dry rustle. "You sweet man. You think this is a matter for solicitors." She reached out, and he took her hand without deciding to; it weighed nothing, that hand, less than the rosary Ruth kept in her pocket. "Here is what I'm asking. Friday night, you sit with me. All night. You've done vigils before—I can tell, you have the vigil-shaped look about you. Sit with me and talk to me, or sit with me and say nothing, I don't care which. But stay awake, and stay close, and when morning comes and they put the pen in my hand—" her grip tightened, surprising strength surfacing from somewhere deep, like a fish rising through dark water— "*don't let my hand be guided.*"

"I wouldn't allow anyone to guide your hand, Agnes. That's not—"

"Not them." Her voice dropped, and the room seemed to lean in. Rain ticked against the glass. "Me. If the night goes badly, if I get tired enough, some part of me will want to sign. Some part of me has wanted to sign since 1911, since I was a girl standing in this room listening to my grandmother die and hearing the bell give itself up for us. That part of me is old and frightened and it wants the debt paid the easy way, and it lives in my hand." She turned her palm up in his, open, an offering or a surrender. "So you watch my hand, Edmund. You're the only one here who doesn't believe in any of it. That makes you the only one whose hand mine can't lie to."

He wanted to say: *I believe you're frightened, and fear makes stories.* He wanted to say: *the bell is wind in a broken tower, the counting is morphine and memory, the names in the bronze fragment are coincidence and acid etch.* He had a whole professional vocabulary for this, polished over twenty years of hospice wards, and it rose in him now like a tide—and broke, uselessly, against the fact of her hand in his, weightless and certain, and the fact of the wheelbarrow in the garden, and the fact that he had not slept a full night through since he came to Bell House because some part of his mind, the part below belief, stayed up listening.

"I'll be here," he said. "Friday night. All night."

"And my hand."

"And your hand."

She closed her eyes then, satisfied, and within minutes her breathing lengthened into sleep, and Ruth touched Edmund's shoulder on her way past, and said nothing, which from Ruth was a paragraph.

* * *

That evening the staff gathered in the kitchen, because the kitchen was where Bell House kept its heart. Marisol had made soup and too much bread, the way she did whenever the world pressed in; Ivo's chair stood empty at the table and no one remarked on it, which was its own kind of remark. Tomas sat turning his cup in slow circles, and Ruth stood at the counter even though her tea was going cold, because Ruth sat down only when she had decided a situation was survivable.

"Saturday," she said at last. "Ten o'clock. They'll come up the drive in their coats and their cars, and they'll be kind, and they'll be gentle, and every single one of them will be watching her hand." She looked at Edmund. "You know what happens if she signs."

"I know what Fenwick says happens."

"Fenwick says the truth the way a butcher says meat." Ruth set her untouched tea down. "The luck holds. The winters stay mild. The fishing stays good. The shop stays open. And the house—" she glanced upward, at the ceiling, at all the ceilings stacked above them like sediment— "the house keeps what it's owed. One signature, and everything stays comfortable. That's the bargain. Comfort. It's not a bad price, if you're the one who never has to pay it."

"It's a terrible price," Tomas said suddenly. His cup had stopped turning. "Because you pay it forever. You pay it every time the bell rings and you don't go to the window. You pay it every time you hear a name in it and pretend it was wind." He was looking at his hands. "I've heard it since I was six. My mum worked here before me. She told me it was owls. Owls, in a tower with no bell." He laughed, short and joyless. "I believed her because believing her meant I could stay. That's what it costs. Not money. Believing things you know aren't true, on purpose, every day, so you can keep your family."

The kitchen was quiet. Rain filled the silence, patient and endless.

Marisol spoke from the stove without turning around. "My sister in Cádiz writes to me. She says, Marisol, come home, there is work, there is sun, why do you stay in that cold wet place with those strange English people. And I write back: I am needed here." She shrugged, a small motion of her broad shoulders. "It is even true. But it is also true that I tried to leave once, ten years ago, and got as far as the station at Ashcombe, and the bell rang my name the whole hour I waited, and I got on the train anyway, and it rang my name at every stop, and by Taunton I couldn't hear the train for the bell, and I came back." Now she turned, and her face was calm in the way of weathered things. "Ruth will tell you it keeps its bargains. Yes. It does. It kept its bargain with me. I am alive, and I am fed, and I am loved. It simply declined to be refused."

"So we're agreed it's monstrous," Edmund said, "and we're all staying."

"We're agreed it's monstrous," Ruth said, "and we're all staying, and on Saturday morning we will stand behind our Agnes while she tells four hundred years of tradition to go to hell, and we will do it knowing what it may cost, because that is what family means. Family isn't who shelters you. Family is who stands in the weather with you when the shelter burns." She picked up her cold tea and drank it anyway, grimly, like medicine. "Now. Friday night. Who sits with her?"

"I do," Edmund said. "She asked for me."

Ruth studied him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face seemed to settle something. "Then the rest of us will be in the house. Doors open. If you call, we come." She hesitated, and then, in a lower voice, the voice she used perhaps twice a year: "Edmund. She chose you because you don't believe. Understand what that means. It means the bell can't find a handle on you yet. But handles get worn in. Ask Ivo's gate." She didn't explain further. She didn't have to. They all knew about the gate, and the dawn, and the man who had come back aged thirty years with earth under his tongue.

* * *

Friday came gray and low, the sky pressing down on the hills like a lid. The village below was busy in a festive, dreadful way—Edmund walked partway down the lane in the afternoon and saw bunting going up between the shopfronts, saw trestle tables being carried into the church hall, saw children chalking arrows on the road pointing up toward Bell House. *This way to the signing.* Arrows. As if it were a fair.

Hale was directing the tables, sleeves rolled, and he raised a hand to Edmund—not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment of inventory.

Edmund walked back up the hill faster than dignity allowed.

At seven, after supper, after Marisol had fussed over Agnes until the old woman threatened her with a spoon, Edmund took his chair to the bedside and sent the others out. Ruth lit the lamp low and left the door ajar, as promised. The rain had stopped; the silence it left behind was worse, a held breath.

For a while they talked of nothing. Seed cake. The wrongness of bunting in November. Ivo, and whether he'd have approved of Marisol planting his winter kale. Agnes's voice grew thinner as the light died, but her clarity held, that awful post-storm clarity of the nearly dead, as if everything nonessential had been stripped away and only the person remained, distilled.

Around eleven she stopped talking mid-sentence, and Edmund looked up from the book he'd been reading aloud—Tennyson, her request, though she'd heckled it cheerfully—and saw her staring at the ceiling.

"It's early tonight," she whispered.

He listened. Wind in the yews. The settling of old pipes. Nothing else.

"Agnes—"

"Listen properly. Not with your ears." Her hand found his again, weightless, urgent. "It knows tomorrow is the day. It's coming to count us early, so we're tired for the papers. So my hand is soft." Her eyes came to his, and in the lamplight they were the color of the bronze fragment in the crypt, old metal gone green at the edges. "Stay with me, Edmund. However it sounds. However it *says*. Stay with me, and whatever I ask you for in the dark—if I ask you for the pen, if I beg you for the pen—remember that the woman asking is not entirely the woman you're holding."

The clock in the hall struck midnight.

And far off, faint at first, from a tower that had been empty of bronze for a hundred and thirteen years, the bell began to ring.

Edmund's blood went cold and stayed cold. Because it wasn't ringing the way bells ring. It was ringing the way voices speak—deliberate, spaced, attentive—and beneath the bronze tone, unmistakable now, undeniable, there was a counting. Backward. Patient. Getting closer.

And then, threaded through the tolling, soft as a hand smoothing a bedsheet, it began to say her name.

"*Agnes. Agnes Marsh. Agnes.*"

Beside him, the old woman's grip tightened on his fingers, and her lips began to move, counting down along with it, and Edmund—who believed nothing, who had built a whole life on believing nothing—took her hand in both of his, held it fast, and prepared to keep watch until morning.
