## 19. Climax: The Zero Hour

The count had reached nine.

Edmund heard it the way you hear your own pulse in a silent room—inside his skull rather than in the air. *Nine.* Agnes lay against her pillows with her eyes closed and her hands folded on the coverlet like a woman posing for a portrait she would not live to see finished. The candlelight moved on her face. Her lips shaped the number without sound now; she had stopped speaking it aloud an hour ago, when the bell had begun to speak back.

*Eight.*

The bell was not ringing anymore. That was the worst of it. Somewhere around two in the morning it had abandoned its bronze voice altogether and found a better one.

"Agnes." Marisol's voice, cracked down the middle. She stood at the foot of the bed with a plate of food no one had touched, as if feeding a dying woman at the zero hour were a thing that could still be done, a ritual older than medicine. "Agnes, mi vida, you don't have to listen."

*Seven.*

"It isn't listening," Agnes said. Her eyes opened. They were clear—terribly clear, the clarity of someone who has set down every weight she owns. "It's remembering. There's a difference, Marisol. You listen to a stranger. You remember family."

The candle flames all leaned east at once, though there was no draft. Ruth, standing at the window with her rosary wound through her fingers so tightly the beads had gone white, made a small sound—not a word, not quite a prayer, something between them that had been worn smooth by use.

*Six.*

And then the voice came into the room.

It came from everywhere—the walls, the floorboards, the space just behind Edmund's left ear—and it was a young woman's voice, bright and unbroken, the voice of someone who had died before her voice could ever grow old.

"Agnie," it said. "Agnie, it's me."

Edmund watched Agnes's composure fracture. Not dramatically—nothing about Agnes Marsh was ever dramatic—but he saw her hands tighten on each other, saw the tendons rise like cables under skin gone thin as vellum.

"Cordelia," Agnes whispered.

"I've been waiting," the voice said, warm as bathwater, patient as rot. "All this time, Agnie, I've been waiting for you to come home. And you can, you know. You can come home tonight. All you have to do is sign."

"No," Ruth breathed, at the window. "No, no—" She had told Edmund once, in the kitchen, in the gray aftermath of her confession, what the bell did with love. It doesn't lie, she'd said. That's the horror of it. It only tells you the truth you already want.

But this—this was not truth. This was something wearing Cordelia Marsh's voice like a stolen coat.

"She signed for you," the voice went on, gentle, coaxing, coming now from the pillow beside Agnes's head, from inches away. "Your mother. She signed so that you would never be hungry, never be cold, never be turned out into the world with nothing. She gave up her sister for you, Agnie. Don't let her gift die in this room. Don't let it end with you."

Edmund stepped forward. He didn't decide to; his body simply moved, the way it had moved through twenty years of deathbeds, toward the center of the suffering.

"Agnes," he said quietly. "Look at me. Not the voice. Me."

Her eyes found his. In them he saw the thing he had spent three weeks refusing to see: terror. Not of dying. Of being alone while she did it.

*Five.*

"You're counting," he said. "You've been counting since the night I arrived. What happens at zero?"

She almost smiled. "I don't know. No one's ever refused long enough to find out." Her breath caught. "My mother counted too, at the end. She got to four. Then she picked up the pen."

"And your sister?"

The room went very quiet. Even the voice seemed to hold itself still, listening, patient as a fisherman watching a line.

"My sister is why I'm counting," Agnes said. "Cordelia answered the bell instead of Mama. She took the toll in Mama's place, the way you're thinking of doing right now, Edmund Villalobos, because I can see it in your face, and I am telling you—" her grip found his wrist, shockingly strong, "—it does not save anyone. It only changes whose name is written."

*Four.*

"That's not true," Edmund said.

And the moment he said it, he knew it was true—that was the trick of belief, the trap he had walked into without noticing. Three weeks ago he could have said *that's not true* and meant nothing by it, and the words would have been armor. Now they were just words. He believed. God help him, after the crypt and the fragment and Tomas bleeding from the ears, after Ivo hollowed out in the flowerbed with grave-dirt between his teeth, after Ruth reciting her daughter's name like a soldier reciting a password—he believed every word of it, and belief had made him naked.

The voice laughed. It was Cordelia's laugh, bright and startled, the laugh of a girl of nineteen, and it filled the room like water filling a lung.

"There he is," it said fondly. "There's our nurse. Oh, we know you, Edmund. We've known you since the night you walked up the drive and heard us ring. Do you know how long it's been since someone new heard us? Since someone new *counted*?"

"Leave him alone," Ruth said, and her voice broke on it.

"He's offering himself," the voice said, delighted. "Did you hear? He thinks he's offering himself. Sweet thing. But the debt isn't his, is it, Agnie? The debt has a name, and the name is Marsh, and the signature must be Marsh, or the door stays open and we must come collect the old way." A pause, silk over stone. "The old way was 1911. Ask the village what the winters were like before. Ask them what we ate."

Tomas made a sound from the doorway—a small, involuntary noise, the noise of a boy remembering stories he'd been told as a child and forbidden to repeat. He was holding the crypt fragment wrapped in a tea towel against his chest like an infant, and Edmund realized the boy had brought it upstairs deliberately, had carried the anchor of the thing into the heart of the siege, because Ruth had said *we don't leave each other alone in this house*, and Tomas had understood it literally.

*Three.*

"Sign," the voice said, and now it was not Cordelia, not entirely—it was Cordelia stretched over something vast and cold, the way canvas stretches over a well. "Sign, and your strays go free. Every one of them. The cook, the boy, the woman with the beads, even the gardener sleeping downstairs with his empty eyes—we'll give him back. We'll pour him full again. One name, Agnie. One signature. Everyone lives. Isn't that what nurses want? Isn't that what *you* want, Edmund? Everyone lives?"

And that was the knife of it, Edmund thought distantly. Not cruelty. Kindness. It had learned kindness the way water learns the shape of a riverbed—by running through human hearts for three hundred years until it wore their shape. Fenwick offered money framed as mercy. Hale offered obligation framed as grace. And the bell offered salvation framed as love, and every offer was the same offer: *your loyalty, purchased at their expense, or their suffering, purchased at yours.*

Agnes was crying. Silently, the way she did everything, tears running into the pillows, her lips still moving.

*Two.*

"Edmund," she said. "Whatever you're going to do, do it now, because I am not strong enough to refuse twice. My mother wasn't either. It's not weakness. It's just—everyone wants their sister back."

He looked around the room. Marisol with her untouched plate. Tomas with the fragment clutched to his chest, blood dried black under his nose from the night's earlier tolling. Ruth at the window, mouthing *Amara, Amara, Amara* like a heartbeat. Ivo downstairs, hollowed, breathing because his body remembered how even if nothing else did.

His found family. His accidental, impossible, unasked-for family. The people who had fed him and warned him and bled for him and asked nothing except that he stop digging or leave—and whom he had stayed for anyway, digging, because some things you cannot not do.

He had spent his whole life not believing. It had been a professional discipline, then a habit, then a wall, then a home. *Never believe the patients.* Because believing meant feeling, and feeling meant grieving properly, and grieving properly meant being broken open again and again by strangers' deaths until there was nothing left of you but the breaking.

He believed now. Completely. Which meant that what he did next would cost exactly what it appeared to cost, with no anesthesia of disbelief.

"All right," Edmund said.

The room stilled. Even the candles seemed to burn straighter.

"But hear me first," he said, and his voice surprised him—it didn't shake. Twenty years of deathbeds had taught him how to speak in the presence of the enormous. "You want a signature. You've wanted one since 1911, and every Marsh who signed bought another season, and every season you grew fatter and the village grew more afraid, and nobody—not one soul in three hundred years—ever read what they were signing."

"We are generous," the voice said.

"You're a creditor," Edmund said. "And I've sat with enough dying people to know what creditors do at the end. They come with papers and kind voices and they get the signature however they can." He turned to Agnes. "May I?"

She looked at him for a long moment. Understanding moved across her face slowly, like weather.

"You can't," she whispered. "The name has to be Marsh."

"The name has to be *given*," Edmund said. "That's what the petition said. Chapter thirteen of this house's ugly history—I read it, Agnes, in the crypt, while you were sleeping. *A keeper invited across by freely given name.* Freely given. Not coerced, not frightened out of anyone, not traded. Given." He felt Ruth's hand close on his arm and gently removed it. "Fenwick's witnesses aren't here. Hale isn't here. There's no pen, no paper, no trust, no village. Just you and me and the thing you invited. So let's do this honestly, for once. Let's make it the only honest signature Bell House has ever seen."

"Edmund—" Ruth's voice, ragged.

"Ruth." He looked at her. "You told me not to do this alone. I'm not alone. You're all here. That's the whole point." He smiled at her, and it hurt, and he let it hurt. "Stay. That's all. Stay in the room. Nobody leaves, nobody walks out when it calls, and whatever it says in my voice afterward—you don't listen. Promise me."

"I promise," she said, and then, furiously, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand: "I promise, you stubborn, impossible man."

"One name," the voice said, and the warmth was gone from it now, and underneath the borrowed girl-voice Edmund could hear the true instrument at last: bronze, vast, patient, counting. "One name, freely given. Speak it."

"Not yours," Edmund said to Agnes. "Yours dies with you free. That was always the plan. I'm just—" he searched for the word and found it, absurdly, from his first night in this house, from the paperwork chapter of his own life, "—amending the document."

*One.*

The number fell into the room like a stone into a well.

And in the silence after it, Edmund Villalobos—who had spent his entire life invisible, unnoticed, the man in the corridor whom families walked past, the nurse whose name no one remembered because he made sure of it, because names were hooks and grief used them—spoke clearly into the dark:

"My name is Edmund Villalobos. I hear the bell. I answer it. Take the toll from me."

For one heartbeat, nothing.

Then the fragment in Tomas's arms grew hot beneath the tea towel—the boy cried out—and every candle in the room flared white, and the voice that came out of the walls was no longer Cordelia, no longer any dead girl, but the bell itself, the true voice, the melted and murdered bell of 1911 finding its one surviving tongue:

**EDMUND.**

Once. Fully. The way a bell rings when it means it—not the half-heard midnight tolling, not the name hidden inside the note, but the whole of him, given and taken, sounded from crown to lip, every atom of the name struck and sustained until the room itself seemed to be made of it, until Ruth's rosary snapped and the beads scattered like planets, until Marisol dropped the plate and Tomas sank to his knees with the fragment blazing against his chest, until Agnes's count—which had hung at *one* like a held breath—released.

Zero.

Silence rushed in after the sound the way silence rushes in after any great bell: enormous, ringing, absolute. The candles steadied. The cold went out of the room. And somewhere far away—down in the crypt, out in the empty tower, in the roots of the hill the house stood on—something vast settled back onto its anchor, sated or satisfied or simply paid, and did not move again.

Edmund was on the floor. He didn't remember falling. His mouth tasted of bronze and grave-dirt, and his hands—when he raised them into the candlelight—were an old man's hands, spotted and veined, aged decades in a single stroke, Ivo's fate written smaller upon him. But he was not hollowed. Whatever the toll was, it had taken years and not taken the self; perhaps because he had given it freely, perhaps because a freely given thing feeds differently than a frightened one.

"Edmund." Agnes's hand found his—her dry, papery hand closing over his ruined knuckles. She was smiling. "You fool," she said, with immense tenderness. "You beautiful fool. It worked. Do you understand? It *worked*. No Marsh signed. The door is closed."

Outside the window, the sky was turning the color of ash, then the color of pearl. Dawn. Gray and quiet and ordinary, the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.

Ruth was kneeling beside him, gathering his head into her lap, saying his name over and over—not the bell's version, hers, warm and human and slightly furious—and Marisol was crying and laughing and trying to pick up the broken plate at the same time, and Tomas sat against the wall with the fragment unwrapped in his lap, staring at it.

"Mr. Edmund," the boy said. "Come look."

Edmund couldn't stand. Ruth helped him crawl, which undignified them both, and together they leaned over the fragment of the bell of 1911, the scrap of bronze etched with the names of everyone the bell had ever called.

The names were fading. All of them—Marshes and keepers and strangers, generations of the summoned and the surrendered—dissolving even as they watched, sinking into the metal like letters into snow, until the fragment was blank and smooth and warm.

Except one.

Near the bottom edge, cut deep and clean and fresh, one name remained. And even as Edmund watched, the sharp edges of the engraving began to soften, to wear, to round themselves the way stone wears under centuries of touching hands—until it was not a wound in the bronze at all but a scar, smooth and pale and permanent.

*EDMUND VILALOBOS*, it had said.

Now it said only: *the keeper.*

Below the window, far down the drive, lanterns were bobbing through the gray light. Fenwick and Hale, coming early with their papers and their witnesses, coming to collect a signature that no longer existed.

Ruth squeezed his shoulder. "Can you stand?" she asked.

Edmund looked at his old man's hands, and at Agnes—breathing still, easy now, her count finished, her face for the first time entirely unafraid—and at the dawn coming up over the hills where the village slept, prospering a little less from this morning forward, and he found that he was smiling.

"Yes," he said. "Let them knock. Someone has to answer the door."
