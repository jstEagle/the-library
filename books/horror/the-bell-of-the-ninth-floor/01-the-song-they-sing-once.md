## 1. The Song They Sing Once

The bus let me off where the road stopped pretending to go anywhere.

That was the first thing I wrote in my field notebook, sitting on my duffel bag with the engine noise fading behind me and the trees closing overhead like a held breath: *Road ends at Ashenmoor. Bus driver would not look at the town when he said its name.* I'd been collecting folk songs for eleven years — lullabies that only grandmothers remembered, work chants that died with the mills, ballads sung at weddings and funerals and once, memorably, at a divorce — and I had learned to read the small hesitations of people who carried something they didn't want to hand over. The driver's hesitation had been a whole silence, a full minute of him staring at his own hands on the wheel before he said, "This is you," as if he were delivering a body.

I should have taken that more seriously. I want to be honest about that from the start, because everything that came after began with my believing that songs were safe things, that recording them was an act of rescue, that the worst a song could do to you was lodge in your head and make you hum it in the shower for a week.

The grant proposal had called Ashenmoor "a hollow community of approximately four hundred residents in the western highlands, notable for its isolation and its unusually dense concentration of pre-industrial musical traditions." That was the polite version. The version that had actually gotten me funding was buried in a footnote of a 1974 dissertation nobody had ever cited: *the harvest song of Ashenmoor appears in no archive more than once. Every collector who recorded it recorded a different performance, and no performer has ever been documented singing it twice.*

No archive holds it twice. I had read that sentence maybe two hundred times in the months since, and every reading had produced the same pleasant little shiver, the one that pays our salaries, the one that means *here is something old and strange and mine.* A song sung only once. A tradition of singularity. In a discipline drowning in variant texts and conflicting transcriptions, here was a song that refused repetition itself — not because singers forgot it, but because, apparently, they wouldn't.

I hoisted my bag — two microphones, a field recorder, a backup recorder for the backup, cables coiled like sleeping snakes — and walked the last half mile into town.

* * *

Ashenmoor announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and apples rotting sweet in the orchard rows, and under both something mineral, cold, like water running over iron. The houses came up out of the trees gradually, gray-shingled and close-kept, gardens trimmed back from the road with a precision that felt less like pride than like compliance. Chimneys, all of them lit though the afternoon was mild. Smoke standing straight up in windless air, nine or ten columns of it, like the town was sending up signals to something above the clouds.

And above everything, visible from every street because the streets all seemed angled to show it, stood the grange hall.

It was enormous — a stone edifice that belonged in a city, not a farming hollow, five stories of dark windows crowned by a belfry whose bell I could just see, green-black with age, hanging motionless. I stopped in the middle of the road to look at it, and a woman passing with a basket of squash looked at me looking at it, and her face did something complicated — not hostility, exactly. More like the expression of someone watching a child reach toward a stove.

"The inn's down past the church," she said, without being asked. "Marta'll give you supper." And she walked on, fast, not looking back at the hall either. Nobody looked at the hall. I noticed that the way you notice a missing tooth in a smile — only afterward, reconstructing it, realizing how many faces had carefully gone elsewhere.

The inn was called the Hollow Rest, which struck me as either honest or ominous, and possibly those were the same thing here.

* * *

Marta Ellery was sixty-something, broad-shouldered, with forearms that said she'd hauled her own firewood for forty years and eyes that said she'd seen everything worth seeing twice and most things three times. She took my money, gave me a room with a quilt older than the state, and fed me a stew so good I forgot, briefly, that I was working.

Then I made my mistake, which was also my profession. Over the second cup of cider, while two farmhands played checkers by the stove and an old man dozed in the corner chair, I set my recorder on the table between us — a modest black rectangle, red light off, harmless-looking — and said the sentence I'd said in a hundred kitchens:

"Mrs. Ellery, I'm told there's a harvest song in this valley that nobody sings but once."

The room changed.

I want to describe this precisely, because precision is all I have left of that evening. It wasn't dramatic. Nobody gasped; nobody left. What happened was quieter and worse. The checkers game stopped mid-move, one farmhand's hand hovering over a piece he never took. The old man in the corner opened his eyes. Marta herself went still with the cider cloth in her hand, and for perhaps four seconds the only sound in the Hollow Rest was the fire ticking and, faintly, far off, a sound I couldn't place — a low tonal hum, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, coming from everywhere and nowhere. From the direction of the grange hall, I thought later. From above the clouds.

"You're the folklorist," Marta said finally. Not a question. "Wendell Sterling." Also not a question. "Word came up the road about you yesterday."

"Word comes up the road about everyone," I said lightly. "It's how towns work."

"Not about everyone." She set down the cloth. She looked at the recorder the way you look at a snake you've found in your kitchen — assessing distance, assessing options. Then she did something I still don't fully understand. She sat down across from me, folded her hands, and said, "Ask me proper, then. Ask me like it matters."

"It matters," I said. "Mrs. Ellery — Marta — I've come a long way. There are archives in three countries that don't hold this song. Whatever it is, it's dying, or it's being kept, and either way it deserves to be heard by someone outside this valley. That's all I want. To hear it. To carry it out."

Something moved behind her face at the word *carry*, some flinch, quickly mastered. She glanced at the corner, at the old man, who gave a single slow nod, like a judge permitting testimony.

"All right," she said. "Four verses. You'll get four verses, and then you'll turn that thing off, and you won't ask me again. Not tomorrow, not ever. Swear it."

"I swear," I said, meaning it the way you mean things at the beginning of stories.

She didn't stand. She didn't perform. She sang sitting down, looking at a point slightly above my left shoulder, at nothing, at the middle distance where memory keeps its house. Her voice was low and unadorned, a working voice, a voice built for carrying across fields rather than concert halls, and the melody was modal, old, pentatonic with a flattened seventh that snagged the ear like a burr in wool.

I turned on the recorder. The red light glowed between us like a small patient eye.

The first verse was addressed to the land itself — *Count the rows and count the rain, / count the apples down the lane* — a plain invocation, lovely, unremarkable. Any harvest festival in any century could have produced it. I remember thinking, with the first cool drop of professional disappointment, that maybe the mystery was overblown, maybe the 1974 dissertation had inflated a simple local custom into legend.

The second verse named a household.

*"The Alder house has bread to spare, / set a plate and say a prayer, / count them in and count them there—"*

I straightened. Because it wasn't metaphor. It was a name — Alder — and I knew from my preliminary research that the Alders were one of the founding families of the hollow, that their farm had passed through six generations and now lay fallow, that no Alder had lived in Ashenmoor since 1988. A census verse. A song that counted actual households, actual families, naming them into the harvest like entries in a ledger.

The third verse named another family. The fourth named another still — and here Marta's voice did something I've replayed a hundred times since, something I can hear in my sleep: on the last line of the fourth verse, her voice dropped almost to a whisper, and she sang a word I couldn't catch, a word that sounded less like a word than like a door opening somewhere below hearing, and the fire in the grate guttered though no draft moved, and the old man in the corner crossed himself, quick and involuntary, the way you swat at a fly in church.

Then silence. Real silence, the kind with weight, the kind that presses on the eardrums. Even the fire seemed to hold its breath.

Marta reached across the table and turned off my recorder herself. Her hand was steady. Her face was not. All the color had gone out of it, drained downward, leaving her gray as the shingles outside, and her eyes when they met mine were wide and wet and full of something I had never seen in a singer's face before in eleven years of asking people to sing for me.

Fear. Not stage fright. Not shyness. Fear — raw, animal, immediate — as if she had just done something enormously dangerous and was waiting to find out whether she'd gotten away with it.

"That's what you get," she whispered. "Four verses. That's all there is to get from me."

"Marta—"

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip. The checkers players flinched. "Don't play it back. Don't ask me to do it again. Don't ask anyone else to do it again in front of you, because they won't, and some of them will be angry enough at you for asking that you'll wish you'd stayed on the bus." She stood, pressing her palms flat against the table as if steadying herself against weather. "You got your song. Four verses of it. Now hear me, girl, because I'm only saying this once too: there are nine verses."

My mouth had gone dry. "What happens in the other five?"

Her eyes went to the window. To the grange hall, black against the darkening sky, its belfry empty of sound and somehow full of listening.

"The other five aren't sung," she said. "They're *owed*." And then she picked up my bowl and my cup and carried them into the kitchen, and the conversation was over, and the whole room knew it, and the checkers game resumed very quietly, and the old man in the corner closed his eyes again, and I sat alone at my table with a dead recorder and four verses and the growing, crawling certainty that I had not collected a song tonight.

I had witnessed a payment.

* * *

I didn't sleep well, which surprised me, because I'm a professional sleeper in strange beds. But the inn creaked in patterns, regular patterns, and around two in the morning I realized the creaking had a rhythm — a slow nine-beat figure, pause, nine beats again — and that it was coming from above me, from the attic, from the direction of the chimney, and that it matched, exactly, the meter of the first verse Marta had sung.

*Count the rows and count the rain.*

I got up and checked my recorder. The tape — yes, I still use tape for the archival masters, magnetic oxide, physical, tamper-evident — the tape had advanced eleven minutes past where Marta had switched it off. Eleven minutes of blank hiss, except that when I put on the headphones and turned the gain all the way up, under the hiss, at the very floor of audibility, there was a voice.

Not Marta's. Not any voice I knew. It was counting.

I didn't listen long enough to count along. I switched off the machine, wrapped it in a sweater, and put it in the bottom of my bag, and then I sat on the edge of the bed until dawn came gray through the curtains, telling myself the reasonable explanations — print-through, bleed from an adjacent reel, my own tired brain pattern-matching voices into noise. All of which were true. All of which were beside the point.

Because here is what I knew, sitting in that dark room while Ashenmoor woke up around me and lit its ten straight columns of smoke: the dissertation footnote had been wrong, or incomplete, about why no archive held the song twice. It wasn't that performers wouldn't repeat it.

It was that the song was not finished yet. Four verses sung, five owed. And somewhere in this hollow — in that great stone hall with its unreachable bell — something was keeping the books.

In the morning I packed my bag, settled my bill, and asked Marta, as casually as I could manage, whether there happened to be a rooming house at the edge of town. Somewhere quieter. Somewhere near the farms.

"There's the Okafors," she said slowly, studying me with those seen-everything-twice eyes. "Edge of the hollow, past the orchards. Adaeze Okafor takes boarders, sometimes." A pause. "You'll be wanting to photograph the old places, I expect. Folklorists always do."

"If they'll allow it."

"They'll allow it." Something flickered across her face — pity, or warning, or both wearing the same coat. "Whether it allows it is another matter."

I didn't ask what *it* was. I was starting to understand that in Ashenmoor, the questions you didn't ask were load-bearing.

I walked out past the church, past the orchards heavy with fruit that smelled almost too sweet, like sweetness going over, and found the Okafor farmhouse at the end of a lane lined with iron fence posts — iron, I noted, every one of them, when wooden posts would have been cheaper — and a woman in her seventies standing on the porch as if she'd been expecting me since yesterday.

"You're the one who records things," Grandma Adaeze called down, before I'd even knocked.

"I am," I said. "If you'll have me."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then at my bag, where the recorder slept in its sweater, and her expression was impossible to read — grief and amusement and something harder underneath, something that had survived things.

"Come in," she said. "But leave the machine on the porch. In this house, we'll start with what you can carry in your own two hands."

I laughed, thinking it a joke about luggage.

I did not laugh again for a very long time.
