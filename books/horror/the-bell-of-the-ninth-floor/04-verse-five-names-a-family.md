## 4. Verse Five Names a Family

Wendell woke to the smell of scorched paper and something else beneath it — rosemary, maybe, or the memory of rosemary — and lay still in the narrow upstairs room of the Okafor farmhouse, listening to the house decide what kind of morning it would be.

The floorboards spoke first. Then a rooster that crowed out of rhythm, three notes and a pause, like a song learning itself. Then Grandma Adaeze's voice below, low and level, saying words Wendell couldn't catch but whose cadence she recognized: the harvest song. Four verses. The same four the innkeeper had sung into her recorder before his face went the color of tallow.

She dressed quickly and came down with her notebook already open.

Adaeze stood at the stove with her back to the room, a small woman built like a fence post driven deep, silver hair braided flat against her skull. Tobias sat at the table pushing an egg around his plate. His mother, Nkiru, was feeding the younger one, and there were — Wendell counted without meaning to, because counting had become a reflex she couldn't put down — six chairs at the table. Six, for five people. She made herself look away from the sixth.

"Mrs. Okafor," Wendell said. "This morning. You were singing."

"I hum." Adaeze didn't turn around. "Humming is not singing."

"It was four verses. The same four Mr. Ellery sang me at the inn. I'd like to ask you about the fifth."

The spatula stopped moving. The kitchen went quiet in a way that had weight to it, the way a room goes quiet when someone has said a name they shouldn't have. Tobias looked up. Nkiru looked down. And Adaeze turned, finally, and looked at Wendell with eyes that were not unkind and not kind either — eyes that were doing arithmetic.

"You're the folklorist," she said. "The one who records things."

"That's right. I'm here for the harvest song. It's not in any archive twice — I've checked every collection from the Library of Congress to—"

"I know what you checked." Adaeze set the spatula down with a small, deliberate click. "Sit down, girl. Not in that chair." A flick of her chin toward the sixth chair. "The other one. Eat your eggs and listen, because I'm only going to say this once, and once is all anything in this hollow ever gets said."

* * *

The fifth verse names a family.

That was how Adaeze began, standing at the window with her arms folded, looking out at the fields as if the answer might be growing in them.

"Not a place. Not a season. Every other verse in that song is about the work — the cutting, the binding, the carrying in. But the fifth verse has a name in it. One household's name, sung plain, so there can be no mistake about who is meant." Her voice was flat as slate. "My mother-in-law heard it sung when she was a girl. The name was Halloway. There was a Halloway farm where our east field is now. Forty acres, a good well, nine children. On the night of the harvest moon in 1911, the whole hollow sang the song at the grange, and when the fifth verse came, the name was Halloway, and nobody remembers who chose it or who sang it loudest, because by morning nobody remembered the Halloways at all except the way you remember a hole in the ground where a tree used to be."

Wendell's pen had stopped. "Vanished," she said. "All eleven of them?"

"The house too, if you believe the old maps. The county redraws its lines every few years, and after 1911 the lines just... closed over it. Like water." Adaeze turned from the window. "You want me to say the word *supernatural*. I won't. I'll say what happened. They were counted, and then they were gone, and the ones who did the counting called it harvest."

"And the extra chair." Wendell kept her voice steady, though her hand wanted to shake. "At my table. In the photographs."

Adaeze crossed the kitchen and pulled open the drawer under the bread box. From beneath a stack of cloth napkins she took a photograph — one of Wendell's own prints, the one she'd thought lost, the one showing the Okafor dining table with ten chairs arranged around it in perfect, patient order.

"You burned these," Wendell said.

"I burned what you left on the dresser. This one you'd tucked inside your notebook, and I don't go through a guest's notebook, because a person's notes are their own soul written small." Adaeze laid the photograph on the table between them, face up, like evidence at a trial. "Look at it. Count them."

"Ten chairs. There are six in this room."

"There are five people in this house and six chairs, girl, and you know it, because you counted them the first morning and you've counted them every morning since. I watch you do it over your coffee." Something almost like humor moved through her face and was gone. "The chair means you've been noticed. That's all it is at first. A notice, like the kind they pin to a door. Your household has been read into the ledger. And the ledger doesn't close on its own."

Wendell looked at the photograph until her eyes watered. Ten chairs. In the print, the light through the window fell wrong — it fell as if the room were larger than the room, as if the camera had seen a version of the house that existed somewhere behind the house, deeper in.

"When does the ledger close?"

"On the ninth verse." Adaeze said it the way you'd say *on the day of the funeral*. "The song is a census. That's the truth of Ashenmoor that nobody writes down and everybody knows. It was never a celebration. It was an accounting. Every harvest moon, the whole town sings the count, and the count takes what it's owed, and the bell keeps the total from running past what the town can pay." She picked the photograph back up and held it by the corner, delicately, as if it were hot. "My husband's people came here in '62. Bought the Halloway ground because it was cheap. Nobody told them why it was cheap. By '68 the fifth verse had their name in it, and my mother-in-law went to the council on her knees, and do you know what they told her? They told her the song chooses. They told her the song had always chosen, and the Voss family rang the bell, and the bell was mercy, and she should be grateful the debt was only one household per generation instead of all of them."

"What happened to them? Your husband's family."

"They paid." Adaeze set the photograph face-down, gently, the way you'd close a dead man's eyes. "Not all at once. The count is patient when the bell rings regular. My husband lost his father first, then his sister, then — in one night, in '79, the night the ringer was sick and the bell went silent till dawn — he lost his brother's whole family. Five chairs empty by sunrise. He was the last of them, and he married me, an outsider, a woman from a city that had never heard the song, and he swore our children would never be verses." Her jaw tightened. "He spent twenty years trying to make that true. You'll hear about how that ended soon enough, from somebody, because this town tells everything eventually except the parts that matter. But hear this now, from me: I am seventy-one years old, and I have buried more family than I have living, and I will not stand in a museum case while some university puts my grief in a footnote."

The words landed like stones dropped down a well. Wendell realized her mouth was dry.

"Mrs. Okafor — Adaeze. I'm not here to take anything from you. I'm here because songs like this die when no one writes them down. That's all documentation is. It's a way of keeping things alive."

"Is it?" Adaeze leaned across the table, and for the first time Wendell saw how much force lived in that small frame, how many decades of holding-up had gone into those shoulders. "Then tell me, scholar. When you write us down — when you put our song in your archive with its little catalog number — who holds the record? Who can burn it? Who can sing it back to us?" She tapped the table once, hard. "A record is a rope. The question is always who's holding the other end. Your grant office? Your university? Or whatever it is under this hollow that taught a song to teach itself?"

Wendell had no answer. She watched Adaeze straighten, watched the woman become again the composed matriarch stirring porridge, and understood suddenly that she had just been let through a door most outsiders never found — and that the door had been opened not out of trust but out of necessity, because Adaeze had looked at the photograph of ten chairs and decided the folklorist was already inside the story whether anyone liked it or not.

"Why tell me this?" Wendell asked quietly. "If documentation is a rope."

"Because you counted the chairs." Adaeze turned back to the stove. "Outsiders who see nothing are safe. Outsiders who see everything and leave — sometimes safe. But an outsider who sees and stays and counts—" The spatula resumed its slow circle. "You're already in the ledger, child. I'd rather you knew the terms."

* * *

Tobias found her that afternoon in the orchard behind the house, where she'd gone to breathe air that didn't taste of burnt photographs.

He came crunching through the fallen apples with his hands in his pockets and the particular walk of a boy who has decided to tell you something and is pretending otherwise. He was twelve, all elbows and questions, and he had been watching her since she arrived with the frank, assessing stare of a child taking inventory of a new animal.

"You asked Gran about the fifth verse," he said.

"I did."

"She never talks about it. Mom says talking about it waters it." He picked up an apple, considered it, put it back. "But I heard her last night. Through the floor. She was telling you about the Halloways."

"Yes."

"My grandpa's family came after them. Same ground." He kicked at the grass. "Gran thinks I don't know the song past verse four. But I know verse five."

Wendell went very still. "Tobias—"

"I'm not going to sing it." He rolled his eyes with the magnificent contempt of the young. "God. I'm not stupid. I just know it. There's a difference." He glanced toward the house, then closer to her, dropping his voice. "There's a difference, right? Knowing isn't doing?"

The question hung there, and Wendell — who had spent her career insisting that knowledge was neutral, that recording was innocent, that the archive harmed no one — found she could not make her mouth form the easy yes.

"Tobias," she said carefully, "where did you hear it?"

"Everywhere." He shrugged. "That's the thing nobody gets. Everybody acts like the song is this secret, but it's in the walls here. It's in how Gran hums. It's in the way the church bell swings even when there's no wind. I think the song isn't something people know." He looked up at her, and for a moment his face was older than hers, older than the orchard, older than the county lines that closed over the Halloway farm. "I think we're something the song knows."

He ran off toward the barn before she could answer, and Wendell stood alone among the apple trees with her notebook in her hand and did not write a single word, because for the first time in her professional life she was afraid of what her own handwriting might count as.

* * *

That evening she developed the rest of the film in the bathroom with the door locked and the towel stuffed under the gap, an old habit from fieldwork in places where images were contraband. The prints came up in the red safelight one by one: the barn, the lane, the east field where the Halloway ground lay under forty years of Okafor corn.

In the last frame — the east field at dusk, shot through the orchard — there was a figure seated in the middle distance on something that might have been a stump and might have been a chair, facing the house, patient as furniture.

It had not been there when she pressed the shutter.

Wendell held the print at arm's length, then close, then at arm's length again, and the figure did not resolve and did not vanish, and downstairs she heard Adaeze begin, very softly, to hum — four verses, stopping precisely where the fifth would begin, the silence after it louder than any note.

The count had noticed her noticing. And tomorrow, she decided, she was going to find out who was supposed to be keeping it quiet — starting with the man whose family had rung the bell for six generations.

She just didn't know yet that Councilman Harrow Voss was already expecting her.
