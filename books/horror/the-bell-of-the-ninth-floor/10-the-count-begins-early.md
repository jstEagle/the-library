## 10. The Count Begins Early

The first chair appeared on a Tuesday, nine days before the moon.

Wendell learned of it the way bad news travels in small places — not by announcement but by absence. She came down to the Okafors' kitchen at half past seven and found Adaeze standing at the window with her arms folded, watching the road, where a pickup truck sat parked crooked in front of the Pell house with its door still hanging open.

"Whose truck?" Wendell asked.

"Pells'," Adaeze said. "Ruth, Dale, and the girl. Nobody's seen them since Sunday." She didn't turn around. "And last night Ruth's sister drove past their windows and saw a light on in the kitchen. And a chair."

"A chair."

"At the table. Facing the door." Adaeze finally looked at her, and Wendell saw that the old woman had slept badly — or not at all. "The ringer's been dead three weeks, Wendell. The bell hasn't rung since. The count isn't waiting for the moon anymore. It's coming early, and it's coming hungry."

* * *

By noon the hollow had gathered itself into the grange hall's ground floor, and Wendell stood at the back of the crowd with her recorder off and her hands cold, watching a town come apart in slow motion.

There were perhaps two hundred people in Ashenmoor, and it seemed most of them were here. Farmers in their good coats. Women holding children who didn't understand why everyone was whispering. Old men who remembered other years like this one and wore the memory like a scar they kept touching.

Councilman Harrow Voss stood on the low stage beneath the banner of the Ashenmoor Agricultural Society, and he was very good at looking calm.

"We have lost three households," he said. "The Pells. The Merricks out past the quarry road. And old Sam Ives, who lived alone above his feed store." He let the names sit in the air. Each one landed on the crowd like a stone dropped into still water. "I won't lie to you. This is early. This has never happened early. In two hundred years, the bell has held the count to harvest night, and on harvest night the ringer has always—"

He stopped. Everyone knew how that sentence ended. The last ringer, Elias Crane, had died three weeks ago in the room on the eighth floor, seated upright in a chair by the window, and the coroner from the county had written *cardiac event* in a file nobody in this hall believed.

"The council is taking measures," Voss continued. "Salt distribution begins tonight. Iron pokers for every threshold. Roads will be restricted after dusk — I'm asking all of you to stay home, keep your families inside, and—" His eyes found Wendell at the back of the room, and something in his face changed. It was subtle. A tightening around the mouth. "And we need to speak about how this began."

The crowd turned. Not all at once — in pieces, the way a field turns when wind moves through it — until Wendell stood at the center of two hundred pairs of eyes.

"Ms. Sterling came to us in September," Voss said, and his voice was warm, reasonable, almost gentle. "A folklorist. A recorder of songs. She asked us — politely, kindly — to sing for her. And some of us did." He paused. "Mrs. Tull sang four verses in the inn. Mr. Okafor's boy has been heard singing on the school grounds. Sound carries in a hollow, friends. You know that better than anyone. Sound carries, and it doesn't stop at the town line, and it doesn't stop at the edge of a field. Some things listen."

"That's not—" Wendell started, and her voice cracked in the silence, too loud, too alone.

"I'm not blaming anyone," Voss said, spreading his hands, the picture of fairness. "I'm saying what every farmer here already knows: you don't sing to the fields before the season turns. You don't call anything before its time. The song was sung out of turn, and out of tune, and in fragments, and now the count has woken early and confused, and it's taking households it wasn't scheduled to take." He shook his head sadly. "I offered Ms. Sterling funding to leave weeks ago. I offered her access, cooperation, anything she wanted — if she'd simply be gone before the moon. She chose to stay. She chose to keep recording."

*Recording.* The word went through the crowd like a current. Wendell saw it move — faces hardening, mothers pulling children closer, an old man spitting deliberately onto the floorboards.

"My equipment is in my room," she said. "Tapes. Nothing has left this hollow. No one outside even knows—"

"No one outside ever knows," said a voice near the front. Mrs. Tull, the innkeeper, her face gray. "That's the whole design of it. That's why we sing once. Because nothing leaves." She wouldn't look at Wendell. "I sang for her. Four verses. I felt it go out of me like blood."

The murmur rose. Wendell stood in it and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the mechanism in front of her was older than the song itself. A town under terror does not look for solutions. It looks for a shape to put the terror in — something with a face, something it can point at, something smaller than the dark. She was smaller than the dark. She had arrived from outside, with machines that caught voices, and she had asked people to sing.

Voss met her eyes across the hall, and for half a second — no longer — the mask slipped, and what was underneath wasn't hatred. It was relief. Almost gratitude. *Thank you*, that look said. *Thank you for being here to carry this.*

Then the mask returned, and he raised his hands for quiet, and gave them orders about salt.

* * *

Adaeze did not go to the meeting. She stayed home and did what she had done for forty years, which was fight the house.

She walked the rooms with the iron poker from the hearth, checking thresholds, checking windows, checking the table in the kitchen where eleven chairs had once stood in a single terrible night while her grandson slept upstairs. Eight chairs now. She counted them twice. She counted them a third time because counting was the only prayer she trusted.

Emmanuel had gone to the meeting. Tobias she had locked — locked, with the key in her apron pocket — in his room with instructions to touch nothing, sing nothing, hum nothing, not even think in melody. She could hear him up there now, pacing, the floorboards talking under his restless feet, and she let him pace. Pacing was safe. Pacing had no words.

She stopped in the parlor, where the photographs used to hang. Bare hooks now, forty years of family pictures burned in the woodstove back in September, the morning after Wendell's prints showed the tenth chair. The wall looked stripped, flayed. But Adaeze had learned something in the last week that she hadn't told Wendell, hadn't told anyone: burning the photographs hadn't helped. Because Ruth Pell had burned hers too, years ago, after her mother's death — and Ruth Pell's household had been counted anyway.

The chairs didn't need the pictures. The pictures had only ever been witnesses.

Adaeze lowered herself into her own chair by the cold fireplace and held the poker across her knees like a rifle. Forty years ago she had watched her husband walk out this door with a folder full of documents — deeds, ledgers, the Voss family's accounts going back generations, proof that the prosperity of this hollow was built on a tithe of its own people. He'd been so certain. *The law is the law everywhere, Ada. Even here.* The sheriff had received him kindly, taken copies of everything, promised to drive up himself within the week.

By morning the sheriff couldn't remember him. By evening the folder was blank paper. By the third day, Emeka was sitting in the extra chair at their table, facing the door, and he was smiling, and he would not answer her, and when she touched his shoulder he was cold as well water, and then he wasn't there at all.

*The law cannot hear this song,* she had told Wendell. She wondered if the girl had understood. She wondered if anyone understood until it was too late to matter.

Upstairs, Tobias stopped pacing. The silence was sudden and total, and Adaeze was on her feet before she knew she'd moved, poker up, listening.

Her grandson's voice came through the floor, low and careful, testing each word like ice on a pond. Not the sixth verse. Something else. Something slower, older, in a cadence she almost recognized — a fragment, overheard, half-remembered, sung wrong.

"*...and the ninth verse names the name...*"

"Tobias!" She was up the stairs two at a time. She threw open his door and he sat on the bed, wide-eyed, hands frozen mid-gesture, and the room was just a room, and the boy was just a boy, frightened and sorry.

"I heard it," he whispered. "In the tapes. Aunt Wendell's tapes. There's a voice on them that isn't hers, Grandma. It sings the parts nobody knows. I only sang one line. I only wanted to know—"

Adaeze crossed the room and took his face in both her hands. His skin was warm. His pulse jumped under her fingers. Alive, alive, alive.

"You never sing again," she said. "Not a line. Not a note. Not a *hum*. Do you hear me? Whatever you heard on those tapes, you forget it. Some knowledge is a door, baby, and once it's open—"

Downstairs, the kitchen clock struck ten.

And below it, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the grange hall, a bell rang once — though there was no hand to ring it, and no stair that reached it, and every soul in the hollow heard it and knew what it meant.

The count had begun keeping its own time.

* * *

Wendell heard the bell from the steps of the grange hall, where the dispersing crowd had left her standing alone in the cold like a mark on a map.

Around her, the town made sounds she would never get out of her head: doors slamming, engines turning over and failing, a woman calling a child's name with a crack straight down the middle. Somewhere a dog began to howl and was quickly silenced. Salt hit porches in handfuls. The whole hollow contracting like a body around a wound.

Voss descended the stage steps and stopped beside her. For a moment neither spoke. The hall behind them was emptying, the last stragglers hurrying into the dark with their bundles of salt.

"You know I don't believe what I said in there," he said quietly. "About your recordings."

"But you said it anyway."

"They needed somewhere to put it." He rubbed his eyes. He looked, for the first time since she'd known him, genuinely exhausted — a man carrying six generations on a spine built for one. "Fear without a shape kills faster than fear with one. I gave them your face instead of the dark. That's not cruelty, Ms. Sterling. That's triage."

"Three households, Voss." Her voice shook. "Ruth Pell taught me a lullaby verse in her kitchen. She laughed doing it. Now she's a *name in your speech*, a statistic, and I'm the reason they'll sleep easier blaming me?"

"You're not the reason." He turned to face her fully, and lowered his voice further, until it was barely breath. "You want to know the reason? The reason is a ledger in my family's keeping that says this town eats itself every generation unless someone feeds the bell. The reason is my great-grandfather deciding whose names went in which verses. The reason is two hundred years of arithmetic, and the arithmetic just lost its ringer." He glanced up at the dark windows of the grange, floors rising into blackness above them. "But none of that fits in a speech. Your face does."

"And what happens to my face, Councilman? When the salt doesn't work? When the next household goes empty and they've already decided whose fault it is?"

He was silent a long moment. When he answered, he didn't answer her.

"Elias Crane left papers," he said. "In the Ringer's Room. Ledgers, mostly. But in the margins — Elias wrote in the margins, always, little notes to himself, things he'd never say aloud." Voss's jaw worked. "I haven't been able to make myself read them. Three weeks, and I can't climb those stairs. Maybe you're braver than a man who was born for it." He pressed a heavy iron key into her palm and closed her fingers over it. "Eighth floor. Don't go past the landing. And Ms. Sterling — whatever you find in those margins —"

Above them, in the tower that no staircase reached, the bell rang a second time.

Both of them flinched. Down the street, a porch light died. Then another. Then another, moving away through the hollow in a line, like footsteps walking the town from end to end, counting the houses, deciding.

"—read fast," Voss finished.

Wendell stood in the dark with the key biting into her palm, and thought of Ruth Pell's kitchen, and the lullaby verse, and the smile Ruth had worn singing it — and understood that whatever Elias Crane had written in his margins, it was the only thing in Ashenmoor that might be more dangerous than the song itself.

She turned toward the grange hall, and the ninth floor waited above her like a held breath.
