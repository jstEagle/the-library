## 11. The Verse Nobody Wrote Down

The Ringer's Room smelled of dust and lamp oil and something older underneath — a sweetness like fruit going bad in a closed drawer. Wendell had been sitting in it for three hours when she finally understood that the ledger was not the book she should be reading.

It was the margins.

Ezra Voss — the last ringer, dead three weeks now, dead the way the town refused to discuss — had kept his official ledger with the neatness of a man raised to believe his handwriting was a form of prayer. Dates of ringing. Hours of the moon. Weather notations. But in the margins, in pencil, in a hand that grew smaller and more cramped as the pages turned, he had written other things. Things that were never meant to be read by anyone who wasn't already a ringer.

*Count came early to the Pell house, 1974. Sang verses one through four at the door. It listened.*

*The bell does not hold the count back. The bell reminds it what it is owed. There is a difference and I wish my father had told me before he taught me the rope.*

*I asked Grandfather why we never sing past eight. He struck me. Then he answered.*

Wendell sat very still on the dead man's bed, the ledger open across her knees, her recorder — her treasured, grant-funded, peer-reviewed recorder — switched off and pushed to the far end of the mattress like something that had betrayed her.

*I asked him why we never sing past eight,* she read again. *He said: because the ninth verse is not for holding. The ninth verse is for letting go. And a thing you let go is a thing you must first name.*

She looked up at the window. The grange's eighth floor looked out over the whole hollow — the crooked streets, the smoke from the Okafor chimney at the edge of town, the stubbled fields gone gold-brown under a sky the color of old pewter. The harvest moon was five days out. A waxing gibbous hung above the ridge like a coin someone had bitten and found wanting.

Five days. Three households already emptied. Chairs appearing in photographs and then in rooms, patient as furniture, waiting to be sat in.

And somewhere in this room, in this dead man's marginalia, was the answer.

* * *

She worked through the afternoon the way she'd once worked through archives at the university — systematically, hungrily, photographing every page with her phone because her camera no longer felt trustworthy, because nothing that produced an image felt trustworthy anymore. The margins thickened toward the back of the ledger. Ezra Voss had grown more desperate as he'd grown older; she could track it in the pressure of the pencil, in the places where the graphite had torn the paper.

*There are nine verses. The song they sing at harvest is eight. The eighth ends where the count begins. The ninth was sung once, at the founding, by the woman who built this place — and what she named in it, she bound into the bell. That is why the bell rings. Not to keep the count out. To keep the NAMED thing in.*

Wendell's mouth went dry.

The founding families. The ledgers. The prosperity built on the ritual — Adaeze had said it with such flat contempt, forty years of grief compressed into a single sentence. Wendell had assumed it meant the founding families had sacrificed others. Outsiders. Newcomers. People whose names would not be missed.

But if the ninth verse had been sung at the founding —

Then the thing in the chair had a name. A specific name. A name given to it deliberately, two hundred years ago, by people who wanted something counted so that everything else could go uncounted.

*Who did they name?* she wrote in her own notebook, and immediately crossed it out, because writing the question felt like knocking on a door.

*What did they name?*

That felt safer. She didn't know why. She wrote it anyway.

* * *

The marginalia turned inward as the years turned. Ezra Voss had been a ringer for fifty-one years, and somewhere around year thirty he had stopped writing about the count and started writing about himself.

*I dreamed of the ninth floor again. In the dream there is a table up there and ten chairs and I am in the tenth chair and everyone at the table has my mother's face.*

*My father rang for forty years and was taken in his sixty-third. His father likewise. We are not a family. We are a schedule.*

*Tonight I sang the first eight verses alone in the stairwell, just to feel them in my chest. On the eighth verse I heard something on the floor above shift its weight. Like a listener leaning forward. I have decided never to do that again.*

*I have decided never to do that again.*

*I have decided—*

The repetition stopped there. The next entry was dated eleven years later, and the handwriting had changed — slower, heavier, the pencil pressed hard enough to emboss the page.

*I found it. God forgive me, I found the ninth verse. It was in the founding ledger all along, written backward, in the binding — the way you hide a thing from a house that reads over your shoulder. I copied it out and then I burned my copy and then I remembered it anyway, which is the problem with knowing anything. You cannot unknow it into the fire.*

*Here is what I learned and here is why no ringer ever wrote it down: the ninth verse unbinds a household from the count. Any household. Say it over a family and their chairs stay empty and their names fall out of the song. But the verse works by naming. You cannot release the count without naming what sits in the chair. You must look at it — truly look, the way the founding woman looked, the way no one has looked since — and call it by its true name, aloud, in the cadence of the song.*

*And the true name of the thing in the chair is not a word. It is a truth. That is what the founding woman understood. The verse does not ask for a label. It asks for an admission.*

*I know what the admission is. I have known for six years. I will die before I say it, and I will die having rung the bell every night regardless, because a coward with a rope is still better than no rope at all.*

Wendell set the ledger down on the dead man's quilt and walked to the window because her legs needed to do something or she was going to be sick.

An admission. Not a name — an admission. Sung aloud, in the cadence of the song, while looking at the thing in the chair.

She thought of the photographs. The tenth chair at the Okafors' table that appeared in every print. The chair in the century-old archive images, always in homes that later lost someone. The presence she had not yet seen but which had, she was certain, already seen her — the sensation that had followed her since the archive room, of being a page someone else was reading.

*You cannot release the count without naming what sits in the chair.*

And what sat in the chair took one of the family before dawn. Every time. For two hundred years.

Which meant the admission — the true name, the unbearable truth — was about the taking itself. About what the taking actually was. About what the founding families had done, or allowed, or arranged, two centuries ago, and had spent six generations ringing a bell rather than saying out loud.

Ezra Voss had known for six years. He had chosen the rope.

Wendell pressed her forehead against the cold glass and watched the light fail over the hollow, and understood, with a clarity that felt less like discovery than like sentence, that she was going to have to find out what he'd known. And that finding out would change what she was. There were things a folklorist could document and remain a folklorist. This would not be one of them.

* * *

She searched the room until dark.

The Ringer's quarters were preserved like a shrine, but shrines are curated, and Wendell had spent fifteen years learning to read what curators leave out. The bed was made. The washstand was clean. The books on the shelf — hymnals, an almanac, a water-damaged Bible — were arranged spine-out with museum neatness.

But the Bible was the wrong size for its shelf. There was a gap behind it, an inch of shadow too deep.

Inside the gap: a slim notebook, cheap paper, wrapped in a page torn from the founding ledger itself — the same backward script Ezra had described, though whether it was a genuine fragment or his own transcription Wendell couldn't tell and didn't touch longer than she had to. The notebook inside was Ezra's. His cramped pencil hand filled it front to back.

The first page read:

*If you are reading this, you have already decided. No one finds this book by accident. The room does not let it be found. I don't know how else to say that. The room decides.*

Wendell's hands were steady. She noted that they were steady, the way she noted everything, because noting was the only discipline she had left that still believed in her.

*I will tell you what I know and then I will tell you why I could not use it.*

*The ninth verse exists. I reconstructed it from the backward text and from the shape of the silence in the harvest song — the song is built around a missing stanza the way a mouth is built around a missing tooth. Eight verses sung, and the melody leans where the ninth should be. Everyone in this hollow leans with it. They have leaned for two hundred years.*

*The verse unbinds. One household per singing. Name the thing in the chair, truly name it, and that household falls out of the count forever.*

*But hear me: the verse does not free the THING. It frees the HOUSEHOLD. The count remains. The debt remains. You can pull your own family off the altar, and the altar does not go away — it simply waits for the next name in the song. That is why no ringer ever sang it. Not because it cannot be done. Because doing it once teaches you what the count IS, and after that you cannot ring the bell honestly anymore, and an honest bell is the only thing standing between this hollow and the full count, all nine verses, every household, in a single night.*

*A partial mercy buys time. The whole truth spends it.*

*I could have saved the Pell family in '74. I worked out the verse in '71. I sat outside their house with the words in my mouth and I could not say them, because I knew what I would see in the chair, and I knew that once I saw it I would never be able to unsee it, and a ringer who has seen it rings false. Three of them died. I rang the bell that night and my hands shook and the bell rang true anyway. The bell is more honest than I am.*

*So: the answer exists. It has always existed. It is written backward in the binding of the founding ledger and forward in this notebook and it is unbearable, and I want you to understand that "unbearable" is not a figure of speech. It is a load rating. Some truths will break the person carrying them, and the founding families built this entire town — the bell, the office, the generations of us — specifically so that no one would ever have to carry it.*

*They built a machine so they would never have to say it out loud.*

*If you are reading this and you are not a Voss, then you came here from outside, and you should know that is the only reason you might survive what you're about to learn. The count takes households the song names. It has never taken a stranger who refused to be named. Remember that. Whatever you decide, remember that the door out is real, even if the road isn't.*

*The verse follows. I wrote it out once and burned it twice and it keeps coming back to my hand, so here it is, third and final, in my own blood if the ink runs out. Sing it looking at the chair. Sing it in the cadence. And may whatever made this hollow forgive us, because we will not forgive ourselves, and forgiveness is apparently not the currency here. Truth is. Truth has always been the currency. That is the whole horror of it. Everything else was bookkeeping.*

Below that, in a hand gone suddenly careful, as if the words were being carried across ice:

*The ninth verse.*

And then the transcription — eleven lines, each one shorter than the last, the language archaic and plain at once, a counting rhyme inverted into a confession. Wendell read it once. Her eyes slid off the middle lines the way eyes slide off a bright road at night. She read it again and forced herself to hold each line, and by the sixth line she understood why Ezra had burned it twice.

Because the verse was not addressed to the thing in the chair.

It was addressed *as* the thing in the chair. First person. *I*. Eleven lines in which the singer confessed, in the voice of the seated one, exactly what it was and why it sat there and who had put it there and what it had been before the founding woman gave it a chair instead of a grave.

It was not a monster's name. It was a eulogy. A confession sung by proxy, giving the thing in the chair the only thing it had never been given:

Acknowledgment. A true account of its own death.

Wendell closed the notebook and sat in the dark of the Ringer's Room for a long time, listening to the building settle around her, listening to the silence above the eighth-floor landing where a stairwell refused to exist.

The answer existed. It was in her hands. Eleven lines, and any household in the hollow could be pulled out of the count — the Okafors, Adaeze and Tobias and Tobias's mother, the family that had fed her and burned her photographs to protect her and refused, with magnificent stubbornness, to become folklore.

One household per singing.

And the count remained. And the debt remained. And somewhere below her, in a warm house at the edge of town, Councilman Harrow Voss was sleeping the sleep of a man whose family had built the machine, and three households were already empty, and the moon was five days from full.

Ezra had chosen the rope. Six years he'd known, and he'd chosen the rope, and he'd died anyway — taken by the bell, the ledger said, the way every ringer was taken, one per generation, the machine consuming its own maintenance.

Wendell stood. She put the founding-ledger wrapping back around the notebook, and then she didn't put it back in the gap behind the Bible, because the room had let her find it, and Ezra's first page had said the room decides, and she chose to believe the room had decided for a reason.

She tucked it inside her coat, against her ribs, where her heartbeat could reach it.

At the door she paused. The stairwell down was black. Somewhere above her — above the ceiling she could touch, above the landing that ended at eight — something shifted its weight, the way a listener leans forward.

"Five days," Wendell said to the dark, to the building, to whatever kept the books. "I'm coming up."

The bell, high in its impossible floor, did not ring.

But she felt it, faint as a held breath, waiting to see what she would carry.
