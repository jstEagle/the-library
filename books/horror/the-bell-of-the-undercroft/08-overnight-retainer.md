## 8. Overnight Retainer

The contract ran to eleven pages, and Gideon read every one of them twice, because Miss Vane's solicitor had the kind of face that suggested he enjoyed finding loopholes on behalf of dead houses.

Clause nine was the one that mattered. *The contractor shall remain upon the premises from dusk until dawn for a period of no fewer than three consecutive nights, said nights to be designated by the client.* It was, Gideon reflected, an unusual term for pest control. Rats didn't keep office hours. Moths didn't check her out at the gate. In thirty years of clearing vermin from the bones of old buildings — grain silos in Norfolk, a decommissioned chapel in Whitby whose bats she still thought of fondly — no client had ever required her to sleep on site.

But the fee was four times her standard rate, and the retainer alone would cover the van's new axle and six months of the storage unit where she kept her mother's things, so she signed. She signed with the same pen she used for everything, the cheap biro with the chewed cap, and she noticed — because she noticed everything, it was the whole of her profession — that Vane watched the signature the way other people watched a surgeon close.

"Three nights," Vane said. "You'll want to be inside the house for at least part of each one."

"I'll be where the work is."

"The work is everywhere." Vane smiled, and it was not an unkind smile, but it had the patience of something that had been waiting a long time and expected to wait longer. "That's rather the point of Harrowmere."

Gideon drove home through rain that came down in grey ropes, and told herself the tightness under her sternum was the coffee.

* * *

She rigged the house the way she'd rig any job: methodically, without romance.

Motion sensors in the undercroft stairwell, one at the top landing, one at the turn where the steps narrowed. Contact strips on the bricked-up door, though nothing had disturbed the grease-prints since she'd photographed them; she'd checked the images against her own hand that evening, holding her palm to the screen, and found the prints were smaller than hers but broader across the knuckles, as if the fingers had been pressed flat by weight or by years of use. Or as if they simply had more of themselves than fingers should.

Vibration plates under the floorboards of the long gallery, where the boards sang a low note when she walked them, a note she could feel in her back teeth. A pressure mat at the threshold of the room Vane called the bell chamber, though there was no bell in it that Gideon had seen — only the rope, descending through a ceiling hatch into darkness, frayed at eye level as if something below had chewed it.

And in the center of it all, her recorder. A Zoom H5, professional grade, three hundred quid and worth every penny. She set it on a folding table in the undercroft itself, because if the house wanted to talk, she intended to have it talking into a decent microphone. Two condenser mics, X-Y configuration, 48-kilohertz sampling. She'd recorded bats with this machine. She'd recorded the sub-audible groan of a subsiding mill at three in the morning and traced the crack it predicted before the surveyors found it.

Machines didn't lie. That was the whole religion of her life, compressed into a sentence. Machines didn't lie, machines didn't flatter, machines didn't believe in anything, and neither did she.

She tested the array at dusk, walking the circuit while her laptop in the van chirped confirmation. Stairwell: trip. Gallery: trip. Threshold: trip. The contact strip on the bricked door stayed silent, and she stood in front of it a moment longer than professionalism strictly required, looking at the small greasy hands pressed flat against the inside of the brickwork, dozens of them, overlapping like leaves drifted against a fence.

"Very funny," she told them.

They didn't answer. They never answered. That was what she liked about evidence.

* * *

She slept in the van, in the layby on the far side of the ha-ha, with the doors locked and the thermal curtains drawn and a flask of coffee within reach of her sleeping bag. The house sat up the slope behind her, black against a sky the color of wet slate, its few unboarded windows catching the last light like eyes that hadn't decided whether to open.

She was asleep by half ten. She woke at five past two with no reason she could name, heart going hard, hand already on the torch.

Nothing. Rain on the roof. The laptop's screen dimmed to its idle glow. The sensor log showed nothing since her own test circuit — a clean column of timestamps, all quiet, all quiet, all quiet.

She made herself lie back down. She made herself do the breathing thing, four counts in, seven counts hold, eight counts out, the trick a therapist had taught her after the job at the children's hospital that she never talked about. All quiet. Houses settled. Old houses settled loudly, and Harrowmere had two centuries of settling to get through, and none of it was her problem beyond the terms of clause nine.

At 2:47 she woke again, and this time she heard it through the curtain glass: a sound from the direction of the house, thin and carrying, that she first took for a hinge and then for a child crying and then, when she sat up and listened properly, for nothing at all — because it stopped the instant she gave it her attention, the way sounds do when they are shy, or when they are bait.

The sensor log, when she checked it, showed a single line:

**02:44:13 — STAIRWELL (LOWER) — MOTION**

One trip. The bottom of the undercroft stairs. Where the recorder was.

She looked at the house. Every window black. The layby silent except the rain, which had thinned to a fine hissing mist that turned her headlights into two pale cones full of drifting motes.

"Right," she said aloud, to the van, to herself, to the profession of pest control generally. "Right."

She did not go in. She wants credit for that later, though there is no one to give it to her. She was contractually bound to remain on the premises, and the premises included the layby, and whatever was at the bottom of those stairs could spend the night talking to a microphone, because microphones didn't lie and neither did she, and if something down there wanted her, it could damn well make an appointment.

She left the sensors armed. She left the recorder running. She drank half the flask and watched the house until dawn came up the color of dishwater, and at 6:02 the log printed its morning summary, and every line of it was tripped.

Every line. Simultaneously. Not in sequence, not cascading up the stairs the way a body would trip them, climbing. All at once, at 3:00:00 exactly, to the second: stairwell lower, stairwell upper, gallery east, gallery west, threshold, contact strip. The contact strip on the bricked door, which meant the door itself had moved, which meant the brickwork had moved, and when she walked the house in the grey morning with her torch and her clipboard she found the bricks exactly where they'd always been, the mortar unbroken, the grease-prints undisturbed, smiling their small flat smiles from the inside of the wall.

Six sensors. One instant. As if something had passed through all of them at once, or as if something had simply reached out and touched the whole nervous system of the house the way you'd run your hand along a piano's strings.

She wrote it up in her log in her flat professional hand: *Simultaneous trigger event, 0300, cause undetermined. Possible power fluctuation / RF interference.* She wrote *possible* twice. She was aware, distantly, that she was building herself an alibi out of adverbs.

Then she collected the recorder, because that was next on the list, and the recorder was the part she wasn't afraid of. The recorder was hers.

* * *

She played it back in the van with the engine off and the rain ticking on the roof, headphones on, volume moderate, the way she'd reviewed a thousand nights of a thousand buildings. Ten hours of file. She scrubbed through it at speed first — wind, wind, rain, wind, the house's timbers cracking in the cold like slow knuckles — and then, near the bottom of the waveform, at 2:44, a shape.

A voice.

She slowed it. Real time. The H5's condensers had caught it clean, better than clean, better than her ears had caught it standing twenty feet away in the dark: Miss Vane's voice, coming from somewhere above, from the direction of the bell chamber, faint but perfectly intelligible, murmuring the way people murmur in sleep.

*"How many?"*

Silence. Then a second voice answered.

It was wet. That was the first thing, the thing her hindbrain flagged before her forebrain could organize anything: the voice sounded wet, sounded like speech produced through liquid, patient and unhurried, each word arriving as if it had been carried a long way and set down carefully.

*"More than you have left."*

Gideon's thumb stopped on the wheel.

She scrubbed back. Played it again. *"How many?"* — pause — *"More than you have left."* The words were real. They were on the recording. They had amplitude and frequency and duration, and they existed in the world independent of her belief in them, and that was supposed to be the comfort, that was the whole architecture of her life: things on recordings were things that had happened.

She kept listening, because stopping was not in her nature, and because some cold professional corner of her mind was already filing this under *client psychosis / hidden speaker / elaborate prank,* and that filing system had served her well for thirty years.

Vane's sleeping voice asked questions for forty minutes. Small questions. Housekeeping questions. *"Is it hungry? Is the door holding? Does it remember my grandmother's voice?"* And between each one, in the pauses, the wet voice answered, toneless, thorough, like a clerk reading from a ledger.

*"Not yet." "For now." "It keeps everything."*

And Gideon sat in the parked van with her hands getting colder and colder around the headphones, because she knew this pattern, she'd heard this pattern before, in the sleep-studies her sister had dragged her to, in the hypnagogic mutterings of her mother's last year — except her mother had never had anything answering her, and the answers here were too apt, too timed, too *conversational*, and no speaker hidden in a wall explained why the answers arrived only in the gaps, only when invited, polite as a servant.

Then, at 4:51, Vane's voice changed. It lost its sleep-slur. It came out clear and deliberate, wide awake or something wearing wakefulness, and it said:

*"What is she called?"*

And Gideon felt the sentence arrive in her chest before her ears finished delivering it, because *she* — there was only one *she* on the property besides Vane, there was only one woman who had walked those stairs and photographed those prints and signed clause nine with a chewed biro —

The wet voice took its time. It let the silence stretch until the silence itself seemed to lean toward her through the headphones.

Then it said:

*"Gideon."*

Not Blackwood. Not *the contractor.* Her name. Her given name, the one her mother had chosen, the one that wasn't on the contract, the one that appeared nowhere in the eleven pages or the invoice or the van's registration — nowhere except her own mouth, and her mother's grave, and now, apparently, the inside of Harrowmere House.

She pulled the headphones off so fast they cracked against the dashboard.

The van was very quiet. The rain had stopped. Through the windshield, up the slope, Harrowmere stood in the early light with its boarded windows and its blind black door, and it looked exactly as it had looked yesterday, and the day before, and that was somehow the worst of it — that nothing had changed, that the house wore the same face it had always worn, and had simply, quietly, learned hers.

She sat there for a long time. Long enough for the coffee to go cold. Long enough for the sun to clear the tree line and lay a pale stripe across the bonnet.

Then she did what she always did, what had kept her alive and solvent and sane through thirty years of other people's haunted houses: she reached for the laptop, opened the audio file, and began, with steady hands and a flat professional heart, to transcribe.

Because machines didn't lie.

And the machine had said her name.
