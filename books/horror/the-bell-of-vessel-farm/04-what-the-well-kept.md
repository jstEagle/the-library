## 4. What the Well Kept

Morning came to Vessel Farm the way water comes into a sinking boat — slowly at first, then all at once, and with a sense that something had been let in that could not be let back out.

August woke in the narrow back bedroom with the window already grey and the smell of cold ash in the grate. For a moment they lay still, listening to the house perform its morning inventory: a beam settling somewhere above, the tick of pipes, a door far off opening and closing on its own weight. Old houses made these sounds. August knew this. They had slept in crofts in Shetland and bothies in the Cairngorms and a shepherd's hut on Dartmoor where the wind had spoken through a crack in the wall all night in what sounded suspiciously like Welsh. Houses talked. It was only people who insisted on hearing words in them.

And yet.

They lay there another full minute because of one sound in particular — or rather, the absence of one. Somewhere below, faintly, through two floors and a century of floorboards, August had been aware — without knowing they were aware of it — of a rhythm. A creak, and a pause. A creak, and a pause. Like someone pacing. Or like something being drawn up, hand over hand, and lowered again, and drawn up again.

When August sat up and listened deliberately, it stopped.

"Right," they said to the empty room, in the bright, slightly too-loud voice they used for killing atmosphere. "Paranoid much?"

They dressed quickly, pocketed the notebook, and went down to find the kitchen empty but warm, a pan on the stove with a skin of grease congealing in it, bread sliced and left under a cloth as though Edith Vessel had prepared breakfast for someone and then thought better of being present while it was eaten. There was no note. August ate standing up, feeling like a trespasser at a funeral, and washed their plate before anyone could see them do it.

The farm spread out behind the house in a series of enclosures that seemed to have been designed by someone with a grudge against straight lines. A cobbled yard, then a paddock gone to tussocks, then an orchard of apple trees so old and lichen-crusted they looked less planted than deposited. Beyond that, outbuildings: a granary with its doors chained, a cart shed sagging at one shoulder, a long barn whose roof was furred green with moss, and beyond everything, at the field's edge, a low stone wall over which the land fell away toward the river, invisible but audible, a constant silver whisper under the birdsong.

Edith was nowhere in evidence. Neither was anyone else. The farmhouse had produced, at dinner the previous evening, Edith; her son Robert, a big quiet man with his mother's flat grey eyes and a habit of looking at the middle distance as if the middle distance owed him money; Robert's wife, Marion, who had smiled at August exactly twice and both times as though the smile cost her something she'd have to account for later; and two children, a boy of about ten and a girl of six, who had eaten in silence and been sent to bed the moment their plates were cleared, which in August's experience was roughly forty minutes earlier than children were usually sent anywhere.

They had not asked about the bell yet. That was tonight's work, August had decided. Tonight, over the good bottle of wine August had brought as a gift, they would get Edith talking about 1911. Today was for the ground itself. Folk songs lived in places the way ivy lived in walls — you couldn't get the whole of them from the people alone. You had to walk the fields the song walked. You had to put your hand on the stone the song leaned against.

So August walked.

* * *

The morning was doing its best. Sunlight lay across the paddock in long bars, and the grass was loud with insects, and somewhere a woodpecker worked away at a dead elm with the industry of a man who believes in what he's selling. August filled their lungs with it and felt the night's unease drain out of them like silt from a bucket.

They started with the orchard. Half the trees were dead, standing grey and leafless among the living, and August counted eleven of them before catching themselves counting and wondering why. Under the largest tree, the ground rose in a slight mound, unmarked by any stone. August stood on it for a moment, then stepped off it, then — annoyed at themselves — stepped back on. Nothing. Just earth, and the smell of apples rotting sweetly somewhere in the wet grass.

The barn next. Its great doors were shut but not locked, and they opened with a groan that scattered pigeons from the rafters in a clatter of grey wings. Inside, the light came down in dusty shafts through gaps in the boarding, and the air smelled of hay and machine oil and, beneath both, something older — the cold mineral smell of deep stone, of cellars, of places the sun had never been invited into.

It took August's eyes a moment to adjust. When they did, they saw that the barn's near half was ordinary enough — a tractor under a tarpaulin, bales stacked in a honeycomb, coils of rope hung on pegs. But the far half, past a partition wall, was different. Cleaner. Swept. Kept.

August crossed to the partition and stopped.

Chalk covered the inside of the far wall. Not graffiti, exactly — not the random scrawl of bored farmhands — but writing, laid out in careful rows, in hands of different sizes and different decades. Some of it was old, the chalk gone grey and ghostly, barely legible. Some of it was fresh, white and confident. And some of it was small and wobbly, written by a child, and it was the child's writing that August read first, because children write what adults won't say aloud.

*One for the well and one for the tower,* it said. *Two for the bed and three for the hour. Four for the road that brings you back. Five for the name that goes to black.*

August read it twice. Then a third time, moving their lips, hearing it in the cadence of a skipping rhyme, a counting-out song, the kind of thing chanted in playgrounds by children who don't know — who are never allowed to know — what the words are actually about.

*Five for the name that goes to black.*

"Well," August said, to the empty barn, "that's cheerful."

Their voice came back at them wrong. Barns echo; this one swallowed. The chalked wall drank the sound and gave nothing back, and August found they had taken two steps backward without deciding to.

They got the notebook out. Their hands were steady — they made a point of noticing that they were steady, which is a thing people do when they're not — and they copied the rhyme out in full, all four lines, and then, because they were a folklorist and this was the job, they copied the older versions too. The grey ghost-writing higher up the wall was the same rhyme, mostly, but with variations. In one version, *two for the bed* was *two for the bier*. In another, the last line wasn't *goes to black* but *comes back black*, which was somehow worse. And in the oldest version of all, faded almost to nothing, high in the corner where only someone standing on a ladder would ever see it, there was a single line that appeared in none of the others:

*the count is kept and the count keeps us.*

August stood on an upturned feed bin to photograph it, and while the shutter clicked they became aware of the silence outside changing quality — the woodpecker had stopped, the insects had dimmed, and in the new quiet there was that same rhythm again, faint, patient, coming not from the house now but from somewhere nearer. From the field beyond the barn.

Creak. And a pause. Creak. And a pause.

August climbed down and went out through the barn's side door into the noon light, blinking, heart going harder than the walk justified.

* * *

The well stood in a hollow between the barn and the field wall, ringed by a circle of stones worn round as loaves, and it was — August registered this immediately, professionally, and then again more slowly, personally — wrong.

Not ruined. Ruined wells were common enough on old farms: capped with concrete, filled with rubble, forgotten. This one hadn't been forgotten. It had been *maintained*. The cap was a slab of the same local stone as the church walls, fitted precisely over the well's mouth, and around its edges the mortar was uncracked, recent, tended. Someone had sealed this well carefully and had come back, over years, to check the seal. And set into the top of the slab, flush with the stone, was an iron staple — a heavy ring bolt, polished bright.

Bright with use.

August circled it once, twice. The grass around the well grew differently than the grass elsewhere — shorter, darker, pressed flat in a fan shape that pointed away from the wellhead toward the barn, as though something heavy had been dragged from here many times. And beside the well, coiled on a wooden stand built for the purpose, its feet sunk into the earth by long habit, lay a rope.

It was a beautiful rope. That was August's first coherent thought, and they hated themselves for it. Twenty feet of it at least, hemp, plaited in a pattern August didn't recognize, and every inch of it was worn smooth — not frayed, not rotten, but *polished*, burnished to the colour and sheen of dark honey, the way a church pew is polished, the way a banister is polished, by the accumulated pressure of ten thousand hands. Generations of hands. The rope had a groove worn into it, a shallow channel running its whole length where grip after grip after grip had passed, and August picked it up and felt the groove fit their own palm so exactly that they put it down again very quickly, as though it had spoken.

There was no bucket. No trough nearby that the water might serve. No reason at all, agricultural or domestic, why anyone would need to draw anything up from a sealed well using a rope worn smooth by a hundred years of use.

Unless, said a voice in August's head that sounded unpleasantly like their own most skeptical self, unless whatever's down there isn't water.

"Don't be absurd," August said aloud. The field absorbed it.

But they knelt at the wellhead anyway, because they were who they were, and they put their ear — gently, the way you'd listen at a door — against the cool stone of the cap.

Silence. Then, deeper than silence, so far down it might have been imagination or might have been the blood in their own ears: a sound. Small. Round. A single tone, held, the way a wet finger holds a note on the rim of a glass.

August lifted their head. The tone stopped. Or continued, and simply stopped being audible to a listening that had withdrawn — August genuinely could not say which, and the not-knowing sat in their chest like a swallowed stone.

They photographed the well from four angles. They measured the cap (three feet by three feet), the staple (iron, hand-forged, older than the mortar around it, which meant the staple had come first and the seal had been built to fit it). They wrote, in the notebook, in their best archive hand:

*Well sealed, maintained. Rope in active use, no practical function. Rhyme in barn references "the count." Ask E.V. directly re: well. Do NOT lead with rhyme — let her offer it.*

Then they sat back on their heels and looked at the thing they had been carefully not looking at, which was this: every instinct they had, every professional instinct, every instinct of the blood they shared with the people in that house — all of it was saying the same word, over and over, in the voice of a child skipping rope.

*Count. Count. Count.*

* * *

Edith was in the kitchen when August returned, shelling peas into a bowl with small precise movements, and August decided, standing in the doorway with the notebook burning in their pocket, to take the direct route after all.

"That's a fine old well you've got," they said. "Sealed, is it? Contaminated water table?"

The peas went on clicking against the porcelain. Click. Pause. Click. Pause. And August realized, with a small cold shock, that the rhythm was the same. The rhythm from the night. The rhythm from the field. Edith shelled peas the way something else drew rope.

"The water's fine," Edith said, without looking up. "Never been anything wrong with the water."

"Then why seal it?"

Click. Pause. "Wells get sealed. It's what people do."

"People seal wells that are bad," August said pleasantly. "Or wells that are dangerous. Or—" they watched Edith's hands, and Edith's hands did not stop, did not even slow, and somehow that was the most frightening thing yet— "or wells that are used."

The peas stopped.

For a moment the kitchen was so quiet August could hear the clock on the mantel, and the river far off, and underneath both — or thought they did, or perhaps only remembered it — that held, round, patient tone.

Edith looked up. Her face was the face from the first evening, courteous and closed, but her eyes had gone somewhere else entirely, somewhere down a long dark shaft, and when she spoke her voice was perfectly level and perfectly terrible.

"You'll want to know about the bell," she said. "Everyone does, eventually. Sit down, cousin. I'll make tea. And I'll tell you what my grandmother told me, and what hers told her, and you can put it in your little machine." She rose, and took the kettle from the hob, and filled it from the tap, and her hands, August noticed, were absolutely steady, which is a thing people do when they are not.

"But we'll have tea first," Edith said. "Because after I've told you, no one in this house will want to talk for a while. And because—" she set the kettle down, and looked at August with something that in a kinder family might have been pity, and in this one was only arithmetic— "because you'll want your hands warm for what comes at midnight."

Outside, far off, the church tower stood empty against the sky, as it had stood empty since 1911.

August sat down, and waited for the kettle, and did not ask what comes at midnight, because some part of them — the part that had heard a child's chalk, and felt a stranger's groove fit their palm, and put their ear to a sealed stone and heard a note held patiently in the dark — some part of them had already begun, quietly, helplessly, to count.
