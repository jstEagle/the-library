## 16. Four

The house had learned to be quiet in a new way since the third ring.

Before, its silences had been the ordinary silences of old buildings — pipes ticking, floorboards settling, the wind worrying the loose slates above the east wing. Now it was quiet the way a held breath is quiet. August felt it every time they crossed the hall: the whole structure listening, leaning inward, as if the walls themselves were counting alongside the bell.

Three rings. Three names scratched somewhere August could not yet see but had begun to feel like a weight behind their own sternum. Edith's brother, smiling in his sleep. The daughter-in-law — Ruth, her name was Ruth, though the village had already begun saying *the Vessel woman* as if she'd been erased from the parish rolls — found at the foot of the stairs with her hands folded and her face turned toward the empty tower, as though she'd been listening for something and had been answered.

And now there were four of them left in the house that watched back: Edith; the two children, Wren and little Ivo; and August, who slept each night with the tape recorder running beside the bed, capturing hours of nothing, of breathing and creaking timber, because the nothing had begun to feel like evidence.

August spent the days in the archive room with the ledger open on their knees, reading and rereading the page where their own name sat in faded brown ink — blood ink, they knew now — in a hand that predated their birth by decades.

*August Yarrow.*

Not a cousin's name added carelessly. A name written down before August had been born, waiting for them the way a trap waits for the fox that hasn't yet taken the path.

They did not tell Edith what they'd found. They told themselves this was strategy. They told themselves a great many things.

* * *

The fourth ring came on a Thursday, three days after the third, and it came early — not midnight but eleven minutes past ten, while the sky still held the last grey light of evening, which somehow made it worse. Midnight was the bell's hour by tradition; this was the bell breaking its own rule, or perhaps simply no longer caring to keep up appearances.

August was in the kitchen helping Ivo butter toast when the sound came through the walls.

It was not loud. That was the thing no one who hadn't heard it could understand. It came through stone and brick and forty yards of dark garden as though the air itself had been struck, one round bronze note that entered the body through the soles of the feet and the soft tissue behind the eyes and rang there, resonant, patient, *counting*.

The knife stopped in Ivo's hand. He was seven, and he didn't cry anymore when the bell rang; he had passed through crying into something older than crying, something August recognized with horror as competence. The children of Hollowmere learned young how to stand still inside a sound.

"Four," Ivo said, matter-of-fact, and went back to his toast.

August was already moving. Through the hall, past the portraits of Vessels whose faces all seemed slightly turned from where the painter must have placed them, out the front door and across the yard, gravel biting through thin shoes, toward the church tower that stood black against the last of the light.

Edith was faster. August found her already at the lychgate, gripping the iron with both hands, her silver hair loose and streaming in a wind that wasn't blowing anywhere else. She was looking up at the tower, and August followed her gaze and saw — as they had seen twice before and would never stop finding impossible — the faint swing of shadow in the belfry arch. Something hanging where nothing hung. Something swinging where nothing swung.

"Who?" August asked, though they already knew the shape of the answer.

Edith didn't answer. She turned and walked back toward the house, and her walk was the walk of a woman who had done arithmetic she refused to show anyone, and August followed her, and the tower behind them fell silent mid-swing, satisfied, four parts fed.

* * *

They found him in the orchard.

Thomas — Edith's son, Wren and Ivo's father, a broad gentle man who had spent the week since Ruth's death walking the farm's boundaries over and over as though measuring the cage — lay among the fallen apples with his arms at his sides and his face toward the sky. His eyes were open. His mouth was open too, and August, kneeling in the wet grass, understood after a moment that he had been singing. Or trying to. The note was still caught in his throat somehow, a hum that lived in the dead air around his lips, and when August leaned close they heard it clearly:

The first verse of the song.

He had been singing it when the bell took him. Or the bell had filled him with it on the way out, the way a bottle keeps the shape of what was poured from it last.

Wren found them there — twelve years old, her mother's chin and her grandfather's stubbornness — and she did not scream. She stood at the edge of the orchard grass with her fists at her sides and said, "He was going to take us away tomorrow. He told me. He'd worked out the roads."

August gathered her against their chest and held her while she shook, and over her head they watched Edith come down the orchard row slowly, an old woman crossing ground she had walked all her life, and stop at her son's body, and look down at him for a long time without touching him.

Then Edith said, quietly, in the voice of someone setting down a heavy load: "We'll need the good sheet."

* * *

That night the house emptied itself of everything except arithmetic.

Edith sent the children to the blue bedroom at the far end of the east wing and told them to bar the door and sing the first verse together until morning — *only the first verse*, she said, twice, looking at Wren with an intensity that made August's skin crawl — and then she sat down at the kitchen table with the ledger between her hands as though it were a Bible and she had finally decided to read the difficult passages.

August sat opposite her. The fire had burned low. Somewhere upstairs, faintly, two children's voices rose and fell in the old tune, steady as a tide.

"You knew," August said.

Edith looked up. Her eyes in the firelight were pale and clear and utterly without excuse in them, and August understood, in that moment, that whatever confession was coming had been rehearsed for weeks, perhaps months, perhaps years.

"I knew," Edith said.

"The ledger. My name. Written in blood before I was born."

"Yes."

"How long have you known what I am?"

Edith folded her hands. Outside, the wind moved through the orchard where Thomas lay under the good sheet, waiting for morning and a grave the ground might or might not agree to keep.

"When your grandmother left Hollowmere in 1951, she was running," Edith said. "You know that much, I think. What you don't know is why she was allowed to run. The bell let her go because she was already dying — it counts the line, August, it doesn't waste rings on those nearly finished. But she made a bargain of her own, the way our family makes bargains. She promised the bell a return. Blood that had gone out would come back. Blood that had never heard the song would hear it." Her voice didn't waver. "She wrote to my mother every Christmas until she died. And in the last letter she mentioned a grandchild. A folklorist. Someone who collected songs."

August's mouth had gone dry. "You wrote to me. The letter about the archive grant. The invitation to record the village songs."

"I wrote to you."

"You told me the parish council wanted its oral history preserved."

"The parish council has been dead for thirty years." Edith said it without apology, without softness, the way she said everything. "I needed you here before the count began. It had to begin with you in the house. That was the terms. The returned blood opens the count, and the returned blood closes it. One way or another."

The kitchen was very quiet. Upstairs the children sang on, sweet and oblivious, the first verse rising and falling like something afloat.

"You brought me here to die," August said. It wasn't a question, and it barely qualified as speech; it was more the sound a person makes when a floor gives way beneath them.

"I brought you here because someone had to pay, and everyone else in this house is innocent." For the first time something moved behind Edith's composure — not guilt, August realized, but fury, banked and ancient. "In 1911 my great-grandfather sold the bell to save his own skin and signed the deed in blood because the buyer demanded it, and the village let us carry the debt alone for a hundred years. They hid the bell in the well and told us it was melted and let us believe we were cursed when we were *billed*. Every ring, every death, every child raised in this house learning to stand still inside a sound — that is interest, August, compounded across four generations. And now the principal comes due, and you want me to feel shame for choosing which account pays it?"

"You could have asked me."

"To do what? Decline?" Edith laughed, short and terrible. "You'd have come anyway, once you'd heard the bell. You're a Yarrow. Curiosity is the family disease. No — I did what my mother did, and hers before her. I arranged the table and set the places and let the debt find its own way home." She pushed the ledger across the wood toward August. "But I will tell you one thing I haven't told anyone, and you may make of it what you can."

August looked down at the open page. Their own name in brown ink. Below it, in a newer hand — Edith's hand, recent, the ink still sharp — a single line:

*The singer stays. The line goes free.*

"That's not in the original bargain," August said slowly. "That's not what the bell wants."

"No," Edith agreed. "It's what Tam told me, the night before he died, with river water filling his mouth for speaking. The bell counts because it was never answered. A hundred years of silence, and silence is the only thing it knows how to punish. Sing to it — the second verse, sung by blood, at its mouth — and the count stops. Not because the debt is forgiven. Because it's *paid*. All of it, at once, by one voice." Her pale eyes fixed on August's face. "I invited a sacrifice. I will not pretend otherwise. But I also invited the only person alive who might choose to be more than one."

August sat very still. The betrayal moved through them like cold water finding every channel — the letter, the welcome, Edith's careful warmth in those first days, the way she had steered them toward the well, toward the barn, toward the ledger, always half a step ahead, opening doors. Every kindness re-weighed itself and came up lead.

And underneath the cold water, absurdly, stubbornly, something else. Wren's fists at her sides in the orchard. Ivo buttering toast with a steady hand because no one had ever taught him it was allowed to shake. Thomas humming the first verse into the dark with the bell's note still in his throat.

They had never belonged to this family. That had been the lie of the whole visit, August's cheerful skepticism, the outsider's notebook. But the ledger didn't care about belonging. The blood didn't care. The bell had counted them from before their birth, and everything they loved about their work — the songs, the keeping, the refusal to let a thing be forgotten — had been leading here their entire life, along roads they hadn't known were roads.

"You knew I'd decide to sing," August said at last. "That's the real reason. Not the sacrifice. You gambled on the sacrifice and hedged with the hope."

Edith's composure cracked — just once, just enough to show the exhausted woman beneath, four rings deep, holding two grandchildren above water with her bare hands. "I'm an old woman in a drowning house," she said. "I've placed every bet I have."

August closed the ledger. Above them the children's voices had finished the first verse and started it again, and again, faithful as the bell itself.

"Tomorrow," August said, "we open the well."

* * *

They went out at dawn, while the mist still stood in the pasture like a congregation, and stood together at the well's lip — August and Edith, and Wren, who had followed without being asked and whom neither of them had the heart to send back. The rope hung in its groove, worn smooth by a century of hands that had lowered things down and, so far as any record showed, never drawn anything up.

August put their palms against the cold stone and leaned over the dark, and far below, deeper than any bucket had ever reached, something green and round and patient waited with their name already scratched inside its crown.

"We'll need the winch," August said. "And we'll need Wren."

Edith drew breath to object — August saw it gather in her chest — and then, for the first time in a hundred years of Vessel arithmetic, she let the number go uncalculated.

"Yes," she said. "All of it. Whatever it costs."

Below them, in the dark, the well rope gave a single slow creak — like something turning over in its sleep, like something hearing its name — and began, very gently, to sway.
