## 4. Three Years of Explanations

The first noise came on a Tuesday in October, which Paloma Ravensworth would later decide was important, because Tuesdays were the day he took the bins down and the bins were heavy and he was already listening to the building when it happened.

It was a knock. Two knocks, precise, patient, from inside the wall behind his bed.

He did what any reasonable man does. He told himself it was pipes. Pipes knock. Pipes settle, contract, complain about pressure the way old men complain about weather. He got up, pressed his ear to the plaster, and heard nothing at all—which was itself a kind of answer, because pipes don't stop for company.

He went back to bed. He lay there with his eyes open until the radiator ticked its way through the small hours, and somewhere around four he slept, and in the morning the wall was just a wall, and he was just a man who had imagined something, and that was fine. That was manageable. He had been managing things like that his whole life.

The second noise came nine days later. The third came four days after that. By the sixth week they had a schedule—not a strict one, nothing so crude as clockwork, but a rhythm, like a guest who has learned your habits and is being polite about them. Scratching behind the skirting board while he ate. A soft dragging weight across the loft above him at two in the morning, though the loft hatch was painted shut and had been since before he moved in. Once—and this was the one he never told anyone, not even later, not even when telling would have saved him trouble—a sound like breathing. Not loud. Not threatening. Just present, just beside his pillow, the way a dog breathes when it has settled close without permission.

Paloma did not call an exterminator. He knew what exterminators said, because he would eventually become one. They said mice. They said squirrels. They said, with the confidence of men who have never once been wrong in their own hearing, *you'd hear it more if you had food out.*

He had no food out. He kept his kitchen like a laboratory. And still the sounds came, courteous as a neighbor borrowing sugar.

So he did the thing that would define him: he explained it away.

Not all at once. Explanation is a craft, and like any craft it improves with practice. At first his explanations were clumsy—wind in the cavity wall, thermal expansion, the building's age (it was forty years younger than he claimed). But each night the sounds returned and each morning he refined the theory, sanding down the rough edges of his own terror until it fit neatly inside a sentence he could carry. *Old buildings make noise.* Four words. He could hold those four words against anything.

And here is what he learned, lying awake in the dark with his heart going like a trapped bird: the explaining helped. Not because it was true—he stopped believing it was true somewhere around month three—but because the act of explanation gave him somewhere to stand. Fear needs a shape, and if you refuse to give it one, it takes yours. So he built a wall of plausible causes between himself and the thing behind the plaster, brick by brick, and he slept behind it, badly but reliably, for two years.

He might have gone on sleeping there forever if the family upstairs hadn't moved out.

* * *

They were the Okonkwos, a couple in their sixties with a grown daughter who visited on Sundays. Paloma knew them the way he knew everyone—by sound and by inference, never by conversation. Mrs. Okonkwo hummed while she cooked. Mr. Okonkwo's knees announced every stair. The daughter laughed at her phone in a way that suggested she was laughing *at* something rather than *with* it.

One Sunday in March, the daughter knocked on his door. This alone was remarkable enough that he opened it before deciding whether to.

"You're the quiet one downstairs," she said. "Sorry—I'm Ada. Listen, have you ever heard anything? In your flat? Noises?"

Every alarm he owned went off at once, silently, professionally.

"Noises," he repeated.

"Scratching. Knocking. Mum says it's the pipes but Dad swears—" She stopped herself, glanced over her shoulder toward the stairwell, lowered her voice. "Dad swears someone's up there. In the roof space. Walking."

"Old buildings," Paloma said, and heard how smooth it came out, how practiced, like a phrase worn soft at the edges from handling. "You get settling, expansion. Sounds travel strangely in these conversions. It can seem directional when it isn't."

Ada looked at him for a long moment, and he understood that she wasn't really listening to the words. She was listening for whether he believed them. And because he had spent two years learning to deliver disbelief convincingly—even to himself, especially to himself—she found nothing to catch on.

"That's what Mum says," she admitted, relieved. "It's just—the reason I ask is they're moving. Selling up. Dad won't say why, exactly, he just keeps saying the house 'doesn't suit him anymore.' After thirty-one years." She smiled the smile of someone carrying something heavier than she'd admit. "Anyway. Sorry to bother you."

She left. Paloma stood in his doorway holding his mug of tea, which had gone cold without his noticing, and thought about the word *directional*, and about how he had used it like a tool, and about how tools don't care what they're used for.

The Okonkwos moved out in April. The new tenants lasted five weeks. Then another family, seven weeks. Then the flat stood empty through the autumn, and during that autumn the noises in Paloma's walls grew—there was no other word for it—confident. The knocking acquired a rhythm he could almost transcribe. The breathing came closer to his face. One night in November he woke at 3:11 a.m.—he checked the time, he always checked the time afterward, and it was always 3:11, always eleven minutes past the hour when the world is supposed to be at its emptiest—to find that the scratching had moved. It was no longer behind the skirting board. It was under the floorboards directly beneath his feet, as though whatever walked below had climbed one story closer and was now waiting, politely, for him to acknowledge the promotion.

He sat up in bed. He looked at the floor. He said, out loud, in the voice of a man addressing a colleague across a desk:

"I don't know what you are. But I am not going to look. Do you understand? I am not going to be the person who finds out."

The scratching stopped. It did not resume that night, or the next, or for a week after. When it came back it was quieter, more distant, almost apologetic—and Paloma lay in the dark understanding two things with terrible clarity.

First: it had heard him. Whatever the rules were, acknowledgment mattered. His refusal had been received as a kind of answer, and answers were currency here.

Second: he could live like this. He could live alongside it, indefinitely, if he never named it, never faced it, never let it fully exist. Denial wasn't cowardice. Denial was architecture. He had built himself a house to live in and the house was holding.

That was the night the idea arrived—not fully formed, but as a seed, dropped gently into the soil of him: *if I can do this for myself, I can do it for money.*

* * *

He registered the business in January. Pest control, domestic and commercial. He chose the name Ravensworth off a list he made at 3 a.m., testing surnames against the feeling of wearing them: Ravensworth sounded like a man with a van and a laminated certificate and no history whatsoever. Exactly right. He printed business cards. He bought coveralls two sizes too large, so that he would fill them loosely, vaguely, a man-shaped suggestion rather than a presence. He learned to speak in the flattest register available to him, the vocal equivalent of beige.

And he discovered he was good. Genuinely, startlingly good—not at catching pests, though he was competent enough at that, but at the other thing. The real thing. People called him not because they had rats but because they had *sounds*, and sounds frightened them in ways rats didn't, and what they needed—what they paid triple for, once he figured out the pricing—was someone to stand in their kitchen and explain the unexplainable back into a box.

He developed a repertoire. There was the Pipe Speech, delivered with a tap and a knowing nod. There was the Settling Structure Lecture, best deployed in older houses, accompanied by a slow walk of the perimeter with his hand trailing the walls like a dowser. There was the Wildlife Assessment—squirrels in the soffits, starlings under the eaves—complete with photographic evidence he sometimes manufactured by photographing his own gloved hand next to a gap in the fascia. And when none of these fit, when the client's face said *I know what I heard and it was not a squirrel*, he had his masterpiece: the sympathetic shrug, the tilt of the head, the words "these places hold sound in strange ways," which meant nothing and forgave everything.

Clients loved him. They recommended him to neighbors. They said things like *he just has a calm manner* and *you feel better the moment he arrives*, and they were right, though not for the reasons they thought. What Paloma sold was permission. Permission to stop listening. Permission to go back to bed. He was, he understood perfectly well, a dealer in the same drug he took himself: explanation, the opiate of the frightened rationalist.

There were jobs that tested him. A cottage outside Marlow where the child's drawings on the fridge all featured the same tall figure standing in corners, drawn in a hand too controlled for a six-year-old. A bedsit where the tenant had taped the wardrobe shut and asked Paloma, twice, in a whisper, "You'll check inside it, won't you?"—and Paloma had checked, and found nothing, and said "clear," and watched the man weep with relief at a single syllable. A farmhouse where every clock had stopped at different times but every calendar page had been turned to the same month, and Paloma had stood in the kitchen doing arithmetic he refused to finish, and quoted the client for damp-proofing instead.

Each time, the procedure was the same. Look. Explain. Leave. Never stay past dark, never take a job that required overnight work, never—this rule he wrote on the inside of his own skull in letters he could read in the blackness—*never acknowledge it where it can hear you.*

For three years it worked. He built a modest reputation, a modest income, a life with no sharp edges. He told himself he was helping people, and some nights he even believed it, and on the nights he didn't believe it he explained that away too, because by now explanation was less a skill than an organ, automatic, involuntary, beating.

Then the call about Harrow Glen came, and the whole careful structure revealed itself for what it had always been: not a fortress, but a dam. And the caller knew his unlisted number.

* * *

Afterward—after everything, in the version of events he would reconstruct a hundred times—he would fix on the moment he should have known. Not the triple fee. Not the whispering caller who spoke as though reading from a script held too close to the mouth. It was simpler than that. It was the drive out, the lane narrowing between hedges, the house appearing at the end of it like something the landscape had been keeping secret—and the feeling that rose in him, unbidden, unwelcome, familiar as his own heartbeat:

*Home.*

Not comfort. Recognition. The same recognition he'd felt the first time the wall behind his bed had knocked—two knocks, precise, patient—as though some part of him had been walking toward this address for three years and had simply, finally, arrived.

He parked the van. He gathered his case: the recorder he told clients was for "baseline acoustic documentation," the flour he used for tracking prints, the flashlight, the clipboard, the entire theater of competence. He looked at Harrow Glen's dark windows and performed, one last time, the ritual that had kept him alive.

*Old house,* he told himself. *Settling structure. Sound carries strangely in these places. Someone heard something, panicked, found my card. Triple rate because it's remote and they're desperate. Nothing more.*

The explanation went down smooth as medicine. It always did. That was the trap of it—that was the thing he couldn't see yet, standing in the gravel with his case in his hand: the explanations had never once been *true*. They had only ever been load-bearing. And he was about to walk into a house where the load was calculated differently, where the arithmetic included names and dates and a blank line, where the thing behind the walls had spent years learning the sound of his footsteps the way he had learned the sound of its knocking.

Two knocks, precise, patient. He would hear them again soon, from below, and this time they would not be asking to be explained.

They would be asking to be answered.

He rang the bell. Somewhere deep under the floorboards—so far beneath him it might have been the center of the earth—something turned over in its sleep, and began, softly, to count.
