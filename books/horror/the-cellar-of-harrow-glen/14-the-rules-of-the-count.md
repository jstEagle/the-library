## 14. The Rules of the Count

Tam Aldous spoke backwards the way other people hummed—absently, tunelessly, as if the syllables were simply falling out of him in whatever order they pleased. Paloma had spent two nights transcribing it, hunched over his recorder in the blue room at the top of the stairs, playing the boy's sleep-talk back at quarter speed until the reversed words resolved into something like grammar.

*Ereh si eht tnuoc.* Here is the count.

*Em llet ton od.* Do not tell me.

He wrote them out on the back of an invoice from a job in Croydon, three dead mice and a wasp nest, and stared at them until the handwriting seemed to belong to someone else. The sentences weren't random. They were answers. Tam was answering questions that hadn't been asked yet—and when Paloma checked the timestamps against his own recordings from the cellar, he found the questions arriving hours later, spoken in that soft second voice, the one that sounded like a woman reading aloud to children she loved very much.

The boy heard tomorrow. The cellar asked yesterday. And somewhere between the two of them, the shape of the thing's rules was assembling itself, piece by piece, like a jigsaw whose picture only became visible once you'd committed to finishing it.

Paloma carried the invoice downstairs on the fourth morning, past the portrait of the grandmother with her folded hands, into the kitchen where Wren sat with the ledger open in front of her and a pot of tea going cold between her elbows.

"I think I know how it works," he said.

She looked up. There were shadows under her eyes that matched his own, twin bruises of the same long week. "Tell me."

"First I need Tam."

* * *

They found him in the garden, standing at the border where the lawn gave up and the nettles began, watching nothing with the particular stillness of a child who has learned that looking directly at things is expensive. Four years since their father had last crossed the threshold; longer than that since Tam had looked anyone in the eye. Wren had told Paloma that much on the second night, in the kitchen, in the voice people use for weather.

"Tam," she said gently. "Mr. Ravensworth needs to ask you something."

The boy didn't turn. But his mouth moved, and what came out was: *Sdrawkcab ti ti ekat t'nod.*

"Don't take it backwards," Wren translated, frowning. "That doesn't—"

"It does," Paloma said. His mouth had gone dry. He crouched down to the boy's level without touching him, without entering the nettle-border of his attention. "Tam. I'm not going to make you look at anything. I just want you to listen. Can you listen?"

A pause. Then, small and clear and forwards for the first time in Paloma's hearing:

"Yes."

Wren made a sound beside him like a stitch tearing.

"The thing in the cellar," Paloma said carefully. "When it counts someone. What makes them countable?"

Tam's eyes stayed fixed on the nettles. When he answered, the words came out forwards but wrong-ordered, shuffled like a dropped deck, and it took Paloma three passes of the recorder later that night to reconstruct them. But Wren caught it live—she'd had four years of practice—and her hand found Paloma's sleeve and gripped.

"It counts who sees it," she whispered. "It counts who sees it. That's what he said. It counts who sees it."

"And seeing isn't just looking." Paloma stood slowly, his knees complaining. "That's why your family stopped looking up. That's why the house is so quiet, why everyone moves through it like it's furniture. You're not numb because you've given up. You're numb because numbness is armor."

"We stopped acknowledging it," Wren said. Her voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of someone walking on ice and listening for the crack. "Mum started it, after Aunt Ivy. She said—we thought she was depressed. She stopped saying goodnight to the house. She stopped saying *anything* to the house. And the noises got quieter. We thought she was getting better."

"She was getting invisible." Paloma thought of himself, thirty-one years of practiced forgettability, of doors that closed before he reached them, of being the man shopkeepers looked through. All those years he'd believed it was a curse. It had been training. "Acknowledgment. That's the first rule. It can only count what acknowledges it—or what it can force to acknowledge it."

"How do you force acknowledgment?" Wren asked.

And Tam, still facing the nettles, said in perfect reversed clarity: *Eman ruoy llet ot uoy stnemom eht.*

The moments it makes you tell your name.

Paloma played it back for Wren that evening, and watched her go white around the mouth. "The dreams," she said. "Everyone has the dreams here. The ones where you're introducing yourself to someone in a room you can't quite see, and you wake up and you feel like you've *said* something. Mum used to wake up crying about it. She said she could taste her own name in her mouth, like she'd been holding it under her tongue all night."

"It takes what it can't be given." Paloma wrote it down, then crossed it out and wrote it again, because the act of writing felt like signing something. "Rule two. Acknowledgment can be taken—in sleep, maybe, or in moments of weakness—but taken names are weaker. Slower. Your mother's been on the ledger for years and she's still here. It's been sipping at her instead of drinking."

"Then why is she next on the list?"

Because she was tired, Paloma almost said, and stopped himself. Because the armor wears thin. Because four years of not-looking is four years of knowing exactly what's behind you.

"Because it's patient," he said instead. "And patience always wins against exhaustion."

* * *

The third rule came from the ledger itself, and it came at two in the morning, when Paloma was alone in the kitchen with the book open under the stove light and a magnifying glass he'd bought for reading pest-control contracts.

Grandmother Aldous's handwriting filled the early pages: firm, slanted, unhurried. Names and dates, names and dates, each struck through on the day its owner "moved away." Paloma had catalogued forty-one entries over three nights of cross-referencing with the parish records in the village library, and every single one of them had died inside these walls, and every death had been preceded by a night of recorded voices. He knew that part. That was arithmetic.

What he hadn't noticed until tonight was the ink.

The strikethroughs changed. For the first twenty entries, the lines were drawn in the same brown-black iron gall as the names themselves—grandmother's pen, grandmother's hand. But starting with entry twenty-two, a girl named Constance Aldous, 1963, the line through the name was different. Thinner. Darker. Drawn with a fountain pen in an ink that had gone slightly violet with age, in a stroke that swept left to right with a little flourish at the end, like a signature.

Someone else had started crossing out the names.

Paloma turned pages faster. Entry twenty-six, Harold. Same violet flourish. Thirty-one, Margaret. Thirty-five, the uncle nobody talked about. Every strike after 1963 belonged to the second hand—and the second hand grew more confident page by page, until by the final pages it wasn't striking through names at all.

It was writing them.

The last five entries—the mother, Tam, the father, and the blank line—were in the violet ink entirely. Not copied. Authored. As if somewhere along the way the ledger had stopped being a record and become a composition, and the thing holding the pen had learned to spell.

But it was the blank line that stopped Paloma's heart, because now, under the stove light, at the right angle, he could see that the blankness wasn't blank. There was an impression in the paper. The ghost-groove of letters written hard and then erased—carefully, thoroughly erased, but paper remembers. He tilted the page against the light and read the indentation the way he'd once read pressed impressions on notepads left in hotel rooms by unfaithful husbands.

Three letters. A short name. And beneath it, in smaller grooves, almost tender: *offered freely.*

His hands were shaking when he set the ledger down.

"A name freely offered can substitute for a name owed." He said it aloud to the empty kitchen, and the house did not answer, which somehow was worse. Somewhere below the floorboards, something had already known this rule for sixty years. Had already tried to use it. Had written a name into the page and then rubbed it away—why? Why erase an offer?

Unless the offer had been refused.

Unless there were conditions. Unless the name had to be true. Unless the cellar had been offered a false name once—a chosen name, a shield, a *Ravensworth*—and had pressed its pen into the page and found the ink wouldn't take, and had erased the failure in something like embarrassment, or hunger, or rage.

Paloma sat very still in the dark kitchen of Harrow Glen and understood, with the cold clarity of a man reading his own autopsy, that the rules had just shown him the exit. One name, freely given, truly given, could buy the freedom of everyone owed. The mother. The father. Tam. Wren.

All it cost was everything he had spent thirty-one years building: a self with no fixed address, a man made entirely of aliases, a person whose greatest talent was that no one, anywhere, held the deed to him.

To pay, he would have to become findable. To save them, he would have to let the cellar learn the name his mother had called him across a kitchen that no longer existed, the name buried under three invented identities and a career of being overlooked. The one name in the world that was actually his.

And he would have to do it before the entity finished taking the mother's name the slow way, sip by sip, dream by dream.

Upstairs, faintly, through the floor, he heard Tam begin to talk in his sleep. Paloma didn't need the recorder anymore. He was learning the language.

*Yadot emoc lliw eh,* the boy said, to no one, to the nettles, to the dark. *Yadot emoc lliw eh.*

He will come today.

Paloma closed the ledger, and in the silence after, from beneath the kitchen floor, polite as a knock, came the sound of a page turning.
