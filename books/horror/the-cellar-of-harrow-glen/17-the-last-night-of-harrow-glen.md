## 17. The Last Night of Harrow Glen

*Paloma*

The house knew before they did.

He understood that at 9:47 in the evening, standing in the upstairs hallway with two suitcases and a duffel bag, when the door to Tam's room — a door he had walked through a dozen times that week, a door he could have found blindfolded — was simply not there. The wall was continuous. Unbroken plaster, water-stained in the familiar places, hung with the same crooked portrait of some Aldous ancestor whose face had gone gray as fog. No door. No seam where a door had been.

"Tam," Paloma said, and knocked on the wall, and the wall answered with silence.

Behind him, down the corridor, another door opened — Wren's mother's room, which had been at the far end of the hall for as long as he'd known this house — and Tam stepped out of it, blinking, holding a shoebox of photographs against his chest like a shield.

"That was Mum's room," Tam said. His voice came out level, but his eyes didn't. "That was always Mum's room."

"I know." Paloma kept his voice low and even, the voice he used on cornered animals, though tonight he wasn't sure which of them was cornered. "It doesn't matter. Rooms are just rooms. We're not leaving by them anyway."

But that was a lie, and the house knew it too. Every way out of Harrow Glen went through its rooms. That was what a house was: a throat made of rooms.

They had agreed on midnight. The plan had been built over three days of whispered conferences in the kitchen, the one room where the recordings never caught anything, as if the thing below considered the kitchen beneath its dignity or above its interest. Wren would take her father. Paloma would take the mother — no. He corrected himself, standing in the shifting hallway, heart hammering. Her mother was already crossed out. Already gone. The plan had been rebuilt around that absence, and every time Paloma thought of it he felt the loss open under him like a stair that wasn't there.

Wren would take her father. Paloma would carry the ledger and the tapes. Tam would walk between them, because Tam was the one the house liked best now, Tam who spoke backwards, Tam whose answers arrived before questions did, and Paloma did not trust the house not to try to keep him.

Midnight. The front door. They'd tested it twice today; it opened onto gravel and moonlight and the long drive away from everything.

Paloma went downstairs to check it a third time.

The front door opened onto the cellar stair.

* * *

*Wren*

She felt her father's hand go rigid inside hers, and she knew before she looked that he was seeing what Paloma had called up to describe in a voice carefully sanded of all feeling: stone steps descending into dark, the smell of wet earth and old flour, the door at the bottom standing politely ajar.

"It's showing us the way it wants us to go," Paloma said. "So we don't go that way."

Her father hadn't left the house in four years. Wren had spent four years learning the geography of his fear, the way his world had contracted room by room until the whole house was a held breath. Now she watched him stand in the entrance hall, coat buttoned wrong, and understand in one long shudder that the house had never been shelter. It had been a mouth, and they had been living in it, and it was closing.

"We go out the back," Paloma said. "Kitchen door. I checked it an hour ago. Gravel path, then the field, then the road. Half a mile in the dark. We can do half a mile."

Her father nodded. It was the smallest nod Wren had ever seen, a nod made of almost nothing, but it moved his feet, and his feet were what mattered now.

They gathered in the kitchen. Suitcases they'd packed that afternoon stood by the door, and looking at them Wren felt something crack sideways in her chest — how small their lives were, how few boxes it took. Her mother's good china stayed in the cabinet. Her mother herself stayed nowhere at all. There was no room in the world that held her anymore except the ledger line with its single stroke through it, and Wren carried the ledger in her bag like an organ removed from her own body.

Tam sat at the kitchen table, shoebox in his lap, saying nothing. Then, softly, in syllables poured out backwards like water running uphill:

"*Eesni ton si ti.*"

Wren's skin crawled. She'd gotten better at hearing through the reversal, the way you get better at hearing a melody played upside down. *It is not easy,* maybe. Or *it isn't seen.* Or —

"What isn't?" she asked.

Tam looked at the kitchen door. The kitchen door they were all looking at. The one that an hour ago had opened onto the gravel path and the moon.

It still opened onto the gravel path. But the path was wrong. Wren saw it immediately, the way you see a face that has been subtly redrawn: the gravel ran toward the gate instead of away from it, and beyond the gate there was no field and no road, only more Harrow Glen, another version of the house, its windows lit, its windows dark, its windows lit.

"The back door opens into the house," she said. Her voice sounded very far away to her own ears. "Both doors open into the house."

"No," Paloma said. He was already moving, checking the window latches, the little sash windows above the sink, the coal chute cover in the scullery, with the quick hands of a man who had spent his life finding the ways small things got in and out of buildings. "No, it's bluffing. Houses can't rearrange themselves. Physics—"

The lights went out.

All of them. Not a flicker, not a brownout — a total, instant subtraction, as if the house had closed its eyes. In the sudden black, Wren heard her father breathing, heard Tam's shoebox creak under his grip, heard Paloma say, very quietly, mostly to himself:

"It's not physics. It never was. Get the torches. Nobody lets go of anybody."

* * *

*Paloma*

Torches. Good. Torches were something he could do. He'd kept three in his kit since the first night of recordings, since 3:11 a.m., since the voice asked *is the counter still coming* and something else had answered in a tone so tender it had taken him a full minute to realize tenderness was the worst part.

His hands found the canvas bag by the pantry door. First torch out, clicked on, beam swinging across the kitchen — and the kitchen was longer than it should be. He measured it without meaning to, the way he measured everything now, the way the cellar had taught him to measure: twelve feet to the far wall when he'd arrived. The beam traveled twenty before it found the cabinets.

"They've moved," he said. "Or we have. Doesn't matter. Stay together, follow my voice."

They followed his voice. That was the whole art of it, he thought, sweeping the light along walls that breathed faintly, that swelled and settled like the flanks of something sleeping. For thirty-one years he had made himself unnoticeable, a man shaped like a gap in a crowd, and it had worked everywhere except here, where the house had decided he existed, had learned his name, had begun saying it gently into recorders at night like an invitation to dinner. Fine. If he was going to be visible, he would be visible usefully. He would be the loudest thing in the dark.

"Kitchen door," he announced. "We try it anyway. A lie repeated often enough starts believing itself — that goes for houses too."

The door opened onto the cellar stair again. Of course it did. But this time the stair was different: longer, the steps narrower, and from below came a sound that stopped Wren's father dead — voices. Many voices, layered, murmuring, and threaded through them, unmistakable, the recordings. All of them at once. Three years of Paloma's overnight tapes compressed into a single choir: his own voice asking questions no one had asked aloud, the answering voice tender and patient, and underneath both, rising now like water coming up through floorboards, other voices. A woman's. A child's. An old man counting in a language nobody in the family spoke.

And then, clear as a bell rung beside his ear, his own name. Not Ravensworth. The other one. The buried one. Spoken the way you speak a name to wake someone gently.

"Back," Paloma said. "Everyone back. Now."

They retreated into the kitchen and he shut the door and put his back against it, and the wood behind him was warm. Warm like skin. He chose not to think about that.

"Windows," Wren said. She was already moving, dragging a chair to the sink, and God, he loved her — he had been loving her for weeks in the careful wordless way of a man who expected to be erased, and now, watching her climb toward the glass with a suitcase strap clenched in her teeth, he let himself feel it at full strength, because tonight might be the last night strength was allowed.

The window wouldn't open. The latch turned; the frame didn't move. It was as if the window had grown shut, years of paint and weather compressed into a single night. Wren hit the glass with her elbow. The glass flexed — bowed inward, actually bowed, like a membrane — and then held.

Outside the window, where the yard should have been, there was the cellar. Seen from above. Steps descending into the dark, and at the bottom, pressed into the flour-dusted floor, five-fingered prints, dozens of them, hundreds, all facing upward. All waving, almost. All welcoming.

"Wren," Paloma said. "Come down. Please come down."

She came down. She was shaking. He took the torch from her hand because hers had gone slick, and in the beam he saw her face do the thing he dreaded most: stop being afraid and start being certain. Fear screamed. Certainty went quiet.

"It's herding us," she said. "Every exit is the same exit. It wants us downstairs."

"Yes."

"Then we don't go downstairs."

"There's no upstairs left," Tam said, forwards, suddenly, shockingly, in his ordinary voice, and they all turned. He was standing in the kitchen doorway pointing at the hall — and the hall was wrong too, the stairs to the upper floors gone, replaced by flat wall, and the ceiling lower than it had been, and the ceiling beyond that lower still, the whole house settling downward like a lung exhaling, room by room, hour by hour, compressing them toward the one place it had been keeping warm.

Paloma looked at his watch. 11:58.

Two minutes to midnight, and the plan had been: the front door, the gravel, the field, the road. The plan was dead. The plan had been dead since the hallway ate Tam's door. What remained was not a plan. What remained was the thing he had been carrying in his chest for days now like a swallowed coal, the trade, the offer, the last blank line in the ledger waiting for ink.

Not yet. Not while they could still fight. He owed them every exit first, even the fake ones. Especially the fake ones.

"Cellar's the only way it will give us," he said aloud, and hated himself for saying it, because it was true and truth was the house's currency. "But it thinks we don't know the rules. It thinks we'll go down begging. So we go down on our own terms, all four of us together, and we don't stop walking, and whatever it shows us—" his voice caught, steadied, "—whatever it shows us, we keep moving toward the outside it's hiding somewhere in its pocket. Houses keep doors. Even mouths have teeth that open."

Nobody believed him. He could see that. He barely believed himself. But Wren picked up her father's suitcase, and Tam picked up the ledger when she gestured, and her father put his hand on the kitchen door handle as if it were the handle of a plow, something to hold while the ground did its worst.

"One more thing," Paloma said. He dug into his bag and brought out the tape recorder, the old one, the faithful one, and set it on the counter, and pressed record. "If we don't make it out, this keeps going. Whatever happens down there, this hears it. And someday someone plays it back, and someone knows."

The red light glowed. Somewhere below, faintly, the counting paused — he felt it pause, the way you feel a listener lean closer — and then resumed, patient, affectionate, almost done.

*...nine,* said the house, in a voice like flour sifting. *...ten.*

There had never been a ten before. The list ended at nine. Wren's hand found his in the dark and gripped it hard enough to hurt, and Paloma understood, with the cold clarity that had been his gift and his curse all his life, that the count had added a number.

It had counted him.

"Midnight," he said. "Let's go down and be disagreeable."

* * *

*Wren*

The cellar stair was forty steps. She counted them because counting was the only weapon she had that the house hadn't already stolen. At step twelve the air went cold and floury. At step nineteen the walls began, very faintly, to breathe. At step twenty-six her father's grip on her arm went slack and she had to haul him onward, whispering *not yet, Dad, not yet, we're nearly at the part where we win,* which was a lie, and the house knew it was a lie, and told her so in her mother's voice, kindly, from just behind her left shoulder: *You were always a bad liar, love. You get it from me.*

At the bottom, the cellar was full.

Not of the entity. Not of anything she could point to. Full of people the way a church is full of people — a presence made of presence, a congregation standing in the dark in rows she couldn't see but could feel, hundreds deep, everyone the ledger had ever counted, everyone who had ever acknowledged it and been acknowledged back. The flour on the floor was churned with prints, five fingers each, slightly too long, and the prints were fresh, and they were all facing the stair.

Facing them. Facing her.

The recorder in her bag — she'd insisted on carrying a second one, Paloma's spare — clicked itself on. Neither of them had touched it. From its little speaker, in the hush, came a voice she knew better than any voice left in the world, and it said:

"Is the counter still coming?"

And the congregation, in a hundred borrowed voices, in her grandmother's voice, in the neighbor-who-never-was's voice, in a voice that might once have belonged to a sister Wren alone remembered, answered with terrible gentleness:

"The counter is here."

Paloma stepped forward. Past her. In front of her father, in front of Tam, in front of her — putting his body between her family and the dark, this man built out of gaps, this invisible man, choosing with his whole visible trembling self to be seen.

"Then count me," he said.

The house went silent. Utterly, hungrily silent — the silence of a held breath four generations long. On the floor, in the flour, the prints began to turn. All of them. Slowly, like flowers finding the sun, like faces at a wedding turning toward the door.

Wren grabbed his sleeve. "Paloma—"

"Not yet," he said, without turning around, and his voice was steady and his hand was shaking and both of those things were true at once, the way everything in Harrow Glen was finally, horribly true at once. "Whatever happens next — whatever you hear me say, whatever name comes out of my mouth — remember the one you already know. Promise me. Promise me that's the one you keep."

The cellar door, behind them, at the top of the stairs, swung slowly shut.

And the darkness leaned in to listen.
