## 20. Morning, Empty House

The light came in wrong.

Wren noticed it before she noticed anything else—that the morning through the kitchen window was ordinary gold, the kind of light that belonged to other houses, other lives, and that it lay across the counter without a single shadow out of place. For four days the house had fought them: doors that migrated, stairs that lengthened by three steps and then five, the recordings playing everyone's voices at once until Tam had pulled the wires out with his bare hands and stood there shaking, holding the tangled cords like something he'd strangled. But now it was morning, and the house was only a house.

That was the cruelest part. It could have looked haunted at the end. It could have given them something to hate.

Instead it gave them a clean kitchen, a ticking clock, a kettle on the stove that someone—she couldn't remember who—had put on to boil.

Her father stood at the back door with his hand on the latch. He hadn't left the house in four years; Wren had built her whole understanding of the world around the fact of his staying, the way you build a fence around a well. Now he was dressed in his good coat, the grey one with the missing button, and he was looking at the garden as if he'd never seen it before—as if, she thought, he hadn't. As if the house had been editing more than memories all these years, editing even the view from its own windows, keeping him looking at a garden that was slightly wrong, slightly dimmed, slightly *theirs* instead of his.

"Dad," she said.

He didn't turn around. "Your mother liked the roses," he said. "Didn't she?"

And there it was—the first test, the first small cruelty of the morning. Wren reached for her mother's face and found it soft-edged, like a photograph left too long in the sun. She knew the facts. Brown hair going grey at the temples. A laugh that came out sideways. Hands that smelled of flour because she baked when she was worried, which was often, toward the end. She knew all of it the way you know a stranger's biography. The face itself was gone, smoothed away sometime in the night while the ledger line struck itself through, and what remained was a shape where a woman had been, warm and beloved and unseeable.

"She did," Wren said. "She loved the roses."

It wasn't a lie. It was the truest thing she had left.

Tam sat at the table with the ledger open in front of him, reading it upside down, which was how he read everything now. His backwards speech had faded since the descent—since Paloma had walked down those stairs and spoken a name Wren still didn't know, a name that had made the counting stop mid-number, mid-*breath*, the whole house going silent as a held note finally released. Tam spoke forwards again. But sometimes, in the quiet, his lips still moved the old way, rehearsing syllables in reverse, as if part of him had learned the house's language and couldn't entirely unlearn it.

"Wren." He turned the ledger toward her. "Look."

She looked. She had looked a hundred times already, but she looked again, because he needed her to, and because some part of her kept hoping the ink would rearrange itself into mercy.

Her grandmother's handwriting marched down the page, name after name after name, each one crossed out on the day they moved away, each one dead inside these walls before they ever packed a box. Her mother's line: struck through, the ink still faintly wet-looking no matter how many hours passed. Below it, Tam. Below that, her father. And below that, the blank line—

The blank line was still blank. That was the thing she couldn't stop touching, running her thumb across the empty space the way you'd touch a scar. It should have held a name now. It should have held *his*.

Paloma Ravensworth. Pest controller. Liar about small things, honest about large ones. The man who had come to their house because the caller knew his unlisted number, and stayed because—she understood this only now, only in the flat morning light—he had heard the same noises once, in a flat of his own, three years ago, and had spent every day since explaining them away for strangers because explaining them away was the only way he survived knowing they were real.

He had lied to her gently, one last time, standing at the top of the cellar stairs with the door open behind him and the dark breathing up out of it like a tide.

"Go out through the front," he'd said. "I'll follow. I just need to check the traps."

He was a pest controller. Checking the traps was the truest sentence he owned. And she had believed him—or she had let herself believe him, which in that moment amounted to the same thing—and she had taken her father's arm and her brother's sleeve and she had walked toward the front door, and the front door had opened onto daylight, actual daylight, the porch, the gravel drive, the world, because somewhere below them a man was giving the house the only thing it had ever wanted.

A name, freely offered, in place of a name owed.

"His line isn't written," Tam said. He tapped the blank space. "He said names get written when the house decides you exist. He existed. Why isn't he written?"

Because the house doesn't write gifts, Wren thought. Because he wasn't counted. Because he was *given*. She didn't say any of it. She closed the ledger instead, carefully, the way you close a book over a pressed flower, and felt the weight of it settle against her ribs where she'd carry it, apparently, for the rest of her life.

* * *

They left at nine minutes past nine, because Tam insisted on nine, because nine was a number the house hadn't used, or because he simply wanted to choose one thing, and after everything, choosing a departure time seemed a small enough sovereignty to grant him.

Wren went through the rooms one last time. Not to pack—they were taking almost nothing, two suitcases and the ledger and her mother's ring, which she'd found on the kitchen counter beside the kettle, placed there neatly, waiting, though her mother had been below the floor for two days. She went through the rooms to say goodbye to the versions of herself the house had edited, the birthdays she half-remembered, the sister whose name lived only in crossed-out ink and in Wren's own unreliable hand. She stood in the hallway where the cellar door was and found it shut, and locked, and ordinary, a door like any other door, and she hated it for being ordinary, and she put her palm flat against the wood anyway.

"I'm taking the book," she told it. "You don't get the book."

The door said nothing. Doors don't. But under her palm, faint as a pulse through a wrist, she felt—or imagined, and would spend years deciding whether she'd felt or imagined—a single knock. Polite. Twice. Then nothing.

The same knock he'd described to her, on the night they'd kept vigil together above the cellar door, sharing stories to stay awake. Her childhood, told in pieces. His childhood, told in inventions so smooth she hadn't caught the seams until later, until he'd confessed that *Ravensworth* was a coat he wore, that underneath it was a name he'd buried so deep even he flinched reaching for it. She had fallen asleep against his shoulder for eleven minutes, she knew it was eleven because he'd told her, and when she woke he'd been listening to the silence with his head tilted like a man hearing music, and he'd said, softly, "It's learning my voice," and she had thought then, absurdly, *good. Let it learn something kind.*

Now the house had learned his voice, and given hers back to her, and kept him.

She took her hand off the door.

* * *

The gravel crunched under their feet like the first bite of an apple. Her father stopped halfway down the drive and turned around, and Wren turned with him, and they looked at Harrow Glen together.

It was simply a house. That was the truth of it, and the truth was worse than any monster. Sagging gutters, ivy pulling at the east wall, windows hazed with grime, a chimney leaning like a tired man against the sky. Peeling paint. A roof that needed work. The kind of house you'd pass on a country road and think, *someone ought to do something with that place,* and then forget by the next bend. All those years of dread, all those crossed-out names, all that careful not-looking-up—and the house's final form, stripped of its counting, was just decay. Just neglect. Just a building that had learned to be hungry because no one had ever taught it anything else.

"It looks smaller," her father said.

"It is smaller," Tam said. "It was always bigger on the inside. Like grief."

Their father looked at his son for a long moment—Tam, who had not spoken a full forward sentence in two years until this week—and then he laughed. It came out cracked and rusted, a laugh from storage, but it was a laugh, and Wren felt it move through her like water through dry ground. He put one hand on Tam's shoulder and one on hers, and the three of them stood in the gravel in the ordinary gold light, a family reduced and remade, missing a mother whose face none of them could fully summon and missing a man whose face they could.

That was the thing. That was the thing she kept returning to, turning over like a stone with something alive beneath it.

The house had taken her mother's face. Had sanded it smooth out of every memory, left only facts where a woman had been. But Paloma's face—long-nosed, watchful, always slightly amused at the world's expense, with the particular stillness of a man who had made a profession out of not being noticed—his face was intact. Perfect. Every line of it. She could close her eyes and see the exact way his eyebrows rose when Tam spoke backwards, the way his mouth pressed flat to keep from smiling when she caught him lying about the flour prints, the look on his face at the top of the cellar stairs, which had not been fear. She had expected fear. What she'd seen was tenderness, aimed at her like a lamp.

The house could edit anything except what it was given freely. Her mother had walked down the stairs and been taken, and so her face was the house's now, filed away among the counted. Paloma had spoken his buried name aloud and been *given*, and a gift, apparently, worked both directions. He had handed the cellar his name, and in exchange something of him had been pressed into her—into her memory, her keeping, her ledger of one—safe from editing, permanent as ink.

He had bought their freedom and paid the fee in himself, and the change he'd gotten back was a face in her head that would never fade.

She didn't know whether that was mercy or the last joke of a very cruel house. She suspected she'd spend her life deciding.

"Come on," her father said. "The car."

They walked. At the gate Tam stopped and looked back once, and Wren watched him mouth something—syllables moving in reverse, a word folded backwards like a letter—and then he turned and didn't look again, and she understood that he was saying goodbye in the only language the house might still be listening to, so that whatever was below would hear it properly.

She didn't ask what he'd said. Some translations you're not meant to have.

* * *

The car started on the second try. Their father drove the way he used to, before the four years, hands at ten and two, checking mirrors, and Wren sat in the back with the ledger on her knees and watched Harrow Glen shrink in the rear window.

At the bend in the road, where the hedge broke and the house showed its whole grey face one last time, she saw a figure in the upstairs window. Small. Still. Standing in the frame of what had been her grandmother's room, watching them go.

She didn't tell her father. She didn't tell Tam. But she didn't look away either, and she lifted her hand—not waving, exactly. Acknowledging. The way you acknowledge someone across a station platform when the train is pulling out and there's nothing left to say that wouldn't break you open.

The figure raised its hand.

Then the hedge swallowed the house, and the road unspooled toward town, toward other houses with ordinary shadows, toward a life with a hole in it shaped like a mother and a second hole shaped like a man named after a bird, and Wren opened the ledger on her knees.

She found the blank line. She uncapped her pen.

She did not cross anything out. Instead, beneath the empty space where his name should have been, she wrote, in her own hand, small and deliberate: *Given, not counted. Owed, not forgotten.* And below that, because the pen was moving and she couldn't stop it and didn't want to: *I will come back.*

Tam twisted around in the front seat and watched her write. "That's not how ledgers work," he said.

"This one's mine now," Wren said. "I make the rules."

He considered that with the grave attention he gave everything lately, the attention of someone who had briefly spoken with the other side and come back with his pockets full of its grammar. Then he nodded, slowly, and faced forward, and after a mile he began to hum—low, absent, a tune Wren recognized with a cold, sweet shock: the melody from the recordings, the one the neighbor who had never existed used to hum through the wall. But Tam hummed it forwards. Straightened out. Turned from a summons into a song.

Outside, the morning went on being ordinary. Fields, fences, a church spire, a sign for a village she'd never visited. Behind them, Harrow Glen sat empty in its hollow, simply a house, simply waiting, and Wren pressed her hand flat against the ledger and felt the weight of the one face the house couldn't touch, and grieved, and was grateful, and did both at once, because that was what surviving cost, and she intended to pay it daily.

That night, in a rented room in town, she would set the ledger on the nightstand, and whisper a chosen name into the dark—"Paloma"—just to see if the air would hold it.

And somewhere far below a decaying house, in a cellar dusted with flour and fingerprints, something would stir at the sound, and answer.
