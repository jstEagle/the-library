## 5. Wren

The knock came at nine in the morning, and it was not a knock so much as an argument conducted through wood.

Three blows, hard enough to rattle the stained glass in the front door. I'd been cataloguing Edith's kitchen — third drawer down, forty years of string and rubber bands and one brass whistle that made my teeth ache to look at — and I stood there with a drawer in my hands, absurdly, as if I might need to defend myself with elastic.

Through the window over the sink I saw her: a woman maybe my age, maybe younger, standing on the gravel with her arms crossed like she was holding the door shut from outside. Dark hair cut short and practical, a waxed jacket gone soft at the elbows, boots planted the way you plant them when you've decided not to be moved. She looked, I remember thinking, like weather about to make up its mind.

I opened the door before the fourth blow.

"You're taking them out of the village." Not a question. Her voice was low and even, the kind of calm that costs something. "The tapes. You've got them boxed up in there. Mrs. Petch told me you loaded six cartons into your car on Tuesday."

"I'm cataloguing them," I said. "For the archive. Everything stays here until—"

"Until what?"

"Until they're processed."

"Processed." She said the word the way you'd spit out a stone. "They're not fish."

I set the drawer down on the hall table — slowly, because I'd learned already that this house punished sudden movement — and looked at her properly. Up close she had a small white scar through one eyebrow and eyes the flat grey of the river in February. She didn't blink much. Later I would understand why. That morning I just thought: hostile.

"And you are?"

"Wren Halloran." She said it like a place name. Like somewhere I might have been warned about. "My grandmother was Mary Halloran. She lived at the end of Chapel Row until 1974, when your aunt recorded her singing and my grandmother walked out of her own kitchen and never came home. So when I ask what you're doing with those tapes, Mr. Nystrom, I'm not making conversation."

Ivo, I almost corrected her. Just Ivo. But something in her face stopped me — the way she'd loaded *your aunt* with all the weight of a charge sheet.

"Edith Nystrom," I said instead. "She died in March. I'm her—" I hesitated over the word. Niece? Nephew? The word that fit the paperwork was *heir*, and heir felt too much like a word for vultures. "I'm family. Sort of. We weren't close."

"No," Wren said. "Nobody was close to Edith Nystrom. That was rather the point of her."

It shouldn't have stung. I'd thought worse things about Edith myself, alone, at three in the morning, holding a tape up to the light like it might confess. But hearing it from a stranger — hearing it delivered with the flat certainty of local knowledge — stung anyway.

"Look," I said. "I know how this looks. Outsider sweeps in, boxes up the village's heritage, drives off with it. But I'm not selling anything. I'm a folklorist. This is literally my job. These recordings are—"

"Yours now. Yes. I read the obituary." She unfolded her arms, and I realized the crossing of them hadn't been aggression. It had been restraint. "I want to hear tape fourteen."

The number landed in me like a dropped glass.

There were forty tapes, unlabeled except for dates scratched into the plastic in Edith's narrow hand. I'd played perhaps a dozen, working through them in order, and tape fourteen was the one I'd stopped on twice — the one where a woman's voice sang eight bars of something about a green door and a low tide, and then stopped mid-word, and Edith's voice came in shaky and young: *"Again. From the top, Mary. Slower."* And the woman — Mary — laughing once, high and wrong, and saying, in a voice I had not been able to stop hearing for three days:

*"You don't want me to sing it twice, love."*

"How do you know the number?" I asked.

"Because I've spent eleven years asking everyone in this village what happened to my grandmother, and the only answer anyone will give me is a number." Wren's jaw tightened. "Old Tam told me, before he stopped talking altogether. Tape fourteen. He said if I ever found it, I should listen to it once and then burn it. He said—" she paused, and for the first time her voice lost its levelness, dipped toward something rawer, "—he said some songs are doors, and doors only open one way, once."

We stood in the doorway while the house breathed behind me. I'd started thinking of it that way — breathing — around the time the mirrors began their lag, though I wasn't ready to say that part out loud to anyone, least of all to a Halloran.

"Come inside," I said.

"I'd rather not."

"It's raining."

"It's drizzle."

"It's coming sideways." I stepped back from the door, leaving the way open, which felt like either hospitality or surrender. "You can stand in the hall and accuse me from under a roof. I'll make tea."

She looked past me into the house for a long moment — and I watched something move across her face that I couldn't name then but would learn to recognize: recognition. The look of someone seeing a room they've seen in dreams.

"The hall mirror," she said quietly. "Is it still there?"

"The big one? Yes. Why?"

"Nothing." But she came inside, wiping her boots twice on the mat, thorough, ritualistic, and stood in the hall with her back to the mirror as if it owed her money.

* * *

I made tea in Edith's kitchen, in Edith's cups, and Wren sat at Edith's table with her hands wrapped around the mug without drinking, and we performed the opening moves of two people deciding whether to be enemies.

She asked where I'd grown up. I said a city she'd heard of. She asked how long Edith had lived here. I said since before I was born; that my mother never spoke of her; that I'd met her four times in my life, and each time she'd looked at me like she was checking a sum. She asked what I remembered of those meetings.

"Tea," I said. "And once, a piano recital I was made to play for her. I was nine. She didn't clap. She just listened with her head tilted, like she was counting something, and afterward she gave me five pounds and said, 'You keep good time.' I thought it was a compliment for years."

Wren looked at me across the table, and something in her hostility shifted gear — not softened, exactly. Recalibrated.

"She said that to people," Wren said. "Keep good time. My gran used to say Edith had a way of listening that made your skin crawl. Like she wasn't hearing the song. She was hearing underneath it."

"That's called analysis."

"That's called *listening underneath*." Wren set her mug down. "Fine. Here's my position, and then you can decide whether I'm mad and throw me out. In 1974, Edith Nystrom went round this village with a reel-to-reel recorder collecting songs. Everyone sang for her once. My grandmother sang for her, and then my grandmother disappeared, and no one ever sang any of those songs again — not in church, not in the pub, not at weddings. Forty years, and this village has a whole repertoire it doesn't sing. You're a folklorist. Tell me that isn't strange."

"It's unusual," I admitted. "Repertoires die all the time."

"They don't die *in unison*. They don't die with everyone going grey-faced when you hum two bars of them." She leaned forward. "I've tried. At the wake for old Mrs. Petch's husband, I hummed the green-door one, the one from tape fourteen, just a few notes, to see who'd join in. Three people left the room. One of them cried and wouldn't say why. And Mrs. Petch grabbed my wrist hard enough to bruise and said, *'Not twice, Wren. Never twice.'*"

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere upstairs, in the closed bedroom, I could hear — or imagined I could hear — the reel-to-reel sitting in its case, patient as a held breath.

"Why never twice?" I asked. "What do they think happens?"

"I don't know. That's the thing. Nobody knows, or nobody remembers, or the knowing has been scooped out of them like flesh from a fruit. All that's left is the rule." She turned the mug in her hands. "Except I don't think it's a rule they invented. I think it's a rule they were given."

"By whom?"

"By whatever Edith woke up." She said it plainly, watching my face, and I understood she'd been waiting years to say it to someone and had rehearsed this exact moment in her head a thousand times, and none of the rehearsals had helped.

I should have laughed. A reasonable person would have laughed, or offered the standard explanations — mass psychogenic illness, folk memory of some old tragedy, the village's grief congealed into taboo. I opened my mouth to offer them.

What came out instead was: "How long have you heard it?"

Her head came up sharply. "Heard what?"

"The lag." My mouth kept going, dry, committed. "In mirrors. Things happening a beat after they happen. Or — no. The reflection happening a beat after you. As if the glass is catching up."

The silence went on long enough that I counted the kitchen clock ticking through eleven seconds. Then Wren said, very carefully, "Since Tuesday."

"Tuesday," I repeated. My voice cracked on the second syllable.

"When you loaded the boxes into your car." She wasn't looking at me anymore. She was looking at the dark rectangle of the hallway beyond the kitchen door, where the big mirror hung. "Before that it was — flickers. Years of flickers. A reflection that finished blinking when I'd already stopped. I put it down to tiredness. Migraines. Then Tuesday night I walked past the chapel and every window showed me the road empty while I was still standing on it. And yesterday my bathroom mirror ran a full second behind, and I stood there and timed it against my watch, and I thought: it started when *she* arrived. When the tapes moved."

"Tapes don't do anything," I said. It sounded feeble even to me. "They're magnetic oxide on polyester. They're inert."

"So am I, mostly," Wren said. "And yet here we both are, being lagged at." She finally drank her tea, grimaced at it — Edith's tea was strong enough to trot a horse across — and pushed the mug away. "Show me tape fourteen."

"Why?"

"Because I've earned it. Because it's half mine. Because—" and here her voice did the thing again, the dip below the level, the place where the person under the armor showed through, "—because that's my grandmother's voice on it, and I was two years old when she vanished, and I don't remember her, and the only thing in the world that remembers her is sitting in a cardboard box in a dead woman's house. So yes. Show me."

I should have refused. Every instinct Edith's notebooks had been teaching me — *it keeps the time it is owed*, the margins crawling with that phrase by the end — said that these tapes were not archives but accounts, and accounts were meant to stay balanced and shut.

But she was looking at me with her grandmother's absence in her face, and I was so tired of being alone with it, and there was a selfish arithmetic underneath everything: if she heard the lag too, then I wasn't mad. Madness is bearable alone. Company makes it real, and reality, however terrible, can be acted upon.

"Upstairs," I said. "But we play it once. Only once. And then it goes back in the box."

"Once," Wren agreed, and something crossed her face — relief, or fear, or both wearing each other's clothes.

* * *

The spare bedroom had become the archive: folding table, cotton gloves, the reel-to-reel squatting on a towel like a black cat that tolerated us. I threaded tape fourteen while Wren stood by the window with her arms folded again, and I noticed she positioned herself so the wardrobe mirror was behind her, out of her sightline, and I didn't comment, because I'd been doing the same thing for days.

The tape hissed. Then Edith's voice, young, thirty at most, bright with the particular greed of a collector: *"Fourteenth of June, 1974. Mary Halloran, Chapel Row. Traditional, source unknown — she says her mother had it from the chapel. Go ahead when you're ready, Mary."*

A pause with rain in it. Then Mary Halloran sang.

Eight bars. Green door, low tide, a lover gone under the hill, come back when the water's counted. Her voice was warm and slightly smoky and absolutely certain, the voice of someone who has sung this a hundred times and been paid in nothing but the singing. Wren made a small sound beside me, one hand rising halfway to her mouth, and I reached over without thinking and gripped her wrist, and she let me, and neither of us looked away from the machine.

The song ended. Or — it ended. There was a silence, and in the silence Edith's voice, softer now, hungry: *"Beautiful. Again, from the top. Slower — I want the ornamentation clean."*

And Mary Halloran laughed — that high, wrong laugh — and said: *"You don't want me to sing it twice, love."*

And then the tape did something tapes do not do.

It kept running. No voices. Just the hiss, deepening, thickening, until the hiss itself seemed to acquire weight and rhythm — and underneath it, so far down I felt it in my sternum more than heard it, something began to count. Slow. Patient. Enormous. Numbers without language, tolling like a bell underwater.

Beside me, Wren whispered, "Turn it off."

I couldn't. My hand wouldn't. The counting rose, one — the sense of one, vast and satisfied — and in the wardrobe mirror, in the corner of my eye, the room reflected a half-second late, and in that half-second the reflection held two figures standing by the machine, and we were only two.

I slammed the stop button with the heel of my hand. The reels spun down. The hiss died. The counting — cut off, not finished, *cut off* — left a silence shaped exactly like itself.

Wren had both hands pressed flat on the windowsill, breathing hard. Outside, the drizzle had stopped, and the light through the glass lay on the floor in a long pale bar, and I watched that bar of light arrive on the floorboards a full second after the sun cleared the cloud — watched it, timed it, knew it — and when I looked at Wren she was looking at the same patch of light, and her face told me she'd seen it too.

"It's not the tapes," she said hoarsely. "Or it's not only the tapes. It started before you played anything. It started when you *came*." She turned to me, and up close her grey eyes had gold flecks in them, and terror had burned off the hostility entirely, leaving something better underneath: alliance. "Whatever my grandmother's voice opened in 1974 — it's been waiting for someone to finish Edith's work. And I think it thinks that's you."

"Then we give it back," I heard myself say. "Whatever she took. Whatever's owed. We find out what Edith did, and we return it, and this ends."

Wren stared at me. Then, slowly, she laughed — a real laugh this time, short and disbelieving, the first honest sound I'd heard from her.

"You've known this house a week."

"I've known the lag a week," I said. "That's longer than most people get."

She looked at the machine, at the box of forty tapes, at the window where the light still lay a beat behind the sky. When she spoke again, her voice had gone quiet and steady, the level restored, but aimed now at something shared.

"My gran's handwriting is on the back of the chapel door," she said. "Everyone says it's graffiti. Scratched letters, under the paint. I've read it a hundred times and never understood it. But I'm starting to." She pulled her jacket straight, soldiering herself back together. "It says: *the cellar keeps what the singer owes.*"

Outside, far off, the chapel bell — rusted, ropeless, forty years silent — rang once.
