## 10. Playback

I didn't sleep. That was the first mistake, though I wouldn't understand it as a mistake until much later, when understanding had stopped being a thing I could afford.

The house at three in the morning has a particular quality of attention. Not silence — silence is passive, an absence. This was the opposite. The house listened. Floorboards held their creaks like breath. The pipes ticked in the walls with what I could only describe as patience, and every mirror I passed on my way down to the kitchen reflected me a half-step behind, so that I kept catching glimpses of myself arriving after I'd already arrived.

I made tea I didn't drink. I set Edith's reel-to-reel on the kitchen table, and beside it the tape labeled 1974 — the one with the child's voice running backwards through the cellar song, syllables unspooling like thread pulled from a hem. I'd played it twice before, both times in daylight, both times stopping it early because something in me flinched. Tonight there was no daylight to hide behind, and no flinch left either. Whatever was happening to this house, to the mirrors, to the one-second gap that had opened between me and my own reflection — I needed to know if it started here. If Edith had left me not just a debt but its terms.

The recorder's reels turned with a soft plastic whisper. I'd cleaned the heads that afternoon with isopropyl and a cotton swab, following a YouTube video filmed by a man in Ohio who restored tape machines for people who missed their fathers. The machine hummed. The tape hissed. And then the child began to sing.

Backwards, still. *Ees eht ni ecalp si hcihw gnol eht* — no, not even that; backwards song isn't phonetic reversal you can parse, it's melody inverted, a tune walking downstairs instead of up. The child's voice was thin and clear and utterly unafraid, which frightened me more than fear would have. Children know when to be afraid. This one didn't. This one sang like someone reading from a page they trusted.

I let it run.

Two minutes. Three. My tea went cold beside the machine and the kitchen window showed me nothing but my own face floating in the dark glass, delayed by a second, mouthing words a moment after I thought them. I watched myself watch myself. Somewhere behind that second of lag, I understood, was where I actually lived now.

Then, at four minutes and eleven seconds — I checked the counter afterward, again and again, because I needed the number to be wrong — the child's voice dropped away, and beneath the hiss, something else came up.

It had been there the whole time. That's what I realized, sitting very still in Edith's kitchen while the tape ran on: it had been underneath from the first second, so low and so slow that my ears had filed it under machinery, under blood, under the sound a house makes when it settles. It wasn't any of those things. It was a voice. And it was counting.

Not numbers. I want to be precise about this, because precision was all I had left by then. It wasn't *one, two, three*. It was older than numbering. It was the feeling of counting — the cadence of tally, the rhythm of a thing being measured out in units, patient as sediment. Each pulse landed like a stone dropped into deep water, and between the pulses there was room enough for a whole life to happen, and the pulses did not care. They were not hurrying. Nothing about them hurried. They were the opposite of hurry. They were the sound of a debt accruing interest in a currency I couldn't see.

Edith must have heard this. Edith must have sat where I was sitting, fifty years ago or five years ago, listening to exactly this, and she hadn't destroyed the tape. She'd labeled it. She'd wound it carefully into its box and shelved it with the other thirty-nine, and she had left them to me, and I understood suddenly that this was not inheritance. It was handoff. A relay runner pressing the baton into the hand of someone who hasn't agreed to run.

The counting swelled — not louder, exactly. Closer. As if the speaker were ascending a long staircase toward the microphone, and each step took a year.

And then, threaded through it, faint as writing on the back of a page, I heard the answering part. The cellar song has two voices. Of course it does. Every call needs a response; that's how work songs function, how lullabies function, how bargains are sealed. The child had been singing one half. This — this vast, unhurried, subterranean patience — was singing the other. It had been singing along the entire time, waiting for someone to play the tape long enough, late enough, alone enough, to hear it.

I reached for the stop button.

My reflection reached first.

I saw it happen in the dark window — my own arm moving a full second ahead of mine, my hand hitting the button before my hand decided to hit it, the reels spinning down while my fingers were still traveling through the air toward them. The kitchen went quiet. The tape hissed into silence. And in the window, my reflection stood with its arm already lowered, looking at me — not mirroring me, not lagging me, but *looking*, with the particular stillness of something that has finished pretending.

Every mirror in the house did the same thing at once. I felt it rather than heard it, a pressure change, a shift in the way the dark lay against the walls. I stood up. My chair scraped. In the hallway mirror, visible from the kitchen doorway, I did not stand up. I was already standing. I had been standing for a second longer than I had been standing, and the difference between us was no longer a delay. It was a lead.

* * *

I walked the house because walking was the only sane response available. Rationalization is a muscle and I worked it hard: adrenaline, sleep deprivation, pareidolia, the well-documented human talent for finding faces in noise. I said these words aloud, actually, pacing the upstairs landing, naming my symptoms like a doctor ruling out diseases. My voice bounced off the bathroom mirror and came back a beat late, and in that beat I heard it arrive slightly changed — flattened, as if it had passed through water on the way.

The bathroom mirror showed the landing. Empty. Except for me — except for the figure standing where I stood, which was me, except that it wasn't behaving like a reflection anymore. When I raised my right hand, it raised its right hand. Reflections do that. But it did it *on its own time*, and there was a moment — half a heartbeat, less — where our hands were both raised and neither of us was copying the other, and we were simply two figures in two rooms, regarding each other across a silvered inch of glass.

I backed away. Downstairs, the tall mirror in the front hall — Edith's good Victorian mirror, foxed at the edges like old paper — showed the hall without me in it. Just the hall. The coat hooks. The door. Then, a second later, me, arriving into my own picture like a man stepping into a photograph taken before he got there.

That was the pattern, I finally understood, standing in the hall with my heart going like a fist on a locked door. Every mirror in the house showed the same room, the same night — but empty. Stripped of me. And then I would enter the frame a second behind, taking up a position that had been held for me, waiting, the way a place is laid at a table.

They weren't showing me a delay. They were showing me a vacancy being kept.

* * *

I called Wren at four-something in the morning, which I knew was unforgivable, and she answered on the second ring, which told me she hadn't been sleeping either.

"It's playing," I said. "The 1974 tape. All the way through."

Silence on the line. Then, quietly: "Did you hear it count?"

The question went through me like cold water through cloth. "You've heard it."

"My gran's sister had a copy of a copy. Half the village did, back then, before Edith collected them." A pause. "Nobody plays them past four minutes. There's a reason the songs get sung once, Ivo. Once is a greeting. Twice is an answer."

"I think it answered anyway."

"Where are you now?"

"The hall."

"Don't look at the mirrors."

Too late. I was looking. In Edith's foxed Victorian glass, the hall stood empty and moonlit, and in the middle of it, where I stood holding the phone, there was a figure — not me, not quite, taller in the way that a shadow is taller than the thing casting it, its outline soft, unfinished, like a photograph of a person developed before the person finished arriving. It stood where I stood. It occupied my position with the ease of long tenancy. And as I watched, it raised one hand — slowly, deliberately, not mimicking anything — and pressed its palm flat against the inside of the glass.

On my side, the mirror was cool and dry. On its side, I swear to God, the surface rippled, rings spreading outward from where the palm pressed, like rain on a pond seen from below.

"Ivo?" Wren's voice, far away. "What do you see?"

I described it. My voice sounded reasonable. That was the horror of it — I could narrate my own undoing in the calm tone of a folklorist citing sources, and some detached part of me was already composing the field note: *informant reports figure in mirror, position identical to subject's, duration of observation—*

"That's how it starts," Wren said. "That's what the margins meant. *It keeps the time it is owed.* You've been paying it since you got here, Ivo. Every second of lag — that's not a delay. That's a withdrawal. It's been taking you a second at a time, and now—" her breath caught, "—now there's enough of you in there to fill the frame."

The figure in the mirror tilted its head. The gesture was almost tender. Almost curious. It was the gesture of someone recognizing a face across a crowded room — or checking a name against a ledger.

"Get out of the house," Wren said.

"And go where? It counted me into existence somewhere between here and your kitchen, Wren. I don't think geography is the problem."

"Then don't be alone with it. I'm coming. Don't play anything else. Don't sing anything. And whatever you do—" her voice dropped, urgent, close, "—don't answer it. If it counts, don't count back. Promise me."

I promised. I hung up. And in the silence after, in the hall of the house I'd inherited along with everything owed on it, the figure in the mirror lowered its palm from the glass and stood waiting, patient as sediment, wearing my shape the way a creditor wears a signature.

Behind it, faintly, from everywhere and nowhere — from the mirrors, from the pipes, from the tape machine in the kitchen whose reels I had stopped but which I could somehow still hear turning — the counting went on.

And I understood, with the last of my rational mind, why the number it had reached felt familiar.

It wasn't counting up.

It was counting down.
