## 3. The Bell House

The taxi would go no further than the gate.

Wren understood why once he stepped out into the rain and looked up the drive. It wasn't a road so much as a suggestion — gravel gone soft with moss, swallowed on both sides by yew hedges that had been allowed, over decades, to forget they were meant to be ornamental. They leaned in overhead like a congregation bowing toward a sermon, and the light that came through them was greenish and underwater, so that walking up the drive felt less like approaching a house than descending into one.

He had his bag in one hand — two bags, actually, because live-in meant living, and living meant books and a good razor and the photograph of no one — and the rain found the gap between his collar and his neck within the first ten steps and stayed there.

The Bell House announced itself gradually. First the gables, three of them, rising out of the trees like the backs of sleeping animals. Then the chimneys, more than any house needed, tall clay stacks each capped with a stone hood against the rain. Then the whole of it: a tall, narrow, slate-dark house in the Dutch style, its brick the color of dried blood, its windows tall and many-paned and holding the grey sky in every one of them so that the facade seemed to be watching itself.

It was beautiful. Wren gave it that honestly, the way he gave patients their dignity even when their bodies had stopped deserving the word. Beautiful houses were like beautiful people; you noticed the beauty first and only later, if you were unlucky, the thing underneath it that made the beauty necessary.

At the foot of the house, where the ground sloped down toward what must have been a kitchen garden gone to seed, there was a door set into the earth itself — a cellar entrance, stone-framed, with steps leading down into shadow. The doorway had been bricked up. Not boarded, not chained: bricked, with newer mortar than the rest of the house, pale as scar tissue against the old dark stone. Someone had wanted that door closed badly enough to pay for masonry, and had wanted it closed permanently enough to make closing it ugly.

Wren stood in the rain for a moment longer than he needed to, looking at it.

Then he went up to the front door and knocked, because he was a nurse, and nurses learn early that the body tells the truth but only when you stop staring at the parts that frighten you.

* * *

The door was opened by a woman in her late thirties with her mother's cheekbones and her own exhaustion, which she wore the way some people wear perfume — deliberately, almost proudly, as though admitting to it were a form of competence.

"You're Underhill," she said. "Thank God. Come in before the weather gets ideas."

Maren Vane shook his hand with a grip that was testing him and didn't mind that he knew it. Her hair was pinned up in a way that suggested she'd done it at six a.m. and had not thought about it since. Behind her the hall opened up, wide and dim, paneled in oak gone honey-dark with age, and Wren's first professional impression — before the furniture, before the smell of woodsmoke and beeswax and something faintly medicinal underneath — was of light behaving strangely.

Not strangely, exactly. Abundantly. There were mirrors everywhere. A tall cheval glass by the stairs. Gilt-framed oval mirrors between the windows. A mirrored overmantel above a fireplace big enough to roast an ox. Each one caught the grey daylight and passed it along to the next, so that the hall seemed deeper than it was, brighter than it should have been, populated at its edges by soft repeated versions of everything.

"Forty," said Maren, following his eyes. She said it flatly, without being asked, which told him she'd been asked before. "Everyone counts. The estate inventory says forty. My grandmother collected them; my great-grandmother before her. It's a family disease." She smiled, and the smile had warmth in it, real warmth, but it sat on her face the way a bandage sits on skin — covering something, doing a job. "You'll want to see your room. And then my mother, if she's having a day."

"Is she having a day?"

"She's having a life," Maren said. "Some days it's better than others."

A boy came down the stairs sideways, which is how ten-year-olds descend stairs when they are pretending to be something else — a crab, a soldier, a ghost. He stopped four steps from the bottom and looked at Wren with the frank, total appraisal children reserve for new adults, cataloguing him the way he'd catalogued the house: face, hands, bag, shoes, done.

"You're the nurse," the boy said.

"I'm Wren."

"Toby." He considered this exchange complete, then added, as an afterthought or perhaps as a warning: "There's forty-one."

"Toby." Maren's voice changed — not sharp, exactly, but tightened, like a sheet pulled taut. "We've talked about counting."

"I'm not counting. I'm telling him the number." Toby looked at Wren again. "She says forty. I counted forty-one. You can count too, if you want. Nobody ever finishes, though."

"Toby, go tell Dev the nurse is here."

Toby went, but backwards, up the stairs, facing Wren the whole way, until the turn of the banister took him. Wren watched him go and felt the first small cold weight settle behind his sternum, the one he'd learned over eleven years of hospice work never to ignore and never, ever to name in front of the family.

Children in dying houses always knew the number. He'd learned that much already, though he hadn't let himself know he'd learned it.

* * *

Dev Okafor was in the kitchen, standing at the counter with a laptop open and a mug of coffee he was clearly using as a prop, something to hold while he stood in a room and did not belong to it. He was a long, careful-looking man with reading glasses pushed up into his hair and the particular stillness of someone who has spent months solving problems that cannot be solved and has begun to find the effort insulting.

"Underhill. Good." He closed the laptop — not slammed, just closed, with the finality of a man ending a meeting he'd already lost. "Agency sent your file. Eleven years hospice, references like a saint's CV. What I want to know is why you said yes."

"Dev," Maren said, from the doorway, in a tone that contained an entire marriage.

"No, it's a fair question." Dev turned to Wren, and his eyes were tired and direct and honest in a way that made Wren like him immediately and distrust the liking. "This house pays absurdly well for a reason. People don't stay. We've had four carers since Christmas. One left in the night — left her wages, left her case notes, left her coat. In February. So: why did you say yes?"

Wren set down his bags. He'd answered this question at interviews, at dinner parties, at his sister's wedding, and he had a version that was true the way a mirror is true — accurate, reversed, and missing whatever stands in front of it.

"The money's good," he said. "And I'm good at end-stage care. Your mother-in-law deserves someone who isn't frightened of her."

Dev held his gaze for a moment longer than politeness required, and Wren understood that the man was measuring him against the coat-less woman, against all of them, against the door they'd all eventually use.

"She'll frighten you anyway," Dev said. "Not on purpose. She thinks it's funny." Something moved through his face then, quick as a fish under ice — worry, maybe, or guilt, or the specific grief of engineers confronted with systems that refuse specifications. "Fair warning. That's all I've got. Fair warning and coffee."

The coffee was excellent. Wren noted that too, and filed it away with the bricked cellar door and the boy who counted to forty-one: dying households were always, in his experience, either loveless or loving past the point of sense, and this one was setting its stakes early on the second kind, which was always harder to leave.

* * *

They gave him the blue room at the back of the house, second floor, overlooking the drowned garden and the bricked cellar door below. It was a good room — wide bed, writing desk, a wardrobe that smelled of cedar and forty years of other people's clothes. On the desk someone had left a lamp, a carafe of water, and a small brass bell on a saucer, polished bright.

"For nights," Maren said from the doorway. "Mother refuses monitors. Refuses the fall alarm, the pressure mat, all of it. She says—" Maren paused, and Wren watched her decide whether to finish the sentence, and watched her lose. "She says the house keeps time well enough. She thinks she's being witty. Ring the bell if anything changes."

"And if she rings it?"

"Then come fast." Maren said it lightly, but her hand stayed on the doorframe, and Wren saw her knuckles whiten around the wood for half a second before she made herself let go. "I'll let you settle. Dinner's at seven. Mother eats downstairs when she can manage the stairs, which she insists on managing, so don't offer to carry her unless you want a fork in your hand."

"Maren." He said it before he'd decided to. She stopped. "Your son counted forty-one mirrors."

"Yes."

"The inventory says forty."

"Yes." She looked at the window, at the garden, at anywhere else. Rain ran down the glass in long fingers. "My mother says the ledger's wrong. She says it's always been wrong, that her grandmother wrote it from memory after a fire destroyed the original, and memory miscounts." She smiled again, that bandage smile. "Or Toby miscounts. He's ten. Ten-year-olds miscount."

"Do you believe that?"

What crossed Maren Vane's face then lasted perhaps a second and a half, and Wren would think about it later, much later, when believing things had become his profession whether he liked it or not. It was not fear. It was the expression of a woman standing at a door she has decided never to open, hearing something move on the other side, and choosing — actively, daily, at cost — to keep her hand off the handle.

"I believe my mother is dying," she said, "and that this family has lost enough people to this house, and that we are going to get through the next few months with as much grace as we can afford. Count the mirrors if it helps you feel professional. But don't tell me the number. Whatever number you get." She stepped back into the hall. "Dinner's at seven."

Her footsteps went down the corridor, quick and even, a woman walking away from a conversation at a pace designed to prove she wasn't running.

* * *

He unpacked slowly, because unpacking was a ritual and rituals kept the mind honest. Books on the shelf. Razor by the basin. The photograph of no one propped against the water carafe, because he'd brought it for twenty years and it showed nothing but a hillside in fog, and explaining why would require a version of himself he didn't issue to employers.

Then, because he was who he was — because eleven years of charting vitals had made inventory a form of prayer — he counted the mirrors.

He started in his own room: one, the wardrobe glass. Down the corridor: two, three. The landing cheval: four. He worked methodically, room by room, floor by floor, ticking them off against the copy of the estate inventory Maren had left in a folder on the desk, and the house cooperated with him the way dying patients cooperate — smoothly, warmly, right up until they don't.

Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine. Forty. The inventory agreed with itself, item by item, gilt frame and plain frame, hall and stair and chamber, and Wren stood on the second-floor landing with his pencil resting against the page and felt the silence of the house press in around him, attentive, patient.

Forty listed. Forty found.

And yet.

He stood very still and listened to the house the way he listened to a chest — not for sounds, but for the spaces between sounds. Rain on slate. Wind in the yews. Somewhere below, the murmur of Dev's voice, the clatter of Toby doing something forbidden with something heavy. All normal. All accounted for.

Except that the hallway behind him — the long hall on the ground floor, visible now through the stairwell's open well, running away beneath his feet like a throat — was full of light that had nowhere to come from. He leaned over the banister and looked down its length, past the cheval glass, past the overmantel, past the gilt ovals catching the grey afternoon and handing it to one another down the whole dim reach of the house.

Every mirror he could see was one he'd counted.

Every mirror he could see was also showing him, small and doubled and trebled, the figure of a man leaning over a banister — and in the furthest of them, at the very end of the hall where the light pooled thick as oil, the reflection appeared a half-breath before he felt himself finish leaning.

Which was impossible. Which was exhaustion, eleven hours of travel, the particular vertigo of old houses whose floors are never level and whose angles are never true. He straightened up. The reflections straightened with him, all of them, obediently, instantly, exactly as physics demanded.

Exactly. He made himself say the word in his head twice.

Downstairs, a door opened and closed. Voices — Maren calling Toby to wash his hands, Toby protesting, Dev saying something dry that made Maren laugh despite herself. A family, warming itself around its own small fire, keeping the dark at the edges of the room where dark belongs.

Wren finished his count properly, because he was a professional. Forty in the inventory. Forty in the house above ground.

Which left, by simple arithmetic, one mirror unaccounted for — and only one place in the Bell House where a mirror could hide from the light.

Through his window, as the rain thickened toward evening, he could see the cellar door below: the stone frame, the worn steps, the pale brickwork sealing it shut, mortar still bright after twenty years or fifty, as if the wound had been cauterized rather than healed.

Somewhere under his feet, behind that wall, the house was keeping something face-down in the dark.

Wren closed the curtain on it, hung his watch on the bedpost out of habit, and went down to meet his patient — telling himself, in the firm clinical voice he used on panicking families and on himself at three a.m., that tomorrow he would ask Edith Vane about the ledger, and she would laugh, and the number would resolve itself the way numbers do.

His watch, hanging on the bedpost, ticked.

From somewhere far below the floor — so far below it might have been the pipes, might have been the wind in the chimney flues, might have been nothing at all — something ticked back.
