## 6. Toby's Game

The game had rules, and Toby had made most of them himself, which was the best kind of game because then nobody could tell you that you were doing it wrong.

Rule one: you had to pick a mirror. Not just any mirror. It had to be a good one, tall and clean, with nothing in the background to mess it up. The one in the front hall was the best in the whole house — a big dark rectangle in a frame of carved wooden leaves, so old that the silver behind the glass had gone cloudy at the edges like breath on a window. When you stood in front of it, the hall behind you stretched away twice as long as it really was, and the chandelier hung upside down from the ceiling of the other world like a chandelier in a house where gravity had gotten tired.

Rule two: the twin had to copy you *perfectly*. Not almost. Perfectly. If you raised your right arm, the twin raised its left arm — which was the same thing, Toby had figured out, because mirrors flipped you, and if you thought about it too hard your brain started to itch, so he'd decided early on not to think about it. The point was that whatever Toby did, the Toby-in-the-glass did at the same moment, like they were two halves of one person folded along a line of glass.

Rule three: you got a point for every ten seconds of perfect copying. Toby kept score in his head, and he was very good at keeping score in his head, because at school they'd tested him and said he had a memory like a filing cabinet, which his mum had laughed at and his granddad — before Granddad died, before the funeral where everyone wore itchy black clothes and Aunt Priya cried into a hanky with lace on it — would have said *that's the Vane in you, boy.*

Toby had been playing the game since they moved into the Bell House in June. It was now the last week of the summer holidays, and his high score was four hundred and twelve.

He had never told anyone about the game. Not because it was a secret, exactly. More because it was *his*, the way the den under the rhododendrons at their old house had been his, the way the space behind the sofa was his when the grown-ups talked in that low smooth voice they used when they thought he couldn't hear. Grown-ups had a voice like that. It was the voice they used for words like *hospice* and *arrangements* and *she's still herself, mostly.* Toby heard it through the floor at night, a murmur with no edges, and he'd lie in bed and press his ear to the cool boards and try to make the words come apart.

The game made the house better. That was the thing nobody understood, because nobody knew about the game. The Bell House was enormous and full of doors that stuck and smells that were old — old paper, old flowers, old soup — and it had Great-Gran Edith in it, who was dying, though everyone said *ill* the way you'd say a light was *off*, like it might come back on if you jiggled the switch. The house was scary in a slow, boring, grown-up way. But the mirrors made it into a game, and a game was something Toby could win.

So on the last Tuesday of the holidays, at four o'clock in the afternoon, with the light coming through the hall windows all thick and gold like syrup, Toby stood in front of the big mirror and began.

He started easy. He touched his nose. The twin touched its nose. He spun in a slow circle, arms out like an aeroplane, and the twin spun with him, and the upside-down chandelier wheeled overhead in its own hall, and for a second Toby felt dizzy in a nice way, like the two houses were spinning around each other like the double stars in his space book.

He hopped on one foot. He made a face — cheeks puffed, eyes crossed. He did the robot, which was his best move, all stiff angles and jerky stops. He mouthed the alphabet backwards, which he'd taught himself from the poster over his desk, and watched the twin's mouth make the same silent letters.

*Z, Y, X, W...*

The hall was quiet except for the tick of the grandfather clock at the end, which was wrong by four minutes and which nobody ever fixed, and the far-off sound of Dev — his stepdad, though Toby was still practicing saying the word without a hitch in the middle — doing something hammer-ish in the back of the house. Mum was upstairs with Great-Gran. The nurse was somewhere. Toby liked the nurse. The nurse was called Wren, which was a bird, which was a good name, and he had long fingers and a way of standing still that Toby approved of. Most grown-ups fidgeted. Wren could stand at a window for whole minutes without moving, like a heron.

*...V, U, T...*

Toby was on one hundred and ninety seconds of perfect copying when the twin stopped.

He didn't notice at first. That was the thing about it — it was like a stair that wasn't there, the way your foot goes down into nothing and your stomach jumps. He was mid-alphabet, mouthing *Q*, and he happened to glance at the twin's mouth, and the twin's mouth wasn't mouthing anything.

The twin was just standing there. Arms at its sides. Head tilted — no, not tilted, straight — looking at him.

Toby stopped. His heart did a small cold flip, the way it did when he woke up and couldn't remember what day it was.

Then he laughed, out loud, in the big empty hall, and the laugh came back off the walls and sounded like someone else's laugh.

"Okay," he said. "Okay, very funny."

Because that was a rule too, an unwritten one, the rule underneath all the other rules: the twin always copied. It had to. It was a mirror. That was what mirrors *did*, the way dogs barked and Dev burned toast. If the twin stopped copying, then either Toby's brain was broken, or the mirror was broken, or —

He didn't finish the thought. He was good at not finishing thoughts. He'd learned it from the grown-ups, who were champions at it.

He raised his right hand, slowly, watching.

The twin raised its hand at the same moment. Perfect. Back to normal. Toby let out a breath he hadn't known he was holding, and his reflection let out the same breath, and the chandelier hung still in its upside-down hall, and the clock ticked, and everything was fine.

Except.

Except that for the rest of the afternoon, in the back of his head, in the place where the filing cabinet was, Toby filed one small folder and marked it *do not open yet.* The folder had a picture in it. The picture was of the twin's face, and in the picture the twin was not copying him, and the twin was not doing anything at all.

The twin was watching.

* * *

He saw it again on Thursday.

This time it was in his own bathroom mirror, the one over the sink with the crack in the corner like a lightning bolt, and it was evening, and he was brushing his teeth, which was not part of the game because the game was only for the big mirrors, but the rules had gone soft in his head lately, like a biscuit left out.

He brushed up and down. The twin brushed up and down. Foam at the corners of both mouths. He leaned close to check his front teeth, the two big ones that had only just finished growing in, and the twin leaned close too, and their noses almost touched across the glass, and his breath made a small fog circle on the mirror that the twin's breath also made, or maybe it was the same breath, and —

The twin blinked. Toby hadn't blinked.

Toby stood very still, toothbrush hanging out of his mouth like a little white flag.

The twin stood very still too. Its eyes were on his eyes. It was not blinking. It was not moving. It was *waiting*, the way a cat waits at a mousehole, except there was no mouse, except the mouse was him.

"Stop it," Toby said around the toothbrush. His voice came out small and bubbly.

The twin did not stop it. The twin did not do anything. It went on watching him with his own eyes, which was the worst part, that they were his eyes, his exact eyes with the fleck of brown in the left one that Mum said was from Great-Granddad Vane, and they were looking at him from the other side of the glass like eyes looking out of a letterbox.

Toby spat, rinsed, and ran the tap until the mirror fogged over completely. Then he wiped it with his sleeve so he could see, because he was not a baby, and the twin was back to normal, and it copied him wiping the mirror, and everything was fine, and he did not open the folder, and he did not tell anyone, because what would he even say? *My reflection looked at me?* Dev would laugh kindly. Mum would do her worried face and ask if he was getting enough sleep, and then she'd go and stand in the hallway for a minute, and he'd hear her not-quite-sighing, and it would be one more small grey stone in the pile of things that made Mum's mouth go tight these days.

Besides. It might have been his brain. Brains did stuff. He'd read about it. Your brain filled in the blind spot where your optic nerve was, and it made faces in the dark, and sometimes when you stared at a word too long it stopped looking like a word. Maybe staring at your own face too long did the same thing. Maybe the twin was a hole his brain was filling in, and sometimes it filled it in wrong.

That was a good explanation. Toby held onto it the way you'd hold onto a rail going down a dark staircase.

He held onto it for six days.

* * *

On Wednesday, Wren caught him at it.

Not on purpose — Toby could tell it wasn't on purpose, because Wren was carrying a folded blanket and a mug of tea and had the walk of someone going from one room to another, which was most of what Wren did all day, back and forth, up and down, like water finding its level. But he stopped when he saw Toby in the hall, in front of the big mirror, mid-robot.

"What's the move called?" Wren said.

Toby froze, one arm out at an angle. "The robot."

"It's a good robot." Wren set the tea down on the hall table, careful, on a coaster, even though the table was a hundred years old and had rings on it older than Dev's whole family. "Very stiff. In a good way."

"Thanks." Toby looked at him. Wren had that stillness again, heron-still, and his eyes went from Toby to the mirror and back, and something in Toby — the part that kept the folders — stood up and took notice. "Do you want to see the game?"

Wren didn't say *what game* in a way that meant he'd already forgotten. He said, "What are the rules?" in a way that meant he'd already decided to play.

So Toby explained. Rule one, the mirror. Rule two, perfect copying. Rule three, points. He demonstrated: a wave, a shrug, the alphabet backwards at speed, which made Wren's eyebrows go up in a way that Toby filed under *impressed.* The twin copied everything. Of course it did. It was a Tuesday afternoon, and the light was ordinary, and the twin was behaving.

"Four hundred and twelve," Toby said. "My high score. That's sixty-eight minutes of perfect copying."

"That's a serious score," Wren said, and the thing about Wren was that he didn't say things like *that's a serious score* in a fake voice, the way teachers did. He said it like it was true, because he'd done the maths.

"You have to watch the whole time, though," Toby said. "You can't look away, not even for one second, or the count starts over. That's the hard part. Your eyes want to look away. Eyes are rubbish at staying."

"Eyes are rubbish at staying," Wren agreed, and then he did something that made Toby's stomach do the cold flip again, but a different kind of flip, an interested kind. He stepped up beside Toby, so they were both in the mirror, Wren tall and thin and Toby small and square, and he watched the twin-Wren with a flat, careful look, like a man checking a lock.

Toby watched him watching.

And because the folder in his head had been sitting there for six days, getting heavier, and because Wren had said *what are the rules* like he actually wanted to know, Toby heard himself say:

"Sometimes he stops."

Wren didn't move. "Stops what?"

"Copying." Toby kept his eyes on the glass. Both Tobys looked back, four eyes, all behaving. "He just stands still and watches me. He doesn't do what I do. He just... watches. Like he's waiting for me to do something. Or like I'm the telly and he's watching the telly."

The clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled with a small wooden click.

"How long does he watch?" Wren said. His voice was exactly the same as it had been for the robot. That was the thing Toby noticed most, and it was the thing that made him keep going, because grown-ups always changed their voice when you said something weird — it went up at the end, or it went too gentle, like you were made of tissue paper — and Wren's voice didn't change at all.

"Ten seconds," Toby said. "The first time. Then longer. Yesterday it was — I counted. Forty-one seconds."

Something crossed Wren's face then, quick, like a bird going past a window, and Toby didn't know what it was but he filed it, because Toby filed everything.

"Forty-one," Wren repeated.

"It was forty-one seconds," Toby said. "And it was the hall mirror. And —" he hesitated, and then the whole folder tipped over and everything came out at once, the way it does, the bathroom and the blink and the letterbox eyes and the fog he'd wiped away with his sleeve, all of it, in a rush, in a voice that came out smaller than he meant it to. "And I know it sounds made up. I know mirrors can't stop. I've read about brains, I know they fill things in. But he *blinks*, Wren. He blinked when I didn't blink. And he was looking at me. Not at himself. Not copying. At *me.*"

The hall was very quiet. The tea steamed on its coaster. The upside-down chandelier hung in its upside-down hall, and both Toby and the twin-Toby stood in the gold syrup light, and everything was fine, everything was behaving.

"Does he do anything else," Wren said, "when he's watching?"

Toby thought about it. He thought about the tilt of the twin's head, which wasn't a tilt, which was straighter than straight, and the way the twin's stillness had a texture to it, like the stillness of the grandfather clock, like the stillness of a thing that had been still for a very long time and was good at it.

"He mouths words sometimes," Toby said. "When he thinks I'm not looking. I haven't worked out what they are yet. They're slow. Like he's talking to someone who's deaf."

Wren turned his head and looked at Toby — at the real Toby, not the glass one — and his long heron face was calm, and his voice, when it came, was calm, and it was the calm of someone standing on a frozen lake listening to the ice.

"Has your mum ever seen it?" he said. "Or Dev?"

"No. They'd say I was imagining it." Toby scuffed his shoe on the floorboards. "Mum's got enough to think about. With Great-Gran."

"Yes," Wren said. "She has."

He picked up his tea then, and Toby thought that was the end of it, grown-ups always ended things by picking things up, and Wren got as far as one step toward the stairs before he stopped. He didn't turn all the way around. He turned half, so that Toby could see his face in profile, and the profile of Wren was also in the mirror, so there were two half-Wrens, one on each side of the glass, and for a moment Toby had the strange sliding feeling that he couldn't remember which one was which.

"Toby," Wren said. "If he mouths words again — the twin — I'd like to know what they say. If you work it out."

"Okay," Toby said, surprised.

"Any time. Day or night. My room's at the end of the east corridor. I don't sleep much."

Then he went up the stairs, and the tea went with him, and the hall was empty except for Toby and the mirror and the clock ticking its wrong four minutes.

Toby stood in front of the glass a while longer, because he was not a baby and he did not run from rooms. He looked at the twin. The twin looked at him. He raised his hand; the twin raised its hand. Perfect. One hundred and eighty-nine seconds of perfect, and counting.

But just before he turned to go, just as he was lifting his foot for the first stair, he saw it — saw it clearly, in the gold light, in the corner of the glass where the silver had gone cloudy like breath —

The twin smiled.

Toby hadn't smiled. Toby had been thinking about stairs.

And the smile wasn't his smile. It was wider than his smile could go, and it was patient, the way the clock was patient, and it sat on the twin's face like something that had been waiting a long time for permission.

Toby went up the stairs two at a time. He did not look back, because eyes were rubbish at staying and he needed his for running.

But that night, lying in bed with the covers up to his chin, listening to the house tick and settle and breathe, he made himself a promise, the way you make promises in the dark where they're easiest to keep: tomorrow, he'd stand at the mirror and not copy. Just once. Just for a second. He'd stand still and let the twin move first — and he'd watch what it chose to do.

Because it was his game. He'd made the rules.

And rule four, the new one, the one he wrote in his head in big letters and underlined twice, was this: *find out what he says.*
