## 18. Toby Is Taken Partway

Toby had learned the twin's language the way you learn any language: by watching its mouth.

That was the trick of it, the thing nobody else seemed to understand. The glass people didn't talk with sound. They talked with shapes. Their lips moved and made no noise at all, like fish in a tank, like the TV when Dad muted it during the boring parts, and if you watched closely enough — closer than anyone was supposed to look, because Mom said staring into mirrors gave you headaches and Wren said it was bad for your eyes even though Toby could tell he didn't really believe that — the shapes meant things.

He'd been practicing for two weeks now. Practicing in secret, mostly at night, with the hallway mirror and the little nightlight that turned everything amber and soft. He'd stand in his pajamas and watch his twin raise its hand, and he'd raise his hand a half-second late so they wouldn't be doing it together, because doing it together was the old game and the old game was over. The new game was harder. The new game was *answering*.

The first word had been easy. **HELLO**. Just four shapes, and the twin had mouthed them slow, patient, like Miss Okafor teaching the new kid phonics. Toby had shaped them back with his own lips, no sound, just breath and muscle, and the twin had done something he'd never seen it do before.

It had smiled without him smiling first.

That was against every rule of mirrors. Toby knew the rules. Mirrors copy. That's what they're *for*. His reflection was supposed to be a follower, an echo, a thing that could only ever do what Toby did one blink behind. But this one smiled on its own, and the smile wasn't Toby's smile. Toby's smiles were quick and crooked, one side higher. This one was slow and even and went all the way up to eyes that stayed flat as coins.

Toby hadn't told anyone. Not Mom, who cried sometimes in the kitchen when she thought nobody could hear through the floor. Not Dad, who measured things with his phone and wrote numbers in a notebook and had started sleeping with his shoes on, which Toby noticed because Dad never used to, and shoes-on-while-sleeping meant a man who expected to run. Not Wren, who was nice and brought Toby those lemon biscuits from the tin in the pantry and always asked real questions instead of grown-up questions. Wren would probably believe him. That was the problem. If Wren believed him, then it was real, and if it was real, then Toby couldn't take it back anymore, couldn't decide tomorrow that it had been a dream or a game or a bit of imagination, which is what Mom called everything now, everything unexplained, in a voice that got thinner each time she said it.

So Toby kept it. Kept it the way you keep a stone in your pocket that's too smooth to throw away.

The words came faster after HELLO. The twin taught them in order, always in order, patient as tide. **GOOD**. Then **BOY**. Those two together, GOOD BOY, and Toby had felt a warm little bloom in his chest before he'd felt the cold underneath it, because good boy was what Grandpa Vane must have said once, maybe, to somebody, in this same hallway, in front of this same glass. The house was old enough to have said it to lots of boys.

Then, last Tuesday, three words that took him all evening to work out, sounding them shape by shape while his own lips went numb:

**STAY FOR THE COUNT.**

Toby didn't know what the count was. That was the strange part. Everyone acted like there was something to count — he'd heard Mom and Dad arguing low about "the delay" and "the seconds," and he'd heard Wren's watch beeping in the night like a tiny trapped bird, and Grandma Edith counted in her sleep, everyone knew that, everyone pretended they didn't — but nobody had ever told *Toby* anything, because he was ten and ten meant you got protected instead of informed, which Toby considered the single worst feature of being alive.

But his body knew. That was the thing he couldn't explain to anyone, the thing that sat in him like a swallowed stone. When the twin mouthed STAY FOR THE COUNT, something behind Toby's ribs had leaned toward the words. Like hearing your name across a loud playground. Like the pull of the last stair in the dark when your legs already know where the next one is.

And on Thursday night, lying in bed with the covers up to his chin, Toby answered out loud.

He hadn't planned to. That mattered to him later, that it hadn't been planned, that it had come out of him the way a sneeze comes, the way a sob comes, some pressure finding its only exit. He'd been saying goodnight to the mirror the way he'd started doing — standing at his bedroom door, looking down the hall to where the big mirror hung between the bathroom and the linen closet, and shaping GOODNIGHT at his twin, who stood in its own amber hallway in its own world one second late.

Except tonight the twin hadn't waited its second.

Tonight the twin had raised its hand *first*. Before Toby moved. And beckoned.

One finger, curled twice. Come here.

And Toby, ten years old, alone in the long amber throat of the Bell House hallway, heard himself say — aloud, in a whisper, in his own voice but not quite, in his voice with a faint doubled edge under it like a recording played over itself —

"Okay."

Just that. Okay. One small word, given freely, into the dark.

The hallway light flickered once, the way it did when the fridge cycled downstairs. Nothing else happened. The twin lowered its hand. Toby went to bed and slept like a stone dropped down a well.

* * *

He woke — if you could call it waking — sometime deep in the smallest hours, in the part of night where even the house seems to hold its breath between heartbeats.

His feet were cold. That was the first fact. His feet were cold and they were moving, carrying him, and Toby was watching from somewhere slightly above and behind his own eyes, the way you watch a dream you know is a dream but can't steer. He was walking down the stairs. He knew the stairs by feel, eleven of them, and his hand trailed the banister the way it always did, except his hand was pale in the dark, paler than pale, moon-colored, and it left no sound on the wood.

*This is a dream,* Toby told himself firmly, because that's what you did with dreams. *Wake up. Pinch your arm.*

He pinched his arm. It hurt in a faraway way, like news from another country.

The ground floor was silver. Moonlight came through the landing window in long panes and lay across the floorboards like ladders laid down, and Toby's bare feet crossed the light and the light didn't warm them. He walked past the kitchen where Dad's notebook lay open on the counter, past the coat hooks where Mom's scarf hung like a sleeping animal, past the door of Grandma Edith's room, where a thin line of lamplight showed underneath and where, very faintly, in a voice worn down to almost nothing, someone was counting.

"...seven... six..."

Toby's body stopped. His head turned. Through the gap under the door he could see the shadow of her bed, the shadow of her hand rising and falling slowly, keeping time.

"...five..."

*Don't,* thought Toby, from his faraway place. *Don't count her, don't—*

But his feet turned away, obedient to something else, and carried him down the back corridor to the cellar door.

The cellar door was bricked up. Everybody knew that. It was just brickwork set into the wall at the end of the corridor, older bricks, darker ones, with a wooden frame around them like a door that had been dressed up as a wall. Dad and Wren had opened a seam in it once — Toby wasn't supposed to know that either, but he'd heard the chipping at night and seen the gray dust on Dad's slippers — and then they'd sealed it again, or tried to.

Toby's body stood in front of it. And the frame around the bricks — the old wooden doorframe, painted over so many times its carvings were soft as melted wax — began to open.

Not the bricks. The *frame*. The darkness inside the doorway deepened, gathered, became a room-shaped absence, and cold air poured out of it over Toby's bare feet and ankles, air that smelled like wet stone and candle smoke and something older underneath, something like the inside of a bell.

*No,* thought Toby. *No no no, I said okay, I didn't mean okay forever—*

He stepped forward. His foot lifted. He felt the threshold under his sole, the raised lip of the old sill, worn smooth by a hundred years of descending feet, and beyond it the first cellar step going down into lamplight that shouldn't have been there, warm yellow light, and the smell of tallow, and somewhere below, a humming like a struck string that never quite stops.

He stepped down. One foot on the top stair. The cold climbed to his knees.

*Toby.*

Someone said his name. Not the twin. Not the thing in the cellar. Someone upstairs, someone alive, someone whose voice cracked right down the middle with fear—

Mom.

She'd found his empty bed. She was coming down the corridor with a lamp held out before her like a shield, and Toby, from his faraway place, screamed at his own body *turn around, turn around*, and his body — slowly, joint by joint, like a gate deciding — began to turn.

Half-turned. That's how they found him. Halfway between the corridor and the cellar, one foot on the sill, one foot on the boards, his body twisted at the waist like a man looking back at something calling from behind, and his face —

His face was the worst part, though nobody said so. His face was smiling. Slow and even, both sides level, all the way up to eyes that were open and fixed on the lamplight below and were not, somehow, entirely his own. And his mouth was moving. Shaping words. No sound, just shapes, fish-in-a-tank shapes, and Maren Vane dropped the lamp — it rolled, didn't break, threw wild shadows up the walls — and grabbed her son by both arms and hauled him backward off the threshold, and the frame snapped shut behind him like a book closing, bricks solid, darkness gone, lamplight gone, humming cut off mid-note.

Toby's knees buckled. Maren caught him. He weighed almost nothing, he was freezing, his skin cold as the bricks themselves, and when she pulled him against her chest he was still mouthing, still finishing the sentence his body had carried down the stairs, and Dev came running barefoot with Wren behind him, and they all saw it, none of them said it: the boy's lips working, silent, patient, and Wren kneeling close, reading the shapes the way you'd read a note slipped under a door.

STAY FOR THE COUNT.

Then Toby shuddered, all at once, like a rope coming slack, and burst into tears — hot, ordinary, ten-year-old tears — and the echo went out of the night, and the house around them stood very still, listening.

They carried him to the sofa and wrapped him in blankets and he drank cocoa he couldn't taste and wouldn't let go of his mother's wrist. Nobody slept. Toward dawn Toby finally spoke, hoarse and small, and asked the question none of the adults wanted asked, the question that had been waiting in the walls for twenty years:

"What number is Grandma on?"

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then, quietly, honestly, because the boy had earned honesty and everyone in the room knew it:

"Four."

Toby nodded slowly, the way children nod at things too large to fit inside them, and pulled the blanket higher, and looked — only briefly, only once — at the hallway mirror at the end of the room.

In it, four figures stood watching the sofa: Wren, Maren, Dev, and a small wrapped boy.

Five, if you counted the tall one standing just behind Wren, smiling without him, waiting its turn.
