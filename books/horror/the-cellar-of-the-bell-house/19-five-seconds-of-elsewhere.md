## 19. Five Seconds of Elsewhere

The lag reached five seconds on a Tuesday, though by then the days had begun to feel like they belonged to the house rather than to the calendar.

Wren knew the exact figure because Dev had built the apparatus for him — two stopwatches, a camera on a tripod aimed at the long mirror in the front hall, and a spreadsheet with columns that Dev filled in each night with the grim diligence of a man documenting his own drowning. *2.4 sec. 3.1 sec. 3.8 sec.* The numbers climbed like water in a flooded cellar, steady, indifferent, unstoppable.

"Five point zero," Dev said that evening, holding up his phone so Wren could see the timestamp. He stood in the kitchen doorway with his sleeves rolled and his tie already loosened, the posture of a man who had stopped pretending he was only visiting. "Five seconds exactly. It's been gaining about half a second a night since we opened the wall."

"We didn't open it," Wren said. "We opened a seam. There's a difference."

"There really isn't." Dev poured himself coffee he wouldn't drink. He'd developed that habit in the last week — making things and not consuming them, as if the ritual of normalcy were a rope he was paying out hand over hand. "Maren wants me to take Toby to her sister's in Corvallis until this is over. Until Edith passes. She thinks it's grief. She thinks we're all grieving in advance and it's making us see things."

"And you?"

Dev looked at him for a long moment. In the window behind him, the dark garden pressed against the glass like something listening. "I'm an engineer, Wren. I don't believe in things I can't measure. But I can measure this." He tapped the phone. "And what I can't explain is why my own reflection started putting its coffee cup down before I did tonight. Not after. Before."

He said it flatly, without drama, which made it worse. Then he left the untouched coffee on the counter and went upstairs to argue with his wife in low voices that carried through the old house's bones like water through limestone.

Wren washed the cup, dried it, put it away. These small acts of order had become his liturgy. And all the while, at the edge of his vision, the dark window held its version of the kitchen — and in it, a second Wren finished drying a cup a full five seconds after his hands had gone still.

* * *

Edith slept through most of it now. Her count had descended from the hundreds into the twenties, and she spoke the numbers the way other dying people spoke the names of the dead — softly, tenderly, with the particular intonation of someone greeting familiar faces at a door.

*Twenty-six. Twenty-five.*

Wren sat with her from eleven until three, as he had every night for weeks, charting her pulse, her breathing, the numbers. The medical chart said *terminal decline, expected course.* The notebook he kept in the drawer beneath it said other things. Two notebooks, two versions of the truth, and he was beginning to understand that he could not serve both much longer.

At 1:47 a.m., he heard Toby's door open down the hall.

He moved quietly. The boy was sleepwalking again — third time that week — and Maren had asked Wren to watch for it, though she'd framed it as a nursing request rather than an admission of fear. *If he gets up, just guide him back to bed. Don't wake him. He gets upset.*

Toby stood in the hallway, barefoot in his pajamas, facing the tall cheval mirror at the corridor's end. The nightlight near the bathroom threw just enough light to silver the glass.

Wren stopped.

In the mirror, Toby's reflection stood facing *outward*. Facing the hallway. Facing Wren.

The boy himself had his back to the glass; there was no way his reflection should have shown anything but the back of his head, the dim slope of his shoulders. But the figure in the mirror was turned around, and it was looking at Wren with an expression of perfect, patient courtesy — the expression of a shopkeeper waiting for a customer to finish deciding.

It raised one hand. Not waving. Gesturing. Come here, the gesture said. Or perhaps: *wait*.

Then Toby stirred, sighed, and turned in his sleep, and the reflection snapped back into obedience like a puppet whose strings had been retensioned. The boy walked past Wren without seeing him, breathing the slow sea-sound breath of deep sleep, and Wren steered him gently back to his room, tucked the covers to his chin, and stood in the doorway shaking for longer than he would ever admit.

He did not write it in either notebook. Some observations, once recorded, become real in a way that cannot be undone, and some part of him was still fighting to keep the door shut.

But the door was not shut. The door had never been shut. He was beginning to suspect it had been bricked over from the inside.

* * *

Three nights later, the five seconds showed him everything.

It happened because of the storm. A Pacific front had come ashore that afternoon, and by evening the Bell House was wrapped in rain and wind, the gables groaning, the forty listed mirrors (and the one unlisted) catching fragments of lightning like animals flashing their eyes at the edge of a road. The power flickered twice and held. Maren had gone to sit with her mother; Dev had taken Toby to the den to build a model bridge, their peace offering after the argument about Corvallis; and Wren, off-shift for exactly ninety minutes, found himself unable to stay out of the hall.

He told himself he was checking the mirrors against the ledger again. He had done it eleven times. Forty-one reflections, forty-one rooms, every surface accounted for. It was a ritual now, like Dev's coffee, like Edith's counting: a thing done to hold the shape of the world in place.

He stood before the great hall mirror — a Georgian piece, gilt-framed, older than the republic — and watched the reflected hall. Lightning strobed. Thunder rolled across the hills and broke against the house like surf.

And in the gap between the flash and its echo, the mirror showed him people.

They were standing in the reflected rooms. Not moving, not gesturing, not doing any of the things the living do. Just standing, spaced through the reflected house the way furniture is spaced, with the same settled quality — as if they had been arranged there deliberately, by someone with taste and infinite patience.

A woman in the reflected parlor, wearing a dress from another century, high-collared, her hands folded. A man on the reflected staircase, mid-step but frozen, his face turned toward the glass as though he'd heard his name called years ago and was still waiting for the rest of the sentence. Two children in the reflected nursery — no, not the nursery; the reflected version of the room that was now Edith's, back when it had been a nursery — a boy and a girl, standing beside a crib that did not exist in the waking house, has not existed for a hundred and fifty years.

They were not ghosts. Wren had worked hospice long enough to have opinions about ghosts, and these were not them. Ghosts, when he'd glimpsed anything worth the name, had been *absences*, holes in the shape of the dead. These were presences. Solid. Lit from within by the same lightning that lit the hall. They stood in the mirrored house the way the family stood in the real one — comfortably, rightfully, like people who had paid for their rooms.

The woman in the parlor turned her head. Slowly, with the creaking deliberation of something that had not moved in a very long time, she looked directly at Wren through five seconds of lagged glass.

Her mouth moved. No sound crossed over. But he read the shapes, the way Toby must have read them, the way anyone reads lips through soundproof glass:

*You're early.*

The thunder faded. The five seconds closed. The mirror became a mirror again, showing only the empty hall, the rain-streaked windows, a tired man in scrubs with his mouth hanging open.

Wren sat down on the hall bench and put his head between his knees and breathed, the way he'd taught a hundred families to breathe, and waited for the rational explanations to arrive the way they always had.

They didn't come.

That was the thing he would remember afterward — not the woman, not the children, not even the words, but the silence where his certainties used to live. For twelve years he had been able to reach for neurology the way other men reached for God: temporal lobe activity, hypnagogic misperception, the well-documented tendency of exhausted brains to pattern-match in low light. Every strange thing at the Bell House had come with a clinical label attached, and he had worn the labels like armor.

But he had slept eight hours. His blood sugar was fine. The lightning had been bright enough to read by. And the woman had *looked at him* — tracked him, adjusted, responded — which no artifact of perception does, because artifacts of perception are not interested in us. They do not tell us we're early.

He thought of the ledger. Forty mirrors listed, forty-one present. He thought of the humming behind the cellar brick, the cold air moving outward like breath. He thought of the mirror-maker whose name he'd pulled from the county archive two nights ago, sitting in his car in a library parking lot with the printout trembling in his hands: *Josiah Vane-Bell, glassworker, d. 1851; wife Constance, d. 1849; children Samuel and Prudence, d. 1849, cause unrecorded.* A man who lost his whole family in two years and then spent the rest of his life alone in this house, working glass, keeping accounts.

*Taught the glass to keep them,* the local history pamphlet had said, in the breezy tone reserved for charming eccentricities. As if it were a hobby. As if it were safe.

Constance. The woman in the high collar. The children by the crib. Samuel and Prudence, standing in a nursery that hadn't existed since before the war, waiting the way children wait — without any sense that waiting might not end.

"Oh," Wren said aloud, to the empty hall, and his voice cracked in the middle of it. "Oh, it's real. It's all real."

Saying it felt less like surrender than like setting down luggage he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body.

* * *

He went upstairs to Edith's room because there was nowhere else to go, and because some part of him understood, finally, that she was the only person in the house who had always known.

She was awake. Of course she was awake; the count kept her awake now more than the pain did. She lay propped against her pillows with the lamp low, and the small vanity mirror on her dresser caught the lamplight and held it, obediently, instantly, the way mirrors were supposed to.

"You've seen them," she said. Not a question.

Wren stood in the doorway. "How did you know?"

"Because you look like my husband looked, the last month of his life. Like a man who's stopped arguing with the evidence." She patted the chair beside the bed. "Sit down, Nurse Underhill. You're white as the sheets. Let me get you through the worst of it — the first believing is the worst. After that it's only arithmetic."

He sat. His legs were glad of it.

"I saw a woman in the parlor," he said. "And children. In your room — in what your room used to be. They've been standing there. Waiting."

"Constance, Samuel, and Prudence Vane-Bell." Edith recited the names the way she recited her numbers, softly, without fear. "The maker's family. They died in the winter of 'forty-nine — fever, the story goes, though my grandmother told me the fever came *after* the glass started keeping time, not before. Josiah couldn't bear an empty house. So he made the house unable to be empty." Her eyes, sunken and fierce, fixed on him. "Everything costs, Wren. That's the first law of mirrors. Everything reflects, and everything costs."

"The count," he said. "The counting. All those patients, all those years—"

"Is the house winding down. My grandmother bargained with it — Vanes stay, house waits, and the waiting is measured. When the measurement ends—" She stopped. Smiled, wry and terrible. "Well. You've read enough of my nights to know how it ends."

Outside, the storm threw a fistful of rain against the window, and in the vanity mirror the lamplight shivered and steadied.

"Why didn't you tell me?" Wren asked. "From the first day. You knew what I'd been hearing at the hospice. You hired me anyway. You must have known I'd—"

"See it?" She reached out and took his hand, her grip still strong, a grip that had managed this household for sixty years. "I hoped you would. I *prayed* you would, though I've had no use for prayer in decades. Because my daughter won't hear it from me — I'm a dying woman with a dying brain, and everything I say will be filed under that. But you—" Her fingers tightened. "You're a man who documents things. A man who refuses meaning. When *you* believe, they'll have to believe, because you'll make them, because you're constitutionally incapable of letting a lie stand, even a kind one. Especially a kind one."

The words landed somewhere tender. He thought of the two notebooks. He thought of all the kind lies he'd told in the last month — *it's probably exhaustion, it's probably grief, it's probably nothing* — each one a brick laid neatly back into the wall.

"Edith," he said carefully, "when the count reaches zero. What happens to the people inside?"

She looked at him for a long moment, and in that moment she seemed to weigh something — how much truth he could carry, or perhaps how much she could bear to give.

"Ask me again when you've slept," she said. "Believing is a wound, and wounds need tending before they need questions." She released his hand and settled back against her pillows, and her eyes drifted toward the ceiling, toward the floor above, toward the attic where the maker's journal slept under dust, though neither of them knew it yet.

"But I'll tell you one thing now, free of charge, because you've earned it." Her voice dropped, and the wry humor fell away from it entirely, leaving something old and level underneath. "The count isn't counting *down* to something, Wren. It's counting down to *someone*. Houses like this don't end. They settle accounts."

Downstairs, the grandfather clock struck two. And in the hall mirror — Wren saw it as he paused at the bedroom door, one last glance back down the corridor — the reflected clock's hands stood still, and the reflected hall was full of quiet, courteous people, all of them facing the stairs, all of them waiting for the family to come down.

Five seconds later, the real clock finished striking, and the real hall was empty, and Wren went to find Dev, because tomorrow — tonight, now — he was going to tell them all of it, and this time he was not going to soften a single word.
