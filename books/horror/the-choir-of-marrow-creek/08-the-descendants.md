## 8. The Descendants

I've been threatened exactly twice in my life. Once by a crane operator named Bud who thought I'd reported him for drinking on shift—I hadn't, but I'd thought about it, which apparently counts—and once by a man from the insurance company who did not appreciate my description of the quarry wall as "a geological apology letter." Both times, the threat came wrapped in a smile, because that's how threats work in a town this size. Nobody in Marrow Creek says *I'll ruin you*. They say *let's be reasonable* and then they ruin you.

So when three black SUVs rolled up to the station on Thursday morning, I recognized the genre immediately.

There were seven of them, and they filed into the station's cramped lobby like it was a church they'd paid for, which, historically speaking, they had. Harlan Pike led them. He's got the kind of face that looks carved for currency—strong jaw, silver temples, a jawline you could bounce a budget off of—and he was wearing a tie the color of a bruise healing nicely. Behind him came the Delacroix woman, whose family owned half the marina; the two Ashford brothers, who between them owned the other half plus the funeral home, which in Marrow Creek is a growth industry; a lawyer named Pruitt whose handshake I'd once described as "a wet envelope"; and two more whose names I knew only from the plaques on the library wing and the hospital annex.

Founding families. The phrase always sounded quaint to me, like they'd been around since the Mayflower. Really it meant their grandparents had been the ones with keys.

"Miss Okafor." Pike said my name like he'd rehearsed it. "Thank you for making time."

"I didn't," I said. "I was already here. It's my job."

A small laugh went through the delegation, the kind people do when they want to signal they're not hostile yet. The Ashford brothers didn't laugh. One of them checked his watch, which read 9:47 in the morning, a strange thing to check unless you were timing something.

August came out of the booth with two mugs of station coffee, which is the same as regular coffee except it has also passed through grief. They set one down in front of me without being asked. We hadn't planned this meeting. We hadn't planned anything, really—we'd spent the last week comparing notes in the way two people compare notes when neither wants to say out loud what the notes add up to. But when Pike called ahead, August had looked at me across the desk and said, "Stay," and I stayed. Whatever was about to happen, I figured we should both be witnesses. That's the foreman instinct. You document everything, because someday somebody's going to ask how the wall fell, and the answer is never *nobody saw it coming*.

"Councilman," August said. "Quite a turnout."

"We're a committee."

"You're a plaque," I said. "You're every plaque in town."

Pike gave me a look that acknowledged the hit without feeling it. "We represent the families who built Marrow Creek, Miss Okafor. And the families who saved it."

"There it is," I said. "Saved it from what, exactly? Because I've read the minutes from 1974, and the word 'saved' doesn't appear. The word 'quarantine' does. So does 'containment.' There's a motion carried by voice vote at two in the morning, which is when towns do their worst thinking."

"Wren," August said quietly. Not *stop*, exactly. More like *pace yourself*. We were going to be here a while.

Pike sat down uninvited, which told me everything about how he understood ownership. The others arranged themselves behind him in a loose crescent, and I realized with a small cold shock that it wasn't casual. It was blocking the door. Or maybe just framing it. In a church, you'd call it a choir arrangement.

"Let me be direct," Pike said. "The drainage project ends. Today."

"That's not your call," I said. "It's a state contract."

"The state takes calls, Miss Okafor. From senators. From donors. From men like me." He folded his hands. "But that's the ugly version, and I'd rather not start ugly. I'd rather start with gratitude. You found the door. You brought it to light. For that, the families are prepared to thank you properly."

The Delacroix woman stepped forward with an envelope. Not a briefcase, not a folder—an envelope, thick enough that I could hear the paper breathe. She set it on the desk between us like she was laying a card face-down.

"Fifty thousand dollars," Pike said. "Each. For your discretion. And there's a second figure attached, if you're willing to leave Marrow Creek for a year or so. Consultancy work. Your résumé would survive it beautifully."

I looked at the envelope. Fifty thousand dollars is a lot of money. It's a new truck, it's my mother's roof fixed properly, it's six months of not waking up at 4 a.m. doing arithmetic. I want to be honest about that, because everyone in these stories pretends the money means nothing to them, and that's a lie the comfortable tell so they don't have to wonder what they'd have done.

It meant something. It also meant nothing, because no amount of money buys back the sound I heard through forty feet of limestone—the hum answering my phone like it had been waiting, patient as sediment, for someone to finally play its tune.

"What's the discretion cover?" August asked. They hadn't touched their coffee. "What am I supposed to not say?"

"The song," Pike said.

The word dropped into the room and nobody moved. Even the HVAC seemed to hold its breath.

"You've been playing it on air," he continued. "Fragments. Letting those—callers—sing. Do you have any idea what you're doing?"

"I have some idea," August said. "I have a grandfather's tape and a ledger with a crossed-out name, so yes, Councilman, I have more than an idea. What I don't have is an explanation from the people who'd know best. So let me ask you directly, since you're all here and the door's still shut: what did your grandparents lock behind it?"

The silence that followed had texture. I've stood in rock cuts where you can feel the mountain listening, and this was that, but indoors, wearing cologne.

"They locked away a danger," Pike said finally. "And the danger stayed locked. Twelve years ago the flood took the old settlement, and whatever else it took, it took quietly, because the seal held. Marrow Creek survived. Children grew up. That is the entire story, and it is a good one, and you two are trying to rewrite it into a confession."

"It is a confession," August said. "That's literally what the fourth verse is. An accounting. Every name."

Something happened to Pike's face then—not anger, worse. Recognition. He glanced at the Ashford brothers, quick, involuntary, the way you glance at a co-signer.

"You know about the verse," he said softly.

"I know pieces."

"Then you know why it can't be sung." He leaned forward, and for a moment the currency-carved face cracked and showed me the man underneath, and the man underneath was afraid. Not of us. Of the math. "You think we're monsters. Fine. Think it. But understand what we understand. That song isn't a leash because it's cruel. It's a leash because it works. Three verses, sung by the living, keep it asleep. Finish the song and you finish the leash. You open the door from the outside. And everything your grandfather's generation bled to contain walks out wearing their voices."

"And if it's just people?" I asked. "People who've been singing in the dark for fifty years?"

"Then they're people who've been *changed*," one of the Ashfords said. The younger one. His voice was flat as a closed casket lid. "You heard them, Miss Okafor. On the radio. Dead men calling a live show. Whatever's left in there, it isn't neighbors anymore."

"And your solution?" August asked. "Because I hear Pike's got concrete scheduled."

Pruitt the lawyer cleared his throat. "The council will be proposing a permanent entombment measure. Public safety ordinance. Very standard."

"Pouring concrete over a door sealed from the inside," I said, "to trap people who are already trapped. That's not public safety. That's a headstone with a budget line."

"It's *order*," Pike said, and there it was—the real word, the one under all the others. "Marrow Creek runs on order, Miss Okafor. Order kept this town alive through the flood, through the disappearances, through things I promise you don't want documented. You want justice. Justice is a luxury of towns that aren't sitting on top of a mouth. We choose order. Every generation, we choose it again. It's the family business."

He said it without shame. That was the part that got under my skin—not the threat, not the money, but the *pride*. Somewhere in 1974, a room full of frightened people made an unforgivable choice, and instead of passing down the guilt, they'd passed down the invoice. *This cost us something. This keeps costing us. Pay it forward.*

August stood up. Slowly. When they spoke, their voice had the broadcast register in it, the one that makes strangers confess on air.

"My grandfather chained that door shut, Councilman. I've heard his voice narrating it. He did it scared, at night, with a mob, and then he spent thirty years hosting a radio show so the town could talk itself to sleep. You want to know what order sounds like after fifty years? It sounds like static at 3 a.m. It sounds like my switchboard lighting up with the dead asking politely for the rest of their song. You didn't bury this in '74. You put it on layaway. And now the bill's come due, and you're standing in my studio offering me hush money."

Pike rose too. The crescent tightened. The older Ashford moved his hand inside his coat in a way that was either a habit or a message.

"Last chance for the friendly version," Pike said. "Take the envelope. Take the consultancy. Or—" and here he smiled, and the smile was the worst thing in the room, worse than anything I'd heard through the stone, because it was *warm*, it was the smile of a man at a bake sale— "or we start asking questions about the site. About the worker who wandered off. About whether Miss Okafor's crew followed proper safety protocols. Accidents happen on drainage projects, you know. OSHA investigations take months. Contracts get suspended. People get blamed." He turned the smile on me full force. "Bud still holds a grudge, by the way. He's on my crew now."

There it was. Not *I'll ruin you*. *Let's be reasonable.*

"You'd endanger my people to protect a secret," I said.

"I'd endanger *anything* to protect this town," Pike said. "That's the job. That's been the job since before either of you was born. The difference between us, Miss Ofafor—Okafor—is that I don't pretend otherwise."

They left the envelope on the desk. All seven of them filed out past me, and the Delacroix woman was the last, and she paused beside my shoulder, close enough that I caught her perfume, expensive and faintly funereal.

"For what it's worth," she murmured, "my grandmother sang the third verse every Sunday until she died. Never missed. Never told anyone why." Her eyes flicked toward the booth, toward the microphone under its dust cover. "She'd have liked your grandfather's show, I think. She listened every night. Right up until the end."

"Until the end of what?" I asked.

But she was already walking out, and the door swung shut behind the last of them, and the lobby was suddenly very quiet and very ordinary, sunlit, smelling of old carpet, as though seven plaques hadn't just offered us fifty grand each to stop telling the truth.

August sat back down. They looked at the envelope for a long moment. Then they picked it up, weighed it in one hand, and walked to the window, where Pike's convoy was pulling out onto the main road.

"You know what the worst part is?" they said. "He's not wrong about the danger. That's the trap of it. Everything he said about the song, the leash, the door—he might even be right. And he's still the villain, because he'd rather bury the question than answer it." They turned the envelope over. "Fifty thousand dollars to stop being curious. My grandfather took a different payment. He took the whole town's silence, and it bankrupted him."

"What are you going to do with it?"

August considered. Then they opened the envelope, fanned out the bills—actual cash, which somehow made it filthier—and fed the lot into the station's ancient paper shredder, the one we used for contest entries. It jammed twice. They fed it slower. Fifty thousand dollars takes a surprisingly long time to die.

"Pike's pouring concrete Friday," they said, watching the strips fall. "Which means whatever happens next happens fast. And Wren—" they looked at me then, and I saw how little sleep was holding them together, "—your crew. Get them off the site. Tonight. All of them. I mean it."

"Why tonight?"

"Because the dead don't wait for permits," August said. "And because Pike just told us exactly how far he'll go, and men like him always count wrong. He thinks the door is the problem. The door is the *lock*. The problem is what's been practicing on the other side for fifty years, getting better at the song, waiting for someone to leave it open."

I drove back to the site with the windows down and the radio off, which for me is the equivalent of screaming. The afternoon light lay flat on the water in the quarry pit, gray-green and still as a held note. Everything looked normal. The generators hummed. The pumps chugged. Tommy Reyes waved from the staging area, and I waved back, and I started counting heads out of pure habit.

Seven. We were eight on days.

I counted again, slow, naming them. Seven.

Tommy saw my face and stopped waving. "Boss?"

"Where's Dale?" I asked. "Dale Merrick. Where is he?"

Tommy shrugged, easy, unconcerned. "Took the early run down to the lower gallery. Said he wanted to check the pump seals before the shutdown." He frowned at whatever was in my expression. "Boss, he's fine. Been down there twenty minutes, maybe."

Twenty minutes. I looked at the tunnel mouth, the dark rectangle breathing cool air, and somewhere far below it, faint as a memory of a memory, I could have sworn I heard humming. A melody Dale Merrick had never learned.

I grabbed the flashlight off the hook and started running.
