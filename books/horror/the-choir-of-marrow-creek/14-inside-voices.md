## 14. Inside Voices

I want to say I went back to the door because of engineering. Because the drainage specs had a discrepancy, because the pressure readings on the east wall didn't reconcile, because a foreman's job is to stand in front of a thing that wants to kill her crew and understand it better than it understands itself.

That's the version I'd give OSHA, anyway.

The truth is I went back because of Tomás. Because he hadn't spoken in six days, and because every morning I checked on him at his sister's place in Harlow Bend, and every morning he was sitting in the same kitchen chair, hands flat on the table, looking at a point about four feet in front of his face like the air there had something worth reading. And because on the fifth morning, when I said his name, his mouth moved. No sound. Just the shape of it, slow and deliberate, the way you'd shape a word for someone who was hard of hearing.

I got close enough to read his lips.

*Harmonize.*

So no. Not engineering.

* * *

The thing you have to understand about Marrow Creek is that the town has a talent for looking away. Twelve years ago the quarry settlement went under the floodwater, and the town held a memorial and a bake sale in the same weekend, and by the following spring the drowned were a cautionary tale about flood insurance and not much else. I didn't grow up here — I came in with the drainage contract two years ago, hired to do what nobody local would: pull the water out of the quarry and see what the flood had been keeping. I used to think the town's silence was grief. Grief, I could work around.

It's not grief. It's maintenance. The whole town is one long, coordinated act of not-listening, and I've spent two years accidentally dismantling the machinery of it, one pump at a time.

August called me the night before I went back down. It was past midnight, which for August means the show had gone off the air and they hadn't.

"You're going down there again," they said. Not a question. August has one of those voices that makes statements sound like diagnoses.

"I'm going to look at the crack," I said. "The hairline fracture in the door face. It wasn't in the original survey, and now it's in the survey, and I don't like things that update themselves."

"Has it gotten bigger?"

"Has what?"

"The crack, Wren."

I hadn't measured. I'd been avoiding measuring, which for me is like avoiding breathing. "I'll measure it."

A long pause on the line. I could hear the station around them — the hum of old equipment, the tick of something cooling. "The callers," August said, "keep asking me to finish the song. And I keep thinking about the fact that they're asking. Politely. Patiently. Twelve years underwater and they say *please*." Another pause. "People who are screaming don't say please. People who are screaming don't have manners. Manners are what you have when you've made peace with something."

"Or when you're trying to lure someone," I said.

"Yeah," August said quietly. "Or that. Be careful down there. And Wren — don't hum. Don't sing. Don't even whistle. I mean it."

"I'm a foreman, not a Disney princess."

"Wren."

"I know. I know. No humming."

I hung up and sat in my truck in the gravel lot and thought about how, three days earlier, I'd caught myself humming the notation from the rubbing while I filled out a timesheet. Four notes, over and over, like a kettle deciding. I hadn't decided to do it. That was the part I hadn't told August. That was the part I wasn't sure I *could* tell anyone, because the moment you say *the song got into me without my permission*, you stop being a professional and start being a case.

I drove to the quarry at four in the morning, because if something was going to happen to me down there, I wanted it to happen during a shift, with paperwork to show for it. There's a certain comfort in bureaucracy even at the end of the world. Maybe especially.

* * *

The drainage tunnel runs four hundred feet into the east face of the quarry, concrete-lined, lit every twenty meters by caged bulbs that the damp keeps killing. The pumps run constant, a heartbeat you feel in your teeth. Two years ago the water stood forty feet deep against the old settlement. Now it stands six, and dropping, and what it's uncovered is a town. Streets. A chapel foundation. A schoolyard fence, rusted to lace. And at the far end of it all, cut into the living rock where the quarry was never supposed to go, the door.

The door is the reason the drainage contract exists, though the county won't say so. Twelve years ago, when the flood came, the water didn't rise over the settlement so much as it *arranged* itself — that's what the old-timers say when they say anything at all. And when our crew broke through the last wall of silt, there it was: a stone door, sealed from the inside, hinges fused, and carved across its face in a clean, confident hand, a line of musical notation.

I took a rubbing of it my first week. I keep it in a plastic sleeve in my truck like it's a permit. Some nights I take it out and look at it, and I've stopped pretending I don't know why. It's not curiosity anymore. Curiosity is a thing you have. This is a thing that has me.

I walked the last hundred feet with my headlamp off. I don't know how to explain that without sounding like I'd already lost. It's just that the light felt rude. Like shining a flashlight into a sickroom. The pumps thudded behind me and faded, and the dark came down soft, and I put my hand on the wall and followed it, and the wall was warm.

Stone isn't supposed to be warm. I've told myself a hundred explanations — geothermal gradient, friction from the water table, a fault line running hot — and every one of them is the professional equivalent of humming to yourself in the dark. The wall was warm the way a held hand is warm. The wall was warm like something on the other side had been leaning against it for twelve years.

I found the crack by touch, about chest height, a hand's width left of the notation. Hairline. When I'd first found it, two weeks ago, I could fit a fingernail in it and nothing more. I brought a feeler gauge — the thin brass blades we use for checking spark plug gaps. The 0.4 millimeter blade slid in. Then the 0.6. Then the 0.8.

The crack was growing. Not fast. But growing, the way a sentence grows when someone is saying it very, very carefully.

I pressed my ear to the stone.

* * *

Here is what I expected: water. Gurgling, maybe, or the groan of settling rock. Here is what I expected second: screaming. I'd made my peace with screaming. Screaming is what people behind a sealed door do, and if the sealed were screaming, then the thing on the other side of this whole nightmare was simple, and simple things can be reported, litigated, concreted over. I'd have taken screaming like a gift.

What I heard was singing.

It was quiet. That's the first thing — quiet, the way a choir is quiet when the director has asked for a piece to be sung *under* the congregation's hearing, a thread of sound you lean toward. It was in rounds. I know rounds the way any kid who survived music class knows them — *Row, row, row your boat*, voices stacked and chasing each other, each one entering two beats after the last. Except this wasn't a children's song. The melody was the notation on the door. All of it. The line I'd rubbed onto paper was not one line of a song. It was the whole song, folded, the way you fold a map, and the voices on the other side of the door had unfolded it and were walking through it in circles.

And they were good. That's the thing I can't get past. They were *good*. Twelve years in the dark behind a fused door, and they sang like a choir that had rehearsed every single day, because — I understood this with a cold, sliding certainty — they had. What else was there to do? What else do you do with forever? You can't scream for twelve years; the body won't fund it. Grief runs out of fuel. But a round doesn't need fuel. A round sustains itself. A round is a machine for staying.

They'd been down there since 1974. That's what August's tape said, what the ledger said, what the town's careful silence said in the way a held breath says it. Fifty years, not twelve. The flood twelve years ago was just the latest lid on a pot that had been sealed since before I was born. Fifty years of rounds. Fifty years of taking the only instrument they had — their voices, the ones the town had used against them, the ones that had been the leash — and turning those voices into a way to remain themselves.

I don't know how long I stood there. My knees ached, so: a while. The rounds turned and turned, and I picked out individual voices the way you pick out instruments in an orchestra. A low one, steady as a cello, holding the root note like a man holding a door shut. A high one that kept wandering up into harmonies the others didn't follow, patient, coming back, wandering again — a child, I thought, and then I made myself not think, because a child's voice, fifty years in the dark, still reaching for notes nobody had taught it —

And then, in a gap between rounds, a voice spoke.

Not loudly. Not from the crack, exactly — from everywhere, the way sound behaves in a room you can't see. It was a woman's voice, older, unhurried, with the particular gentleness of someone who has learned that gentleness is the only volume that doesn't frighten people.

"Is someone there?"

I didn't answer. My whole body had become a held breath.

"You've been standing there a while," the voice said, kindly. "The stone carries a little. Footsteps, mostly. And your heart. Hearts are the easiest thing to hear through stone. Did you know that? Everything else gets muffled. Hearts come through clean."

I put my hand flat on the door. I don't know why. To steady myself, I'd have said. To feel for vibration, I'd have said, if anyone had asked, and no one would, because no one was there.

"You're not one of the committee," the voice went on. "They walk loud. They walk like they're sorry they came. You walk like you're measuring." A pause, and then, with something that was almost humor, worn soft as an old coin: "Foreman, are you? The one who pulled the water out."

My mouth was dry as chalk. "How do you know about the water?" It came out as a whisper, and I hated myself for answering at all, and I'd have answered again.

"The water's been getting thinner for two years," she said. "You can hear it change. Water has a voice when it's deep. It hums against the stone, low, like a man who doesn't want to be talked to. And now it's gone thin and talkative, and the pumps go *chug, chug, chug*, and the light through the crack at noon is different." She paused. "You've given us the noon light back. We'd forgotten about noon."

I thought: *she's been watching a hairline crack for two years, waiting for light.* I thought: *I did that. My pumps did that.* I thought about how you can do a job well and still be doing something monstrous, and how nobody puts that on the safety briefing.

"Are you—" I started, and stopped, because the question was *are you alive* and I didn't want the answer and I didn't want to ask it in case asking made it true in some direction I couldn't take back.

"Ada," the voice said, as if I had asked a different question, a polite one. "Ada Rill. I taught piano, before. On the hill. You'd have been too young, maybe." A soft sound that might have been a laugh. "Fifty years. I've had a long time to think about what I'd say if anyone ever came to the door. I had speeches. Angry ones, at the start. Then clever ones. Then none at all." The rounds swelled gently behind her voice and receded, and it was like hearing weather. "And do you know what I always come back to? What I'd actually say, if it were real, if someone were really standing there?"

I said nothing. My hand was still flat on the stone. The stone was warm. The stone was warm like a hand.

"I'd ask if they'd come to apologize," Ada said. "Not to let us out. Not to finish the song. Just — to apologize. Because I think that's the thing nobody ever gave us. Not the door, even. The door is terrible, but the door at least is *honest*. It's the apology they skipped. They chained us in here and they sang the leash-song and they went home to their suppers, and not one of them — not one — ever said *we did this to you and it was wrong*. They said *it was necessary*. They said *the disappearances had to stop*. They said *God forgive us*, which is a way of asking God to do your apologizing for you." Her voice stayed gentle the whole time, and that was the worst of it. You can hear rage through gentleness, if you listen the way I was listening, with your whole skeleton. "Fifty years, and I've decided the apology matters more than the door. Isn't that something? Fifty years in the dark and I've become a philosopher. The dark will do that. The dark is very educational."

"The disappearances," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "Before the sealing. What was it? What was taking people?"

The rounds paused.

Not stopped — paused, the way a room pauses when someone says the wrong name at dinner. And in the pause I heard the other thing, the thing underneath, the thing the rounds had been laid over like a tablecloth over a hole in the floor. A low sound. Not a voice. A want, given a frequency. The sound of something beneath the water that had been sung to sleep so long it had forgotten how to be awake, and was now, with the water draining, beginning to remember.

Ada's voice came back, and it was careful now, and the carefulness was worse than anything.

"You don't want to know what it was," she said. "You want to know what it *is*, and I'll tell you that much, because you've earned the noon light. It is patient, and it is hungry, and it is asleep, and the song is the only thing holding all three of those together. Three verses from the living side. The living sing the leash." A breath. "But a leash has two ends, foreman. And the fourth verse — the one only we know, the one we've kept alive down here for fifty years, the one we sing in rounds so no one voice has to carry it alone — the fourth verse isn't the leash at all."

"What is it?"

"It's the letting go," Ada said. "It's the apology, set to music. It's every name, spoken into the song, so the thing beneath knows exactly whose debt it's been fed on and whose voices have kept it sleeping. You can't sing it halfway. You can't learn it halfway. That's the cruelty of it — the whole song, or nothing, and *nothing* is what the town has been choosing for fifty years." The rounds resumed behind her, soft, circling, and her voice threaded into them like a hand into a hand. "The water's almost gone. When it goes, the song goes thin, and the thing starts remembering, and then it won't matter what any of us sing. You have weeks. Maybe less, if your county keeps its schedule."

"I don't know the fourth verse," I said. "Nobody up here does."

"No," Ada agreed. "Nobody up here does. But you know someone with a microphone." The gentleness in her voice turned, for just a moment, into something older and sadder and much more afraid. "And I know someone on this side who can teach it. Half of it. Only half — the rest has to be given whole or not at all, and I won't give it whole, not through a crack in a door, not to someone who might be standing there for the wrong reasons." A pause. "Come back tomorrow. Noon, if you can manage it. I want to hear the noon light again while we talk."

I took my hand off the door. The stone stayed warm where I'd touched it, a handprint of heat that faded slow, like something letting go reluctantly.

"Ada," I said, before I could stop myself. "Why are you helping me? You've been in there fifty years. If it were me, I'd want the whole town to burn."

The rounds turned. Somewhere in them the child's voice wandered up into its high, untaught harmony, and came back down.

"Because," Ada said, "we've had fifty years to become a choir, and I'll tell you what a choir is, foreman. A choir is a group of people who have agreed that none of them gets to be alone with the song." And then, gently, in the tone of a piano teacher correcting a student's posture: "You've been down here ten minutes and you're already humming."

I wasn't. I was sure I wasn't.

Then I heard it — my own breath, in the dark, moving in four notes. Over and over. The notation on the door. The kettle deciding.

I walked out of that tunnel without running, which is the hardest thing I have ever done, and I sat in my truck until my hands stopped shaking, and then I did the only thing that made sense, which was the thing I'd been avoiding for two weeks.

I called the radio station. At five in the morning. August answered on the first ring, like they'd been sitting there all night with the phone in their hand, and before I could say anything at all, they said:

"She talked to you, didn't she. Ada Rill. She talked to you too."

*Too.*

"Wren," August said, and their voice had gone strange and quiet, the voice of a person standing at the edge of something they'd been digging toward for months. "There's a name crossed out in the ledger. Someone got out the night of the sealing. I've been trying to figure out who for three days, and I just — I just realized I already know. Everybody in this town knows her. She's been teaching piano on the hill for fifty years."

Down the line, faint, under August's voice, I could hear the station's old monitor speaker. And on it, so quiet it might have been my imagination, might have been bleed from a dream I hadn't woken up from, a round was turning. Four notes, patient, circling, waiting to be finished.

"Pick me up at eight," I said. "And August — bring the ledger. I think it's time somebody read every name out loud."
