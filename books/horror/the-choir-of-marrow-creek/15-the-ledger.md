## 15. The Ledger

The Historical Society opens at ten and closes at noon on Wednesdays, which tells you everything you need to know about how much Marrow Creek values its history. I got there at 9:52 and stood on the steps like a man waiting outside a confessional, except the priest inside was a card table of dead flies and a volunteer named Dot Fenwick who has been eighty years old for as long as I can remember.

"August Underhill," she said, unlocking the door. "Your grandfather used to bring me butterscotch."

"He did?"

"He did not. But he should have. Come in before the heat gets in."

The Historical Society is the old bank, which means it has a vault, which means the town's memory has always been kept somewhere you'd have to break into. Dot made coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during the Carter administration and left me alone with the archives, which was either trust or a trap, and after the last two weeks I no longer believed in the distinction.

I told her I was researching the flood. That was even true, in the way that a noose is technically a necklace.

* * *

Here is what I knew going in. Twelve years ago the old quarry settlement went under—not the quarry itself, which has been dry and ugly for a century, but the low streets below it, the ones built by the quarry families when the quarry was still eating men for a living. The flood took eleven houses and thirty-one people, according to the plaque by the bridge, and the plaque is very proud of that number, the way plaques are, like grief is a thing you can bronze.

What I did not know—what I had spent two weeks not knowing, at increasing volume, on air, with the drowned calling in to remind me—was why the water was there at all. Because here's the thing I'd finally said out loud on Thursday's broadcast, the thing that got the station's first angry letters in a decade: the quarry settlement sat on high ground. The flood maps didn't make sense. The water came uphill. I'd said it like a joke, the way you say terrible things, and then Denny Voss's voice had come through the board and said, *Ask them why they built the dam, August. Ask them what the dam was for,* and the joke had stopped being funny somewhere around my sternum.

So. The archives. The founding families. The Underhills, who founded the town along with the Pikes, the Merricks, the Coles, and a handful of others whose names are on the water tower like a hostage list.

Dot had the family papers in acid-free boxes, labeled in her careful hand. UNDERHILL, 1911–1949. UNDERHILL, 1950–1989. And a box with no label, just a question mark, which I took to be Dot's way of saying *I don't know what this is and I've decided not to find out,* which is the official town religion.

The question mark box was heavy. That should have been my first clue. Archives aren't heavy. Archives are the opposite of heavy; they're the weight of things already gone.

I opened it on the card table under the flickering fluorescent and the first thing I found was the ledger.

* * *

It was a church ledger originally—you could tell from the embossed cover, FIRST CONGREGATIONAL OF MARROW CREEK, PROPERTY OF—but someone had scraped the congregation's records out of it, or pasted new pages over the old, and the new pages were a different kind of record entirely.

It was a list. Names, dates, and a column on the right-hand margin that at first I took for damage, water damage, of course, everything in this town is water damage, until I looked closer and saw it wasn't damage at all.

It was musical notation.

Every name had a line of it beside it. A few notes each, hand-inked, precise as surgery. And beneath each line of notation, in the same hand or one of a few rotating hands, a word or two: *baritone. alto. second. lead.*

They hadn't written down who died. They'd written down who *sang.*

I turned pages with hands I didn't fully recognize as mine. The dates went back further than I expected—back before the flood, back before the dam, back to 1948, when the entries started appearing between what looked like minutes of a church committee meeting. *Motion to repair the steeple, carried. Motion regarding the Voss property, carried.* And then, in the margin, small and neat: *E. Voss, tenor. Verse assigned: second.*

Verse assigned. Like a chore rotation. Like bringing deviled eggs.

I want to tell you I understood it right away. I didn't. Understanding came the way the flood came—sideways, uphill, all at once when it was already too late. What I understood first was smaller and worse: I recognized the handwriting. The early entries were my grandfather's. I'd spent my childhood watching him write out broadcast logs in that same forward-leaning cursive, the *g's* like fishhooks, and here they were, fishhooks and all, fastening names to notes.

E. Voss, tenor. Denny Voss's grandfather, or great-uncle. The Vosses had been singing in this ledger since before I was born.

I kept turning. The entries got denser through the fifties and sixties. Whole families, verse by verse, voice by voice, the notation growing more elaborate—harmonies, I realized, harmonies being assigned and reassigned, a soprano moved to alto after some entry marked only *illness,* a baritone line left blank for a full year with the word *vacant* beside it, and then filled again with a new name, a Cole, a child, the notation smaller like the hand writing it had hesitated.

Children. There were children in the ledger. Ages noted, some of them. *M. Pike, age 9, descant.* Nine years old and already carrying a line of the song that kept something asleep under the town.

I sat in that vault with the dead flies and the Carter-administration coffee and read my grandfather's handwriting assigning a nine-year-old a descant, and I thought about every caller who'd come through my board in the last two weeks, every patient drowned voice asking me to *finish what your family started,* and I finally, finally understood what they meant. Not the sealing. Not yet. I understood it grammatically before I understood it morally: *your family* had a role in this. A standing role. A part.

The song had a conductor's chair, and it had been in my family since 1948.

* * *

The entries changed in the seventies. I could tell without reading a word, because the handwriting changed—my grandfather's fishhooks giving way to a rounder, harder script I didn't know, and the notation getting longer, more complex, the margin notes shifting from *baritone* and *alto* to things like *full round, all verses, nightly* and *do not miss a night, we nearly lost the week of the 14th.*

We nearly lost the week of the 14th. Lost it to what? To silence? To sleep? To a night somebody decided the song was superstition and stayed in bed?

Then came October 1974, and the ledger stopped being a choir roster and became something else.

The page for October 1974 is different paper. Thicker. Inserted, not written in sequence, like someone understood this page would need to survive apart from the rest. The heading, in that round hard script: *The Settling.*

And below it, names. Not voices now. Just names, in columns, with ages. Thirty-one of them.

I counted twice. Thirty-one. The number on the plaque by the bridge. The number the flood took, the town has said for twelve years, in its sleep, in its prayers, on its commemorative tote bags.

The flood didn't take them. The flood was the receipt.

I read the names and my mouth went dry around them. Vosses. Coles. Merricks. A whole column of Pikes, which I noted with the distant part of my brain that was already doing arithmetic about councilman Harlan Pike and his family's stake in all this—thirty-one names, and the Pikes had been on the singing side of the ledger for decades, and here were Pikes on the other side too, sealed behind a door with everyone else, because the song doesn't care whose blood carries the tune.

And there were children. Of course there were children. *R. Cole, age 4.* The ledger didn't assign her a verse. Four-year-olds don't carry lines. The ledger just listed her, the way you list a dependent, the way the notation column beside her name said only: *covered.*

Covered. By her family's voices, presumably. The way a child is covered by a blanket. The way a crime is covered by a town.

I got to the bottom of the first column, and that's where I found the crossed-out name.

* * *

Everyone else on the page was written clean. Final. The way you write the dead when you've made your peace with what you did to them. But one name, near the bottom of the second column, had a single line struck through it—deliberately, evenly, not in rage but in record-keeping—and beside it, in the margin, in smaller script, someone had written:

*Did not answer the knock. Gone before the door. Search suspended.*

Gone before the door. Someone had run. Someone had been on the list—on the *song,* which meant they'd been born into it, which meant running had cost them everything, family, town, name—and they'd run anyway, the night of the sealing, and thirty people had gone behind that door and this one hadn't, and the town had searched and then stopped, and then, I'd have bet my broadcast license, had never spoken of it again.

I put my finger under the crossed-out name to read it properly, and my hand was shaking hard enough that I had to press it flat against the page.

*Ada Rill.*

Miss Ada. Piano lessons in her front parlor, fifty years, two generations of Marrow Creek children plinking through *Chopsticks* under her patient hand. The woman who'd taught me middle C. The woman who, three days ago, had called the station—not on air, on the office line, a voice like dry paper saying only, *"August. When you're ready to learn it right, come see me. Not before. You'd only hurt yourself with half of it."*—and hung up before I could ask what she meant.

I'd thought it was a crank call. I'd thought, in the taxonomy of my current life, that a crank call was the *normal* option.

She'd been on the list. She'd been one of the sealed, by name, by blood, by verse—and she'd run. And she'd spent fifty years in the town that locked her family behind a door, teaching its children piano, keeping the fourth verse folded up inside her like a blade carried flat against the spine, waiting.

Waiting for what? For the last Underhill to be ready, apparently. I hadn't known whether to feel chosen or hunted. I still don't. It's both. It's the family business.

* * *

There was one more page. I almost missed it, because it wasn't a page at all but a loose sheet folded into the back cover, and when I opened it the handwriting hit me before the words did, because it was my grandmother's.

I know her hand the way you know a face. She wrote the grocery lists. She wrote the birthday cards, the ones I still have, the ones that said *love, Grandma* in letters that leaned like they were walking into wind. And here was that same windblown script, dated October 1974, the night of the Settling, and it was not a grocery list.

*It is done and I want it written in my hand so there is no hiding behind his. Harold sang the closing round at the door and it took, and the knocking stopped at 2 in the morning, and I will not pretend I did not weep, but I will also not pretend I did not sleep. That is the price. You sleep. You sleep like the dead sleep, which is to say you don't, but you tell yourself you do. To whoever holds this book after me: the song is a debt, not a gift. We did not save the town. We spent thirty-one people to save it, and a debt spent is still owed. The verse that names them was never taught to us. Harold said the sealed would keep it, that they'd have no choice, that a prisoner always learns the whole hymn. He said it like it was a mercy. Finish it or don't. But know that if you don't, the interest compounds.*

*The interest compounds.*

My grandmother. The woman who taught me to make biscuits. Who hummed while she made them—oh God, she hummed while she made them, all three verses, I know that now, I've known it since the switchboards lit up, the whole town humming in its kitchens—and she'd stood at that door and heard the knocking stop and called it sleep.

The last entry in the ledger was hers, and it wasn't a record. It was a bill. Passed down like the good china, like the broadcast logs, like the microphone I've been speaking into for ten years without once asking whose voice it was built to carry.

I sat in the vault for a long time after that. Dot came in once, looked at my face, and set down a fresh cup of coffee without a word, which is the most Marrow Creek thing that has ever happened. The town has always known when not to ask. That's how a thing like this works for fifty years. Not silence enforced. Silence *offered,* and accepted, and passed down with the recipe for biscuits.

The interest compounds. Twelve years of callers. Twelve years of drowned voices being polite on my frequency, patient, asking so nicely, and all of it interest accruing on a principal of thirty-one names, and the drainage crew is emptying the quarry week by week, and when the water goes, the collateral goes, and the bank comes calling.

I folded my grandmother's letter back into the ledger and took the whole book. Dot watched me tuck it under my arm and said, "Sign the card out," and I said, "Dot, this is evidence of—" and she said, "Sign. The card," and I signed the card. Some rituals survive anything.

* * *

It was dark when I got back to the station, and the parking lot had a car in it I didn't recognize: an old sedan, faded green, parked crooked, the way you park when your hands aren't steady. The office light was on. The office light is never on; I'm the only one with a key who works nights, and I was standing in the lot.

Through the window I could see someone at my desk, in my chair, under the dead microphone. Small. Old. White hair like a halo that had given up.

The door was unlocked. Of course it was. Everything in this town is unlocked; it's how they got to everyone's door in 1974.

Miss Ada Rill turned on the swivel chair, slow, the way her generation does everything, and looked at me with eyes that had been on the list and lived anyway.

"You found the book," she said. Not a question. She'd known I would. Maybe she'd left the box unlabeled herself, fifty years ago, a question mark in Dot's handwriting or her own, a message in a bottle set on a shelf to wait for the last Underhill.

"Sit down, August," she said, and her hands, on her knees, were trembling. "We need to talk about what your grandmother owed. And we need to talk fast—because I heard the mixer trucks on the highway, and your Mr. Pike isn't waiting for the water to drain."

She was right. I'd passed them too, on the drive in—Pike's trucks, lined up at the quarry gate in the dark, headlights off, engines idling.

They weren't waiting for the drainage schedule.

They were going to pour.
