## 17. Concrete Night

Harlan Pike called an emergency session of the drainage crew at six a.m. on a Tuesday, which is how you know a man has stopped listening to anyone. Emergency sessions before coffee are never about engineering. They're about fear wearing a hard hat.

"County's been calling," he said, standing at the head of the trailer like the trailer was a pulpit. "State too. There are people who think we should stop the project." He let that hang there, the way he did, like stopping was a thing only cowards and communists considered. "We're not stopping. We're finishing. Tonight we pour."

I raised my hand. I'm foreman; it's technically my job to raise my hand.

"Wren."

"You can't pour over a load-bearing anomaly you haven't surveyed. The door is structural. If the chamber behind it collapses, we lose the tunnel, we lose the drainage line, and we lose—" I almost said *the song*, which would have gotten me a psych evaluation in front of the crew, so I course-corrected. "—we lose months."

"We're not pouring over the door," Pike said, with the patience of a man explaining arithmetic to a child he suspects is stupid on purpose. "We're pouring a plug. Forty feet back from the face. Seal the tunnel approach. Nobody touches the door. Nobody even looks at it."

"And then what? It's still there."

"It'll be *entombed*," he said, and there it was—the word he'd been carrying around for weeks like a stone in his boot. Entombed. You could hear how much he liked it. Some men want a legacy. Harlan Pike wanted a lid.

I looked around the trailer. Nine faces. Delmar, who'd been with the crew eleven years and hadn't spoken since I dragged him out of the tunnel with harmonies coming out of his mouth like water from a cracked pipe. The Ruiz brothers, who took this job because it paid time-and-a-half and had stopped asking questions somewhere around the night their radios started playing the third verse backward. Young Theo Okafor—no relation, though he calls me Auntie now, after everything—which I try not to read into.

Nobody met my eyes except Delmar, who doesn't meet anything anymore, but whose head turned toward me when I spoke, slow as a weathervane finding wind.

"Fine," I said. "But I want it on record that concrete is a suggestion, not an argument."

"On record," Pike agreed pleasantly. "Wren Okafor objects. Noted for posterity." And he smiled at me like posterity was something he owned stock in.

* * *

The mixer arrived at dusk on a flatbed, forty yards of it, drum turning with a sound like a stomach digesting something it regretted. Pike had hired two extra pump operators out of Corvallis—men who didn't know Marrow Creek, didn't know the story, didn't know why the whole town had gone quiet in that particular way towns go quiet when half of them have been sleepwalking toward a quarry in their nightgowns.

I walked the tunnel one last time before the pour. Forty feet from the door, Pike's surveyors had staked the plug line with orange flags, little bright teeth in the dark. Past them, the door itself sat in its alcove, and I made myself look at it, because looking felt like a discipline, like if I kept looking it couldn't sneak up on me.

It hadn't changed. Still sealed from the inside—hinges fused, no handle, no keyhole, just that line of notation carved across the face, the one I'd rubbed onto paper my first week and played through my phone speaker while forty feet of rock hummed back at me like a struck bell. I'd stopped playing it. Obviously. But I still carried the rubbing, folded in eight in my breast pocket, because some part of me had decided it was evidence, and evidence is the last religion I have left.

Through the hairline crack along the door's edge—I'd found it in chapter fourteen of my own private nightmare, the night I pressed my ear to the stone and heard them singing rounds, twelve years sustained, patient as geology—I could hear nothing tonight. That was worse. Silence from inside meant they were listening to us instead.

"Foreman?" One of the Corvallis guys, clipboard out. "We good to stage?"

"We're good," I said, and hated how much I sounded like Pike.

* * *

The first yard went in clean. Honestly, it nearly worked. That's the thing nobody tells you about futility—it looks exactly like competence right up until it doesn't. The pump hummed, the line throbbed, and concrete slumped out of the hose into the forms like the world's grayest pudding, and I stood there thinking: maybe mass is the answer. Maybe the dead can be outvoted by tonnage. Maybe every horror story is just a story told by people who ran out of cement.

Then the mixer jammed.

Not gradually. One second the drum was turning, the next it seized with a clang that rang down the tunnel like a churchbell falling downstairs. The operator killed the engine and climbed up top with a flashlight, and I heard him say, "Huh," in the tone men use right before they quit.

"What," I said.

"There's—" He shined the light into the open hatch. "There's stuff in the aggregate."

"Rocks are in aggregate. That's what aggregate means."

"Not rocks." He wasn't being difficult. He was being scared, which sounds different. "Come look."

I did not want to come look. I came look.

The drum's interior, under the work light, was full of ordinary wet gray mix—and threaded through it, pale and unmistakable, were things that used to be buttons. Bone buttons, shell buttons, a brass uniform button with a quarry company stamp worn nearly smooth. A wedding ring, gold, caught in the fins of the drum like a fish in a net. And small, dense shapes I refused to identify until my brain identified them anyway: teeth. Dozens of them. Human teeth, distributed evenly through four yards of ready-mix, as though the flood silt we'd been dredging for months had finally decided to introduce itself properly.

"That's not possible," the operator said. "That's not—we screen the aggregate. There's screening."

"I know," I said.

"These came from *somewhere*."

"They came from the old settlement," I said. "They've been down there twelve years. They're just... punctual."

He looked at me. I looked at him. Neither of us wrote it up.

* * *

Here's where I should describe the names. Everyone always asks about the names, afterward, in the way people ask about accidents—they want the detail that makes it a story instead of a thing that happened to them.

The names came later, during the second attempt, after we'd cleared the jammed drum by hand—nine of us with shovels and buckets, transferring mix into the pump hopper like the world's grimmest barn raising. It was while we shoveled that the sound started. Not from the mixer. From the aggregate itself, from the wet mass in the hopper, a low churning voice-noise, and threaded through it—

My name.

Not shouted. Not whispered. Spoken the way you speak a name when you're checking a list. *Wren.* Then Delmar's. Then the Ruiz brothers, both, in order of age. Then Theo's, gentle, almost fond. Each name surfacing once, in turn, like bubbles rising through setting concrete, and between each name a pause exactly long enough for the named person to feel personally addressed.

Delmar dropped his shovel. Theo dropped his. The older Ruiz brother, Mateo, stood very still and said, "No. No, I don't accept this," which is more composure than I managed, because what I said was nothing, because my mouth had gone dry as the chamber behind that door.

Because here's what I understood, standing over a hopper full of talking gravel: the names weren't a threat. They were a *roll call*. Attendance. The song is an accounting—I know that now, though I didn't fully know it then—and whatever waited behind the door was taking attendance, marking who was present for the pour, adding us to its ledger the way our town had once added its neighbors to theirs.

"It knows we're here," Theo whispered.

"It's known since we broke ground," I said. "This is just it being polite."

* * *

Pike, to his credit—or to his damage, it's hard to tell the difference in that man—ordered the pour to continue. "Rocks talk," he said, over the radio, from the surface, where the air was presumably still breathable. "Flood silt gets in everything. Pour."

So we poured. And the second batch went smoother than the first, and the third, and by midnight the plug was chest-high in the forms, a gray wall growing across the tunnel throat, and I let myself believe again, the way you let yourself believe in a lottery ticket. The pump throbbed. The level rose. Forty feet behind us, the door said nothing, and silence, I told myself, was surrender.

Two crew members joined the sleepwalkers that night. I want to be precise about that, because "joined the sleepwalkers" is the phrase the town uses, and phrases like that are how communities launder horror into logistics.

The first was young Ruiz—Esteban, twenty-three, funny, fast with a trowel. At 12:40 a.m. he set down his float, walked past me without seeing me, and climbed out of the tunnel with the unhurried gait of a man late for nothing. I found him an hour later at the quarry rim, standing among the thirty-odd others—the bakers and teachers and councilmen's wives who'd been drifting out of their houses for weeks—facing the water line, singing under his breath. Harmonies. Parts he'd never learned. His eyes were open and dry and aimed at something below the waterline, and when I took his arm he weighed what a sleeping man weighs, all his cooperation withdrawn at once, and he came with me the way furniture comes with you.

The second was the Corvallis pump operator. He didn't even make it out of the tunnel. At 2:15 I turned around and his station was empty, the hose still pulsing in his absence, spitting concrete in neat arcs like a heart beating without a body. We found him at the plug face, palms flat against the wet wall he himself had built, pressing his ear to it. Listening. When I pulled him back, his hands came away printed with the texture of the aggregate—little fossil impressions of button and bone—and he looked at his palms, and then at me, and smiled.

That smile is going to live in me a long time. It wasn't creepy. That's the hell of it. It was *relieved*. It was the smile of a man who'd been trying to hear something all night and finally got close enough.

* * *

By three a.m. the plug was done. Full height, full width, cured enough to hold its shape, a gray monolith squatting in the tunnel like a tombstone for a thing that wasn't dead. Pike came down at dawn with a county photographer and had us pose in front of it, hard hats off, arms crossed, the whole civic-tableau routine. "Marrow Creek solves its problems," he said for the camera.

And that's when the photographer lowered his camera and said, "Who did the lettering?"

Nobody answered, because nobody knew what he meant, until we all turned and looked, all at once, the way cattle turn.

The face of the fresh concrete—our concrete, poured and floated and finished by human hands, screeded smooth by Esteban before he left us—was carved. Fine lines, deep and crisp, cut into concrete that had been wet six hours and should have taken a week to cure hard enough to hold an inscription. Lines of musical notation. Staff lines running the full width of the plug, bar after bar after bar, and notes upon them, dozens of them, precise as engraving.

Mateo put his finger near—not touching, nobody touched it—and traced the air above the first phrase. "That's the same," he said hoarsely. "That's the same as the door."

"No," I said. Because I'd spent weeks with that rubbing in my pocket, weeks staring at the door's notation until I could read it the way you read your own address. "It's not the same. The door has one line. One verse." My mouth kept going without my permission, the way mouths do at the scene of the inevitable. "This is longer. This is four verses."

Four verses. Three known, sung by the living for a hundred years to keep something beneath the water asleep. And a fourth that existed only among the sealed—only among the drowned who hadn't drowned—taught in fragments over August Underhill's late-night frequency, hidden in a station safe, memorized unsung for fifty years by a piano teacher who'd escaped through a door that locked from the inside.

The concrete had it all. Every note. Cut into the plug meant to bury it, spelled out in the very material of the silencing, the way a hostage writes a message on the inside of the tape over her own mouth.

"Get a trowel," Pike said. "Grind it off. Float it smooth."

Mateo brought a trowel. He ground at the first staff line and the line didn't blur. It deepened. He ground harder and the notation surfaced *through* the abrasion, re-cutting itself under the steel, crisp and patient, and Mateo threw the trowel down and walked out of the tunnel and did not come back, and none of us blamed him, and several of us envied him.

* * *

I stayed till sunrise. Somebody had to log it, and logging things is my last religion too.

At 6:04 a.m., with the tunnel empty except for me and the plug and the door forty feet beyond it, the crack along the door's edge exhaled. I heard it—a soft pressure-change, a sigh through stone—and then, faint, from inside, the rounds began again. But different this morning. Looser. Brighter. Twelve years of sustained note suddenly inflected with something I could only call *anticipation*, and underneath the rounds, so quiet I had to press my palm flat against the cold rock to catch it, a new voice. Thin. High. Patient in a way that made my skin try to leave my body.

Counting.

Not sheep, not seconds. Names. Working through a list, unhurried, contented, the way you count change. And I stood there in the gray dawn light with my hand on the stone and realized the list was getting shorter every night, and that the pause after each name—the pause where the counted person was supposed to answer *present*—was starting to fill in on its own.

From the other side.

I took my hand off the door. I walked out of the tunnel. In the parking lot, my phone buzzed: a message from August Underhill, whom I'd traded notes with like two kids passing contraband in study hall.

*Ada says the verse has to be sung AT the door, not AT the town. Pike's planning something for tomorrow night — he bought airtime. All of it. Every station on the dial.*

I stood in the gravel with the sun coming up over a quarry full of sleeping singers and a concrete monument that had transcribed itself, and I typed back the only status report that mattered:

*Concrete didn't take. It's learning the song faster than we are.*

Then, because the truth deserved its own line:

*And I think it just finished the fourth verse.*
