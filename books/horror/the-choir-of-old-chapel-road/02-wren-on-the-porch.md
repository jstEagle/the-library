## 2. Wren on the Porch

The light was going out of Old Chapel Road the way it always did in October — not fading so much as being drawn away, pulled down the street toward the chapel like water finding a drain. Dominic noticed that now. He noticed everything now. Three days in the storm drains with a laser measure and a headlamp had rewired his attention, and he had come home each evening with his notebooks fuller and his sleep thinner, the way a man comes home from courting someone who frightens him.

He climbed his porch steps with his boots crusted in orange silt and his mind still down there, in the brick throat beneath the road, running the numbers again. They still wouldn't match. He'd measured the tunnel twice — once with the laser, once the old way, with a cloth tape and a pencil like his father had taught him — and both times the passage came out longer than the space it occupied. Forty feet of tunnel inside thirty feet of earth. He'd written the discrepancy in the margin of his survey and then, in a smaller hand, as if the smaller hand might make it less true: *the tunnel is bigger than the hole it's in.*

That was when he saw the boy.

He was sitting on the bottom step, at the edge of the porch light's reach, knees drawn up, chin resting on them, watching the street with the patient stillness of a child who has been told to wait and has decided to make waiting his whole occupation. A small boy in a white sailor collar, the wide collar lying flat across narrow shoulders, dark shorts, bare feet despite the cold. His hair was fine and brown and cut in a way no one had cut a boy's hair in fifty years.

Dominic stopped with one foot still on the top step.

He knew that collar. He knew the way the boy's heels hooked over the edge of the step, the way his thumbs hooked into his armpits when he was thinking, the exact cowlick at the crown of his head that stuck up no matter what. He knew him the way you know a scar — not because you study it but because it has always been part of you.

"Wren," Dominic said, and his voice came out strange, half a laugh, half a prayer.

The boy turned his head. He had a thin face, a serious mouth, and eyes that were gray the way the tunnel bricks were gray, and when he saw Dominic his whole face changed — not into a child's grin, which is what Dominic had expected, what thirty years of forgetting had promised him, but into something older. Something that looked, for a moment, like pity.

"Hello, Dominic," Wren said.

His voice was exactly as it had been. That was the thing that undid him. Not the face, not the collar — the *voice*, high and clear and slightly hoarse at the edges, a boy's voice with a boy's small imperfections, the voice that had narrated a thousand afternoons in the hollow behind the Hendersons' fence, the voice that had promised him, at seven years old, that the dark under the porch was nothing to be afraid of because Wren had already been down there and checked.

"You're real," Dominic said. He came down the steps slowly, the way you approach a deer, the way you approach a fire. "You're — God, you're actually real. I thought —" He laughed, and the laugh shook. "I thought I made you up. I told everyone I made you up. Mom asked about you for years and I told her I'd grown out of you."

"You didn't make me up," Wren said. "But you told a good story. Stories keep things safe. That's what they're for."

Dominic crouched down a few feet away, unwilling to close the distance, unwilling to test whether the boy would hold still under his hand or come apart like smoke. Up close the details were unbearable in their precision: a scab on one knee, half-healed, the exact scab from the summer Dominic was eight. A faint smell off him, chalk and river water and something green, like the inside of a snapped stem. Children smelled like that. Dominic had forgotten that children smelled like that.

"How are you here?" he asked. "You haven't changed. I've changed, Wren. I'm forty-one years old and I have a bad knee and you're still —" he gestured, helpless, "— eight."

Wren considered this with the gravity he had always given large questions, the gravity he'd given *where do you go when I'm asleep* and *is the chapel haunted, really truly haunted.*

"I'm not eight," he said finally. "I just stopped being allowed to be anything else."

The words went into Dominic like a splinter, small and clean and deep.

* * *

They sat on the steps together, the man and the boy, and for a while it was almost sweet. That was the trap of it, Dominic understood later — the sweetness was real, and that was what made the rest so unforgivable. Wren asked about the hollow behind the Hendersons' fence (gone, a deck there now), and about the dog they'd imagined together, the enormous invisible mastiff called Admiral (Dominic had forgotten Admiral and wept a little, quietly, at getting him back), and about whether Dominic still kept his notebooks, and Dominic had gone inside — leaving the boy alone on the step, trusting him there the way he'd trusted him at seven — and brought out the whole shelf of them, thirty years of documentation, and Wren had turned the pages with careful, chalk-smelling fingers and said, "You were always writing things down. Even then. You wrote down everything I told you."

"You told me good things," Dominic said.

"I told you true things," Wren said. "It's not always the same."

The street had gone quiet around them in a way that Dominic's body registered before his mind did. Old Chapel Road was never loud, but it was never *silent* — there were always dogs, always the highway's distant wash, always someone's television murmuring through a wall. Now there was nothing. The silence had texture, like felt, like the inside of a coffin, and Dominic realized he had been hearing it for several minutes without letting himself know.

He looked up the street.

The Hollands' porch light was off. He was almost sure it had been on — old man Holland kept it burning from dusk to dawn, had done it for years, said the dark on that road was bad for his heart. It was dark now, the whole house dark, though the hour was barely past six.

Then the Pruitts' light. Then the one across the street, the Kowalczyks'. Then the next, and the next, going out one by one up the road toward the chapel, each one dying with a small flicker — not the clean snap of a switch but a stutter, a struggle, as if something were being smothered rather than turned off — until the only light left on Old Chapel Road was the one above Dominic's own door, and the last gray rind of dusk in the west.

"Okay," Dominic said. His mouth had gone dry. "Okay, Wren. What is this. What's happening."

The boy didn't answer at once. He was looking down the street at the dark houses with an expression that didn't belong on a child's face at all — an expression of long, worn familiarity, the look of someone watching weather he had seen a thousand times and would see a thousand more.

"They always do that," he said. "When one of them can see. The lights go out so they can listen better. They're very polite about it. They were polite in 1897, too."

"Who's *they*, Wren?"

The boy turned and looked at him, and in the porch light his gray eyes were not a child's eyes at all. They were the tunnel. They were the tally marks scratched in brick. They were old the way the chapel was old, old the way the town's money was old.

"You found the door," Wren said. "Now they know you can see."

"I don't understand —"

"You will. You're good at understanding. You write everything down." Wren unfolded himself from the step and stood, small and straight, the sailor collar white against the dark street, and Dominic felt the full vertigo of it — thirty years of a made-up boy standing on his porch in the flesh, and the flesh was the frightening part, the flesh meant the boy had never been made up, which meant everything else Dominic had told himself was also a story, and stories, Wren had said, keep things safe, and nothing on this street was safe tonight.

"Three heirs," Wren said. "That's all I can tell you. Three heirs, and only two signatures. You'll find it where the dead keep their papers." He paused, and for one moment the hoarse little voice cracked, and he was eight years old again, and terrified. "Dominic. When you find it — don't read it at night. Read it in the daytime. In front of people. Promise me."

"Why? Wren, why —"

Down the street, in the total dark where the chapel stood, something knocked. Once. Politely. The sound carried the whole length of the road as if the road itself had been built to carry it, and Dominic's skin crawled with the animal certainty that whatever had knocked was not asking to be let in, but letting someone know it had arrived.

Wren was already backing toward the steps' edge, toward the dark, and the dark was taking him the way dusk had taken the light — not fading, being drawn.

"Three heirs," he said. "Two signatures. And Dominic —" the gray eyes caught the porch light one last time, "— they know your name too. It's in the same book."

Then the porch light flickered, stuttered, and went out, and the boy was gone, and Old Chapel Road held its breath around Dominic Winterbourne in the dark, waiting to see what he would write down next.
