## 4. What Was Taken in '74

Kestrel found her at the Fox and Bell, because of course it was called that, because of course the village had never once been subtle with itself. Maren sat in the corner booth with a glass of something amber and a look on her face like a woman who had been waiting fifty years for someone to ask the right question and was furious that the someone had finally come.

He set his recorder on the table between them. She looked at it the way you'd look at a snake.

"Off," she said.

"It's just—"

"Off, boy. What I've got to say isn't folklore."

He turned it off. He left it on the table anyway, a small black monument to the version of himself that had come to Hollowmere to collect songs instead of sins. Then he sat down across from her and waited, because he'd learned that from her too, back when she was teaching him the lullaby — the half lullaby, the one she'd cut off like a hand on a throat.

Maren drank. Her hand wasn't steady. He had never seen her hand not steady, and that frightened him more than the bell had.

"You found it," she said. "The minutes."

"I found my grandfather's name."

"Aye." She turned the glass a quarter turn on the table, precise, ceremonial, like she was setting a clock. "And you've been up the hill. To the house."

"How did you—"

"Because you've got the look. Everyone gets the look after the house looks back at them." She drank again, deeper. "Sit still. I'm going to tell you a story, and you're not going to interrupt, and when I'm done you're going to wish to God you'd stayed in London recording sea shanties from people with no teeth."

He sat still.

"In 1974," Maren said, "the bell came back."

* * *

Maren had been thirty-one that year. She remembered it the way you remember a wound — not the moment of cutting but the whole season of it, the heat, the smell, the way the light came through the kitchen window on the last ordinary morning.

The bell had been melted in 1911. Everyone knew that. There was a photograph in the parish archive of the foundry men standing beside the broken pieces, proud as if they'd won a war. The vicar of the time had declared the thing done, finished, silenced, and for sixty-three years Hollowmere had believed him, because believing is what villages do instead of thinking.

Then, in the spring of '74, old Tam Osgood heard it toll at midnight. One stroke. Just one. And in the morning Tam's hair had gone white as frost, and he sat in his garden and told anyone who'd listen: *It said my name. It said my name, and it said what I took.*

Nobody asked Tam what he'd taken. That was the first mercy the village failed to extend, and it would not be the last.

The bell tolled again three nights later. Then two nights after that. Then the next night. Always one stroke, always midnight, always a name inside the sound — not heard with the ears, Maren said, but set down inside you like a stone dropped into a well, so you felt it land.

"It called six names that spring," she said. "Six. And the six of them — we met in the vestry, after the sixth tolling, and we didn't pray. You understand? Six grown Christians met in the house of God and nobody prayed, because we all knew what the bell was. It wasn't a warning. It was a *ledger*. It was reading us out. Every name it called, it called with a debt attached."

Kestrel's mouth was dry. "Debts for what?"

"For what we'd taken." Maren's voice dropped, and the pub noise seemed to lean away from her, giving her room. "The Bell House kept accounts. That was its whole nature, the way a spider's nature is silk. Things taken from the dead and never returned — it held the tally. And in '74 it started calling in the debts."

* * *

Kestrel drove home with the windows down even though it was cold, because the car smelled of Maren's whisky and his own sweat. He kept seeing his grandfather's name in the vestry minutes. *E. Sandoval.* His grandfather, who had taught him to whistle through his thumbs, who had carried him on his shoulders through the harvest fair, who had died when Kestrel was nine and been mythologized ever since by a boy who needed his dead to be good.

The bell tolled as he parked outside the cottage. One stroke, at midnight exactly, though his watch said 11:58 and he would spend a long time not thinking about that.

He sat in the car and listened to the silence after, and the silence had a shape, and the shape was a question.

* * *

Maren came to the cottage the next evening. Not the pub — the cottage, uninvited, carrying a bottle of her own and a face like a woman walking to her own trial. She'd clearly been drinking before she arrived and intended to keep drinking after, and Kestrel understood that this was armor, not appetite.

"Six names in the spring of '74," she said, without preamble. "Tam Osgood. Myself. The churchwarden's father. Two others dead now, and one more." She looked at him. "Your grandfather."

The floor did not tilt. Kestrel had expected it to tilt, the way it does in stories, but instead everything stayed horribly level and still, the way a room goes still when something inside it has decided about you.

"What did he take?" Kestrel asked.

"Nothing." Maren poured two glasses with a hand that shook only a little. "That's the hell of it. He took *nothing*. He was the lantern. The others dug; he held the light. And the bell called his name anyway, because the house doesn't care whether you swing the spade or only show the way. A debt is a debt. Witnessing is a kind of taking — you take the truth and carry it, and if you never give it back, it rots in you."

She drank. He didn't touch his glass.

"There was a child," Maren said. "Edith. She died in 1908, six years old, fever. And her mother — a widow, half mad with grief, the village said, though I've wondered all my life whether she was mad at all or only the only honest woman in Hollowmere — she wouldn't put the girl in the churchyard. She buried her in the Bell House garden. Said the house had been asking for her. Said the tallies on the walls were counting down to a name and the name was Edith."

Kestrel thought of the child's shoe on the mantel. He said nothing. His throat had closed.

"For sixty-three years the house kept her," Maren said. "And the village lived quiet. No bell. No ledger. Whatever arrangement the house and the dead had made, it held. And then in '74 the bell woke, and it began to call *our* names — the six of us who'd done things, small things, mean things, the ordinary cruelties of a small place — and the vicar of the time, he got it into his head that the house had woken *hungry*. That it wanted more than it had. That it would keep calling until it had a name for everyone."

"So you decided to feed it."

"We decided to *cheat* it." Maren's eyes were wet and furious. "There's a difference, boy, and the difference is everything. We went up the hill at night — six of us, your grandfather with the lantern, God forgive him, he held it so steady, I can still see his hands — and we dug the child out of the garden where her mother had laid her, and we carried her down, and we buried her under the altar of St. Brannoc's. Consecrated ground. Church ground. We thought — we *told* ourselves — that the church's claim would outrank the house's. That the house couldn't follow onto holy land. That the bell would stop."

"And it did."

"Fifty years," Maren said. "Fifty years of quiet. We told ourselves we'd saved the village. We told ourselves the child was better off under the altar, prayed over every Sunday, than rotting alone up that hill." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Kestrel had ever heard from her. "We told ourselves so many things. That's what guilt is, Kestrel. It's property. You inherit it. Your grandfather held the lantern and the debt came down to you like the cottage, like the name, like the blood."

The fire had burned low. Kestrel looked at his own hands and saw, superimposed over them, a memory he'd never had: an old man's steady hands, a lantern, a hole in the ground opening like a mouth.

"The bell's ringing again," he said.

"Aye."

"And it's getting closer together."

Maren stood. She took her coat off the chair with the care of someone handling something fragile, and at the door she stopped, and she did not turn around, and she said:

"It called six names in '74 before we cheated it. It's called four since you came back to Hollowmere. Do the arithmetic, boy. It's not reading the old debts anymore. It's reading *yours* — yours and everyone the old debts came down to. And when it gets to the end of the list—" She finally turned, and her face in the doorway was old and terrified and, beneath the terror, unbearably kind. "When it gets to the end of the list, it will want what's under the altar. And God help us, I think it will want *you* to carry her home."

The door closed behind her. Outside, far up the hill, the bell tolled once — and for the first time, Kestrel heard the name inside it.

It was his.
