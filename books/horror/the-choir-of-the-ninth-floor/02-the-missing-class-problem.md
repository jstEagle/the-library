## 2. The Missing-Class Problem

The roster was waiting on his desk when Mirabel arrived at 7:14 in the morning, a single sheet of paper laid square against the blotter, corners aligned with the desk's edges as though someone had measured them. He hadn't asked for a printed roster. He'd assumed, the way new teachers assume things, that he would pull one from the system himself, that the system would be some creaking relic of software from the previous decade, and that he would spend his first week reconciling misspelled names and duplicate entries like every other teacher at every other school.

Instead: thirty-one names, typed, alphabetical, each with a student ID and a locker assignment. The paper smelled faintly of toner and something else beneath it—something mineral, like chalk dust that had been sitting in a closed room for a long time.

He read the names twice. *Abernathy, Cole. Ashford, Priya. Bellamy, June.* On through the alphabet to *Wrenfield, Tobias*, and then back up again, because counting was what he did when his hands needed something honest. Thirty-one. He counted a third time with the tip of his pen touching each line, an old habit from a childhood spent in houses where doors multiplied if you stopped paying attention, and the number held. Thirty-one students in fourth-period American Literature, room 9C.

Room 9C. He still hadn't found it. The ninth floor's corridor had offered him, yesterday, rooms 9A and 9B and then a stretch of wall with a water fountain and a fire extinguisher and no door at all, as though the building had simply run out of interest in numbering things. Voss had waved off the question with the ease of a man swatting a fly he'd been swatting for years. *"Renumbering after the annex construction. You'll be in the old seminar room until the dust settles."* The old seminar room turned out to be 9B, which meant, presumably, that 9C existed somewhere behind the plaster, waiting its turn to be remembered.

Mirabel folded the roster into his jacket pocket, where it sat over his heart like a small, incorrect document, and went down to the faculty lounge to make coffee he didn't drink.

* * *

The lounge occupied a corner of the second floor with windows facing the quad, and by eight-fifteen it held the full morning congregation: Devi Okafor already at the whiteboard, writing equations no one would ever erase because no one understood them; Hollis the groundskeeper passing through with mud on his boots and a nod that managed to include everyone and commit to no one; Ruth, who did not belong in a faculty lounge but came anyway, carrying a mop she never used indoors, standing near the coffee machine the way lighthouse keepers stand near their lamps.

And Voss, holding court by the window with a mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST FISHERMAN, a joke so worn it had gone soft.

"Blackwood." Voss smiled at him across the room, and the smile arrived a half-second before the rest of his face agreed to it. "Settling in?"

"Finding my way." Mirabel poured coffee he had no intention of drinking, because the ritual of holding a warm cup gave his hands employment. "I had a question about my roster, actually. Fourth period lists thirty-one students."

"Good enrollment," Voss said. "We're proud of our numbers."

"Twenty-two showed up yesterday."

The room did not go silent. That was the strange thing. In any other faculty lounge, in any other school, a sentence like that would have dropped a small cold stone into the conversation, and someone would have laughed or frowned or said *kids these days*. Instead the room continued exactly as it had been continuing—Devi's marker squeaking, Hollis's boots scuffing toward the door—and yet Mirabel felt the temperature of the attention shift, the way you feel eyes on the back of your neck without turning around. Everyone had heard him. Everyone had decided, simultaneously and without consultation, not to have heard him.

"First-week absences," Voss said comfortably. "Schedules in flux. Transfers, family holidays. It'll settle."

"Nine students is a lot of flux."

"It's Halloway," Voss said, as though that were an explanation, and perhaps to him it was. "Boarding schools breathe differently than day schools, Blackwood. Students arrive late, leave early, move through us like weather. You'll learn the patterns."

Ruth, by the coffee machine, looked at her shoes.

It was Devi who broke the pattern, and she did it without looking up from her whiteboard, in the flat tone of a woman asking about the weather in a language she didn't quite trust. "Which nine?" she said.

Voss's mug paused halfway to his mouth.

"Of your missing nine," Devi continued, capping her marker with a click that sounded very loud. "For my seating charts. I like to know who's actually in the building."

"Okafor." Voss said her name the way a chess player says *check*—not hostile, just noting the position of a piece. "You'll have your rosters Thursday, same as everyone. Accuracy over speed. That's the Halloway way." And then, to Mirabel, with the smile again, arriving first: "Give them a week. If anyone's truly lost, they'll find their way back to you. They always do."

*They always do.* The phrase followed Mirabel out of the lounge and up two flights of stairs, and by the time he reached the ninth floor he had turned it over enough times to notice what was wrong with it. Not *they'll come back*. Not *we'll track them down*. *They always find their way back*—as though the missing were not missing at all but merely wandering, as though the school itself were the fixed point and the children the ones with poor homing instincts.

Room 9B had twenty-two desks. He counted them before first period, because counting was prayer and arithmetic was exorcism, and the number matched his attendance sheet exactly, twenty-two bodies for twenty-two chairs, as though the room itself had been furnished to accommodate only the students who would actually arrive. As though somewhere, a supply order had been placed decades ago for precisely this many desks, and no one had thought to add nine more.

He taught *The Lottery* to juniors who took notes in pencil and never once looked out the window, and when the bell rang they filed out in an orderly hush, and Mirabel stood alone in room 9B among the twenty-two desks and felt, for the first time since accepting the post, the specific quality of the silence in a room built for more people than it held.

* * *

The library occupied the basement level, which struck Mirabel as architecturally backward—libraries belonged in towers, in attics, in high bright places—but Halloway's library had the deep-carpeted hush of a place that had made peace with being underground. The librarian, a narrow man named Pell whose cardigan predated color photography, stamped returns without looking up and pointed toward the yearbooks with a finger that had clearly done so ten thousand times.

"Archives are open till five," he said. "No food, no pens, cotton gloves in the drawer."

"I'm looking for class photos. Historical ones."

"Shelf six." A pause, the stamp hovering. "Curriculum research?"

"Something like that. I'm new. I like knowing a school's history before I teach inside it."

Pell looked at him then, properly, for the first time, and his eyes had the flat courtesy of a man who has answered this question before and been sorry both times. "Shelf six," he repeated. "Blue spines are even years."

The yearbooks ran back to 1931 in unbroken sequence, and Mirabel pulled them with the reverence of a man handling bones. 1962: rows of blazered boys and girls squinting into sun. 1968: sideburns and peace signs inked into margins by hands long grown. He worked forward, and the school changed around him decade by decade—hemlines rising, hair lengthening, the photography shifting from formal grids to candid sprawl—and everything was normal, aggressively normal, normal the way a stage set is normal, until he reached 1974.

The spine of the 1974 yearbook was blue. It was also, he noticed as he drew it from the shelf, slightly tighter than its neighbors, as though the binding had swollen, or as though the book had been handled less, or as though the shelf itself pressed against it with particular care.

He carried it to a reading table under a green-shaded lamp and opened it to the junior class.

The page was titled THE CLASS OF 1975 — JUNIORS, and beneath the title, where the photograph should have been, there was nothing. Not a gap—the photo had not been removed, the paper had not been cut away. There was a photograph-shaped region of solid black ink, applied with a brush or a pen of fine control, covering the image entirely, edge to edge, bleeding slightly into the gutter of the book. Someone had inked over the entire class picture with the patience of a monk illuminating a manuscript in reverse, and they had done it so carefully that the black rectangle sat within the photo's border like a negative space, a doorway cut into the page.

Below the photo, the individual portraits remained untouched. Thirty-one small faces in three rows, each with a name beneath it. Mirabel counted them without meaning to. Thirty-one.

His pen was out of his pocket and in his hand before he'd decided to move. He compared the names against the roster folded over his heart. *Abernathy, Cole.* Third row, second portrait, a boy with a cowlick and a grin missing one tooth. *Ashford, Priya.* Second row, far left, dark-eyed, unsmiling. *Bellamy, June.* First row, center, the smallest face on the page, wearing the expression of someone listening to something the camera couldn't hear.

Every name on his fourth-period roster appeared in the 1975 junior class portraits. Every single one. Which was impossible, unless—

Unless the roster was wrong. Unless some clerical error forty years deep had propagated forward, a database seeded from a corrupted record, thirty-one ghosts copied into the present tense. That was the rational explanation. Mirabel held onto it the way a drowning man holds a plank, and the plank held, mostly, right up until he noticed the handwriting.

Someone had written in the margin beside the inked-over photograph, in pencil so faint he had to tilt the page into the lamplight to catch it, in a child's careful cursive:

*nine of us still practice*

He sat very still. The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the stacks, a book settled on its shelf with a sound like a sigh.

When he looked up, Pell was watching him from behind the circulation desk, and the librarian's face wore the same expression Voss's smile had worn—the expression of a man who knew exactly what Mirabel had found, had known it for years, and had already composed the sentence that would make it harmless.

"Old books," Pell said gently, "pick up a lot of graffiti over the years."

"Thirty-one names," Mirabel said. "On my roster. For a class meeting in 2024."

"Database errors happen." Pell extended his gloved hand for the yearbook. "I can flag it for the registrar, if you like. She's very thorough."

"That would be good," Mirabel heard himself say, because institutional politeness was a current and it was easier to float than to swim against it, and he surrendered the 1974 yearbook and watched Pell slide it back onto shelf six, between 1973 and 1975, pressing it home with both hands as though tucking in a child.

* * *

He walked the long way back to the ninth floor, past the trophy case, past the display of photographs documenting Halloway's history—groundbreaking, first graduating class, the gymnasium under construction in 1958—and stopped at the case because something at its edge had snagged his attention. A photograph dated October 1974: the choir, posed on risers in the old music room, robes dark, faces small. The caption read HALLOWAY CHOIR, FALL CONCERT PROGRAM.

There were thirty-one students in the photograph.

He counted twice. He counted a third time with his fingertip against the glass, and the glass was cold in a way that had nothing to do with the building's heating, and the number stayed thirty-one, and every face in the photograph was looking directly into the camera except one—a girl in the second row, center, whose head was turned slightly, as though listening to something behind her, something the photographer had not thought worth capturing.

Her name, according to the tiny caption key, was T. Vale.

Mirabel stood in front of the trophy case for a long time. Around him the school went about its afternoon—lockers slamming, a whistle blowing on the quad, footsteps in the stairwell, all of it ordinary, all of it warm, all of it performing normalcy with the tireless commitment of professionals. And beneath the performance, so faint he might have imagined it, might have invented it out of roster anxiety and bad lighting and the specific loneliness of being new, he heard—or almost heard, or wanted to hear—something rising through the floorboards from the floors below, from the foundations, from wherever buildings keep the things they're built on top of.

Not music. Not yet. Just the memory of music. A hum with structure to it, four voices wide, patient as masonry.

That night, updating his attendance records in the empty classroom, Mirabel wrote *22/31* at the top of the sheet and then, beneath it, without letting himself think about why, wrote the nine absent names in a separate column. Abernathy. Ashford. Bellamy. Down through the alphabet to Wrenfield. Nine names, nine empty desks that didn't exist, nine portraits staring out of a blacked-over photograph from fifty years ago.

He told himself he was keeping records. Teachers kept records; it was what teachers did.

But he knew, folding the list into his pocket next to the roster, that he was doing something older than record-keeping. He was counting. And somewhere below him, in the dark under the gymnasium where the excavators would break ground in a matter of days, something that had been counting for fifty years felt, for the first time, a second voice keeping time alongside it—and began, very softly, to hum.
