## 3. Lights On, Doors Counted

The first week at Halloway Prep, Mirabel Blackwood learned that the building had a pulse.

Not metaphorically—or not only. He felt it most clearly at night, when the radiators ticked down through their cooling cycle and the old bones of the school settled into themselves with long, creaking exhalations, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. He'd taught at three schools before this one. Old buildings all. None of them had ever sounded like they were *breathing*.

So he developed a ritual.

It started small, the way rituals do—out of nervousness dressed up as diligence. On his second night in the faculty apartment on the eighth floor, he couldn't sleep, and rather than lie there listening to the walls exhale, he got up and walked the corridor outside his door, checking that each classroom lock was seated. All fourteen doors. He counted them under his breath as he tried each handle: *one, two, three.* The counting helped. Counting always helped. It gave the shapeless dark a border, an arithmetic it had to respect.

By Friday he'd expanded the circuit to the whole floor. By the following Wednesday, to the stairwells.

He told himself it was about security. A new teacher, new building, new habits—it made sense to learn the layout, to know where every door stood and how it sat in its frame. But he knew, in the honest part of himself he kept locked away like a drawer of letters from someone dead, that it wasn't about security at all. It was about the ninth floor.

Because the ninth floor was where the darkness breathed.

He'd noticed it his first night, before any of the ritual, before Ruth or Voss or the keyring with its single useless key. He'd taken the stairs up—one flight too many, a wrong turn born of unfamiliarity—and stepped out into a corridor that should have been identical to every other corridor in Halloway Prep: linoleum gone amber with age, plaster walls the color of weak tea, classroom doors spaced at regulation intervals. Identical, except that standing in it felt like standing underwater. The air was thicker. Cooler. And it moved—not a draft, nothing so explainable. It moved *rhythmically*. In through the seams around the windows, out through the gap beneath the far door. In. Out. Slow enough that you could convince yourself you were imagining it, if you wanted very badly to.

There were no numbers on the ninth-floor doors. That was the other thing. Every classroom in the building wore its number in brass: 101 through 108, 201 through 208, up through the eights. The ninth floor's doors were bare. Just wood and paint and hinges, eight of them, anonymous as coffins.

Mirabel had stood there perhaps thirty seconds before the breathing seemed to notice him—a change in the rhythm, subtle as a sleeper shifting—and then he'd been back down the stairs without any memory of deciding to move.

After that, the ritual became non-negotiable.

* * *

Every night at ten-fifteen, after the dormitory floors went quiet and the last stragglers of homework light winked out across the quad, Mirabel took up his ring of keys—the headmaster's gift, one actual key and a dozen blanks that fit nothing—and began his circuit.

Eighth floor first. Fourteen doors, each handle tested, each lock confirmed with a soft click of metal into metal. He'd learned their individual temperaments by now: 803's handle stuck unless you lifted it slightly; 811's lock turned with a grinding reluctance, like an old man being asked to get up; 814, directly across from his apartment, shut so precisely it sealed with a faint sigh of displaced air. Fourteen doors. Fourteen clicks. Then the stairwell, where he paused on the landing between eight and nine—not going up, never going up, but acknowledging the door the way you acknowledge a dog you don't trust—and then down through seven, six, five.

The lower floors were easier. The dark down there was ordinary dark, dumb and passive, just the absence of light. It didn't watch. It didn't swell against the windows like a tide. He could walk the fourth-floor corridor at midnight and feel nothing but the pleasant ache of tired legs and the small satisfaction of order maintained.

It was only near the top that the ritual became a gauntlet.

On the seventh-floor landing, he always stopped and listened. This was not part of any sensible security procedure; it was superstition, plain and simple, but he'd found he couldn't skip it. If the air moving down the stairwell from above was still—if it smelled of dust and cold plaster and nothing else—then the night was a normal night, and he could finish his rounds in peace. But some nights—and these nights were becoming more frequent, though he refused to chart them, refused to give them data because data would make them real—the air coming down carried something else. A warmth. A faint sweetness, like flowers left too long in water. And underneath it, so low he might have invented it, a sound that wasn't sound: a pressure in the ears, a fullness, as if somewhere above him a great many mouths had opened at once and were holding a single note.

On those nights, he did not go up to check the ninth-floor doors. He told himself there was no need—they weren't his responsibility, no classes met up there, the floor appeared on no map of the school he'd ever been given. He told himself this while his hand hovered over the banister and his legs argued fiercely in favor of climbing.

He always lost the argument eventually. Not that night, maybe. But eventually, before morning, he would find himself on the ninth floor, counting eight bare doors by the light he'd left burning, and the relief when the count came out right was so profound it frightened him more than the alternative.

Because what would it mean, someday, if the count came out wrong?

* * *

Ruth caught him at it on the eleventh night.

He was on the third floor, kneeling to inspect the gap beneath the door of 306—he'd begun noticing which doors sat flush and which left a sliver of black beneath them, and 306's sliver had seemed wider than usual, though he knew how that sounded—when a flashlight beam swung across his back and a voice said, "You'll want to start on four."

He came up too fast and cracked his head lightly on the door handle. When he turned, squinting into the light, he saw a broad-shouldered woman in gray coveralls, gray hair cropped short as a brush, a custodian's cart parked behind her with the tidy geometry of long habit. Her name tag read RUTH OYELARAN, and beneath it, smaller, almost worn away: 31 YEARS OF SERVICE.

"Start on four," she repeated, unbothered by his scrambling. "Ground floor last. You're doing it backwards."

"I'm sorry—I didn't think anyone—"

"Nobody thinks anybody's awake at this hour. That's the point of the hour." She clicked off her flashlight, and in the dim emergency lighting of the corridor her face settled into lines that might have been carved rather than aged. "You count the doors."

It wasn't a question. Mirabel felt heat climb his neck. "I'm learning the building," he said. "New teacher. It seems prudent to—"

"You count the doors." She said it again, flatly, and something in her tone made him stop performing. She studied him for a long moment, the way you might study a crack in a foundation—assessing whether it was cosmetic or structural. Whatever she saw, it decided her. "Come on, then. Walk with me. I'll show you the routes that are safe after ten."

"Safe?"

She was already pushing the cart, wheels whispering on linoleum. "You want to keep doing this alone and learn it the hard way, that's your business. I've buried enough teachers." She glanced back, and in the half-light her eyes were dry and very old. "Figuratively. Mostly."

He went with her. Of course he went with her.

* * *

What Ruth showed him over the next hour was, on its surface, nothing. A custodial tour. Which stairwells had motion sensors and which ran on timers that died at eleven. Which corridors held warmth and which collected cold the way gutters collect leaves. Where the night lights were wired to the emergency grid—those stayed on even when a breaker tripped—and where they were merely bulbs, mortal and unreliable.

But underneath the surface, it was a map of fear, drawn in fifty years of small corrections.

"Avoid the east stairwell after midnight," she said, walking him down the third-floor corridor at a pace just short of brisk. "The timer on that landing fails maybe once a month. Dark stairwell, bad idea, any building. This building especially." She tapped a wall panel as they passed, and a bulb flickered obediently to life. "These are on the emergency line. These—" she gestured ahead, at a stretch of corridor lit by ordinary ceiling fixtures, "—are not. If the power goes, you come back along the emergency line. Even if it's longer. Especially if it's longer."

"Why especially?"

"Because the short way passes the ninth-floor stairwell door." She said it the way she said everything—no emphasis, no drama, a fact among facts, which somehow made it worse. "Ground floor, I mean. The door to that stairwell is on the ground level too, by the gymnasium corridor. You've seen it. Gray door, window painted over."

He had seen it. He'd assumed it was a storage closet.

"The lights near that door fail more than the timer explains," Ruth continued. "Bulbs blow. Breakers trip. Once, the whole fixture came off the ceiling—screws and all, laid neat on the floor like it had been unscrewed by hand. Maintenance replaced it. It lasted nine days." She stopped at the corridor's end, where the hallway branched left toward the science wing and right toward the gymnasium, and pointed right with her chin. "We don't go right. After ten p.m., we don't go right. You want the gymnasium, you go in daylight, and you take the west approach, past the loading dock."

"That's absurd," Mirabel said, and immediately wished he hadn't, because it came out sounding like a man trying to convince himself.

Ruth didn't bristle. She looked at him with something that was almost gentleness, the way you look at someone reciting a prayer they don't yet believe. "Mr. Blackwood. How many nights have you been doing your little circuit?"

"Eleven."

"And how many times have you ended up on the ninth floor, even though you told yourself you wouldn't go?"

The heat came back to his neck, hotter now. "How did you—"

"Thirty-one years." She resumed walking. "You think you're the first? There's always one. Every few years, a new teacher who counts the doors. Who notices the numbers missing upstairs. Who can't sleep until they've checked." She pushed through the double doors into the main stairwell, and the building's breath was loud here, moving up through the shaft like wind through a chimney. "The others, the ones who don't count—they're fine. They sleep like babies. It's the counters who end up either leaving by Christmas or..." She let it hang. The stairwell swallowed the rest of the sentence and echoed it back down at them from somewhere above.

"Or what?" Mirabel asked, though every instinct screamed not to.

Ruth pressed the button for the fourth floor instead of answering. When the elevator—old, cage-doored, slower than the stairs and chosen anyway—began to rise, she spoke again, quietly, to the closing gate rather than to him.

"Or they get invited to stay."

* * *

They rode down together after that, floor by floor, and Ruth narrated the safe routes like a woman reading a litany: fourth floor, all clear, emergency lights on both ends; third floor, avoid the east wing after the heating shuts off at one a.m., because the cold pooled there had a quality she declined to describe; second floor, fine, always fine, "the building likes the second floor"; ground floor, the litany grew longer and her voice grew flatter.

"Main corridor, fine. Science wing, fine until two, then the far lab smells like pond water and you shouldn't investigate why. Library annex—" here she paused, hand resting on the cart's handle, knuckles pale, "—library annex is fine, but the lights over the periodicals hum. Not electrical hum. If you hear it, walk faster, don't run. Running is worse."

"Worse how?"

"It follows runners." She said it with the finality of a woman who had tested the hypothesis and did not wish to discuss results.

At the ground floor they stepped out into the entrance hall, where the school's founder glared down from his portrait with the permanent disappointment of men who end up in oil paint, and Ruth finally turned to face him fully. Up close, in the good light of the hall, Mirabel could see that she was older than thirty-one years of service suggested—sixty at least, maybe older, worn smooth like river stone.

"Your rules," she said, holding up fingers. "One: lights stay on. All of them you can keep on, keep on. Darkness in this building isn't empty, and it isn't stupid. It notices patterns. A corridor that's bright every night stays ordinary. A corridor that goes dark even once—" she shook her head. "Don't give it an opening. Two: count whatever you need to count, but count it the same way every time. Same order, same floors. Routine is armor. Three—" and here her voice dropped, and she leaned in slightly, close enough that he could smell lye soap and something herbal underneath, feverfew maybe, or rue, "—when you reach the seventh-floor landing and you hear it, whatever you hear, you turn around and you go down. You don't climb. Not alone. Not ever alone."

"Hear what?" he asked, though he knew. God help him, he knew exactly what she meant, the sweet warm pressure of held notes seeping down the stairwell like gas.

Ruth straightened. For a moment something moved behind her expression—grief, or guilt, or both wearing each other's clothes—and then it was gone, packed away with the efficiency of a custodian closing up a room.

"If you hear singing," she said, "you come find me. Boiler room, sub-basement, any hour. I sleep light."

She pushed the cart toward the service corridor, then stopped, half-turned.

"One more thing, Mr. Blackwood." The hall clock ticked behind her, loud in the silence. "The key Voss gave you. The one that fits nothing."

Mirabel's hand went involuntarily to his pocket, where the strange blank key rode against his hip every day like a splinter. "You know about that?"

"Everyone knows about that. Everyone pretends they don't, same as everything else here." Her eyes held his. "Keep it on you. Don't try to make it fit anything. And don't—whatever happens, however curious you get, however sure you are that you've finally found the lock it belongs to—don't use it. Not yet."

"Not yet," he repeated. "Meaning someday—"

But she was already gone, cart wheels fading down the service corridor, leaving him alone in the entrance hall with the founder's painted glare and the ticking clock and the slow realization that everyone in this building seemed to speak in sentences that ended one word before the important one.

* * *

That night, the ritual changed shape. It was still his circuit, still the same order—eight down to ground, the litany Ruth had blessed—but now he walked it differently. He checked the emergency-line lights first, confirming each bulb with a glance and a silent thank-you. He avoided the east stairwell. He passed the gray door by the gymnasium corridor at a steady walk, neither hurrying nor lingering, and noted—as he had promised himself he wouldn't note—that the air near it was several degrees warmer than the air ten feet away, and that the warmth pulsed. Faintly. Patiently.

In. Out.

At the seventh-floor landing, he stopped, as he always stopped, and listened.

The air coming down was still. Dust and cold plaster. Nothing else.

He should have felt relief. Instead, standing there in the cone of the emergency bulb's amber light, he felt the particular dread of a man who has learned that the quiet nights are just the building catching its breath. Ruth had given him rules, and rules were armor, and armor was better than nothing—but armor implies a battlefield, and battlefields imply an enemy, and the enemy here was the building itself, patient as masonry, keeping its own kind of attendance.

He climbed the last two flights because he always climbed them eventually, and counted the ninth floor's eight bare doors by the light he'd left burning. Eight. Correct. The breathing moved around him, in and out, unhurried, and for the first time he noticed something new—something that hadn't been there on the previous nights, or that he hadn't been ready to see.

Down at the far end of the corridor, past the eighth door, the wall was dirty. Not stained—*disturbed*. A rectangle of cleaner plaster, sharp-edged, about the size of a door, where something that had hung or stood or been bricked over for years had recently been removed. Or recently *arrived*, and the wall around it was still adjusting.

The breathing, he realized, was loudest there.

Mirabel stood in the amber light with his useless key in his pocket and counted the doors one more time, and then, because the alternative was standing there until dawn, he went down to bed and lay awake listening to the school breathe, and thought about what Ruth had said: *there's always one.*

Below him, faint through two floors of old pine and plaster, came a sound he would spend the next days pretending he hadn't heard—a single sustained note, low and sweet, like a tuning fork struck in a room full of sleeping children.

Somewhere in the building, something was warming up.
