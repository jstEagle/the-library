## 13. The Conductor's Ledger

Devi Okafor had a rule about archives: never trust a room that wants to be found.

She'd developed it over fifteen years of science teaching, most of it spent watching students mistake a tidy hypothesis for the truth. Tidy things were usually tidied. Someone had done the arranging. The question was never *why is this organized* but *who benefits from me believing it was always this way.*

The Halloway archive wanted very much to be found.

It occupied the basement of the west wing, behind a door marked RECORDS — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL in paint so fresh it still smelled faintly of linseed. That was the first wrong thing. Everything else in the basement was fifty years old at minimum: the boiler pipes sweating rust down the walls, the fluorescent tube flickering its dying insect rhythm overhead, the smell of cold stone and old paper and something sweeter underneath, like flowers left too long in a closed drawer.

The second wrong thing was the light. The bulb above the door worked. The one inside worked. Ruth had told her, back in September, that half the fixtures in this building hadn't functioned since the Nixon administration, and that you learned to carry a flashlight the way other people carried keys.

Devi carried both. She also carried a thermos of black coffee she wasn't drinking, because her hands were already unsteady enough.

The pact they'd made three weeks ago—four teachers and a bottle of contraband rye passed around a card table in Hollis's groundkeeper's cottage—had been simple in the way all desperate plans are simple. Investigate together. Tell no one else. Trust each other more than the institution. Devi had taken the paperwork because paperwork was what she understood. Mirabel took the building itself, counting doors like a man praying a rosary he didn't believe in. Ruth took the routes, the rhythms, the safe hours. Hollis took the grounds and said little, which at the time she'd read as grief rather than guilt.

She would think about that later. She would think about it a great deal later.

For now there was only the archive, and the archive was waiting for her with the patience of something that had waited before.

* * *

The room was smaller than she expected. Three rows of steel shelving, gray-green, bolted to the floor as if against earthquake or theft. Boxes labeled by decade in a hand that changed every few years but never changed its character: neat, unhurried, institutional. Attendance records. Board minutes. Maintenance invoices for a boiler that appeared, from the invoices, to have been replaced eleven times without ever being replaced.

She started where any scientist starts: with the anomaly. The class of 1974.

The box was on the second shelf, third position from the left, exactly where a box containing an entire vanished class should not have been—should have been misfiled, buried, lost. Instead it sat at eye level, spine facing out, labeled in careful block capitals: **1973–74. COMPLETE.**

Complete. As if completeness were the point.

Inside: attendance sheets. Report cards. A field trip permission slip folder, thin, mostly unsigned. And beneath those, wrapped in acid-free tissue paper like something precious, a ledger.

Not a school ledger. She knew school ledgers—their grumpy columns, their coffee-ringed margins. This was bound in dark leather gone soft as skin at the corners, and it opened flat on its own, the way a book does when it has been opened to the same place ten thousand times.

The heading, embossed in faded gilt across the top of the first page:

**VOICES RETAINED**

Below it, columns. Name. Date. Verse assigned. And a final column, narrow, ruled in red: **Signature of Record.**

Devi stood very still for a long moment, listening to the boiler tick somewhere behind the wall, telling herself the cold she felt was just the basement.

Then she read the names.

*Whitlock, Thomas E.* — *October 12, 1931.*
*Ferreira, Ana M.* — *November 3, 1931.*
*Achebe, Daniel O.* — *March 22, 1938.*

They went back further than 1974. Further than the yearbook page Mirabel had shown them, the class photo inked over with such loving care that the faces beneath were still ghosts of themselves if you held it to the light. The ledger went back to the founding. Back past living memory, past the oldest teacher's oldest story, in the same slanting hand decade after decade—or in different hands, she corrected herself, different hands writing the same sentence, the way different mouths sing the same hymn.

Every name had a verse number beside it. Not random numbers. Ordered. First soprano, second soprano, alto, tenor, bass—a full choral arrangement assembled across eighty years, one stolen voice at a time, each child filed into their part like components into a machine.

And every line was signed.

That was the detail that made her sit down on the cold concrete floor with the ledger open across her knees. Not the names. Not even the children's names, though she would carry those. It was the signatures. Because signatures meant witnesses. Signatures meant someone had stood in a room, holding a pen, and chosen.

The early ones were flourished, Victorian, confident: *E. Blackwood, Headmaster*, the ink brown with age. Then a war-era hand, cramped and fast, as if the man signing had wanted it over quickly. Then the fifties, the sixties, each headmaster's name arriving like a relay baton passed in the dark.

She turned pages with a curator's care and a coroner's stomach. Some entries had small annotations in the margin, and these were worse than the rest, because annotations implied deliberation:

*Ferreira — strong upper register; reassigned from alto to first soprano at induction.*

*Induction.* The word sat there in 1931's handwriting, casual as a note about a lab partner.

*Whitlock — resisted initially. Resolved after second hearing.*

Second hearing. Of what? Of what?

Her coffee went cold in her hand untouched. Somewhere above her, the school went through its ordinary motions—bells, sneakers, a locker slamming like a small gunshot—and she felt the whole structure of it tilt, the way you feel a ship list before your eyes confirm it. Every polite deflection. Every practiced smile when she'd asked about missing students. Colleagues who loved her, who had shielded her, who had poured her whiskey and told her their real fears—they had all, every one of them, walked past this shelf. Or been kept from it. She didn't yet know which was worse.

She photographed everything. Phone flash, two angles per page, the shutter sound absurdly loud in the silence. Her hands steadied once the work began; work always did that for her. Method was a raft. You could cross almost anything on method.

She reached 1974 near midnight, by her watch, though the basement had no windows and time down here felt less like a river than a held breath.

The class of 1974 filled four pages. Thirty-one names. She recognized some from Mirabel's roster—the phantom students who never appeared, whose empty desks the other teachers' eyes slid past like water off wax. There they were in black and white: *Tamsin Reeve, first soprano.* Eleven years old. *Verse assigned: IX (pending).*

Pending.

Ninth verse, pending, since 1974. Fifty years of pending. She stared at the notation until the letters swam, and thought of the hymnal cavity the renovation crew had exposed—hundreds of hymnals in a hidden wall, each generation adding verses in children's handwriting, the song growing longer every decade, and the last page holding a blank stave labeled *the ninth verse.* Waiting. The ledger agreed with the wall. The institution's memory was perfect. It had simply been distributed across paper and plaster and children so no single record could be burned.

Whoever built this had understood redundancy better than any archivist she'd ever met.

* * *

It was near the end of the 1974 section that she found the annotation that stopped her heart.

The final entry of the class was signed not by the headmaster of record—whose name, *Aldous Pemberton*, appeared on every preceding line in a hand growing shakier toward spring—but by a countersignature. Smaller. Neater. Almost tender.

*Countersigned, mentorship: E. Voss.*

Voss.

She read it five times. The letters didn't rearrange themselves into anything kinder. *Mentorship.* In 1974, Aldous Voss had been—young. A junior master, maybe. Twenty-five. Old enough to know better, young enough to be learning how, and here was the proof that he had learned it under supervision, that his first act of complicity had been witnessed and approved, that the man who now offered tenure and warm tea and gentle warnings had signed his apprenticeship in the blood-ink of thirty-one children.

She thought of his office. The good bourbon. The way he'd said *we protect this school, Dr. Okafor,* with such sincerity that she'd almost believed protection was what it was. The hum through the wall—four-part harmony, faint as a memory of music—that he either couldn't hear or pretended not to.

He hadn't inherited the machine. He'd been *trained* on it. Mentored into it the way she'd been mentored into her first lab, taught the protocols, praised for precision. Somewhere there was probably a file on him too. Somewhere in these shelves, decades forward, his own signature repeated—she checked, flipping ahead with dread climbing her throat—and yes. 1989. 1996. 2003. Each decade, new names, new verses assigned, the same steady hand, until the hand was the only constant left, outlasting every colleague, every board, every parent's grief.

Voss hadn't kept the secret for fifty years.

Voss had kept the *choir* for fifty years.

* * *

She might have left then. She wants to say she would have. But the scientist in her—one more control, one more variable, one more page—kept her on the cold floor while the boiler ticked and the fluorescents buzzed their one long note.

It was that note that made her notice the last thing.

The archive hummed. Very quietly. The same pitch, sustained, coming not from the lights but from the shelving itself—from the steel, or through the steel, from the floor, from everywhere and nowhere, a tone so low and patient she'd mistaken it for silence for four hours.

She put her palm flat against the shelf. The metal was warm.

Steel in an unheated basement is never warm.

She snatched her hand back and looked at the ledger in her lap with new eyes—not as evidence now but as bait. *Never trust a room that wants to be found.* The door had been freshly painted. The lights had worked. The box had been at eye level. She had assumed an oversight, a careless predecessor, luck.

There was no luck in this building. There was only intention wearing luck's clothes.

The archive hadn't been hard to find because it was forgotten. It had been easy to find because it was a lure—or a test, or a feeding trough, or all three. How many curious teachers had sat exactly where she sat, reading exactly these names, feeling exactly this cold? And how many had walked out again? The ledger's most recent entries were dated—

She flipped to the last written page, fast now, no longer careful.

The most recent entry was dated this semester. Three weeks ago. Before the pact, before the whiskey, before any of them had spoken aloud what they suspected.

One name. One line. Verse assigned: *IX (pending).*

Signature of record: *A. Voss.*

The name printed there was hers.

*Okafor, Devi N.*

She didn't remember standing up. She remembered the ledger closing under her hands with a sound like a mouth finishing a word, soft and final, and the hum in the shelves deepening by a fraction—not threatening, nothing so crude—welcoming. Patient. The way a tuning fork waits.

Her phone showed forty-one photographs. All intact. All damning. She backed them up twice, cloud and drive, hands moving on autopilot while her mind ran the calculations she hated: if the ledger already contained her, then her investigation was not a threat to the Choir. It was a *verse in progress.* She had been enrolled the moment she asked her first careful question in the faculty lounge, and everything since—the archive's open door, the working lights, the box at eye level—had been curriculum.

Unless.

Unless the ledger recorded intent, not fact. Unless a name could be struck through as easily as written. Unless the ninth verse stayed pending for people who refused to learn it—which was, she realized, exactly what Tamsin's ghost had been trying to tell Mirabel in the corridor, mouthing her silent warning: *don't learn the last verse.*

Knowledge was the mechanism. Curiosity was the hook. The song needed voices that knew it, and the school had simply been teaching her, one archival hour at a time, the way it had been teaching all of them.

She put the ledger back exactly as she'd found it. Wrapped in its tissue. Box relidded. Shelf aligned. If the room was a test, she would not give it the satisfaction of panic. But as she reached the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and spoke aloud to the humming dark—because she was Devi Okafor, and she had never once let an experiment have the last word:

"I read your names. I didn't sing them."

The hum held its note. Unmoved. Unmoving.

But as she climbed the stairs toward the ordinary world—toward the bells and the lockers and the colleagues she now had to decide whether to warn or protect—her phone buzzed once in her pocket. An internal memo, school-wide, cheerful as a greeting card:

***Reminder: Annual Harvest Festival planning committee meets Thursday. All faculty encouraged to attend. — Office of the Headmaster***

Attached was a calendar invitation.

The date was eleven days out.

The night of the harvest moon.
