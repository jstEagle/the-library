## 20. Halfway Down

The stairwell behind the gymnasium had never been on any floor plan. Ruth had told us that much before we went down—she'd been the one to find it, three days after the excavation crew broke through, when she'd gone looking for a water main and found instead a door where a wall should have been.

"It wasn't there in September," she'd said, and her voice had that flat quality she got when she was holding something down with both hands. "I've walked this building for fifty years. I would have known."

Now, at eleven minutes past midnight, I stood at the top of those stairs with Devi's flashlight trembling against her knee, Hollis's crowbar resting on his shoulder like a farmer's scythe, and Ruth's lamp held high—the old kind, with a wire cage over the glass, because she said electric light lied about distance.

"Rules," Ruth whispered. It wasn't really for our benefit. It was for hers. "Nobody touches anybody we find. Nobody answers if something calls your name. And nobody—" she looked at me, and I knew what was coming, "—nobody sings. Not humming. Not under your breath. Nothing."

"I remember," I said.

"You remember," she agreed. "Remembering is easy. Doing is the hard part."

We went down.

* * *

The stairs were wrong in a way I couldn't name at first, and then could: they were too long. Not steeply, not dramatically—they descended at a perfectly ordinary angle, through perfectly ordinary turns—but the gymnasium above should have put us into bedrock within two flights, and we passed four, five, six landings, each with a door marked GYMNASIUM STAIRWELL in the same institutional stencil as every other stairwell in Halloway Prep. The paint was fresh. The air grew warmer, not colder, which felt like a violation of some law so basic I didn't know how to be afraid of it properly.

"Counting?" Devi asked me quietly. She knew my habit by now. Everyone did.

"Forty-one steps between landings," I said. "Every time. Exactly forty-one."

"That's consistent, at least."

"It's *too* consistent." I ran my hand along the rail. "Real stairs drift. People build them tired. These are machine-perfect all the way down, like someone measured them once and then just... repeated the measurement forever."

Hollis grunted from the rear. "Like a song repeating."

Nobody answered him. Nobody wanted to answer him.

At the seventh landing, the doors stopped saying GYMNASIUM STAIRWELL. They began saying other things. ROOM 9-A. ROOM 9-B. The ninth floor's missing numbers, the ones I'd noticed my first week and been told, with practiced smiles, that I must have miscounted. Here they were, filed away underground like evidence in a trial no one intended to hold.

Ruth stopped at the landing and pressed her palm flat against the wall. For a long moment she just stood there, an old woman touching plaster the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

"This is where I hid," she said. "In '74. There was a boiler room. There was always a boiler room, right off the choir room corridor. When Aldery called roll, I was down here with my hands over my ears." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she cleared her throat hard. "Fifty years I thought I dreamed the layout. I didn't dream it. It's real. It's just... below now."

"Below what?" Devi asked.

"Below everything," Ruth said. "Come on. We're close."

* * *

The corridor opened without warning—one moment stairs, the next a hallway so familiar my stomach dropped through the floor. It was Halloway's second-floor humanities wing. The same wainscoting. The same flickering fluorescent fixtures, except these weren't flickering; they glowed steady and warm, warmer than any light Halloway had ever managed. The same trophy case outside the history rooms, filled with the same tarnished cups—

Except the plaques on the cups read years that hadn't happened yet. 1988. 2003. One, small and silver, engraved with NEXT YEAR in letters that seemed to shift when I looked directly at them.

"It's mirrored," Devi breathed. She was walking the hallway with her flashlight off, reading the room numbers. "Not reflected—mirrored in the older sense. A copy. A model. This whole school exists twice, and this is the second time."

"Why build a copy?" Hollis asked. His crowbar had come off his shoulder at some point; he was holding it like a weapon now, and I noticed his knuckles were white. "Who has the money? Who has the *reason*?"

"The school does," Ruth said simply. "The school built it. Or grew it. I don't think there's a difference anymore."

We moved down the corridor together, four shadows in a place that had no right to hold them, and the classrooms began.

The first door stood open. Inside, a mathematics lesson hung suspended mid-sentence—or mid-*decade*. Chalk dust hung in the air like a photograph of snowfall, each particle fixed and glittering. At the board, a teacher's hand hovered, chalk poised, though the teacher himself was only partially there—a suggestion of shoulders, a smear of sleeve, the chalk itself sharp and real. On the board, half an equation trailed off into nothing: *if x approaches infinity, then the class will*

Devi stepped inside. I grabbed her arm.

"Don't touch anything."

"I'm not touching. I'm *reading*." But her voice had gone thin. "Mirabel, look at the date on the corner of the board."

September 14, 1974.

"They weren't taken somewhere else," I said slowly, understanding arriving like cold water. "They were taken *here*. And here kept the day they left. Forever. It's still September fourteenth, 1974, in this room. It's been September fourteenth for fifty years."

"Then why is the trophy case dated 2003?" Hollis asked.

None of us had an answer. That became a pattern.

* * *

The second classroom was an English room. My subject. Desks arranged in the seminar horseshoe I favored, and for one vertiginous moment I wondered if the building had copied the school or the school had copied something older, something that had been waiting for teachers like me to arrive and fill its shapes. On the desks: composition books. Open. Filled with handwriting that started neat and deteriorated page by page into lines of the same phrase, over and over, in thirty different hands:

*we sing so the walls will hold*
*we sing so the walls will hold*
*we sing so the walls will hold*

And beneath the last line of the last book, in pencil, in a child's careful cursive: *it hurts less if you mean it.*

Ruth made a sound I will hear until I die. She closed the book gently, the way you'd close the eyes of the dead, and set it back exactly where it had been.

"We keep moving," she said.

* * *

It was the third classroom where we found them.

Devi saw the light first—a warm, gold, impossible light spilling under a door at the corridor's end, the kind of light that comes through windows in late afternoon, honeyed and forgiving. Except we were forty feet beneath a foundation slab, and there were no windows, and it was past midnight.

The door was labeled CHOIR ROOM.

Of course it was.

Ruth stopped ten feet from it and would go no further. "This is it," she whispered. "This is the one. The original. Everything else down here is—it's the stage set. This is the theater." Her lamp shook. "I can't. I'm sorry. I hid from this door once already and it cost me fifty years of lights left burning. I can't stand in front of it again."

"You don't have to," I told her. And I meant it, and something in my chest ached at how much I meant it—this ragged quartet of ours, the misfits Voss tolerated and the institution ignored, and here was Ruth asking nothing except not to be forced, and here was me, giving her the only thing I had, which was permission.

Hollis stayed with her. He didn't say he would; he just shifted his weight back a step, so that his body stood between Ruth and the door, casual as a man leaning on a fence. Devi and I went forward alone.

The door was unlocked. Of course it was. Things that are certain of themselves don't lock their doors.

Inside, the light came from everywhere and nowhere, and the room was small—smaller than any classroom, barely larger than my office—and it was full of children.

Thirty-one of them. I counted out of habit, out of terror, out of the desperate animal need to impose arithmetic on horror. Thirty-one desks in rows, and in the desks sat the class of 1974.

They were breathing.

That was the thing. That was the thing that unmade me. I had prepared myself for skeletons, for ghosts, for the pale and drifting dead—I had spent three weeks of nightmares rehearsing every version of horror the mind can manufacture. I had not prepared myself for *breathing*. For the gentle rise and fall of small chests. For the soft, synchronized rhythm of it, thirty-one children inhaling as one and exhaling as one, a tide of breath that filled the little room and emptied it, filled and emptied, patient as geology.

They looked alive. They looked *asleep*. Their cheeks had color. Tamsin—Tamsin, who appeared in my classroom pale and shivering and gone by lunch—sat in the third row with color in her face for the first time since I'd known her, her hands folded on her desk, her eyes closed, her lips slightly parted around the ghost of a note.

On the chalkboard behind them, in a teacher's elegant hand, the first eight verses of the hymn were written out in full. Below them, a ninth line had been begun:

*Verse nine: and when the new voice—*

The sentence stopped. The chalk lay in the tray, worn to a nub, as if the writer had been interrupted mid-word fifty years ago and simply never resumed.

"Mirabel," Devi whispered. "Their attendance sheet is on the desk. The teacher's desk."

I crossed the room. Every footstep sounded obscene in that breathing hush. The ledger lay open, and it was the twin of my own attendance book—the same format, the same columns, the same blue-ruled lines. Thirty-one names. And beside each name, in a column headed PRESENT, a single mark.

Not checkmarks.

Musical notes.

Each child's presence was recorded as a pitch. Whole notes, every one of them. Held. Still being held.

"Devi." My voice came out sanded flat. "Look at the bottom of the page."

At the bottom, below the thirty-one names, space remained for more. And in fresh ink—ink that glistened, ink that had not dried, ink that could not possibly be fifty years old—two new lines had been added.

The first read: *Okafor, D.*

The second read: *Zhou, M.*

Both marked present. Both marked with whole notes.

The breath in the room changed. I felt it before I understood it—the synchronized tide stuttering, deepening, thirty-one chests rising a fraction higher, and beneath my feet the floor began, very faintly, to hum. Not the building's hum. A hum with intention. A hum that had noticed us the way a great eye notices a moth.

One by one—not all at once, God, not all at once, that would have been mercy—the children began to open their eyes.

Tamsin first. Her gaze found mine across the little golden room, and her mouth shaped words I couldn't hear over the rising harmony, words I read on her lips with a clarity that burned them into me:

*Run. Now. Before the verse ends.*

Because the verse hadn't ended. I understood that then, standing among the breathing dead who were not dead, among the sleeping class that was not asleep. The first eight verses had been sung for fifty years without pause, sustained by thirty-one pairs of lungs, and we had walked into the middle of a song that had never once stopped, and the song had just become aware of its audience.

The children turned toward us. All of them. Together. Faces sweet and young and terribly awake, mouths opening in unison, and the harmony that poured out was beautiful—that was the worst of it, it was the most beautiful sound I had ever heard, and every instinct in my body leaned toward it like a plant toward sun—

Ruth's rule. *Nobody sings.* And its shadow-rule, the one she hadn't spoken: nobody listens too closely, either.

I seized Devi's wrist. Her eyes were glazed, her lips parting, her throat beginning to work. I hauled her backward through the doorway as the first true chord swelled, and behind us I heard—felt—thirty-one voices rise together, and beneath them, faint and ruined and horribly tender, a man's voice keeping them steady, keeping them calm, conducting from somewhere deeper in the dark.

Mr. Aldery. Still teaching. Still singing first, so the children would follow.

We ran. Ruth and Hollis met us at the stairwell, and none of us spoke until we'd climbed six landings, seven, until the gymnasium's ordinary darkness closed over our heads like water.

It was Devi who finally broke the silence, in a whisper, staring at her own hands as though they belonged to someone else.

"He wrote our names down," she said. "Fifty years ago, he wrote our names down. He was waiting for us before either of us was born."

Above us, faintly, through fifty feet of concrete and earth, the Choir sang on.
