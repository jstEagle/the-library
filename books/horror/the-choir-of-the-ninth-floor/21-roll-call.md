## 21. Roll Call

The chalk dust hung in the air like it had been told to wait.

Mirabel stood at the threshold of Room 4-B—the mirrored one, the one that shouldn't exist beneath the gymnasium—and watched thirty-one children breathe. That was the word his mind kept returning to, because there was no better one. They sat at their desks in rows, spines straight, hands folded, uniforms pressed and decades out of fashion: wide collars, brown plaid, the particular geometry of 1974. Their chests rose. Their chests fell. In perfect unison, all thirty-one of them, like a single lung wearing thirty-one bodies.

They had not turned to look at him yet.

He understood, with the cold clarity that had been settling over him for days now, that this was worse than if they had.

*Don't run,* he told himself. *Ruth is upstairs holding the stairwell light. Devi has the map. Hollis—*

Hollis. He set that thought down carefully, the way you set down a glass you suspect is already cracked, and did not pick it up again.

The classroom was preserved the way a fly is preserved in amber. A lesson remained on the board in a teacher's slanted hand: *Diagram the sentence. Underline every verb.* The date was written in the corner—October 14th, 1974—and beside it, a small notation in red: *Harvest moon Friday. Rehearsal moved up.* The clock above the door read 11:52 and had read 11:52, Mirabel suspected, for fifty years. Dust motes drifted through the slanted window light and did not land anywhere so much as hover, mid-fall, patient as an audience.

He had come down here with a plan. Devi had drawn it on a napkin in the faculty lounge like they were planning a heist, which, in a way, they were: get in, get close, get proof. Names, faces, something they could carry back up into the daylight world where things were still made of ordinary matter. But plans were for people who believed the ground would hold still under them, and standing here, watching the dead breathe, Mirabel felt his plan dissolve like sugar in warm water.

What remained was the roster.

It was folded in his breast pocket. He'd copied it from the archive himself, Devi's flashlight shaking over the ledger pages while Ruth stood watch at the archive door and hummed—not the Choir's hymn, never that, but an old cleaning song about mops and Mondays, off-key on purpose, a kind of armor. Thirty-one names in Voss's predecessor's handwriting, each followed by the same notation in the margin: *Retained.*

He unfolded it now. The paper sounded very loud.

The nearest student—a boy in the second row with cowlicked hair and a face full of freckles—did not move. His eyes were open. They were fixed on the board, on the sentence-diagramming exercise, and they had the quality of eyes that are open because someone left them that way, like lights in a house whose owner stepped out and never came back.

"Okay," Mirabel said quietly. His voice came out strange in the room, too alive, too rough-edged. "Okay. I'm going to do this now."

He cleared his throat. It felt obscene, clearing your throat in front of the dead. Then he looked down at the roster, and began.

"Aldous Wren."

The boy with the freckles turned his head.

Not fast. Not slow. At exactly the speed of a living child hearing his name called on a Monday morning, which was somehow the most horrifying speed available. His neck rotated and his body stayed perfectly still, hands folded, chest rising and falling in time with the others, and his eyes found Mirabel's and *focused*—and in that focus there was a flicker, a candle-flame guttering awake after fifty years in a draftless room. His mouth worked. No sound came out. But his eyebrows lifted, just slightly, in the ancient universal grammar of students everywhere: *Me? You mean me?*

"Yes," Mirabel said, and his voice cracked on it. "Aldous Wren. Present."

Something happened in the room. He couldn't have described it if you'd held a knife to him. The dust shifted its drift by a degree. The suspended moment tilted, like a picture frame knocked a hair askew. The other thirty heads remained forward-facing, but the room *knew*, the way a sleeping dog knows when one person in the house wakes.

"Beatrice Okonjo."

Third row, window side. A girl with braids beaded in yellow turned, and her whole face changed—not animated, not yet, but *assembled*, features finding their old arrangement the way furniture finds its familiar corners in the dark. Her lips parted. Air moved through them, and what came out was not a sound but the shape of one, the ghost of a syllable, a first letter reaching for the rest of a name.

"Present," Mirabel said for her, gently. "You're present, Beatrice. I've got you."

*Names give them a flicker of self.* He hadn't known that when he came down here. He knew it now, knew it in his marrow, because he could see it happening: each name was a hand reaching down into dark water, and each time, something small and human grabbed hold of it from below.

"Cormac Bellamy." Fourth row, back corner, a big kid with a jawline that would have been handsome at forty. Turned. Flickered. His fists unclenched—one centimeter, two—as if some part of him remembered being angry about something, and anger, Mirabel realized with a lurch, was *memory*. Anger required a before. Anger required an injustice. Cormac Bellamy remembered that something had been done to him.

"Dahlia Frost."

Row five. The girl who turned had Tamsin's pale complexion and none of Tamsin's wariness; her face was smooth as an egg, waiting to be written on. She turned and she smiled.

Mirabel's blood went cold. It wasn't her smile. It was the Choir's smile, borrowed, worn like a coat—the smile of something that had noticed what he was doing and found it interesting. For half a second Dahlia Frost's borrowed face regarded him with a patience older than the building, and then the flicker came anyway, fighting up through the smile like a sprout through pavement, and her eyes went wet, and her mouth shaped a word: *help.*

"I know," Mirabel whispered. "I know. I'm working on it."

He kept going. Down the list. Each name a match struck in a wet cave. Elspeth Marsh, present. Franklin Osei, present. Greta Lindqvist, present. Some of them wept without tears. Some of them laughed without sound, a hunching of shoulders, a crinkling of eyes, fifty years of relief compressed into a single silent convulsion. One boy—Hollis's son's namesake, no relation, or maybe every relation, Hollis had never said—turned toward Mirabel and mouthed *teacher?* with such naked hope that Mirabel had to grip the edge of a desk to keep going.

"Isolde Vane."

And then—

"Tamsin Reeve."

She sat in the last row, by the door, the seat closest to escape, and Mirabel understood in one cold flash that she had chosen it in 1974 and had been sitting in it, reaching for it, for half a century.

Tamsin turned, and she *came back.*

There was no other way to say it. Where the others had flickered, she ignited. Color flooded into her face like water into a dry creekbed. Her eyes—which he had seen in the daylight world, pale and cold and always slightly out of focus, always looking at him from slightly wrong angles—snapped into terrible clarity. She gasped, actually gasped, a real breath drawn through real lungs that had no business working, and her hands flew to the desk in front of her and gripped it, knuckles white, and she said:

"—bel. *Mirabel.*"

His name. His actual name, in her actual voice, rusty and thin and cracking like ice on a pond, and it was the first time anyone down here had spoken aloud in fifty years and the room *shook* with it—or maybe the room didn't shake, maybe the building shook, maybe the whole buried mirror of Halloway Prep shuddered on its impossible foundations, because somewhere far above them and also all around them, something took notice.

"Tamsin," Mirabel breathed. "Hey. Hey, I'm here. I found your trail. I found the—" *hymnals, the ledger, the door,* "—I found everything. We're getting you out. All of you. There's a way, there's a verse, there's—"

"Stop," she whispered.

Her eyes weren't on him anymore. They were on the middle distance, on nothing, on everything, and her face was doing two things at once: joy, enormous and starving, and terror laid over it like a sheet of glass.

"You have to stop counting us," she said. "You have to stop—it hears the names. It keeps the names. Every name you say, it files away, it—it learns which ones still pull. Mirabel, please, you're making them *hope*, and hope is—"

The harmony began.

It did not start. There was no first note, no downbeat, no conductor's raised hand. It simply *was*, suddenly, the way pressure is, the way gravity is: eight voices woven into one fabric, coming from the walls themselves, from the desks, from the mouths of the twenty-nine students who had not yet fully surfaced. Aldous Wren's lips had closed. Beatrice Okonjo's face had gone smooth again, the beads in her braids catching light that came from nowhere. And the sound rolled through Room 4-B like a tide coming in, gentle, gorgeous, absolutely merciless.

Mirabel knew the melody. God help him, he knew it. Verse three—he'd heard it leaking through Voss's office wall, he'd heard it in his dreams for a week, he'd caught himself humming it in the shower and bitten his tongue until it bled. It was a simple tune. That was the obscenity of it. Children could learn it in an afternoon. Children *had.*

"No," Tamsin said. She was gripping her desk hard enough that the wood creaked. "No, no, no—not yet, I almost—I was *almost*—"

Her voice was fraying even as she spoke, the clarity draining out of it like color from a photograph left in the sun. Her eyes were losing focus. Her shoulders were squaring, her hands unfolding, her spine straightening toward the posture of attention that all thirty-one of them wore, and Mirabel watched the girl he'd met in his classroom—the cold girl, the waving girl, the girl who mouthed *don't learn the last verse* across three weeks of lunches she couldn't eat—watched her sink back down into herself like a swimmer pulled under by something with infinite patience.

"Tamsin!" He crossed the room in four strides and dropped to his knees beside her desk, and he did the only thing he could think of, the thing that had worked, the thing that was hope and therefore poison and therefore the only medicine anyone down here had. He said her name again. "Tamsin Reeve. Tamsin. Look at me. October 14th, 1974. You were going on a field trip. You wore a green coat—you hate the coat, you told me, your mother made you wear it. Your best friend is Dahlia and you split a chocolate bar on the bus and you never got to finish your half—"

Her eyes came back.

For one blazing second they came back, wet and wild and *her*, and her hand shot out and seized his wrist with a strength no dead girl should have had, and she pulled him close, close enough that he could see the frost forming in the air between her lips, and she said, fast, urgent, each word costing her visibly:

"The teacher sang *first.* Understand? He wasn't taken. He *volunteered*, and they let him keep enough of himself to conduct, and he thinks he's keeping us calm but he's keeping us *kept.* Don't trust the conductor. Even the one below. *Especially* the one below. And Mirabel—when you learn it—"

The harmony swelled.

It wasn't louder, exactly. It was *closer.* The eight voices gathered around Tamsin's desk like concerned adults around a child having a nightmare, and the melody bent itself tenderly around her name, weaving *Tamsin* into the fabric of it, dissolving her into the chord the way sugar dissolves into warm water, and her grip on his wrist was slipping, her fingers going slack one by one, and her eyes were glazing, and the last thing she managed was barely a breath:

"—don't sing along. Whatever happens. However good it feels. It will feel like coming home. It lies."

Then her hand released his wrist, and Tamsin Reeve sat back, and folded her hands, and faced the board, and breathed in unison with the other thirty, and the harmony settled over the room like snowfall, complete, undisturbed, satisfied.

Mirabel knelt on the floor of a classroom that had been dead for fifty years, holding his own wrist where her fingers had been, and shook.

* * *

He didn't remember standing up. He found himself at the front of the room, facing the class, the roster crushed forgotten in his fist. Twenty-nine faces watched the board. Two—Tamsin and, faintly, stubbornly, Aldous Wren—watched *him*, from behind the glass of their stillness, and he understood that those two flickers were the entire budget of resistance remaining in this room, and that he had just spent most of it, and that the Choir had let him spend it because hope, as Tamsin said, was how it fed.

*Hope is how it harvests.* It didn't drag them down. It let them rise, just high enough to see the surface, and then it pulled.

Behind the teacher's desk, on the chalkboard, beneath the sentence-diagramming exercise, new writing was appearing. Not written—*condensing*, the way breath condenses on cold glass, letters assembling out of the dust in the air itself, in a hand Mirabel recognized with a sick drop of his stomach because it was neat and careful and belonged to a man who offered tenure like a gift:

**Lovely work, Mr. Blackwood.**

**But rehearsal is nearly over, and you are needed upstairs.**

**Your verse awaits.**

Below the words, in the same elegant script, someone had drawn a single musical staff. Five empty lines, hanging in the chalk dust.

And on the fifth line, in handwriting that was unmistakably, horribly his own, the first note of the ninth verse had already been written.

Upstairs, faintly, through fifty feet of earth and foundation and lie, Mirabel heard the bell ring.
