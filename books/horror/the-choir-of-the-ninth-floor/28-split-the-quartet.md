## 28. Split the Quartet

**DEVI**

Devi Okafor knew something was wrong before she opened her classroom door, because the hallway smelled of bleach and the fluorescent lights were the wrong color—too white, too steady, stripped of their usual tired flicker. Halloway's lights had always flickered. She'd catalogued it in her first week like a specimen: *Halloway Prep, electrical system, chronic arrhythmia.* Now they burned flat and constant, like eyes that had stopped pretending to blink.

A notice was taped to every door. OFFICIAL MEMO: ASBESTOS REMEDIATION — EAST WING CLOSED. SIGNED: A. VOSS.

"Asbestos," she said aloud, to no one. "In October."

She'd taught at three schools before Halloway. Asbestos remediation meant plastic sheeting and men in respirators and a semester of portable classrooms. It did not mean the east wing doors being replaced overnight with solid steel panels painted to look like oak. It did not mean the phones in the faculty lounge gone from their cradles, leaving pale rectangles of unfaded paint on the walls, like the school had grown around them and then thought better of it.

It did not mean Voss waiting for her at the end of the corridor, hands folded, smiling his gentle smile, with Hollis standing two steps behind him like a shadow that had learned to wear a flannel shirt.

"Dr. Okafor." Voss's voice was warm as bathwater. "Walk with me?"

"Where are the phones."

"Being serviced. You know how old buildings are—they develop habits we have to correct." He fell into step beside her without waiting for permission. "I wanted to speak with you privately because I believe you're a reasonable woman surrounded by unreasonable circumstances. And because I think you want what I want."

"And what's that?"

"For everyone under this roof to be safe." They passed a window. Outside, the grounds were empty—not weekend-empty but *held*-empty, the way a stage is empty between acts. Even the crows were gone from the water tower. "Mirabel is a fine teacher. Sensitive. Imaginative. But imagination is a fire, Dr. Okafor, and right now he's carrying it through a house full of drapes. Mr. Hollis has told me about your little meetings. The whiskey. The maps."

Devi kept her face still, which took more effort than any experiment she'd ever run. *Hollis.* The word turned over in her chest like a stone in a tumbler, grinding everything smooth and cold. She thought of him pouring drinks with those big careful hands, laughing at Ruth's rhymes, drawing tunnel diagrams on napkins with the concentration of a man defusing something.

"I don't know what he told you," she said, "but—"

"He told me the truth, which is rarer than loyalty and considerably more useful." Voss stopped at the stairwell door—the one that led up, not down, though Devi had never once thought about the ninth floor in this corridor until now, and hated herself for thinking about it now. "Here is my offer, and it is the only one I will make. Your laboratory. The real one. Climate control, fume hoods, a budget that doesn't require bake sales. The room you described to the search committee four years ago, in your interview, when you thought no one was listening. I was listening. I listen to everything in this building."

The laboratory. She had described it once, half-drunk on hope, to a panel of strangers: north light, double sinks, a door that locked from *her* side. She had built it so many times in her head it had furniture.

"In exchange for what?"

"A report. Weekly. On Mirabel Blackwood. What he knows, what he's learning, what he hums when he thinks no one can hear." Voss's smile didn't move but something behind it did, some deep musculature of patience flexing. "You wouldn't be betraying him. You'd be *monitoring* him. For his own good. There's a difference, and good scientists respect differences."

Through the wall behind them—through the plaster, the lath, the decades of paint—something began to hum. Four notes, then four again, layered, patient. Devi felt it in her molars.

Voss tilted his head as if listening to a favorite movement. "They're practicing," he said fondly. "Harvest is close. So. Do we have an understanding?"

Devi looked at Hollis. Hollis looked at the floor.

"Yes," she said. "We have an understanding."

Voss beamed and pressed a small brass key into her palm, warm as if it had been held in a mouth. "I knew you were sensible. Oh—one more thing. Ruth has taken ill. She's resting in the infirmary. You may visit during posted hours." His smile widened by a millimeter. "Posted hours are whenever I say they are."

**RUTH**

Ruth had hidden in the boiler room in 1974 while roll call went on above her, and she had spent fifty years learning every way a building could lie, so she recognized the infirmary for what it was within minutes of waking there: not a room but a throat. Long and narrow, windowless since the renovation nobody remembered approving, with a door at each end that didn't open so much as *swallow*—the hinges made a sound like a gulp.

Her hands wouldn't rhyme anymore. That was how she knew it was bad. Since her descent below—since she'd stood in front of the Choir and offered her fifty years of swept floors and burning lights like coins at a tollbooth—words came out of her bent into pairs, *day/away, keep/sleep*, whether she wanted them or not. But lying in the infirmary bed, trying to shape even a crooked prayer, she got nothing. Silence where the rhymes had been. As if whatever lived downstairs had taken back its change.

Or as if it was saving her voice for something.

There was an IV in her arm. She hadn't been sick. She'd gone to bed in her room above the boiler room, in her own narrow bed with its fifty years of grooves, and woken here, strapped not by leather but by architecture—the bed placed precisely in the center of the room, equidistant from both swallowing doors, the way you'd position a tuning fork.

"Hello?" she tried. Her voice came out flat. Single notes. No pairs. It frightened her worse than anything the Choir had ever done, and the Choir had once sung her name in her dead mother's register.

The lights overhead were the wrong kind of white. Steady. Unblinking.

Somewhere beyond the far door, faintly, she heard Devi's voice—raised, controlled, the particular tone Devi used when she was being lied to by someone she'd decided to forgive anyway. Then Voss's answer, too soft to parse, and then footsteps going away, and then nothing, and the nothing had texture. It had harmony underneath it, very low, like a refrigerator hum if refrigerators could hold a grudge.

Ruth pulled the IV from her arm with the calm of a woman who'd cauterized her own split knuckles over boilers for decades. The needle came out clean. Her blood was ordinary. That was almost funny.

She got up and put her palm against the near door. Warm. Not mechanically warm—*breathed-on* warm, the warmth of a thing that had recently been singing.

"Fifty years," she said to the door, and her voice cracked on the last syllable, mercifully, into something that almost rhymed with itself. "Fifty years I kept your lights on. You owe me a door that opens."

The door, gently, opened six inches. Enough to see the corridor beyond. Enough to see Mirabel Blackwood being walked down it between two maintenance men she'd never seen before, men who moved slightly out of sync with each other, like footage of walking played at two speeds. His head hung. His lips were moving—counting, she'd have bet her pension, counting doors, counting anything—and his hands were empty except for a keyring he clutched like a rosary.

He looked up as they passed. Through the six-inch gap their eyes met, and his mouth shaped a word at her, silent, deliberate:

*Thursday.*

Then the gap closed, swallowed, gulped, and Ruth was alone with the humming, doing the arithmetic of days with fingers that suddenly felt fifty years older than the rest of her.

**MIRABEL**

He woke to the smell of wet concrete and the sound of his own name being hummed.

Not spoken. Hummed—two syllables set to the first four notes of verse one, *Mi-ra-bel*, rising and settling, rising and settling, coming from the direction of the excavated door.

He was in the annex. He knew it before his eyes opened, from the smell alone: raw earth, sawn lumber, the mineral breath of ground that hadn't seen air in centuries. When he did open his eyes he found himself on a cot that hadn't been there yesterday, under a work lamp that glowed the same wrong steady white as the infirmary, in a space that had been an open construction pit a week ago and was now a finished room. Walls of poured concrete. One steel door, no handle on the inside. And beside it, framed in rough timber, the door.

The door from beneath the foundations. Brass plate gleaming: CHOIR ROOM. Sealed from the inside for fifty years, clawed at from within by hands that couldn't decide which direction out was. It sat in its frame like a portrait of a person who was awake.

And it was humming his name.

Mirabel sat up slowly. His body reported its inventory: bruised temple, no shoes, keyring present—he'd slept clutching it, and the mystery key had pressed its teeth into his palm hard enough to leave a mirrored bite. The room had no windows. It had, he noticed with a lurch, exactly one other feature: a small wooden chair, positioned facing the door, at the precise distance a person would sit to listen.

Someone had prepared this room for him. Someone had measured the distance between a man and a song.

"All right," he said aloud, and his voice sounded obscene in all that concrete, human and cracking and mortal. "I'm awake. Whatever you're selling—"

The door hummed louder. Not verse one now. Something newer. Something with a melody he almost recognized, and the almost was the worst part, because when he reached for where he knew it from, he found the memory already wearing his own handwriting: the blank stave in the hymnal cavity, filling itself in night after night, note by note, in a hand that was his.

Verse nine. Partial. Being written in him whether he consented or not, and now the door was *singing it back to him*, checking its work, the way a teacher hums along while a student reads aloud.

"No." He pressed his palms against his ears, which helped exactly as much as it ever helps. "I'm not learning it. I'm not—I won't—"

But he was. He could feel it settling into him like sediment, each phrase finding its shelf. And beneath the terror, treacherously, was beauty. The ninth verse was beautiful. That was the trap inside the trap: it wasn't a punishment, it was a gift, and gifts are so much harder to refuse than chains.

He thought of Tamsin's mouth shaping *don't learn the last verse*. He thought of Ruth behind her six-inch door, mouthing nothing he could read. He thought of Devi's laugh, Hollis's napkin maps, whiskey passed in paper cups in a storage room that smelled of turpentine and defiance—his people, his strange brave broken people, scattered now or worse, and no way to know which.

The steel door boomed. Once. A sound like a knuckle on a coffin lid.

Then, muffled but clear, a voice he knew—warm as bathwater, patient as erosion.

"Mr. Blackwood. I do apologize for the accommodations. But harvest is tomorrow, and I find I need you somewhere quiet." A pause, and Mirabel could hear the smile in it. "You'll want to rest your voice. You'll want to rest it very badly. What you're going to do with it tomorrow—well. Mr. Aldery left instructions, and I intend for you to follow them perfectly."

Footsteps receded. The lamp stayed on. The door went on humming his name, sweetly, insistently, teaching him a song he was already learning, and Mirabel Blackwood sat down in the chair that had been measured for him, gripped the key that fit nothing, and began—to drown it out, he told himself, only to drown it out—to count.

One door. Two doors. Three.

Behind him, softly, the excavated door counted along.
