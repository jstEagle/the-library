## 1. Attendance

The rain had been falling on Marrowgate Academy since roughly the invention of rain, or so it seemed to Coral Lockridge as he stood at the foot of the drive with his one suitcase and his one satchel and watched the school materialize out of the gray like a photograph developing in slow motion.

It was a handsome building, he'd give it that. Old stone gone the color of wet ash, mullioned windows catching what little light the morning had to offer and hoarding it. Ivy climbed the eastern wing with the confidence of something that had been given tenure. A clock tower rose from the center of the main block, its face reading twenty past eight, which was either correct or a lie Coral would learn to live with.

He checked his watch. Twenty past eight.

"Well," he said aloud, to no one, because that was the sort of man he was — the sort who narrated to himself when nervous, who kept receipts for purchases as small as a pencil, who had once, at age nine, written down a list of every fear he could think of and then crossed them off one by one as they failed to kill him. The list had taken three days to compile and eleven years to retire. He still had it, folded into eighths, in a drawer in his mother's house two hundred miles south.

Marrowgate Academy. His first real post. Assistant teacher of English and History, lower forms, probationary, salary modest but regular. He had read the letter of appointment so many times he could recite it, and had, on the train, quietly, under his breath, until the woman across the aisle had moved her newspaper closer to her face.

He was twenty-three years old, he stammered when tired or frightened, and he had never been more hopeful about anything in his life.

The hope was important. He wanted to be clear about that, later, whenever anyone asked him how it began. It began with hope. It began with a young man standing in the rain outside a beautiful old school, rehearsing his introduction — *Good morning, Headmistress, I'm Coral Lockridge, I'm your new assistant* — and getting the words right on the third try, silently, lips barely moving, the way he'd taught himself to do since he was six years old and the letter C had become a wall between him and the rest of the alphabet.

*C-C-Coral.* Three knocks before the door opened. That was the rule. You knocked three times and eventually someone let you in.

He picked up his suitcase and walked up the drive.

* * *

The front doors were oak, dark with age, and opened before he reached them — which startled him until he saw the small boy holding one of them open, a thin child of perhaps eleven in a uniform slightly too large for him, tie knotted with the desperate precision of someone who had been told twice about his tie.

"You're the new sir," the boy said. Not a question.

"I'm the new sir," Coral agreed. The words came out clean. Good omen. "Coral Lockridge."

"Odd name," said the boy, without malice, in the way of children everywhere, and held the door wider. "I'm Pell. Imogen's brother. Tobias."

"Imogen?"

"My sister." Something flickered across the boy's face — quick, like a fish turning under water — and then it was gone, replaced by the polite blankness children deploy around adults they've only just met. "She's in Third Form. You'll have her. Had her. Will have her." He frowned at his own tenses as though they'd misbehaved. "Come on, then. Miss Vane's expecting you. She's always expecting people. It's a bit unnerving, actually."

"Is it?"

"She said 'Mr. Lockridge will arrive at twenty past eight' on Sunday. At assembly. In front of everyone." Tobias led him through an entrance hall that smelled of beeswax and cold stone, past a display case of tarnished trophies, toward a staircase wide enough to drive a cart up. "Nobody told her when you were coming. We checked. Matron swears she never got a letter."

Coral considered several responses to this and settled on: "Perhaps I'm simply predictable."

"You don't look predictable," said Tobias Pell, with the frankness of the very young. "You look like a librarian who's been caught outdoors."

It was, Coral would reflect later, the most accurate description of himself he would ever receive, and he received it within four minutes of arrival.

* * *

Headmistress Vane received him in a study on the first floor, a long room lined with books whose spines had faded to identical shades of brown and dignity. She stood behind her desk to shake his hand — a tall woman, silver-haired, with the kind of composed face that seemed designed by committee — and her grip was dry and firm and exactly two seconds long.

"Mr. Lockridge. Welcome to Marrowgate." Her voice was warm the way a radiator is warm: functional, even, and slightly difficult to locate the source of. "How were the trains?"

"D-Delayed," Coral said, "but only once, and only briefly."

"Good. Punctuality is a virtue we prize here." She gestured him into a chair and sat opposite him, folding her hands. "You come well recommended. Your references speak of thoroughness."

"I do like things written down," Coral admitted.

"A man after my own heart. Records are the spine of a school. Without them we are merely a crowd of children shouting in a large house." She smiled, and the smile reached all the way to her eyes, which Coral noted with approval, because he had read somewhere — he wrote these things down too, in a notebook labeled *People*, sub-labeled *How To Tell* — that smiles which didn't reach the eyes were worth watching for. Hers reached. He relaxed by perhaps four percent.

"Now. The practicalities." She produced a folder and dealt out papers like a card sharp: timetable, duty roster, a map of the buildings annotated in a fine copperplate hand, a list of form names that sounded less like classes than like weather systems — Second Marsh, Third Hollow, Fourth Fen. "You'll take English with Third Hollow and History with Second Marsh and Fourth Fen. Mr. Quill will show you the ropes; he's been here nineteen years and remembers most of them. Boarding staff eat at high table on Thursdays. Chapel is voluntary but attendance is mysteriously universal."

"Mysteriously?"

"The pews are comfortable and the hymns are short," said Vane, in a tone that closed the subject like a drawer. "Any questions?"

A dozen occurred to him. He chose the safest. "The — the school. How old is it?"

"Older than the town, younger than the church, and considerably older than either would like to admit." Again the smile, again reaching the eyes. "Founded 1712 as a charity school for the parish. Rebuilt several times. The east wing dates from the 1860s; the west wing from 1931; the boiler room from nobody quite knows when, and the less said about the cellar levels the better — you'll find nothing down there but pipes and regret. Any other questions?"

"That was rather a lot of information delivered very quickly," said Coral.

"Yes," said Vane pleasantly. "It's a technique. It prevents follow-up questions. Welcome aboard, Mr. Lockridge."

* * *

They gave him the rest of the morning to settle in, which meant they gave him a room in the masters' corridor — narrow, clean, a bed, a desk, a window looking out on the quadrangle where the rain continued its patient work — and left him alone with it.

He unpacked the way he did everything: systematically, and with documentation. Clothes in the wardrobe, left side shirts, right side trousers. Books on the shelf, alphabetical, because a book out of order was a small scream only he could hear. And then, last, from the satchel, the equipment: two notebooks (one current, one reserve), a bottle of black ink, a tin of pen nibs, a rubber stamp reading RECEIVED that he had bought himself for reasons he had never fully examined, and the tape recorder.

The recorder was his one extravagance. A portable reel-to-reel, secondhand, purchased with three months of savings from his previous position tutoring in Leeds. It was heavier than it looked and looked heavy. He set it on the desk, squared its corners with the desk's edges, and felt some knot behind his sternum loosen by a degree.

Here was the truth about Coral Lockridge, the truth he did not put in applications: he recorded things because the world had a habit of telling him he hadn't heard correctly. Teachers, doctors, his own father — *no, that's not what happened, Coral, you weren't listening, you never listen* — and the stammer hadn't helped, hadn't helped at all, because a boy who repeats himself three times per word learns early that speech is unreliable, that words go out into the air bent and broken, and the only place they keep their shape is on paper. Or tape. Or carbon paper, God bless it, that lovely invention whereby one act of writing became two, three, four permanent witnesses.

If it was written down, it had happened. If it had happened, it could be survived. This was not a philosophy he had chosen so much as one that had chosen him, at age seven, in a doctor's waiting room, when he had written *the doctor will say it is nothing* on the back of an appointment card, and the doctor had said it was nothing, and the sentence had held true like a rail holding a train.

He would begin his log tonight, he decided. Dictation, proper, spoken into the machine — good practice for the stammer besides, since the machine never interrupted and never finished his sentences wrong.

But first, the tour. There came a knock, three precise raps, and Mr. Quill entered without waiting — a round, rumpled man with spectacles thick as portholes and the air of someone perpetually mid-sigh.

"Lockridge? Quill. Classics, nominally; in practice, whatever Vane hands me. Come along, I'll show you the geography before it shows you."

* * *

The tour took an hour and covered, by Coral's count, forty-one rooms, three staircases he was assured were forbidden, two staircases he was assured were merely discouraged, one chapel, one dining hall, one library ("the books are catalogued, technically, in the sense that a battlefield is organized"), and one classroom that would matter more than any of the others, though neither man knew it yet.

Third Hollow's room sat at the end of the west wing, north-facing, its windows giving onto a strip of lawn and, beyond that, a low stone wall and the beginning of the grounds proper, all dripping laurel and gravel paths going nowhere in particular. Twenty-eight desks in five rows. Blackboard, easel, a cupboard that exhaled chalk dust when opened.

"Your kingdom," said Quill. "Third Form. Decent lot, on the whole. Eleven-year-olds are the best age — old enough to reason with, young enough to still believe reasoning works." He consulted a clipboard. "Twenty-nine pupils on the register. You'll meet most of them tomorrow."

"Most?"

Quill paused, in the manner of a man stepping on something soft in the dark. "One absent. Imogen Pell. Family matter. Been away a fortnight."

"A fortnight is a long family matter."

"It happens." Quill said it lightly, but he said it fast, the way one steps over a threshold without breaking stride, and Coral — who noticed things, who had trained himself since childhood to notice things precisely because noticing and recording were the same muscle — filed the speed of it away. "Right. Staff photo's on the wall there, if you want to see the faces that go with the names. Vane likes new staff to study it. Says it builds collegiality."

The photograph hung beside the door, framed in dark wood: the full student body of Marrowgate Academy, arranged on the front steps in the fashion of such photographs, back rows standing on benches, front row cross-legged on the stone. It was dated, in white ink in the corner, *Michaelmas 1971*. Three years old. The children in it squinted into sun that seemed, looking out the rain-streaked window now, to belong to another geological era.

Coral studied it, because he had been told to and because studying photographs was among his chief pleasures. Faces, faces, rows of them — and then, in the third row up, slightly left of center, a gap in the pattern that stopped his eye the way a wrong note stops an ear.

One child's face had been inked out.

Not scribbled. Not defaced. *Inked* — filled in with a careful, patient application of black, the whole oval of the face rendered solidly, smoothly dark, the way a censor blocks a word in a letter. Whoever had done it had worked with a fine brush or a very steady pen, staying neatly inside the outline of the head, not touching the hair, not touching the collar. The body remained: grey blazer, striped tie, hands folded. Only the face was gone, replaced by a rectangle of perfect night.

"Huh," said Coral.

"What?" Quill had drifted to the window and was watching the rain with the expression of a man negotiating with it.

"This photograph. Someone's inked out one of the students."

Quill turned. He looked at the picture for a moment — Coral watched his face while he did it, and what he saw was not surprise, and not embarrassment, but something quieter and worse: recognition, followed by decision. Quill's eyes went from the inked face to Coral and back again, and then he shrugged, a whole-body shrug that rearranged his cardigan.

"Old damage," he said. "Photographs get marked up. Children with pens, you know."

"With a brush?" said Coral. "That's — that's very neat work for a child. It's inside the lines."

"Neat children," said Quill. "We enroll them young." He smiled, and Coral, watching, observed that the smile arrived at Quill's mouth first and set out toward his eyes at a leisurely pace, like a commuter who suspected he'd missed the train. "Come on, I'll show you the staff room. There's a kettle. There is, in fact, only a kettle; the entire culture of this institution is built around it."

Coral followed him out. But at the doorway he stopped, and looked back at the photograph, and made himself do the thing he always did, the thing that had gotten him called obsessive by tutors and thorough by examiners: he counted.

Back row: fourteen. Second row: thirteen. Third row: twelve, including the inked face. Front row, seated and cross-legged: ten. Forty-nine children, Michaelmas 1971.

And the frame — he tilted his head — the frame's glass, old and slightly wavy, showed faint marks beneath the surface reflection, ghost-rows of shadow where the light caught it sideways. As if the photograph had been reprinted, or trimmed, or as if other pictures had hung in this exact spot before this one, for years, facing the same door, being seen by the same passing eyes.

He memorized the position of the inked face — third row, fourth from the left, directly above the head of a grinning boy holding what appeared to be a frog — and let it go. First day. First days were for kettles.

But that night, in his narrow room with the rain still working the windows, he set the tape recorder on the desk, threaded the reel, pressed record, and spoke his first log into the warm hiss of the machine:

"*Log one. Monday the — the ninth. Arrived Marrowgate Academy, twenty past eight, as predicted by Headmistress Vane two days prior to any notification of my travel arrangements, which is either remarkable efficiency or something else. Staff appear pleasant. Building appears sound. One pupil, Imogen Pell, absent a fortnight, cause given as family matter. One photograph, Michaelmas 1971, containing forty-nine pupils, one of whom has had their face removed with what I can only describe as care.*"

He paused. The reels turned. Rain ticked against the glass.

"*I am probably being silly,*" he added. "*New places make me notice too much. I once spent a week convinced my landlady in Leeds was poisoning me because she hummed while cooking. She was just humming.*"

He stopped the tape. Rewound it. Played it back, checking the stammer — two hesitations, acceptable — and noting, with satisfaction, that the machine had caught everything exactly as it happened.

The machine always caught everything exactly as it happened.

That was what he loved about it.

Down the corridor, faintly, through two closed doors and the sound of the rain, Coral thought he heard singing — a single low note, sustained, somewhere below the floorboards, the kind of sound a boiler makes, or a pipe, or a building settling into its own foundations. He listened for a moment, decided it was the boiler, wrote *boiler, note it if recurring* in the margin of tomorrow's page, and went to sleep.

The note, whatever it was, went on for another hour, steady as a held breath, and then stopped — precisely, Coral would later swear, precisely when his breathing slowed into sleep, as if it had been waiting to see him safely down.
