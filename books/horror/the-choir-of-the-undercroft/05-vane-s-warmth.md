## 5. Vane's Warmth

The invitation arrived on cream cardstock, hand-lettered in a copperplate so perfect it looked printed, and Coral spent a full minute admiring the penmanship before he registered what it actually said.

*Headmistress Vane requests the pleasure of Mr. Lockridge's company at a Welcome Tea, Friday at four o'clock, in the Long Gallery. Dress is comfortable. Company will be warmer.*

*Company will be warmer.* He turned the card over twice, looking for the joke, the second meaning, the small print. There was none. At Marrowgate Academy, apparently, warmth was simply announced, like fire drills.

He had been at the school nine days. Nine days of rain-gray mornings and corridors that smelled of beeswax and old stone, nine days of teaching Third Form English to children who were, without exception, polite, attentive, and faintly translucent — present in body, but as though someone had turned their volume down to three. Nine days of Imogen Pell's empty desk, which he had stopped touching each morning because the warmth of it had begun to feel less like an accident and more like an invitation.

And now: tea.

"You'll want to wear something with pockets," said Mrs. Halloway, the matron, when he asked her about it in the corridor. She was a broad, brisk woman with a face like a kindly hatchet, and she was carrying a stack of folded towels for no reason Coral could determine, having passed her three times that day with the same stack. "The Headmistress's teas go long."

"Long how?"

"Long," she said, with the finality of a woman closing a ledger, and walked on.

* * *

The Long Gallery ran along the east face of the main building, all tall windows and darker portraits, and by four o'clock it had been transformed into something almost aggressively pleasant. Bunting — actual bunting, in a school where two students had gone missing inside a term. A tiered stand of sandwiches with the crusts removed so thoroughly it was unclear whether bread remained at their centers. A samovar. A *samovar*, at a rural academy in the north of England, polished to a shine that suggested either deep tradition or a very recent purchase.

Headmistress Vane stood at the center of it like a lighthouse made welcome.

Coral had met her properly only once before, on his first morning — a tall woman in dove-gray wool, silver hair pinned with mathematical precision, and a smile that arrived a half-second before any reason for it. She had shaken his hand with both of hers, which he'd privately filed under *enthusiasm* and now, watching her work the room, refiled under *technique*.

"Mr. Lockridge!" she called, before he'd even cleared the doorway, as though she'd been tracking his approach from orbit. "There he is. The newest voice in our little choir."

The phrase landed oddly. He watched to see if anyone else noticed. Nobody did. The assembled faculty — perhaps fifteen of them, arranged in a semicircle with the practiced ease of people who had done this many times — smiled at him in unison, a wave of goodwill rolling toward him like weather.

"Thank you for having me," he said.

"Oh, we're *delighted* to have you." Vane took his arm. Her grip was light and her hand was warm — genuinely, unusually warm, like a stone left too near a fire. "Come. You must meet everyone properly. Tea first, I insist. Milk?"

"Please."

"Sugar?"

"No, thank you."

"One sugar," she said pleasantly, and put one sugar in his tea.

* * *

The faculty, it must be said, were lovely. That was the thing Coral kept circling back to over the following hour, the way you worry a loose tooth: they were *lovely*. Warm, funny, generous with the sandwiches. Mr. Prentiss, the groundskeeper, told him about the time a swan got into the swimming pool. Miss Aldery, who taught mathematics, described the annual Founders' Day pageant with such relish that Coral found himself laughing along. Dr. Fenwick of the sciences did an impression of a particularly pompous inspector that had Vane dabbing her eyes.

It was all delightful. It was also, Coral realized somewhere around his third cup of tea, identical.

He'd been introduced down the line — Prentiss, Aldery, Fenwick, Mrs. Coyle of the kitchens, old Mr. Tarn of the chapel — and each of them had told him an anecdote, and the anecdotes were not merely similar. They were the same. Word for word.

Prentiss: "You'll find the children here are different from anywhere else you've taught. Softer-spoken. We think of it as the house style." Then the swan story.

Aldery: "You'll find the children here are different from anywhere else you've taught. Softer-spoken. We think of it as the house style." Then the pageant story.

Fenwick: "You'll find the children here are different from anywhere else you've taught. Softer-spoken. We think of it as the house style." Then the inspector impression.

Coral set down his cup and listened harder. It wasn't just the opening line. The stories themselves had a shared architecture, the same beats arriving in the same order, the same pauses for laughter landing at the same syllables. When Prentiss told the swan story, he paused after "*and the beast looked me dead in the eye*" — a full second, exactly where Aldery paused after "*and the man looked me dead in the eye*," and exactly where Fenwick paused after "*and the fellow looked me dead in the eye*."

Three stories. Three tellers. One skeleton wearing three coats.

He glanced at Vane. She was watching him — not obviously, not rudely, but with the mild attention of a gardener checking whether a cutting had taken. When their eyes met, her smile widened by a precise, calibrated degree, and she raised her own cup to him across the room.

*Well,* Coral thought, *either this is a very strange coincidence, or these people rehearse their hospitality.*

He thought about his carbon copies upstairs, the neat triplicate record of every attendance sheet and incident note he'd filed since day one, and felt the familiar small comfort of them. Whatever this was, it would go in the log. Everything went in the log. Things written down became real, and real things could be survived.

"More tea?" said Mrs. Coyle, appearing at his elbow with the pot already tilted.

"Thank you — actually, could I trouble you for water? Just water."

"Of course, dear." She poured. "You'll find the children here are different from anywhere else you've taught."

Coral looked up at her. Mrs. Coyle was sixty if she was a day, flour still dusting one forearm, kind eyes crinkled with genuine warmth. She said the line the way you say your own name — without thinking, without weight, the way a clock says *tick*.

"Softer-spoken," Coral supplied carefully.

"We think of it as the house style," she agreed, beaming, and moved on to refill Dr. Fenwick's cup.

His skin prickled pleasantly, unpleasantly, the way it does when a dream follows you into daylight. He drank his water and smiled at everyone and began, quietly, methodically, to count.

* * *

The tea lasted two hours. Vane was right; they went long. And through all of it, Coral performed the part of the charmed newcomer while some quieter clerk behind his eyes took minutes.

He noted that nobody asked him a single question about himself. Not where he'd trained, not whether he had family, not why a man of thirty-one would take a post at a school this far off any sensible map. Fifteen colleagues, two hours, and the sum total of personal inquiry was Mrs. Coyle asking whether he preferred his toast buttered on one side or two. (One side, he'd said. She'd brought him two.)

He noted that the conversation never touched the missing girls. Not once. Two students gone — Imogen Pell vanished three weeks before his arrival, and before her, a girl named Sallow whose name appeared in the registers with a small, neat annotation in violet ink: *family matter.* Fifteen adults who must all have known them, and the closest anyone came was Mr. Tarn remarking, apropos of nothing, that "the term feels lighter this year, doesn't it," and everyone nodding as though he'd said something comforting rather than horrifying.

He noted the bunting. Somebody had hung bunting for a welcome tea at a school with empty desks, and every single person in the room seemed entirely at peace with that arithmetic.

And then, near the end, when the crowd had thinned and the samovar had gone quiet, Headmistress Vane drew him aside beneath the longest portrait wall and showed him the photograph.

"Now," she said, "I wanted you to see this properly, away from the crush. This is our faculty as it stood in — oh, let me think — 1968, I believe. My first year." She lifted a framed picture from its hook with both hands, tenderly, the way one lifts a sleeping child.

It was a formal group shot, two rows of staff arranged on the front steps of the main building, squinting into a sunnier decade than this one. Coral counted automatically — it was a habit, counting, the stammering boy's oldest trick, because numbers never caught in the throat.

"There are twenty-two people in this photograph," he said.

"There are indeed." Vane's voice had gone soft and fond. "Fine colleagues. Fine years."

Sixteen faces looked out at him clearly. The other six had been inked out.

Not scribbled, not defaced — *inked out*, with a fine brush or a very steady pen, each face replaced by a smooth oval of black so uniform it might have been printed that way. No rage in it. No grief. Just careful, patient erasure, the work of someone with good light and all the time in the world.

"That's unusual," Coral said. His voice came out level. He was proud of that.

"Mm?" Vane tilted the frame, considering it. "Oh — photography, you know. Some of the older prints didn't take. Light damage. Silver salts do peculiar things over the decades." She said it lightly, reasonably, the way one explains damp in an old cellar. "We had them restored as best we could."

"Light damage," Coral repeated. "In ovals."

"The restorer worked in a particular style." Her smile was serene. "He's since retired."

She hung the photograph back on its hook, straightened it by a millimeter, and patted the frame once, gently, the way you'd pat the shoulder of an old friend who'd said something embarrassing.

But Coral was still looking at it, because his counting habit had betrayed him again, run ahead of his manners, and delivered its finding before he could stop it.

Twenty-two figures. Six inked faces among the *faculty*.

And he thought of the student photographs in the entrance hall — the class pictures going back year upon year, the ones he'd studied on his first morning, where he'd noticed a single inked-out child in the 1971 group and assumed it a one-off, a private grief, a family's request. He hadn't counted those. He should have counted those. He was suddenly, urgently certain that if he went downstairs tonight with a pencil and a tally sheet, the ovals in the student photos would number fewer than the ovals here — that the staff of Marrowgate Academy had lost more of their own, decade upon decade, than the children ever had.

That the teachers' photograph was a mass grave with frames.

"Mr. Lockridge." Vane touched his sleeve. "You've gone quiet."

"Just taking it in," he said. "It's a wonderful institution."

"It is," she agreed warmly. "It takes care of us. That's what people never understand about old schools — people imagine we keep *them*." She gestured at the stones around them, the gallery, the whole gray edifice, with an affection so unguarded it was almost childlike. "But it's the other way round. Marrowgate keeps *us*. Every one of us, in our turn. Isn't that a lovely thought?"

It was not a lovely thought. It was the least lovely thought Coral had ever been offered with a smile, and he felt it slide down into him like cold tea.

"Very lovely," he said.

"You'll stay for supper? The kitchen's made pie."

"I should get back to my marking," he said, and was already moving toward the door, already composing the log entry in his head — *Friday, 4:00 p.m., Long Gallery, welcome tea attended, fifteen staff present, anecdotes verbatim-identical across subjects, faculty photo 1968, six faces obscured, see also entrance hall class photos, COUNT THE OVALS* — when Vane spoke again behind him, pleasantly, in the tone of a hostess remembering a small courtesy.

"Oh — one more thing, Mr. Lockridge."

He turned. She stood beneath the portrait wall with the lamplight gilding her silver hair, hands folded, smiling her half-second-early smile.

"Do sleep well," she said. "The house can be noisy at night for newcomers. Old buildings settle." A pause, delicate as pastry. "And whatever you hear — don't write it down."

The gallery was silent. Somewhere below, a door closed softly, the way doors do when they're being closed carefully.

Coral smiled, thanked her for a lovely afternoon, and climbed the stairs to his room with his heart going like a trapped bird — because he had never told anyone at Marrowgate about the tapes.
