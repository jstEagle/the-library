## 7. The Sleep Tapes

The recorder arrived on a Tuesday, in a box that had been dropped at least twice by the look of it, and Coral Lockridge set it on his desk with the reverence other men reserved for religious objects.

It was a secondhand Uher, bought from an electronics shop in town that smelled of solder and dust and the particular loneliness of machines nobody wanted anymore. The proprietor, a stooped man with reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair, had tested it for him twice.

"Good machine," the man had said. "Older than you, probably. They built them to last back then." He'd tapped the casing with one yellowed fingernail. "What's it for, if you don't mind my asking? Dictation?"

"Something like that," Coral said, because *I want to record myself sleeping so I can find out if I stammer in my dreams* was not a sentence he had ever managed to say aloud without the stammer making it worse.

He carried it back up the hill to Marrowgate through a drizzle that couldn't decide whether to be rain or fog, and by the time he reached his room in the masters' wing, his shoes were soaked and his mood was — surprisingly — good. There is a particular comfort, Coral had found, in acquiring a new instrument of documentation. A fresh notebook. A full ink cartridge. A machine with reels waiting to be fed. Each one was a small promise: *this will hold. This will keep. Whatever happens, there will be a record.*

His room looked out over the quad, where the rain had darkened the flagstones to the color of wet slate. Somewhere below, the boiler room exhaled its low, patient heat through the building's bones. Coral unpacked the recorder, threaded the tape with the care of a surgeon, and tested it.

"*Testing,*" he said, and winced at himself. "*T-testing. One, two.*"

On playback: *Testing. T-t-testing. One, two.*

Honest as a mirror. That was the point. He had spent thirty-one years being lied to about his own voice — kindly, mostly, by teachers who said *take your time, Coral* in voices that meant *hurry up* — and he had decided somewhere around his fourteenth birthday that if the world would not give him an accurate account of himself, he would make one.

The carbon copies were for the school. The tapes were for himself.

* * *

The idea for the sleep recordings came, as most of his ideas did, from a problem he couldn't stop turning over.

Imogen Pell had been absent for eleven days now. Eleven days of *a family matter*, delivered by six different colleagues in six different tones that somehow all sounded like the same voice wearing different coats. Eleven mornings of Desk Thirty-One sitting empty in his register, and — this was the part he did not write down, the part he only admitted to himself in the dark — eleven mornings of that desk being warm.

He had checked. He wasn't proud of checking, but he had checked, pressing his palm flat against the wood before first period while the classroom filled around him with the ordinary noise of children who existed. The desk held warmth the way a hand does. Not residual warmth from a radiator — the radiator stood four feet away and gave off nothing but a faint tick. It was body-warmth, specific and recent, as though someone had been sitting there minutes before and risen when they heard his footsteps in the corridor.

He had started leaving the classroom window cracked open overnight, on the theory that whatever sat in Imogen's desk might prefer to leave the way it came. In the morning the window was always closed. The desk was always warm.

So: the sleep tapes. Because three nights running he had woken with the vague sense of having been talking — his jaw sore, his throat dry, the taste of having said something long and important fading faster than a dream — and because his stammer had been behaving strangely all week. Not worse. *Stranger.* It slipped away from him at odd moments, whole sentences arriving smooth as poured cream, and then returned doubled, tripling the simplest words into rubble. His mouth felt less like his own instrument every day and more like a shared one.

"If I'm talking in my sleep," he told the recorder that evening, threading a fresh reel, "*then I w-want to know what I'm saying. That's all. That's the wh-whole of it.*"

He positioned the microphone on his bedside table, angled toward his pillow. He pressed RECORD, checked the reels turned, checked again, and lay down in the dark of the masters' wing with the small red eye of the machine watching over him.

It should have been comforting. Mostly it was. But as he drifted off, he became aware of a sound beneath the floor — or through it, or inside the walls themselves, it was impossible to say — a low tone, steady as a held breath. The boiler, he told himself. Old buildings settle. Old boilers hum.

He fell asleep telling himself that, which is perhaps why the dream he half-remembered featured a congregation standing in water, facing away from him, singing something he could almost — almost — catch.

* * *

Morning arrived gray and punctual. Coral woke, silenced the machine, and went about his day with the tape coiled safely on its reel like a letter he hadn't opened yet. He taught Third Form Latin (*amo, amas, amat* — the children chanting it back at him in a ragged unison that made him think, briefly and unpleasantly, of something else). He took the register. Desk Thirty-One: absent. Warm. He did not touch it this time. He was trying to establish boundaries with furniture, which he recognized, even as he did it, as the behavior of a man losing an argument with a building.

Only after evening prep, when the corridor outside his door had gone quiet, did he sit down with the playback head and the little monitor speaker, a mug of tea going cold beside him, and press PLAY.

The first hour was nothing. Room-sounds. The radiator ticking. Once, distantly, a door closing somewhere in the wing. His own breathing, slow and even — deeper than he'd expected, almost theatrical, the breathing of a man in a play about sleeping.

At one-seventeen in the morning, according to the counter, the breathing changed.

It didn't quicken, exactly. It *organized*. The ragged rhythm of sleep smoothed out into something deliberate, measured, and then — Coral leaned toward the speaker, tea forgotten — came the voice.

"*...and the register must be kept current,*" said Coral Lockridge, clearly, fluently, without a single hesitation, "*because the register is how we know who is still owed.*"

Coral sat very still.

It was his voice. That was the horrible thing, the thing his mind kept reaching for and sliding off of: it was unmistakably his voice, his particular grain, the slight flatness of his vowels. And yet it was not. It was his voice the way a portrait is a face — accurate in every line and wrong in some essential way he couldn't name. Something in the *placement* of it. Something in the certainty.

"*Owed,*" the voice repeated, savoring the word. "*That's what attendance means, underneath. Not presence. Debt. Every name on the list is a name the school is holding. Some are redeemed. Some are kept.*"

There was a pause, long enough that Coral checked the machine hadn't stopped. Then, in the same fluent calm:

"*Desk Thirty-One is warm because she visits it. She remembers her lessons. She wants to be marked present. We let her sit there while the halls are empty. It's kind. Kindness matters here.*"

Coral's hand found the pause button and pressed it harder than necessary. The reels clicked to a stop. The room was utterly silent except for the radiator and his own heart, which had taken up a rhythm entirely unlike the measured breathing on the tape.

He made himself a fresh pot of tea. He told himself, in the reasonable daylight voice he used for calming frightened students, that sleep-talk was a known phenomenon, that people said nonsense all night long, that his own subconscious had simply stitched together the day's worries — Imogen, the register, the warm desk — into a bad monologue. That was the sane explanation. He wrote it down in his log, actually wrote it, *probable somniloquy; content consistent with waking preoccupations*, and the act of writing steadied him the way it always had since he was seven years old and discovered that a stammered word trapped on paper stopped being frightening and became merely a fact.

Then he played the rest of the tape.

Because he was Coral Lockridge, and he had never in his life left a record unexamined.

* * *

At two-forty, the voice began answering questions.

Not questions Coral had asked. Questions Coral had *thought* — that was the only way he could describe it, though he knew how it sounded, knew exactly how it would read in any rational account: the voice on the tape responded, in order, to the things he had wondered at the edges of sleep. Whether Imogen was cold down wherever she was. (The voice said, gently: "*No. The water keeps its own temperature. She doesn't feel it as cold. She feels it as company.*") Whether the other staff heard it too. ("*They hear it the way you hear your own heartbeat. They've agreed not to listen.*") Whether Headmistress Vane knew.

Here the voice paused — a real pause, a thinking pause, Coral's own thinking pause, the one he made before difficult words — and then said:

"*Miss Vane knows what the well costs. She signs for it annually. You'll understand the arithmetic soon. Everyone here learns the arithmetic eventually.*"

And then, at three-eleven, in a tone so mild it was worse than any menace could have been, the voice said:

"*You're wondering if you stammered tonight. You didn't. You never do, down where we are. Down here, everyone's voice comes out finished.*"

The tape ran on for another forty minutes of breathing. Coral let it run to the end, because stopping it felt like flinching, and flinching was the one thing his whole system of carbon copies and careful logs had been built to prevent.

When the final reel wound out with a soft mechanical sigh, he sat in the lamplight for a long time, looking at the machine. Then he did what he always did. He labeled the reel — *Sleep Tape No. 1, Oct. 14–15* — dated it, initialed it, and locked it in the steel box under his bed with the rest of his records.

The lock clicked shut with a sound far too small for what it was holding.

He got into bed. He did not sleep so much as lie in ambush for morning, and when morning came he took the register, and Desk Thirty-One was empty, and warm, and he pressed his palm against the wood before he could stop himself, and the warmth was fainter today. Fainter, and — he bent close, telling himself he was checking for dust — slightly damp.

While the children filed in, chattering about nothing, Coral stood at his desk and found his pen hovering over the attendance book, and realized his jaw ached, and his throat was dry, and he had the distinct, unforgivable sense of having said something long and important in the night that he could not remember saying.

He flipped open the steel box that evening and threaded Sleep Tape No. 2 onto the reels before he could talk himself out of it, because whatever was using his mouth at night had mentioned Miss Vane, and had mentioned arithmetic, and Coral Lockridge intended to know the sum.

But it was the last line of the first tape that followed him down the dark toward sleep, the line he had not written in the log because he could not make his hand do it:

*"Down here, everyone's voice comes out finished."*

And beneath the floorboards, faint and patient, the boiler — or whatever wore the boiler's hum — seemed to agree.
