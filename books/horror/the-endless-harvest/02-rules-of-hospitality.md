## 2. Rules of Hospitality

Maren Hale cooked the way other people performed surgery: with focus, economy, and an air of not being observed. I sat at her kitchen table — scrubbed pine, older than my grandmother's opinions — and watched her turn a chicken, three onions, and a handful of pale carrots into something that smelled like every good memory I'd ever borrowed from other people's families.

"You'll want bread," she said. It was not a question. She set down a loaf so dark and dense it might have been a building material and cut it with a knife that had clearly been sharpened by someone who loved it.

"I should say," I began, "that I really am grateful for the room—"

"Top of the stairs, end of the hall. Window sticks; lift and push." She said this to the gravy rather than to me. "Bathroom's across. The tap runs brown at first. Let it."

"Let it," I repeated, because apparently I was going to spend this trip repeating things.

"It clears." She glanced up then, briefly, and I got the full force of her attention for perhaps two seconds — grey eyes, level, tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep — before she returned to the gravy as though the exchange had cost her something.

I want to be honest about my state of mind that evening, because honesty is the whole point of what follows. I had been in a hundred kitchens like this one. Rural hospitality has a grammar, and I am fluent in it: the food offered before you can ask, the room described in practical terms, the host who talks to the cooking because looking directly at a stranger costs more than they can spare. I catalogued Maren Hale under *reserved, possibly lonely, grieving someone by the look of her* and moved on.

That is the last entry in which I was entirely wrong about everything.

* * *

The stew was excellent. The bread was better. I ate like the traveling woman I was, and Maren watched me do it with an expression I couldn't place until later — not pleasure exactly, but relief, the way you feel watching someone drink water when you know something about the well.

"There's a question I've been wanting to ask," I said, when my bowl was half empty and my manners were losing the argument with my curiosity. "About the song. This afternoon, when I asked for another verse—"

"I heard what you asked." The ladle stopped moving.

"You went white. I don't mean to pry into anything private, but if there's a reason the song can't be sung twice, that's precisely the kind of thing my work exists to document. Beliefs like that don't come from nowhere. They're usually the fossil record of something real — a bad harvest, a plague, a death. If I understood why—"

"Dr. Zhou." She set the ladle down with great care, the way you put down a sleeping animal. "May I tell you some things? Not about the song. About the house."

"Of course."

She sat down across from me, wiped her hands on her apron, and folded them like a woman preparing to hear difficult news from a doctor.

"The window in your room sticks. Leave it stuck after dark. It's nothing — old frames swell — but leave it stuck."

"All right."

"Don't hum. Anything. Not the song especially, but anything, after sundown. Whistle if you must, but low, and not a tune anyone here would know."

"That's—" I smiled, because the alternative was to point out that I had spent twenty years collecting exactly these rules and had never once heard that particular variant. "That's a new one on me. Usually it's the reverse — you hum to keep spirits away."

"Mm." She did not smile back. "Don't write the words down after dark. Words written after dark stay written."

I felt my eyebrows go up despite myself. "Stay written?"

"As in, they don't stay where you put them." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd report a leaking roof. "Ink moves. Pencil smudges upward, against the grain of the page. And whatever you do, however curious you get, however friendly someone seems — don't ask anyone to sing it twice. Not once more, not a repeat, not *go on then, just the chorus*. Once is a gift. Twice is a request. And Hollowmere does not make requests of that song."

The kitchen was very warm and suddenly I was aware of how dark the windows had gotten. There are no streetlights in Hollowmere worth the name; beyond the glass, the fields went on being night at each other indefinitely.

"Maren," I said, gently, because I liked her and because condescension dressed as kindness is still my worst habit, "you know I study this professionally. These customs — they're fascinating, genuinely, and I'd never mock them. But you don't need to protect me. I've slept in houses with far spookier reputations than yours and woken up fine."

Something moved through her face then. Not anger. Something worse — a kind of pity, quick and suppressed, like a flinch she'd practiced out of herself years ago.

"I expect you have," she said. "And I expect those houses let you leave."

* * *

I took my notebook to my room, because I am who I am, and wrote while there was still light enough to justify it — technically, legally, according to no rule anyone had stated yet.

*Field notes, day one. Subject: harvest song, local name unrecorded (informants decline to name it). Distribution: apparently universal among adult villagers; performance restricted to once annually, once per singer, incomplete. Taboos recorded from innkeeper M. Hale, ca. 60s, third-generation local:*

*1. No second request. Repetition framed as "requesting" — significant verb choice. Implies the song confers something on first hearing that a second hearing would... claim? Demand? Compare Breton case, 1911, where repeated carols were thought to "call the dead to count themselves."*

*2. No humming after dark. Inversion of standard apotropaic humming. Here sound attracts rather than repels. Note: she said "not a tune anyone here would know" — suggests the danger is recognition, not noise itself.*

*3. No transcription after dark. "Words written after dark stay written." Most unusual clause. Suggests text is believed to have agency independent of performance — or that writing constitutes a kind of performance. Cf. cultures where naming summons.*

*4. Windows sealed at night. Practical explanation available (drafts). Probably practical. Probably.*

*I like her. Important to note that too. She fed me like family and lied to me like family — badly, and only about the things that matter.*

I read that last line back and almost crossed it out. I didn't, and I've been glad since, because it's the first true sentence in this entire account.

* * *

There was a knock at my door around nine — soft, double-rapped, hesitant. Ivo Hale stood in the hallway holding a plate of shortbread like a peace offering he wasn't sure would be accepted. He was maybe sixteen, all elbows and restless energy, with Maren's grey eyes but none of her stillness, as if the same material had been poured into a much less patient mold.

"Mum said to bring these up." He said *Mum* with the particular emphasis teenagers reserve for mothers whose rules they find excessive. "She's gone to bed. She goes early. Always has."

"Thank you." I took the plate. He didn't leave. He shifted his weight and looked past me into the room with open envy at the recording equipment laid out on the desk.

"Is that real?" He nodded at the recorder. "Like, actual reel-to-reel? My mate in Ashford's got a cassette player his uncle brought back from Germany and even that's basically witchcraft to us."

"It's real. Older than you, but real."

"Could I—" He caught himself, shoved his hands in his pockets. "Do you actually go places? Like, proper places? Cities? Did you ever meet anyone famous?"

I laughed, and the sound seemed to surprise both of us. "I met a man in Romania who claimed to have invented the accordion out of spite. I interviewed a woman in Louisiana who remembered the flood of '27 and sang hymns to keep the river polite. I once spent three days in a pub in Orkney waiting for a fiddler who turned out to have died in March, though nobody had told him, or possibly he hadn't told anybody."

"Nobody had told him," Ivo repeated, delighted. "See, that's the sort of thing that happens everywhere except here. Nothing happens here. Nothing has happened here since—" He stopped. Recalculated. "Since forever. That's the joke. Hollowmere. Hollow. Empty. Ha ha."

"What were you going to say? Since when?"

"Nothing." Too fast. "It's just a boring place. Everyone says. That's why everyone leaves." But he said it the way people recite things they've been made to memorize, and his eyes went to the window — the stuck window — and away again quickly.

"Ivo," I said, "your grandmother — Maren — gave me some rules tonight. About singing, and writing things down. Do you know about those?"

He looked at me with sudden, total attention, and for a moment the teenager fell away and someone much older looked out — someone doing arithmetic.

"She gave you the rules your first night?"

"Yes."

"Then she likes you." He said it simply, as though it explained everything, and maybe it did. "Most people don't get the rules till they've earned them. Some people never get them at all, and then—" He shrugged elaborately. "Shortbread's going stale."

"Ivo. Then what?"

But he was already backing down the hall, grinning, seventeen kinds of relieved to escape the conversation. "If you hear anything in the night, it's just the house settling. Old houses settle loud here." A pause at the stairs. "Don't answer it, though."

"Don't answer what?"

"Goodnight, Dr. Zhou!"

His footsteps clattered down and away, and I stood in my borrowed room holding a plate of shortbread and feeling, for the first time since the bus dropped me at the crossroads, the specific cold that comes not from fear but from the moment a pattern starts assembling itself faster than you can pretend not to see it.

* * *

I did not sleep well, which I attributed to the mattress, the silence, and the quality of light in a village with no sodium lamps — the darkness out here is not empty but *populated*, full of moving shapes that resolve, when you finally look, into nothing at all.

Around what I judged to be two in the morning, I heard the wheat.

Not metaphorically. Wind moves through a wheat field with a particular hushing voice, a long exhalation, and I know that voice well; I've fallen asleep to it in three countries. This was different. This was rhythmic. Long pause, short rush, long pause, short rush — like breathing held and released, held and released, patient as a tide. I lay in the dark and timed it against my own breath until I realized, with a clarity that felt less like thought than like temperature dropping, that it was keeping pace with me. When I held my breath, the field held its.

Wind doesn't do that. Wind doesn't wait.

I got up — quietly, absurdly, as though the field could hear floorboards — and stood at the stuck window. Below, the lane ran silver-grey toward the black mass of the crop. Everything was still. Everything was ordinary. Somewhere a dog barked twice, lazily, at nothing, and the night resumed its business of being merely night.

I sat back down at the desk. It was, I noted, well after dark. I opened the notebook anyway, because I am a professional, because superstition is data, because rules exist to be documented —

— and found myself unable, for a long moment, to remember the words of the verse Maren had sung that afternoon. All of it gone, the melody and the words together, a smooth blank where four lines had been. I could remember the kitchen, the gravy, the ladle set down like a sleeping animal. The song itself had been removed, or filed somewhere I couldn't reach, and the absence had the specific texture of something done *to* me rather than forgotten *by* me.

I wrote instead:

*Day one, addendum. The field breathes in time with the listener. The song cannot be recalled after dark — or will not recall itself. Maren's rules are not folklore. Folklore is memory wearing costume. Whatever this is, it is current. It is operational. It is, I think, awake.*

*Also: I like them. Maren, Ivo, even the dog. God help me, I already like them, and I know from long experience that liking people is the most dangerous thing a folklorist can do. You stop studying them and start protecting them, and then you're no longer documenting the story.*

*You're in it.*

Below the window, faint and far off, the wheat exhaled once more — and this time, unmistakably, in rhythm with the scratch of my pen.
