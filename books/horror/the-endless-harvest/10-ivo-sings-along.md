## 10. Ivo Sings Along

The trouble with being seventeen in Hollowmere is that you are, by definition, the most interesting thing in any room that doesn't contain a television, and even then it's close. So when Paloma Zhou came to supper with her city clothes and her recorder and her questions about everything — buses, football, whether I'd ever seen the sea (I hadn't) — I did what any self-respecting boy does when handed an audience.

I showed off.

I want that on the record. Not "I was tricked." Not "the song made me." I was showing off, the way you'd do a wheelie on the lane or smoke behind the chapel, because there was a woman at our table who had seen actual cities, and I wanted her to think Hollowmere had produced something worth looking at besides bad weather and better gossip.

It started innocently enough, which is how these things always start. Gran had gone to bed early — she'd been going to bed early all week, since the door in the quarry field opened, though nobody said that's why. Mum was washing dishes with her back to us, which meant she couldn't see my face, only hear me, and there's a whole essay in that alone about why I chose that exact moment.

"Paloma," I said, leaning back in my chair until it creaked, "did Tam ever sing you the second verse?"

She looked up from her notebook. She'd been writing something, and she closed it a little too fast, the way Mum closes the biscuit tin when she hears Gran's footsteps.

"He did not."

"Well." I cleared my throat, buying time, enjoying the attention like a cat enjoys a sunbeam. "He sang it for me once. When I was little. He said never to ask twice, so I'm not asking anything, I'm just telling you I know it."

"You don't have to prove anything to me, Ivo."

That, of course, is precisely the sentence that makes a boy prove everything.

I hummed it.

I'd like to tell you I remembered it perfectly, but that's the thing — I didn't. It was more like remembering a dream of it. The shape was right: lower than the first verse, slower, the way Tam used to drop his chin when he got to the part about the counting. I got maybe four bars in before the melody went somewhere I didn't recognize, somewhere my mouth kept going anyway, smooth as water finding a downhill. Five bars. Six. My lips were doing this without me, the way your feet do stairs in the dark.

Paloma had gone very still. Not frightened-still. Folklorist-still, which I would learn is its own kind of frightened, just with better posture.

"Ivo," she said quietly. "Stop."

But here's the thing I've thought about every day since: I didn't want to stop. For those six bars, the kitchen felt *bigger*. Not physically — the walls hadn't moved — but the room felt like it had more room in it, the way a church does, the way a field does at dusk when you're the last one out there. Something in the humming was holding a door open inside me, and warm air was coming through, and it smelled — God help me — it smelled like harvest. Like grain drying in the sun. In November. In a kitchen that smelled of dish soap and yesterday's stew.

Seven bars.

Mum turned around.

I've seen her angry maybe four times in my life. This wasn't anger. Her face did something I had no word for then and still barely have words for now — it went white around the eyes while staying normal everywhere else, like frost on a window that hasn't touched the middle yet.

"*Out*," she said to Paloma. Not cruelly. Almost gently. "Thank you for coming. Please go."

Paloma gathered her things without a word. At the door she looked back at me, and I gave her a grin, because I was seventeen and stupid and I thought I'd gotten away with something.

Then Mum locked the door behind her, took the dish towel off her shoulder, folded it once, set it on the counter very precisely — and hit me across the face hard enough to ring my ears and split my lip.

I'll wait while you absorb that, because I had to wait too, standing there with copper in my mouth and my hand halfway raised to my cheek, too shocked even to say *ow*.

"That's medicine," Mum said. Her voice shook. "Old medicine. My grandmother did it to my uncle. You don't stop a song by asking nicely, Ivo. You stop it by making the body remember it has a body."

"Mu—"

"Don't." She put both hands on either side of my face, careful of the lip, and looked into my eyes like she was checking a lock. "Say nothing till morning. Nothing sung, nothing hummed, nothing whistled. If you catch yourself doing it in your sleep, wake up. Do you understand? Wake *up*, Ivo. However deep you are. Wake up."

I understood nothing. I nodded anyway, because when your mother holds your face like that, nodding is reflex.

* * *

Here's what I remember of that night, and I want to be honest about how much of it might be invention, because memory after that night is a field after a flood — some things standing, some things moved, some things gone entirely and leaving a shape where they used to be.

I remember lying in bed listening to the house settle. I remember thinking about Paloma's face when I stopped humming — not Mum's, Paloma's — and how she'd written something in her notebook before I even finished, fast, like taking dictation.

I remember that around two in the morning — I know the time because I checked my phone, a habit, checking the time when I can't sleep — I heard singing.

Not from outside. That's the detail that matters. From *underneath*. From below the floorboards, below the foundations, below whatever clay and stone sits under a house in Hollowmere, there was a sound like a choir heard through a wall of earth. Muffled. Patient. And underneath the muffle, unmistakably, a boy's voice keeping the melody.

My melody. The seven bars I'd managed before Mum cut me off.

And here's the part I've never told anyone except the pages of this notebook: I hummed along. Half-asleep, half-dreaming, I lay in my bed and harmonized with the thing under the house, and it was the most beautiful sound I have ever been part of, and I would give quite a lot to un-know that.

I woke at dawn with dirt under my fingernails.

Both hands. Packed under every nail, fine grey-brown soil, the kind you'd find deep down, the kind that's never seen weather. My window was still latched. My door was still shut. There was no dirt anywhere else in the room — not on the sheets, not on the floor, nowhere but under my nails, as if my hands had gone somewhere without the rest of me and come back punctual.

I sat on the edge of the bed and laughed, actually laughed, because the alternative explanation was standing right there in the corner of my mind wearing a hood, and I wasn't ready to look at it.

Then I tried to say good morning to Mum, and that's when the laughing stopped.

* * *

My voice had changed pitch.

Not cracked — cracked I knew, cracked was familiar territory, every boy in Hollowmere sounds like a gate hinge for a year around fourteen. This was different. This was my voice *shifted*, like a photograph moved a few degrees off true. When I said "Morning," it came out a third lower than it should have, smooth where my voice had always had a rasp, and there was a resonance in it, a doubled quality, like two speakers playing the same recording a fraction out of sync.

"Mum," I tried again, and the doubling was worse, and worse than the doubling was this: I could hear, faintly, under my own voice, the seven bars. Like a song stuck in your head, except it wasn't in my head. It was in my throat. Sitting there. Waiting.

Mum was at the stove. She turned when I spoke, and I watched her face do the frost thing again, and then do something new: a flicker of pure blankness, half a second, like a screen losing signal.

"What?" I said. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing." She turned back to the eggs. Her shoulders were up around her ears. "Eat your breakfast."

"Mum, look at me. What's wrong with my voice?"

"Nothing that won't heal," she said, in a tone I'd never heard her use, flat as a ledger line, and I understood suddenly that she was reciting something. A formula. Words someone had given her, long ago, against exactly this morning. *Nothing that won't heal.* Like a charm. Like a lie you tell on purpose because the truth has weight and the floor is old.

I ate my eggs. They tasted of almost nothing — not bland, but distant, like eating through a phone call. Everything did, that morning. The tea, the toast, the smell of the woodsmoke. The world had gone one pane of glass further away from me overnight, and I kept touching things to check they were real, and they were real, they just felt *filed*. Catalogued. Like I'd been moved from the shelf marked *Ivo Hale, resident, seventeen* to some other shelf whose label I couldn't read from the inside.

It was when I tried to comfort myself — this is the part that undid me — that I found the hole.

I was scared, properly scared for the first time, and I wanted my mum. Not the woman downstairs frying eggs; I wanted *my mum*, the specific one, the one who used to sing the first verse wrong on purpose to make Gran laugh, the one with the gap between her front teeth and the burn scar on her left forearm from the oven when she was nineteen, the one whose face I had looked at roughly ten thousand times over seventeen years.

I reached for her face in my memory the way you reach for a light switch in your own hallway.

And there was nothing.

Not darkness — that I could have borne. Darkness would mean it was hidden. This was worse: it was a *shape* where her face should be, a warm, loved, utterly featureless shape, like a portrait painted by someone who had been told about her but never seen her. I knew facts. Brown hair. Laugh lines. The gap in the teeth — no wait, was that her, or was that Auntie Sef? The scar — left arm, definitely, but which elbow direction? I stood in the middle of my bedroom trying to assemble my own mother's face out of parts like a police sketch, and the parts wouldn't stay where I put them, and I realized with a calm that frightened me more than panic would have that I could describe her perfectly and picture her not at all.

Something had taken her face. And it had taken it *specifically* — I could still see Tam Broadbelt's creased walnut countenance clear as daylight, could still see Denny Okafor's crew-cut and his laugh, could see the faces of kids I'd gone to school with who'd moved away years ago. All the cheap faces stayed. Only the expensive one was gone. Only the one that cost something.

I went downstairs with my heart going like a rabbit's.

"Mum. What color are my eyes?"

She looked at me like I'd lost my mind. "Brown. Ivo, sit down, you're white as flour—"

"What's my middle name?"

"Thomas. After your grandad. Sit *down*—"

"Do you have a scar? On your arm?"

And she stopped. She stopped moving entirely, wooden spoon suspended, and looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her understand — watch it arrive in her face like weather coming over the hill — exactly what I was asking and exactly why.

"Yes," she said carefully. "Left forearm. Oven. I was nineteen."

"I can't—" My voice did the doubled thing again, and I hated it, hated hearing the other singer in there under me. "Mum, I can't see your face. I know everything about you and I can't see your face. What did I do? What did I *do*?"

She crossed the kitchen and held me, and I let her, and over her shoulder I caught our reflection in the darkened window — the two of us, her face turned away from the glass, mine visible — and even in a reflection, even right then, I had to work to match the boy in the glass to myself. He looked like me. He looked like me the way a cover of a song sounds like the song.

"It was showing off," I said into her shoulder. "I was showing off for her. I only wanted her to think I was interesting."

"Oh, love." Her hand found the back of my head, the way she did when I was small. "You were interesting. That was the problem."

* * *

The slap happened an hour later, and I need to tell it right, because I've heard people describe it since — it got around, everything gets around in Hollowmere, faster than the buses we don't have — and they describe it like Mum lost her temper. She didn't. I was there. Losing your temper is hot and loud and sorry-for-itself afterward. What Mum did was cold and exact and done with a straight back, and if anything scared me more than the slap itself, it was watching her decide to do it.

Because I slipped. That's the truth of it. Mid-morning, carrying firewood in from the lean-to, I slipped — the way you slip on ice, without deciding to, your body simply going somewhere — and I heard myself humming. Seven bars. The seven bars. My mouth had picked them up again like a dropped rope, and my feet had stopped on the path, and my eyes had turned toward the quarry field without my permission, and I was humming, and somewhere far under the frozen ground something was humming *with* me, in tune, delighted.

Mum came out of the house fast. She didn't shout my name — later I understood why; you don't call a name toward a thing that's holding the other end of it. She just walked up, took my jaw in one hand to turn my face away from the field, and struck me across the same ear as the night before, open-handed, full force, splitting the scab on my lip.

The humming stopped like a cut wire.

I stood there bleeding a little and gasping, and the world snapped back into focus so hard it hurt — the cold, the wood in my arms, the smell of the stove through the wall, *her*, my mother, her face still a warm blank shape in my memory but her presence suddenly enormous and real in front of me, real as weather.

"Medicine," she said. Her voice only shook a little. "Second dose. There's an old rule in this family, Ivo: once for the body, twice for the blood. My gran did it. Her gran did it. It's not cruelty. It's the only thing that works."

"It doesn't work," I said, and my broken-pitched voice cracked on *works* into something almost like harmony. "Mum, it doesn't work, I can still feel it. It's not in my head anymore. It's in my *throat*. It lives there now. How many times do I get slapped before it stops living there?"

She didn't answer. That was the worst part. She looked at me — at my face, which I couldn't return the favor on — and her eyes were wet and furious and terrified, and she said, "Come inside. We're going to the church basement tonight. The whole village. There's going to be a vote."

"A vote? On what?"

"On what we do about the door," she said. "And about you. And about everyone the tally board named." She paused on the step, hand on the latch, not looking at me. "Ivo. Whatever happens tonight — whatever they say, whatever they decide — you hold on to one thing. Can you do that? One thing, and it can be small, but you hold it like a rail."

"What thing?"

"Anything," she said. "Anything true. Hold it, and don't let the song take it. Because that's how it works, love. It doesn't take you all at once. It takes you one held thing at a time, until there's nothing left in your hands."

That night I sat in the church basement between my mother and Paloma Zhou, my split lip throbbing, my borrowed voice silent, and I held on to the scar. Left forearm. Oven. Nineteen. I held it like a rail in the dark, and under the floorboards, faint and patient, something began to hum the opening bars of the first verse — the one everybody knows, the one they sing once and never twice — as if to say: *your turn soon.*

Above us, the village started to argue.
