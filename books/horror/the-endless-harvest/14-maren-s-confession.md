## 14. Maren's Confession

There are two kinds of silence in a kitchen. The good kind, when the bread is proving and the kettle is thinking about it and nobody needs anything from anybody. And the bad kind, which is the kind that sits down at your table uninvited and starts eating.

Paloma Zhou had brought the bad kind with her, though she didn't know it. She sat at my table with her notebook closed for once and her hands wrapped around a mug of tea she wasn't drinking, and the silence between us had teeth in it.

I'd been washing dishes I'd already washed. It's what my mother did when she couldn't say a thing, and her mother before her, and I expect if you dug far enough back you'd find a Hale woman at a stone basin scrubbing a pot that was already clean, working up the courage to say whatever needed saying. We are a family that confesses to crockery.

"You're going to ask me again," I said to the window. "About my grandmother."

"I wasn't going to," Paloma said. Which was a lie, and a kind one, and I appreciated both parts.

"You were. You've been circling it for three days like a crow over a lambing field." I set the pot down. My hands weren't steady, so I gave them nothing to hold. "Sit properly. This isn't a story you take standing up."

She sat properly.

* * *

My grandmother was called Edith Hale, and everyone in Hollowmere who remembers her remembers her as a saint, which should have been the first clue. Saints are expensive. Somebody always pays for the halo, and it's never the saint.

The famine year was 1931, though the parish books call it something politer. The wheat came up grey that summer — not diseased, just grey, like it had been left out in ash — and what little we cut went soft in the barns inside a week. People were eating nettle soup and pretending there was butter in it. The Tarrant family had six children and no father; he'd gone into the quarry field the autumn before and come back wrong, and by spring he'd stopped coming back at all.

Every family in Hollowmere knew the arrangement, even if we didn't say it aloud even to ourselves. Two names per generation, struck through on the tally board in the church, and the fields stayed generous enough to live on. Not rich. Generous enough. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and most of England has forgotten it because most of England never had to learn it.

The Tarrants owed their name that year. Everyone knew it. Mrs. Tarrant had already started sewing the burial clothes, because that's what you did — you didn't run, you didn't hide, you made sure they went into the ground decent. The debt was old and the terms were old and the terms were fair, or as fair as anything gets when the other party is under the ground.

Edith Hale went to the Tarrant house on the last night of September and offered to take their place.

That's how the story is told, anyway. That's how *she* told it, at the end, when she was blind and sweet and everyone forgave her everything. A sacrifice. An act of mercy. Saint Edith of Hollowmere, stepping into the dark so a widow could keep her children.

I'm the only one left who knows how she told it to my grandfather, late, after drink, when the sweetness had worn off her the way varnish wears off a table.

She didn't offer. She *traded*.

* * *

"Traded," Paloma repeated. Her voice was careful, the way you're careful walking on ice that you've already heard crack.

"The Hales had a debt too. My great-uncle Silas — his name was on the board from 1901, and the year came due in '31, and my grandmother was not going to let it be paid." I turned the dishcloth over in my hands. "So she went to Mrs. Tarrant and she didn't say *let me die instead*. She said: the thing below doesn't count heads, it counts *weight*. One name or another, the harvest balances. But debts don't transfer clean. They transfer with interest. Take my family's obligation and fold it into yours, and your children will owe double — but they'll owe it later, and maybe never, and mine will be free."

"And Mrs. Tarrant agreed?"

"She had six children and a grey field." I looked at Paloma straight on, because this part deserved looking at. "What would you have said? What would anyone? Edith walked out of that house a saint and the Tarrants walked into fifty years of doubled owing, and every one of those children grew up hungry in years when nobody else was hungry, and two of them went into the field young, and two more after them, and the family's gone now — gone entirely, root and branch, the way a family goes when the ground keeps taking its share plus the interest."

The kitchen clock ticked. Outside, the light was doing the thing it does now, early in the afternoon, going amber and grain-coloured, as if the whole sky had been promoted to harvest.

"So the Hales have been paying early ever since," Paloma said. Not a question. She'd read the tallies. She'd seen the pattern — I'd watched her see it, in the church archive, and I'd wanted to put my hand over the page and tell her to stop, and I hadn't, because stopping her felt like admitting there was something to stop.

"Early payers," I said. "That's what my mother called us. Proud of it, near enough. Every generation, a Hale steps forward before the lot falls, pays a little more than strictly owed, keeps the account tidy. My mother went in '74. Same year as the drowned girl — the flood took Betsy Crane and the field took my mother, and the village got a fat harvest off a year that should have starved us, and everyone ate well and said what a tragedy, what a fine woman." I laughed, and it came out wrong, the way laughs do when they've been kept too long in a cellar. "Fine woman. She taught me to bake. She taught me to keep accounts. She taught me that if you pay early and pay extra, the thing below leaves your children alone a while longer, and I believed her right up until the day I understood that all I'd done was teach my grandson the family trade."

"Which is?"

"Paying," I said. "We're payers, the Hales. It's all we are. Everything else — the inn, the bread, the welcome — it's all built on top of the paying, like a house on a cellar. And now the door's open and the cellar's talking, and Ivo—" My voice went, and I let it go for a moment rather than force it. "He hummed the second verse. Did Tam tell you? No, of course, why would he. He hummed it, showing off, being sixteen and stupid in the way sixteen-year-olds are stupid, which is to say completely, and by morning his voice had dropped half an octave and he looked at me across the breakfast table and asked me who I was."

* * *

Paloma set her mug down. "Maren—"

"No. Let me finish, because I've been finishing this speech in my head since the night the dogs howled, and if I stop I'll never start again." I sat down across from her, finally, properly, the way you sit with someone when the talk is going to cost. "You're a folklorist. You collect songs. Well, here's the song nobody sings, so write it down and call it fieldwork.

"There's a version of me that's a good woman. I know because I've met her — she's the one who fed you and gave you the good room and warned you about humming, and she meant every kindness, and the kindnesses were real. But underneath the good woman there's an accountant, and the accountant has been doing sums since the door opened, and the sums go like this: the tally board has your name on it. In different handwriting. Fresh ink. And the Hales are early payers, and early payment means finding something to give before the thing below comes to collect, and I am not going to give it my grandson."

"You think I'm the payment."

"I think the song wrote your name down, and the song doesn't make mistakes, and I think you came here with your recorder and your skepticism and your lovely open face asking questions twice, and somewhere between the first verse and the tally board you became *ours*, and the ground noticed. The ground notices everything now. The door's been shut two hundred years and it's awake and it's hungry and it is doing arithmetic, and you, Paloma Zhou, are a number in its column."

"That's not how folklore works," she said, but gently, the way you correct a child who's frightened rather than wrong. "Debt metaphors, tally systems, substitutionary payment — it's a coherent symbolic structure, Maren, but structures aren't agents—"

"It's not a metaphor!" The word came out of me louder than I'd meant, loud enough that the pans on the rack rang faintly in sympathy, and I lowered my voice and hated how it shook. "Two hundred years I've lived on top of this. Two hundred years my family has. You think we keep the superstitions because they're picturesque? Don't ask twice. Don't hum it back. Don't write the words after dark. You think my grandmother invented those rules for fun? They're load-bearing, girl. Every one of them. You pull one out to look at it and the whole house comes down on the people living in it."

She was quiet a moment. Then, in that reasonable voice of hers: "Your grandmother traded debts. That's bookkeeping. Bookkeeping can be audited. If the terms are what you say — weight, balance, interest — then there's a ledger, and ledgers have errors, and errors have corrections—"

"Oh, you *would* say that." And I don't know whether I loved her or hated her in that moment, and I suspect it was both, in the exact proportion that makes family. "You'd audit it. You'd sit down with the thing beneath the fields and go through its columns line by line and find the rounding error and fix it, and it would let you get all the way to the end before it told you what the correction costs. Because that's the shape of you. I've watched you three days. You can't leave a pattern unfinished. It's why your name's on the board."

Her hand went, very slightly, toward her notebook, and stopped.

* * *

Here's the thing I haven't said yet, the thing I washed dishes around for an hour.

When I was nineteen, my mother took me out to the field edge at dusk, the way her mother took her, and showed me where the rows bowed toward the door, and told me the whole of it — the trade, the interest, the early payments — and then she said: *one day it'll be your turn to explain it to your child, and you'll hate yourself, and you'll do it anyway, because the alternative is letting them find out alone.*

And I stood there thinking: no. Not me. I'll break the chain. I'll tell Ivo nothing and let him be a boy without a ledger.

And then the door opened, and I found myself standing in the kitchen teaching him which verses not to hum, and I understood that the chain doesn't run through obedience. It runs through love. You break it only by loving them less, and I have never once managed that, not for a single day, and so here I am, the latest link, polished smooth by all the hands before mine.

"I need you to leave," I said.

"Maren—"

"Not forever. Just — now. Tonight. Before the song finishes learning itself. Take your tapes and your notebooks and drive down to the county road and don't stop until Hollowmere's a story you tell at parties." I leaned forward. "Because I've seen what happens to the ones the song wants. Tam Broadbelt sang three verses into your recorder and then walked into the field with his boots off, neat as Sunday, and he *chose*, Paloma, that's the horror of it, he chose smiling. And you're kinder than Tam and braver than Tam and the song knows it, and when it comes for you it won't come like hunger. It'll come like a vocation. It'll make you feel chosen. And I cannot — I will *not* — watch another person I love walk into those rows believing it's her idea."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a rook called once and thought better of it.

"You love me?" Paloma said. Carefully. Like testing ice.

"Don't fish. It's undignified in a grown woman." I stood and started stacking wood into the stove, because my hands needed work and the fire needed feeding and neither of us needed to look at each other. "Three days you've been here and you've become the granddaughter I'd have picked, if picking were a thing. You eat what I cook and you listen properly and you looked at Ivo's drawings in the dirt and you didn't laugh and you didn't flinch, and that's rarer than you'd think. So yes. I love you. It took four days, which in Hollowmere counts as a long courtship."

"Maren, I can't leave."

"You can. The road's still a road. Denny's crew got out as far as the crossroads before it turned them back, but they went in daylight and they went in a group and they went *wanting* to come back. You don't want to come back. That's your advantage. Go tonight, go alone, go hating us a little if it helps — hate makes good traction."

"And Ivo?"

The log I was holding cracked down the middle in my hands. Clean split, like it had been waiting.

"Ivo stays," I said. "Ivo's a Hale. We're early payers. Whatever's coming for this village, my family has always stepped forward first, and I am not going to break two hundred years of arithmetic to spare my own feelings." I fed the halves into the stove and watched them catch. "But you — you still have a road. Use it."

She stood. She gathered her mug and her notebook, and at the doorway she stopped, and I kept my eyes on the fire because I knew if I looked at her I'd beg, and begging undoes everything the begging is for.

"For what it's worth," she said, "your grandmother's trade — the folded debt, the transferred interest — I've been thinking about it. About whether a debt can be moved twice."

"Don't," I said.

"A debt that transfers once might transfer again. That's all I'm saying. That's just the bookkeeping."

And she went upstairs, and I stood in my kitchen listening to the fire eat, and I thought: *that girl is going to audit the thing beneath the fields.*

And I thought: *God help us, she might even balance it.*

And then, from the direction of the church, thin and clear across the evening air, someone began to sing — a voice I didn't know, a voice that shouldn't have had any breath left in it at all — and it was singing the second verse, all the way through, for the first time in two hundred years.
