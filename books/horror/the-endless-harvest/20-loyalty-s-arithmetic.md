## 20. Loyalty's Arithmetic

I have done sums my whole life. Flour against mouths, eggs against debts, hours of daylight against the work they had to hold. A woman who keeps an inn learns arithmetic before she learns to pray, and she trusts it more.

So let me do the sum now, plainly, the way I'd write it in the ledger with the good pen: one boy, sixteen years old, marked on a board that writes itself. One mother, fifty-one, half a voice left and a debt already three generations deep. The arithmetic is not complicated. The arithmetic has never been the problem.

The problem is that everyone else in that church basement kept arriving at a different total.

* * *

We met at dusk, because dusk was all we had. The night before had refused to end properly — dawn came late and thin, like tea brewed twice — and the field had walked another two rows closer to the houses while we slept, if you could call it sleeping, that communal tossing with the first three verses stuck in everyone's teeth like gristle. Ivo had been home four days since we carried him out of the rows screaming, and he was himself again, mostly, which is to say he was sullen and hungry and kept humming under his breath until I put a spoon in his hand and told him to peel potatoes where I could hear him not singing.

Paloma called the meeting. She'd been up two nights straight transcribing, and it showed — she had the look of a woman arguing with her own handwriting and losing. Denny came with his ruined hand cradled against his chest like something he'd borrowed and didn't want to break further. The vicar came leaning on his stick, grey as porridge. A dozen others filled the pews of the church basement, the ones who hadn't already nailed wheat sheaves over their doors or fled down a road that wouldn't let them leave.

I stood up before anyone else could arrange the agenda to their liking. I'd rehearsed it peeling potatoes, so it came out steady.

"I'll go instead of him."

The room did what rooms do when you say a thing plainly — it went quiet and looked at its shoes.

"You've heard me," I said. "My voice is already half-paid. It's been half-paid since I was nine years old and my gran told me what our family owes. The song took most of it at the door when I tried for Ivo. There's less of me to take. I'm cheap, is what I'm saying." Somebody made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "And I want terms. I'm not offering myself into those rows out of love of the harvest. I want the rest of you to hide him. Properly hide him — not 'we'll keep an eye,' not 'he can stay at ours a night or two.' Out of Hollowmere if the road allows it, down a shaft, behind a wall, somewhere the tally can't read. You help me hide my grandson, and I walk to that door at the next false dawn and sing verse four in whatever's left of my voice."

"That's not how it—" the vicar started.

"Don't tell me how it works," I said. "Nobody in this village knows how it works. Two hundred years of paying interest on a loan nobody will show me the paperwork for, and every one of you acts like the terms are scripture. They're not scripture. They're habit."

Paloma was watching me with that expression she gets — the folklorist's face, the one that takes notes on you even while it's breaking for you. Denny's jaw worked. Old Sorrel Petch, whose family had paid on time every generation like churchgoers, said quietly, "Maren, love, hiding won't hold. My granddad hid his brother in the chalk pits for a month. The board found him anyway. Found him *faster*, some said, like it enjoyed the sport."

"Then we try something that hasn't been tried," I said. "Two hundred years of standing in rows and singing on schedule, and where's it got us? A full pantry nobody dares eat from and a door that wants a name freely given. Well. Here's a name. Freely given. Take it and let the boy go."

It was Tam's empty chair that shut me up, in the end. Nobody said anything about Tam. We all just looked at it, at the neat space where he used to sit and complain about his knees, and the arithmetic sat there in the silence doing its cold work: Tam had gone willingly too, and the field had taken him and asked for seconds.

But I held my terms. I made them repeat them back — hide the boy, and I sing. Eleven heads nodded, some eagerly, some the way men nod at a funeral when they agree the deceased is dead.

I believed eleven nods. That was my error, and I want it on the record that I knew better. I keep accounts. I know what a promise weighs in this village, and I signed anyway.

* * *

The Wrens were the eleventh nod, and the Wrens were the ones I needed most.

You have to understand what the Wren protection means to people in my position. Their house sits at the crook of the lane where the field can't quite reach, and their door stays bare of wheat sheaves because it doesn't need marking. Nothing of theirs fills with grain overnight. Nothing of theirs hums. When the tallies were struck through in matched pairs across the centuries, no Wren ever appeared between the lines. They are the only family in Hollowmere that the arithmetic skips, and every unmarked household on the lane looks at their chimney smoke the way drowning men look at a boat.

I went up after the meeting, past dark, with a bottle of the good cider and Ivo's hand in mine like he was six years old. Edith Wren opened the door herself. She's eighty if she's a day, small and dry as a seed head, with eyes that have watched this village eat itself twice and taken notes both times.

"Maren Hale," she said, before I'd got my breath. "Come to ask for shelter."

"How did you—"

"Because everyone comes. Every generation, someone comes, usually carrying a child, always carrying that same face you've got on — the one that thinks asking politely changes what's being asked." She stepped back to let us in, which surprised me, and poured tea, which surprised me more, and then she sat with her hands folded and refused me so completely that it took a moment to register there'd been no argument at all.

"The boy can't stay here," she said.

"Edith, they'll take him. He's marked. You saw the board."

"I saw the board." She sipped her tea. "I've seen every board since 1911, girl. And I'll tell you what my grandmother told a Hale just like you, in a famine year, with a Hale boy just like yours standing where yours is standing: our protection isn't ours to give. It isn't a coat we can lend. It's a wound that never closed, and it covers exactly the ground it covers and not one foot more."

"What does that mean?" Ivo asked. His voice cracked on it, poor lad, still settling back into itself.

Edith looked at him a long moment, and something in her face went old and tired and almost kind. "It means my great-great-grandmother sang that song to the end, alone, in the winter of '46 — 1846, before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath. And the thing below the fields let her live. Do you understand what a miracle that seemed? She walked out of the rows and the wheat bowed to her and the debt passed over our house like the angel of death, and she wrote in her diary that God had shown mercy." Her teacup clicked against its saucer. "She was wrong about the mercy. But the shape of the bargain holds. What's covered is covered. What's outside is outside. Your boy sleeps here tonight, and tomorrow morning the board grows a new line, and I don't know whose name is on it, and neither do you, and I am not spending eighty years of my family's suffering to find out."

"We're not asking you to spend anything," I said. "We're asking you to share."

"No," said Edith Wren. "You're asking us to spend. Sharing would mean we could afford halves."

I should have left then. Instead I did the thing I'd sworn off doing since I was old enough to know better — I begged. I laid it out, the whole sum, Ivo's name in fresh ink, my offer at the door, the eleven nods. I heard my own voice go thin and whining and hated it and couldn't stop. And Edith sat through all of it with her hands folded, and when I ran dry she said only:

"My grandmother turned away a Hale once. Do you know what happened to that Hale?"

"No," I whispered.

"Neither do I," she said. "That's the point. Nobody knows what happens to any of us. We're all guessing at the terms of a contract written in a language dead two hundred years, and you want me to gamble my grandchildren's names on a guess that feels generous." She stood, and her knees cracked like green wood. "Take your boy home, Maren. Bar your door. Sing loudly and wrongly, like the rest. And forgive me, if you can manage it, for being the last honest coward in Hollowmere."

Ivo didn't cry until we were halfway down the lane. Then he stopped walking and cried the way sixteen-year-olds do — furiously, silently, furious at himself for the silence — and I held him in the cold with the field breathing behind us, and I thought: *she's right, she's a coward, she's honest, and I would burn her house down myself if it would strike one line off one board.*

Remember that thought. Hold onto it. Because that night I learned the difference between thinking a wicked thing and doing it, and the difference has a smell, and the smell is burning tar.

* * *

The barn went up around two in the morning.

I was awake — everyone's awake now; sleep is a country we've lost our passports to — and I saw the orange bloom through my window before I heard anything, a second sunrise sitting wrong on the horizon. By the time I got into the lane there were a dozen of us in our nightclothes watching the Wren barn burn, and the fire was behaving strangely, the way everything behaves strangely now: it burned hot and clean and fast, and it made no move toward the house, and the wind that should have carried embers toward the thatch simply wasn't blowing.

Denny arrived with buckets nobody used. Paloma arrived with her recorder running, because that woman documents her own grief, and I understood it and forgave it and envied it. And Edith Wren stood on her own doorstep in a shawl, watching forty years of hay and harness and her father's tools go up, and she wasn't screaming. She was reading faces. I watched her read them — moving down the line of us in the lane, one by one, looking for the guilty look.

She found it. Or she found several, which may amount to the same thing in a village this frightened.

Because here is what I saw, standing in my nightclothes with my grandson's shoulder under my hand: the fire had help. The wind was wrong, yes, and the flames were tidy, yes — but I saw young Aldous Petch standing apart from the bucket line with soot already on his cuffs, and I saw the Cartwright brothers exchange a glance when someone shouted about the far door being barred from outside, and I saw old Mrs. Talloway turn away from the blaze with an expression I recognized from church, from funerals, from the particular satisfaction of the righteous.

Human hands. Human feet carrying human cans of paraffin — the smell of it cut through the smoke plain as a confession. The exempted family, hoarding their safety while children starved at tables stacked with grain none of us dared eat, and some neighbor or neighbors had finally done the sum the way frightened people do it, which is quickly and with other people's property.

"They think it was us," Ivo said. He'd gone very still. "They think we did it. For the exemption."

"Does it matter who they think?" I said, and then heard myself, and put my arm around him. "No. That was cruel. It matters. Everything matters now, that's the whole horror of it."

By dawn the barn was a black ribcage and the fire had died as neatly as it had lived, leaving the house untouched, the lane untouched, the wheat untouched — the field's edge stood maybe thirty yards off, upright and patient, and I swear on my grandmother's grave that not one stalk had bent toward the heat. Whatever owed the Wrens their protection had honored it right down to the property line. It was the village that hadn't.

Edith came out mid-morning with a carpet bag. She didn't look at any of us. She walked to the county road with her granddaughter's hand in hers, and the road — our road, the looping, lying road that walks Denny's crew back to us no matter which way they drive — let them go. I watched until they crested the hill. The road opened for the Wrens like a hand releasing a bird.

Protection expired, Paloma had said the Wrens' bargain was doing. Maybe Edith read the expiry in the fire. Maybe she simply did the final sum: a village that burns its way toward your safety is not a village your safety can survive.

Or maybe — and this is the thought I keep circling like a sore tooth — maybe the fire wasn't only human. Maybe something below the fields looked at that burning barn and that departing family and considered the account settled, the wound finally closed, the last exemption cashed out. Maybe we weren't watching arson. Maybe we were watching a creditor accept final payment and close the book.

Which leaves the rest of us in a ledger with nothing left to seize.

* * *

Ivo and I bar the door now, both bolts and the chair under the handle, though we both know doors are a courtesy. He sleeps in my bed like when he was small, and I lie awake listening to him breathe and doing the only arithmetic left to me: one voice, half-spent, offered freely. Eleven nods, worth perhaps four. A barn burned by neighbors. A family gone down a road that opens for no one else.

In the small hours I heard something from the direction of the mute laborer's cottage — Denny's man, the one who came back smiling and wordless from the threshold. A scratching sound, rhythmic, patient. Paloma says he writes the same word over and over on every surface they'll let him near, walls, floors, the backs of his own hands, in a careful schoolroom hand.

One word. Over and over. She wouldn't tell me what it was. She only looked at me with that note-taking face, and behind it something new, something almost like hope, which frightened me worse than the fire did.

Whatever the dead want spelled out so plainly, I have a feeling it's going to change the sum entirely.
