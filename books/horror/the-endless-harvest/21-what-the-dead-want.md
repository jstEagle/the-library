## 21. What the Dead Want

The mute laborer's name was Callum Reeve. I learned this from a laminated county ID badge he still wore on a lanyard around his neck, the photo showing a man with a sunburned grin and a hard hat tipped back, someone who looked like he'd have been first in line for last orders and last to leave. The man sitting at Maren's kitchen table now wore the same face the way an empty house wears its windows. His eyes were open. His smile was still there, faintly, like a stain that wouldn't wash out.

He hadn't spoken since they'd found him at the threshold — couldn't, we assumed, though "couldn't" was doing a lot of quiet work in that sentence. Denny had tried shouting his name, shaking him, once even slapping him across the face with an open palm that sounded like a door slamming. Callum had just kept smiling, patient as furniture.

It was Ivo who discovered the writing.

Maren had given up trying to keep the boy indoors — you might as well have tried to keep weather in a basket — and he'd taken to trailing around after Callum the way teenage boys trail after anything broken and interesting. On the third morning after the door opened, I came downstairs to find Ivo standing very still by the window, holding one of Maren's good tea towels, his face the color of porridge.

"He's been doing it all night," Ivo said. "I heard the pencil."

Callum sat at the table with a stub of carpenter's pencil in his fist, and on every surface within reach — the tabletop, the tea towel, the back of his own hand, the whitewashed wall beside him — he had written the same word, over and over, in a careful blocky hand that didn't match anything about him:

FINISH. FINISH. FINISH. FINISH.

The wall was the worst part. He'd had to get up to do the wall. At some point in the night, this smiling hollowed-out man had risen from his chair, walked to the plaster, and written FINISH eleven times in a neat vertical column, then returned to his seat and continued on the table as if nothing had happened. The letters were identical each time. No degradation, no drift, no sign of exhaustion or frenzy. It looked less like compulsion than like transcription. Like a man taking dictation.

"Has anyone asked him what it means?" I said, which was a stupid question, because the boy gave me a look that said *he can't talk, that's rather the point*, and because I already knew the answer wasn't going to come from Callum's mouth.

But I sat down across from him anyway, because I am a folklorist, and folklore is mostly the discipline of asking stupid questions until something answers.

"Callum," I said. "My name is Paloma. Can you hear me?"

The smile didn't move. The pencil did. He turned over the tea towel, found a clean patch, and wrote: FINISH.

"The song," I tried. "Is it about the song?"

FINISH.

I pulled my notebook toward me — against every rule Maren had ever given me, and by then I'd stopped counting rules anyway; the rules were like the flood barriers in a town that's already underwater — and wrote the word myself. Then I drew a question mark after it.

Callum looked at the question mark for a long time. Something moved behind his eyes, some deep and distant flicker, like a light in a house at the bottom of a lake. And then, slowly, with the deliberation of a man crossing a frozen pond, he reached out and tapped the page. Once. Twice. Three times.

Then he pointed at himself.

Then he pointed out the window, toward the field, toward the door.

I want to tell you I understood immediately. I didn't. My first interpretation was that he wanted to go back, that whatever stood behind the stone had left a hook in him and he was being reeled in by inches. That seemed consistent with everything else — the rows, the singing, Ivo walking the wheat mouthing lyrics. The thing below took people. Taking was its whole business model. Of course its leftovers would want to return to it.

It took me another day, and two more conversations with a man who could only write one word, to understand that I had it exactly backwards.

* * *

Here is what I knew by then, laid out in my notebook like evidence at an inquest:

The parish tallies went back to 1741. Names struck through in matched pairs, always two per generation, always clustered in famine years — 1741, 1783, the hungry forties, 1879, 1926, 1974. Two names per generation, struck through, and the harvests that followed were always recorded as abundant. Miraculously so. Suspiciously so.

The song had verses. Everyone agreed there were more than three; nobody would say how many. Tam Broadbelt had sung me three before walking into the wheat with his boots neatly paired at the field's edge, and I had begun to suspect — the way you begin to suspect a diagnosis you've been avoiding — that the song was not a song at all. Not originally. Songs are things people make. This was something else wearing a song's clothing.

And the Wrens were exempt. One family, generation after generation, untouched by the tally while their neighbors paid in pairs. Old Mrs. Wren had told me, with the flat pride of a woman describing a family recipe, that her great-great-whatever-grandmother had *sung it through*. Sung the whole song, alone, start to finish, and walked away whole. That was the exemption. That was the precedent everyone in Hollowmere was too frightened to look at directly, because if one woman had finished it and survived, then finishing was possible — and if finishing was possible, then all these generations of pairs, all these struck-through names, all this interest paid in children and neighbors, had been a choice.

A choice the village kept making. Or kept failing to unmake.

I sat with that thought for most of an afternoon, watching Callum write FINISH on things, and somewhere between the fourth cup of tea and the moment Maren quietly turned the tea towel over so she wouldn't have to see the word, it landed on me the way landings do — all at once, bruisingly, obvious in retrospect.

It wasn't stealing.

It was collecting.

There's a difference, and the difference is everything. A thief takes what isn't theirs and wants you not to notice. A creditor takes what *is* theirs and wants you to remember. Every horror story Hollowmere told itself — the thing beneath the fields, the hunger under the ground, the eater in the dark — cast it as appetite. But appetite doesn't wait two hundred years. Appetite doesn't keep accounts in matched pairs, in famine years, in tidy generational installments. Appetite doesn't leave a family exempt for honoring terms. Appetite doesn't stand at a threshold smiling, waiting.

Debt does. Debt is patient the way nothing alive is patient. Debt doesn't need to chase you. It knows where you live. It has always known where you live, because your address is written into the original instrument, and the instrument has never been closed.

The village had stopped singing the song. That was the sin. Somewhere around 1741 — I'd stake my career on it, such as it remained — Hollowmere had made a bargain sealed in verse, a contract sung aloud, and the final verse had named its terms. And then, whether through fear or shame or simple forgetting, they had stopped. They'd sung the first three verses once a year, carefully, never twice, never to the end, like people paying the minimum on a card they're afraid to read the statement for. And the balance had compounded. Two per generation. Interest collected in kind.

Callum wasn't asking to go back. He was delivering a message. He was, in whatever remained of him, a courier for the creditor, and the message was the same message every collections letter ever sent, reduced to its single irreducible word:

FINISH.

Settle the account. Complete the instrument. Sing it to the end.

Or keep paying. Forever. We've got time. We're underneath your fields. We literally have all the time.

* * *

I tested the theory that evening, because theories that can't be tested are just fears with footnotes.

I took my recorder out to the field's edge — daylight only, I wasn't suicidal, merely methodical — and played back Tam's recording of the third verse at low volume, facing the door. The wheat bowed toward it in its planted rows, upright since the door had opened, standing at attention like soldiers who'd finally received the order they'd been waiting on.

When Tam's voice reached the end of the third verse, there was a pause on the tape. I'd noticed it before and filed it under tape hiss. Now, with the theory in my head, I listened to the pause properly, headphones on, volume up, and heard what had been waiting in it all along: a held breath. An expectation. The musical equivalent of a sentence stopped mid-clause, hanging, waiting for the next word.

And beneath it, so faint I had to scrub the passage nine times to convince myself, a hum. Low. Patient. The sound of something picking up exactly where the melody left off and then stopping itself, deliberately, because the next part wasn't mine to give and wasn't Tam's anymore either.

The dead weren't singing the song forward because they were enslaved to it. They were singing it forward because they were *owed* it. Every voice on my tapes — the drowned girl from '74, the laborers, the paired names from the tallies — was a payment made on a debt that could never close, because the instrument itself had never been completed. They were installments. They were interest, walking the rows, mouthing lyrics, waiting for a principal payment that the living were too cowardly to make.

Two centuries of compound interest. Paid in neighbors. Paid in children. Paid in Tam Broadbelt's boots, set neatly side by side at the field's edge like a man undressing for bed.

I sat down in the dirt at the field's edge and laughed, briefly and horribly, the way you laugh when a joke lands three weeks late and turns out to be about you. Because I'd come here to record a song. That was the whole assignment. One verse, documented, archived, done. And the entire time, the actual work of Hollowmere had been the opposite of recording — it had been *not finishing*, a two-hundred-year collective performance of leaving a sentence unsaid, and I had blundered into town with my notebooks and my recorder and my professional instinct to transcribe things completely, asking questions twice, humming refrains back, writing words down after dark, dismantling with academic thoroughness the single flimsy dam keeping the arithmetic at bay.

They hadn't needed a folklorist. They'd needed literally anyone else.

* * *

Maren found me sitting there and didn't ask what I'd concluded. She'd gone pale in a new way — not the fear-pale of the first week but a grayer shade, the pallor of someone who has run the numbers herself and gotten the same total.

"You understand it now," she said. It wasn't a question.

"It's not a monster," I said. "It's a mortgage."

She flinched at the word, which told me she'd already gotten there, probably decades ago, probably as a girl, and had spent her whole life not saying it out loud because saying it out loud made it real and un-saying it was the family business.

"My grandmother used to say the ground keeps books," Maren said. "I thought it was just talk. Old people talk." She wrapped her arms around herself despite the mildness of the evening. "Paloma. If it's owed a completion — if the whole thing stops when someone sings it through — why hasn't anyone? Two hundred years. Someone must have tried."

"Mrs. Wren's ancestor," I said. "She sang it through. Alone. And she was spared."

"And the rest of us weren't," Maren said. "That's the part nobody says. She sang it through and *she* was spared, and the next famine year, the tally started again. Two names. Fresh pairs." Her whisper-ruined voice caught. "If finishing ends it, why didn't hers?"

I didn't have an answer. I said something soothing and academic about corrupted transmission, about oral texts degrading over generations, about how maybe the song she'd sung wasn't the song as originally constituted. Maren nodded the way people nod at doctors, hearing words without believing them.

But the question lodged in me like a splinter, working deeper with every hour. Because if completion ended the debt, the Wren exemption was proof of salvation, and the tally's resumption was proof of failure, and those two facts could not comfortably coexist. Unless —

Unless finishing didn't end anything. Unless finishing *transferred* something. Unless the song was less a contract than a hot coal, passed hand to hand, and the Wren woman hadn't put it out — she'd just dropped it, and the whole village had been burned ever since.

I needed records. Not parish tallies — I'd been through those until my eyes bled. I needed the other archive. The suppressed one. Every village like Hollowmere has one: the box the church keeps locked, the pages cut from hymnals, the version of events the survivors agreed to forget. And I needed it soon, because Callum had started writing on the windows now, FINISH traced in the condensation of the glass, and this morning I'd found the word added, in fresh ink, in handwriting I did not recognize, to the last page of my own notebook.

Not Callum's blocky capitals. A different hand entirely. Older. Looped. Confident.

FINISH, it said. And beneath it, smaller, almost gentle, the way a bank adds a courteous postscript to a final notice:

*We have kept your room ready.*

The vicar had been avoiding me for a week. Tomorrow, I decided, watching the wheat bow and rise in wind that touched nothing else, tomorrow I was going to stop letting him avoid me.

Somewhere in that church was the rest of the song. And I was beginning to suspect that whoever found it first would find out exactly what kind of ending a debt like this allows — and exactly how many endings it forbids.
