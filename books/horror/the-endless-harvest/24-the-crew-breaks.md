## 24. The Crew Breaks

I've blown things up for a living for nineteen years, and the thing nobody tells you about explosives is how boring most of the job is. Ninety percent paperwork, permits, safety briefings, guys standing around in hi-vis waiting for the county man to finish his coffee. The actual bang is maybe four seconds of your week. The rest is making sure the bang happens exactly where you want it and nowhere else.

So when I say the plan was simple, believe me. Simple. Bore the shaft collar, pack the charges, clear a radius, press the button, and the door under Hollowmere becomes a memory under forty tons of collapsed limestone. The county had signed off. The vicar had prayed over it, which I figured couldn't hurt. Maren Hale had watched us load the truck with an expression I couldn't read and didn't want to.

Four of us left. That's the part I keep circling back to. I'd walked onto this job with a crew of eleven and a schedule, and now it was me, Ruiz, the Callaghan brothers, and a truckload of emulsion explosive that the ground apparently had opinions about.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me tell it in order, because in order is the only way I can make it sound like something a reasonable man participated in.

* * *

We left the yard at four in the morning. Dark, obviously. There'd been no proper dawn in Hollowmere for going on five days now — the sky just went from black to a sort of bruised grey and back again, like the sun was calling in sick and sending a note. The church clocks had all stopped agreeing with each other. One of them was running backward, which Ruiz said was impossible and then refused to look at again.

The plan was to drive out to the quarry field, set the charges, blow the shaft before first light — such as it was — and then drive to the county line and keep driving until Hollowmere was a bad memory and a line item on an expense report.

I want you to hold onto that word. *Drive.*

The Callaghan brothers rode in the back with the crates. Sean kept up a running commentary the whole way out — he was that kind, filled silence like potholes — and his brother Declan kept telling him to shut it, and that was the whole texture of their relationship, and God, it sounded normal. It sounded like every job I've ever worked. I clung to that normalcy the way you cling to a rail in rough water.

Ruiz sat up front with me and didn't talk. He'd been the one who found the laborer standing at the open door, smiling, unable to speak, and I think a piece of Ruiz had stayed down there with him. He kept flexing his hands on his knees like he was warming up for something that never started.

"Foreman," he said, about two miles out. "You notice we passed that bent signpost before?"

"It's a country road, Ruiz. They're all bent."

"The one with the cow painted on it. We passed it. Then the crossroads with the broken wall. Then the cow again."

I checked the mirror. The road behind us was the road behind us. I said, "It's dark. You're tired."

"I don't sleep," he said, which was true, none of us really did anymore, and he said it without drama, the way you'd say *I don't smoke.* Just a fact about his life now.

We passed the cow sign a third time. Sean, from the back: "Denny, are we lost, or is the road taking the piss?"

* * *

The quarry field was waiting for us like it had been set for a photograph. The wheat — the wheat that nobody had planted, that stood in rows no farmer made — had gone strange overnight. It had been bowed toward the door since the beginning, all of it, a whole field leaning like a congregation. Now it stood upright. Every stalk. Dead straight, dead still, in a wind that was definitely blowing, because I could feel it on my face and hear it in the wires overhead. The wheat just declined to participate.

"Right," I said, because I'm a professional and professionals say *right.* "Sean, Declan, collars. Ruiz, you're on the primer with me. Nobody touches a crate until I've checked the circuit twice."

The shaft mouth gaped at the center of the field where we'd cut the access, and the cold came up out of it, that ripe-grain cold, sweet and wrong, like standing over a cellar full of harvest that had no business existing. I'd been down there. I'd seen the granary the size of a cathedral, full to the vaults, in a village that hadn't had a real harvest in decades. I'd heard it counting. I don't talk about the counting. Ask me about the counting and I'll tell you about the weather.

We worked. That's the mercy of the trade — the work holds you. Bore, load, tamp, wire. My hands knew what they were doing even while the rest of me stood slightly to one side of myself, watching. Sean whistled while he worked, some pop song, and then about forty minutes in I realized he'd stopped whistling the pop song and started whistling something else, something in a minor key with a lilt to it, and Ruiz and I looked at each other across the shaft and neither of us said anything, because what would we say? *Sean, stop whistling the harvest song*? He didn't know it. He couldn't know it. He was whistling it anyway, note-perfect, and when I asked him what it was he blinked at me like a man coming up from underwater and said, "Dunno. Been in my head all week."

Declan found the dust problem.

"Den," he called, from the collar. "Come look at this."

The bore holes — we'd drilled six of them, clean as you like — were packed. Not collapsed. *Packed.* Full of fine grey-gold dust, tamped down tight as flour in a jar, all the way down. I scraped one out with my knife and the dust came up in a little ribbon and smelled like a bakery. Like a bakery in a dream, is the nearest I can get to it. Sweet and warm and somehow old.

"Grain dust," Ruiz said. "The whole formation's got to be shot through with it. That's why it smells like that down there."

Grain dust. Now, here's a thing I know, because it's the kind of thing a blasting man knows the way a sailor knows weather: grain dust explodes. That's not folklore, that's OSHA. A cloud of fine grain dust in a confined space is more dangerous than the powder we were carrying — it's how whole elevators have gone up, silos, mills, men vaporized by their own breakfast raw materials. Anywhere there's dust and a spark, the air itself becomes the bomb.

Which meant the ground under Hollowmere was, in the most literal sense available to engineering, already loaded.

"Doesn't matter," I said. "We're not setting it off. We're collapsing the shaft from the collar, outside the dust zone. Charges go, rock comes down, door goes under. Done."

I said it the way you say things when you need the saying to be true.

* * *

The first charge failed the way charges don't.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I've got left. We fired the circuit. I checked the continuity twice, I checked the blasting machine, I checked that Ruiz was clear and the brothers were behind the truck, and I pressed the plunger, and the machine fired — I felt it fire, that little jolt through the handle like a caught breath — and the ground did nothing. No heave. No cough of smoke. Not even a sound. Six holes packed with commercial emulsion and the earth absorbed it like a sponge takes water.

We fired it twice more. Nothing.

Declan went out to check the wires — against every regulation I have, and I let him, because we were past regulations, we were in the part of the job where the manual runs out — and he came back holding the end of a lead wire and wearing the face of a man who's found something in the soup.

"Den. The wires are full."

"Full."

"Of dust. Inside the insulation. The whole run." He held it out. I took it. The cable was soft and warm and when I bent it, fine golden dust sifted out of a hairline split in the sheath, dust that had gotten *into* the wire somehow, into the copper, into the heart of the thing meant to carry the fire.

Sean said, quietly, all the jokes gone out of him: "The ground ate it."

"The ground didn't eat anything," I said. "We'll re-drill. We'll use a bigger—"

And that's when Ruiz grabbed my arm, hard, and pointed at the field.

The wheat had moved. Not bowed — moved. The rows had shifted, all together, while we weren't looking, and now they didn't point at the door. They pointed at us. Every row in that whole vast field stood aimed at the four of us and the truck like compass needles finding north, and the wind was still blowing, and the wheat was still not moving, and I have stood on blast sites with my ears ringing and my heart trying to climb out through my teeth, and I have never in my life been as afraid as I was looking at a field of grain that was paying attention.

"Load the truck," I said. "We're done here. We're coming back with the county, with the army, with whoever. Load the truck."

Nobody argued. That's how you know it's bad, on a crew — when nobody argues.

* * *

We drove. I drove, because if my hands were on the wheel then I was a foreman doing a foreman's job and not a man fleeing a field.

The Callaghans lasted eleven minutes.

I'll give them this — they tried to be quiet about it. Sean said he'd ride in back and keep an eye on the crates. Then Declan said he'd go keep Sean company. Then there was a low, urgent conversation back there that I pretended not to hear, the way you pretend not to hear your own heart, and then Sean knocked on the cab glass and said, "Den, pull over a sec, Declan's sick," and Declan wasn't sick, Declan was *done*, and when I stopped the truck they both got out and stood in the road in the grey not-light and Sean said, "I've got kids, Denny," and Declan said, "We'll walk. We'll walk to the line. It's not far. It's — where's the line, Den?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. Where was the line.

"Get back in the truck," I said. "I'll drive you out myself. We'll go round the long way, through Marleigh, through—"

"There isn't a long way," Ruiz said from the passenger seat. He said it without heat, the way he'd said *I don't sleep.* "Denny. There isn't a long way. I've been watching the signs. There's only the one road now. It goes out and it comes back. I counted the cow."

I counted the cow too, that drive. Four times. The same bent post with the same faded heifer, the same broken wall, the same crossroads where a sign that had said MARLEIGH 6 the day before now said HOLLOWMERE 2, and the paint on the new letters wasn't faded at all. It was fresh. It was, if anything, still slightly wet.

The Callaghans stood in the road and looked at me and I looked at them and there was nothing to say that a foreman could say, so I said the only true thing: "I know. I know. Get in. We'll figure it out."

They didn't get in. They walked. They walked back the way we'd come, back toward the village, because the road only went one place now, and I watched them go in the mirror until the bend took them, and Ruiz said, "They'll be all right. It's not angry. It's — patient. It's the most patient thing I've ever stood on top of."

"You've been down there," I said. "How would you describe it?"

He was quiet a long time. The road unspooled ahead of us, grey and empty, and the cow sign came up to meet us like a friend.

"Like a ledger," he said finally. "Like the whole ground is a ledger and we're entries. And you don't run from a ledger, Denny. You settle it or you don't. Running's just — it's not one of the options. It was never one of the options."

* * *

I drove the loop three more times after Ruiz dozed — or pretended to doze — in the passenger seat. Out past the quarry field, past the cow, past the crossroads, past the church with its backward clock, and out again, and back again. The odometer ticked up honest miles and delivered us nowhere. At one point I stopped at the crossroads and got out and walked fifty yards down the Marleigh road on foot, into the grey, and I'm telling you the road just — continued. It continued and continued, hedgerows and wires and the smell of wet earth, and then it continued into the crossroads again, from the other side, and I was standing where I'd started, and my boots had the same mud on them, and somewhere behind me the truck engine idled like nothing was wrong, because nothing was wrong, because the road was fine, the road was perfect, the road was a sentence that only had one word in it.

I sat on the verge for a while. I'm not ashamed of it. A man should sit down sometimes when the world changes shape.

The wheat in the field across the road was bowed again. All of it, every row, leaning toward the door, toward the village, toward — I understood this then, sitting in the dirt with dust still gold under my fingernails from Declan's wire — toward whatever it was owed. It wasn't menacing. That was the worst of it. It looked like a field waiting for a harvest that had already been agreed.

Ruiz's voice, from behind me, gentle as you'd wake a man: "Denny. There's a light on at the inn."

There was. One yellow window in all that grey, and I thought about Maren Hale in it, and the boy, Ivo, and the folklorist — Zhou, Paloma Zhou, the one with the notebooks and the recorder and the look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like — and I thought about the tally board in the church with its fresh ink, and I remembered, with the clarity you get at the bottom of a thing, the column that had appeared with our names in it. My crew's names. My name. Written in a hand that wasn't anybody's.

The explosives were still in the truck bed. Dead weight now. The ground had eaten the fire out of them the way it ate the road's exits, the way it ate the dawn, the way it ate men — politely, completely, without hurrying.

"Come on," I said to Ruiz, and to myself, and to the road, which didn't answer. "We'll go see Maren."

Because here's what I knew, driving back down the one road into the one village at the end of the world, past the cow sign one last time: you can't blast your way out of a debt. I'd tried. I'd brought nineteen years of professional certainty and a truck full of the biggest argument available to modern man, and the ground had tamped our charges with grain and filled our wires with flour and turned the county road into a loop of string, and it had done all of it without one flicker of effort, the way a sleeper turns over.

There were people in that yellow window who were trying something else. Something with words in it. A song, they kept saying, like it was a plan and not a symptom.

I parked the truck in the yard and left the explosives sitting there under the grey sky, harmless as seed corn, and I went in to tell them the road was gone — and that from the look on Paloma Zhou's face when she opened the door, she'd already written it down before I said it. In her notebook. In fresh ink.

She held it up so I could see the page. My own handwriting was on it.
