## 26. The Wren Bargain

The Wren house sat at the end of Cooper's Lane like a held breath. Every other door in Hollowmere had sprouted its wheat sheaf by now — bound bundles nailed above lintels, tucked into window frames, some of them so fresh the chaff still drifted onto the sills. The Wrens' door was bare. Bare, and yet untouched. Their pantry had not filled with grain in the night. Their name did not appear on the tally board. Whatever the thing beneath the fields wanted from this village, it had never once asked it of the Wrens.

I'd spent three days telling myself I wasn't going to knock on that door.

On the fourth morning — the morning after the pantries filled, the morning old Mrs. Callow woke up humming a harmony she swore she'd never learned — I knocked anyway.

Edith Wren opened the door before my knuckles landed a second time, which told me she'd been standing behind it, deciding whether to answer. She was eighty-something and built like the last fence post standing after a flood: weathered, straight, immovable. Behind her, in the dim of the hallway, her son Aldous hovered with the particular posture of a man who has argued about this exact moment and lost.

"Paloma Zhou," Edith said. Not a question. "The song-catcher."

"Folklorist."

"Mm." Her eyes were pale as river-washed flint and just as hard to read. "You'll want the story. Everyone wants the story eventually. Come in, then, before the lane sees you standing there. We're not ashamed. We've just never been *asked* properly."

* * *

Their kitchen was the only room in Hollowmere that smelled of nothing. No bread, no woodsmoke, no grain. Just clean air and lemon polish, as if the house itself were holding its tongue. Aldous made tea without asking whether I wanted any, and his hands shook on the second cup.

"You've seen the board," Edith said, settling across from me. "In the church."

"I've seen it update itself, if that's what you mean."

"Then you'll have noticed our name isn't on it. Never has been. Not once in two hundred years." She wrapped her hands around her own cup — she didn't drink either; it was for holding, an anchor against drifting. "My great-great-great-grandmother. Sable Wren. Famine year, 1797. Same as always: two names per generation, drawn or chosen or however they managed it back then, God forgive them all. And Sable's daughter was on the list."

Aldous turned toward the window. He didn't leave the room. He just stopped participating in it.

"Sable went down to the door," Edith continued, "on the night of the harvest, alone, and she sang the song. All of it. Beginning to end. Nobody had ever done that — the rule even then was sing it once, never twice, never to its end. But Sable had lost a husband the winter before and a son the spring after, and she'd decided the thing under the field could take what it liked because there was nothing left in her it could reach." A dry sound, almost a laugh. "Grief makes theologians of us all."

"And it spared her," I said.

"It *thanked* her." Edith's voice didn't waver, but her knuckles whitened on the cup. "That's what the diary says. It thanked her, and it let her walk home, and it never took a Wren again. Two hundred years. Plague, famine, war — the tally came round every generation and passed over us like weather. We ate when others starved. My mother called it a blessing at church and a debt at home, and she was right both times."

"Because it transferred."

Edith looked at me then, and I saw what it cost her — the flint cracking down one clean line. "You've been reading the parish tallies. You tell me. Two names every generation, famine years, matched pairs. Except 1798. The year after Sable sang. That year there were four."

Four. I'd seen it myself, days ago, in the church archive — the anomaly I'd noted clinically, assuming clerical error. Four names struck through in matched pairs, doubled, in a year when the harvest had been adequate. When the village should have owed nothing at all.

"The debt didn't vanish," I said slowly. "It moved."

"It spread." Aldous spoke for the first time, to the window rather than to me. "Sable paid her family's portion and the interest accrued to everyone else. Every generation since, the village has carried what we should have carried. Our exemption is their burden. That's the fine print." His jaw worked. "We've known for two hundred years and we've said nothing, because saying something means giving it up, and giving it up means—"

"Means tonight," Edith said flatly.

* * *

The tea went cold between us while she explained it. The protection wasn't permanent — it was a term. Sable's bargain ran two hundred years to the season, and the season ended at dark. Edith knew because her grandmother had known, because Sable's diary said so in her own hand, in ink gone brown as old blood: *Two hundred years the door will know my name. After that, let them learn theirs.*

"We tried to renew it," Aldous said. "My father walked out to the field in '61 and sang the first verse at the door. Just the first verse, respectful, an offering." He flexed his right hand, which I now noticed was stiff, the fingers curled slightly inward. "The ground drank the words and gave him back silence and this. Cold took the feeling in it and never gave it back. Fifty years, and it's still winter in those fingers. The door doesn't renegotiate. It only collects."

"So why tell me?" I asked. "Why now? You could let the night come and say nothing. By tomorrow you're on the board like everyone else, and nobody would ever have known you weren't before."

Edith set down her cup with a click that sounded final as a bolt throwing home.

"Because of your notebook," she said. "Because Tam Broadbelt walked into the field with his boots off and his heart full, and because that mute boy from the crew writes FINISH on everything he can reach, and because I am eighty-three years old and I will not carry this into the ground with me." She leaned forward. "Sable thought she'd finished it. She thought completion was payment. It wasn't. Completion was *transfer*. The song doesn't end, girl — it moves. Whoever sings it whole takes the debt and chooses where it lands. Sable chose her descendants' safety and damned the parish arithmetic. That was the choice. There is no version where nobody pays. There's only the choosing."

The clock in their hall ticked, and I thought about how clocks in Hollowmere had started running backward, and how this one — this one alone — still ran true. As if the house were keeping honest time precisely because its people couldn't afford not to know how little of it remained.

"The vicar showed me a page," I said. "The original fourth verse. Cut from the hymnals. He died before he could tell me what completing it actually does — whether the singer becomes keeper or payment."

"He wouldn't know," Edith said. "Nobody knows. Sable's diary is the only account that exists, and Sable didn't finish the fourth verse. She couldn't. It requires—" She stopped. Looked at her son. Looked at me. Something passed over her face like cloud-shadow over wheat.

"Requires what?"

"A name freely given," Aldous said quietly. "The final line needs a name, spoken by the singer, given by choice. That's why the village never finished it. That's why it's been collecting interest for two centuries instead of principal. Nobody would give a name. So it takes instead of receives. Takes pairs, takes voices, takes years off Denny Okafor's hand and Maren Hale's throat." He finally turned from the window, and his eyes were his mother's flint, cracked the same way. "It's not a monster, Ms. Zhou. It's a creditor. And we are two hundred years past due."

* * *

I walked back through the village as the light went long and amber, the kind of light that photographers call golden and undertakers call honest. Wheat sheaves on every door. Pantries full of grain nobody would eat. Somewhere down the lane, a child was humming the second verse, and three mothers converged on the sound from different directions like firefighters answering an alarm.

The precedent sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

There was no clean ending. Edith had said it plainly and the diary confirmed it: the song could be finished, the debt could be settled, but settlement meant someone stood at that door and sang it whole and gave a name freely given — and whatever that made them, keeper or payment, they chose where the weight landed next. Sable had chosen her bloodline and broken a village. The Wrens had lived two hundred years on the difference.

And their protection expired at dark.

I reached Maren's kitchen as the sun touched the quarry field. She was at the stove making food neither of us would taste, moving with the careful economy of a woman whose voice was a whisper and whose grandson slept upstairs with wheat dust in the seams of his hands. Ivo was grounded inside, doors locked, windows latched, singing forbidden — and still, Maren told me, he hummed in his sleep. In tune. In parts.

"Maren," I said. "Sit down. I need to tell you what the Wrens told me, and then I need to tell you what I'm going to do about it."

She turned from the stove. Outside the window, the last light lay along the field like a blade laid flat, and the wheat — the wheat that bowed toward the door, the wheat that stood in rows no farmer planted — began, very softly, all together, in the windless evening, to sway in time.

Like something counting down.

Like something that had waited two hundred years, and was done waiting.
