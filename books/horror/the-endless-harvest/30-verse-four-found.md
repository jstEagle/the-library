## 30. Verse Four, Found

There is a particular kind of morning that arrives after you've stopped sleeping properly — a gray, grainy light that makes everything look like it's being viewed through old film stock, including yourself. I woke in Maren's spare room with my notebook open on my chest and a crease pressed into my cheek like a scar I hadn't earned yet, and I lay there for a while listening to the house breathe.

The clocks had been running backward for two days now. The one on the mantel downstairs ticked with the confidence of a metronome and moved in the wrong direction entirely, which meant that when I finally got up, it was either very early or very late or possibly neither, because time in Hollowmere had stopped pretending to be a straight line sometime around the night the door opened.

I made tea I didn't drink. Then I sat down at the kitchen table with the three things I'd been avoiding bringing together, because I have a superstition of my own, professionally acquired: some sources are harmless apart and catastrophic combined. Tam's recording. The vicar's page. The dead girl's harmony.

I laid them out like surgical instruments.

* * *

Let me be precise about what I had, because precision was about to become the only weapon anyone had left, and weapons deserve inventory.

Item one: Tam Broadbelt's last recording, made eleven days ago in his cottage while he sat facing away from me, as he always did, his voice coming over his shoulder like something thrown rather than offered. He'd sung three verses clean and then, without announcing it, without any change in his breathing, he had begun a fourth. He got nine lines into it before his voice did something I still can't describe without my hands going cold — it didn't break, it *branched*, as if two men were singing through one throat, and then he stopped, said "that's all I hold true," and thanked me for the tea.

He walked into the field at dusk that same day. His boots are still paired neatly at the field's edge. I checked again this morning. They're patient boots. They'll wait forever.

Item two: a single page in the vicar's cramped, apologetic hand — the suppressed fourth verse, copied out of an 1811 hymnal that no longer exists anywhere except in his memory and his transcription, which he showed me four days before he died with the expression of a man handing someone a lit match and apologizing for the fire. The page was incomplete. The last lines were water-damaged into illegibility, ink bloomed into gray flowers, and the vicar had died — quickly, quietly, in his sleep, which in Hollowmere lately counts as a mercy — before he could tell me what he remembered of them.

Item three: my own tapes. Specifically the playback recordings, the ones that had started acquiring voices the way a wall acquires damp. The mute laborer's humming. The drowned girl from 1974 — Edith Marsh, though nobody had said her name aloud to me until I found it myself in the parish records, and even then the name arrived in my head already knowing it belonged to her, which is not how names usually work. And underneath all of them, threading through every corrupted track like a bass line under a hymn, the harmony that didn't belong to any living throat.

Three sources. Three partial witnesses. Any folklorist worth her salt knows that triangulation is how you recover a text that people have spent two centuries trying to lose.

Any folklorist worth her salt also knows that sometimes the text doesn't want to be recovered, and the people who lost it were right.

* * *

I started with Tam, because Tam was the only one of the three who had chosen to give me what he gave.

His recording was clean — my equipment was good, and whatever else Hollowmere did to sound, it seemed to respect a live take. I played the fourth verse fragment back at half speed, then quarter, transcribing as I went. Nine lines. The meter was the same as the first three verses — that rolling common time, the kind of tune designed to be sung by many mouths doing mindless work — but the words were different in kind. The first three verses were instructions dressed as celebration. Bring in the sheaves, count the pairs, thank the field that feeds you. Gratitude with a ledger hidden inside it.

The fourth verse wasn't gratitude. It was *address*. It spoke directly to the thing beneath the fields, in the second person, the way you speak to someone across a table when the negotiation has gone on too long and everyone's tired.

*You counted our fathers' fathers' grain,* Tam sang, or sang through, *and we let the count stand.*

That was the shape of it. Acknowledgment. Not apology — apology would have been easier, apology implies the debt might be forgiven. This was a debtor reading terms aloud. Line by line, the verse built toward something. Toward a completion. You could feel the syntax leaning forward like a runner at a tape.

And then, at line ten, Tam branched, and the verse fell off the edge of what he could hold true.

* * *

The vicar's page filled in different stones of the same wall. His transcription was in the old notation, shaped notes, the kind they used when singing schools taught farmers to read music so the psalms wouldn't die in their mouths. I can read it, slowly, the way I read Latin — competently and without pleasure. Where Tam's version had been oral and therefore fluid, the vicar's was fixed, frozen, and the differences between the two told their own story. Two hundred years of singers had smoothed a roughness here, sharpened an ambiguity there. Oral tradition is a river; print is a photograph of the river. Both are true. Neither is complete.

But the vicar's page had something Tam's memory didn't: the structure of the ending. Even water-damaged, even with the final lines bloomed into illegibility, I could see where the verse was *going*. The rhyme scheme demanded a closing couplet. The melodic line — I hummed it against my better judgment, quietly, in daylight, with salt in my pocket like a tourist — climbed through its phrases the way the other verses never did. The first three verses circled. The fourth ascended. It ended high. It ended on a held note.

Songs end on held notes when someone has to sustain them alone.

I sat with that observation for most of the afternoon, turning it over, finding it worse each time I looked at it, and meanwhile the light outside did something I'm still not sure I saw correctly: it came up once more after it should have been down, briefly, like a lamp turned on in another room of a house you thought was empty.

* * *

The dead girl's harmony was the key. Of course it was. It's always the quiet part that carries the architecture.

I isolated the track around two in the morning — or what the backward clock insisted was two in the morning, which may have been noon, which may have been last year. Headphones on. The tape hissing like something breathing through its teeth. And there, underneath the mute laborer's broken humming and the wind that shouldn't have been on a recording made indoors, Edith Marsh's voice, fifty years drowned and still perfectly pitched, singing the fourth verse's melody in harmony.

Not the words. Just the melody, harmonized a third above, the way you'd sing along to a song you loved.

Except she wasn't singing along. She was singing *ahead*. I noticed it on the third pass, on the fifth, on the tenth: her harmony kept arriving a fraction early, anticipating the melody, the way a duet partner does when they know the song better than the lead. She knew where it went. She'd known since 1974. Maybe the field had been teaching her the whole verse for fifty years, patiently, note by note, the way water teaches stone.

I transcribed her line. Then I set it against Tam's fragment and the vicar's notation, and I did the thing I'd trained twenty years to do, the thing I'd come to Hollowmere believing was innocent: I reconstructed the text.

It took six hours. It should have taken six weeks. But the pieces didn't resist each other the way genuine fragments do; they *attracted*, snapping together with the eagerness of magnets, and twice I caught myself writing down a word I hadn't heard on any tape and hadn't read on any page — a word that was simply *there*, waiting in my hand the way a familiar staircase is there in the dark. The song was helping me finish it. That should have been the warning. When a locked thing hands you the key, check whose pocket the key came out of.

By the time the false dawn came up gray and wrong, the fourth verse lay complete on my kitchen table, in my handwriting, in my notebook, written after dark in direct violation of the first rule anyone ever gave me here.

Maren is going to kill me, I thought. If there's a Maren left and anything left of me to kill.

Then I read it through, top to bottom, as a whole, for the first time.

* * *

Here is what the fourth verse says. I won't transcribe it fully here — not from cowardice, or not only from cowardice, but because I've learned that this song reproduces through transcription, and I refuse to be a vector. But I will tell you its logic, because the logic is the trap, and the trap deserves to be seen clearly by someone.

The first eight lines settle accounts. They name the famine years. They name the pairs — two per generation, struck through in matched columns, the arithmetic Hollowmere has been paying since before anyone alive was born. They acknowledge the interest: the vanished laborers, the taken children, the years of failed harvests, the road that loops. All of it itemized. All of it *correct*, and that's the worst part — I checked the figures against the parish tallies, and the song's accounting is flawless. Whatever lives under those fields has kept better books than the church ever did.

Lines nine through twelve are the offer. Completion. The song has been sung three-quarters for two hundred years, and an unfinished contract accrues, and the entity beneath the wheat has been patient the way geology is patient. The verse offers to close the account. To sing the contract whole. To end it.

And then the final couplet. The water-damaged lines. The ones the vicar died before explaining.

They're simple. That's what undid me — their simplicity, the folk-song plainness of them, the way the cruelest things are always said in the plainest words. The verse does not ask for blood. It does not ask for a body or a sacrifice or any of the theatrical currencies horror stories trade in. It asks for exactly what the Wren ancestor's diary said the original bargain asked for, and what the tally board asks for, and what every version of this arrangement across four centuries has asked for:

*A name, freely given.*

The final line requires the singer to state their own name. Plainly. Willingly. As the price of the closing bar. Whoever sings the fourth verse to its end signs it, the way you sign a lease, the way you sign a confession, the way you sign — I kept circling other comparisons and kept coming back to this one — the way you sign a marriage register. Because that's what completion means. Not death. *Keeping.* The vicar's dying riddle resolved itself at last: whoever completes the song becomes its keeper **or** its payment, and the final couplet makes clear these were never two options. They're one option described two ways. The keeper stays. The payment stays. The door is sealed from the inside, and the bolts are thrown by the person within, and the fingernail grooves in the mortar — God, the grooves — are the signature, written in the only ink available.

I understood, sitting there in the grainy light with my tea gone cold and my handwriting staring back at me, why the village had stopped singing two hundred years ago. Not cowardice. Arithmetic. Someone, somewhere back down the generations, had gotten far enough into the fourth verse to understand its last line, and had put the song down mid-phrase, and had convinced everyone else to put it down too, and the interest had been running ever since. Every vanished pair. Every famine year. Every child walking the rows with wheat through the floorboards. All of it late fees on a contract the village was too wise to finish and too bound to abandon.

And I understood — with the calm, ringing clarity that I've learned to distrust more than fear — why the song had been writing itself into my notebooks since the day I arrived. Why my name was on the tally board in handwriting that wasn't mine. Why the dead girl sang ahead of the melody like a teacher prompting a slow student.

The song hadn't chosen me despite my skepticism. It had chosen me *because* of it. A stranger owes nothing to the village's guilt, holds none of its exemptions, carries none of its inherited bargains. A stranger can give a name freely, because a stranger's name was never entangled in the old debt. I was the clean instrument. I was the unencumbered party. I had walked into Hollowmere with professional skepticism and recording equipment, and somewhere beneath the wheat, something patient had noted my arrival the way a lender notes a new credit-worthy account, and had begun, gently, note by note, verse by verse, to teach me the song I would need to sign.

I closed the notebook. The room was very quiet. Outside, the wheat stood in rows no farmer planted, bowed toward the door, and the false dawn hung on without committing to anything, and somewhere — below, within, behind the walls, in the speakers of a recorder I'd left running on the table — faintly, warmly, someone hummed the next phrase. Encouraging me. Prompting me.

Waiting for me to learn my line.

* * *

Maren found me like that. I don't know how long she'd been standing in the doorway; she moved like the house itself when she wanted to, soundless. She looked at the notebook, and at my face, and she knew. Women like Maren always know; it's the burden of having paid attention your whole life.

"You finished it," she whispered. Her ruined voice, the one the field kept part of, made even accusation sound like prayer.

"I finished it."

"And?"

"And the last line needs a name." I turned the notebook toward her, then thought better of it and turned it back. Some texts shouldn't be shared. Some knowledge is contagion; I'd written that rule in my journal in week one, clinically, like a fool cataloguing other people's superstitions. "Freely given. The singer's own."

Maren sat down across from me. She was quiet for a long moment, and I watched her arrive at the same place I'd arrived, watched the understanding move through her face like weather, and I knew — before she said it, before she drew the breath her whisper would ride on — exactly what she was going to say next.

"Paloma," she began, and her hand crossed the table toward mine, and upstairs Ivo slept the thin, harvested sleep of the half-taken, and outside the field stood one row closer to the houses than it had yesterday,

"—there's something you need to know about my grandmother."
