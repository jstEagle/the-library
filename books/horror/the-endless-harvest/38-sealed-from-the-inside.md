## 38. Sealed From the Inside

I have spent my whole life learning how to hold a thing together with my hands — dough, grief, a grandson's collar when he tried to run from the doctor's needle. So you'd think I'd know by now that hands are not the same as holding. That some things come apart no matter how tight you grip, and some things were never yours to grip in the first place.

But I'll tell it in order. I owe her that much. Order was the one gift she gave me that I could actually keep.

* * *

The false-dawn had come and gone twice by then, and the sky over Hollowmere had settled into that bruised grey that doesn't promise anything to anybody. The field stood at the garden gates of the last houses on the lane, wheat tall as a man and utterly still, like an audience waiting for the overture to end. We had all learned by then what stillness meant out there. Stillness meant listening.

Paloma walked down the lane toward the door with her recorder in one hand and nothing else. No rope, no torch, no knife. She'd left everything that mattered behind her on my kitchen table: letters, instructions, tapes labeled in her neat hand, and a list of things Ivo should learn first if he ever took up the work. She'd kissed the top of his head like he was eight years old instead of fifteen, and he'd let her, which told me more about the state of him than any doctor could have.

"I'll want the last word," she'd said to me at the table, wry as Sunday. "You know how I get."

"You always got it," I said. "Even when you were wrong."

"Especially then." And she smiled, and I let her go, because there are moments when love looks exactly like standing aside, and you don't get to choose which ones they are until afterward, when it's too late to have done anything different.

Denny carried her as far as the field's edge would allow, which wasn't far; the rows parted for her the way water parts for a keel, and closed behind Denny like a door in the face. He came back to us with his ruined hand tucked against his chest and his jaw set so hard I could hear his teeth. He didn't say goodbye. He said, "Eight bars, Zhou. You promised me eight bars of sense," and she laughed, and the laugh carried across the wheat without echoing, which is a thing I will hear for the rest of my life whether I want to or not.

The rest of us stood at the lane's end because she asked us to. All of Hollowmere, what was left of us — forty-odd souls in nightclothes and coats thrown over, holding candles we had no business lighting in a wind that smelled of ripe grain. Old Mrs. Wren held her granddaughter's hand and did not look away once. The vicar, grey as ash and half-dead already, mouthed something that might have been a prayer and might have been the first three verses, sung wrongly, defiantly, the way we'd been singing them all through the long night.

And Paloma stood before the stone door with her back to us, and began to sing.

I won't write the verse down. Don't ask me. Some rules survive even when everything else is spent, and besides, I couldn't. What I can tell you is what it did. It went into the ground the way rain goes into cracked earth — you could feel the village drink it. The wheat bowed, row after row after row, all together, like a congregation called to its feet. The cold air coming up out of the shaft went warm. Somewhere beneath our feet, something enormous turned over in its sleep and, for the first time in two hundred years, stopped counting.

She sang the fourth verse, the one that names the singer. She named herself plainly — Paloma Zhou, folklorist, guest, friend — and the name went down into the dark like a coin into a well.

Then she sang the fifth.

There was never supposed to be a fifth verse. Every hymnal, every tally, every scrap of parish paper agreed on four. But she told us afterward — no. She told Denny, who told me, because she wrote it in a letter I keep under the flour tin where I can pretend I haven't read it eleven times. There was always a fifth verse, unwritten, because it belonged to whoever sang fourth. It was blank the way a signature line is blank. Waiting.

And the song offered her everything. I felt it happen. We all did. The air went sweet as harvest festival, and Ivo — standing beside me with his hand in mine, thin as a winter branch — suddenly straightened, color flooding back into his face, and I heard my own voice come back into my throat like a bird returning to a loft, full and clear and mine. For one whole minute I could have sung the roof off the church. Somewhere below, the granary filled past its cathedral rafters, and the road unlooped itself, and every debt on every tally board faded to blank vellum.

All she had to do was say yes.

I know what you're thinking, because I thought it too, standing there with my voice restored and my boy made whole: *say yes, girl, take it, who would blame you.* And that's the filthiest part of the whole affair — that the bargain was good. That it would have cost nothing but her staying, and staying is such a small word for such a large forever.

She declined.

Denny swears he heard her say it — "No. Thank you. No." — polite as declining a second helping, and then she named herself again, freely, plainly, no gifts attached, and sang the fifth verse as her own composition. Not payment. Terms. She changed the terms. I'm an innkeeper, not a scholar, and I've listened to Denny explain it six times and I still only half follow, but here's the shape of it as I understand it: the song wanted a meal, and she gave it a keeper. Something to hold the door, not fill the granary. A resident, not a ration.

The wheat bowed once more — all together, the whole field, deeper than before, like a congregation at the close of service — and went still in a new way. Not listening. Resting.

* * *

I watched her hands on the bolts.

That's the part I can't put down, so I may as well pick it up. The door stood open at the bottom of the shaft, and she climbed down to it, and she put both hands on the iron bolts — the ones thrown from the inside two centuries ago by whoever paid the toll before her — and she drew them across, one, two, three, deliberate as a woman bolting her own front door on a winter night.

From the inside. As they always were. As they always would be now.

Before the third bolt seated home, she looked up the shaft at us. The floodlight caught her face and she was smiling — not brave-smiling, not the face people put on for funerals, but genuinely, warmly amused, as if the whole thing had turned out to be a very good joke and she was the only one who'd gotten it from the start.

"Maren," she called up, and her voice carried clean as a bell, "the stew's on the stove. Don't let it burn. Ivo knows which shelf the good tea's on."

"Don't tell me how to run my own kitchen," I called back, and my voice broke on the second word, and she heard it, and she laughed, and the laugh echoed this time — properly, humanly, bouncing off stone — and then the third bolt went home with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence, and the echo stopped, and the silence underneath it was the ordinary kind. Field-silence. Night-silence. The kind that means nothing at all is paying attention.

The wheat went down. Not cut, not trampled — it simply lay down, all at once, like a dog settling by a fire, and began, even in that frozen hour, to go brown at the tips. Fallow. The word came to me from somewhere deep, from my grandmother's grandmother maybe: fallow isn't death. Fallow is a field being allowed to rest. Somebody had finally told the ground it could stop.

We stood there until the candles burned our fingers. Nobody spoke. There was nothing to say that wouldn't have been smaller than the quiet.

* * *

Ivo woke at actual dawn — real dawn, gold and grudging and late, the first honest sunrise in nine days — and he remembered everything.

I want you to understand what that means, because I didn't, not at first. When he was taken, the field took pieces of him as toll: his mother's face, his voice's pitch, whole weeks of himself. When the debt cleared, it gave them back. All of them. At once. Unsorted.

He woke screaming my name, and I was there before the second scream, and he grabbed my arms hard enough to bruise and looked at me and said, "You slapped me. When I hummed the second verse. You slapped me hard enough to bleed and you called it medicine, and you were right, Mum, you were right, and I hate that you were right," and then he cried like the child he'd been when all this started, and I held him on the floor of his own room with wheat stalks still poking up between the floorboards, going brown and brittle around us like the world's saddest decoration.

That's the wound nobody warned us about. We'd all assumed forgetting was the mercy. That whatever the field took, better lost. But it gave everything back, and Ivo woke up remembering the rows being warm. Remembering *nobody's lonely here.* Remembering meaning it. Remembering wanting to stay. He remembers singing in step with the drowned girl from 1974, whose name was Alice, and he remembers liking her. He remembers the exact temperature of belonging to something vast and patient, and now he has to be a fifteen-year-old boy in a falling-down house in a village of forty people, and every morning for the rest of his life he'll wake up having dreamed of the warm rows, and every morning he'll have to decide, again, that he's glad she pulled him out.

Some days he decides fast. Some days I find him at the window looking at the fallow field with an expression I recognize from mirrors, from the year my Thomas died, and I put tea in his hands and don't ask. Asking twice is forbidden. Besides, some questions answer themselves eventually, or worse, they don't, and either way the tea helps.

But here's the other thing about Ivo, and I'll brag because God knows I've earned one brag: three days after, he found Paloma's notebooks and her tape labels and her letter, and he read all of it sitting at my kitchen table, and when he finished he said, "She left the work to me. Not the guilt. The work." And he started transcribing Tam Broadbelt's last recording in her notation, carefully, in pencil, checking each bar against the tape, asking nothing twice. His handwriting is already starting to look like hers. I don't know whether that comforts me or breaks me a little more each time I see it, and I've decided I'm allowed not to know.

* * *

It was a week before I could make myself walk down to the shaft.

The county had already been by — Denny handled them, with his forty-year-old hand and his flat foreman's voice, and told them the site collapsed, unstable ground, nothing down there but mud and old stone, and the survey crew's report says exactly that, because Denny Okafor can lie to a government the way other men say grace. They fenced the field and posted a notice about subsidence and drove away, and the road let them go, because the road was only ever keeping us in, not others out.

The shaft had been boarded over per the official story, but Denny left one board loose, and he told me which one, and he didn't ask why, because Denny understands that some people need to stand near a grave even when there's no body in it.

I went at dusk, with my whisper of a voice and my coat buttoned wrong, and I knelt at the gap in the boards, and I looked down into the dark where the floodlights used to reach.

Her recorder was on the threshold.

Just inside the door, just past where the bolts sat home, sitting on the stone as neatly as if she'd placed it there herself — which, knowing Paloma, she had. It was still running. The little wheel turning, turning, turning, patient as arithmetic, taping a darkness that had nothing left in it to say.

I reached through the gap. My arm isn't long enough — I knew it wouldn't be, I tried anyway, because grief makes you do arithmetic you already know the sum of — and I couldn't touch it. An inch short. Half an inch. The machine sat there in the dark with its wheel going round, recording the inside of a sealed door for an audience of nobody, and I knelt on the cold ground with my arm in the hole up to the shoulder, crying without sound, because sound above a whisper isn't mine anymore. She'd bought my silence with hers, in a way. Or the field kept it. Same difference, most days.

I told myself I'd leave it. Let it run. Let the tape record ten years of nothing and be the truest document in folklore: the sound of a debt fully paid.

But on my way back up the lane I met Ivo coming down with the crowbar, because of course he'd already thought of it, because of course he'd been down twice already, because the boy has her instincts and none of her patience. He'd rigged a lamp on a string. He was lowering it on a fishing rod, of all the ridiculous, brilliant things, angling for the recorder like it was a carp in a pond.

"Denny says the tape's probably full by now," he said. "But she rewound it before she went down. She always rewound. Said a fresh side for a fresh start." He glanced at me, quick, checking — the way he checks now, always checking, that I'm still me and he's still him. "Mum. If there's anything on it. Anything she recorded after the bolts. Do you want to hear it?"

I thought about lying to him. I've earned the right to a few lies, I figure; I've paid retail for wholesale truths these nine days. But he was holding the fishing rod with both hands, and his eyes were hers — not in blood, because there's none, but in the way she taught him to look at a thing he was afraid of, straight on, notebook ready.

"Yes," I whispered.

The lamp swung down into the dark, and the hook caught the strap on the third try, and Ivo reeled her recorder up out of the earth slow and steady, like hauling a bucket from a well, and when it came over the lip of the boards into the last of the light, the wheel was still turning.

Still running. Fresh side. Ninety minutes of blank tape, minus whatever she'd had time to say.

Ivo pressed stop. The click was very loud. Then he pressed play, and turned the volume all the way up, and we leaned our heads together over the little speaker like children over a Christmas parcel, and out of the dark under the field, in a voice as clear and warm and wry as the day she arrived, Paloma Zhou began to speak.
