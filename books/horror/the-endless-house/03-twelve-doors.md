## 3. Twelve Doors

I have counted the doors of this house every night for four months, the way other men count sheep.

It began as a joke I told myself — a professional's trick for winding down after a broadcast, something to occupy the mind while the kettle cooled and the last caller's voice still hung in the rooms like smoke. Eleven doors inside. Twelve if you counted the front door from the outside, which I did, because the discrepancy bothered me in a way I couldn't name and so I named it instead: *the house has one more face than it has mouths.* I said that to myself sometimes, standing in the hall with my tea going cold. The house has one more face than it has mouths. It sounded like a line from a novel. Everything in Hollowmere sounded like a line from a novel eventually; that was the trouble with living inside a story you hadn't agreed to be in.

Eleven inside. I could walk them in the dark: the front door with its swollen frame and its brass knob worn to a dull shine by hands I would never meet; the two doors off the landing, mine and the spare room where the transmitter hummed like a hive; the three downstairs — study, sitting room, the little box-room under the stairs that I used for storage and never opened more than a foot because the hinges sang a note I disliked. Then the pantry door, narrow, painted the green of old bottle-glass. Then the back door to the scullery yard. Then the cellar hatch, which was not properly a door but had a handle and a hinge and therefore counted, because counting is a discipline or it is nothing. That made nine. The tenth was the tall cupboard in my bedroom, its door painted to match the wall so well that guests — Nell, when she visited, before she stopped staying past dark — always asked what was behind it, and were always disappointed to find coats. The eleventh was the door of the airing cupboard beside the bathroom, small and white and warm as a held breath.

Eleven. I knew them the way you know your own teeth. Which is why, on the morning after Edith Marsh's second call, I came down the stairs at half past six and stopped on the fourth step with my hand still on the rail, because there was a twelfth.

* * *

It stood between the pantry and the stairs, in the stretch of wall that had been blank plaster since I moved in — plaster gone the colour of weak tea, water-stained in one corner in a shape I had privately decided looked like a hare. There was no gap in the wall there. There had been no gap in the wall there yesterday. And yet a door stood in it now, flush and proper, with an architrave and a lintel and a panelled leaf, as though the house had grown it overnight the way a mouth grows a tooth: slowly, secretly, out of material that was always there.

The paint was wet.

That was the detail that held me on the stairs longer than any fear did — not the impossibility of the thing but the *freshness* of it. The door was painted a deep, glossy black, and the gloss moved. Not rippled, exactly. Not dripped. But when I tilted my head the sheen shifted across the panels like light on the surface of standing water, slow and heavy, and I understood with a certainty that arrived whole, without argument, the way certain knowledge does: the paint was wet because it had been painted very recently, and it was black because black is what you get when you mix every colour together, and somewhere in this house — in some room I had never found, on some stair I had never climbed — there was a brush still damp, and a hand that had set it down.

I did not touch it. I want that recorded somewhere, even if nowhere is the only place left to record things. I stood on the fourth step in my dressing gown with the cold coming up through the boards and I did not touch it, though my hand rose halfway of its own accord, the way a hand rises toward a flame or a face, and I had to close it into a fist and put the fist in my pocket like a schoolboy suppressing a question.

Instead I did what I do. I observed. I logged.

The door was six feet, perhaps six-one, taller than the pantry door beside it. Its panels were raised, Victorian in style, wrong for the age of the house, which was older than style. There was no keyhole. There was no handle either — and it was only when I noticed the absence of the handle that I noticed the absence of everything else a door requires to be a door. No hinges visible. No letterplate, no knocker, no bell-pull, no number. A door that opened onto nothing and could be opened by nothing. A door that existed, as far as I could tell, purely to be *counted.*

Twelve inside now. Thirteen from the outside.

I went and made tea with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be, and I drank it standing at the kitchen window watching the mist lie in the hollows of the fields like spilled milk, and I thought about Edith Marsh, who had died last Tuesday and who had called me at ten past midnight to request a hymn she said her mother used to sing, humming four bars of it down the line in a voice dry as paper, before the static took her. Four bars. I had caught myself humming them while the kettle boiled. I had stopped mid-note with the sound dying in my throat like a swallowed word, and I had stood very still in my kitchen and listened to the house listen to me.

Then I went to the shed and got the nails and the hammer.

* * *

Nailing a door shut is a strange act when the door has never opened. It is a message rather than a measure. You are not keeping anything in or out; you are saying, in the only language wood and iron speak: *no.* I drove six-inch nails through the architrave into the stud wall on both sides, eight of them a side, then four more through the leaf itself near the lock edge, angling them downward so they'd bite. The hammer reports rang through the house with a brightness that felt almost cheerful, and I found myself swinging harder than the work required, putting my shoulder into it, breathing through my teeth. Somewhere around the tenth nail I realized I was angry, and that the anger was better than the other thing, the cold thing underneath it, and so I let myself be angry. The house creaked in the way old houses creak, settling, sighing, and I chose to hear nothing in it but timber doing what timber does.

When I finished, the door wore its nails like a bristle of grey hair. The wet paint had begun to tack beneath them, gripping the shanks, and I remember thinking — with the detached editorial part of my mind that never stops taking notes, even now, even here — *there's a sentence in that. Save it.*

I slept badly, which was normal. What was not normal was the dream, or the absence of one. I sank toward sleep the way you sink in deep water and then simply — stopped, held, suspended in the black just below consciousness, and in that suspension I heard, not singing exactly, but the *space* where singing would go. A rest. A held breath between bars. Forty-some voices inhaling together and not letting it out. I woke at dawn with my heart going like a second, smaller heart trying to escape through my ribs, and lay listening until I was sure the house was quiet, and then I got up and went down to make breakfast and to look at my nailed-shut door and feel, briefly, like a man who had won an argument.

The nails were on the kitchen table.

All twenty of them. Laid out on the scrubbed pine in two neat rows, heads aligned, points all facing the window — and arranged, when I made myself look properly, when I made myself see it, in groups. Three, then three, then two. A pause. Two, then two, then one. A long pause. One alone at the end, slightly apart, slightly turned, as if it had been placed last and placed carefully, the full stop at the end of a phrase.

I know music notation the way most men know road signs — enough to follow, not enough to read fluently. But you don't need fluency for this. Three, three, two. Rest. Two, two, one. Rest. One. It was rhythm. It was the opening rhythm of the hymn Edith Marsh had hummed to me down a dead woman's telephone line, the lilt of it, the rocking 6/8 sway of it, rendered in iron on pine. The nails had not merely come out of the door. They had been *transposed.*

And the door —

I walked back through the hall with the hammer still in my hand, absurdly, the way a man carries a weapon he has forgotten how to use. Between the pantry and the stairs the wall was blank plaster, tea-coloured, hare-shaped stain in the corner. No architrave. No lintel. No seam, no scar, no nail-holes. The plaster was unbroken, smooth as the skin over a healed wound, and when I pressed my palm flat against it the wall was cool and faintly damp, like the cheek of someone recently wept over.

Twenty nails on the table. Twenty holes that did not exist. The house had taken back its argument without conceding a syllable of it.

* * *

I broadcast that night as I always do, eleven until two, *The Night Desk*, voice steady, records cued, the red ON AIR lamp painting the studio walls the colour of an open wound. The calls came as they come. A man named Albert Prowse, who I happen to know was buried on Thursday, wanting to hear Vera Lynn and telling me the allotment beans had come in lovely. A young woman whose name I didn't catch, laughing softly at her own jokes, asking whether I remembered the summer the quarry froze and everyone skated on it — which never happened, which cannot have happened, the quarry hasn't frozen in sixty years and drowned three boys the last time it tried — and I said yes, I remembered, because that is what you say, and she sighed with happiness and rang off into static.

Between callers I sat in the dead air with the fader down and looked at the studio door — the real one, the one that opens onto the landing, the one I had checked twice before going on air, counting, confirming, eleven, still eleven, still the arithmetic I understood — and I thought about what a door is, actually, stripped of its hardware. It is a decision about a wall. It is a wall that has agreed to be elsewhere sometimes. And a house that can grow a door overnight, paint it wet, accept twenty nails and return them arranged as music — such a house is not a container. It is a participant. It is not where the haunting happens. It is *how.*

At ten past one, the board lit. Line three.

"Cyrus." Edith Marsh's voice, closer than before, clearer, as if she had moved from the far end of a corridor to just outside the studio door. "You nailed it shut."

My hand was on the fader. I don't remember putting it there. "Good evening, Edith," I said, and my voice did what voices on the radio do, found its level, kept its warmth. "You're through to the Night Desk."

"You nailed it shut," she said again, and there was something in her tone I had not heard from any of them yet — not longing, not the mild foggy contentment they all carried like weather. It was urgency. It was almost fear. "It wasn't for you, love. It was for us. It's how we get in to rehearse."

"Rehearse," I said.

"The hymn. We're not allowed to finish it, none of us, we've been stuck on the same verse for — oh, years, dear, you've no idea what years are — but we practice. Every night. Through the doors." A pause, and beneath the pause, faintly, through the line and through the walls and through the floorboards of the endless house, I heard it: the held breath of forty voices, in, and in, and not out. "He painted it fresh for you," Edith said. "That's a courtesy. He doesn't paint for everyone."

"He," I said. "Who is he?"

But the static came up then, soft as snowfall, and Edith's voice thinned into it, and the last thing through, barely words, was: *"Don't learn the words, Cyrus. Whatever you do. Don't learn—"*

The line clicked empty. The ON AIR lamp burned. I sat in the red light with my hands flat on the desk and listened to my own breathing and did not move for a long time.

* * *

Before bed I did the count again. All eleven present and correct — front door, landing pair, study, sitting, box-room, pantry, back, cellar hatch, bedroom cupboard, airing cupboard. I touched each one as I passed, palm to wood, a ritual now whether I wanted one or not. In the kitchen the twenty nails still lay on the table in their rows, three-three-two, rest, two-two-one, rest, one, and I did not move them. Some part of me understood that moving them would be an answer, and I was not ready to answer. Instead I laid a sheet of glass from a picture frame over them, weighting the corners with teacups, sealing the notation away like a specimen, like evidence, like a score I refused to conduct.

Then I stood in the hall a final time, in the dark, in the smell of cold plaster and old varnish, and I spoke aloud to the house — first words I had addressed to it directly in four months of living inside its patience.

"I'm not singing," I told it. "Whatever you're building toward, whatever verse this is, whoever 'he' is — I don't sing."

The house received this in silence. Houses are good at silence; it is their native tongue. But as I climbed the stairs, hand trailing along the wall between the pantry and the landing, the plaster under my palm was cool and faintly damp, and high up near the ceiling, where I had never looked before, where the lamplight had never been asked to reach — the hare-shaped stain had moved. It sat lower now. Closer to eye level.

And it had ears.
