## 5. Edith's Last Request

The call came at 2:47 in the morning, which was early even for the dead.

I had been sitting in the studio with my feet up on the mixing desk, watching the VU needles lie flat against their stops, that particular silence a dead house makes when it's listening. The lamp above the board buzzed its one thin note. Outside, Hollowmere lay under a fog so complete it seemed poured rather than fallen, and I remember thinking—idly, the way you think anything at three in the morning—that the fog had a texture like wet wool, like something spun rather than weathered.

Then line two lit up.

I knew before I answered. There's a quality to the dead callers' line—a faint subsonic hum underneath, like a wire strung through water—that the living never bring with them. I let it ring four times, because I am a coward in small, disciplined ways, and then I pressed the button and pulled the fader down and said, in my best radio voice, the voice I keep like a coat between myself and the cold:

"You're on the air with Cyrus Fairweather. Who's calling?"

"Cyrus." And it was Edith Marsh, but not the Edith of last Tuesday's call, not the sweet, wandering Edith who'd spent forty minutes telling me about her dahlias as if she were still alive to tend them. This voice was stripped down to its joists. Clear. Urgent. A woman leaning into a wind I couldn't hear. "Cyrus, listen to me. Don't do your voice. Don't do the radio voice. I haven't got time for the radio voice."

I took my hand off the fader. "Edith."

"Good." A breath came down the line, wet and long, the breath of someone whose lungs have been full for six days. "You visited the churchyard yet?"

"No."

"You will. That's why I'm ringing. Listen now, love, because I can only be this clear once, maybe twice, and after that I go back to—" something moved behind her words, a sound like many mouths opening underwater, "—back to the rest of them. Sunday. You have to come before Sunday."

"Why Sunday?" I asked, though some cold cellar of me already knew, the way you know the shape of a thing by the hole it leaves.

"Because that's when they sing." Her voice dropped, and I turned the monitor up without thinking, and the hum beneath her deepened as if it resented being heard. "Cyrus, I need you to write this down. Are you writing?"

"I'm writing," I said, and I was, though my hand had gone strange and far away, a hand on the end of someone else's arm.

"Don't learn the words."

I stopped writing. The pen left a small ink scar on the log sheet, a little comet tail where it had skidded. "Edith—"

"That's the message. That's all of it. Four words and I've carried them across six days and whatever's between, so don't you dare lose them." She laughed then, briefly, and it was such an Edith laugh—dry, self-deprecating, the laugh of a woman who'd buried a husband and two cats and still kept geraniums going through frost—that my eyes stung, sudden and stupid. "Don't learn the words. Not from me, not from the tapes, not from that lovely hymn book in St. Aldhelm's, third pew from the back, the one with the water damage. Especially not that one. It wants to be learned. Everything about it wants to be learned."

"What happens if I learn them?"

Silence. Long enough that I checked the board—the line was live, the needle trembling in its narrow groove like a pulse. When she spoke again her voice had gone quieter, and closer, as though she'd leaned right up against whatever served her for a mouth.

"You'll know what happens. That's the cruelty of it. Nobody tricks you, Cyrus. Nobody puts a gun to anyone's head. They just teach you a song, and it's beautiful, and by the time you understand what it costs you'll already know every note by heart." A pause. "My Albert learned it. In 'eleven. He was in the quarry that day. He got out because he stopped singing."

The fog pressed against the studio window. I became aware, distantly, of the house around me—the eleven doors inside, the twelve outside, all of them shut, all of them, I wanted to believe, just doors.

"Edith," I said carefully, "you died last Tuesday. I went to the wake. I saw the coffin."

"I know what I did," she said, with terrible mildness. "It doesn't stop the wanting to help. That's the other cruelty. You'd think dying would take the worry out of you. It doesn't. It just gives you more to worry about and less time to worry in." Another of those wet breaths. "Come to the churchyard tomorrow. Daylight, mind—not dusk, dusk belongs to them now, the whole village is theirs after sundown and they don't even know it. Come in daylight, find my stone, and read what's on it. Then come home and don't sing anything. Not along to the wireless, not in the bath, not under your breath. Nothing. Until Sunday's done."

"And after Sunday?"

She didn't answer that. Instead: "Cyrus. Sweetheart. How many doors does your house have?"

The question went through me like a nail finding old wood. "Eleven inside. Twelve outside."

"Mm," she said, in the tone of a woman looking at a bill she'd expected. "And how many did it have when you moved in?"

I opened my mouth and found I didn't know. I genuinely did not know, and the not-knowing sat in my chest like swallowed river water. Edith made a soft sound, almost tender.

"There it is," she said. "That's what learning costs. Not all at once. Bit by bit. A door here. A name there. You've already paid some and you didn't feel the change, did you? That's how it works. That's how it always works." Her voice frayed at the edges then, and beneath it I heard the choir-sound again, nearer, patient, a congregation clearing its collective throat. "I'm losing the clear. Cyrus—churchyard. Daylight. Read the stone. Don't learn the words. And when it gets hard—and it will get hard, love, harder than you think—you remember that some of us liked you. Alive or otherwise. Some of us listened to your show every night because your voice was the only honest thing on the airwaves, and we'd hate for it to end up singing *that*."

"Edith—"

"Log me out, sweetheart. Caller number nine. Tell Nell she should eat something. She goes pale."

The line clicked into static, but the static wasn't empty—it had a shape, a held note decaying slowly, like the last syllable of a hymn in a stone church, and then it was only static, and then the needle lay flat again, and I sat in the buzzing lamplight with four words on a log sheet and ink on my fingers and the certain knowledge that I had, at some point I could not remember, lost a door.

* * *

I did not sleep. Sleep would have meant dreams, and since the bell rang on Thursday night I had distrusted my own sleep the way you distrust a dog that's started staring at a corner. So I made coffee I didn't drink and watched the fog work itself loose from the fields as the sky went the grey of dishwater, and at half seven, when the light was honest if not warm, I put on my coat and walked down into Hollowmere.

The village in daylight was almost worse than the village at midnight. At midnight the wrongness hides; in daylight it has nowhere to go and stands about in plain sight. Mrs. Tredwell was sweeping her step and swept the same three feet of pavement over and over, her broom keeping a rhythm—*one-two-three, one-two-three*—that I refused to count the beats of. Two men outside the post office were talking about football, and both of them paused at the same instant, mid-word, heads tilting toward the church tower like dogs hearing a whistle no one else hears, and then resumed exactly where they'd left off. Nobody looked tired. That was the worst of it. The whole village wore the smooth, rested faces of people who are sleeping very well indeed.

St. Aldhelm's churchyard sits on the rise above the village, walled in lichen-eaten stone, and the gate hinge cried out the same two-note complaint it has cried for a century. I told myself the walk up the path was just a walk. I told myself a great many things.

Edith Marsh's grave was easy to find. Fresh earth, brown and raw as a wound, the mound still settled unevenly, wreaths laid out with village precision—Albert's photograph in a frame, a spray of white chrysanthemums, a small tin of boiled sweets because everyone in Hollowmere knew Edith kept a tin in her handbag and offered them round like communion.

The headstone was granite, ordered, I supposed, weeks ago, in the ordinary way of things. The inscription was cut clean:

**EDITH ALICE MARSH**
**1941 – 2023**
***AT REST***

Ordinary. Final. Except.

Below *AT REST*, in the same face, the same depth of cut, the same unweathered granite dust still sitting in the grooves—as fresh as if the mason had packed up his chisels five minutes ago—ran a second line:

**AND YET SHE SINGS — UNTIL THE 14TH**

Today was the eighth.

I stood there a long while. A blackbird worked the yew hedge, unhurried, indifferent. Somewhere below, Mrs. Tredwell's broom whispered its three-count against the pavement. I read the line perhaps twenty times, hoping it would resolve into something innocent—a family joke, a misprint, a mason's whimsy. Granite does not do whimsy. Granite does not do misprints. And the dust in the letters was still bright, still new, and I found myself doing arithmetic I did not want to do: six days until the fourteenth. Six days until Sunday.

I crouched and touched the carved words. The stone was cold in a way the morning air couldn't account for, cold the way the bottom of a quarry is cold, and when I drew my finger along the hyphen in the second date it came away damp. Not dew. Damp from below, rising, as if the stone had been standing in water all night.

"Until the fourteenth," I said aloud, and my voice sounded obscene in that quiet, and the blackbird stopped working the hedge to look at me.

Because here is what I understood, crouching there with wet fingers and a heart going like a trapped bird: the stone wasn't a warning. Warnings are for people who might turn aside. The stone was a schedule. Edith hadn't said *come before Sunday* because something might happen on Sunday. She'd said it because something had already been arranged for Sunday, booked, inscribed in granite by hands that presumably believed they were cutting an ordinary stone—or by no hands at all—and the second line had simply arrived, the way the thirteenth door arrives, the way the calls arrive, the way everything in Hollowmere lately arrived: quietly, freshly cut, and impossible to un-know.

*At rest.* And yet she sings. Until the fourteenth. After the fourteenth, then, she would stop singing—or start singing something else—or the word *until* would finally mean what it said, and whatever part of Edith Marsh had been holding itself lucid for six days to make one phone call would be folded back into the choir with the rest of them, thirty-one voices strong and waiting for a thirty-second.

For a Fairweather.

I got up. My knees cracked; I was forty-one years old and felt excavated. Down in the village the broom had stopped. I noticed the silence of it before I noticed anything else, and then I noticed the men outside the post office had stopped too, and the blackbird had gone, and the whole of Hollowmere was holding its breath in the way of a congregation waiting for the organist to begin.

And then, faint and far off, from the direction of the flooded quarry beyond the ridge—though the wind was blowing the wrong way to carry any sound from there, and though there was nothing at the quarry but black water and drowned machinery—came a sound I will hear until whatever day they carve my own second line:

Thirty-odd voices. In rounds. Singing the first bars of a hymn I did not know and recognized anyway, the way you recognize a face across a crowded station platform, the way you recognize your own name spoken in another room.

I did not hum along. I clenched my teeth until my jaw ached and I did not hum along, and I walked down out of the churchyard with Edith's four words repeating in my skull like a bell that wouldn't stop ringing:

*Don't learn the words.*

Behind me, on the fresh-cut granite, the damp crept a little further up the stone—and the date waited, patient as water, six days out.
