## 1. The Long Road In

The bus had been climbing out of the valley for an hour when the fog decided to let it through.

Ulyana Brightwater noticed this in the way one notices a wrong note in a familiar song—not immediately, but with a slow accumulation of unease. Fog on a moorland road was ordinary. Fog that behaved with intention was not. It lay across the hills like spilled milk, thick and unbroken, and then, as the bus approached the pass where the road dipped toward Hollowmere, it drew back. Not dispersed—drew back, folding itself against the hedgerows and stone walls on either side of the road like curtains being held open by unseen hands.

Ulyana pressed their forehead to the cold glass and watched the mist hold its position as the bus passed between it. Behind them, in the wing mirror's small distorted world, the fog closed again over the road they had traveled, erasing it entirely.

*Just weather*, Ulyana told themselves, and reached for their bag to check the letter of appointment for perhaps the ninth time. *St. Brannoc's School. Headmaster A. Vane. We are pleased to offer you the position of Form Teacher, effective the first of September.* The paper was real. The ink was real. The salary was modest but sufficient, and the cottage above the bakery came with the post, which meant no rent, which meant Ulyana could finally begin paying down the debts of a decade spent drifting from one temporary classroom to another. This was supposed to be the settling. The long stay. The place where the drifting stopped.

The bus groaned down through the pass, and Hollowmere appeared below them all at once, the way things appear in dreams: complete, sudden, and slightly too still.

It sat in a bowl of hills beside a grey mere that gave the town its name, a cluster of slate roofs and chimneys around a church spire, smoke rising straight up into windless air. From this height it looked almost picturesque—a postcard town, the kind of place that sold jam and fudge to tourists who never stayed past dark. But even from the bus window, Ulyana noticed what the postcards would never show: the way the streets seemed arranged not around the market square but around the old chapel at the town's heart, every road bending toward it like iron filings toward a magnet. And the fog—it had followed them down after all, or some of it had, pooling now in the low streets, moving with a patience that weather does not have.

"First time in Hollowmere?" The voice came from across the aisle. An elderly woman in a brown coat, hands folded on a wicker basket, smiling with genuine warmth.

"Is it that obvious?"

"You've got the look. Teachers always do." She chuckled. "I'm Mrs. Tregowan. I run the post office. You'll be the new one at St. Brannoc's, then."

"Ulyana Brightwater." They offered a hand, and she shook it, her grip dry and bird-light.

"Well, welcome, Miss—or is it Mister? Never mind, doesn't signify." She waved the question away before Ulyana could answer, and something about the swiftness of it—the refusal to know, the deliberate not-seeing—prickled at Ulyana like a burr caught in wool. "You'll like it here. Quiet town. Good people. Keep your head down and it'll keep you right."

"That sounds less like advice and more like instructions," Ulyana said, aiming for lightness.

Mrs. Tregowan smiled again. It was the same smile as before, exactly the same, deployed like a piece of equipment. "The bakery's your stop. Tell Marta I sent you." And she turned to the window, conversation concluded, watching the fog slide along the hedgerow as if expecting it to wave back.

* * *

The bus hissed to a stop outside a shopfront with HOLLOWMERE BAKERY painted in fading gold leaf across the glass. Warmth and the smell of yeast and burnt sugar poured out when Ulyana pushed the door open, and for a moment the day felt ordinary again, gloriously mundane. A broad woman with flour to her elbows stood behind the counter, and a girl of perhaps fifteen was stacking loaves in the window with the careful precision of someone arranging evidence.

"Marta Kessler?" Ulyana asked. "I'm the new teacher. Ulyana Brightwater. Mrs. Tregowan sent me."

Marta looked up. Her face did something complicated—arrived at warmth by way of something else, something wary, the way a dog decides whether to bark. "Ah. Yes. The room's ready. Up those stairs there, door at the top. Rent's included with the post, as agreed." She said it quickly, all business, as if rehearsed. Then, softer: "You'll want to get settled before dark. Days are shortening."

"It's barely three o'clock."

"Aye," said Marta, and turned back to her dough.

The girl in the window had stopped stacking. She was looking at Ulyana—not rudely, but with a fixed, searching attention, the way children look at adults they are trying to decide whether to warn. Her eyes were pale grey, nearly the color of the fog outside, and when Ulyana smiled at her, she looked away so fast she knocked a loaf sideways and had to catch it with both hands.

"Ilse," Marta said without turning around. "Mind the bread."

"Yes, Mum."

Ulyana took the stairs at the side of the shop. They were narrow and worn hollow in the centers of each tread, and the wooden handrail was smooth as river stone from fifty years of palms. At the top, a door painted a green that had once been cheerful opened onto two rooms and a view: the high street below, the mere glinting grey-green beyond the rooftops, and the chapel spire rising black against the white sky, closer than it had any right to be. Ulyana could see the top of its tower from the window, and the weathervane upon it—which was not a cockerel but something else, some elongated shape that might have been a fish or a serpent or a hand pointing down.

The rooms were clean, simply furnished, smelling faintly of beeswax and cold ash. A bed, a table, a chair, a washstand. On the wall hung a mirror in a dark oval frame, and Ulyana caught their own face in it—tired, travel-flushed, thirty-something and pretending otherwise—and thought, absurdly, *you look like a person arriving at the beginning of a story*. They laughed at themselves, unpacked, and put the kettle on.

Outside the window, the fog was gathering in the street below. Not thinning with the afternoon, as fog should, but thickening, rising from the gutters like breath. People moved through it with their collars up, walking quickly, and Ulyana noticed—they were quite sure they were not imagining it—that none of them walked alone. Every figure on the high street had another figure close beside them, shoulder to shoulder, as if the town had agreed upon a rule that no one would ever be the last one home.

* * *

They went out at dusk to buy bread and see the town, because Ulyana had learned long ago that the fastest way to feel at home anywhere was to walk it until your feet believed in it. The bakery sold them a loaf still warm enough to steam through the paper bag. Ilse rang it up without meeting their eyes. Outside, the fog had become a presence, soft-edged and silver under the gaslamps, and the town moved within it like figures in a photograph developing slowly.

Hollowmere was beautiful. That was the first thing. Slate and stone and crooked lanes, gardens gone riotous with late roses, the mere at the town's edge lying flat as hammered pewter. The second thing, noticed more slowly, was that it was empty in a way towns are not empty. There were people—there were lights in windows, voices behind doors—but the streets belonged to the fog. Doors closed as Ulyana passed. Not slammed; closed, gently, the click of latches following them down the lane like polite applause.

At the corner by the chapel, an old man was locking the churchyard gate. He nodded to Ulyana, a crisp downward dip of the chin. "Evening. New teacher, are you? Vane'll be pleased. Good man, Mr. Vane. Looks after everyone." His eyes went past Ulyana's shoulder to the fog-filled street behind, and he added, in a tone of mild remark that landed with the weight of scripture: "Best not be out alone after the lamps are lit. Town rule."

"I wasn't aware there were rules."

"There's always rules," the old man said pleasantly, and walked away into the white.

Ulyana stood a moment in the mouth of the churchyard lane. The chapel rose above them, older than the parish church by centuries if the stonework was anything to judge by, its walls furred with moss, its windows narrow and deep-set. There was no light inside it. There was, Ulyana realized after a moment, no lock on its door either—only a hasp, empty, the padlock hanging open on a nail beside it as if locked and unlocked so often it had been left ready. As if whatever needed tending inside was tended often.

From somewhere beneath the ground—beneath the chapel, beneath the lane, beneath their own feet—came a sound. Faint. Rhythmic. Like a great slow heart beating in the earth, or like something tapping time to music only it could hear. Three beats. A pause. Three beats.

A goose walked over Ulyana's grave, their grandmother used to say. Ulyana had never believed in such things. They were a rationalist by constitution, a list-maker, a keeper of mark books and lesson plans, a person who believed that most hauntings were draughts and most monsters were neighbors. So they catalogued the sound—subterranean percussion, possibly water in a culvert, possibly machinery from the mill—and filed it under *investigate later*, and walked back to the bakery with their collar up and their heart beating faster than the evidence warranted.

* * *

That night, in the little room above the bakery, Ulyana wrote the first page of a new notebook. They were a great one for notebooks; each new school year began with one, its first pages full of optimism, its last pages full of everything optimism hadn't survived.

*Observations, Day One,* they wrote.

*1. The town is beautiful and the people are kind, in the way of people performing kindness from a script.*

*2. Nobody meets your eyes twice. First look: assessing. Second look: avoided.*

*3. Everyone knows who I am before I introduce myself. Fine in a small town. Except they also seem to know what I am FOR.*

*4. The fog behaves strangely. (Weather. Don't be fanciful.)*

*5. Something under the chapel keeps time. (Water. Machinery. Don't be fanciful.)*

*6. "Keep your head down and it'll keep you right." — Mrs. Tregowan*

*7. "Best not be out alone after the lamps are lit." — churchyard man*

*8. Twenty-six desks. Nineteen students enrolled. Ask Vane about it tomorrow.*

They paused over the last line. They hadn't seen the classroom yet—hadn't been inside St. Brannoc's at all—but the number twenty-six had been sitting in their mind since the bus, unbidden, the way a tune sits in the mind. Perhaps it had come from the appointment letter. Perhaps from nothing at all. Ulyana was a rationalist, but they were also a teacher, and teachers learn early that the mind counts children the way the heart counts heartbeats: automatically, and sometimes ahead of the facts.

Below, through the floorboards, came the muffled sounds of the bakery closing—trays stacked, chairs upturned, the bolt shot home with a sound like a full stop. Then quiet. Then, very faintly, the girl's voice, Ilse, saying something to her mother in a language Ulyana didn't recognize, and Marta answering in the same tongue, and the tone of the exchange was not conversational but urgent, hushed, the tone of people arguing about a fire they can smell but cannot yet see.

Ulyana blew out the lamp and lay in the narrow bed listening to the fog press against the window like something reading over their shoulder.

They had wanted this, they reminded themselves. The settling. The long stay. A town small enough to matter in, a schoolroom of their own, children to teach and protect and send out into the world better than they'd found them. Cautious optimism—that was the honest phrase for what they'd carried up the hill today, and it was still there, mostly. Tomorrow they would meet the headmaster. Tomorrow they would stand in their classroom and count the desks and laugh at themselves for the strange arithmetic that had followed them off the bus.

But sleep came slowly, and when it finally took them, it was shallow and full of corridors. In the dream, Ulyana stood at a doorway at the end of a long stone passage, and from behind the door came a sound like a congregation humming, and beneath the humming, keeping perfect time, something tapped. Three beats. A pause. Three beats. And a voice made of many small voices said, patiently, the way one says a thing one has said for fifty years:

*You're late. But you've brought the counting with you. We can hear it. We can always hear the counting.*

Ulyana woke before dawn with the taste of chalk dust in their mouth and the number twenty-six written on the fogged window glass—in the condensation, from the inside—in a child's careful hand.

They told themselves the landlord must have a previous tenant's ghost of a habit about them. They wiped it away with their sleeve.

It did not come back. But all through the morning, as Ulyana dressed and ate and stepped out into the silver street to walk the short way to St. Brannoc's for their first day, they had the persistent, prickling certainty—rationalist, list-maker, keeper of mark books—that the fog parted ahead of them as they walked. Parted reluctantly. The way a crowd parts around someone it has already decided about.

And behind them, softly, it closed.
