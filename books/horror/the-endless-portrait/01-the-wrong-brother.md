## 1. The Wrong Brother

The dead man in the casket was not my brother.

I want to be careful with that sentence, because I know how it sounds. Grief does strange arithmetic; it subtracts and adds and carries the one into places it doesn't belong. I've read enough about bereavement to write a passable paper on it. The mind rejects the unacceptable. The eye refuses the evidence. Fine. Noted. Filed under *things people will say to me in soft voices over ham*.

But I have spent thirty-four years looking at Julian Eastwick's face — most of them voluntarily, some of them across a chessboard at two in the morning while he cheated with a serenity I could never master — and the face in the casket belonged to no one I had ever met.

It was a good face. That's what unsettled me most, I think. It wasn't a botch job, not some wax approximation assembled by an indifferent mortician working from a photograph. It was *plausible*. The jawline was right. The hair was right, that particular Eastwick brown that goes iron at the temples before it goes grey, as if we age like expensive hardware. Even the small scar above the left eyebrow was there, the one from the bicycle incident of 1997, which Julian maintained was my fault and which I maintain was entirely his fault for braking when I said don't.

Everything was right except everything.

The mouth was wrong. Julian's mouth sat crooked, permanently half-smiling, the mouth of a man who knew the punchline before you finished the joke. This mouth was symmetrical. Patient. A mouth that waited to be told things. And the eyes — closed, thank God, because I don't know what I would have done if they'd been open — even closed they were wrong. Julian's eyes sat deep, shadowed by those heavy Eastwick brows. These eyes were shallow-set, placid, the eyes of a man who had never once wondered what was behind a locked door.

I stood at the casket in the front parlor of Eastwick House, surrounded by cut lilies doing their best against forty years of cigarette smoke soaked into the wallpaper, and I looked at my brother's corpse, and I thought: *who are you?*

"Sebastian." Aunt Cordelia touched my elbow with her ringed hand, gentle as a parking ticket. "You shouldn't stand so long. He'd hate that, you know. All that fuss."

"He hated fuss," I agreed, because Julian did hate fuss, and this man — whoever he was — struck me as someone who would have found fuss restful.

Cordelia patted my arm and drifted off toward the sherry, and I stayed where I was, because someone in this family had to look directly at things.

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about the Eastwicks: we are a family that has survived its own history by the simple method of declining to record it. No photo albums. No letters saved. Uncle Aldous burned a crate of "clutter" in 2009 that I'm fairly certain included every birth announcement issued since the war. We are people who keep our pasts the way other families keep their silver — wrapped in cloth, in a dark room, brought out only under supervision.

So maybe it's genetic, what I did next.

I went up to my room — the blue room, the one I've slept in since childhood, the one that still smells faintly of the lemon polish Maren uses on everything she can reach — and I took a notebook out of my bag. A plain one, black cover, unlined pages, bought in a stationery shop in Lisbon three years ago and carried around ever since like a talisman I kept forgetting to believe in. On the first page I wrote:

**Documentation of Anomalies. Re: Julian Eastwick, d. age 34.**

Then, underneath:

**1. The face is wrong.**

It helped. I'll say that for the ritual, whatever else became of it later. Writing it down moved the thing out of my chest and onto the page, where it could be examined instead of merely endured. My therapist — the one I saw for six months after the divorce, before she moved to Sedona, which tells you everything — used to call this "externalizing." I call it being a novelist. Same technique, worse insurance.

I went back down.

The viewing was nearly over. The funeral home people had that particular professional patience of people who handle grief the way dockworkers handle cargo: respectfully, efficiently, without wondering too much what's inside. Relatives milled. Cousins I hadn't seen since Aldous's seventieth embraced me with the desperate warmth of people who suspect they should call more often. Everyone, at some point, made their pilgrimage to the casket, looked in, and said some version of the same sentence.

*"He looks peaceful."*

*"Just like him. Just like sleeping."*

*"God, he looks exactly like you."*

That last one came from my cousin Theo, who gripped my shoulder as he said it, and I looked at him — really looked, the way you look at a witness you're about to depose — and Theo looked back at me with clear, uncomplicated sorrow, and I understood that he meant it. He saw Julian. They all saw Julian.

Which meant either the entire family was mad, or I was, or —

Or there was a third option, and the third option was the one that made my hands go cold, standing there in the smoke-and-lily air of the front parlor.

Because here is the thing about the Eastwicks. We don't look. We have never looked. We are a family of magnificent, hereditary not-looking. Ask us about Great-Uncle Ambrose and we'll tell you he "went abroad." Ask us about the east wing and we'll tell you it's "structural." Ask Maren how many children were born into this family and she'll pour you a drink. We have survived four centuries of our own history through the disciplined application of a blind spot the size of a cathedral.

Everyone else saw Julian because everyone else had been trained since birth to see Julian. To see whatever the family needed seen.

I had trained myself out of it. Twenty years of writing fiction does that to you. You spend your career noticing that the detective never asks about the second glass on the table, and eventually you become the sort of person who asks about the second glass.

* * *

The hands did it, finally. The hands made it undeniable.

The mourners had thinned. Cordelia had gone to lie down, announcing it loudly enough that we all understood it as a command performance. Dario — the excavation foreman, a broad, sun-cured man who'd been hired that week to dig foundations for the funeral tent and who had the wary politeness of a contractor working inside someone else's family drama — was stacking chairs at the edge of the room. And I was alone with the casket, which felt less like an accident and more like an appointment.

I looked at the dead man's hands.

Every Eastwick carries the same mark. Left palm, base of the thumb: a pale crescent scar, half an inch long, curved like a fingernail clipping. Nobody knows where it comes from. Every one of us has it — me, Julian, Maren, Theo, Cordelia's lot, cousins twice removed, all of us, always. Aldous calls it "the family sign" when he bothers to acknowledge it at all, which is to say he calls it nothing and glares if you mention it. Babies get it in the cradle. There's no wound, no story, no origin. It's simply there, the way the house is simply there, the way the tithe — but I'm getting ahead of myself, and God knows I didn't know about the tithe yet. That knowledge was still three days and one furious old man away.

The point is: I turned the stranger's hand over — gently, apologizing under my breath to him, to Julian, to whoever he was — and the left palm was smooth. Unmarked. Whole.

No crescent. No scar. No Eastwick.

I stood there holding a dead man's hand in the house I'd grown up in, and the floor seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a ship tilts when the engine changes pitch and you realize, with animal certainty, that you're moving.

He wasn't just not-Julian. He wasn't Eastwick at all. Someone had gone to extraordinary trouble — the face, the hair, the scar above the eyebrow, all of it rendered with loving precision — and yet they had missed the one mark that every single member of my family carries like a brand. As if the forger had studied photographs. Photographs of the face.

But nobody photographs Eastwick hands. We don't take pictures. Remember?

Whoever built this man built him from the outside. From what could be seen. From what the family would bother to look at.

And the family had looked, and the family had seen Julian, and the family was upstairs drinking sherry and discussing casseroles.

* * *

I drew him.

I don't know why drawing felt necessary when writing didn't, but it did. I sat in the window seat of the blue room with the notebook on my knee and I sketched the dead man's open left hand from memory — I have a decent memory for hands; novelists collect them the way birders collect songs — and beside it I sketched my own, palm up, the little crescent sitting at the base of the thumb like a moon that never sets. Then Julian's, from memory, identical to mine. Three hands on one page. Two marked. One blank.

Underneath, I wrote:

**2. No Eastwick scar. Whoever this is, he was never one of us. So whose son is he? Whose brother? And where — **

My pen stopped. Because the next question arrived fully formed, the way the worst questions do, wearing no clothes, making no apology:

**— and where is Julian?**

Not *was*. Not the question the casket answered. The question the casket opened.

I sat with that for a long time. Outside the window, the light was going amber over the east lawn, where Dario's excavator sat parked and patient as a grazing animal, ready to break ground in the morning for the tent foundation. Somewhere below me, in the bones of the house, pipes knocked and settled, the way they always had, the way I'd never once listened to before tonight.

I wrote one more line at the bottom of the page, pressing hard enough to emboss the paper:

**3. If the body isn't Julian's, then the funeral is for no one. And if the funeral is for no one, then someone wanted us all to stop asking after him. Which means someone knew we might otherwise start.**

Then I closed the notebook, and downstairs the front door opened, and I heard Maren's voice in the hall — tight, bright, pitched a half-step too high, the voice of a woman carrying something heavy and pretending it's luggage — saying to someone I couldn't see:

"No. He hasn't been down there. Nobody goes down there. Why would anyone go down there?"

The house knocked its pipes, once, like a throat being cleared.
