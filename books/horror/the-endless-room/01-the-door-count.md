## 1. The Door Count

The bus let Coral off at the bottom of Marrow Creek's only hill at seven-forty in the morning, and by seven forty-one she understood that the town had been built facing away from something.

She couldn't have said what gave her the impression. The houses along Creek Road all had their porches on the far sides, their backs to the school, as if the whole street were a row of people politely declining to look at it. Even the trees seemed to lean east, away from the building's flat gray face. Marrow Creek School sat at the top of the hill like a tooth that hadn't been pulled yet—three stories of brick and tall windows, a clock tower with hands frozen at 10:12, and a flagpole flying a flag so faded its stripes had gone the color of old meat.

Coral shifted her bag on her shoulder and climbed.

She was thirty-four years old, and this was her fourth school in six years, and she had learned not to read too much into first impressions. Towns were towns. Old buildings were old buildings. A clock that didn't run was a budget problem, not an omen. She repeated these things to herself the way other people said their prayers—not because she believed them, but because the rhythm steadied her hands.

The front doors were oak, heavy enough that she had to put her hip into them. Inside, the air was warm—warmer than October had any right to make it—and it smelled of floor wax and pencil shavings and, underneath both, something faintly mineral, like the inside of a cave. Like wet stone in a place no rain had ever reached.

"Ms. Sterling."

The woman coming toward her across the entry hall was small and square-shouldered, with white hair cut close as a hedge and glasses on a chain of amber beads. She extended a hand with the crisp efficiency of someone who had shaken ten thousand hands and intended to outlast them all.

"Principal Voss. Welcome to Marrow Creek." Her grip was dry and brief. "You're earlier than I expected. Good. There are things we should go over before the students arrive."

"I'd like that," Coral said. "I like to know a place before I teach in it."

Something moved behind Principal Voss's eyes—too quick to name, gone before it finished arriving. "Yes," she said. "Well. Most people prefer not to know a place at all. It makes the living easier."

She led Coral down the main corridor without waiting for a reply.

The hallway was wide and high-ceilinged, lined with classroom doors painted a deep institutional green, each one numbered in gold leaf that had dulled to the color of weak tea. Overhead, fluorescent tubes hummed in their fixtures, every single one of them lit, though the morning sun came through the windows strong enough to throw long bright panels across the linoleum. Coral noticed that the lights over the stairwells were lit too. And the lights in the alcoves where there were no classrooms at all, just drinking fountains and bulletin boards soft with decades of staples.

Every light. Burning.

"We keep them on," Voss said, without turning around. Coral hadn't realized she'd been staring. "All of them. Day and night. The custodian handles the evening rounds, but if you're ever here late—if you're ever anywhere in the building late—you leave your lights on when you go. Every room you've passed through. Is that understood?"

"It seems wasteful," Coral said carefully.

"It is." Voss stopped at the water fountain and looked back at her, and for a moment her face did something complicated, the way ice does before it decides whether to hold or crack. "The electric bill is eleven hundred dollars a month, Ms. Sterling, and the school board has complained about it every year since 1975, and every year I tell them the same thing: pay it. You'll find we do a great many wasteful things here. Consider them part of the curriculum."

* * *

Her classroom was Room 9, second floor, north side. Twenty-six desks. A chalkboard green as pond water. A window looking out on the playground, where a single maple dropped its leaves onto asphalt with a sound like slow applause.

The day itself was almost ordinary. Fourth period, she taught fractions to children who chewed their pencils and stared out windows and asked to go to the bathroom in shifts, exactly as children everywhere did. Fifth period, a boy named Marcus drew a door on the corner of his worksheet—a little arched door with a knob—and when she leaned over his desk he covered it with his forearm, fast, the way boys cover answers they've copied.

"That's nice," she said. "What is it?"

"Nothing." He scrubbed it out with his eraser until the paper tore. "Sorry, Ms. Sterling."

He wouldn't look at her for the rest of the period.

It was in the last hour of the day that she met Wren.

Wren Halloway sat in the back corner by the window, a thin girl with a brown braid going gray at the temple—actually gray, Coral noticed, two strands of silver-white in all that dark hair, though the girl couldn't have been more than thirteen. She did her work quickly and correctly and never raised her hand, and when the bell rang she was the last one packing up, moving with the deliberate slowness of someone waiting for the room to empty.

When the last of her classmates had gone, Wren approached the desk.

"You're new," she said. Not a question.

"This year. First day."

Wren nodded, as if confirming a suspicion. Then she glanced at the doorway, at the empty hall, and lowered her voice to something barely above breath.

"Don't take the room across from yours."

"The room across from mine?"

"Room 10." The girl's eyes flicked toward the wall, as if the room might hear itself named. "It's fine during the day. But don't grade papers in there after dark, and don't—" She stopped. Started again, choosing words like footsteps on ice. "There's a room on this floor nobody uses. Room 12. It's not on the fire drill map. If you ever feel warm air coming from under a door, and there shouldn't be anyone in there—"

"Wren." Coral kept her voice gentle. "Is someone bothering you? Another teacher?"

The girl shook her head slowly, braid swinging.

"It's always warm," she said. "Room 12. Even in January. Even when the heat's broken, which it always is, because the boiler's older than my grandpa. Everybody knows it's warm. Nobody says why." She hitched her bag higher on her shoulder. "You seem like somebody who asks why. So I'm telling you now, so you can decide not to."

And she left, her sneakers silent on the linoleum, leaving Coral alone in Room 9 with the lights burning overhead and the maple leaves applauding outside the window.

* * *

She stayed late. Of course she stayed late—first days generated paperwork the way wounds generated blood—and by the time she climbed back down to the main floor to return her keys, the corridors had gone dim except for those faithful fluorescents, and the windows showed nothing but her own reflection and the black hill beyond.

That was when she heard the counting.

It came from around the corner near the auditorium doors, a man's voice, low and unhurried, speaking numbers into the empty hall the way a shepherd counts sheep.

"—eleven. Twelve. Thirteen."

Coral stopped.

"—fourteen. Fifteen." A pause, long enough that she thought it had ended. Then, quieter, almost tender: "Sixteen. All sixteen. All sixteen accounted for."

She should have announced herself. Instead she stood in the shadow of the trophy case—its shelves holding a single basketball trophy from 1973 and nothing else—and watched a man come around the corner pushing a janitor's cart.

He was old, sixty at least, with forearms like rope and a face weathered down to its essentials. His name tag said AMOS. He walked the corridor slowly, touching each doorframe as he passed it, and when he reached the end of the hall he turned and looked directly at where Coral stood, as though he'd known she was there since the eleventh door.

"You count the doors," she said. It came out more accusation than question.

"I do." He didn't seem embarrassed. He resumed stacking his rags. "Every evening. Been doing it thirty-one years. Before me it was a fellow named Ellery, and before him a woman called Mrs. Tabb, God rest her."

"Why?"

Amos considered her for a moment. He had eyes the color of creek water, pale and patient, and they made her feel like something being weighed.

"Doors are how it keeps track," he said at last. "Of the building. Of us. Long as the number comes out right—sixteen on this floor, fourteen on the second, twelve on the third—long as the count holds, everything stays where it belongs." He pushed the cart past her, then paused. "You'll want to be careful with questions like *why*, Ms. Sterling. This school runs on not asking. Fifty years of everybody agreeing, all at once, never saying it out loud. It's the only reason there's still a school here at all."

"There's still a school here," Coral said, "because children live in this town."

"Yes," Amos said. "They do." Something crossed his face—grief wearing work clothes—and he gripped the cart's handle until his knuckles paled. "Get home before full dark, teacher. And when you lock your classroom tonight, check the number on your door twice. Just to see it's yours."

* * *

Outside, dusk had come down like a lid. Coral walked to the edge of the parking lot and stopped, because she had forgotten her scarf, and because—she told herself—it was only that.

She looked back at the school.

Every window on every floor glowed yellow against the gathering dark, dozens of them, all the way up to the clock tower, where the hands still pointed at 10:12 and would point there, she suspected, long after everyone currently alive had forgotten why. The building blazed with light like a ship keeping lamps lit for a harbor that no longer existed—or for something in the water that needed to believe the harbor was occupied.

Room 12 is always warm. Never let a hallway go dark. Sixteen, all sixteen accounted for.

Everyone knew. That was the thing she couldn't shake as she turned up the hill toward her rented house on Creek Road, her breath smoking in the cold. Not one person, not a secret cabal—the entire town, an entire agreement, fifty years deep, held together by silence the way a wall is held together by mortar. They had all decided, together, without ever saying so, that some questions cost more than the answers were worth.

Behind her, high up on the third floor, a single light went out.

Then another.

Then, floor by floor, window by window, the school began putting itself to bed—except for the ground floor, which stayed lit, every bulb burning, all night long.

And in the dark between one extinguished window and the next, Coral could have sworn she saw movement in the clock tower glass. Small shapes. Twenty-some of them, maybe more, pressed close to the pane like children at a shop window at Christmas.

Watching her walk away.

By morning she would tell herself it was pigeons. Tonight, climbing the cold hill with her collar up, she let herself believe what she'd seen—and what she'd seen was twenty-eight small hands, waving goodbye.
