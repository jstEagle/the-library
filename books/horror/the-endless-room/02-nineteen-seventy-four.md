## 2. Nineteen Seventy-Four

The archive room of Marrow Creek School lived in the basement, behind a door that had been painted so many times it no longer closed properly. Coral found the key taped to the top of the frame on her third day, exactly where the note in her welcome folder said it would be—*for records inquiries, see below stairs*—and she stood for a moment in the corridor with the key cold against her palm, listening to the building breathe around her.

It was 4:40 in the afternoon. She knew because Amos had told her that at 4:45 he would begin his rounds, and that if she intended to stay past dark she should let him know, *so I don't count you*, he'd said, with a small dry smile that hadn't reached his eyes.

She had laughed when he said it. He had not.

* * *

The basement smelled of paper and damp and something faintly sweet underneath, like apples going soft in a cellar. Fluorescent tubes hummed overhead, and Coral counted them without meaning to—four, all burning—which was becoming a habit she didn't like. The room itself was long and narrow, lined with gray metal filing cabinets and cardboard boxes stacked in uneven towers, each labeled in fading marker: *ATTENDANCE 1961–68*, *ATTENDANCE 1969–73*, *ATTENDANCE 1974–79*, and then nothing after that, as though the school had simply stopped keeping track of itself.

She pulled a folding table into the middle of the room, set her bag down, and began.

She told herself it was curiosity. That was the word she used, anyway—the respectable word, the teacher's word. But the truth was uglier and simpler: she had been unable to stop thinking about Room 12 since Wren Halloway had whispered about it through the gap in her teeth, leaning across two desks as though the walls were listening. *Nobody has students in there. Nobody teaches in there. But it's always warm.* And the way the other children had gone quiet—not frightened, exactly, but careful, the way you go quiet near a sleeping dog whose temperament you don't know.

Coral did not believe in haunted rooms. She believed in neglected children, underfunded districts, and the particular cruelty of small towns that kept their grief locked in basements. Twenty years of teaching had taught her that every strange rule had a boring explanation, and every silence had someone's shame inside it.

So she started with attendance records, because shame always leaves paperwork.

* * *

The registers from the early seventies were written in a looping, patient hand—Mrs. Edda Voss, it turned out, who had taught fourth grade for thirty-one years and dotted her i's like little crosses. Coral read the October pages slowly, letting the names become children in her mind. Marcus Pell. Odette Crane. Tobias Finch. The Halloway girl—Sylvie, not Wren; Wren wouldn't be born for another decade. Good attenders, most of them. A smattering of absences, chicken pox going around in September, a funeral noted here and there in Mrs. Voss's tidy marginalia.

Then she reached October 14th, 1974, and the handwriting changed.

Not dramatically. That was what unsettled her most, later—the change was almost nothing. The letters were still neat, still patient. But they had gone slightly smaller, slightly tighter, the way writing looks when the hand holding the pen is very tired or very afraid. Twenty-eight names. All present. Mrs. Voss had recorded the morning's fire drill in the margin: *10:15 — drill conducted, all exits practiced.*

And then, on October 15th, twenty-eight entries in a column, each one identical:

*Transferred.*

No destination schools. No receiving districts. No forwarding addresses. In fifty years of teaching, Coral had processed dozens of transfers, and every one of them generated a trail—a request form, a records release, a phone call, a new register waiting somewhere else with the child's name already inked in. Transfers left footprints.

These left none. Twenty-eight children, erased in a single day, in a single word, in handwriting that trembled only if you held the page up to the light and looked very hard.

She checked the next week. The next month. She checked the other fourth-grade class, the third grade, the fifth. Nothing. It was as if Room 12 had been full on a Monday and had never been full again. When she looked up the staffing records, Mrs. Voss herself appeared on the payroll through June of 1975 and then vanished too—no resignation letter, no pension withdrawal, just an absence shaped exactly like a person.

Coral sat back in the folding chair and became aware, gradually, of the sound of the building above her: pipes ticking, a door closing somewhere, footsteps crossing the hall overhead in a rhythm she almost recognized. Amos, doing his rounds. Counting.

"Transferred," she said aloud, to the empty basement, and the word came out sounding like what it was—an accusation no one had ever bothered to make.

* * *

The photograph was in the last box she opened, wedged between a PTA budget from 1973 and a mold-eaten scrapbook of newspaper clippings. It was a class photo, the kind taken every fall on the steps by the flagpole: two rows standing, one row seated, twenty-eight children squinting into an October sun that gilded the brick behind them.

They looked like any class photo from any year. Gap-toothed grins. A boy in the back row making rabbit ears over a friend's head. A girl in the front row holding the class sign—*ROOM 12, MRS. VOSS*—with both hands, gripping it the way children grip things they're proud of.

Coral counted them twice. Twenty-eight. She matched faces to names from the register, and it was strangely easy, because Mrs. Voss had written their names on the back in that same patient hand, each one numbered, each one accounted for.

Marcus Pell, rabbit ears. Odette Crane, front row, second from left, a missing tooth and a smile like a struck match. Sylvie Halloway, seated beside Odette, hands folded in her lap, looking directly into the camera with an expression Coral could only describe as *waiting*.

They had been ten years old. If any of them were alive, they'd be in their sixties now. Grandparents, maybe. Retirees. And instead they existed only here, in a basement, in a box, reduced to a single bureaucratic word that meant *we have decided to stop asking questions.*

Coral realized her eyes were stinging and pressed the heel of her hand against them, annoyed at herself. This was the part nobody warned you about, in all the training on compassion fatigue—that the worst of it wouldn't come from the children in front of you, loud and living and demanding, but from the ones history had quietly decided to stop mentioning.

That was when the lights went out.

All four tubes, at once, with a sound like a held breath being released. The darkness was total—the archive had no windows—and Coral heard her own heartbeat, heard the paper in her hands tremble, heard, distantly, from the floor above, a door open and close.

Then, before she could even find her voice, the lights came back on. All four. Burning steadily, as though nothing had happened.

Except that the photograph was no longer in her hands. It lay flat on the table in front of her, face-up, squared neatly with the table's edge, which she had absolutely not done.

Coral stood very still for a long moment. Then she picked up the photo, put it carefully back in its box, closed the box, and walked up the stairs without running, which took more discipline than anything she'd done in years.

* * *

Amos was at the end of the main hallway with his ring of keys and a clipboard, and he watched her come toward him with the expression of a man who had been expecting this conversation for a long time.

"The lights went out," Coral said. "In the archive."

"Mm." He made a mark on the clipboard. "Old wiring. Happens."

"It happened right after I found the 1974 attendance records."

Amos looked at her then, fully, and she saw that his eyes were the color of wet slate and very, very tired. "Come sit down, Miss Sterling."

"I'd rather stand."

"All right." He leaned the clipboard against the wall, and for the first time she noticed how old he was—not elderly, exactly, but worn, the way stone steps wear, hollowed in the middle by decades of the same feet. "How much did you read?"

"Twenty-eight transfer entries. Same day. No receiving schools. No follow-up." She held his gaze. "And a class photo where every single child is smiling, and every single one of them is lying about something, because children know things, Mr. Amos, even when adults pretend they don't. Look at Sylvie Halloway's face. She isn't smiling. She's *listening*."

Something moved behind his eyes—grief, or its fossil. "You've been here four days."

"Long enough to notice you count the doors every night. Long enough to notice the janitor's closet has more flashlights than a hardware store. Long enough to notice that nobody, in four days, has once said the words 'Room 12' out loud where I could hear them—except a nine-year-old, in a whisper." She crossed her arms. "I'm not leaving, Amos. So you can either tell me, or I can spend the next thirty years finding out badly."

He was quiet for a while. Down the hall, a classroom light flickered—just once—and both of them looked at it, and both of them pretended they hadn't.

"They weren't transferred," he said finally. His voice was low, pitched for her alone, though the hallway was empty. "There was a fire drill. October 14th, 1974, 10:15 in the morning. Routine. The class filed out like they were supposed to, except—" He stopped. Started again. "Except they went the wrong direction. All twenty-eight of them, and Mrs. Voss, calm as Sunday. Walked straight down the east wing and into Room 12, and the door shut behind them, and when the fire marshal opened it three minutes later the room was empty. Desks empty. Coats still on hooks. Twenty-eight pairs of shoes, some of them still warm."

"That's impossible."

"Yes," Amos agreed, without argument, and somehow that was worse than any explanation would have been. "The district called it transfers because the alternative was calling it nothing at all, and nothing doesn't fit on a form. Mrs. Voss lasted eight months. She used to sit outside Room 12 during her lunch hour, every day, just sitting, like she was waiting for class to let out. Then one morning her car was in the lot and she wasn't in the building, and that was the end of the record-keeping, more or less." He picked up his clipboard again, holding it like a shield. "I was sixteen that year. My mother cleaned this school. I've worked here forty-one years, Miss Sterling, and I am telling you as plainly as I know how: leave. Take the job in Cedar Falls. There's always a job in Cedar Falls."

"Why?" Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that. "Why does everyone keep the lights on? Why do you count doors? What is it you think is still *here*, Amos?"

He looked down the east wing, toward Room 12, and Coral followed his gaze. The corridor was empty. The lights burned steady and warm. And yet she felt it again, the thing she'd felt on her first day and told herself was imagination: the sensation of a held breath, of a room somewhere keeping very, very quiet, the way children do when they're playing a game and don't want to be found.

"Because," Amos said, "the ones that are still in there get lonely. And lonely things find ways to ask people to stay." He turned back to her, and his voice gentled. "Go home, Miss Sterling. Sleep. And whatever you do tonight—"

He paused. Whatever he was going to say next, she watched him decide against it, swallowing it whole, the way you swallow a word that might summon something.

"—go home," he finished. "That's all."

Coral walked out into the blue October dusk with the photograph's faces printed on the inside of her eyelids. Twenty-eight smiles. One girl, hands folded, listening for something no one else could hear.

At home, she made tea she didn't drink and stood a long time in front of the bathroom mirror, telling herself the unease in her chest was just grief for strangers, just the ordinary weight of an old injustice uncovered.

The mirror agreed with her.

A half-second too late.
