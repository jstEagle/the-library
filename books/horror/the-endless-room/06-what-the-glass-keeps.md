## 6. What the Glass Keeps

Wren had learned to count things the way other children learned to breathe.

Doors, mostly. Lights. The seconds between the bell's last ring and the hallway going quiet. Counting was what Amos did every evening with his ring of keys and his low voice, and Wren had decided years ago that if counting kept the school standing, she would count too. She counted the tiles between her classroom and the girls' restroom—forty-one on even days, forty-one on odd days, always forty-one—and she counted the stalls, six, and she counted her own breaths when the lights buzzed too loud.

She did not count the mirrors. There was only one in the girls' restroom, bolted above the sinks, and she had spent two years perfecting the art of not looking at it.

It was easier before Miss Sterling came.

* * *

The thing about grief, Wren thought, was that it didn't live where people said it lived. People said grief lived in your chest, a stone behind the ribs. But Wren's grief lived in her hands. It lived in the muscle memory of reaching for someone who wasn't there anymore—a hand at shoulder height, at exactly the height of a girl three years older, a girl who used to yank Wren's braid when she was being annoying and hold it like a rope-line when they crossed the street.

Wren's hands still reached sometimes. In crowds. In sleep. In the space beside her bed where a second pillow had once been stolen for fort-building.

Her sister's name was Ivy. Everyone at Marrow Creek knew the name the way you knew the name of a storm—the class of '74, twenty-eight children transferred all on one October day, as if the school district had simply misplaced an entire room of people and filed the paperwork anyway. Wren had grown up inside that absence. She'd grown up in a house where her mother set four places at the table until Wren was nine and finally asked why, and her mother had looked at the fourth plate like it had grown there overnight, like moss, like something she couldn't stop doing because stopping would mean admitting it was finished.

Ivy would be fifty-three now, if she were anywhere at all. But in the photograph—the one Miss Sterling had found, the one spread across the library table with its edges soft as old cloth—Ivy was nine forever, gap-toothed, squinting into a sun that had gone down half a century ago.

Wren touched that photograph more than she'd ever admit. And maybe that was why the mirror started waiting for her.

* * *

It happened on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Terrible things should happen on Fridays, or during storms, or at least after dark. Not on a gray Tuesday in October with the radiators clanking and someone's gum stuck under the third stall's door.

Wren came into the restroom alone, fourth period, having begged a hall pass from Mrs. Okafor with the specific, practiced limp of a girl who just needed five minutes away from algebra. The fluorescent light over the sinks did its stuttering thing—on, off, on—and Wren counted the flickers out of habit. Three. Always three.

She washed her hands because it gave her something to do with them.

And then, because she was tired, because she was fourteen and grieving a sister who was older than her mother now, because grief makes everyone a willing door—she looked up.

The mirror lagged.

She had known this, in the abstract way you know the school has a haunted room. Miss Sterling had told them—her and Amos and Nurse Delphine, huddled over burnt grilled cheeses in the teachers' lounge on Sundays—about the lag. One second late, then two, then three. Mirrors that remembered you wrong. Wren had nodded along solemnly and privately thought: *not mine.* Her mirror was above the sinks in the girls' restroom and it had never done anything but show her a thin girl with dark circles and a braid coming undone.

But today the mirror showed her raising her hands to splash water on her face a full heartbeat after she'd already done it. Two heartbeats. The girl in the glass moved like a film run through syrup, and Wren stood with wet hands dripping onto the tile and watched herself finish an action she had already completed.

"Okay," she whispered. "Okay."

Her voice hit the mirror and the reflection's mouth moved a beat later, forming the word back at her like an echo that had gotten lost on the way home.

She should have left. Miss Sterling had been explicit: *if a mirror lags, you walk away. You don't test it. You don't talk to it. You come find me.* Wren had promised. She'd even crossed her heart, which she hadn't done since she was eight, because Miss Sterling had that way of making promises feel like they mattered, like they were load-bearing.

But the reflection didn't walk away. The reflection stayed, and tilted its head, and—

And it wasn't quite her.

That was the thing Wren could never have explained to anyone who hadn't seen it. The face in the glass was her face, her crooked incisor, the freckle by her ear shaped like a comma. But the expression was wrong. Older. Patient. The look of someone who had been waiting a long time and was very good at it.

"Ivy?" Wren said.

The name came out small. Names are small things; they're just sounds we agree to answer to. But this name had weight—it had fifty years of unset places at tables behind it—and when it left Wren's mouth the lights stopped flickering. All three stutter-stops simply ceased, and the fluorescents burned steady and white, and the whole restroom went still as held breath.

The reflection smiled with Wren's mouth.

Then the glass changed.

Not shattered, not rippled—*changed*, the way a page changes when ink bleeds through from the other side. Wren's own face thinned like smoke and beneath it, surfacing slow as something rising through pond water, was another face. A woman's face. Brown hair gone silver at the temples, laugh lines carved deep around eyes that were still, unmistakably, the eyes from the photograph. Gap-toothed Ivy, aged the way the room had refused to age her. Fifty-three years old. Alive-looking. *Looking* alive, some cold voice in Wren corrected, but the correction came faint and far away, because her sister was right there, right there, close enough that if the glass weren't between them Wren could have reached out and touched the silver streaks and asked if they hurt when they came in.

Ivy's mouth began to move.

No sound came through. The glass kept everything but the shape of the words, and Wren watched her sister's lips form them with the careful exaggeration of someone teaching a child to read: round shapes, open shapes, a rhythm underneath them that Wren's body recognized before her mind caught up.

*Skip, skip, over the creek.*

The skipping rhyme. The one that had hummed itself out of Wren in class last month and made every light in the wing shudder until Amos slammed the piano lid hard enough to crack his knuckles. The song. The endless room's song, sung in rounds by whoever was inside, holding the doors of it open like fingers prying apart a seam.

*Ivy, don't,* Wren tried to say, and heard herself instead begin to hum.

That was the horror of it, the part she would carry afterward like a swallowed stone: it wasn't a decision. Her lips parted and the melody came up out of her the way a sob comes up, the way her mother's four plates came up, automatic and ancient. The first line rose in her throat—*skip, skip, over the creek*—and somewhere below the floor, below the foundation, below whatever thin membrane separated Marrow Creek School from the place it had been bleeding into since 1974, something turned toward her like a sunflower finding the sun.

The mirror brightened. Not the light—the *glass*, the surface itself gaining a luster like oil on water, and through it the restroom fell away at the edges, tile going soft, stall doors blurring, and the space behind Ivy deepening into a corridor Wren could almost see, warm-lit, golden, full of doors.

Full of October. She could smell it through the glass—leaf-smoke and pencil shavings and the milk-and-eraser smell of 1974, her sister's actual childhood rising off the surface like heat off summer asphalt.

*Come sing the next line,* said Ivy's moving mouth. No sound. All the sound in the world was gathering in Wren's own throat instead, the second line loading itself onto her tongue like a round chambered into a gun. *Skip, skip, don't look back—*

"Wren!"

Hands on her shoulders—strong, certain, real—and Coral Sterling spun her away from the sink so hard her sneaker squealed on the tile. The melody tore in half in Wren's throat, dying in a cracked, ugly note, and the lights dropped dead all at once, every bulb in the restroom, plunging them into a dimness broken only by the gray window light.

"No," Coral was saying—not to Wren, to the mirror, fierce and low, the way you'd speak to an animal. "No. She's not yours. *None of them are yours.*"

In the darkened glass, Ivy's face lingered a moment longer. Older. Patient. And then—this was the worst part, the part Wren would not tell anyone for a long time—Ivy's expression shifted into Wren's own face again, Wren's exact face, wearing an expression of such tender disappointment that Wren's knees nearly went. *You wanted this,* the face said without words. *You've always wanted this.*

Then the glass was only glass, showing a dim restroom and two girls breathing hard, perfectly synchronized, one second late or not at all.

* * *

Coral didn't let go of her. She steered her out of the restroom, down the forty-one tiles, into the empty music room, and sat her down on the piano bench with the lid locked and something heavy—Amos's toolbox, Wren realized dully—dragged against the door.

"She looked fifty-three," Wren said. Her voice sounded borrowed. "She looked *old*. She looked like Mom." A laugh came out of her, jagged and wrong. "She looked like what she'd be if—"

"If the room let her go," Coral said quietly. She crouched so their eyes were level. She had that way of crouching, Wren had noticed, like she never wanted to loom over anyone, like she'd learned somewhere that height was a weapon. "Wren. Listen to me. What you saw wasn't Ivy. It was the room wearing Ivy, the same way it wears your face in the glass. It learns what we're starving for and it serves it up hot."

"You don't know that." It came out crueler than Wren meant. "You've lost things, fine. But nobody in your family walked into a room in 1974. You get to be rational about it."

For a moment Coral said nothing, and in the silence her face did something complicated—grief moving under it like a fish under ice. "You're right," she said finally. "I don't know what it's like to lose a sister. But I know what it's like to want a version of your life back so badly you'd walk through glass to get it. And that wanting? That's exactly what it farms. Grief makes everyone a willing door, Wren. I've felt it working on me. I *am* one."

Wren stared at her hands. They were shaking. They had been reaching, she realized—her hands had been reaching for the glass, palms forward, fingers spread, the exact posture of someone pressing against a window on a train platform watching someone leave.

"It knew the rhyme," she whispered. "It started mouthing it and my mouth just—joined in. Like it was already in me. Like it's been in me my whole life, waiting."

"Because it has been." Coral's voice dropped. "Amos thinks the song doesn't stay in the room. He thinks it seeds itself. Into the town. Into families." She hesitated, and Wren saw her choose to say the next part. "Into sisters. Wren—if Ivy's been singing in there for fifty years, keeping the room open in rounds, then you're not just hearing the song. You might be the other half of the round. The part outside."

The radiator clanked. Somewhere down the hall a bell rang for fifth period, absurd and ordinary, and neither of them moved.

"The mirrors," Wren said slowly. "You said they lag worse the closer you get to hearing too much. Mine never lagged before this month. Before you came. Before we started—" She stopped. Started again. "Miss Sterling. My bathroom mirror at home lagged three seconds yesterday. I timed it against the clock on the stove."

Something shifted in Coral's face—not fear exactly, though fear was in it, but recognition, the look of someone watching a pattern complete itself.

"So did mine," Coral said. "Three seconds. Exactly."

They looked at each other across the locked piano, and Wren understood, with the terrible clarity the glass had given her, that the room wasn't choosing one of them anymore.

It was weaving them together.
