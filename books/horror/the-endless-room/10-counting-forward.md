## 10. Counting Forward

Coral woke on the floor of the east hallway with her cheek pressed against cold linoleum and the taste of silence in her mouth.

Not the ordinary silence of an empty school at night—she had learned that one well in her months at Marrow Creek, the hush of sleeping radiators and settling wood. This was a deeper quiet, a finished quiet, like the moment after a held breath is finally let go. The hallway lights burned steady above her. No flicker. No stutter. Just light, plain and constant, doing nothing more than its job.

She sat up slowly. Her body ached everywhere, a deep bruised ache, as though she had been carried a long way and set down gently. Her palms were gray with dust. When she lifted them she saw, faintly, on both wrists, marks like the ghost of handprints—small ones, child-sized, where twenty-eight hands had gripped her and pulled.

The children were there.

They lay scattered down the corridor the way children sleep anywhere they drop—curled against lockers, tangled two and three together, one boy asleep with his shoes still on and his head pillowed on a teacher's edition of *Our Town* that must have been fifty years old. They were breathing. That was the thing Coral kept coming back to, checking again and again, the way you touch a sore tooth: they were breathing. Their chests rose and fell. A girl near the window murmured something in her sleep and turned over, and the sound of it—a small, living, unremarkable sound—made Coral's eyes flood so suddenly she had to press her wrist to her mouth to keep from waking them with weeping.

Twenty-eight. She counted them twice, touching each name in her mind like beads on a string. Ruthie Calloway. The Okafor twins. Marcus, who had taught her the fourth verse. Little Ada Finch, who had held her hand in the tearing dark and never once asked if they were going to make it out, only whether Coral would stay with her until they knew.

They were here. All of them. Unaged, unchanged, wearing the clothes they'd walked into Room 12 wearing on the morning of October 14th, 1974—and alive, impossibly, stubbornly alive, breathing the dust-and-chalk air of a school that had spent half a century pretending not to grieve them.

Coral got to her feet, steadying herself against the wall, and looked at the long row of windows along the east side. In every pane, her reflection moved when she moved. Not before. Not after. With her, exactly, faithfully, a second late no longer. She raised her hand; the woman in the glass raised hers at the same instant, and there was nothing in her face but her own tired, tear-streaked, ordinary face.

"Good," said a voice behind her, rough as gravel and shaking worse than gravel ever could.

Amos stood at the end of the hall in his coveralls, holding a flashlight he didn't need, his eyes wet in the lamplight. Delphine was beside him, still in her nurse's smock, one hand pressed flat against her sternum as though holding her own heart in place. And between them, small and fierce and awake, stood Wren.

Wren's face was blotchy. Her fists were clenched. She was staring down the corridor at the sleeping children with an expression Coral had no words for—hope and terror braided so tight they were indistinguishable—and then she saw the girl curled nearest the door, the one with dark hair and a gap between her front teeth that showed even in sleep, and Wren made a sound that wasn't quite a word and wasn't quite a scream and ran.

Coral stepped back to let her pass. Wren dropped to her knees beside her sister and gathered her up, and Ruthie Calloway—Ruthie, who had been eleven years old for fifty years—stirred, blinked, and said, muzzily, "Wren? You got *old*."

"I know," Wren sobbed into her sister's hair. "I know. I'm sorry. I'm sorry I couldn't—I'm sorry it took so long."

"You came back," Ruthie said, with the simple ferocity of a child stating fact. "You always said you would." She yawned. "Did you bring dinner?"

And that broke the last of it. Delphine went to the children then, all of them, moving down the line with her practiced hands, pressing fingers to wrists, lifting eyelids, murmuring names. Amos lowered himself onto the floor beside a sleeping boy and just stayed there, one huge palm resting on the child's shoulder blade, feeling him breathe, saying nothing at all. Coral watched her found family do what found families do—which is hold the unbearable thing together by standing close enough that it cannot come apart alone.

Outside the windows, dawn was coming up gray-gold over Marrow Creek. It looked, Coral thought, exactly like any other dawn. That was the miracle of it. The world hadn't changed. Only the school had stopped holding its breath.

* * *

Room 12 was cold.

Delphine opened the door at seven o'clock, before the buses came, before anyone else arrived, because they had agreed it needed doing together. The four of them stood in the doorway while the morning light fell through the windows and across the desks—twenty-eight desks, arranged in neat rows, each one still bearing the ghost-marks of small elbows and the faded tape outlines of names.

For fifty years, Amos told them later, in the voice of a man confessing a sin he'd committed simply by enduring, Room 12 had been warm. Warm in winter, warm in summer, warm on the coldest February mornings when the boiler failed and frost ferned every other window in the building. He had never told anyone. He had cleaned around it, counted doors past it, left its lights burning like everyone else, and told himself the warmth was pipes, was wiring, was anything but what it was: the room breathing. The room waiting. The room keeping its place setting laid for the next class that might wander in singing.

Now Coral put her bare hand flat against the chalkboard and felt nothing but cold slate. She touched the desk nearest the door—cold wood. She stood in the center of the room, in the exact spot where the mirror had hung, where the corridor of doors had opened, where she had held her silence while the room tore itself apart trying to find a verse she would sing—

—and felt only the honest chill of an empty classroom in October.

"It's done," Amos said. His voice cracked on the second word. He cleared his throat and tried again. "It's done."

There was no mirror in Room 12 anymore. There was no mark on the wall where it had hung, no brighter rectangle of paint, no outline of anything. As if the room had never wanted one. As if the glass had only ever been a door propped open from the other side, and now that the song had ended—the round broken, the trap completed by its own refusal—the wall was simply a wall.

Coral thought about the last verse. About how it had never been written down, because writing it down would have been singing it, and singing it would have sealed the room forever with everyone inside. About how the true ending, the one the children had whispered to her in the dark as the walls began to fold, was not a verse at all. It was the absence of one. The rest between notes. The breath you take and do not give back to the song.

Silence, held like a hand held out.

She had held it. They had all walked out through her held silence, twenty-eight children in single file, gripping the hem of her cardigan like a rope thrown into deep water.

"We should tell someone," Delphine said quietly. "The state. The families. Someone has to—" She stopped, looking at the desks. "Fifty years of paperwork can't be harder than what we just did."

"No," Coral agreed. "But not today. Today they eat breakfast."

* * *

They ate breakfast in the cafeteria, all thirty-three of them, and it was chaos, and it was wonderful.

The kitchen yielded oatmeal, canned peaches, a truly heroic quantity of toast. The 1974 children ate like small wolves, delighted and suspicious by turns, asking questions that broke the adults' hearts in new and inventive ways. What year was it. Was there still such a thing as television. Why did the milk cartons look funny. A boy named Elias asked Amos if he was the janitor, and when Amos said yes, Elias nodded gravely and said, "You used to count the doors. We could hear you. It helped. It sounded like somebody knew we were in there."

Amos had to leave the table for a while after that.

Later—much later, after the state people came with their clipboards and their gentle voices, after the first stunned phone calls went out to families who had grown old waiting, after the story broke outward from Marrow Creek like a stone dropped in still water—there would be doctors, and questions, and a world that would want explanations none of them could give. But that first day belonged to no one but the people in the cafeteria, eating burnt toast under steady lights.

When evening came, the strangest and most natural thing happened. Amos stood up, walked to the end of the hall, and began to count the doors.

"One," he said. "Two. Three."

He did it the way he had done it every evening for fifty years—but slower now, and louder, and the others followed him. Wren took Ruthie's hand and walked the corridor counting aloud. Delphine counted. Coral counted. The children counted, racing ahead and doubling back, their voices overlapping in the stairwells, and it stopped being a ritual of fear somewhere around door nine and became something else entirely: a roll call. A census. A way of marking, door by door, everything that had come home.

"—twenty-six," called a small voice from the top of the stairs. "Twenty-seven! Twenty-eight!"

"Twenty-nine," Amos finished, at the front doors, and smiled for what Coral suspected was the first time in decades. "That's us. All accounted for."

* * *

One mirror remained.

It hung in the girls' restroom off the west wing—the one where Wren had nearly sung herself through the glass, where her sister's older face had pressed close and mouthed the opening lines. The frame was old, the silvering gone spotty at the edges like frost that never melted.

Delphine wanted it broken. Amos stood ready with a hammer. But Coral shook her head.

"No," she said. "We don't break things anymore. We cover them."

So they draped it—together, all five of them, the sheet passing from hand to hand like something being shared rather than hidden—and tied it at the corners with twine. Underneath, the glass waited, whole and patient and harmless now, just glass, just a surface, just a thing that showed you your own face when you stood in front of it. But they covered it anyway. Not out of fear. Out of memory. Some things deserved to be remembered by being set aside.

On the cloth, in Wren's careful handwriting, they wrote the date. October 15th. The day after.

Then they turned off the lights—all of them, every bulb in the west wing, for the first time in fifty years—and locked the school, and walked out into the cold clean dark together, twenty-eight children chattering about television, and four adults walking among them like people learning to breathe again.

Behind them, in the covered dark of the girls' restroom, the mirror held its silence.

And if, some nights, the cloth stirred without wind—if, once or twice, a shape seemed to press against the fabric from the inside, patient, polite, waiting to be uncovered by whoever came next—well. Marrow Creek had learned to count its doors forward instead of backward.

But it kept the twine tied.
