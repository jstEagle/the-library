## 1. The House That Measures Back

The Halloway house sat at the end of Corben Lane like a sentence someone had started and abandoned mid-clause. I'd appraised four hundred properties in my eleven years with Meridian Land & Survey—flood-damaged duplexes, a cannery that smelled like regret, one mobile home that turned out to be legally two mobile homes welded together—and I want to be clear that I arrived that Tuesday morning as a professional. Clipboard. Laser rangefinder calibrated the week before. Two backup batteries, because I am the kind of woman who has backup batteries.

I did not arrive believing in houses.

The county wanted it gone. The Halloway parcel had been tied up in probate since 1974, a knot of dead heirs and misfiled deeds, and now a developer named Grubb wanted the lot for a storage facility, which is what land becomes when nobody loves it anymore. My job was simple: measure every room, document condition, certify the structure's boundaries so the demolition permit could clear. Forty pages of paperwork standing between an old house and an excavator.

I stood in the gravel drive and looked up at it. Three stories, Victorian in the way that means "built by someone showing off," with a widow's walk like a raised eyebrow. The paint had gone the gray of old dishwater. Every window on the second floor was dark except one, which held a light the color of weak tea.

Nobody had lived here since 1974.

I wrote that down too. *East window, second floor, illuminated. Source unknown.* Professional habit. Also, apparently, a death wish, though I didn't know that yet.

* * *

Inside smelled the way all empty houses smell—dust, mouse, the ghost of heating oil—but underneath it there was something sweeter, like fruit going bad in a drawer somewhere. I started in the parlor, because you always start in the parlor, and I measured it the way I measure everything: corner to corner, wall to wall, ceiling height at three points, doorways logged with widths.

The parlor was eighteen feet by fourteen.

I know this because my Leica Disto said so, twice, in its cheerful little beep. Then I walked the room again to check the baseboards against my numbers—the kind of redundancy that gets me teased at office parties—and the laser said twenty-two feet by nineteen.

I stood in the middle of the parlor holding a $400 instrument that was telling me the room had grown six feet while I stood in it.

Houses settle. Houses shift. Foundations heave, joists warp, and a good surveyor knows that no measurement is ever perfectly repeatable. But measurements drift by inches. They do not drift by feet. And they certainly do not drift *while you watch.*

I re-measured a third time. Sixteen by twelve. The room had not only changed, it had changed back, and overshot in the other direction, like it was nervous.

"Okay," I told the parlor. Talking to houses is not professional. But there was nobody there to hear me be unprofessional, which is the whole point of being alone at a job site.

The hearth was bricked over. Above it hung a portrait, water-stained past recognition except for the mouth, which some accident of damp had preserved in perfect detail: a wide, even smile, the kind portraitists give to people who paid in advance. The eyes above it had been eaten away to blank ovals.

I photographed the room. Flash, then ambient, standard documentation protocol. On the little screen, the parlor looked fine. Eighteen by fourteen, or close enough. Except behind me in the photograph—where I had been standing, where the doorway to the hall should have been—there was a staircase. A narrow wooden staircase going up into darkness, banister worn pale by hands.

There is no staircase in the Halloway house. I checked the original 1898 plat that afternoon, and I checked the hallway, and I checked both again, and again, until checking stopped being methodology and became prayer.

* * *

By two o'clock I had documented eleven rooms and disbelieved forty-one measurements. The kitchen was either nine feet wide or thirty, depending on whether the house liked me that minute. A bedroom on the second floor contained a closet whose interior depth exceeded the exterior wall of the building by four feet, which is geometrically impossible unless the closet opens into some other dimension, or some other *house*. When I measured the closet a second time, the number came back correct, apologetic, normal—and I felt a flash of relief so intense it frightened me more than the wrongness had.

That's the thing I want to be honest about, since honesty turns out to matter here: the house wasn't just lying to my instruments. It was lying to *me*, specifically, personally, with intent. It let me get comfortable and then moved the walls. It showed me a child's chalk drawings on the attic plaster—hopscotch, a sun with too many rays—that were not there when I photographed the attic from the landing, and were gone when I climbed up to touch them, leaving only clean gray plaster and my own reflection in the dust, smiling.

I wasn't smiling. I want that on the record too.

Dale Okafor, the excavation foreman, called at four. His crew was staging equipment at the lot line for a Thursday start, and he wanted my boundary certification before they brought the excavator in.

"House behaving?" he asked. He had the easy voice of a man who has demolished many things and been haunted by none of them.

"It's got some irregular interior dimensions. Old construction."

"Old construction." I could hear him grinning. "You surveyors and your old construction. Hey, weird question—you see anybody up there? Kids, maybe? Marco swears somebody waved at him from the east window when we drove past this morning."

The east window. The one lit like weak tea.

"Nobody's lived there since '74," I said.

"Yeah, that's what the file says." A pause, the sound of a man deciding not to think about something. "Anyway. Thursday. Bring me paper I can hand the county."

I hung up and looked at the east window from inside. From here it was just glass and gray afternoon, no light, no wave, no child. But the glass was warm under my palm. All the windows were cold. Only that one held warmth, faint and steady, like something breathing against the other side of it—or like a lamp left burning for someone expected home.

* * *

I finished the ground floor at dusk, because I am thorough, and thoroughness is a virtue right up until it isn't. In the front hall I laid out my final set of measurements on the clipboard and added them up, the way I always do, a closing ritual: total finished square footage, cross-checked against the exterior footprint minus wall thicknesses.

They didn't match. Not by inches. The interior of the Halloway house contained nine hundred square feet more than its own exterior could hold. Nine hundred square feet of extra house, folded in somewhere, hidden between the rooms like a word left out of a contract.

And here is where I'll admit the affront, because it curdled in me all the drive home and never quite soured into fear until later, when fear would have been the sensible option. I was insulted. Eleven years of exactitude. Calibration certificates. Backup batteries. Some rotting Victorian con job on Corben Lane had looked at everything I am—every careful, verifiable, sworn-to-be-true thing—and decided to lie to my face. To my *instruments'* faces. Instruments don't have faces, but if mine had grown one that day, the house would have lied to it too.

At the gate I turned back for one last look, because surveyors photograph facades for the record. I raised my phone. Framed the house. Took the shot.

On the screen, every window of the Halloway house was lit. Warm tea-colored light in all of them, top to bottom, and standing in each window—a figure. Different figures. Men and women in clothing out of different decades, high collars, polyester, a hard hat. None of them moving. All of them facing outward.

All of them smiling. The same wide, even, paid-in-advance smile from the portrait over the parlor hearth, repeated across every face like a watermark.

I lowered the phone. The house stood dark and dead against the last of the daylight, exactly as a house should.

When I got to my car, I found I'd written something on the clipboard without meaning to, in my own handwriting, in the margin of the measurement log where anomalies go:

*It's not measuring the rooms. It's measuring me.*

Thursday was two days away. I had forty pages of paperwork to finish, a boundary certification to sign, and a demolition permit with my firm's letterhead sitting in a tray at the county office, waiting on my numbers.

Somewhere inside that house, nine hundred square feet of something was waiting too.
