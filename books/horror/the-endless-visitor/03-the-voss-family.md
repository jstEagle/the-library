## 3. The Voss Family

I want to be clear about my policy, because it matters later: I don't do dinner. I don't do overnight. I quote a price, I do the work, I leave an invoice and a card with the number of a colleague who handles "unusual situations," which is pest-control shorthand for "please stop calling me." Twelve years in this trade teaches you that the clients who want you to stay are the ones you should run from. The house is never the problem. The house is just where the problem keeps its things.

So when Edda Voss said, "You'll stay for supper at least," I should have said no.

What I actually said was: "Depends what you're serving."

It had been a long day. The drive out to Hollowmere was forty minutes of gravel and hedgerow, and by the time I pulled into the yard the light had gone the color of weak tea. The farmhouse sat low and stubborn against the wind, stone underneath, whitewash peeling off it like old sunburn. A barn leaned slightly to one side, as if listening. And there — dead center of the yard, exactly where a well should not be built directly beside a kitchen door, but people build wells where they build them — stood the well. Capped. Soldered shut, per my last visit, with a bead of fresh metal still bright around the seam.

Edda Voss met me at the door wiping her hands on her apron like a woman in a painting about hardship. She was maybe fifty-five, built like the farm itself: low, sturdy, weathered. Her handshake was honest. Her eyes were not calm, but they were trying very hard to be.

"Miss Dunmore. Thank you for coming back."

"Your message said handprints." I stepped past her into the hall before she could decide whether to invite me. That's another policy: never let them set the geography. "Ceiling of the nursery, you said."

"Upstairs. Left." She hesitated. "I should warn you—"

"Save it. Show me."

* * *

The nursery ceiling told me three things, and I didn't like any of them.

First: the prints were real. Greasy, gray-brown smears in the shape of small hands, five fingers each, pressed into the plaster between the joists. Not dragged. Pressed. Someone had gone up there and put their palms flat against the ceiling, deliberately, in maybe a dozen places, like a child playing itsy-bitsy spider on the underside of the world.

Second: they were fresh. The grease hadn't oxidized. Whatever left them had done so within days of Edda's call.

Third — and this is the one I keep coming back to, the hairline crack in the glass of my professional detachment — they were too small. I've got a reference chart in my van, laminated, for distinguishing rat, squirrel, and human sign. I held my own hand up against the largest print. My hand is average for a woman. The print came up to my knuckles. A toddler's size, maybe smaller. And the spacing of the fingers was wrong — too long relative to the palm, like something that had been stretched, or something that had grown in water.

"Whose child made these?" I asked, because there's always an answer if you ask plainly enough.

"No child made these," Edda said from the doorway. She wasn't looking at the ceiling. She was looking at me looking at it, and I understood suddenly that she'd brought me up here less to show me the prints than to watch whether I'd flinch. "We have one child in this house. Wren. She's eleven. These are smaller than hers were at four."

"Attic access?"

"There isn't one. The ceiling's the underside of the roof space. No hatch. My grandfather sealed it when he re-roofed in '81."

"So how does someone put handprints inside a sealed cavity?"

"That," said Edda Voss, "is the question we've been asking for a year."

A year. I filed that away. On the phone she'd said weeks. People understate timelines when they're ashamed, and they're always ashamed.

I took photographs. I swabbed the grease — it smelled faintly of pond, of silt, of the green-black smell you get lifting a stone off a drainage ditch in August. Then I went downstairs, quoted her a price for a full interior survey plus exclusion work, and prepared to leave.

That's when the girl appeared at the bottom of the stairs.

She was small, dark-haired, wearing a man's flannel shirt buttoned wrong so one side hung longer than the other. She looked at me with total, unblinking attention, the way children look at animals they can't classify. Then she turned and walked into the front room without a word.

"She doesn't speak," Edda said quietly. "Not since last spring. Doctor calls it selective mutism after acute stress. We call it—" She stopped herself. "Supper's at seven. Hal will be in from the east field. Please. It's stew. There's plenty."

And here is the thing I will defend to no one: I was hungry, the light was going, and the nearest motel was back down forty minutes of gravel. Those were my reasons. They were good reasons. They had nothing whatsoever to do with the fact that the girl had looked at me like I was the first new thing in a very long time.

"I'll stay for supper," I said. "One hour."

* * *

The kitchen was warm and yellow-lit and smelled of lamb and rosemary, and it did its treacherous work on me immediately. Edda cooked like someone who'd been feeding frightened people for years — heavy, salted, unapologetic. Her brother Hal came in smelling of diesel and cold air, big through the shoulders, with a face that looked like it had been holding an apology for a long time and couldn't remember who it was for. He shook my hand too hard, apologized, shook it again more gently.

"Tamsin Dunmore," I said. "Pest control."

"He means it kindly," Edda translated. "Hal's not good at first impressions."

"I'm good at second ones," Hal said, and sat, and the meal began.

Wren ate across from me, silent, methodical, and between bites she drew. She had a sketchbook — a fat one, spine cracked white from use — and a pencil worn down to a stub, and while the adults talked she drew with the fluency of long practice. I watched her over the rim of my glass. Same face. Every page, near enough, the same face. Round-cheeked, eyes closed or nearly closed, mouth open a little, hair spread out and streaming upward from the head in long lines, as though the whole drawing were upside down, or underwater.

"You're good," I said to her.

She didn't answer. She turned the sketchbook toward me instead, and tapped the face with her stub of pencil, twice, like punctuation.

"That's Wren's way of talking," Hal said. His voice had gone careful. "She draws that one. Only that one, lately. Pages and pages of it."

"It's very consistent," I said, which was the wrong thing, professionally speaking, but I was off duty and eating stew. "Most kids vary their subjects."

"She started it before she stopped talking," Edda said. "Same week, near enough. Last May. She drew it once, then again, then it was all she drew. And then one morning she opened her mouth at breakfast and nothing came out, and she hasn't spoken since." Edda refilled my glass without asking. "You'll think we're mad."

"Madness isn't in my remit," I said. "Vermin is. Tell me why you called a pest controller about a voice."

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. In her first message, months ago now, buried under the rodent complaint, Edda had written: *there is something in the walls that talks.* I'd filed it under eccentric and quoted her for rats.

Edda set down her spoon. Hal looked at his plate. Wren kept drawing, pencil whispering, the drowned little face taking shape again beneath her hands.

"We haven't slept in separate rooms in a year," Edda said.

I waited. It was clearly a non sequitur only to me.

"When it started — the sounds, the talking — Hal moved into Wren's room first. Slept on the floor by her bed. Then I couldn't bear being alone in mine, so I brought a cot in. Now none of us sleeps alone. Nobody in this house has slept alone since last May." She said it steadily, but her hands had gone still on the table, and stillness, in a woman like Edda Voss, is louder than shaking. "You sleep alone every night, Miss Dunmore?"

"Every night," I said. "It's one of my better habits."

"And nothing has ever asked you anything in the dark?"

The fire ticked. Somewhere in the walls — and I noted this, professionally, wryly, telling myself it was pipe expansion — somewhere in the walls, something settled with a sound like a long exhale.

"What kind of questions?" I asked.

Edda looked at her brother. Hal looked at Wren. Wren, without looking up, turned her sketchbook around again and tapped the face twice.

"Simple ones, at first," Edda said. "When we were falling asleep. A voice, low, like someone talking through water. Asking things. *Are you awake. Are you the ones above. Is it cold up there.* We thought it was Wren sleep-talking, except Wren was asleep, properly asleep, and the voice was coming from the wall behind her headboard. From inside the wall." She laughed, short and awful. "My brother wanted to tear the plaster out. I wouldn't let him. Because lately—" She stopped.

"Lately?" I prompted, because I am, despite everything, a professional.

"Lately it stopped asking," Edda said, "and started answering."

The kitchen felt smaller. Hal pushed back his chair, stood, crossed to the window, and stood looking out at the dark yard where the capped well sat under the moon like a plugged tooth.

"Answering what?" I asked. "Nobody's asked it anything."

"Oh, but we have," Edda said softly. "For years. Before any of us knew there was anyone down there to hear. You say things, at night, in your own house. Everyone does. Things you'd never say in daylight. Griefs. Regrets. Names." Her jaw tightened. "It heard us. All those years, it heard us, and it was patient, and now it knows everything we've ever whispered into the dark of this house, and it has begun repeating it back to us. Kindly. Like it's helping."

Wren's pencil stopped.

The silence that followed had texture. I've been in empty houses all my working life and I know the difference between a house that's merely quiet and a house that's listening. This was the second kind. And every one of them — Edda with her folded hands, Hal with his back to us at the window, Wren with her motionless pencil — every one of them was oriented, without seeming to know it, toward the same corner of the room. The corner nearest the kitchen door. The door that led out to the yard. To the well.

"One hour," I reminded myself aloud, and checked my watch, and found that two had passed.

Then Wren did something she had not done all evening. She tore the top sheet from her sketchbook — carefully, along the perforation, like a receipt — and slid it across the table to me.

The face. Of course. But drawn differently this time: larger, worked over, the streaming hair rendered in dozens of fine lines, the closed eyes given lashes, the open mouth given teeth. And below it, in a child's careful capitals, a single line of pencil:

**SHE ASKED ME YOUR NAME TODAY**

I looked up. Wren was watching me with those unblinking eyes, and she pointed — not at the paper, not at herself, but past me, at the corner by the door, and then she laid her finger flat against her own lips.

Stay, said the gesture, as clearly as speech. Or possibly: don't tell it.

"Right," I said, standing, gathering my coat with the brisk cheer of a woman fleeing a burning building at a dignified pace. "It's late, the survey starts at eight, and honestly, Ms. Voss, I'll get more done at dawn. I'll be at the motel in Marleigh if—"

"The Marleigh Arms burned down in March," Hal said, without turning from the window. "Nothing else out that way till Crossford. Forty minutes. Roads flood near the ford when it rains."

It was, in fact, beginning to rain.

I stood in the warm yellow kitchen of Hollowmere Farm, holding a drawing from a mute child, being invited — by the weather, by the roads, by the family, by the corner of the room nobody would look at directly — to spend the night in a house where nobody slept alone.

Against every policy I have, I heard myself say: "Have you got a spare room?"

"We've got a cot in Wren's room," Edda said, and there was something in her face that was not quite relief and not quite triumph and was, I realized much later, hope — the terrible, expensive kind. "But you won't need it. Nobody in this house sleeps alone anymore. You'll take the sofa in the front room, and I'll bring mine down next to yours, and Hal will be upstairs with Wren, and we'll leave the doors open."

"That's insane," I said.

"Yes," Edda agreed placidly, clearing plates. "Kettle's on."

* * *

I did not sleep, which I want on the record. I lay on the sofa with my boots on and my torch in my hand and listened to the rain, and to the house ticking, and to Edda breathing two feet away on her cot with the steady discipline of a woman who has trained herself to sleep anywhere, beside anyone, rather than be alone.

At some hour past two, I heard it.

From upstairs, muffled through the floor, thin as thread: a voice. Not Hal's. Not Wren's. A small voice, wet-edged, patient, rising and falling in the pattern of conversation — question, pause, question — coming from the direction of Wren's room, where Hal had left the door open, where Hal lay on the floor beside his daughter's bed the way he had every night for a year.

And then, threading under it, almost too soft to catch, another voice answering. Hal's. Whispering. Steady. Hour after hour, it seemed, the two of them talking in the dark — the thing in the wall and the man who had decided that whatever it was, it would have to go through him to reach his daughter.

I lay in the dark of the front room with my torch off and understood, with the particular dread of my profession, that I had not been hired for rats at all. I had been hired the way you hire a doctor when you already know the diagnosis. Edda Voss had seen the handprints on her nursery ceiling and called the one person whose job description includes going toward the thing everyone else runs from.

The voice upstairs paused. In the silence I heard — or thought I heard, and I want that on the record too — the floorboards by the front-room door creak once, gently, like someone small shifting their weight to look at me.

I clicked the torch on. Nothing there. Rain on the windows. The well cap in the yard, soldered shut, doing its best.

The voice upstairs resumed, and this time I caught a fragment, one question, clear enough to chill the stew in my stomach:

*"Is the new one staying?"*

I didn't sleep. At dawn I made notes in my van, and the first line reads, in my own handwriting: **Client family unit intact, cooperative, terrified. Recommend immediate full assessment. Do not leave them alone with it. Do not leave it alone with them.**

Underneath, after a long gap, I wrote a second line, which I have never shown anyone until now:

**Neither will I.**
