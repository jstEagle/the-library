## 14. The Eighth Step

The ledger said *witnessed by blood*, and the ledger was very specific about what that meant, because people in 1911 had no concept of ambiguity as a mercy.

*The eighth step shall be performed before the founders' line entire,* the faded ink read, in a hand that pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn through. *The seal is not a wall but a door, and a door must be held from both sides. Let those who share the water's debt stand at the stone and see what their house has done.*

I read it aloud to Edda and Hal in the kitchen, because I'd learned by then that the Vosses absorbed bad news better through the ears than the eyes. Wren sat at the table with her pencils, drawing — the same face, always the same face, though lately she'd begun adding details. A wet hem. Hands cupped together, like someone asking for something small.

"So," I said, closing the ledger. "It needs both of you there. Watching. Participating, if it comes to that."

Hal turned his coffee mug in a slow circle without drinking from it. "Participating how?"

"Nobody knows. The founder who wrote this died before he could be annoyingly thorough." I tapped the page. "There's a gap in the text here where something's been scratched out. Deliberately. With a knife, by the look of it."

Edda leaned over to look. "What do you think it said?"

"I think," I said carefully, "that whatever it said scared them enough to gouge it out of a book they were otherwise treating like scripture. Which means we're doing this ritual with step eight partially redacted and step nine missing entirely, in front of a well that refills itself with something that isn't water, three days before the solstice."

Silence. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere in the walls, faintly, something hummed back — a habit the house had developed over the past week that nobody commented on anymore, the way families stop commenting on a limp.

"We'll do it," Edda said.

"Edda—"

"We'll do it," she repeated, and looked at her brother until he nodded. "Tamsin, that thing has been talking to my daughter for a year. It knows her voice now. It uses her voice. Whatever the eighth step costs, it costs less than another winter of that."

I wanted to argue. I want you to understand that — I stood in that kitchen with every professional instinct I owned telling me to quote a price, pack my kit, and drive until Hollowmere was a story I told at parties. But Wren looked up from her drawing at exactly that moment, and she wasn't looking at her mother or her uncle. She was looking at me. And she turned the paper around so I could see it.

The drowned child. Except this time the child wasn't alone. There was a second figure, tall, standing at the edge of the well, holding out a hand.

The figure had my coat.

"Okay," I said, to no one in particular. "Tomorrow at dawn."

* * *

Dawn at Hollowmere in late June arrives gray and apologetic, the sun coming up somewhere behind cloud cover like a performer who's lost his nerve. We gathered at the well just after five: me with the ledger, the salt, and the iron rod from the founders' cache; Edda and Hal on either side of the well cap, which Hal had unsealed the night before with a wrench and a face like a man opening a coffin he'd personally nailed shut.

Wren we left in the kitchen. That was Edda's one condition, and I agreed with it, and I want it noted for posterity that we were all wrong.

The cap came off with a sound like a gasp.

The smell hit first — not rot, exactly. Pond water. Old pond water, the kind that's been sitting still so long it's forgotten how to move. Below it, the shaft dropped into darkness that my flashlight refused to properly enter; the beam went down maybe ten feet and then just gave up, diffusing into murk like it had been swallowed.

The waterline was high. Higher than yesterday — I could tell because Hal had marked the stonework with chalk, a habit he'd started without telling anyone, a little ladder of dates climbing the inner rim. June 14th. June 16th. Each mark higher than the last. Today's level lapped at the newest mark already, and Hal had made that mark an hour ago.

"It's faster," he said quietly.

"I know."

"Tamsin. It's *faster.*"

"I know, Hal." I opened the ledger to the eighth step and read it through twice more while Edda and Hal took their places. The instructions were simple enough, which should have been my first warning. Rituals are never simple. Simple rituals are like simple surgery — the simplicity is yours, not the procedure's.

*Let the line stand witness. Let each speak the name of the house. Let the salt close the circle and the iron touch the water. What rises must be seen to rise.*

"Names of the house," I said. "You first, Edda."

She squared her shoulders. For a moment she looked less like a farmer and more like what she probably was underneath — somebody's daughter, raised on this land, taught its weather and its silences. "Voss," she said. "Edda Voss. Daughter of Marta, daughter of Aldous, daughter of Bram — who built this house and dug this well and started this." Her voice cracked slightly on *started*. "I witness it."

"Hal."

He swallowed. "Hal Voss. Son of Marta. Grandson of Aldous." A pause. His jaw worked. "Great-grandson of Bram." Another pause, longer, and when he spoke again it was barely above a whisper. "And nephew of Peter. Who I haven't said out loud in thirty years."

The air changed. I can't describe it better than that. Not temperature, not pressure — something more like attention. The whole farm leaning in.

"Salt," I said, and poured it.

The circle closed easy, white against the dark earth, and I lowered the iron rod until the tip touched the water.

The water stopped.

Not drained. Not receded. *Stopped* — the way a clock stops, the way a sentence stops mid-word. Every ripple died at once. The surface went flat and bright and perfectly still, a black mirror at the bottom of a stone throat, and in it I could see our three faces looking down, distorted, arranged like figures on a card.

For eleven seconds — I counted, because counting is what I do instead of screaming — nothing happened.

Then Edda exhaled, a shaky, disbelieving laugh. "Is that it? Is it—"

The water rose.

Fast. Not the creeping inch-a-night refill of the past weeks but a surge, a tide, the level jumping six inches, a foot, eighteen, climbing the shaft with a low grinding sound like the well itself clearing its throat. The iron rod in my hands went cold enough to burn and I dropped it — it fell end over end into the dark and never made a splash. I heard it land. I heard it land on something that was not water.

"*Out of the circle,*" I said. "*Now.*"

We scrambled back. The water kept rising — up the shaft, past the rim marks, past every mark Hal had ever chalked, past the point where any honest aquifer could explain it — until it stood at the lip of the well, flush with the stone, trembling but refusing to spill. A full column of black water balanced above ground level, defying several laws I'd previously trusted, and across its surface moved ripples again, spreading outward from the center, in patterns.

Spirals. The same spirals the droppings had made in the barn, the first morning, when this was still a job about mice.

And beneath the spirals, rising slowly through the black like a photograph developing, a face.

Small. Young. Eyes closed, or nearly — slitted, the way someone looks at light they've gotten unused to. Hair drifting upward in the impossible current. The mouth open, working, shaping words that didn't carry across the air but arrived anyway, directly behind my eyes, in a voice like a recording played underwater:

*You saw me. You finally saw me. Do you know how long—*

The face broke apart. The column of water shuddered, sank two feet, surged three. Behind us, from the direction of the farmhouse, Wren began to scream.

* * *

Here's what I understood, running back toward the house with Edda and Hal pounding along beside me: the ritual had half-worked, and half-worked was worse than not working at all.

Because for eleven seconds the water had been still. For eleven seconds the thing in the well had felt what it was asking for — witnessed, named, *seen* by the blood that put it there. And then the ritual ran out of steps, because there weren't any more steps, because dead men don't finish their sentences, and the thing had been given a taste of completion and then had the plate snatched away.

Grief, I could have handled. Grief was what I'd been preparing for since Wren's drawings. You mourn a drowned child, you bury her proper, you say her name. I had a plan shaped like grief.

But grief, denied, curdles. And what I'd seen surfacing in that black column hadn't only been sad. It had been *hungry*. The distinction between *see me* and *take me in* had collapsed somewhere in those eleven seconds, and the visitor had come out the other side wanting something it no longer knew how to ask for politely.

Wren was in the kitchen, standing on a chair, pointing at the window over the sink.

Every glass in the farmhouse was fogged. Every pane, every window, front and back, inside and out — covered in condensation on a dry June morning, and written into it, from the outside, in a child's careful hand, over and over and over:

*almost*

*almost*

*almost*

*ALMOST*

"She was fine," Edda was saying, holding Wren, who had stopped screaming and gone rigid and silent, shaking like a struck bell. "She was eating breakfast, she was—"

"The water," Hal said. He'd gone to the window and was looking out across the yard toward the well, and his voice had gone strange and flat. "Tamsin. The water."

I looked.

The column was gone. The well stood ordinary, capped by nothing, its rim dark and wet — and the overflow was moving across the yard. Not flooding. *Traveling*. A sheet of black water perhaps an inch deep, sliding over the grass without disturbing a blade of it, flowing uphill in places, fanning outward from the wellhead in slow, deliberate tendrils. Toward the house. Toward the barn. Toward everything with foundations, everything with walls, everything hollow enough to fill.

"It's leaving the well," I said. My mouth was very dry. "It doesn't need the well anymore. The solstice was going to bring it to us. We just saved it the trip."

Edda turned to me, Wren clutched against her shoulder, and asked the question I'd been dreading with the calm of a woman who has already decided she will do whatever the answer requires.

"How long do we have?"

I did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. The refill rate, doubling daily. The distance the overflow had covered in the ninety seconds we'd been watching. The solstice, forty-eight hours out, when whatever boundary the founders had spent seven steps building would thin to tissue paper regardless of anything we did.

"Less than two days," I said. "Maybe less than one."

"And the ninth step?" Hal asked. "The one that's missing. The one they died before writing down."

I looked at the fogged windows. At the word *almost*, repeating like a pulse. At Wren's face over her mother's shoulder — calm now, terribly calm, watching the black water cross the yard with the expression of someone listening to music only she could hear.

"There is no ninth step," I said. "That's the point. The founders never finished it because the ninth step isn't instructions. It's a choice. Somebody has to decide how this ends, and write it down themselves, in their own hand." I picked up the ledger. The blank final page seemed colder than the rest of the book. "And I think I already know whose hand it has to be."

Outside, the water reached the doorstep and stopped, pooling against the threshold in a perfect, patient line.

Waiting to be let in.
