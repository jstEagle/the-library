## 16. The Overflow

We spent solstice eve the way people spend the night before a hurricane: doing small, useless things with great seriousness.

Edda salted every threshold twice and then did the windowsills a third time because Hal had walked through one of the lines carrying firewood, and she didn't trust him to have noticed. Hal stacked the founders' cache on the kitchen table — iron nails gone soft with rust, a brick of grey salt, the ledger with its water-swollen spine — and arranged them like a surgeon laying out instruments he hoped never to use. Wren sat at that table drawing.

I checked my phone eleven times in an hour. No signal at Hollowmere, which I already knew, but checking it gave my hands something to do besides shake.

"You could still leave," Edda said. She said it without accusation, which was worse. "Two counties over worked fine for a week."

"It didn't work fine. It worked exactly as badly as staying would have, just with worse coffee." I turned the ledger toward me so I wouldn't have to look at her face. "Besides. If this goes wrong, I'd rather be here than hear about it later."

That was only half true, and we both knew which half. The other half was that I had written my name in that book three days ago, and names don't travel well. Mine was rooted here now, the way a tooth is rooted in a jaw. I could leave. I just couldn't leave *all* of me.

Wren looked up from her paper. She'd been drawing all evening — not the drowned child this time, but the well itself, over and over, cross-sections of it, and each drawing went deeper than the last. The final one was less a picture of a well than of a throat. It narrowed, and narrowed, and then opened out into something vast and dark at the bottom, and she had drawn tiny figures down there. Not one figure. Several. Small ones, huddled together, waiting.

"Wren," I said carefully. "How many are down there?"

She held up one finger. Then, after a moment, she folded it down and held up nothing at all. Her mouth worked around words that wouldn't come, and her eyes were bright and furious with the effort, and finally she tapped the empty space where a number should be and looked at me with an expression I can only describe as *apologetic*.

"None," I translated. "Or — not none. Not a number."

Hal set down his mug too hard. "It's one girl. The ledger says one girl. 1911, the Aldous family's daughter, fell in during the digging—"

"The ledger says what the men who wrote it wanted it to say," Edda said quietly. "Hal. We've been through this."

He hadn't been through it, actually. He'd been told it, repeatedly, and had declined to absorb it, and I understood why. Because if there was more than one thing in the well — or something that had never been countable at all — then the ninth step wasn't a rescue. It was an opening. And everything we'd built our courage on for three weeks assumed we knew what we were being brave about.

I closed the ledger. "Midnight," I said. "Let's get through midnight first."

* * *

At eleven-forty, the house went quiet in a way houses shouldn't.

Not silent — quiet. There's a difference. Silence is the absence of sound. This was the presence of a held breath. The refrigerator hum stopped mid-note. The wind outside, which had been working the eaves all evening, laid down like a dog told to wait. Even the salt seemed to stop doing whatever salt does, some patient mineral patience going out of it, and I know how that sounds, but you spend enough nights in that house and you start believing minerals have moods.

We stood in the kitchen. Nobody suggested we go look at the well. Looking at the well felt like answering a knock.

The smoke detector in the hallway chirped once. Then again. Then, instead of the usual interval, it chirped in a pattern — two short, one long, pause, two short — and my skin went cold all over, because I knew that pattern. That was the pattern it had used in my motel room in March, the night it asked me my name through a device designed to warn people about dying in their sleep.

"It's early," Hal whispered. "It's not midnight yet."

"Then it's not waiting for midnight," I said. "Midnight is when it stops asking nicely."

Edda took Wren's hand. Wren took mine. I want to note that: a mute ten-year-old reached across a kitchen table and took the hand of the pest control lady, and I let her, and my hand was shaking and hers wasn't, and somewhere in the last month I had apparently become someone a child would stand behind.

The chirping stopped.

In its place came a sound I will be hearing for the rest of my life, however long the rest of my life turns out to be. Water. But moving upward. You know the sound water makes going down a drain — that swallow, that spiral-gurgle? This was that, reversed, coming from everywhere at once: under the floorboards, inside the walls, up through the foundations, a great slow inhale of liquid rising through the bones of the house.

"The well cap," Hal said. "It's soldered shut. It's been soldered shut since—"

"I know what it's been since," I said. "Come on."

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about dread: it has a gravity. You fight it, and then one moment you're standing in a kitchen holding a child's hand, and the next you're walking toward the thing you'd rather die than see, because not seeing it has become worse than seeing it. The unknown doesn't stay unknown. It inflates. It fills every room you're not looking into.

We went out the back door into a night that was too warm for June and smelled like pond bottom.

* * *

The yard was flooded.

An inch of standing water everywhere, black and perfectly still, reflecting stars that weren't in the sky above us — I checked, I actually stood there with my neck craned comparing reflections, because the reflected sky had different constellations, older ones, arrangements of stars no one had named in any book I've ever read, and the real sky above was ordinary and half-clouded and indifferent.

The water wasn't spilling from anywhere. That was the detail my brain kept snagging on, trying to reject. Floods come from somewhere. Rivers overbank, drains back up, rain falls. This water was simply *arriving*, seeping up out of the ground as though the whole farm had become the underside of a lake, as though we had always been at the bottom and had only just now noticed.

And it was climbing.

Not spreading — climbing. Where the barn wall met the floodwater, the water went *up* the planks, a sheet of it, defying every law I'd slept through in school, rising without spilling, without dripping, clinging to the vertical surface the way mercury clings to glass. It climbed the barn. It climbed the farmhouse. By the time we reached the well enclosure it had reached the roofline, and the whole farm stood wrapped in still black water that touched nothing else — not us, not the grass, not the air — as though we were beneath it, or it was beneath noticing us.

The well cap was gone. Not broken. Gone. The solder had run off it like candle wax, pooled on the stone lip in bright silver tears, and the cap itself was nowhere, and the hole where it had been breathed cold air upward.

I made the mistake of looking down.

The waterline was thirty feet below the rim. I know because I've measured this well; I measured it in week one, back when I still believed in measurements. Thirty feet of dark shaft, and at the bottom of it, the water. Except the water was rising as I watched — visibly, smoothly, without a single ripple — and it was rising along the walls too, climbing the curved stone in that same impossible sheet, so that the shaft was becoming a column of water with a bubble of air trapped in its middle, and the bubble was shrinking.

And in the shrinking bubble, something was coming up.

Slowly. Politely, almost. A pale shape ascending through the dark like a diver who had all the time in the world, except divers kick, and this shape didn't kick. It simply rose, arms at its sides, hair drifting around a small face in the slow motion of deep water, and the face was the face from Wren's drawings — every drawing, a century of drawings — round-cheeked, solemn, eyes enormous and dark and wet.

A girl. Maybe eight years old. Dressed in clothes from 1911 that the water hadn't rotted because, I suspect, the water had promised to take care of them.

"She's coming out," Hal said. His voice had gone thin and high. "Tamsin. Tamsin, she's coming *out*—"

"Yes," I said.

"We should — the ledger, the eighth step, we did the eighth step, the water stilled—"

"It stilled for six hours, Hal. Grief does that. So does hunger." I put myself between the well and the others, which was absurd — I was five foot four against a phenomenon — but bodies do what they do. "Stay behind me. Whatever happens. And whatever she asks—" My voice caught. I swallowed it down. "Whatever she asks, don't answer for me."

The girl reached the rim of the well.

She didn't climb out. The water carried her, lifting her gently over the lip and setting her down on the wet stones as though setting down something precious, and the column of water behind her stayed attached to her back like a cloak, like a wing, like an umbilicus running down into the deep. She stood in the flooded yard, barefoot on stone, and the black water all around us trembled once, all of it at once, the way a struck bell trembles.

She looked at me.

Her eyes were the worst part, and I need you to understand that I mean that in the most precise way possible. They weren't dead eyes. They weren't hungry eyes, not yet, though hunger lived in them the way weather lives in a horizon. They were *waiting* eyes. Eyes that had asked a question for a hundred and fourteen years and had watched every person they asked either flee or lie, and had learned to expect both, and had decided, with terrible patience, to ask anyway.

She opened her mouth. What came out was not one voice. It was several layered together — a child's voice, and beneath it older voices, worn smooth as river stones, and beneath those something vast and slow that I refused to let myself name — but the child's voice rode on top, and it was gentle. That was the horror of it. After everything, after the walls and the spirals and the questions in the dark, it was *gentle*.

"What is your name?"

Behind me, Wren made a small sound. Edda's arm came around her.

"No," I said.

The girl tilted her head. Water ran from her hair down her cheeks like she was crying, though her face didn't cry.

"What is your name?" Softer now. Not a demand. A request. The way you'd ask someone who looked sad, someone you wanted to help, someone whose sadness you recognized.

"I know what you're doing," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I hated that. "You asked me in March. Through the smoke detector. You asked me in the motel in April, through the taps. Every night for a month, through the radiator, through the pipes, through the hum in my own head when it got quiet enough. And I said no then, and I'm saying no now, because I know what a name is for. The founders wrote nine steps, and the ninth step needs a living name freely given, and you've been shopping for one for a century."

The girl was quiet. The water was quiet. Somewhere far below us, down the throat of the well, in the vast open dark Wren had drawn, something shifted — a slow displacement, large beyond deciding — and settled again.

Then the girl said, still gently:

"That isn't why."

"*Isn't* it."

"I want to say it," she said. "No one has said my name in a hundred years. The men who dug the well erased it. They wrote their own names in the book and left a blank where mine was, and a blank is a terrible thing to be." She took a step toward me, and the water took a step with her, and I did not move, mostly through paralysis. "You have a blank too. I've seen it. Down where you keep things."

"Don't," I whispered.

"What is your name?" she asked, and this time it wasn't a ritual question, wasn't a step in anyone's design. It was naked. It was the question of a drowned child who had been buried improperly by frightened men, sealed under solder and salt and silence, asking the only thing left to ask: *who am I, who will say it, please.*

And God help me, I wanted to answer. That's the confession of this chapter, written down before whatever comes next takes the pen out of my hand. Standing in that flooded yard with the impossible water climbing the walls and the sky reflected wrong in the flood, I wanted to tell her my name more than I have ever wanted anything, because she looked at me the way you look at the one person in a room who might understand, and I did understand, and understanding is a door.

The water reached the top of the farmhouse and hung there, a black ceiling over the world, and began, very slowly, to bend inward — toward us, toward the well, gathering itself like a lung filling for one long exhale.

The girl spread her small hands. Patient. Endless. Gentle as drowning.

"What is your name?"

And behind me, in a voice that hadn't spoken in a year — clear, steady, and absolutely certain — Wren answered her.
