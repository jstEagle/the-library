## 7. The First Vanishing

Uncle Marlowe was the kind of man who believed in three things: the Lord, the torque specs on a '94 Ford, and himself. In that order, though he'd have argued the order depended on the day of the week.

He did not believe in the well. He had said so at dinner the night before, with his mouth full of Wren's cornbread, in the tone of a man laying down a winning hand. "A hole's a hole," he'd announced. "You put a cap on it, it stays capped. Everything else is stories old women tell to keep children out of the mud."

Wren hadn't looked up from her plate. "Everything else," she said, "is bookkeeping."

Marlowe had laughed so hard he knocked over his sweet tea, and Danny had laughed with him, and for about four minutes the kitchen had felt like a kitchen instead of a courtroom where we were all waiting to hear what we were charged with.

That was the last time I heard him laugh. I want to be precise about that, because precision is all I have left of him now, and even that is leaking.

* * *

It was nearly eleven when I heard the screen door slap shut. I was in Theo's room, which I had begun to think of as my office, if an office can be a place where you sit on the floor surrounded by photographs and slowly lose your grip on the difference between then and now. I heard the diesel rattle of Marlowe's truck starting up—or not starting up, I realized later, just turning over, idling, because he never left the yard. I heard him crunch across the gravel toward the back field, boots heavy on the frozen ground, and I remember thinking: *there goes a man going to prove a point.*

I should have gone after him. I want that recorded too. I sat there with a photograph of the 1987 county fair in my hands—the one where the extra chair had appeared between Aunt Dell and her husband, both of them long dead, both of them apparently still expected at dinner—and I thought, *he'll be back in five minutes, and he'll come in stomping and pleased with himself, and I won't have to be the one who says the crazy thing out loud.*

Five minutes passed. Then ten. The truck kept idling out front, a low animal sound in the cold.

I went to the window. The headlights were on, throwing two cones of yellow into the dark, aimed at nothing. The truck sat in the yard like something parked by a driver who had stepped away to check the mail and would be right back.

I got my coat.

* * *

The night had that particular quality winter nights get out here, where the cold isn't a temperature so much as a presence, something with opinions. My breath came out in flags. The grass crunched under me like packing foam. And ahead, past the dead garden, past the leaning fence posts, the well sat under its stone cap with the patient geometry of a thing that has never once been in a hurry.

"Marlowe?" My voice came out smaller than I intended. "Uncle Marlowe?"

Nothing. Just the truck behind me, ticking as its engine cooled—no, wait. It wasn't cooling. It was still running. I could hear it idling behind me, steady as a pulse, and somehow that was worse than silence. A truck doesn't idle itself. A truck doesn't keep its lights on for nobody.

I came around the side of the well house and found his flashlight first. It was lying in the frost, still on, beam pointed up at the sky like it was signaling something. Then his gloves, folded neatly on the cap of the well. Not dropped. *Folded.* And then—

His boots.

His boots were standing upright in the grass, side by side, laces tucked inside, the way you leave them when you're planning to step back into them in a minute. Socks inside them, balled at the toes. His footprints in the frost led from the direction of the truck to the well and stopped at the cap, clean as a sentence ending in a period.

No prints leading away.

I stood there doing the math every horror movie teaches you to do, and the math came out wrong in every direction. People don't evaporate. People don't step out of their boots and their socks and their skin and walk off barefoot into a January field without leaving a single print. Unless—

Unless they didn't walk anywhere at all.

I put my hand on the stone cap. It was warm. Not sun-warm; there was no sun. Warm like a sleeping animal, warm like bread, warm like the kitchen on a morning when somebody who loved you was making pancakes. The smell rose off it faintly—coffee, cinnamon, cut grass in July—and I snatched my hand back like I'd touched a stove.

"Marlowe." Louder this time. My voice cracked down the middle. "MARLOWE."

The well did not answer. Wells don't. But the warmth under my palm pulsed once, slow and content, like something turning over in its sleep, and I swear to God—I know how this sounds—I swear the air above the cap moved the way air moves above an open oven door.

I ran back to the truck. Empty. Keys in the ignition, radio playing low—some gospel station out of Carthage, half-static, a choir singing about the river. His thermos sat in the cupholder, still hot when I touched it. Still *hot.* He'd been standing at that well minutes before I got there. He'd been alive and drinking coffee and disbelieving in holes, and now there was a pile of clothes in the frost and a warm stone and a truck full of his voice on the radio, some call-in show where a woman was asking whether grief was a sin, and I turned it off because I couldn't listen to a stranger talk about grief while my uncle's socks were freezing in the grass.

I carried the boots back to the house. I don't know why. It felt like evidence. It felt like a body. I carried them against my chest the way you carry a sleeping child, and behind me the truck idled on with its lights on, and I didn't have the keys to turn it off, or maybe I did and couldn't make myself reach into that cab, and either way it ran all night. I checked. It ran until dawn, and then it simply wasn't running anymore, and nobody could tell me who had turned it off.

* * *

By the time I got inside, boot-prints of frost melting off my sleeves, the family had gathered in the kitchen the way families do—not called, just summoned by some frequency of wrongness only kin can hear. Wren at the table with her hands flat on the wood. Mom wrapped in a quilt she'd had since I was nine. Danny on the stairs, halfway up, like he wanted to see without being part of it. Cousin Patrice holding her phone with the flashlight on, pointing it at nothing.

"He went out to the well," I said. "He's gone. The truck's there, his boots are there, there's nothing else. There's *nothing else.*"

Patrice started dialing him. We all listened to his ringtone go off somewhere upstairs—of course he'd left his phone, Marlowe treated his phone like a leash—and the sound of it, that tinny little banjo tune bouncing down through the ceiling, made three people flinch at once.

"Call the sheriff," Mom said.

"And tell him what?" Wren asked. She didn't say it cruelly. That was the terrible part. She said it the way you'd read a weather report. "Tell him a man walked out of his boots at a capped well on land this family has owned for two hundred years? Sheriff Dawes went to school with Marlowe. He'll bring dogs. The dogs will circle the property line and come back confused, and by Thursday everyone in town will know the Brightwaters have finally gone round the bend, and Marlowe—" she stopped. Her hands pressed harder on the table. "Marlowe will still be wherever he is."

"You don't know that he's—" Patrice began.

"I know exactly where he is," Wren said, and the room went very quiet, the way rooms do when someone says the true thing instead of the kind thing.

* * *

Here is what I cannot get straight, even now, even writing it down with the page held at arm's length like it might bite: by morning, they had already started losing him.

Not grieving him. *Losing* him. There's a difference, and the difference is everything.

At six a.m., Mom stood at the counter making coffee and said, "We should figure out what Marlowe would want us to do about the service," and then frowned at the coffee maker like it had asked her a question in a foreign language. "What was I saying?"

"About the service," I said.

"Whose service?"

And I watched her try to hold onto it. I watched her face do the thing a face does when a word is on the tip of the tongue—that reaching, grasping expression—but it wasn't one word slipping. It was a whole man, sliding. She shook her head. "Someone died recently," she said slowly, testing each word like ice on a pond. "Didn't someone die? Theo. Theo died. Why am I thinking about a service?"

"Theo's service is Saturday," I said. "Mom, Marlowe went missing last night. You were there. You were standing right there when I told you."

She looked at me with such gentle, worried pity that I understood, in one vertical drop of the stomach, that to her I sounded insane. Worse than insane—*sad*, sad in a way that made her want to fix me cocoa and call Dr. Prasad in town.

Danny came down the stairs then, and I grabbed him by the shoulders like a drowning man grabbing driftwood. "Danny. Last night. Uncle Marlowe. Tell her."

Danny looked at me. And here's the thing—Danny had laughed with Marlowe twelve hours earlier. Danny had counted them aloud at dinner, eleven voices where ten belonged, and Marlowe's laugh had been the loudest one in the room. Danny looked at me and said, "Who?"

Not cruelly. Not even dismissively. *Curiously.* The way you'd ask who a stranger in a photograph was.

"Your uncle," I said. "Big guy. Gray mustache. Drove the blue F-250, always smelled like diesel and Winterfresh gum. He taught you to drive in the hayfield last summer, you killed a fence post, he told your mom it was his fault—"

"Felix." Danny's voice had gone careful, the voice you use near an open wound. "There's no uncle like that. There's never been an uncle like that."

I let go of him. I walked out to the yard in my socks—it was that kind of morning, the kind where you stop caring about small protections—and the truck was there, blue, F-250, registration in the glove box reading MARLOWE BRIGHTWATER, insurance card, a hay receipt from the co-op signed in his blocky carpenter's hand. The world was full of his paperwork. The world was full of his objects. Only the people were empty of him, emptying by the hour, like water finding its level.

Only Wren and I remembered. By noon, I'd tested everyone. Patrice remembered "somebody used to fix the porch steps, I think," but couldn't attach a face. Mom cried without knowing why, which was somehow the worst version—grief arriving intact while its object dissolved, like a letter delivered with the name burned off the envelope. The body knows. The body keeps the ledger even when the mind's copy has been confiscated.

Wren sat at the kitchen table with the ledger open in front of her, and she had written, in her spidery hand, at the top of a fresh page:

*M.B. — taken in lieu of balance outstanding. Paid against account. NOT SETTLED.*

"What does that mean, not settled?" I asked.

"It means," she said, "that the well took him early. Took him against future earnings. Which means the account is still short, and it will come collecting again, and next time it may not take a man who went outside of his own free will." She looked up at me, and her milky eyes found my face with unnerving accuracy for a woman who supposedly couldn't see. "Or it may not take a man at all."

Outside, the truck sat in the yard with its headlights off, and the frost was coming back, and somewhere under the stone cap the water was warm and patient and, I understood now, *hungry in a way that hunger doesn't cover*—hungry the way a bank is hungry, hungry the way arithmetic is hungry.

I went upstairs to Theo's room and took down every photograph I'd collected and laid them out on the floor in chronological order, and I began, with cold and shaking hands, to count chairs.

Because if the chair appeared when a memory was paid—if Marlowe's taking had a mark, a notation, a seat reserved—then the ledger wasn't just a record.

It was a schedule.
