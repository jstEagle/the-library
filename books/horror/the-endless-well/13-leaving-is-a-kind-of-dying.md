## 13. Leaving Is a Kind of Dying

Here is what I learned in the forty-eight hours after the family meeting: hope is a currency, and the well accepts counterfeit.

Three of them decided to test it. Priya, my cousin, who had spent the meeting with her arms crossed like a woman guarding a door; her husband Dale, a large, kind, unremarkable man whose chief virtue was that he owned a car with a full tank; and Aunt Sable, who was not really my aunt but had been folded into the family so long ago that the distinction had worn smooth, the way a river stone does.

They left at 6:40 in the evening. I know the time because every clock in the house had finally agreed on something, and what it agreed on was wrong by eleven minutes, and Priya had stood in the kitchen doorway with her keys already in her fist and said, "We're leaving before it does whatever it's going to do. You can all stay here and be brave and dead, or you can come with us."

Nobody moved. Danny looked at his shoes. Wren looked at Priya with an expression I can only describe as pity wearing a mask of patience.

"You're making a mistake," I said.

"Probably," Priya said. "But it'll be my mistake, made on a highway, in a car that runs. Not here, in this house that breathes." She kissed my cheek. Her lips were cold. "I love you, Felix. I'm sorry I'm not the kind of brave that stays."

And then they were gone. Headlights sweeping the yard, gravel popping under the tires, the sound of the engine thinning down the county road until it was just another night noise, indistinguishable from wind.

I stood at the window and watched the dark take them, and I remember thinking — and I want this on the record, because it is the last fully honest thought I had for some time — *they're going to make it.*

The well does not release what it counts. But that night, I believed in exits.

* * *

They were gone an hour when the first wrong thing happened, and the first wrong thing was small, the way a hairline fracture is small.

Wren was telling a story. She did that now, more than ever — filling the silence the way you'd sandbag a flood, talking and talking, her blind eyes aimed at the middle distance where, I suspected, she could see more than any of us. She was telling us about a summer, decades back, when she and her sister had gotten into the preserves and eaten an entire jar of brandied cherries and blamed the dog.

"—and Sable, of course, Sable swore up and down it was the dog, and Mother knew better, but Sable stood there with her chin up and cherry juice on her collar and lied like it was a sacrament—"

She stopped.

Not the way you stop when you lose your place. The way you stop when the floor isn't where you left it.

"What was I saying?" she asked.

"Cherries," Danny said. "The dog."

"Whose dog?"

Nobody answered. The question hung there, and I felt something shift in the room, a pressure change, like the moment before weather. Wren's hand went to the table and gripped the edge of it, and her knuckles went white, and she said, very quietly, in a voice I had never heard her use — a voice with no sharpness in it at all — "Felix. Who is Sable?"

And I opened my mouth to say *she's my aunt, she just left, she's in the car with Priya and Dale,* and what came out was:

"I don't know."

I said it and then I heard it, and the hearing of it was like watching your own hand do something you hadn't told it to. I knew the name. I could feel the name in me the way you can feel a word on the tip of your tongue — the shape of it, the weight of it, a woman, older, folded into the family so long the seams had worn smooth. But the woman herself was gone. There was a hole where she had been, and the hole was shaped exactly like my certainty, and the well had taken the certainty and left the shape.

"No," I said, out loud, to the kitchen, to the walls, to whatever was keeping the books. "No. Sable. Aunt Sable. She was at the meeting. She sat—" I pointed at a chair. The third one on the left. There was a dent in the cushion. There was a coffee mug in the sink that wasn't mine, wasn't Wren's, wasn't Danny's, wasn't Priya's.

"Who's Priya?" Danny said.

And I turned on him with something close to violence in my throat, because Danny was the one who'd counted them aloud at dinner, Danny was the one who'd run to town and come back whispering about the corn, Danny of all people should have known better than to let a name go — but his face stopped me cold. He wasn't being careless. He was frightened. He was holding his own head like it might come loose, and he said, "There were people in the car, right? There were three people, I remember three people, I remember counting—" and his voice climbed, "—I remember *eleven*, Felix, I counted eleven, so who were the other—"

"Stop counting," Wren said.

He stopped. We all stopped. In the silence I could hear the house settling, or not settling — a long, low exhalation from under the floorboards, from the direction of the pantry, from the direction of everything underneath, and it sounded, God help me, it sounded *satisfied.*

* * *

We found the car at 9:15, or what the clocks claimed was 9:15. Flashlights, because nobody wanted to wait for morning and nobody said so out loud.

The property line is marked by an old stone post, half-swallowed by honeysuckle, at the bend where the county road leaves our land. The car was straddling the line. Engine off. Hood warm. I put my palm flat on it and it was warm the way a body is warm — not hot, not recently driven hard, just warm, the residual heat of something that had been alive and running and had stopped.

The doors were unlocked. The keys were in the ignition.

Priya's purse was on the passenger seat. Her phone was in it, dead, and when we got it charged — Danny held the flashlight, Wren held my elbow, though she'd never admit needing it — the last text was to me. Timestamped 6:47. *Almost out. Love you. Tell everyone—*

It ended there. No period. Tell everyone what? I sat on the shoulder of the road with a dead phone in my hand and a sentence with no ending, and I understood that the ending had been taken, that somewhere in whatever ledger the well kept there was now an entry with a blank where a life used to be, and the blank was *tidy.* That was the worst part. It was tidy. There was no struggle in the car, no broken glass, no scuff in the gravel. Three people had driven to the property line at forty miles an hour and had simply — ceased, the way a note ceases when you lift the needle.

Dale's jacket was folded on the back seat. Folded. Who folds their jacket when they vanish? Something had. Something had taken its time.

"Felix." Wren's hand on my arm. "Say their names. All three. Say them now."

"Priya," I said. "Priya Brightwater. And Dale — Dale, her husband, Dale —" and I reached for his last name and found a smooth wall. "Dale. And Sable. Aunt Sable."

"Again."

"Priya. Dale. Sable."

"Again, and don't stop until we're back inside."

We walked back down the county road in the dark, three people chanting six names like a spell that wasn't working, and the honeysuckle along the stone post was in bloom, and the smell of it was sweet and thick and underneath it, faint but unmistakable, was the smell of the farmhouse kitchen. Bread. Warmth. The smell of a table set for more than the living.

* * *

Here is how a person disappears when the well does it: not all at once. In pieces, in conversation, in the middle of a sentence, so that you feel it happen and can do nothing about it, like watching a photograph develop in reverse.

It was past midnight. Danny had finally slept, curled in the armchair in the front room with a kitchen knife on the floor beside him, which I chose not to comment on. Wren was in her chair. I was at the table with the ledger and my grandmother's notes and a legal pad, mapping debts against the family tree, and I was talking — I don't even remember what about, something about the dates, something about the intervals — and I said, "Priya always said the intervals didn't matter, she said it was the *headcount*, she said—"

And the sentence ended.

Not because I stopped talking. Because the name came out of my mouth and nothing was attached to it. I heard myself say *Priya* and it was a sound with no one in it, a coat on a hook, and I sat there with my pen still moving and felt the last of her go — the particular way she crossed her arms, the cold kiss on my cheek, *I'm sorry I'm not the kind of brave that stays* — all of it draining out through some wound I couldn't find, and what was left was a word. Just a word. A name for a person I was certain I had loved and could not, for the life of me, picture.

I wrote her name on the legal pad so fast the pen tore the paper.

"Write them down," I said, and my voice didn't sound like mine. "Write down everything. Everything we remember, right now, before it goes. Dale. What do we know about Dale?"

Silence.

"Dale," Wren said slowly, tasting it. "Was he the one with the laugh?"

"He was married to Priya."

"Who's Priya?"

"*The one I just wrote down.*"

Wren's face did something terrible then. It didn't crumple. It went still, the stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she already knows the answer to, and she said, "How many names are on that page, Felix?"

"Three."

"And how many do you still know?"

I looked at the page. Priya. Dale. Sable. Three names in my own handwriting, and behind them, a fog. I could feel the fog pressing in even as I watched. Sable was already thinning — the brandied cherries, the dog, the chin-up lie — going soft at the edges like a watercolor left in the rain.

"One," I said. "Maybe. Ask me again in an hour and it'll be none."

Wren reached across the table and put her hand flat on the legal pad, over the names, the way you'd press a wound. "Then we write faster," she said. "And we write everything, and we never stop, and when we forget what the names mean we write *that* down too. Because it can take the memory, Felix, but it can't take the record. Not if the record keeps outrunning it."

"And when it catches up?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. The house answered for her — a long groan through the joists, the sound old houses make, except old houses don't make it in rhythm, and this was in rhythm, slow and patient, and if you were the kind of person who counted things — and God help me, I was becoming that kind of person whether I wanted to or not — you might have counted the beats and found there were more of them than there should have been.

Eleven, I thought. It sounds like eleven.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Around four, I went to the pantry and pried up the floorboard again and sat with my grandmother's records spread around me on the cold dirt, reading by flashlight, and I found the thing I had missed the first time, because grief is a bad archivist.

In the margin of the oldest page, in handwriting gone brown and spidery, my great-great-somebody had written a single line. It wasn't about trades or debts or memories. It was a warning, and it was underlined twice, and the ink had been pressed so hard it had gone through to the next page:

*The well does not chase. It does not need to. Nothing that is counted may cross the line, for the line is the edge of the ledger, and the ledger has no outside.*

I sat in the dirt and read it until the words stopped being words.

The car hadn't failed to escape. There had never been an escape for it to fail at. Priya and Dale and Sable — Sable, whose name was already fog in my mouth — had driven at the property line the way a moth drives at a window, and the well hadn't even had to lift a finger. It had just been patient. It had just kept the books.

And upstairs, faint through the floorboards, Danny started screaming in his sleep — not words, at first, and then words, and the words were: "There were three of them, there were THREE, I counted, somebody has to remember, SOMEBODY HAS TO REMEMBER—"

I put the boards back over the dark and went up to hold the record together with my bare hands, one name at a time, while the house counted us in the dark — and it was while I was holding Danny, while I was saying *Priya, Dale, Sable* over and over like a rosary with the beads falling off, that the tap in the kitchen turned itself on, all the way, and the water that came out was not water.
