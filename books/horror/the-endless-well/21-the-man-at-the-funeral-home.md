## 21. The Man at the Funeral Home

The paperwork outlived the dead. That was the joke I'd been telling myself for three weeks, and it was only half a joke, because it turned out that dying was the easy part—dying took an afternoon, give or take a well—but *documenting* a death, certifying it, filing it, notarizing it into the permanent record of the state of wherever-the-hell-we-were, that took forever. The county wanted forms in triplicate. The insurance company wanted a certified copy of the death certificate, which required a signature from a physician who had never examined Theo, who had never seen him, who—as far as the world outside our property line knew—had never existed at all.

That last part wasn't quite true. Theo existed on paper. He had a Social Security number, a birth certificate with my own face's timestamp on it—we'd been born eleven minutes apart, he always said he got the better half of the day—and a driver's license that had expired two years ago because he never remembered to renew anything. Paperwork remembered him even when nothing else did.

I sat across from a funeral director named Mr. Aldous Prewitt in an office that smelled like lilies and carpet cleaner, and I tried to explain why there was no body to release.

"It was a closed casket," I said. "Family decision."

"Of course." Prewitt nodded with the smooth patience of a man who had heard every possible version of this sentence and had learned to stop asking follow-up questions sometime in the late nineties. "And the remains were interred privately?"

"They were... handled privately."

"Handled." He wrote something down. His pen made a small dry sound, like a beetle crossing paper.

The thing about grief without an object—the thing nobody warns you about—is that your body doesn't know the difference. My body still woke at six-fifteen every morning expecting to hear Theo clattering around whatever kitchen we were sharing, humming something off-key, burning the first attempt at coffee. My body still turned to say something to him when something funny happened, which meant roughly forty times a day I turned to speak to air. Forty times a day, air declined to answer.

But here was the cruelty of it: I didn't know what I was grieving *for*. Not exactly. I knew Theo Brightwater, born eleven minutes after me, died at thirty-four, cause listed as "undetermined" because Dr. Vasquez at the county office had finally just written that so I would stop calling. I knew all the facts the way you know facts about a historical figure. What I didn't have was him. The texture. When I tried to picture his face I got a blur, a warm smudge where a brother should be, and when I tried to remember his voice I got static shaped vaguely like laughter.

The doctors had a word for it, probably. Grief counselor number two—I'd stopped going after she used the phrase "complicated bereavement"—had suggested trauma-related memory suppression. "Sometimes the mind protects itself," she'd said, in the gentle voice people use when they think you're fragile, and I had laughed, actually laughed, because if she only knew. If she only knew what my mind had been protecting me *from*.

Wren remembered everything. That was her punishment, or her privilege; she'd never decided which. She called me every Sunday at four o'clock, and every Sunday she started the same way: "Do you remember—" and then she'd stop herself, because she'd figured out by the second call that I didn't, that I never would, and now she just said, "Never mind, boy. You're here. That's enough."

It wasn't enough. But it was more than the others had.

Danny didn't remember Theo at all. Danny remembered that there had been a funeral, sort of—a shape of an event, a casserole-based occasion—and he remembered crying at it and being embarrassed about crying, but when I asked him once what he'd been crying about he looked at me with those hollow eyes and said, honestly, "I don't know, Felix. I think maybe I just cry now. Like a hobby."

Marlowe was gone entirely. Not dead—gone. There was a man-shaped hole in the family tree, a gap where an uncle should be, and none of us could tell you his name except Wren, who kept saying it out loud at dinner like a woman throwing bread to ducks. "Marlowe," she'd say. "Your uncle Marlowe. He liked fishing. Say it back." And we'd say it back, obedient as children, and it slid off us like water off the well cap.

So I handled the paperwork. Somebody had to, and apparently the universe had decided that somebody was me, the twin, the one whose signature matched, the one whose grief had a legal category even though it had no content.

* * *

Prewitt's office had one window, and through it I could see the rain starting—not a dramatic storm, just a fine gray drizzle, the kind of weather that makes the whole world look like it's being slowly erased. Appropriate. Everything felt slowly erased these days. I'd catch myself reaching for memories the way you reach for a light switch in a house where you've moved out—habitual, hopeful, and meeting nothing.

"There's just the matter of the personal effects," Prewitt said, sliding a box across the desk. "We received them some weeks ago. From the property."

I looked at the box. It was shoebox-sized, cardboard, unremarkable, and my hands started shaking before I touched it, which was interesting, because my brain hadn't reacted to anything in three weeks except the smell of lilies.

"What is it?"

"That's rather the question, isn't it." Prewitt smiled thinly. "Inventory says personal effects of the deceased. Contents are..." He consulted a card. "One photograph. One child's drawing. One pocket watch, non-functional."

My mouth went dry. "Open it."

"I can't, I'm afraid. Policy. It has to be released to next of kin, and you're the only next of kin who's signed anything." He pushed it another inch toward me. "Though between you and me—and I've been in this business thirty years, Mr. Brightwater, I've seen things that would curl your hair—that box arrived already open. Sitting on our receiving table one morning in June. No courier logged. No delivery slip. Just the box, and the inventory card filled out in handwriting nobody recognized."

"What did the handwriting look like?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Something flickered behind his professional composure—not fear exactly, but its cousin, the one that lives next door and doesn't visit often.

"Familiar," he said. "That's the strange part. It looked familiar. Like handwriting I'd known once and lost." He cleared his throat. "Anyway. Sign here."

I signed. My hand left a smear of sweat on the carbon copy. I picked up the box and it weighed almost nothing, which somehow made it worse—heavier boxes mean more person, and this box weighed less than a loaf of bread.

"The invoice," Prewitt said, and then paused. "There's no charge."

"There's always a charge."

"Not this time." He was looking past me, at the window, at the rain. "Consider it settled. Some accounts get settled by other means." He stood, offered his hand, and shook mine with a grip that lingered a half-second too long. "Mr. Brightwater. For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much, coming from a stranger—you have the look of a man who paid something expensive. I see it sometimes. In the ones who come out the other side of something they can't name." He let go. "Go home. Get out of the rain."

* * *

I sat in my truck in the funeral home parking lot with the box on the passenger seat and the drizzle ticking against the windshield, and I couldn't make myself drive.

Inside the box: one photograph. One child's drawing. One pocket watch, non-functional.

I knew—I knew the way you know things in dreams, without evidence, without process—that if I opened it, the photograph would be of two boys at a kitchen table. Ten chairs. Or eleven. I knew the drawing would be crayon, blue water, a stone circle. I knew the watch would be Theo's, the one Grandmother gave him, the one that had never once told the right time in its entire life, and I knew—I knew with a certainty that lived somewhere below thought—that the watch would be stopped at 6:15.

Six-fifteen. The time my body woke every morning expecting to hear him.

I didn't open the box. I want that on the record, in triplicate, notarized: I did not open it. I set it on the floor of the passenger side, face down, and I drove home with the radio on loud enough to hurt, because the alternative was silence, and silence around me had developed a habit of filling up with voices.

* * *

It was still raining when I saw him.

I'd stopped at the gas station on Route 9, the one with the single pump and the bell over the door that hadn't worked since the Clinton administration. I was buying coffee I didn't want, because buying coffee was a thing a functioning person did, and I was performing functionality the way Wren performed remembering—loudly, daily, against all evidence.

He came in behind me.

I heard him before I saw him—the wet slap of shoes on linoleum—and something happened in my chest. Not recognition. Recognition requires data. This was older than recognition, deeper, the way a dog knows its person's footsteps before the door opens. Every nerve I owned leaned toward the door like a plant toward light.

He was tall. Rain-dark hair plastered down. Mid-thirties, maybe, wearing a coat that was too thin for the weather, and he moved with a slight favoring of the left leg that I found inexplicably, unbearably sad, though I couldn't have told you why. He nodded to the clerk, who nodded back with the flat courtesy people give strangers, and the man smiled at him—warm, crooked, a smile with a hitch in it, like a laugh that had gotten halfway out and changed its mind.

I knew that smile.

Not from anywhere. From nowhere. There was no file in my head labeled with that smile. But my whole body responded to it like a struck tuning fork, and my eyes were suddenly hot, and I stood there holding a bad cup of gas station coffee and shaking.

He noticed. Of course he noticed—people notice when a grown man starts trembling by the creamer station. He looked at me, and his expression did something complicated: surprise, then a softening, then something that looked almost like apology.

"You okay?" he asked.

His voice. God help me, his voice. It landed in some room inside me that had been locked for three weeks, and I heard it echo down halls I couldn't see, touching doors I couldn't open.

"Yeah," I managed. "Yeah, I'm—sorry. You remind me of someone."

Something crossed his face then. Fast. A flinch, almost, quickly smoothed over, and he said, quietly, "People say that."

"Do they?"

"No," he admitted. "You're the first." He picked up a candy bar off the rack, considered it, put it back. "Actually, you're the first person who's looked at me like that in a long time. Like they're trying to place me."

"Am I? Placing you?"

"Trying to." He smiled again, that crooked hitching smile, and it went through me like a blade through warm butter. "Don't strain yourself. I'm nobody. Just passing through."

He bought his candy bar—decided on it after all—and a newspaper he surely wouldn't read, and while the clerk rang him up he glanced back at me and said, in a tone so mild it took a moment to register as strange:

"Heard about the farm out your way. Bad business, families losing people." A beat. "Or thinking they did."

The rain ticked on the roof. The clerk chewed his gum. Somewhere under the world, very faintly, I thought I heard water moving—but that was impossible, because the well was forty miles behind me and capped and silent and paid in full.

"Which family?" I asked.

He shrugged. "Brightwaters, somebody said." He took his change. "Sad story. Twin brothers, I heard. One of them gone." He looked at me then, straight on, and his eyes were brown and ordinary and the most familiar objects I had ever seen in my life, and he said, softly, so the clerk couldn't hear:

"Thanks, Felix."

My name. I hadn't given him my name.

"For what?" My voice cracked on it.

He considered the question honestly, head tilted, rain dripping off his sleeve onto the floor. Whatever answer he found, he decided against sharing it. He just shook his head, that same half-laugh caught in the motion, and said, "Take care of them. Wren especially. She carried enough for everybody."

Then he pushed the door open—the broken bell didn't ring, of course it didn't ring—and walked out into the rain without a coat collar turned up, without hurrying, unhurried as a man walking home from work on an evening he knows by heart. I watched him through the dirty glass until the drizzle took him, first his outline, then his color, then the rest, and the road stayed empty after he was gone longer than it should have, as if the road itself was reluctant to admit he'd ever been on it.

* * *

I don't remember paying for my coffee. I do remember standing in the parking lot getting soaked, because I deserved it or because I'd forgotten how weather worked, and I remember crying—really crying, ugly crying, the kind with hiccups—for the first time since the funeral. Since the closed casket. Since the empty box of a ceremony we'd held for a body that had never been in it.

And here is the thing I could not explain to the clerk, or to Wren on the phone that night, or to myself in the mirror afterward, red-eyed and wrung out: I was crying for someone, and I did not know who.

Grief needs an object. That's what the books say. You grieve *for*, you mourn *someone*, the pain points at a person like a compass needle. Mine pointed everywhere and nowhere, a needle spinning uselessly, and yet the grief itself was so specific. It had a shape. It had a voice with a hitch of laughter in it. It favored its left leg. It bought a candy bar at a gas station on Route 9 and thanked me by name for something I couldn't remember doing.

When I finally got home, soaked and hollowed, Wren was waiting up in the kitchen with tea going cold in front of her. She took one look at my face and set down her cup.

"What happened?"

"A man," I said. "At the gas station. He thanked me."

"Thanked you for what?"

"I don't know."

She studied me for a long time, her clouded eyes doing their best with my drowned ones, and then she reached across the table and gripped my wrist hard, harder than an old woman should be able to grip, and held on.

"What did he look like?" she whispered.

I opened my mouth to describe him and discovered, with a horror that arrived a full second before its meaning, that I couldn't. Tall, I thought. Brown eyes. A crooked smile. But the details were already going soft at the edges, already dissolving like sugar in rain, and by tomorrow I knew I'd have nothing but the feeling—the tuning-fork hum, the ache with no address.

"Wren," I said, "why am I crying?"

She didn't answer right away. She looked past me, toward the dark window, toward the fields beyond it, toward the low stone circle forty miles east that we had sealed and silenced and sworn never to visit again. And when she spoke, her voice was steady in the way that costs everything.

"Because love keeps its own ledger, boy," she said. "And some debts get paid in a currency the heart remembers even when the mind's been robbed blind." She squeezed my wrist once more and let go. "Now sit down. I'll make fresh tea. And Felix—"

She hesitated. Outside, the rain kept falling, patient as arithmetic.

"In the morning," she said, "we're going to need to talk about what I found in the pantry floor. Because the records weren't finished. Your grandmother kept writing after the last page—and the last entry isn't a debt at all."

She slid a yellowed envelope across the table toward me, addressed in handwriting I almost, achingly, recognized.

"It's addressed to you. And it's dated the day after tomorrow."
