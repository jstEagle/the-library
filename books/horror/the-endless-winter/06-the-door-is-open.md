## 6. The Door Is Open

The call came in at 6:47 in the morning, while Ulyana was still rinsing his coffee mug in the station's narrow kitchenette, and he knew before he answered it that the world had changed shape overnight.

"Ulyana Brightwater?" The voice was young, breathless, cracked at the edges by wind and something else—something that sounded like a man trying very hard not to scream. "This is Tom Okafor, over at the tower site. We—we need you to come out here."

Ulyana set the mug down with exaggerated care, the way you do when your hands want to shake and you refuse to let them. "Tom. It's early. Whatever it is, it'll keep until—"

"It won't keep." A pause, filled with static and ragged breathing. "We found something under the old Brightwater place. Under the foundation. And I need someone to tell me what it is, because nobody on this crew will say its name out loud, and I keep thinking—if anyone knows, it'd be you. It's got your name on it, man. Your *family's* name."

The kitchenette light buzzed overhead. Outside the window, Vesper Hollow lay gray and blue under the snow, chimney smoke standing straight up in the dead air like columns holding up nothing.

"I'll be there in twenty minutes," Ulyana said.

He did not remember driving there. That was the first thing he noticed afterward—that the whole route had vanished from behind his eyes, the way a nightmare does when you wake, leaving only the fact of arrival. He was standing at the edge of the excavation pit with snow melting through his boots, looking down at a door.

* * *

They had cut into the hillside where the Brightwater homestead had burned—or been burned—in 1911, clearing ground for the new cell tower's footing. Everyone in town knew the story: fire, tragedy, ruins, a century of scrub pine growing over the cellar hole. Children dared each other to go there. Teenagers drank there. Nobody built there, though no ordinance said they couldn't. Nobody ever said why.

The crew had dug through four feet of frozen topsoil, then through rubble that should have been the collapsed foundation—and found instead a wall of fitted stone, dry and unweathered, as if the century above it had never happened. Set into that wall was a door of black wood, grain so dense it looked less like timber than like frozen tar, and it was sealed from the inside.

Ulyana stood at the pit's rim and understood, with the terrible clarity of a man recognizing a face in an old photograph, that he had seen this door before. Not in life. In the margins of memory—the half-dreams he'd had since childhood, the ones he'd never told anyone: a hallway, warm air moving against his skin like breath, and a door of black wood with a crest carved at eye level. A circle bisected by a line, with three small marks descending from the center like tears falling upward.

It was carved there now, worn smooth by hands or by time or by touching.

"That's the Brightwater crest," he heard himself say. His voice came out flat, professional, the voice he used on callers who were clearly lying. "My family's mark. It was on the homestead lintel, according to—" He stopped. According to whom? He had never read that anywhere. He had never been told. He simply knew it, the way you know your own heartbeat.

Tom Okafor climbed up beside him, cheeks raw with cold, eyes too wide. "Sealed from the inside," Tom repeated, for maybe the fifth time that morning. "You get what that means? Somebody shut that door and then—they were on the other side when they did it. Or something was."

"And it's warm," said Delia Marsh.

She was the fourth worker, the only woman on the crew, mid-forties, practical, the kind of person who fixed her own truck. She crouched at the pit's edge with her gloved hand pressed flat against the stone wall, and she wasn't looking at the door. She was looking at Ulyana with an expression he would think about for the rest of his life—which, given how the rest of his life went, was saying something.

"The wood's warm," she said. "Not the stone. Just the door. Like something's leaning against it from the other side."

"Body heat," one of the others offered weakly. "Ground's warmer than you'd think below frost line—"

"It's twenty degrees out here and that door is blood-warm," Delia said. "I put my bare hand on it. Don't ask me why. It felt like putting my hand on somebody's chest."

Nobody spoke after that. The wind moved through the pines above the pit with a sound like a long inhale, and Ulyana noticed that all four workers kept their backs to the door even while talking about it, the way people instinctively turn away from a thing they don't want watching them.

"We're not drilling today," Tom said finally. "I called it. Company can fine me. I'm not putting a bit anywhere near that thing until somebody tells me what my grandfather wouldn't." He looked at Ulyana. "Your family lived here two hundred years. What's under your house?"

And Ulyana Brightwater, who had spent ten years explaining away the voices of the dead, who had built a fortress of reasonable explanations so seamless he could live inside it without seeing the walls—he opened his mouth to say *I don't know*, which was true, and also the last lie he would have time to tell.

What came out instead was: "Nothing good."

* * *

He left the site because staying felt dangerous in a way he couldn't articulate, and he had learned long ago to trust the animal part of himself that knew when to leave a room. He drove back toward town with the radio off. He always kept the radio off in the mornings; the dead didn't call before dark, and he needed the silence the way other men needed coffee.

Halfway down Ridge Road he passed Maren walking along the shoulder, hood up, duffel over her shoulder, thumb out.

His sister. Eleven years since she'd left Vesper Hollow and stopped answering his letters. He pulled over without deciding to, the way you wake up already sitting upright from a bad dream, and she got in without a word, and for six miles neither of them spoke, and the silence between them was so old it had its own weather.

"You got Mom's letter," he said finally. It wasn't a question. He'd never forwarded it. He'd told himself it was mercy—Maren had built a life in Duluth, a real life, and their mother's dying words were a rope, and he hadn't wanted to see what she'd hang from it.

"I got it." Maren looked out the window at the white fields going by. "Two years late. You know what's funny? Everything she wrote in it turned out to be true anyway. I just had to come home to check."

"Maren—"

"There's a door under our house," she said, still not looking at him. "Warm on one side. Sealed from the inside. And you've known about the calls for ten years, and you never once picked up a phone and said, *Maren, something is wrong with our family.* So don't start apologizing now. Save it. I have a feeling we're going to need all the words we've got."

She was right, God help him. She was right about everything, and she hadn't even seen the ledger yet.

* * *

The calls started at sundown, and they were different.

Ulyana had rules for broadcast nights, rules refined over a decade like scar tissue: take every call, log every voice, explain everything away before the explanation could fail. Grief makes people hear what they need. Hoaxers love a ghost show. Static lies. Ten years of those three sentences, laid down like bricks, and he had lived inside them comfortably enough.

At 9:12, the board lit up, and the first caller said, "Ulyana, it's Peter Aldous. I fell through the ice on Kettle Lake in '98. I've been waiting for you to say my name right."

Peter Aldous. Ulyana had taken that call a hundred times over the years—a wet, gargling whisper that he'd logged as *prank, lake accident reenactment*. But tonight the voice was clear. Tonight it was a middle-aged man's voice, patient, tired, the voice of someone who had been underwater for twenty-six years and had finally gotten around to being annoyed about it.

"You always say it wrong," Peter said. "You say *Aldus*. There's an O. My mother put it there. She wanted me to have a round sound, like a coin dropping in a well. Say it right, Ulyana. Please. It's been so long since anybody said it right."

"Aldous," Ulyana said, and heard the O land, round as a coin, and heard something on the line exhale—a sound like relief, or like water draining.

"Thank you," said the drowned man. "That's all I wanted. That's all any of us want. But some of the others want more, and they're coming up the hill, and Ulyana—you should know the door is open now. We can feel it. All of us. It's like spring."

The line went dead. Then lit again.

At 9:31, a woman who identified herself as Constance Brightwater—no relation, she said, and then laughed, and the laugh was his grandmother's laugh, which he had heard exactly twice in his life, both times in dreams—described the interior of the house he'd grown up in, room by room, including the water stain on the ceiling of his childhood bedroom shaped like a running fox. He had never told anyone about the fox. He had forgotten the fox himself until she said it, and then it came back whole, and he sat in the studio with his hand pressed over his mouth, logging the call in shaking handwriting: *hoax — impossible detail — verify.*

At 10:04, a child's voice asked him if he still slept with the window cracked in winter because he liked the cold on his knuckles. He did. No one alive knew that.

By midnight there were forty-one calls logged. By one, the switchboard was full and stayed full, every line held open simultaneously, a chorus of the patient dead breathing softly into the station like fog pressing against glass. They weren't asking for help anymore. That was the thing he couldn't explain away, the crack running through the whole fortress: none of them were asking for help. They were asking for him. By name. By childhood. By the exact weight of the griefs he'd never spoken aloud.

And beneath the voices, underneath it all, he could feel the door. Twenty miles of snow between him and the homestead hill, and he could feel it the way you feel a tooth you've bitten wrong—warm, waiting, sealed from the inside no longer.

* * *

Delia Marsh came into the station at 3:40 in the morning, and she came in the way floodwater comes under a door—all at once and silently, appearing in the lobby without the bell above the entrance ever ringing.

Ulyana was on air with a rerun of organ music, mic hot but his mouth shut, staring at the wall of lit phone lines. When he saw her, he killed the music and opened the door, and she fell past him into the studio smelling of woodsmoke and cold earth, and she was talking before he caught her arms.

"—hallway, there's a hallway inside the hill, I'm not crazy, I know how it sounds, there's a *hallway*—"

"Delia. Sit down. Breathe."

"They went in." Her teeth were chattering. Her gloves were gone; her bare hands were filthy to the wrist, and the nails were split, and there were splinters in them—black splinters, dense as tar. "Tom said we should cover it back up, just shovel the dirt back over and walk away, and Ray laughed and said let's at least look, and the door—it wasn't locked, Ulyana. It was sealed from the inside, but it wasn't *locked*. It opened like it had been waiting for someone to try the handle. Warm air came out. Warm air, in January, out of a hole in the ground, and it smelled like—" She stopped. Her face did something complicated. "Like my grandmother's kitchen. Bread baking. My grandmother's been dead thirty years and it smelled exactly like her kitchen and I *knew* it was a trap, I knew it, and I followed them in anyway, because that smell—I would have followed it anywhere."

Ulyana poured her coffee from the pot that was always on, always terrible, and wrapped her hands around the cup. "Slowly. From the beginning."

"The hallway goes back farther than the hill is big." She said it flatly, the way you report an impossibility you've stopped arguing with. "That's the first wrong thing. The hill's maybe sixty feet through. The hallway—I walked five minutes and it didn't end. Doors on both sides. Old doors, new doors, some of them just outlines in the stone, like they hadn't finished becoming doors yet. And every single one has a name on it."

"Names," Ulyana said. His own voice sounded far away to him, as if it were coming down a long hallway of its own.

"Carved. Brass plates on some. Painted on others. Names and dates." She looked up at him, and her eyes were the eyes of someone who had seen the machinery of the world exposed and could no longer pretend it was anything else. "I looked for mine. Of course I looked. Everybody looks. Mine's there. DELIA MARSH, and a date that hasn't happened yet, and I didn't open it, I want you to know I didn't, I *ran*—"

"Whose names, Delia?"

"Everyone's." A shudder went through her whole body. "People from town. People I knew. Mr. Halloran who taught me driver's ed. My ex-husband. Kids. There was a door with a little brass plate, knee-high, and the name on it was a girl I went to school with who died of meningitis when we were nine, and the door was *warm*, and I could hear—" Her voice broke. "I could hear her in there. Humming. She used to hum. Nobody hums like that. Nobody else in the world hums that song."

The studio was very quiet. On the board, all forty-one lines glowed steady red, patient as coals.

"Three of them went deeper," Delia whispered. "Ray, and Tom, and the kid, Marcus. They started reading the names out loud, calling them out like they were checking a list, and the deeper they went the happier they sounded, and I called after them and my voice just—stopped. Didn't echo. The hallway ate it. And then I saw the door at the very end, bigger than all the others, oak and iron, and the crest on it, and under the crest, the name."

Ulyana already knew. He felt it arrive in him like cold water finding its level.

"Brightwater," he said.

Delia nodded slowly. "BRIGHTWATER. And it was the warmest door in the whole hallway, Ulyana. Every other door was cool, or cold, or barely anything. Yours was warm as a sleeping animal. And when I ran past it—when I ran past it to get out—" She set the coffee down because her hands had begun to shake too hard to hold it. "The handle turned. From the inside. Just a little. Just enough to make the latch click. Like something testing whether it was time."

Outside the studio windows, the snow had thickened into a white roar, and the wind had changed direction, and it was blowing uphill—toward the station, toward the hill, toward the door with his family's name on it—and somewhere out in that roaring white, Ulyana could not have said how he knew, but he knew: three men were walking home through the storm, unhurried, smiling, and they were never going to arrive.

Delia grabbed his wrist. Her grip was astonishingly strong, the grip of a woman who fixed her own trucks.

"There's one more thing," she said. "The date on your door. Under the name. I memorized it because it scared me worse than anything else in that hallway, and I need you to tell me I'm wrong, I need you to tell me I misread it in the dark."

"What was the date?"

"Tomorrow," Delia Marsh said. "The date carved under BRIGHTWATER is tomorrow."
