## 12. Voices on the Line

The generator coughed twice before it caught, and then the station hummed with that low, stubborn drone Ulyana had come to think of as a heartbeat. Outside the glass, the snow fell without wind—straight down, unhurried, as if the sky were emptying itself deliberately, one white grain at a time. And beyond the snow, at the edge of the parking lot where the floodlight gave out, they stood. The dead. Dozens of them now, maybe more, arranged in no order he could discern, faces tilted up toward the transmitter tower like sunflowers toward a sun that had never done anything for them.

Ulyana had stopped counting at forty.

He sat in the broadcast chair with his hands flat on the console, the ON AIR light burning red above him, and he talked. He had been talking for three hours. He told Vesper Hollow about the ledger Maren had found, about the Arrangement, about the door beneath the homestead and the thing his family had fed for two hundred years with other people's remembering. He spoke in the calm, level voice he'd used for ten years of night shifts, and only he knew how much of that calm was performance, how much was prayer.

"—and if you're hearing this," he said, "stay indoors. Don't answer knocking. Don't open for anyone you love, because right now anyone you love may not be anyone you love at all. It can only wear what you remember. So—" his voice caught, just slightly, on the next words, "—so maybe tonight is a bad night to be someone who's remembered well."

The board lit up.

Not one line. Not two. All of them. Every button on the switchboard flared amber at once, and the console began to shudder faintly under his palms, like a dog dreaming, like something beneath the floor turning over in its sleep. The call counter—the little digital readout that had never in ten years climbed past nine—rolled upward past thirty, past sixty, past a hundred, digits flickering so fast they blurred into a single glowing smear.

"Maren," he said quietly, not taking his eyes off it.

She was already there. She'd been sleeping—or pretending to sleep—in the break room, wrapped in her coat, and she came through the door with her hair loose and her mother's ledger clutched against her chest like a shield. She looked at the board and went very still.

"That's not possible," she said. "There are twelve lines coming into this building."

"I know."

"There are *twelve*—"

"I know, Mare."

The first line picked itself up. There was no click, no breath, no pause for connection. The voice simply arrived, filling the studio the way cold fills a room when a door swings wide—and it was not one voice. It was many voices speaking in perfect unison, layered like sediment: Edith Kroll's papery rasp underneath, the drowned boy Tomas Pell's wet gurgle threading through, old Henrik Voss's gravel, and beneath them all dozens more, men and women and children whose names Ulyana had logged over ten years in notebooks stacked three deep in the closet. A chorus with one throat.

**"Ulyana Brightwater."**

He reached for the fader and then didn't touch it. His hand hovered. Some animal part of him understood that touching the board while it spoke would be an answer, an invitation, a handshake.

"You're on the air," he said instead, to the room, to the town, to whatever was listening. "And I'm not opening any doors."

**"We know."** The chorus was almost gentle. That was the worst part. Ten years of whispers, of static-muffled names, of demands that had grown teeth since the excavation—and now, at the end, they sounded kind. **"We are not here to frighten you. We are here to make an offer."**

"Nothing you have is worth anything to me."

**"That isn't true."** A ripple moved through the unison, voices falling away until only a few remained, and those few arranged themselves into something almost like a single person. Almost like—Ulyana's stomach dropped—almost like her. **"You have spent ten years explaining us away. Grief. Hoaxes. Static. You have been very good at not knowing what you know. But there is one thing you have never explained away, Ulyana. One hole in yourself you have never filled."**

"Don't." His voice cracked on the word, and he hated it, hated how young it made him sound, how transparent.

**"Your mother died on a Tuesday in March,"** the chorus said, soft as snowfall. **"You were not there. You were on the air. You learned about it from a voicemail you played eleven times and then deleted. And here is what you do not have: her last words. The sound of her breathing at the end. What she said your name meant to her. We have all of it. Every moment of her that you missed, every moment of her that faded from you before you could hold it—we kept them. We keep everything."**

The studio seemed to tilt. Ulyana gripped the edge of the console. On the glass, his reflection looked gray and hollowed, a man-shaped absence.

"We buried her," he said. His mouth was dry. "I buried her. I said the words at the graveside. I remember—"

**"You remember the funeral. You do not remember her. Do you know what her hands smelled like after kneading bread? You did once. It is gone now—it went into us years ago, the way everything goes. Do you remember the lullaby she sang in the homestead kitchen, the one in the old language, the one she never sang for Maren because Maren had already left? We do. We could give it back to you. Note for note. Her voice, not our imitation of it—the true recording, kept safe in the dark for twenty years, waiting."**

Beside him, Maren made a small sound. He glanced at her. Her face was wet; she hadn't noticed herself crying. "Ulyana," she whispered. "Don't listen. It's building you a door out of her."

But he was already halfway through it. That was the obscene genius of it—he understood that now, even as he stepped across the threshold. The entity couldn't create. Edith had told him that, urgent and pitying, two nights ago. It could only take what was remembered, hoard what was forgotten, and offer it back shaped into a key. And every human being was a lock. Every grief was a door left off its hinges.

"What do you want," he heard himself say. Not *what are you*. Not *why*. Just the transaction, plain and naked, and some distant part of him registered with horror that he was negotiating.

**"Open the station door. Let us in. That is all. One door, one keeper, freely opened. In exchange, we return her to you—not a shape at your window, not a voice on your line. Memory. Real memory, poured back into you, hers and yours together. You will have her whole again inside your own head. You will fall asleep to her lullaby every night for the rest of your life."**

"And everyone else in town?"

A pause. The chorus hesitated the way a liar hesitates, the way his callers had hesitated for ten years whenever he asked a question they hadn't rehearsed.

**"The town has forgotten enough already. It will not miss what remains."**

"There it is," Ulyana said. He laughed—a short, broken sound with nothing in it. "There's always a price hidden in the fine print. Two hundred years of Brightwaters signing things without reading them."

He looked at the switchboard. All hundred-and-some lights burning steady now, patient, amber as candleflame. He looked at the front door of the station, visible down the short hallway through the studio's inner window. Snow had drifted against it in a smooth white ramp. Beyond the drifts, the dead stood in their silent congregation, waiting, and among them—his breath stopped—among them stood a figure slightly apart from the rest, smaller, gray-haired, wearing the blue coat she'd been buried in.

It wasn't her. He knew it wasn't her. It was built from the memory of her, assembled by something that had eaten her out of his head piece by piece over decades and saved the scraps. Knowing it changed nothing. His legs knew her walk. His chest knew the exact height of her shoulder.

"Ulyana." Maren's hand closed around his wrist, hard, her nails biting in. "Look at me. Look at *me*, not the door. Whatever it gives you back won't be yours. It'll be a leash. You'll spend the rest of your life doing whatever it asks to keep her voice fresh. That's what the Arrangement always was—that's what it did to Mom, don't you see? She traded pieces of us to keep pieces of herself. She forgot me so it wouldn't take her. She wrote you that letter instead of calling because she couldn't risk saying my name out loud." Her voice broke. "I got that letter, Ulyana. I've had it for a year. I came because of it. And you're going to stand up and walk to that door for a *lullaby*?"

"It's her voice," he said. It came out small. Childish. "Mare, I haven't heard her voice in twenty years. I don't—I can't even—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "I can't remember her face anymore. Just photographs of her face. There's a difference. There's a difference and nobody who hasn't lost it understands the difference."

The chorus softened further, if that were possible. It had found the wound and it poured itself into it like warm water.

**"Her face, Ulyana. You could have her face. You could have Tuesday mornings in the homestead kitchen, flour on her wrists, steam on the windows. You could have the day you were seven and the storm took the power and she held you in the dark and told you that some winters are sent to teach us what we cannot live without. Open the door. Let winter in. Be warm again."**

His chair scraped back. He was standing. He had no memory of deciding to stand. The hallway stretched ahead of him, twelve feet of linoleum between the studio and the front door, and it might as well have been the length of his whole life. Behind him the ON AIR light burned red, and somewhere in the town people were huddled around battery radios listening to him breathe, and none of it weighed anything against the smell of bread and the sound of her saying his name the way only she had ever said it—*Ulyana*, with the emphasis falling soft on the second syllable, like a hand smoothing hair.

One step. Two. The linoleum was cold through his socks.

Three steps. Four. Down the hall. The door's brass handle gleamed dully under the emergency lights. Frost had bloomed across the glass in fern-patterns, and behind the frost, close, closer than the parking lot, a shape waited in the blue coat with its hands folded the way she folded her hands when she was waiting for him to be brave.

He raised his hand.

"Ulyana, *no*—"

Maren hit the breaker.

She had found it earlier—she'd told him that much during the long afternoon, cataloguing the building like a soldier mapping a fort—the master cutoff for the phone trunk, a gray steel box bolted beside the electrical panel in the back hall, installed by a phone company that hadn't existed in thirty years. She threw the lever and the whole station seemed to exhale. Every amber light on the switchboard died at once. The chorus cut off mid-word, mid-*syllable*, and the silence that rushed into the gap was so total and so sudden that Ulyana heard his own blood moving.

His hand hung in the air six inches from the handle.

Cold radiated off the door like heat off a stove. Through the frosted glass, the shape in the blue coat had not moved—but its stillness had changed quality. It was no longer patient. It was *attentive*.

"Step away from the door." Maren's voice came from behind him, ragged, furious, terrified. "Step away or I swear to God I will knock you down and drag you back myself."

"She was going to give her back to me."

"No. It was going to give a *hook* back to you, dressed as her. Ulyana." Footsteps; her hand landed on his shoulder, turned him. Her face was blotchy and set, and she was crying and glaring at the same time, and God, she looked exactly like their mother when she looked like that, which was its own cruelty. "Listen to me. If it could really give her back—if it were really mercy—it wouldn't need the door opened. It offered because the door is the only currency it accepts. Everything it loves comes through doors. That's the whole religion of the thing. And you were going to open one."

"I nearly did." The words came out of him flat and strange, spoken like a report from someone else's life. "Mare, I was three steps from—I don't even remember walking. I don't remember deciding."

"I know." Her grip tightened. "That's what it does. That's what it's *always* done, to this family. It doesn't push. It just makes the door seem like your idea."

He looked back down the hall at the frosted glass. The shape was gone. Of course it was gone. It didn't need to wait anymore; it had learned what it needed. It knew now exactly how far he would go, and exactly what would stop him, and both of those facts were weapons it would use again before sunrise.

They walked back into the studio together. The switchboard sat dark and dead, a field of extinguished stars. The generator hummed. The transmitter hummed. On the console, the ON AIR light burned its steady red, and Ulyana realized with a lurch that he had never taken his hand off the fader, that the whole terrible negotiation had gone out over the airwaves, that half of Vesper Hollow had listened to their night-shift host nearly sell them all for a song.

He sat down. He pulled the microphone close. His voice, when it came, was wrecked and honest and better than it had been all night.

"If you're still with me—you heard all of that. You heard what it offered me and you heard how close I came. I'm not going to pretend otherwise. That's the deal tonight: no pretending. It knows what we love. It's been collecting what we love for two hundred years, keeping it in the dark under the hill, and it will offer every one of you your own private version of my mother. Don't open your doors. Don't open for her voice, for his laugh, for the baby you lost or the friend you buried. It can't build anything new. Everything it shows you is something you already grieved—which means everything it shows you, you already survived losing once. Survive it twice."

Beside him, Maren lowered herself into the guest chair, the ledger open on her knees, her finger moving down columns of handwriting two centuries deep. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking for something. And when she found it, Ulyana saw her go pale in the red light of the ON AIR lamp, and she turned the book toward him and tapped one line with a trembling finger, and the line read:

*The keeper who opens the door willingly need not be the keeper who closes it.*

Below it, in fresher ink, in their mother's hand: *God forgive me. I think it wants Ulyana to open it. It has wanted him since the day he was born.*

Outside, the snow kept falling, straight down, patient as a congregation. And on the dark switchboard, one by one, without power, without phones, without any connection at all—the lights began to come back on.
