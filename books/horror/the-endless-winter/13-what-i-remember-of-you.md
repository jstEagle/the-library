## 13. What I Remember of You

The phone lines were dead. Maren had cut them herself, both hands shaking, the pliers cold as riverbed stones in her grip, and now the silence where the switchboard had been was worse than the chorus of voices had been. At least the voices had been *something*. The silence was a held breath.

Outside the studio windows, the snow had stopped falling and started *hanging*—flakes suspended mid-air like the town had been paused, like someone had lifted the needle off the record. The dead stood in the parking lot in their rows, patient as churchgoers, their faces tilted toward the transmitter tower. Every few minutes one of them would shift, almost imperceptibly, the way a sleeper turns toward a sound.

Maren pressed her back against the transmitter rack and looked at her brother.

Ulyana sat at the broadcast desk with his headset half-off, one ear still covered, as if he couldn't quite bring himself to sever even that thread to the outside world. His face in the console's green light looked older than fifty. It looked *used*, the way a tool looks after a long job—worn to the shape of what it had been doing for ten years.

"They're not leaving," he said quietly.

"No."

"And the phones—"

"Are cut. All of them." She set the pliers down on the desk with a deliberate click. "They were talking to you, Uli. Not to the town. Not to the listeners. To *you*. So I took away their mouths."

He flinched at the childhood name. She watched him flinch and felt a small, ugly satisfaction, and beneath the satisfaction something softer that she refused to examine.

"They could still come through the door," he said.

"Then we'd better not be silent when they do."

* * *

It had been her idea, though later she would not be able to say where the idea came from—whether it rose from her own desperate animal mind or from somewhere older, some instruction written in a hand she hadn't read yet in the ledger's final pages. She only knew that when she sat down across from him, dragging the guest chair over the cables, and said, "Tell me about the winter we were nine," the studio's hum changed pitch. Just slightly. Just enough.

Ulyana looked at her as if she'd spoken in a language he'd forgotten he knew.

"What?"

"Tell me about it. Out loud. The winter we were nine." She pulled her coat tighter. The generator was holding, but the cold had found its way in through the walls, through the old building's joints, and their breath hung faintly silver between them. "The ledger says the thing under the house eats memory. Eats *remembering*. So we're going to remember. Out loud. All night if we have to."

"You think that helps?"

"I think," Maren said, "that when I was reading Mother's papers, the rooms felt colder wherever the writing had faded. And the entries in her own hand—the ones about you, about the calls—those pages are the only ones that are still crisp. Like they'd been *touched*. Like remembering something keeps it warm." She met his eyes. "So we keep things warm. That's all. We keep things warm until sunrise."

For a long moment he said nothing. The dead stood in the lot. The generator throbbed. And then, so quietly she almost missed it, Ulyana Brightwater began to speak.

* * *

"The winter we were nine," he said, and his voice found a register she hadn't heard from him in thirty years—the register of a boy narrating his own life to keep it. "The winter we were nine, the snow came so early that the pump handle froze before Halloween, and Mother made us carry water from the creek in dented pails, and you cried about it for three days until I showed you how to walk on the ice without slipping—lean forward, small steps, like you're apologizing to the ground."

Maren laughed. It came out of her like something dislodged. "You said that. You said, *apologize to the ground*, and I thought it was the stupidest thing I'd ever heard, and I did it anyway, and I never slipped once."

"Because you were stubborn. You'd have apologized to the ground all the way to spring rather than admit I was right about anything."

"I slipped once."

"You did *not*."

"February," she said, and the memory came back to her as she said it, arriving whole, arriving warm. "The ice over the deep pool, where the creek bends past the willow. I went through to my waist. And you—Uli, you lay down flat on the ice and crawled out to me and pulled me up by the wrists, and you never once said I told you so. Not once. Not even when Mother was rubbing my legs and I was screaming. You just sat there dripping on the kitchen floor, smiling."

He was smiling now. It transformed his face, took ten years off it, took the hollowed look out of his eyes. "I was so scared," he said. "I don't think I ever told you. I thought—I was nine, and I thought if I stopped pulling, you'd go down and the creek would keep you. I used to dream about it. The creek keeping you."

The studio lights flickered. Maren felt it—a faint pressure against the walls of the building, like a great ear leaning closer. But the pressure *slid off*. The hum steadied. The suspended snow outside seemed, if anything, to loosen, a few flakes completing their interrupted fall.

"It's working," she whispered.

"It's working," he agreed, and there was wonder in his voice, and fear too, because they both understood what it meant: that the thing in the dark fed on what went unspoken, on what faded, on the cold rooms of forgotten things—and that their mother had known this, and had chosen silence anyway, and had let her son sit alone with the dead for ten years rather than speak.

Maren's throat tightened. "My turn. The lullaby."

Ulyana's face changed.

"You remember it," she pressed. "Tell me the words."

"I haven't thought about—"

"*Tell me the words.*"

He closed his eyes. And in the green-lit dark of the studio, with the dead standing in their patient rows outside, Ulyana Brightwater began to sing. It was barely a song—four lines, a melody that rose and fell like breath, words half in English and half in something older that their mother had claimed was just nonsense syllables, just sounds to soothe a child. *Sleep, the hill is holding its breath. Sleep, the door is shut. What walks at night walks past our house. Sleep, my bright water, sleep.*

Maren sang the answering lines. She hadn't known she remembered them. They came up out of her like water from that long-ago creek, cold and clear and impossibly sweet: *Sleep, the hill is holding its breath. The keeper keeps. The keeper keeps.*

When they finished, neither of them spoke. The generator throbbed. Somewhere far off, muffled by the hill, by the snow, by the strange paused quality of the night, something made a sound—not a voice, not quite a groan. A sound like a very large door being tested in its frame.

"The keeper keeps," Ulyana said at last, hoarse. "She sang that to us our whole childhood, Mare. And I thought it was nothing. I thought it was *nothing*."

"It wasn't nothing. It was an instruction manual set to a melody." Maren wiped her eyes with the back of her hand, angry at the tears, angry at their mother, angry at the tenderness that kept welling up in her through all the anger like water through a cracked wall. "She was teaching us the Arrangement before we could read."

"And I stayed," he said. "And I kept it. Without knowing. Ten years of taking calls from the dead, and I thought I was just—broken. Just a man who couldn't stop answering the phone."

"You weren't broken."

"I was the *keeper*, Mare. She named me in the ledger and never told me, and I sat in this chair every night for ten years holding the door shut with nothing but my voice, and she let me believe I was sick."

The old resentment flared in him, and Maren felt her own rise to meet it—*and what about me, what about the daughter who fled, who you never once called*—but she caught it. She caught it the way you catch a door in the wind, and she thought: *not tonight. Tonight the wind wins if we let it in.*

"Tell me the first time you knew the calls were wrong," she said instead. "Not the first call. The first time you *knew*."

* * *

Ulyana was quiet for a long time. Outside, one of the dead figures turned its head—slowly, like a weathervane finding the wind—and Maren's stomach dropped, but it turned back, and she made herself keep looking at her brother.

"Third year," he said finally. "A man called, said his name was Tomás Reyes, lived out past the gravel pits. Wanted to dedicate a song to his wife. I played it. Standard call, standard night." His hands moved on the desk, straightening papers that didn't need straightening. "Two weeks later his wife called the station. Wanted to know who had taken the request, because Tomás had died before he could phone it in himself. He'd told her he was going to call. She'd been waiting for the show. And I—Mare, I checked the log. I still have the log. The call came in at 11:42. The coroner's report said he died at 11:15."

"Twenty-seven minutes."

"I told myself the coroner was wrong. They're often wrong, right? Time of death is an estimate. I told myself that for eight years." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and terribly young. "The first call I ever took, Mare. The very first one, ten years ago. Do you know who it was?"

Maren's heart went cold. "Who?"

"Mother. Her voice, clear as day, asking me to play something soft because she couldn't sleep." His voice cracked on the last word. "She'd been dead six days. And I played it. I played the soft song, and I said *goodnight, Mother*, on the air, to an empty town, and I went home and I didn't sleep and I never told a soul, because what kind of man hears his dead mother on the radio and doesn't—"

"Uli."

"—doesn't *say something*—"

"*Uli.*" She reached across the desk and took his hands. They were ice. "She wasn't haunting you. Listen to me. She was checking on her keeper. She'd made the bargain, she'd done the selective forgetting, she knew what was coming for you, and she couldn't stop being your mother even from—" Maren stopped. Swallowed. "Even from wherever the door leads. She called to see if you were all right. That's all that was. That's the truth of it."

He wept then, quietly, the way men weep who have had no practice at it—stiffly, as if the tears were someone else's, as if he'd borrowed them. Maren came around the desk and held him, this stranger-brother, this boy who had pulled her from the creek, this keeper who had kept alone for ten years, and she felt the studio steady around them like a ship finding its keel.

When he could speak again, she returned to her chair, and it was her turn.

* * *

"The first time I knew something was wrong," she said, "I was seventeen. You'd already left for the city. Mother had stopped singing the lullaby—I thought she'd just forgotten it, the way she'd started forgetting other things. Where she put her keys. The neighbors' names. My birthday, one year, and I was so hurt, Uli, I was so *cruel* about it." Maren looked down at her hands. "I didn't know she was paying. I didn't know every forgotten thing was a coin on the table, buying us another quiet year. I just thought my mother was letting me go."

"Mare—"

"Let me finish, because I never finished, not to anyone." She breathed. "I came home late from school in March. The house was cold—she'd let the fire go down again. And I found her sitting at the kitchen table with the ledger open, and she was reading it aloud. To herself. Every entry, every name, every date, going backward through the generations, and she was crying, and she didn't see me. And when she got to the end of a page she'd start it again. Like she was trying to hold water in her hands. Like she could feel it running out and didn't know why."

The suspended snow outside had begun to move again—falling properly now, in slow, heavy flakes, as if the night itself had been told a story good enough to let it rest. Maren watched it fall and kept her voice level.

"I never asked her what she was reading. That's my sin, Uli. Not leaving. *Not asking.* I was seventeen and I was angry and I walked past my mother while she was drowning and I went upstairs and packed a bag, and I told myself the town was small and the house was cold and she'd forgotten my birthday, and I left, and I let you be the one who stayed." She looked at him straight on. "So when you say I abandoned you to the curse—yes. Yes, I did. And when I say forgetting was mercy, I was lying to myself, because forgetting was just the family disease, and I caught it in the usual way. I caught it from wanting to be comfortable."

Ulyana reached across and gripped her wrist—hard, the way he'd gripped it in the creek, pulling.

"You came back," he said. "Thirty years, and the dead are standing in the parking lot, and you came back, and you cut the phone lines with your own hands, and you're sitting in the coldest room in Vesper Hollow telling me the worst thing you ever did. That's not the family disease, Mare. That's the cure."

The hum of the transmitter deepened, warmed, and the lights burned steady and green. And in the pause that followed—in the small, held quiet between two people who had finally said the unsayable—Maren heard it.

Not through the cut phone lines. Not through the door.

Through the *wall*.

A knock. Soft. Patient. Three raps, spaced like the opening notes of a lullaby.

Then a voice, muffled by plaster and lathe, sweet and familiar and impossible, calling in the exact tone their mother had used on winter nights when the fire was low:

*Ulyana? Maren? It's so cold out here. Let your mother in.*
