## 20. Sunrise

The storm did not end so much as surrender.

Maren felt it happen before she saw it—a loosening in the air of the studio, a pressure lifting off her chest like a hand withdrawing. The wind that had screamed against the transmitter shed for hours went quiet all at once, the way a crowd goes quiet, and then there was only the hum of the generator and the sound of her own breathing and, beneath both, the faint residual crackle of the open line where her brother's voice had been.

She looked down at the ledger in her lap. The final page was blank now. Not empty—blank, as though the paper had never held ink at all, as though two hundred years of entries had been drawn back into whatever mouth had waited beneath the Brightwater homestead. She ran her thumb across it and felt only smoothness, cold and ordinary, and that ordinariness frightened her more than anything the night had shown her.

Outside the station window, the sky was turning the color of old pewter, then oyster, then—the first time in what felt like years—the pale, unremarkable gray of a winter morning in Vesper Hollow. Light came up over the ridge behind the water tower and lay itself across the snow, and the snow was unbroken.

That was the thing she would remember later, when she could bear to remember anything: the snow was unbroken. All night the dead had stood in the drifts beyond the fence line, hundreds of them, faces tilted toward the tower like flowers toward a sun that wasn't theirs, and now there were no tracks at all. No prints leading away. No hollows where bodies had stood. Just a field of white lying smooth and innocent under the sunrise, as if the night had been a dream the ground had agreed to forget.

"Ulyana," she said, and her voice came out cracked, half-swallowed. "It's over."

He didn't answer.

* * *

She found him in the studio, still seated at the broadcast desk with his headphones around his neck and the ON AIR sign glowing its tired red above the glass. He was sitting very straight, hands flat on the console, the way he must have sat for ten thousand nights, and he was looking at the microphone with an expression she had never seen on his face—not exhaustion, not relief, not grief. Curiosity. The mild, patient curiosity of a man waiting to be told something.

"Ulyana."

He turned. His eyes found hers and they were warm, they were kind, they were his eyes—and there was nothing behind them. Not emptiness exactly. Emptiness would have been easier. It was more like a room after the furniture has been carried out: the shape of everything still pressed into the carpet, the pale rectangles on the walls where pictures used to hang. You could see where a life had been. You could not find the life.

"Hello," he said pleasantly. "Are you here for the tour? I'm afraid we're between programs right now."

The words went through her like a blade of ice. She gripped the doorframe.

"It's me," she said. "Maren. Your sister."

Something flickered across his face—recognition's ghost, a shadow passing over a window—but it settled nowhere. He smiled apologetically, and it was such a familiar smile, the one he'd used their whole childhood to soften bad news, that she nearly broke right there in the doorway.

"I'm sorry," he said gently. "I don't—I know I should know you. You have the feeling of someone I ought to know. Like a song I can't place." He touched his temple, embarrassed. "There's a lot like that, actually. A lot of songs I can't place."

* * *

She made herself cross the room. Made herself sit in the guest chair beside the console, the one where callers' families sometimes waited, the one she'd sat in just last night while he recited himself into the seal—every memory, every name, every shame, spoken aloud into the microphone in a voice that grew quieter and quieter and quieter until the last thing he said was *I had a sister and her name was Maren and she came back*, and then nothing, and then the door closed somewhere beneath the town with a sound like a book shutting, and the lights steadied, and the dead were gone.

She had thought, in the hours since, about what she would say to him. She had rehearsed truths. *You are Ulyana Brightwater. You kept this station alive through ten winters of voices from the grave. You gave yourself away so that a town full of strangers could keep their mothers' faces.* She had rehearsed all of it, and now, sitting across from him, watching him watch her with courteous blankness, she understood that every true word would be a wound. That truth, delivered to a man with no floor to stand on, would only teach him the depth of what he'd lost.

So she lied.

"You're my brother," she said softly. "You stayed."

He blinked. "Stayed?"

"When everyone else left Vesper Hollow. When it got hard." Her throat worked. "You stayed, and you took care of things. Of people. Of—" she gestured helplessly at the console, the tower, the town outside the frosted glass, "—all of this. This is yours. You built it."

"I did?" He looked around the studio with wonder, touching the fader knobs as though they might be hot. "It's a good place. I can feel that it's a good place." He laughed—a small, bewildered laugh. "Isn't that strange? I don't know anything and I can feel that it's good."

"Yes," Maren said, and her voice held, barely. "That's exactly who you are. Someone good, who doesn't need to know why."

* * *

They walked out together into the morning because he asked to see the town, and she could not refuse him anything.

Vesper Hollow was emerging from its houses like survivors of a flood, standing on porches in coats thrown over nightclothes, looking at the clean sky and the unmarked snow and each other. And here was the miracle and the horror braided together: they remembered. They remembered everything. Old Tom Vess, who had forgotten his wife's face three days ago mid-sentence at the kitchen table, stood in his yard calling her name, and she came out crying and struck him on the chest and then held onto him so hard his knees buckled. The Marsh family stood at the edge of the excavation site, whole again, weeping over the names of the workers who had walked into the hill—Delia among them, wrapped in a blanket in the bed of a pickup, staring at the sealed-over pit with the expression of someone who has decided never to sleep again.

Everyone remembered everything.

Except him.

A woman Maren recognized from the diner—Ruthie, Ruthie Ansel—came hurrying across the square, saw them, slowed, smiled politely at Ulyana the way you smile at a stranger, and then seized Maren's hands. "You're the Brightwater girl. You're home. Thank God you're home." Her eyes were red-rimmed and shining. "Last night—I heard it, we all heard it, on every radio in town, even the ones that weren't plugged in—he told us everything, and God forgive us, Maren, we'd started forgetting, we'd already lost—" She stopped, pressing her knuckles to her mouth. "Whoever he was. Whatever he gave. We'll carry it. We'll carry all of it."

"He'd want that," Maren managed.

"And your brother?" Ruthie glanced past her, kind and puzzled. "Is he visiting?"

"He lives here," Maren said. "He's always lived here."

"Oh." Ruthie's brow creased faintly, a hairline fracture of doubt, and then smoothed over, because the mind protects itself, because a town that had spent a week being eaten by its own missing memories would seize any explanation that let it stop looking. "Well. Welcome to him, then."

Ulyana shook Ruthie's hand and told her it was nice to meet her, and meant it, and Maren stood in the bright cold morning and understood the exact shape of the price. Not death. Death would have been simple. This was erasure with the body left walking around inside it—a man made of courtesy and warmth and nothing else, greeted by no one, mourned by no one, because there was no one left who knew there was anything to mourn.

Except her.

* * *

By afternoon the power was restored and the generator coughed itself into retirement, and Maren drove him back to the station because he kept drifting toward it, the way a compass needle drifts north. He moved through the building touching things—the record shelf, the coffee mug with the chipped handle, the wall of cassette logs labeled in his own handwriting, ten years of nights—and at each touch his face did the same thing: brightened, reached, failed.

"There's a name on all these boxes," he said, crouched before the archive. "Brightwater. Is that—is that mine?"

"It's yours," Maren said from the doorway.

"Why does it feel like reading someone else's mail?" He laughed again, that gentle baffled laugh, and stood, and dusted off his knees. "I'm sorry. I keep waiting to feel worse than I do. Shouldn't I feel worse? Everyone keeps looking at me like I've survived something terrible, and I feel—" he searched for it, honestly searched, "—light. Like a house after the movers leave. Isn't that awful?"

"No," Maren said. "No, it isn't awful. It's rest." And she thought: *you paid ten years of nights and one entire self, and if the ledger is gone and the debt is paid, then rest, please God, rest.*

But that night, going through their mother's papers one more time—looking for anything, any scrap, any photograph, any grocery list in his handwriting that might serve as a splint—she found a single sheet that had escaped the blankness. Their mother's handwriting, shaky, dated last October:

*If it takes him, he will not grieve, because grief requires a self to grieve with. That is the mercy in it. But someone must hold the other side of the scale. Someone must remember him enough for two. That is the second duty of a keeper, and it is heavier than the first, and no one warns you.*

Below it, one more line:

*Do not tell him what he lost. Tell him what he was.*

Maren sat on the floor of the dead woman's study and read it until she had it by heart, and then she folded it into her pocket next to the blank ledger, which she could not bring herself to throw away, because a blank page in that book was still a page, and pages could be written on.

* * *

At seven o'clock that evening, the station's signal light blinked on, and Maren walked into the studio and sat down at the console, and Ulyana followed her in and stood uncertainly by the door.

"Do you mind if I listen?" he asked. "I like it in here. I don't know why."

"Sit anywhere," she said. "You own the place."

She put on the headphones. She pulled the microphone close. And at 7:02 p.m., for the first time in eleven years, a new voice went out over the airwaves of Vesper Hollow.

"Good evening. You're listening to the night shift. My name is Maren Brightwater, and tonight I'm going to tell you a story. It's about a man who lived in this town his whole life. Some of you may have met him."

Across the studio, Ulyana settled into the guest chair with his mug of coffee, leaning forward slightly, his face lit with the particular pleasure of a man hearing the opening chapter of a good book.

"His name was Ulyana," Maren said, "and he had a laugh like a door hinge he never got around to oiling, and he was afraid of deep water, and he once walked four miles through a whiteout to deliver batteries to Edith Kroll because her radio was the only company she had left—"

And so it began. Night after night, story after story. The time he fell through the ice on Miller Pond and came up laughing. The way he said her name—*Mah-ren*, two syllables, the second one always softer. The letters he wrote and never sent. The calls he took from the dead for ten years and never once told anyone, carrying it alone, carrying it alone, carrying it alone, until the night he didn't have to anymore.

Some nights he interrupted with questions—"Did I really do that?"—and she answered, and the answering was its own kind of rite, its own kind of keeping. Some nights he cried without knowing why, and she let him, and never explained, because their mother had been right: grief needs a self, but warmth doesn't. Warmth can live in a furnished room or an empty one.

And some nights—this was the part she never said on air, the part she kept folded in her pocket with the blank page—some nights, near dawn, he would go quiet in the guest chair, and look at her with those kind, unfurnished eyes, and say, "Sometimes I dream about a door."

"Tell me about it," she'd say, steady, ready.

"It's black wood," he'd say slowly, "and it's warm, and I'm on the inside of it, holding it shut. And I'm not scared. I just think—someone should know I'm holding it."

The calls had stopped. Mostly.

But the board was lit now, one soft amber eye glowing in the dark of the studio, and Maren looked at it, and then at her brother, smiling without knowing why, and reached out and answered the line.

"Night shift," she said. "You're on the air."

"Anytime," she whispered.
