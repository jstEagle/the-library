## 9. The Rite of Cold Spring

The belfry stairs had never seemed so long.

Tamsin climbed them with Wren's hand crushed in theirs, and the wood groaned beneath their feet like something waking. Above them, through the louvers, the ninth ring was still dying away—a sound that had no business existing, that had been scrap metal in 1911, that rang anyway because Cold Spring had always paid its debts in other people's voices.

Below, in the nave, the council had Wren's mother's chair—the one they'd carried up from the Ashby house, the one Ruth Okafor had kept in her storeroom all these years without ever saying why. Tamsin had seen it through the window as they ran: Dr. Pell standing at the rope, Mayor Hensley holding the ledger open to a page that was not Abigail Crane's handwriting but older, wetter, as though the ink had been bled rather than written. And Ruth, gentle Ruth, who had slipped extra biscuits into Tamsin's coat pocket every winter for twenty years, kneeling beside the boy with a length of bell-cord in her hands, tying his wrists with the tenderness of a nurse arranging a shroud.

*They were going to feed him.* Four years old when it took his voice, nine years old now, and they were going to give it the rest of him because Silas Crane had died before he could be made useful, because the count had reached seven and the village was afraid.

Tamsin's legs wanted to stop climbing. Twenty years of not-noticing had built muscles of avoidance; the body knew how to turn around, how to log another coincidence, how to go home and make tea and let the world do what the world did. But Wren's hand in theirs was small and cold and trusting, and Edith Marlowe had counted down from one hundred in her sleep and stopped at zero with her mouth open on a word she'd never gotten to say, and Tamsin was done.

They came up into the belfry into air that tasted of scorched rope and old rain.

The space where the bell should have been was not empty anymore. It hung there like a bruise in the shape of a bell—dark, swelling, half-real, gathering itself out of a century of hunger. The scorched circle of rope on the floor had become a ring of ash, and inside the ash the boards were black and soft as grave soil. The thing below Cold Spring was close now. Tamsin could feel it through the soles of their feet, patient and vast, an absence wearing a hundred borrowed voices the way a crow wears other birds' feathers.

"Tamsin." Hensley's voice, from the stairwell—breathless, wheedling, the voice of a man who had talked his way through forty years of council meetings. "Tamsin, thank God. Hold the boy still, we only need him quiet until—"

"Until you bind him." Tamsin did not turn around. They stepped between Wren and the room, between Wren and the bruise-shaped dark, and heard their own voice come out level, the voice they used at deathbeds, the voice that lied gently: *it won't hurt, it won't be long, you're doing fine.*

"He volunteered," Pell said. He was coming up the stairs now, white coat gone, an old man in suspenders with a doctor's certainty. "That's the requirement, Tamsin. Freely given. We asked him."

"You asked a child who can't say no."

Silence on the stairs. In it, Tamsin heard the thing below shift—not moving, never moving, but *attending*, the way a spider attends to the tremor in its web. And beneath their feet, faint as breath, they heard the counting. Not numbers anymore. Names. A hundred years of names, murmured in a hundred stolen voices, and threaded among them, clear as a bell:

*Edith. Edith. Edith.*

Tamsin knelt and cut Wren's bonds with the small scissors from their breast pocket—the ones they'd used to clip sutures, to trim beard hair for the dying, to open letters they'd never had the courage to send. The boy's wrists were red. He looked up at Tamsin with eyes too old for his face, and he didn't run. He took Tamsin's sleeve in both hands and held on.

"I know," Tamsin said softly. "I remember it. Every word. I've had twenty years to learn how to listen."

They stood. They faced the bell-that-was-becoming, and they began to speak.

Not loudly. That was the first thing Silas Crane had whispered, in the last hour of his long dying, when his granddaughter's eyes had closed and the room had gone strange and still: *not loudly, girl. It hears everything. It's only ever needed one honest voice.*

"Abigail Crane bound you," Tamsin said, and the ash ring at their feet stirred like a sleeper turning over. "She bound you with a debt, and the debt was renewed every generation by a keeper who gave their name, their sight among neighbors, their voice. You have fed on Cold Spring for a hundred and fourteen years. You have taken the dying early. You have taken a child's words. You have worn my patients' voices like coats."

The dark swelled. From somewhere below—below the floor, below the hill, below the world—it spoke, and it used Edith Marlowe's warm rasp, and Tamsin's knees nearly buckled.

*Nurse Whitlock. You were always so careful. So quiet. Walk away, and I will take only the guilty. The ones who knew. The Hensleys and the Pells and the Okafors. You could watch. You could finally see.*

It was a good bargain. That was the horror of it. Tamsin stood in the ringing dark and felt the temptation move through them like fever—twenty years of rage at the complicit, twenty years of logging coincidences while the counting went on, and here was permission, here was *justice*, handed over by the thing itself. Let it eat the guilty. Let it eat them slowly. Tamsin would stand at the window and watch, and this once, watching would be right.

Wren tugged their sleeve. He held up his slate, and on it, in chalk worn to a nub, he had written the word he'd shown Tamsin in the reservoir's shadow, the word his mother had taught him before the thing below took her voice into itself along with his:

*Mom is in there.*

Tamsin closed their eyes. Opened them.

"No," they said. "You don't get to choose your meal. That's not how debts work. That's how *feeding* works, and I am done feeding you."

They spoke the rite.

Silas had given it to them in fragments, between the sixes and the fives, his voice failing like a tide going out: words in no language Tamsin knew, transcribed phonetically in the margins of a hospice chart, syllables that tasted of iron and well water and the cold spring the village was named for—the spring that had never frozen, the spring that had sung under the founders' first winter, the spring they had capped and buried and built their church above. Tamsin's tongue found the shapes of the words the way a hand finds a banister in the dark. They were not prayers. They were arithmetic. They were the closing of an account.

And the belfry changed.

The bruise-shape of the bell solidified, bronze pouring out of nothing, metal remembering itself—and Tamsin understood, with the last calm part of their mind, why the rite required a keeper. The bell was not a warning. The bell was the *lock*. It could only exist while someone held the door, and the door could only be held by a voice given freely, and a hundred years of keepers had given theirs to renew the debt instead of closing it because closing it meant—

Tamsin didn't finish the thought. There wasn't time, and it wouldn't have mattered.

"—and I give mine freely," they said, and their voice cracked on *freely*, and they said it again, louder, and the third time it came out whole. "Not to renew. To close. Take back what you're owed—but take it from me. Every voice you hold. Every name. Give them back to the air, and take me in trade, keeper for keeper, debt for debt, and let the count end at zero with no one after it."

The thing below went very still.

Then it laughed—with Hensley's laugh, with Pell's, with a dozen dead elders', with Edith's—and the laughter turned to something else, something like respect, something like hunger, and the bell began to swing though no rope touched it.

*Tamsin Whitlock,* it said, in Tamsin's own mother's voice, dead eleven years, a voice Tamsin had mourned and missed and would now hear again only as bait. *Accepted.*

The bell rang.

Once.

The sound went through Tamsin like cold water through cloth, and then the voices came—all of them, a century of them, pouring up through the floorboards and into Tamsin's open throat. Edith's warm rasp. Wren's mother, singing something low and sweet. Silas Crane, mid-sentence. Abigail Crane herself, reciting the rite in the original tongue, and Tamsin's mouth moved with hers, twin voices in one throat. Farmers and shopkeepers and children and the nameless first keeper whose name had been spent so long ago even the Ledger had lost it. They poured in and Tamsin poured out—gave their own voice as the vessel, hollowed themselves to hold them, felt their name dissolve off the surface of the world like frost off a warming pane. Somewhere below, in the nave, Mayor Hensley opened his mouth to shout an order and produced nothing at all.

The bell rang once more—its true toll, its only toll—and shattered.

Not into fragments. Into *sound*. A hundred years of muffled ringing released in a single instant, every suppressed peal of 1911 and every silenced alarm since, and the noise of it went out over Cold Spring like a wave going over a seawall, and where it passed, things ended. The count ended. The debt ended. Down in the drained reservoir, in the ritual ring, the vast patient absence met the one thing it had never been offered—a keeper who chose the closing over the keeping—and it ended too, quietly, the way a held breath ends.

Tamsin fell to their knees in the ash ring.

The belfry was ordinary. Morning light through the louvers, dust turning gold, pigeons startled onto the roof. No bell. No bruise-dark. Just a scorched circle of rope, and a boy with a slate pressed to his chest, staring at Tamsin with his mouth working, working—

A sound came out of him. Rough, rusted, a hinge unused for five years.

"*Tam—*"

He couldn't finish it. His voice was still mostly gone, given back to the air with all the others, scattered over Cold Spring like seed. But he had the first piece of it, and his face as he clutched the slate was the single brightest thing Tamsin had ever seen.

Tamsin tried to answer and heard, instead, a chorus. Faint, layered, a hundred voices braided into one throat—Edith saying *there's a good nurse*, Silas saying *girl*, a woman singing. Their own voice was in there somewhere, deep down, one thread among many. Maybe it would surface someday. Maybe it wouldn't.

Below, on the stairs, three people stood nameless. Tamsin could see it now—see everything, the way the keepers must have seen, the way the invisible are cursed to watch. Hensley groping at his own mouth like a man searching pockets for keys he'd sold. Ruth Okafor weeping without a name to weep for. Pell mouthing *doctor, I'm Doctor Pell, I am* to no one, and the words falling off him like water off glass.

The village owed them nothing now. Neither did anyone else.

Wren took Tamsin's hand—the same grip, small and cold and no longer afraid—and together they started down the stairs, past the hollow elders, out into the morning, toward the reservoir, where a woman's voice was waiting in the wind to be told it was free.

Behind them, in the empty belfry, the scorched circle of rope lay quiet.

And far out over the hills, so faint it might have been memory, something answered Wren's broken word with a single soft chime.
