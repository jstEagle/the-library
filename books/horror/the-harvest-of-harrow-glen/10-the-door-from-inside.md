## 10. The Door From Inside

The light was going out of the Glen the way it always did at the end of October—not fading but draining, as if the day were a basin and someone had pulled its plug. Juniper parked at the mouth of the old farm lane because the lane itself had been chewed to mud by the county's trucks, and he stood a moment with his hands on the warm hood, letting the engine tick down, letting himself pretend—just for the length of a cigarette he didn't light—that he had come out here for any reason other than the one he had.

He had told Dale he was checking on property. He had told himself the same. Both of them had let it stand, the way the town had let a hundred things stand, and he was beginning to understand that this was how Harrow Glen worked: not by lying, but by agreeing not to finish sentences.

The trench ran along the south slope of the hill behind the ruined barn, forty feet long and eight deep, shored with plywood and steel posts. The excavator sat at its end like a dead insect, bucket resting on the earth, hydraulics slack. Ruthie's crew had left it that way three days ago, when the driller quit and the soil started answering back, and nobody had come to move it since. Rain from two nights ago had pooled in the tracks. The whole machine wore a skin of rust-brown mud, and the mud had dried in long drips that looked, in the failing light, like the thing had been weeping.

Juniper climbed down into the trench.

The stone door was set into the hillside at the bottom, half-cleared now, and it was bigger than he had imagined from Ruthie's phone photos. Granite, or something wearing granite's face, six feet tall and four wide, fitted into a lintel and jambs of the same gray stone that had no business being under a farm hill in the Ohio Valley. No quarry within two hundred miles cut stone like that. No quarry cut it at all, probably. It looked less like it had been built and more like it had been *allowed*—permitted to show its edge above the soil, and then buried, politely, by men who understood what they were permitting.

The door itself was flush, seamless, without handle or hinge or keyhole. The only interruption was the bolts. Three of them, iron, running through sleeves drilled in the stone, their throw-plates on the *inside*. He knew this because Ruthie had told him, and because the ends of the bolts protruded through the door's outer face by an inch each—stubs of iron worn round and smooth, like knuckles pressed against skin from beneath.

He had brought his grandmother's ledger in a plastic sleeve against the rain. He did not take it out yet. First he did what he had driven forty minutes to do and would not have admitted to anyone: he put his ear against the stone.

The granite was cold in a way stone shouldn't be in October, cold like a river bottom, and it smelled of wet iron and, faintly, of the studio—of carpet dust and hot vacuum tubes, of the transmitter room where he'd spent half his childhood. The tower's hum lived in his bones most hours of the day; out here, a mile from it, he felt its absence like a pulled tooth. But the stone had its own sound.

Scratching.

Not frantic. That was the thing he would try and fail to explain later, to Dale, to Ruthie, to anyone. If it had been frantic—scrabbling, clawing, the sound of something desperate—he could have filed it under animal, under raccoon, under pipes. But it was slow. Measured. It moved in strokes, in groups of strokes, with pauses between the groups, and the pauses had weight to them, the weight of a man lifting his pen.

It sounded like handwriting.

Juniper stood with his forehead against the granite and listened to something on the other side of four feet of stone write, patiently, in a hand he could not see, and he understood with a certainty that bypassed his mind entirely and settled in his spine: it was writing the same thing it had been writing for a hundred years. Over and over. Learning the strokes. Practicing the letters. The way a child fills a copybook, line after line, *the quick brown fox*, waiting for the day the words mean something.

He stepped back. His breath came out ragged in the cold.

* * *

He had spent the day, before driving out, in his grandmother's attic, and the ledger lay against his chest now in its plastic sleeve like a second heart.

Edith Lockridge had kept the broadcast logs from 1961 until her death in 2019—fifty-eight years of nightly entries in a hand that started round and girlish and ended thin and upright, like the woman herself. Juniper had grown up in that house. He had played in that attic. He had never once opened the cedar chest under the north window, because his grandmother had told him, when he was seven, in the only voice he'd ever heard her use that was not kind: *That chest is mine, Juniper. Some things a person keeps so the house stays quiet.*

He had thought she meant the house literally. He had thought a great many things.

The logs were meticulous. Weather. Signal strength. Duration. And at the bottom of every single entry, every night for fifty-eight years, the same sign-off, in the same careful script:

*Invitation maintained.*

He had read four hundred pages of it sitting cross-legged on the attic floorboards while the afternoon went amber around him. He had watched his grandmother's handwriting change through the decades—watched it wobble in 1968 and steady again, watched it press so hard in the winter of 1987 that the pen tore through three pages beneath. He had found entries where the log was the only thing holding her together, you could see it, the way the letters got very small and very precise on the bad nights, the way a person writes carefully when they are afraid that if they write carelessly the whole structure will come down.

And he had found the final entry. Dated three days before her death. The handwriting was different—not worse, not shakier, but *resolved*, the letters of a woman who had stopped bracing.

*The tower does not broadcast to the town. It never did. The town was always incidental. Ezra built it to speak downward, and we have spent a century pretending it spoke outward.*

*It cannot open the door. Only we can. That is the mercy. That is the trap.*

*It has learned our voices. It will use them. It is patient because patience is all it has ever been given. Do not give it more, Juniper. Do not be kind to it. Kindness is how the first word got through.*

*The invitation is not a ceremony. It is a habit. Every night we maintain it. Every night we could end it. I did not end it. I am telling you why: because I was afraid, and because the town was comfortable, and because the door was holding, and because tomorrow it would still be holding, and that is how a hundred years happens. Not all at once. One maintained invitation at a time.*

*If you are reading this, then the digging has found it, and you are the last, and I am sorry. I have loved you the whole way through, and I have lied to you the whole way through, and those were the same act. Forgive the dead their comfort. We bought it with your name.*

Juniper had sat in the attic until dark, the ledger open on his knees, the dust settling around him like a slow snow that had been falling for fifty years.

*We bought it with your name.*

He hadn't understood that last line yet. He suspected he would.

* * *

The light in the trench was nearly gone. He turned on his phone's flashlight and held it over the bolts.

Ruthie had said they moved. She'd said it carefully, in the tone of a woman reporting a surveying error, but she had said it: the bolt-ends that protruded through the outer face had extended since the first day of the dig. A quarter inch, maybe less. She'd measured against a photograph. She was not a woman who mis-measured.

Juniper crouched and laid the edge of his palm against the nearest bolt-stub. Iron, cold, wet. And then—there was no other way to say it, and he was alone, and alone was the only condition under which he could afford to say it—there was a *motion* in it. Not movement. Motion. The faint, subsonic tremor of a mechanism under strain, the way a doorknob hums when someone on the other side is turning it slowly, so slowly, with all the strength they have, and the latch holds, and they do not stop.

The bolts were being drawn. A fraction a night. From inside.

He did the arithmetic he did not want to do. Ruthie's photograph was eleven days old. A quarter inch in eleven days. The bolts were four inches long. Sixteen times eleven days. A hundred and seventy-six days, give or take—call it six months, call it less if it was accelerating, call it *spring*, call it *before the thaw*—and the door stood open on a hill above a town that had spent a century buying its comfort with his name.

His hand came off the iron as if it had been scalded, though the iron had not changed temperature. It was his own skin that had gone hot, a flush of something that was not quite fear and not quite shame. Because here was the thing the ledger had put its finger on, the thing he could not stop turning over as he climbed the trench ladder and stood in the last gray light with the dead machine at his back:

The door was not a wall. It was a *promise*, and the promise was his family's, and the thing on the other side had never once broken faith. It had stayed behind its stone. It had learned its letters. It had waited with a patience no living thing could sustain, because it was not living, because patience was the only currency it had ever been paid in—and every night, for a hundred years, some Lockridge had stood at a microphone and paid it again. *Invitation maintained.* Not an invitation to enter. He understood that now. An invitation to keep *waiting*. To hold the door shut from the inside, with its own hands, on its own prison, because the family had asked it to—had *bound* it to—and the binding was renewed nightly by a voice on the air.

And the moment a single night passed without the sign-off, without the maintenance, without the word—

The door would be his to open. Not the thing's. His. It could scratch and strain and learn its letters for another hundred years and never turn that iron. Only a Lockridge's hand, or a Lockridge's silence, could do it.

*That is the mercy. That is the trap.*

His grandmother had known. She had known and she had logged it and she had died with the invitation maintained, one more night bought, one more night of comfort charged to an account with his name on it.

Juniper walked the length of the trench slowly, the flashlight beam shaking slightly in his hand, and looked at the door the way you look at a sleeping animal you have been feeding for years and have just realized is not a dog.

* * *

The scratching had not stopped. If anything, as full dark came down and the stars came out hard and cold over the hill, it grew more distinct—or he grew more attuned, which amounted to the same horror. He sat on the trench's edge with his feet dangling over the door he could not see in the dark but could feel, the way you feel a stair that isn't there, and he listened.

Stroke. Stroke. Stroke. Pause.

It was not random. He was nearly sure it was not random. There was a rhythm to the groups—three, then four, then a long single stroke, then a pause—and the rhythm repeated, and the repetitions were not identical but they were *related*, the way a man's signature is related to itself across forty years, the same bones with the flesh of each day's hand laid over them.

He thought: *it's writing a name.*

He thought: *it's practicing mine.*

The night wind came down off the hill and moved through the dead corn at the field's edge, and the corn made its dry rattle, and under the rattle, under everything, he could hear the tower. A mile away, invisible, humming. He had grown up inside that hum the way a fish grows up inside water, and only now, sitting at the door it had spoken to for a century, did he hear it for what his grandmother's logs said it was: not a broadcast. A *conversation*. One side of one, anyway. The tower spoke down into the hill every night at 2 a.m., in the voice of whichever Lockridge sat at the microphone, and the hill answered in the only language it had—

In handwriting. In iron. In fractions of inches.

His phone buzzed in his pocket and he nearly went off the edge of the trench. Dale.

"You're still out there?" Dale's voice was thin, tinny, relieved. "Man, you've been gone four hours. I've been—look, there's a caller on the line. Says it's for you. Says—" A pause, and Juniper heard Dale swallow. "Says her name's Maren."

"Maren Halvorsen is dead, Dale."

"I *know*," Dale said, and his voice cracked on the word, and that was worse than anything the hill had done all night. "I know. That's why I'm calling you at a construction site in the dark instead of just—Juniper, she's *on the line*. She's holding. She says she doesn't have long. She says—" another swallow, "—she says the bolts are further along than you think. She says come home and *listen*."

Juniper looked down into the black trench, at the place where he knew the stone door stood, and the scratching, which had paused, resumed—three strokes, four, a long single one—and then stopped altogether, in a silence that felt attentive.

"Tell her," he said, and his voice came out steady, which surprised him, "that I'm on my way."

He climbed out of the trench and did not run, because running felt like something the thing below would enjoy, but he walked fast, up the lane, to the car, and as he pulled the door shut he heard—through the metal, through the glass, through the mile of dark hill between—the tower's hum change pitch. Very slightly. Like a throat clearing.

Like something, at last, about to speak.
