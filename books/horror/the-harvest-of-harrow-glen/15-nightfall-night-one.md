## 15. Nightfall, Night One

The call came at 4:17 in the afternoon, and it was Ruthie Vasquez's voice on the line, stripped of all its bureaucratic patience, which frightened me more than anything she actually said.

"Juniper. You need to come see this. And I need you to drive slow, because I don't want you arriving here already dead."

I went anyway, fast as the Glen's broken roads allowed.

The old Lockridge farm sat at the end of a track that hadn't been graded since my grandmother was alive—two miles of washboard dirt and volunteer sumac scraping the truck's flanks like fingers. The county crew had carved a wide trench along the hillside's southern face for the cell tower upgrade footings, and from the road I could see the machines standing in the long gold light of late October: an excavator with its arm folded like a sleeping bird, a skid steer, a flatbed stacked with rebar cages. All of it still. All of it wrong. A working site at quitting time should have been settling, ticking, breathing diesel and dust. This one held its breath.

Ruthie met me at the trench edge. She'd put on her hard hat for the occasion, I think, because she needed something to hold onto. Her face had the look of a woman who had spent the day arguing with herself and losing.

"It let go," she said, without greeting. "The trench wall. The whole south face, four o'clock this afternoon, no warning, no sloughing first—just *let go*, like a hand opening."

She turned and led me along the steel-edged lip to where the collapse ended.

I had seen photographs of the door in the crew's earlier reports—a stone lintel, a frame of fitted granite set into the hillside like a mouth in a face, buried under six feet of clay and root. I had told myself it would look different in person. Smaller. More geological. Less like what it was.

It stood exposed now in the raw wound of the collapsed earth, and the earth had not fallen *onto* it. That was the thing I couldn't stop seeing. The soil had parted around it, slumped to either side in two smooth shoulders, the way snow slides off a roof, leaving the door itself untouched, clean, framed in open air for the first time in a hundred years. The granite was dark with a century of damp, but where the light hit it, it gleamed like something recently licked.

The bolts were visible through the seam. Iron, hand-forged, thick as my wrist, driven across the gap between door and frame. There were six of them. My grandmother's logs had described seven.

"You counted too," Ruthie said quietly, watching my face.

"One's gone."

"It didn't break. Look." She crouched at the edge of the trench, careful, the way you'd crouch near a sleeping animal, and pointed. Where the missing bolt had been, the iron stub remained in the frame—and the hole in the door itself was empty, clean-rimmed, unsplintered. "That bolt slid. Out of its channel. Like a deadbolt turning. Something on the inside drew it back."

The wind moved through the sumac behind us, and every dry seed head rattled at once, and I felt the sound go through me like a shiver through a coat.

"How long since anyone worked the site?"

"Crew cleared out by three-thirty. My driller, Okafor—he quit at noon. Left his tools. Left his *truck*." She said it flatly, but her jaw was tight. "Juniper, I've been doing ground work for twenty-two years. Ground talks. It settles, it groans, it freezes and thaws. This—" she gestured at the door, at the smooth shoulders of collapsed earth cradling it, "—this isn't geology. The soil around that frame is soft. Turned. Like something's been pacing behind it. For how long, I don't want to guess."

I looked at the door a long time. The light was going amber and then copper, sliding down the hillside, and the shadow of the trench crept up the stone like water rising.

"Condemn it," Ruthie said. "Fill it. I'll pour concrete myself tonight if you say the word. You're the landowner. You're the last Lockridge. Say the word."

And here is the truth I could not tell her: I heard my grandmother's final log entry in my head, the one I'd found that morning in the attic, written in her small upright hand sometime in the winter before she died:

*It cannot open it. Only we can. That is the mercy. That is the trap.*

If I filled that door with concrete, whatever patience lived behind it would simply wait for the concrete to crack, or for some future generation to dig again, or for nothing at all—it had waited a century; it could wait under cement as easily as clay. But worse: the logs suggested the signal, the tower, the nightly voice of Lockridges going out over Harrow Glen, wasn't only feeding it. It was *pinning* it. Holding it pressed against that door from the inside like a hand flattened against a lid. And the door being exposed—the door feeling open air for the first time in a hundred years—that changed the arithmetic in ways I didn't understand yet and was afraid to learn.

"Not yet," I said.

Ruthie stared at me. "Not yet."

"I need until morning."

"Juniper—"

"Until morning, Ruthie. Then I'll give you your answer, and you can pour as much concrete as you want."

She looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw in my face made her take off her hard hat and hold it against her chest like a hat at a funeral. "I'll be at the motel," she said. "My radio's on channel four. If anything happens out here tonight—if you hear anything, see anything—you call me. I don't care what hour. I don't care how crazy it sounds." She paused at the truck. "And Juniper? Don't stand so close to it after dark."

I drove back to town with my headlights cutting through a dusk that seemed thicker than it should have been, and in the rearview mirror, until the hill swallowed it, the door stayed visible somehow longer than it should have—catching the last light like an eye that refuses to blink.

* * *

By seven o'clock the sky over Harrow Glen had gone the color of a bruise healing, and I was in the studio, and Dale was pretending he wasn't afraid.

He'd shown up at six with two gas station coffees and a duffel bag, and when I raised an eyebrow at the duffel he shrugged and said, "Blankets. Batteries. My dad's old police scanner. In case." In case of what, he didn't say, and I didn't ask, because we were both men who understood that naming a fear gives it an address.

"You could go home," I told him.

"Yeah," he said, and plugged in the scanner, and didn't leave.

At eight I took the air. I didn't have a show prepared. I never do, really—the whole architecture of *Night Frequencies* is just me and an open line and the town's insomniacs drifting in like moths—but tonight the silence between songs felt different. Charged. Every pause on the board seemed to lean toward me.

"Maren," I said into the second hour, more to the room than to the microphone. "If you're there. If any of you are there. I could use a voice that tells the truth right now."

Static. The long, oceanic static that had become, over these strange days, almost companionable—the sound of the dead holding their positions, patient as tide.

Then, faintly, the board lit. Line two.

"Juniper." Maren's voice, thin as paper held to a flame. "We're still here. Some of us. It's harder tonight."

"Why?" I asked, though I knew. God help me, I knew. "Why is it harder?"

"The door." A sound crossed the line that might have been wind or might have been her trying to steady herself. "A hundred years we've leaned on it from our side, Juniper. All of us. Every Lockridge, every soul that ever got pulled under or chose to stay and hold. We put our backs against it and your family's voice on the tower kept us strong enough to push. But tonight the door can feel the sky. Tonight it knows the world is right there, one bolt closer than it's ever been." Her voice dropped. "It drew back the first bolt weeks ago, while the crew was digging. Slow. So no one would hear the metal."

"How many left?"

"Five." A pause. "Four. Oh—" and her voice did something I had never heard a dead voice do. It cracked with fear. "Juniper, they're going quiet. One by one. Ada just now. She was talking to me and then she was—gone. Not gone like leaving. Gone like a candle pinched out."

"Where do they go?"

"I don't know. I think—I think it's eating them, Juniper. Their voices. It's been learning words off your tower for ninety-nine years, and now it doesn't need to practice anymore. Now it has a choir to swallow." Static swelled, receded. "Listen to me. Whatever calls you tonight that sounds like someone you love—test it. Make it prove itself. And whatever happens, do not go to the farm alone after midnight. Promise me."

"Maren—"

"Promise me, Juniper."

"I promise."

"And Juniper—" her voice was fading now, thinning like a signal losing altitude, "—your grandmother says the sentence is nearly finished. Ninety-nine years it's been saying it. One word left. Don't be the one who says it. No matter whose voice asks. No matter how much it hurts."

Then line two went dark, and the little red light died, and I sat in the studio with my hands flat on the board like a man steadying himself on a rail in high seas.

Dale was staring at me from the producer's chair, his face gray in the console glow. He'd heard none of it. He never heard any of it. To him the last five minutes had been me sitting in silence, listening to static, mouthing half a conversation with nobody.

"What happened?" he asked.

"They're going out," I said. "One by one. Like lights failing."

"Who is?"

I looked at him—at this soft, stubborn, terrified man who had packed a duffel bag and driven into a siege he couldn't even perceive, because I might need company more than I needed safety—and I decided he deserved the whole truth, or as much of it as I had.

"Everyone who's ever died in this town," I said, "is on the phone lines, Dale. And something underneath the old farm is hanging up."

* * *

Nine o'clock. Ten. Eleven.

I kept broadcasting, because going silent felt like surrender, and because Maren had told me once that my voice fed them—my honest voice, my true voice—and if they were failing, then the least useless thing I could do was talk. So I talked. I read the weather, absurdly, faithfully—clear skies, low of thirty-one, frost by morning—as though the town were a town still, as though tomorrow were a thing with shape. I played records. I spoke to the static like a man speaking through a cell door.

At 11:40, line four lit, and a voice said, "Juniper, it's your mother."

I knew it was not my mother, because my mother had been dead eleven years, and because the real one had called me *Junebug* only when she was angry, and this voice used it tenderly, and tenderness was the wrong key. But oh, the voice itself. The exact grain of it. Eleven years of forgetting, undone in four seconds, and my chest hurt like a bruise being pressed.

"Come to the farm, Junebug. Come open the door. It's so cold in here, baby. Your grandmother's waiting. We're all waiting."

"Prove you're her," I said, and hated how my voice shook. "Tell me what she called me when I broke my arm."

Silence. A long, thoughtful silence, the silence of something consulting files.

"She called you brave," it said at last, warm and wrong.

My mother had called me a *little idiot* and kissed the cast and cried in the hallway where she thought I couldn't hear. I killed the line with my thumb, and my hand wouldn't stop shaking for ten minutes, and Dale watched me and asked nothing, because somewhere in the last few hours he had learned that asking was a luxury the night didn't stock.

Midnight passed. At 12:20, line one flickered—an old man's voice, Mr. Aldous Petrie, dead nine years, wanting to chat about the weather, his cadence subtly, horribly perfect—and went dark mid-sentence. At 12:55, line three. At 1:15, a woman I didn't know, singing a hymn I did, and the hymn slowed as she faded, note by note, like a record player dying, and then nothing.

By 2 a.m., the board was black. Every line. The static itself had changed character—thinner, farther away, like an ocean retreating from a shore. And in that new quiet I understood what I was hearing: I was hearing the silence after the last lamp in a house goes out. I was hearing a building emptied of everyone who had been keeping watch.

"Dale," I said. My voice sounded borrowed. "How many lines do we have?"

"Six." He was leaning forward, scanning the dead board. "Why?"

"Because there used to be hundreds."

He looked at me then, really looked, and I watched him make a choice—the same choice he'd made at six o'clock with the duffel bag, remade now at higher stakes. He got up, dragged his chair around next to mine at the board, close enough that our elbows touched, and sat down facing the dark array of lights with me.

"Okay," he said. "So we wait together."

"You can't even hear them."

"Don't care." He cracked his coffee, cold now, and drank it like medicine. "You shouldn't be the only one awake in this town, Juniper. That's how they get you. Alone is how they get you."

Outside the studio window, Harrow Glen lay under a sky rinsed clean and vicious with stars, every streetlight burning, every house dark, the whole valley silent as held breath. Somewhere north, two miles past the sumac and the washboard road, a granite door stood open to the sky for the first time in a century, with four bolts left between the world and whatever had spent ninety-nine years learning to speak.

At 3:04 a.m., every light on the board came on at once.

All six lines. Simultaneously. And from each handset, faintly, in unison, came the same sound—not a voice, not yet, but the wet, deliberate clearing of a throat, a hundred throats, practicing.

Dale's hand found my shoulder and gripped it.

"Juniper," he whispered. "What do we do?"

I reached for the master fader, and my voice, when it came, was steadier than I had any right to expect.

"We answer."
