## 8. Ruth's Last Night

Ruth Calloway had spent eighty-one years learning how to be quiet in her own house, and on the last night of her life she was grateful for every one of those years, because the house on the other side of the fence had gone still in a way that made silence feel like a held breath.

She could see it from her kitchen window — the Reyes house, dark except for one upstairs room where a lamp burned behind frosted glass. The girl was still awake. Of course she was. On the longest night, nobody in that house slept easy. Ruth remembered the winters of her childhood, when the Voss family — before they were Reyeses, before the name changed like a coat passed down — kept their windows lit from dusk to dawn, every candle they owned, as if light were a wall and the dark were something with shoulders.

Her mother had told her once, in the flat voice her mother used for true things: *They pay so we don't have to. Be kind to them, but don't thank them. Never thank them.*

Ruth filled the kettle without turning on the light. Her hands knew the kitchen the way a tongue knows its own teeth. She had decided this afternoon, sitting in the cold on her porch steps watching the sun go down over Old Chapel Road, that tonight would be the night. She was not a dramatic woman. She did not intend to make it dramatic now. She had simply run out of reasons to wait.

The papers were in the tin box under the floorboard beneath the rocking chair, where her father had put them in 1963 and where no one had touched them since. She knelt — slower than she used to, one hand braced on the chair arm — and pried up the board with the flat screwdriver she kept for exactly this purpose, though until today the purpose had been hypothetical, a duty deferred the way you defer a letter to someone dying.

The tin was cold. Everything under the floor was cold; the house's warmth sat on top of things like frost on a pond.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay the covenant.

It wasn't a grand document. That was the thing no one believed when she tried to describe it, back when there had been anyone to describe it to. It was two pages of thin paper, browned at the edges, written in a hand that started confident and got smaller toward the bottom, the way handwriting does when the writer is tired or frightened or running out of the thing that makes words possible. The ink had faded to the color of dried blood, which she supposed was appropriate, though she'd never known if it was literal.

*In consideration of protection rendered,* it began, in her great-grandfather's hand, *the undersigned household consents to the following terms, binding upon all heirs and assigns—*

And then the terms. She didn't read them again. She knew them the way you know a scar. One memory per generation, freely given, freely forgotten — freely, that was the word the paper loved, *freely*, repeated like a hammer on a nail. A memory of a person, surrendered whole, so that the thing beneath Old Chapel Road would have something to eat that wasn't the family itself. And in exchange: the house would stand. The family would stand. Nothing on Old Chapel Road would come for them, so long as the feeding continued.

Her father had given up his brother, Walter, who drowned in the quarry in 1944 — except Ruth had found, decades later, a photograph of three boys, not two, and no record anywhere of a Walter Calloway, and when she asked her father he looked at her with the mild blankness of a man being asked about weather on Mars. *We were only ever two boys,* he said. And he believed it. That was the cruelty of it. The thing didn't just take the memory; it took the grief too, sanded the hole smooth, left the wood looking untouched.

Ruth had escaped by accident. She'd been away at nursing school in the winter her father should have paid, and he'd paid anyway, alone, and when she came home in spring there was a gap in the house she couldn't name — a bedroom kept shut, a chair no one sat in — and by the time she understood what had happened, she was already half outside the covenant's reach. Distance, she'd learned, thinned the leash. Not broke it. Thinned it. All her life she had felt it, faint as a wire against the wrist: the sense that some part of her belonged to the ground under Chapel Road, that when she died there would be an accounting.

Well. Tonight there would be an accounting, but it would go the other way.

The kettle began to sing. She poured the water over the loose tea her daughter sent from Portland — chamomile and something floral she couldn't identify and had stopped trying to — and carried the cup to the hearth, where she'd already laid the fire an hour ago and let it burn down to a good red bed of coals. She set the tin on the table beside her chair. She drank her tea slowly, looking out the window at the Reyes house, at that single lit window, and she thought about the girl.

Lena. That was the name she went by now, though the journals used another, older one, and Ruth had heard it whispered through the fence line on bad nights, in a voice that was almost the girl's voice but came from lower down, from under the porch, from under the road. The child had come to her door twice this month, asking questions with a notebook in hand, professional and careful, and Ruth had answered what she could and warned her about the knocking, and watched the girl write it all down as if dread were data. She was her grandmother's granddaughter, all right. Marta Voss had been the same — chin up, pen moving, terror converted into documentation because documentation felt like control.

Poor lamb. There was no control to be had. There was only the choice, and the choice was terrible either way, and no one had ever shown these children that a third door existed.

Ruth set down her empty cup.

*You can burn it,* her mother had said, once, near the end, when the morphine made her honest. Ruth had been fifty-three, sitting by the hospital bed, and her mother had gripped her wrist with surprising strength and said it plainly: *You can burn your copy and die free. But it takes everything, Ruthie. Everything it ever fed on comes back to be accounted, and you won't know your own name at the end. You'll die a stranger in your own head.* Then her mother had wept — not from fear, Ruth realized now, but from envy — and said, *I'm too far in. I gave them your father's face, you know. I can't remember his face anymore. I look at photographs and I see a stranger holding baby-you.*

Ruth had thought about it for twenty-eight years. That was not cowardice. That was arithmetic. There had been grandchildren to meet, gardens to plant, mornings she wanted to keep. But the garden was under snow now, the grandchildren called on holidays, and her heart had begun keeping its own counsel — skipping beats, murmuring warnings her doctor described with the gentle vagueness doctors use. She would not get another winter. She knew it the way animals know weather.

So: tonight. Before dawn, while the year was at its longest and thinnest point, when the veil between owing and owed hung slackest. Her mother hadn't explained why solstice mattered, but the journals all hinted at it — the bargain renewed at midwinter, the hunger peaking, the walls between rooms going soft. If the thing was busiest negotiating with the Reyes house tonight, it might not notice one old woman's small rebellion until it was already ash.

Ruth unwrapped the oilcloth. The pages crackled like insect wings.

She read the first line once more, for ceremony. *In consideration of protection rendered.*

"No," Ruth Calloway said aloud, to the paper, to the house, to the old hungry dark under the road. "No consideration. No rendering. We're done being considered."

She leaned forward in her rocking chair and pressed the corner of the first page into the coals.

It caught faster than paper should have caught — flared green-white, hissing, and the smoke that came off it smelled wrong, smelled like the cellar of the Reyes house, like wet stone and old pennies and something underneath both that her mind declined to name. The fire turned colors it had no business turning. The coals seemed to lean toward the burning page the way plants lean toward sun.

Ruth held the second page with the poker's tip and fed it in too, and as it burned she felt it — there was no other word — she felt it *leave*. A sensation like a long braid being unpicked from the back of her skull, strand by strand, each one tugging loose something she hadn't known she was carrying. Her father's laugh. The smell of Walter's — no. Gone already, whatever it was; she felt the absence of the absence, a socket where a tooth had been removed so long ago the gum had healed over. Names went. Faces went. Her mother's hands kneading bread. The name of the boy she'd kissed behind the Harrow's Bend church in 1958 — she reached for it and found smooth nothing, and laughed, actually laughed, because it didn't hurt, that was the miracle, it didn't hurt at all, it was like setting down luggage she'd forgotten she was carrying.

The fire settled back to ordinary orange. The smoke smelled like smoke again.

Ruth sat in her rocking chair, lighter than she had been since 1944, and discovered she could not remember her mother's face, or her own middle name, or why the tin box was open, or — she tested it gently, the way you test a bruise — she could not quite remember her own name. There was a shape where it had been. *Ruth*, maybe. Or something like Ruth. An old woman in a warm chair, that much remained. An old woman who had chosen, that remained most of all.

That was enough. That was more than enough.

Outside, far off, something under Old Chapel Road noticed. She felt it turn toward her house like a great slow eye, and felt its attention pass over her — and slide off, finding nothing to grip, no name to hold her by, no thread running back into the dark. She was, for the first time in eighty-one years, unclaimed.

Ruth Calloway — or the woman who had been — closed her eyes in her rocking chair, folded her hands, and let the long night carry her off toward morning, smiling.

* * *

Lena found her at seven, when the sky was still the color of dishwater and the frost on Ruth's porch boards hadn't yet given up.

The old woman looked peaceful. That was the worst part, or the best part — Lena couldn't decide, standing on the porch with her breath smoking, staring through the window at Ruth in her rocking chair with her hands folded and her face eased into an expression Lena had only ever seen on people in paintings. The paramedics would say heart failure, quiet, in her sleep. They would be right in every way that mattered to paperwork.

But it was the porch that stopped Lena's breath.

There, in front of the door, in a shallow iron dish that must have been brought out deliberately, lay a small drift of ash. Not wood ash — finer, grayer, with a curl of half-burned paper at the edge, and on that fragment, in faded brown ink, Lena could make out four words before the wind worried at it:

*...binding upon all heirs...*

Lena crouched. Touched the ash with one finger. It was cold — hours cold — and yet as she knelt there she felt something shift in her chest, some wire she'd lived with so long she'd mistaken it for a tendon, and the shifting felt like loosening. Like a knot worked halfway free.

Escape was possible. That was what the ashes said, plainer than any journal. Escape was possible, and here was the price, written in a dead woman's empty face: everything remembered, surrendered. Every name. Every face. You walked out free and you walked out blank, and the thing under the road couldn't follow you into a self that no longer existed.

Behind her, across the fence, in the house on Old Chapel Road, every mirror in every darkened room reflected the gray dawn light one full second late.

And in the bathroom mirror, in the house where Lena slept — though Lena was not asleep, and not home, and would not sleep again — her reflection smiled, and mouthed a single word it had been practicing for weeks:

*Soon.*
