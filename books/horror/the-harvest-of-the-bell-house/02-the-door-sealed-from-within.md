## 2. The Door Sealed From Within

Noor Coldharbour had a system for impossible things. He'd developed it over twenty years of night radio, where the impossible arrived nightly in the form of men who wanted to talk about their ex-wives at three a.m. You didn't argue with the impossible. You documented it, you thanked it for calling, and you took the next caller.

So at 9:14 the next morning, bleary and unshowered, he sat at his kitchen table with a legal pad titled *EVIDENCE* and wrote down everything.

*Call 1: Marta Ellery. Died Tuesday. Buried Saturday. Called at 2:07 a.m. Voice: hers. Mid-sentence: "—and that's why I never told Harold about the ring." Cut off by sound like a door breathing.*

He underlined *door breathing*, then crossed it out, then underlined it again. He was aware this made him look insane to no one but himself, which was somehow worse.

The pad had four pages now. Four callers, all dead within the last month, all buried by the funeral home on Cresset Street — his funeral home, the one that sponsored his show, which Noor chose not to read anything into because reading things into it would require believing something was happening.

"Keep it professional," he told the coffee maker. The coffee maker did not answer, which he appreciated.

Here was what unsettled him most, and he'd spent an hour circling it on page three: they weren't scared. Marta hadn't sounded frightened. She'd sounded *interrupted*. Like a woman at a dinner party whose story kept getting stepped on. And when she'd said *let me finish my sentence*, her voice had carried the specific patience of someone who had all the time in the world and knew you knew it.

At noon he drove past the Bell House.

He told himself it was a coincidence of route planning, which was true in the sense that all lies are technically composed of truths arranged badly. The old place sat on its hill above the harbor like a jaw missing most of its teeth, wrapped in scaffolding and orange mesh, and around its base, for the first time in forty years, there were trucks.

A sign staked into the mud read: **HARLOW & KASK EXCAVATION — HARD HATS BEYOND THIS POINT**.

Noor sat in his car with the engine off and looked at it for a long time. He didn't know why. That was the thing nobody told you about dread: it rarely came with an itinerary.

* * *

Denny Kask had been digging holes for thirty-one years and he wanted it on the record that he had never once found anything interesting in one.

Gas lines, sure. A 1974 Buick, once, buried nose-down like an ostrich with financial problems. But nothing interesting. So when the young guy on the mini-excavator — Tyler, twenty-three, phone permanently welded to his palm — started waving his arms and killing the engine, Denny's first thought was that the kid had hit another gas line and was about to have the worst day of his life.

"What," Denny said, climbing down into the pit.

Tyler didn't say anything. He just pointed.

They'd cut through the old foundation slab of the Bell House's east wing, and beneath it — beneath it, which was wrong, foundations don't sit on top of rooms — there was a void. And set into the far wall of the void, flush with stone that predated the house itself, was a door.

Iron. Riveted. About six feet tall, which meant whoever built the room around it had been either very tall or very considerate. It looked less like a door and more like something that had been *grown* into the shape of a door, the way a tree grows around a fence wire.

"Bolted from the inside," Tyler said. His voice had gone up an octave. "Look."

Denny looked. There were four bolts, thick as his wrist, thrown across the door from the room side. Which meant that whatever was behind the iron had locked itself in. Or been locked in by something with access to the inside, which raised questions Denny filed firmly under *later*.

"Get the county on the phone," Denny said. "And get out of the pit."

"Denny. Feel it."

"I'm not going to feel it."

"Just—" Tyler put his hand flat against the iron and immediately snatched it back, then put it back on, slower, like testing a stove. "It's warm. It's been down here since before the town had plumbing and it's *warm*, Denny."

Denny felt it. The kid was right. Not hot — warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, or a hand that's just been holding yours. And underneath the warmth, faint as a pulse through a wall, there was a vibration. A low, steady hum, rhythmic, patient.

It sounded, though Denny would never say this out loud, like breathing.

"County," Denny said. "Now."

That night he lay awake next to his wife, who slept like the dead, which was a phrase Denny decided he would never use casually again. Around two a.m., unable to stand it, he got up and turned on the radio low, the way he did most nights — Coldharbour's show, the only thing on after midnight that wasn't selling mattresses.

The host's voice filled the dark kitchen, warm and steady, and Denny realized with a small cold shock that the hum of the radio static was sitting at almost exactly the same pitch as the hum in the pit.

He turned the radio off. Then, after a minute, turned it back on. Because the kitchen had felt worse without it, and Denny Kask was thirty-one years too old to pretend he didn't know what that meant.

* * *

By the second night, Noor had stopped pretending the calls were grief hallucinations, mostly because grief hallucinations don't hold for call screening.

Rhee had run the board beside him all week, saying nothing, watching him with the careful expression of a person deciding whether to stage an intervention or start taking notes herself. Tonight she'd brought a thermos of soup and a legal pad of her own, which Noor chose to find touching rather than alarming.

Caller five was Gus Pell, dead eleven days, and instead of finishing the sentence he'd been cut off from — some story about a boat, a storm, a woman named Delphine — Gus said:

"Keep talking."

Noor blinked at the board. "I'm sorry?"

"You're doing fine. Don't stop. Keep talking, keep it asleep."

"Gus, I think maybe we should talk about what happened to you—"

"It's not asleep yet." Gus's voice was calm in the way of a man bailing water, unhurried because panic is a luxury. "You slowed down last night. Between the weather report and the second hour. There was a gap. It stirred."

Noor looked at Rhee. Rhee looked at Noor. On the board, the ON AIR light glowed red and patient.

"Gus," Noor said slowly, "what is 'it'?"

"The thing under the bell house. The thing your voice has been sitting on top of since before you were born." A pause, and then, almost gently: "You didn't know? They never tell them anymore. Cheaper that way."

"Who's *they*?"

But the line went soft — that breathing-door sound, closer tonight, fuller, like lungs filling — and Gus was gone, and caller six was already waiting, and caller six was Marta Ellery again, and Marta said, before Noor could speak:

"He's right. You slowed down. We can feel it when you slow down." Her voice dropped, urgent now, the first fear he'd heard in any of them. "Noor. Please. Talk until sunrise. Whatever happens, whatever you hear — do not go quiet."

And Noor Coldharbour, twenty years a professional, leaned into the microphone and began to talk — about the weather, about the harbor, about nothing, about everything — while somewhere beneath a derelict house on a hill, something that had been asleep for a very long time shifted in its sleep, listening.

Rhee wrote one line on her legal pad and slid it across the console so he could see it without looking away from the mic:

*The station's frequency. Check when it was assigned.*

Noor talked. And in the back of his mind, quiet as a bolt sliding home, a thought settled into place that he would spend tomorrow trying to dig up like a foreman afraid of his own hole:

*This show was never broadcasting outward. It was holding something in.*
