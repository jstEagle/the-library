## 3. What Was Invited In

The archive was behind the supply closet, which Noor had always assumed was a supply closet, because it contained a mop and a bucket and forty years of promotional coffee mugs from businesses that no longer existed. THE VOICE OF HARBORVIEW — WE'RE UP WHEN YOU'RE DOWN. A mug from a dentist who had, according to local legend, fled to Reno in 1987.

The mop was a lie. The mop was camouflage. Behind the mop, once you moved it — and Noor only moved it because the wall hummed when he pressed his ear against it, a hum pitched exactly like his own show, delayed by three seconds, like the station was listening to itself and enjoying it — there was a door. Not iron. Not warm. Just a plain gray door with a keypad that had no business being in a building where the fire code inspection had been conducted, apparently, by nobody at all.

He tried 1-2-3-4 because he was tired and it felt like the kind of night for it.

It opened.

"Of course," Noor said aloud, to the empty hallway, to the mop. "Why would it be hard. Everything else about this week has been so reasonable."

* * *

The archive was smaller than it should have been. That was his first thought: this room is bigger on the outside of itself, or I'm smaller than I used to be, and honestly either option explains the last six days. Shelves ran floor to ceiling, packed with reel-to-reel tapes, cassettes, DATs, minidiscs, and — near the end, in a plastic milk crate — burned CDs labeled in Sharpie with dates going up to last month. His show. Every show. Someone had been archiving him without his knowledge, faithfully, obsessively, the way he'd been documenting Marta Ellery's phone call.

There were labels on the shelves too. Host names, hand-lettered, decades deep.

GARRICK MOSS, 1961–1974.
DELPHINE OYELARAN, 1974–1990.
SAM TRENCH, 1990–2003.
THE QUIET ONE, 2003–2011.
BIRDIE HALCOMBE, 2011–2022.
NOOR COLDHARBOUR, 2022–?

The question mark had been written recently. The ink still looked wet. He touched it and his finger came away clean, but the question mark stayed wet anyway, patient as a held breath.

He pulled a tape at random from Delphine's shelf. The label read: *My name is Delphine Oyelaran. I am reading my name into the frequency. This is how the lock is fed.*

He fed it into the ancient deck mounted on the shelf's end — the deck was warm, also humming, also listening — and pressed play.

Her voice filled the little room. Warm, professional, a little hoarse with the hour. "My name is Delphine Oyelaran. My mother called me Della. I hated olives until I was thirty-four and then I loved them, and I want that remembered, that people change, that I changed. My name is Delphine Oyelaran. Take it. Take all of it. Keep them asleep."

Then a long silence, and then her voice again, but *flatter*, as if someone had taken the recording and wrung the person out of it. "Keep talking," she said. "Keep talking. Keep talking." For eleven minutes. Then the tape ended.

Noor sat down on an upturned milk crate and did not cry, mostly because he was afraid that if he started, something in the walls would start with him.

* * *

He worked through the night the way he knew how: methodically, one tape after another, cataloguing. Garrick Moss had given his name, his daughter's laugh, the smell of his father's workshop. Sam Trench had given everything down to his favorite chair. Birdie Halcombe — Birdie, who had trained him, who had retired to the coast, who sent him postcards of lighthouses twice a year — Birdie's tape was the worst, because halfway through it she stopped describing herself and started describing *him*. "The next one will be kind," she said. "I can hear him already, up there, being kind to strangers at two in the morning. Kindness won't save him but it will make the work easier."

Noor turned off the deck. His hands were steady, which felt like a betrayal of his hands.

"So every host," he said to the shelves, "has been feeding themselves into the frequency. Voluntarily. Piece by piece. To keep—" He gestured vaguely downward, toward the Bell House, toward the door bolted shut from the inside by whatever had been inside when they locked it. "—that, asleep."

The room answered. Not with words. With a warmth, spreading through the floor, through the crate under him, gentle as a hand on the shoulder. And then, in a voice assembled from every tape on every shelf — Delphine's vowels, Garrick's consonants, Birdie's kindness, and underneath all of it something older that made the fillings in his teeth ache — it spoke.

**You could stop.**

Noor didn't jump. He was past jumping. He was in the territory beyond jumping now, where things simply happened and you took notes. "Could I," he said.

**Walk away. Forget. You are very good at your job, Noor Coldharbour, but jobs end. Live. Marry. Grow old. Drink bad coffee and complain about it warmly. We know what you want. We have been listening to you want it for two years.**

"That's creepy," Noor said, "and also accurate, and I resent both."

**One voice holds the lock. Yours, or another's. Bring us another voice, and you go free. You will not remember this room. You will not remember Marta Ellery asking to finish her sentence. You will sleep, and it will be dreamless, and it will be yours.**

And here was the terrible thing, the thing he would never say out loud if he lived a thousand years: for four full seconds, Noor wanted it. Four seconds of imagining a life with the volume turned back down. Rhee's face unhaunted. Mornings that were just mornings. He stood in that warm humming dark and let himself want it, and the wanting felt like sinking into a bath drawn by someone who loved him.

Then he thought of Rhee saying, last Tuesday, over cold takeout: *"You've got the voice, Noor. I just press buttons. But God, what a voice."*

Rhee pressed buttons. Rhee kept him on the air. Rhee had been pressing the right buttons for two years without knowing what the buttons were for.

"No," Noor said.

**You hesitate,** said the thing, and there was something almost tender in it, which was worse. **That hesitation is enough. Bring us a voice.**

"I'm not bringing you anyone."

**You don't have to bring anyone. You only have to stop protecting them. Voices come to doors, Noor. They always have. All you must do is step aside and let one knock.**

"Noor?"

He spun. Rhee stood in the doorway of the archive, holding two coffees and a clipboard, hair doing its usual heroic defiance of gravity, looking at the shelves, the tapes, the wet question mark, and finally at him.

"How long have you been standing there?" Noor asked.

"Long enough," Rhee said quietly. "Long enough to hear it offer you the exit." They set the coffees down on a shelf, next to thirty years of dead hosts' names, and picked up a tape — Birdie's — and turned it over in their hands like it might bite. It might. Everything this week might.

"Rhee—"

"Don't." Rhee's voice cracked, and they laughed at it, that helpless producer-laugh Noor knew from every crisis broadcast. "Don't do the noble speech. I've heard your noble speech, I edit it out of your shows, it runs long." They looked up, and their eyes were wet and absolutely certain. "It offered you a door out if someone else walks in. Right? That's the deal?"

"Rhee, no."

"You know what I was before this job? Nothing, Noor. A person with good headphones and no one to talk to. You gave me a life that *matters*, at two in the morning, to insomniacs and truckers and grieving widows. Two years of mattering." They stepped further into the archive, toward the humming deck, toward the warmth. "So here's my counteroffer to that thing in the ground: take mine instead. I volunteer. I'm volunteering. Write it down, put it on the log, initial it — I know how much you love documentation."

And behind the shelves, beneath the floor, down under the Bell House where a door waited bolted from the inside, something exhaled — slow, satisfied, and horribly, patiently pleased — and began, in a voice like a choir warming up, to learn Rhee's name.
