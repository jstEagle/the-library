## 1. The Extra Chair

The house had been holding its breath for eleven days.

Paloma felt it the moment they unlocked the front door of their grandmother's flat for the first time since the funeral—the particular stillness of a place where someone had lived for sixty years and then simply stopped, the air gone thick and unclaimed, waiting to be told what it was now. Dust hung in the light from the landing window like something suspended mid-decision. The radiator ticked in the hallway, though Paloma had not yet turned on the boiler.

"Hello," they said, to no one, because you did that in a dead woman's house. It seemed polite. It seemed necessary.

The flat smelled the way it always had: lavender polish, black tea gone cold in a pot, and beneath all of it something older and harder to name—a smell like the inside of a piano, like felt and wood and time pressed together. Paloma stood in the entryway with a box of bin bags under one arm and their grandmother's spare key warm in their pocket, where it had lived since they were nine years old, and let the silence settle over them like a coat being placed on their shoulders by hands they could not see.

Grandma—*Obaachan*, when Paloma was small enough to be corrected; *grandma* after the correction stopped mattering—had died on a Tuesday in March, in her sleep, in the bed she'd been born closer to than anyone knew. Ninety-one years old. The doctors called it a good death. Paloma's mother had wept at the funeral in the controlled, efficient way she wept at everything, and Paloma's brother had said *she had a long life, didn't she*, which was true, and true things can still be knives.

Paloma had inherited the estate. Not the money—that was split three ways—but the flat itself, and everything in it, because Paloma was the one who used to visit, who used to sit at the kitchen table on Sunday afternoons while grandma peeled apples in one long unbroken ribbon and told stories that never quite ended the same way twice.

They started in the living room, because starting anywhere else felt like trespassing.

* * *

The photographs were in the sideboard, second drawer down, wrapped in tea towels the way grandma wrapped everything—eggs, ornaments, the good scissors—as if the world were full of things that might break if you loved them carelessly enough.

There were dozens of them. Black-and-white squares with scalloped edges from the fifties; orange-tinted rectangles from the seventies; glossy prints with dates stamped in the white border in red ink. Paloma spread them across the carpet in chronological drift, oldest nearest the window, and sat cross-legged among fifty years of family like a child sitting in the middle of a story.

Here was grandpa, young, squinting into a sun that had set before Paloma existed. Here was mum as a baby, held up to the camera like an offering. Here was the whole Ishikawa family crammed into the kitchen in 1968, everyone laughing at something outside the frame, grandma's face turned toward whatever it was with an expression Paloma had never seen on her in life—unguarded, wide open, lit from inside.

Paloma worked through the pile slowly, and it took them nearly an hour to notice, and when they noticed, they wished very much that they hadn't.

It was the dining table.

In photograph after photograph—birthday dinners, New Year's, a christening, an ordinary Tuesday in 1983 where someone had caught the family mid-meal and everyone looked annoyed except grandma, who was looking directly into the camera—the table was set against the far wall of the kitchen-dining room, and around it stood the chairs.

Eight chairs. Paloma counted them the way you count anything you don't yet understand: once, twice, a third time with your finger touching each one in the image. Eight wooden chairs, dark-stained, with curved backs, the set grandma had owned for as long as anyone could remember. Paloma had eaten dinner in that room hundreds of times. There were eight chairs. They could have drawn them from memory: four on each long side, none at the heads, because grandma said head seats were for people who thought too much of themselves.

But in the photographs, there were nine.

Not in all of them. In the ones from before 1974, eight chairs, always eight, and Paloma checked every single pre-1974 image twice, counting, recounting, feeling foolish and then feeling something colder than foolish. But somewhere in 1974—in a birthday photo dated in faded ballpoint on the back as *June '74, Ren's 7th*—there was a ninth chair.

It stood at the head of the table.

It was the same make as the others, or almost the same, but wrong in ways Paloma couldn't fix with words: slightly too tall, its back a little straighter, its shadow falling in a direction that disagreed with the shadows of the other eight. And no one was sitting in it. No one ever sat in it. In every photograph from June 1974 onward—forty-nine years of photographs, birthdays and funerals and Christmas mornings and one blurry shot of Paloma themselves at age six, gap-toothed, holding up a drawing—the ninth chair stood at the head of the table, empty, patient, present.

Paloma got up and walked into the actual kitchen-dining room, leaving the photos scattered behind them like shed skin.

Eight chairs. Four along each wall, tucked in neatly, the table bare except for a ceramic bowl of plastic fruit that had been there so long it had become furniture. Paloma stood in the doorway and looked at the head of the table, at the empty stretch of floor where a ninth chair would go if the world had made room for one, and felt the flat listening.

"Don't be ridiculous," they said aloud, and their voice came out smaller than they intended.

They went back to the photographs.

* * *

By evening the light through the landing window had gone the color of weak tea, and Paloma had catalogued forty-three photographs and found the chair in thirty-one of them. Every image from June 1974 forward. Without exception. Even the ones taken elsewhere—even a snapshot from cousin Aiko's wedding in 1991, taken in a hotel function room with rented chairs, where the ninth chair stood at the head of the top table, unmistakable, impossible, ignored by every guest leaning and laughing around it.

That was the part that made Paloma's skin prickle: not the chair, but the *ignoring*. In the wedding photo, a waiter stood directly beside it, reaching past it with a tray. Nobody looked at it. Nobody's body angled away from it. It occupied space in these images the way a stain occupies a ceiling—visible only if you went looking, invisible if you belonged to the life it haunted.

Paloma called their mother.

"Mum. When you look at the old photos—the dinner ones—how many chairs do you see?"

A pause on the line, long enough that Paloma heard the fridge humming in their mother's kitchen two hundred miles away. "What kind of question is that?"

"Just answer it."

"Eight." Another pause. "Four and four. Your grandmother never let anyone sit at the ends, you remember that. Why?"

"No reason. Sorting things. Talk later."

They hung up and sat on the floor of the dead woman's living room with her photographs fanned out around them and understood, with the calm clarity that arrives just before dread does, that they had seen something that was not for seeing. That the flat had been waiting eleven days for exactly this—for someone to come in and sort and count—and that it had known, perhaps for fifty years, that the one who would finally count would be them.

Because here was the thing Paloma had not said to their mother, the thing they barely admitted to themselves: they remembered the ninth chair. Not from the photographs. From *life*. From Sunday dinners, age six, age seven, sitting with their legs swinging off a seat too high for them, and sometimes—only sometimes, out of the corner of the eye, in the way you see your own reflection in a dark window—there had been a ninth chair at the head of the table. And grandma would catch them looking and say, in the voice she used for important things, *"Eyes on your plate, little one."*

And Paloma had looked at their plate. For forty years, they had looked at their plate.

* * *

The last photograph in the drawer was the oldest of the recent ones and the strangest: undated, unstamped, printed on paper that had yellowed to the color of broth. It showed the dining table on what must have been a winter evening, the lamp on, the window behind the table gone black with reflected lamplight. Grandma sat on the near side, younger than Paloma was now, her hair still dark, her hands folded in front of her as though she'd been interrupted mid-prayer. Across from her sat a man Paloma didn't recognize—young, hollow-eyed, wearing a cardigan buttoned wrong—and beside him, half out of frame, a small shape that might have been a child, blurred as if it had moved during the exposure.

At the head of the table: the ninth chair.

And this time, Paloma saw, the chair was not quite empty. There was something on its seat. A softness. A cushion, maybe, or the suggestion of one—or the suggestion of something that had learned to hold the shape of a cushion because cushions were what chairs were supposed to hold.

Paloma reached out and touched the photograph.

They meant only to tilt it toward the lamp. Their fingertip met the surface of the print, and the surface was cold—not paper-cold, not glass-cold, but the cold of a stairwell, of stone that has never once been warmed by sun—and the room tilted.

Not metaphorically. The living room swung sideways like a boat taking a wave, the bookshelves slewing, the lamp swinging on its cord though Paloma knew—it knew even as it happened—that the lamp did not swing. Their stomach dropped the way it drops on stairs missed in the dark. And rising through the vertigo came smells that did not belong to this room: chalk dust, dry and mineral, thick as weather; old carpet, damp wool and glue; floor polish; and underneath, faintly, milk that has been left out too long.

A sound came with the smells. Distant, rhythmic, muffled by walls: a counting voice. Small. Patient. Reciting numbers the way children recite numbers when they are passing time in a place where time is the only thing they have.

*...forty-six, forty-seven, forty-eight...*

Paloma snatched their hand back.

The room righted itself. The lamp hung still. The flat was silent except for the radiator ticking its idiot tick, and the photographs lay on the carpet in their careful chronological drift, and the last one—the winter one, the one with the man whose cardigan was buttoned wrong—lay face-down where Paloma's hand had knocked it.

They hadn't turned it over. They were sure they hadn't turned it over.

Paloma sat very still for a long time, breathing through their mouth, tasting chalk dust that was not there. Outside, the streetlight came on, and its glow crept up the wall and laid the window's reflection over the darkened glass, and in the reflection Paloma watched the kitchen doorway behind them, and the stretch of floor at the head of the table beyond it, where nothing stood, where nothing had ever stood, where nothing would stand tonight or any night, obviously, certainly, *eyes on your plate, little one*.

They slept on the sofa with every light in the flat burning, and told themselves it was because climbing to the bedroom felt like more stairs than they had in them.

* * *

Morning arrived gray and guiltless. Paloma made tea in grandma's dented kettle and drank it standing at the counter, and when the shaking stopped, they did what they always did with things that frightened them: they got organized.

They sorted the photographs into boxes—keep, keep, ask mum—and set the winter photograph apart, face-up now, because running from it had gotten them nothing but a stiff neck. In daylight it was only a photograph. An old one. A young woman, a tired man, a blurred child, a chair.

Except.

Paloma leaned close, breath fogging the print, and saw what the lamplight and the fear had hidden: on the back, in pencil, in handwriting they knew better than their own—grandma's handwriting, the careful schoolroom script she'd kept her whole life—was written a single line:

*We agreed to give it one and it agreed to stop at one.*

No date. No signature. Just that, and below it, smaller, pressed so hard the pencil had nearly torn the paper, as if the hand writing it had wanted the words buried in the page where no one would think to read:

*Someone broke it.*

Paloma stood in the kitchen of the flat they had inherited, in the room where the ninth chair had sat unseen for fifty years, holding a sentence their grandmother had hidden on the back of a photograph instead of saying it aloud to another living soul. The radiator ticked. The tea went cold. Somewhere far above them—above the eighth floor of this building, above the roof, above the sky that had any business being there—Paloma felt, with a certainty that had no evidence and needed none, a corridor. Long. Lined with doors. Quiet, in the way a classroom is quiet when the counting has stopped because the counter knows someone is listening.

They put the photograph in their jacket pocket, against their heart, where it lay cold as a stairwell stone.

Then they went to find the diary. Because grandma had kept a diary—blue cloth, brass clasp, kept in the sewing box on the top shelf of the wardrobe—and Paloma had read it once, at fourteen, and been bored by lists of grocery prices, and had not thought about it again until this exact moment, standing in a dead woman's kitchen, remembering that the diary began in January 1974.
