## 4. The Missing-Class Problem

The archive room lived in the basement of the old library, down a staircase that smelled of cold stone and the ghost of pipe smoke, and it was there—on a gray Thursday when the fog had pressed itself against the windows like something listening—that Tamsin Galloway found the first missing student.

They hadn't been looking for one. That was the thing Tamsin would remember later, the detail that made the discovery feel less like investigation and more like being *chosen*: they had gone down for something mundane, a box of past exam papers to calibrate their marking against Cold Spring's particular standards, and had found instead a shelf of yearbooks bound in green buckram, spines cracked with age, arranged in an unbroken line going back further than any school had any right to keep its records.

Tamsin had always been a fidgeter. Standing in a room of old books with ten minutes to spare, they did what any restless teacher would do: pulled a volume at random and opened it to the photographs.

The class of 1987 looked out at them from glossy paper, thirty-two faces arranged in three rows on the chapel steps, squinting into sunlight that seemed almost violent after the basement's gloom. Tamsin counted them without thinking—the habit of a teacher who took attendance by instinct—and then counted again.

Thirty-two faces. But the caption beneath read: *Form IV, 1987 — 33 pupils.*

Tamsin stood very still in the cold air, holding the book, and felt the first faint pressure of something that was not yet dread but was certainly its cousin.

*Probably a typo,* they told themselves. *Probably a boy who left at half-term. Probably nothing.*

But "probably" had never once survived contact with Tamsin's curiosity. They pulled the next volume. 1986: twenty-nine photographed, thirty listed. 1985: thirty-one photographed, thirty-two listed. They went backward through the decade, faster now, fingers smudging the brittle gloss, and the discrepancy held like a heartbeat—one, every year, always one, never two, never zero. A pulse. A tithe.

By the time they reached the volumes from the sixties, Tamsin had stopped telling themselves it was probably nothing.

* * *

The older records were kept in a locked cabinet whose key hung, unremarked, on a nail beside the door—as though whoever maintained the archive wanted the cabinet found, or as though no one had bothered to move the key in fifty years because no one had ever come looking. Inside were the ledgers: enrollment registers in fading iron-gall ink, the handwriting shifting decade by decade, Victorian copperplate dissolving into wartime haste dissolving into the tired ballpoint of the seventies.

Tamsin carried four of them to the long table beneath the single caged bulb and began, methodically, to build a list.

It was slow work, and it should have been tedious work, but there is a particular thrill that lives in the space between suspicion and proof—a hum in the blood, a narrowing of the world until only the page exists. Tamsin knew that thrill intimately; it was the same current that had carried them through a doctorate and a first novel nobody read. They gave themselves to it gladly, ruling columns in pencil, entering names and dates, feeling the pattern assemble itself beneath their hands like a photograph developing in solution.

1974: Margaret Ellery, enrolled September, struck through the following June. No note. No destination recorded. In every other case where a pupil left mid-year, the register said so—*transferred to Harrowgate*, *family emigrated*, *withdrawn, ill health*. Margaret Ellery's entry simply ended, the line drawn through it with a ruler, neat as a grave.

1961: Thomas Wren. Struck through. Nothing.

1952: Alice Fenwick. Struck through. Nothing.

Tamsin sat back. The name snagged somewhere behind their ribs—not Wren, surely not; Wren was a common enough name—but it was the other one that made the cold walk up their spine on small deliberate feet.

Fenwick.

*Coincidence,* they thought. *A common Cornish name. It means nothing.*

They wrote it down anyway.

The pattern, once seen, could not be unseen. Going back through the ledgers, decade into decade, century's edge approaching, the struck-through names appeared with the regularity of solstices. Always one per year. Always in the final term, in the weeks before graduation. Never remarked upon in the school magazines Tamsin cross-referenced—no memorial notice, no farewell, not even the small bureaucratic courtesy of *left us this year*. The students simply ceased, and the institution around them closed over the absence like water over a dropped stone.

One hundred and eleven years of records. One hundred and eleven struck-through names.

Tamsin counted twice because the number refused to be believed, and then sat for a long moment in the bulb's yellow circle, listening to the building breathe above them—the tick of cooling pipes, the settling groan of old timber—and understood that they were sitting inside the answer to a question the school had spent a century making sure no one asked.

*Where do they go?*

And beneath it, quieter, more terrible, the question they didn't yet know how to frame:

*Why does no one miss them?*

Because that was the true horror of it, Tamsin realized. Not the vanishings—schools lost children, tragically, occasionally, to accident and illness and the ordinary cruelties of the world. But those losses left marks. Grief has a paperwork. There would be inquests, notices, weeping mothers at gates. Here there was nothing. A hundred and eleven children erased with the tidiness of a clerk's pen, and not one trace of mourning anywhere in the record. As if the grief itself had been struck through along with the names.

As if everyone agreed, all at once, every year, to forget.

* * *

Tamsin might have gone on until midnight if the bulb hadn't flickered—once, twice, in a rhythm that felt less like faulty wiring than like impatience—and reminded them that the fog outside had thickened to milk, that dinner had come and gone, that they were new here, and new things that dug too deep had a way of being noticed.

They photocopied pages until the machine in the corner groaned, folded the copies into their satchel, reshelved the ledgers exactly as found, and climbed the stairs with the careful casualness of someone carrying stolen fire.

Mr. Fenwick was waiting at the top.

He was leaning against the doorframe of the archive stairwell with a mug of tea going cold in his hand, a thin, stooped man of about sixty with a face like a crumpled letter and eyes that had seen too many autumns at Cold Spring to be surprised by anything except surprise itself. He taught history. He had, according to the staffroom grapevine, taught history at the school since roughly the reign of Victoria, and he regarded Tamsin now with an expression that was not hostile, exactly. It was worse than hostile. It was weary.

"Long day," he said. Not a question.

"Archives," Tamsin said lightly. "I wanted to look at past exam standards. Get a feel for the place."

"Mm." Fenwick sipped his tea. Over the rim, his eyes did not leave Tamsin's face. "Exam standards. In the enrollment registers."

The stairwell was narrow and dim and smelled of stone, and Tamsin became suddenly, acutely aware of the weight of the photocopies against their hip, of how loud their own breathing sounded in the quiet.

"The registers were near the exam boxes," they said. "I'm curious by nature. Occupational hazard."

"Yes." Fenwick set the mug on a windowsill. Outside, the fog had swallowed the grounds entirely; the window might have opened onto the inside of a cloud, or onto nothing at all. "You are. I read your file before you came. The novel, the doctorate, the—" he waved a hand vaguely, "—the appetite. You're a digger, Ms. Galloway. Mr.—sorry, *Mx.* Galloway. Old habits." He didn't sound sorry. "Diggers are useful. Diggers are also, historically, a problem here."

"Is that a warning?"

"It's a weather report." He pushed off from the wall and came closer, and up close Tamsin could see that his eyes were the color of wet slate and ringed with a tiredness that went deeper than sleep. "Look. You're good in the classroom. The students like you—they like you quickly, which is unusual, and worth more than you know. So take my advice, which I am only giving once, and which I have never given anyone before, which should tell you something." His voice dropped, not to a whisper but to something lower than a whisper, a register meant to pass beneath the hearing of whatever else might be in an old building at night. "Stop looking."

"I haven't found anything to stop looking *at*."

"No," Fenwick agreed. "You've found a hundred and eleven things. I watched your light burning down there through the floorboards of my office. I know exactly what you found, because thirty years ago I found it too, sitting in that same chair under that same dying bulb, counting the same struck-through lines." He smiled, and it was the saddest smile Tamsin had ever seen on a living face. "Do you know what I did with my list?"

"You threw it away."

"I burned it. In the incinerator behind the kitchens, on a night much like this one. And then I went to bed, and the next morning I taught history, and the morning after that I taught history, and I have taught history every day since, and I have never once taught them the truth about this school." He picked up his mug. The tea had gone stone cold; he drank it anyway, grimly, like a penance. "There are teachers here who've given forty years to Cold Spring. Good years. Warm classrooms, bright children, decent lives. Whatever the ledger says happens in June, the other eleven months are real, and they're good, and the price of keeping them is that you don't ask the ledger questions."

"And the children?" Tamsin heard their own voice go quiet and hard. "The ones in the ledger. Did they get eleven good months?"

Fenwick stopped in the doorway. For a moment his shoulders sagged as though the question had physical weight, and he looked, briefly, like a man standing at the edge of a confession he'd spent three decades walking around.

"They get everything they're promised," he said at last. "That's the worst part, Mx. Galloway. Nobody here breaks a promise. The house keeps its word to the letter." He glanced back, and in the dimness his slate eyes caught the light and held it flat, like coins at the bottom of a well. "It's the fine print that kills you. It's always the fine print."

Then he was gone, footsteps receding up the corridor, unhurried, a man returning to a life built carefully on top of a grave.

* * *

That night Tamsin sat in their rooms above the east corridor with the photocopies spread across the desk, the caged bulb of the desk lamp throwing a warm circle over a century of vanished children, and tried to make the pattern mean something less than it plainly did.

There were rational explanations. There were always rational explanations; Tamsin had built a career on hunting them down and pinning them to the page. Runaways, perhaps, quietly managed by a school protecting its reputation. Expulsions laundered into administrative silence. A century of institutional shame, ugly but human, the kind of rot that grew in any organization old enough and proud enough.

Except.

Except that runaways left bereaved parents, and bereaved parents left paper trails, and Tamsin had checked—had spent the evening's last hour in the staff library pulling the local newspaper archives on microfilm, scanning decades of the *Cold Spring Advertiser* for the smallest ripple: a search party, a coroner's inquiry, a desperate advertisement, *HAVE YOU SEEN*. Nothing. Not once in a hundred and eleven years. The outside world's record matched the school's record perfectly, silence answering silence across the boundary walls, as though the vanishings happened in a place the newspapers couldn't see, or as though the forgetting leaked.

Except, too, the rhythm. Shame was chaotic; it missed years, doubled up, scattered. This was a metronome. One per year, final term, harvest-adjacent—Tamsin had checked the dates against an almanac, and the struck-through entries clustered in late September and early October with a precision that made their skin prickle. Every year. Like rent. Like a debt serviced faithfully across three centuries of headmistresses who had never, apparently, compared notes—or who had never needed to.

Like a ritual.

Tamsin wrote the word on a scrap of paper, looked at it, and laughed out loud at themselves, alone in the lamplight, because the alternative to laughing was admitting that their hands were shaking.

*Ritual.* What was next—sacrifice? Chalk circles and hooded figures? They were a novelist; they knew better than anyone how easily a pattern became a plot, how the hungry mind dressed coincidence in ceremony. Fog and old buildings and a colleague's ominous muttering: it was atmosphere, nothing more. Atmosphere was their trade.

But the laughter died quickly, the way laughter does in old houses at night, and what it left behind was the list. A hundred and eleven names in Tamsin's own handwriting, each one a child who had sat in a classroom like theirs, who had learned from teachers like Fenwick, who had walked these corridors in the last golden week of a final term and then stepped, somehow, out of the world.

Margaret Ellery. Thomas Wren. Alice Fenwick.

Alice Fenwick.

Tamsin stared at the name until the letters blurred. Then they pulled the oldest ledger back open—the one from the turn of the century, its pages soft as cloth—and ran their finger down the register for 1898, and there it was again, in faded brown ink: *Fenwick, A.*, enrolled, attended, struck through in June. And below it, in the same hand, a second Fenwick: *Fenwick, E.*, enrolled the following autumn.

Brothers. Or cousins. A family that had sent its children to Cold Spring for generations, and lost them here at the rate of one per lifetime, and kept sending them anyway.

Or—

Tamsin's pen hovered over the page.

Or a family that had learned, early, what the house demanded, and had made its peace with the arrangement. A family that served. A family that stayed. A history master named Fenwick who had burned his own list thirty years ago and had been teaching ever since, patient as furniture, watching every new arrival for the glint of a digging mind.

Watching, Tamsin thought, the way a shepherd watches a flock. Not for affection.

For the count.

The lamp flickered. Once, twice—in that same rhythm, that impatient double-blink, as though something in the wiring had been listening to their thoughts and found them interesting. Down the corridor, a door that Tamsin had locked before dinner stood open at the end of the hall, swinging gently on its hinges, showing a strip of darkness beyond.

Tamsin rose, closed it, turned the key, and stood a moment with their palm flat against the wood, feeling the grain cool and slightly damp, like the flank of something sleeping.

"One per year," they said aloud, to the door, to the dark hallway, to the house. Their voice sounded small and defiant in the silence. "Not me. I'm just passing through."

The house said nothing. Houses usually did.

But as Tamsin turned back to the desk, they noticed—for the first time, though they would wonder forever afterward how they'd missed it—that the class photograph from 1987, the one with thirty-two faces and thirty-three names, had been lying on the top of their photocopy pile all evening. And in the margin of the copy, where the original's buckram binding had cast a shadow, there was a thin white sliver of unexposed paper.

As if a thirty-third figure had stood at the edge of the frame.

As if it had been cropped out.

Tamsin looked at that blank margin for a long time. Then, deliberately, they squared the papers, switched off the lamp, and lay down in the dark with the list of a hundred and eleven names repeating behind their eyes like a rosary—*one per year, one per year, one per year*—and told themselves, in the firm voice of a skeptic running low on armor, that tomorrow they would ask Mrs. Aldous, straight out, about the enrollment figures.

Tomorrow.

Outside, the fog pressed against the glass, patient as ever, and somewhere far below the foundations, in the deep and dreaming dark where the school kept its oldest records, something turned a page.
