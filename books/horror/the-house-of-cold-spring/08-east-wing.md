## 8. East Wing

The rule was the third one on the laminated card, and Tamsin had read it so many times in the two weeks since Aldous pressed it into their hands that the words had worn smooth as river stones. *Do not enter the east wing.* No explanation. No exception clause. Just that single imperative sitting between *no recording after midnight* and *no answering if called by name after dark*, like a stone in a row of stones.

Tamsin had laughed about it with Fenwick over bad coffee in the staff room, holding the card up to the fluorescent light as if the joke might be printed on the back. Fenwick had not laughed. He had looked at the card the way a man looks at an old scar—familiar, unloved, not worth discussing—and said only, "It's easier if you don't." Then he had gone back to his marking, red pen scratching, and the conversation had closed like water over a dropped coin.

That was the thing about Cold Spring Academy. Every question Tamsin asked sank without ripples.

So on a Thursday evening in late October, when the fog came up out of the valley early and thick, and the students had been herded into their dormitories by prefects moving with that uncanny synchronization Tamsin still hadn't gotten used to, they stood at the end of the west corridor and looked down the length of the school toward the east wing, and felt the rule pull at them like a hook through soft flesh.

The corridor was empty. The radiators ticked. Somewhere far off, a door closed—not slammed, just closed, with the deliberate gentleness of something that did not want to be heard.

"You're being ridiculous," Tamsin told themselves aloud, because saying it aloud made it true, or at least made it audible. "It's a locked hallway in an old boarding school. There's probably nothing in there but damp and dead wasps."

But even as they said it, they noticed what they had been trying not to notice for days: the way the corridor lights dimmed slightly as they approached the east end, as though the bulbs were conserving themselves; the way the air changed temperature three steps before the junction, cool and mineral, like standing near a cave mouth; the way the floorboards underfoot sounded different there—denser, older, laid before the rest of the house, as if the east wing had come first and everything else had been built around it, apologetically, the way a body grows around a wound.

Tamsin went anyway.

* * *

The east wing door was oak, black with age, fitted with a lock of greened brass that looked Victorian and probably was. Tamsin had expected to need tools—a credit card, a screwdriver, something from the maintenance shed. They had even planned the theft, walking past the shed twice on Wednesday, cataloguing padlocks.

They needn't have bothered. The handle turned at their touch.

Not stiffly, not with the reluctant groan of a mechanism unused for years. It turned smoothly, oiled, *expectant*, and the door swung inward on hinges that whispered rather than creaked, and cold air flowed out over Tamsin's face carrying a smell they couldn't immediately name—candle wax, yes, and old paper, and beneath both something sweeter and more troubling, like flowers kept too long in a closed room, like the memory of flowers rather than flowers themselves.

"Right," Tamsin said quietly, to no one, to the dark. Their voice came back smaller than they'd sent it.

They had brought a torch, and its beam cut a pale cone into the gloom. Dust lay thick on the floor of the passage beyond—but not evenly. A path ran down the center of the corridor, swept clean, polished by feet. Recent feet. The dust on either side rose in soft grey drifts undisturbed for what looked like decades, but the path itself gleamed.

Someone walked here. Someone walked here often.

Tamsin stood a long moment with their hand still on the door, feeling the absurd urge to apologize—to the door, to the dark, to Mrs. Aldous—for coming in. Then they thought of Wren Halloway's face on that first morning, nineteen desks and twenty names, and the girl's hand rising in the third row, and the question she'd asked with such terrible mildness: *Why does the register have twenty names?*

And they stepped inside, and pulled the door shut behind them, which was either very brave or very stupid, and they would think about that later, much later, standing in other kinds of dark.

* * *

The passage ran straight for perhaps forty meters, lined with doors on both sides. Tamsin tried none of them. The torchlight showed brass plates beside each: *Music Room. Natural Philosophy. Chapel.*

Chapel.

There was no chapel on any plan of the school Tamsin had seen. The prospectus described the east wing as "disused staff accommodation, pending restoration"—a phrase that had always struck Tamsin as administrative for *don't ask*. But here it was, engraved into tarnished brass: CHAPEL. And beneath the word, almost worn away, a second line in smaller letters that Tamsin had to crouch and angle the light to read:

*Founded MDCCCXCVII*

1897. The year the school opened. The year, if Fenwick's grudging fragments could be trusted, when the founders—there had been four, once, though no one ever named them—had dedicated the place to something. Education, presumably. Enlightenment. All the things schools are dedicated to.

Tamsin's mouth had gone dry somewhere back along the corridor. They wiped their palms on their trousers and pushed open the chapel door.

* * *

The room beyond was larger than the corridor should have allowed. That was Tamsin's first coherent thought, and they held onto it because it was concrete, geometric, checkable—the chapel was bigger than the wing that contained it, extending back and back into darkness beyond the reach of the torchbeam, and no amount of squinting or reasoning made the proportions right. The walls did not meet the ceiling so much as give up trying. The air hung still and cold and tasted faintly of iron.

In the center of the space stood a circle of chairs.

Twelve of them, plain wooden things, arranged with their backs to the center, facing outward—no. Tamsin blinked and counted again, and the chairs were facing inward now, twelve seats around an empty middle, and they understood with a lurch that the circle had not moved; only their seeing of it had. The chairs were old but not ruined. The seat of the northernmost one was worn paler than the others, polished by use, and Tamsin found themselves thinking, unhelpfully, *that was someone's chair. Someone sat there every time. Everyone has a chair, and everyone knows which chair is theirs,* and the thought arrived with the weight of memory though Tamsin had never been in this room before in their life.

Above the circle, where a church would hang its god, the ceiling was bare plaster—except that the plaster was stained, in great spreading blooms of brown and umber, and the stains, if you let your eyes unfocus the way you do with clouds, resolved into shapes. Figures. Tall ones, standing in a ring, arms raised or lowered, Tamsin couldn't tell which, couldn't tell whether they were dancing or drowning.

"Don't," Tamsin said to themselves, and looked away, and their eyes went back anyway, the way a tongue goes back to a broken tooth.

On a small table beside the founder's chair—the worn one, the northern one—lay a book.

It was bound in leather gone the color of tea leaves, cracked along the spine, swollen with damp and age. There was no title on the cover. There didn't need to be. Tamsin knew what ledgers looked like; this was one; and some animal part of their brain, older than skepticism, older than language, advised them very clearly and calmly to leave it where it lay.

They picked it up anyway. Of course they did. They were a teacher. Curiosity was the whole disease of the profession.

The pages were heavy stock, cream gone ivory, ruled in faded violet lines. The handwriting inside was copperplate, beautiful, the kind of penmanship they don't teach anymore, and it began, on the first page, with a date:

*Michaelmas Term, 1897.*

Below it, a list of names. Eleven names. Each written in full, each followed by a small annotation in the same hand—*present*, *present*, *present*—and then, after the eleventh name, a twelfth line, ruled but blank, waiting.

Tamsin turned the page. Another term. Another list. Eleven names again, mostly the same, a few dropping away, new ones added. And always, at the bottom, that ruled blank line. Term after term. Year after year. Decades of lists, hundreds of pages, the penmanship degrading slowly from copperplate to a cramped modern scrawl, different hands taking up the work as the old ones set it down, generation after generation of someone—someones—sitting in these chairs and writing out the names of the school's children, term after term after term.

And always, at the bottom, the blank line.

Except not always. Tamsin's thumb stopped on a page dated autumn, forty-one years ago. The list ran its eleven names. And below it, in ink darker than the rest, pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages, a twelfth name had been entered:

*Margaret Aldous.*

Struck through. Not crossed out carelessly—struck through with a single deliberate stroke, and beside it, in tiny letters crammed into the margin, a note in that cramped modern hand:

*taken instead — E.V. — God forgive us all*

Tamsin stood in the cold chapel with the ledger open in their hands and felt the floor tilt gently beneath them, the way a ship tilts when you've been on land too long and the sea comes back to claim your balance. Margaret Aldous. Mrs. Aldous. The headmistress who smiled with warmth that never reached any particular destination, who handed out laminated rules and answered questions with pleasant nothing, whose name sat struck through in a founders' book like a debt forgiven by someone else's payment.

E.V. Who was E.V.? Whose name was not on the list at all, whose sacrifice had bought the future headmistress another sixty years of life, and what exactly had it bought her *from*?

Tamsin turned pages faster now, hunting, the torch shaking enough to make the light stutter. Names, names, names. And then they began to see it—the pattern they had spent weeks excavating from dusty archives, the missing student per year, unremarked, unreported—here it was in the ledger's own grammar. Every autumn list ended with a final name added in a different ink. Older ink. Darker. As if the pen used for it were kept somewhere else, somewhere special, brought out only once a year.

One name per year. One hundred and twenty-seven years of autumns. Tamsin did the arithmetic and wished they hadn't.

The last entry was recent. This year's list, eleven names in the current registrar's tidy hand—and below them, in that older, darker ink, still wet-looking though it had been written weeks ago, a twelfth name:

*Tamsin Galloway.*

* * *

The sound of the torch hitting the floor was very loud in the chapel. Tamsin didn't remember dropping it. They remembered the name, their own name, written in a book older than their grandparents, in ink that predated their birth, and they remembered the cold coming up through the soles of their shoes like the building breathing in, and then the torch was rolling in a slow arc across the flagstones and the light swung wildly across the walls—

—and showed them the drawings.

They covered the eastern wall, floor to vaulting, hundreds of them, pinned and pasted and taped, overlapping like feathers, like scales. Children's drawings. Crayon and pencil and paint, some on proper paper, some on the backs of worksheets, some on bits of cardboard torn from cereal boxes. Different hands, different decades—one in careful colored pencil, one in the furious scribble of a six-year-old, one done apparently in biro on exam stationery. A century of children, and every single one of them had drawn the same thing.

A door.

Not a doorway in a wall. Not a door with a house around it. Just the door itself, floating on the paper, rendered over and over in every style a child can manage: tall and narrow, arched and iron-banded, panelled and plain. Some of the drawings showed it ajar. Some showed it shut. In a few—these were the oldest, the crayon waxy and smeared—it stood open, and behind it the child had colored the space solid black, pressing so hard the paper shone, and around the blackness they had drawn small stick figures walking in, one after another after another, a procession of little people going into the dark, and above them, in the wobbling letters of someone just learning to write, a caption:

*IT KEEPS US*

Tamsin crouched and picked up the torch with a hand that did not feel entirely like their own hand. The beam steadied. The wall of doors stared back, patient as a congregation.

Every drawing was of the same door. And every drawing, Tamsin saw now, looking properly for the first time, had been pinned to this wall by adult hands—neatly, in rows, curated, preserved. Someone collected these. Someone wanted them here, in this room, facing this circle of chairs, a century of children's testimony arranged like evidence at a trial no one intended to hold.

The newest drawing hung at eye level, alone on a clear patch of wall, pinned with a fresh brass tack. It was in pencil, precise, unhurried—older than a child's hand, or younger than anyone's, impossible to say. It showed the door. Closed. And beneath it, in small block capitals:

*YOU'RE LATE.*

Tamsin knew those letters. Had heard that voice on a recording made in their own sleeping mouth, answering questions in their own cadence, patient and intimate and wrong. The voice had said it once already, weeks ago, on a crackling playback at three in the morning: *You're late.* Tamsin had told themselves it was a dream artifact, a hypnagogic echo, sleep-talk nonsense.

It was an appointment. It had always been an appointment. Their name was in the book, in the dark ink, and the door in the drawings was closed, and somewhere beneath the flagstones under their feet something that had waited a hundred and twenty-seven years for the founders to finish what they started had begun, at last, to count the days.

Behind Tamsin, in the circle of twelve chairs, the northernmost chair—the worn one, the founder's chair—creaked.

As if someone had just sat down in it.
