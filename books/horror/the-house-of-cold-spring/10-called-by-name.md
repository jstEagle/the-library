## 10. Called by Name

The ledger's ink had dried into Tamsin's thoughts the way rain dries into stone—slowly, invisibly, until the whole structure of them was darker for it.

They had copied the names out by hand that afternoon, in the staff room, on the back of a lesson plan: one per year, going back to 1897, each written in a hand that was not the founders' but belonged to the school itself somehow, a hand that changed with the decades and never changed at all. And near the end of the page, in the row for the current harvest cycle, in ink that looked wetter than the rest, fresher, almost embarrassed by its own newness:

*Tamsin Galloway.*

Not Tamsin. Not the initial they used on the class register, the neutral T. Galloway they'd asked everyone to use since their first day. The full name. First and last, spelled correctly, including the second *l* that even their own mother sometimes dropped.

They had not told anyone the ledger knew that.

* * *

That night the fog came up from the spring again, filling the quadrangle like milk poured into a bowl, and Tamsin lay in their room in the east residential corridor listening to the building settle around them. Old buildings settle. They had told themselves this for weeks now. Old buildings tick and groan and sigh through their pipes; old buildings have doors that swell in damp weather and drift on their hinges; old buildings are full of the small mechanical noises that a lonely mind will happily organize into meaning.

But old buildings did not, as a rule, learn your name.

The recorder was running. It had been running every night since the sleep clinic, a small red eye on the desk, and Tamsin had stopped pretending the experiment was a joke. They played back each morning now the way other people checked the weather—braced, ritual, half-hoping for nothing. Most mornings lately there was nothing but their own breathing and the building's groans, and the nothing was worse than the static, because the nothing meant it was waiting.

At some hour they could not name—three, or four, or a time the clocks had simply declined to record—they woke without knowing they had woken. The room was dark in the particular way Cold Spring rooms were dark, the darkness not an absence of light but a presence, something with weight and texture that lay over the blankets like a second body.

And down the corridor, a door opened.

Not the sound of a door swelling or sticking or sighing loose. The sound of a door being *opened*—the deliberate compression of the latch, the swing, the careful, courteous click of it closing again. Then footsteps. Not approaching. Just *moving*. Someone walking the corridor at the pace of a person who has all the time that has ever been made.

Tamsin lay still. The old armor came up on instinct, the skeptic's litany: *pipes, wind, another teacher, a sleepwalking student, the building settling.* But the litany had holes in it now, and the footsteps walked straight through them.

The door at the end of the corridor—Mrs. Okafor's, empty since the autumn—opened. Closed. The door opposite. Opened. Closed. It was going door to door. It was *checking*.

And then it stopped, and Tamsin knew without seeing that it stood outside their door, on the worn boards where the moonlight came through the corridor window in a long pale stripe, and it did not knock, and it did not breathe, and it waited with a patience that had been practiced for a hundred and twenty-seven years.

"Tamsin Galloway."

The voice came through the door the way cold comes through a window—no effort, no volume, just arrival. And it was Tamsin's voice. Not an impression of it. Not an echo with the edges sanded off. It was their own voice, with their own slight hoarseness at the end of a long day, their own habit of letting the second syllable fall a half-tone, the intonation they had heard on the playback recordings a dozen times now and had run out of explanations for.

"Tamsin Galloway. You're late."

They did not answer. Their whole body was a held breath. The rule surfaced from the laminated list, absurd in daylight, load-bearing now: *Do not answer if called by name after dark.* They had laughed at it. They could remember laughing, the exact shape of the laugh, the way they'd shown it to Fenwick with an arched eyebrow, and the memory of that laugh felt like a photograph of a person who had since died.

The voice said it again. Closer. Not louder—closer, as if the door had become a suggestion rather than a fact, as if the wood had agreed to be mostly elsewhere.

"Tamsin Galloway."

And here was the thing Tamsin would not be able to explain afterward, the thing that broke the armor not with a blow but with a key: some small, traitorous part of them *wanted* to answer. Not out of fear. Out of recognition. The voice was theirs; the name was theirs; some deep animal part of the mind insists that when you hear your own voice calling you, the correct response is to come home to it. It felt less like being hunted than like being *remembered*—and they had been so lonely here, in this beautiful cold place where every colleague spoke in the same careful cadence and every student smiled on the same beat, that the pull of being known, even by a thing like this, was almost sweet.

Their lips parted. The word—*what*, or *yes*, or simply the name itself, offered back like a hand—was already shaped behind their teeth.

They bit down on it. Hard enough that they tasted copper.

The corridor was silent for a long moment. Then the footsteps resumed, unhurried, moving away, and a door opened somewhere below—two floors below, Tamsin was nearly certain, which was impossible, because there were no doors on the ground floor of the residential wing except the fire exit, and the fire exit had been chained since before they arrived.

They lay awake until the window went the grey of dishwater, and did not sleep again.

* * *

In the morning, they checked the doors.

Every door in the east residential corridor stood exactly as it should, latched, undisturbed, the corridor swept and ordinary and full of morning light that made the night feel like a fabrication. Except: the fire exit on the ground floor. The chain was still on. The chain was still locked. But the door stood open six inches, the chain stretched taut as a violin string, and through the gap Tamsin could see the fog-thick pines and, at the treeline, the dark mouth of the path that led down to the spring.

The chain had not broken. The chain had not been cut. It was simply *longer*, or the door had simply decided the chain was a formality, and Tamsin stood looking at it for a long time while students filtered past on their way to breakfast, nodding to them in that synchronized way, saying good morning in that unified cadence, none of them glancing at the door at all.

They fetched the recorder. The night's file was there, timestamped 3:33 a.m. to 3:41—a duration, Tamsin noted with a distant, hysterical part of the mind, of exactly eight minutes, the same duration as every anomalous recording so far, as if the thing kept appointments.

They played it in the empty staff room with the door locked and the kettle going for cover-noise.

Their own breathing. The building's groans. Then, faintly, the doors—one, another, another, the courteous clicks like someone dealing cards. Then the pause outside their room, forty seconds of silence so complete the recording's hiss seemed to lean away from it. And then the voice, quiet and perfect and theirs:

*Tamsin Galloway. You're late.*

And then—Tamsin's hand froze on the laptop, the kettle screaming forgotten—something they had not heard through the door. Because they had been holding their breath, and the recorder had not been.

Under the voice, beneath it, woven through the forty seconds of silence like a thread through cloth: breathing. Many. The slow, synchronized respiration of a room full of sleepers, or of one sleeper multiplied, drawn in and let out in unison, and at the very edge of audibility, beneath the breathing, a sound like pages turning.

The ledger. It had been reading the ledger. Or the ledger had been reading it. Tamsin could no longer say where the book ended and the house began.

They closed the laptop. They made tea they did not drink. They wrote in their notebook, in handwriting that shook only a little: *It knows my full name. It learned it from the book. Therefore the book is not a record of the ritual. The book is part of it. Therefore my name has been in the book since before I came. Therefore I was chosen before I applied for this job.*

They looked at that last sentence for a long time.

Then they wrote: *I still don't believe it. I just believe everything else.*

* * *

The day passed in the strange double-exposure that Cold Spring days had become. Tamsin taught *Macbeth* to the fourth-years—someone in the office had assigned it, and they had not yet found the courage to wonder whether that was a coincidence—and watched nineteen bright faces track them with nineteen identical attentions, and thought: *one of these desks is for twenty.* Wren sat in the second row with her chin on her hand, and when Tamsin's gaze snagged on her, the girl gave the smallest shake of her head. *Not here. Not now.* As if she could hear the questions forming.

At the end of the day Wren lingered while the others filed out in their eerie unison, and stood by the desk turning her pencil end over end.

"You didn't answer," she said.

Tamsin's stomach dropped. "I'm sorry?"

"Last night." She said it the way she said everything, flat and precise, a child reporting the weather in a country where the weather was terrible. "It did the doors. It does the doors some nights. It did them the night before too, but it didn't say anything those nights. Last night it used your whole name." Her pencil stopped turning. "It only uses the whole name when it's nearly time."

"Nearly time for what, Wren?"

The girl looked at them with an expression far too old for her face, an expression Tamsin had last seen on the face of a woman in a documentary about coastal towns being swallowed by the sea—people who had stopped fighting and started taking measurements.

"The moon," Wren said. "You've seen the ledger. Don't make me say it in a classroom."

"Wren—"

"It said my sister's whole name too. The last week. Every night, a little closer to her room. And then it stopped saying it, because it didn't need to anymore." She set the pencil down on the desk, aligned it parallel to the edge, a small compulsive tidying that broke Tamsin's heart. "You nearly answered. I can always tell, the next day. The ones who nearly answer have a look. Like a door left open a crack."

She left. Tamsin sat alone in the classroom as the light went amber and then bruised, looking at the twenty names on the register and the nineteen desks, and for the first time they did not reach for an explanation. They let the wrongness just *be* wrong, unexplained, and found that the world did not end, and that this was somehow the most frightening thing of all: that they could sit in the middle of the impossible and feel it settle over them like a coat, tailored, warm, waiting to be worn.

* * *

That night they did not go to their room.

They told themselves it was strategy—if the voice came to the corridor and found them elsewhere, they would learn something about its range, its habits, its rules. This was a lie with a scholarly accent. The truth was simpler: they could not bear to lie in that bed again and listen to the doors.

They took blankets to the old sleep clinic in the west wing, the wellness room with its faded posters of smiling brains, and set the recorder on the desk, and lay down on the cot beneath the observation window where the fog rose off the spring like breath off a mirror. The room smelled of lavender sachets someone had placed years ago and dust and, faintly, underneath everything, the mineral cold of deep water.

They slept. They dreamed of a door drawn in crayon.

They woke before dawn with the certain, sick knowledge that something had happened in the night—not a sound, but an *absence* of something, the way you wake knowing a phone has rung. The recorder's red eye was dark. Not paused. Not out of battery. Dark in the way a closed eye is dark, and when they pressed the playback, the file for the night was empty—not deleted, *empty*, zero seconds, as if the machine had been asked to record a room that did not exist.

And pinned to the corkboard above the cot, where Tamsin had pinned nothing, was a page torn from a ledger.

One line, in that hand that changed with the decades and never changed at all:

*You're late, Tamsin Galloway. The moon is three days from full. Come down and we won't need to call again.*

Below it, pressed into the paper hard enough to emboss it, as if written by a finger rather than a pen:

*Or keep hiding. We have been patient for a hundred and twenty-seven years. We can be patient with you.*

Tamsin stood in the grey light holding the page, and the skeptic's armor—the litany, the pipes, the wind, the sleepwalking students, the elaborate machinery of not-believing—lay around their feet in pieces, and they understood that it had not been struck off. It had simply been *outgrown*, the way a child outgrows a coat, and that they had been walking around all this time in something that had never once fit.

They believed. Belief had arrived the way weather arrives, unwanted, total, changing everything it touched.

Three days.

Outside the window, the fog was lifting off the spring, and for one clear moment Tamsin could see down through the water to where the light gave out—could see, or thought they saw, far below, the pale suggestion of a shape like a door, standing open at the bottom of the cold.
