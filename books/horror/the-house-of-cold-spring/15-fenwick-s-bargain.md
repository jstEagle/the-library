## 15. Fenwick's Bargain

The staff room at Cold Spring Academy smelled of old varnish and older secrets, and Fenwick had learned to love it the way a man learns to love the smell of the cell he's resigned himself to.

He was grading essays he would not remember assigning in three days' time—no, that wasn't right. He corrected himself with the small mechanical patience of long practice. He was grading essays that might outlive him, if the house kept them. The house kept everything. That was rather the whole of the problem.

Outside the tall windows, the pines stood in their usual congregation, dark and patient. No birds. There had never been birds, not in nineteen years, and he had stopped noticing the silence the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat—until something reminds you it could stop.

Fenwick set down his pen.

He had been watching Tamsin Galloway for eleven days now, and he knew the signs the way a doctor knows the flush of fever. The too-long pauses in the corridor. The way their eyes tracked the east wing door as they passed it, quick and guilty, like a tongue returning to a broken tooth. The shadows under their eyes deepening from gray to bruise. And last Tuesday, in the faculty meeting, when Aldous had read aloud the date of the harvest festival and Tamsin's hand had jerked hard enough to spill tea across their own notes—Tamsin hadn't blotted it. Hadn't moved at all for a full ten seconds. Fenwick had counted. He always counted now. Counting was how you stayed sane in a place where arithmetic itself had been conscripted: twenty names on a register, nineteen desks, one name fed into the dark each autumn like a coin into a machine that never gave change.

He had been that coin once.

* * *

It was 2006, his third year teaching history at Cold Spring, and he had found what Tamsin was finding now—the gaps in the archives, the smooth-edged absences where a student should have been. He'd been younger then. Arrogant in the specific way of young men who think understanding a thing is the same as mastering it. He had stood in the chapel in the east wing with the founders' ledger open under his flashlight, reading names in a spidery Victorian hand, and he had thought: *superstition. Institutional folklore. A hazing ritual for teachers.*

The ledger had disagreed with him.

He remembered the night it happened with the terrible clarity of a memory the house had chosen to preserve—perhaps as a courtesy, perhaps as a leash. It had been October, the moon fat and low over the quadrangle, and he had woken at 3:17 in the morning to find his bedroom door standing open when he had locked it. He always locked it. He had walked to the door—he did not remember deciding to walk—and stood looking down the corridor, and the corridor was wrong. Longer than it should be. The windows along its length showed not the quad but a darkness with texture, a darkness that breathed, and from far down at the end of it, past the point where the hallway should have met the stairwell, something had said his name.

Not called it. *Said* it. The way you'd read it off a list. The way you'd confirm an order.

"Edmund Fenwick."

And God help him, he had opened his mouth to answer.

What saved him—or damned him, the two had become difficult to distinguish—was Mrs. Aldous. She had appeared at the end of that impossible corridor like a figure surfacing through black water, candleless, coat thrown over her nightdress, and she had said, "Not this year," in a voice so flat and final that even the darkness seemed to consider it. Then she had taken his arm—her grip cold through his sleeve, her face gray with exhaustion—and led him back to his room, and sat with him until dawn, drinking cold tea, explaining in a low, level voice exactly what he was and what the school was and what the ledger required.

"You were meant to be taken," she told him, somewhere around four in the morning, while the light through the window went from black to iron to the pale, apologetic gray that passes for sunrise in these hills. "Your name came up. It comes up every few years—a teacher, sometimes, though we prefer students. Less paperwork." She had almost smiled at that. It had been the worst thing he'd ever seen. "But there is a provision. An old one. The bargain doesn't specify *whose* name, only that a name be given. A living name, freely offered by someone who knows the truth of the house."

"Then offer yours," he had said, young and stupid and shaking.

"I did. Forty years ago." She had looked out the window at the pines. "Someone offered theirs instead of mine. That's how I'm here. That's how any of us are here, Edmund. Every adult in this building is alive because someone else isn't. You should understand that before you decide anything."

"Decide what?"

She had turned to him then, and her eyes had held the particular weariness of a woman who has carried a body up a mountain and must now carry it back down. "You have until the moon. Find someone willing, or find yourself willing. Those are the options. They have always been the options."

* * *

He had not found anyone willing. That was the truth he swallowed every morning with his coffee, the stone at the bottom of every cup.

What he had found, in the last desperate week of that October, was a loophole wearing the face of a kindness. Her name had been Marguerite Osei. Seventeen. Clever, funny, forever borrowing books from him and returning them with marginalia better than his lectures. She had come to him two days before the moon—not summoned, not sent, just worried, because she'd heard him being sick in the staff lavatory and seen the state of him, and Wren-like in her stubbornness she had refused to leave until he told her why.

So he told her. All of it. The ledger, the corridor, the provision. He told himself he was doing it because she deserved the truth, because secrecy was the school's original sin, because sunlight was disinfectant. And some part of him, some small honest clerk of a soul buried deep beneath the panic, wrote the true entry all those years ago and had been filing copies of it ever since: *you told her because you were drowning, and she reached out her hand, and you took it and pushed her head under.*

She had listened. She had gone very still, the way the students here went still, that synchronized stillness he'd never been able to unsee since. And then she had said, "If I do it—if I give my name—what happens to me?"

"I don't know," he had said. Which was true. What he had not said, what he had known and not said, was the other thing Aldous had told him: that the taken didn't die. That they persisted. Echoes, Aldous called them, memories given form, walking their last days in circles below the school like moths in a jar. Alive enough. Alive the way a recording is alive.

Marguerite had smiled at him. He could still see the smile; the house made sure of that, served it up to him on bad nights like a course at a banquet. "Someone saved me once already," she'd said. "My mum, when I was small. Fever. She sat up with me three nights and the doctors said she shouldn't have been able to. So maybe it runs in families. Maybe it's my turn."

And he had let her believe it was heroism. He had let her walk into Aldous's office on the night of the harvest moon and say the words—*I offer my name freely*—and he had stood in the corridor outside and listened to the floorboards settle and the clocks tick backward once, all together, like a great indrawn breath, and he had felt the pressure lift off his chest, the corridor-attention withdraw from the back of his neck like a hand unclenching, and he had known, with absolute certainty, that he had just committed the only sin that mattered.

Nineteen years. Nineteen harvest moons. And every one of them, he had done his part. Not the offering—that was Aldous's grim office, or fate's, or the ledger's—but the quieter work. The recruitment. The softening. He was the one who talked to the marked ones first, because students trusted him, because he was funny, because he kept biscuits in his desk drawer. He was the one who mentioned, casually, over weeks, that the school had strange old traditions, that the woods were older than the buildings, that some things here weren't quite explicable. He tilled the soil. He never planted anything—he was careful about that, careful as a man laundering money, keeping each transaction deniable—but he watered whatever the house put in the ground.

And every year, when the moon rose and the name was given and the school exhaled into another winter, he told himself the same story: *I didn't choose this. I was chosen. There's a difference.* And every year the story fit worse, the way shoes fit worse as you grow, until now, at fifty-one, he could barely hobble along inside it.

* * *

He found Tamsin in the library at dusk, in the carrel by the east-facing window where the light died earliest. They had the archive boxes out again. Of course they did. Fenwick watched them from behind the biography stacks for a moment—watched the lamplight catch the exhaustion in their face, the frantic industry of someone who has stopped sleeping and started digging—and felt the old machinery begin to turn inside him, the gears of the speech he had delivered six times before, each time hating it more.

"Galloway." He set a mug of coffee on the carrel. Good coffee, not the staff room swill. Bribery, of a kind. Everything was, here. "A word?"

Tamsin looked up. Guarded. Tired. Bright underneath it, though—bright the way Marguerite had been bright, the way they were all bright, the house marking them early like a farmer tagging livestock. "Mr. Fenwick."

"Edmund, please. We're about to be past formalities." He glanced at the boxes. "The class of 1911?"

"The gaps," Tamsin said. "Every year. One name, no record, no transfer, no death certificate. A century of it. You knew."

It wasn't a question, and he didn't insult them both by pretending otherwise. He pulled the second chair around and sat, heavily, knees cracking, and took a sip of coffee he didn't want. Buying time. He'd had nineteen years to write this speech and he still hadn't written it.

"I knew," he said. "I've known since 2006. Since I was the one whose name came up."

He told them. All of it, in order, in a low voice under the library's dead quiet: the corridor, the voice saying his name like a clerk confirming an order, Aldous in her nightclothes, the provision, the tea going cold. And then Marguerite. He made himself tell that part plainly, without the softening architecture he usually built around it. He described the smile. He described letting her believe. He watched Tamsin's face go through horror and pity and arrive somewhere colder, and he deserved the coldness, and he took it like a man taking medicine.

"So you've been feeding it," Tamsin said quietly. "Ever since. Finding them. Preparing them."

"Tilling," he said. "I never—" He stopped. Closed his eyes. "No. I'm not going to do that. Not with you. Yes. I've been feeding it. Six times, counting you. Each time telling myself I had no choice, which is true, and each time finding that the truth makes a very comfortable blanket if you're tired enough." He opened his eyes. "I'm tired, Galloway. I have been tired for nineteen years. And here is the thing I need you to hear, the only thing, and then you can hate me properly and get on with whatever you're going to get on with."

He leaned forward. The window beside them had gone fully dark, and in the glass their two reflections floated over the black mass of the pines like ghosts consulting.

"The provision is real," he said. "A name, freely offered, by someone who knows. It doesn't have to be yours. It doesn't have to be a student. It can be anyone in this building who understands what the house is—including you, including me, including, God forgive me, anyone you could convince." His voice dropped. "Three nights from now, the moon rises, and the house will take what it's owed. If no name is given, it takes indiscriminately—I've seen the year that happened, 1974, there are photographs in the archive with faces scratched out, and Aldous won't say why, and you don't want me to guess. But if a name is given—any name—it settles. It sleeps another year. And the one who gives the name walks away. Whole. Alive. Remembering."

He let it sit there between them, ugly and gleaming, the exact shape of the thing that had lived in his chest for two decades.

"That's the bargain," he said. "That's what I'm offering you, because someone owes you honesty in this place, and it might as well be the man who's least worth trusting. Save yourself. I'll tell you how it's done. I'll stand with you when you say the words, if you want company in it. I'll hate myself afterward either way—that part's traditional." He managed something that was almost a smile. "But understand what I'm really offering, Galloway. I'm not offering you survival. I'm offering you my seat. My sin, gently used, nineteen years old, still under warranty. Because I cannot bear"—and here his voice finally broke, quietly, like old wood—"I cannot bear to do the tilling again. To watch another bright one dig and dig until they find the ledger. To smile and pass the biscuits and know what October costs. If you take the deal, I'm free of it. Don't you see? Either way I'm free. Either you're damned or I am, and after nineteen years, God help me, I find I no longer care which."

The library clock ticked. Somewhere far below them—below the cellar, below the foundations, in the dark under the dark—something shifted its weight, patient as geology.

Tamsin hadn't touched the coffee. They were looking at him with an expression he couldn't parse, and then they said, very softly, "Who's next year, Edmund? If I refuse? Who do you till?"

And Fenwick opened his mouth to answer—to say *Wren*, because it was true, because the girl had been asking questions about her sister for two years and the house had noticed, the house always noticed the ones who asked—and found that he could not. Not because the house stopped him. Because Tamsin was looking at him, waiting, and for the first time in nineteen years, Edmund Fenwick discovered he wanted to lie to protect someone other than himself, and had no idea how.

Before he could speak at all, the lights went out—all of them, the whole wing, at once—and from the dark at the end of the stacks, in a voice like his own played slightly too slow, something said:

"Edmund Fenwick. You're late."
