## 2. One Second Late

August Halloran did not believe in haunted houses the way some people did not believe in God: not with conviction, but with the tired certainty of someone who had simply never been given a reason to.

Belief required evidence, and evidence was August's trade. They had spent eleven years measuring other people's property — basements that flooded, attics that sagged, walls that hid rot the way a smile hides a bad tooth — and in all that time they had never once found anything that could not be explained by water damage, poor craftsmanship, or the human talent for lying to themselves about what their money had bought. Houses were machines for keeping weather out and secrets in. That was all. That was ever all.

So when the mirror in the east bedroom lagged, August's first instinct was not fear. It was irritation.

The morning light came through the shutters in slats, striping the floor like old bandages, and August stood before the tall cheval glass with a laser measure in one hand and a clipboard under their arm, doing what they always did on the first full day of an appraisal: establishing baseline dimensions. Room by room. Wall by wall. The discipline of it was the closest thing August had to prayer. Numbers did not lie. Numbers did not leave. You could build a whole life out of numbers if you were careful, and August had been careful for a very long time.

The east bedroom measured four meters and twelve centimeters along its north wall. August knew this because they had measured it yesterday, twice, and written the figure down in their small, square handwriting, and because August's memory for figures was the single vanity they permitted themselves.

The wall now measured four meters and nineteen.

They remeasured. Four-nineteen again. They checked the laser against the hallway wall outside — dead on, four meters even, exactly as recorded. They went back into the bedroom and aimed the little red dot at the same plaster scar they'd used as a reference point yesterday, a pale crescent near the baseboard where something long ago had struck the wall and the wall had declined to mention it.

Four meters, nineteen centimeters.

"Huh," said August, to the empty room, because talking to empty rooms was another habit of a life spent alone in other people's houses, and because saying *huh* out loud made the discrepancy feel like a problem rather than a symptom.

The room did not answer. But the mirror —

August caught it peripherally first, the way you catch movement in a field at dusk: not seen so much as felt, a wrongness in the corner of the eye. They turned fully toward the cheval glass and looked at themselves looking back, and everything seemed ordinary. Tall figure, rumpled field jacket, hair going gray early at the temples the way their mother's had — no. The way hair did, sometimes, in some families. Clipboard. Laser measure hanging loose from two fingers.

Then they raised the measure, and the reflection raised its measure a half-beat late.

August stood very still.

Glass, they thought. Old glass. Old glass does terrible things. It sags. It ripples. Silvering flakes off the back in continents and archipelagos, and light bends through the flaws at slightly different speeds, and a warped pane can absolutely produce the impression of — of desynchronization. Of delay. There were carnival mirrors, there were optical illusions, there was a famous photograph from 1911 of a ballroom where every face appeared doubled because the window glass behind the photographer had been poured unevenly. August had read about it. August read about everything; reading was how they filled the evenings that other people filled with people.

They stepped closer to the glass and breathed on it. Their breath bloomed across the surface — and the reflected breath bloomed a fraction of a second after their own had already begun to fade.

Faulty glass, August told themselves firmly, and wrote *cheval mirror, east bedchamber — silvering degraded, replace/credit* on the clipboard in small square letters, and moved on to the dressing room, and did not look back, and thought about the mirror approximately none of the time for the next forty minutes, which is to say they thought about nothing else.

* * *

The house had more mirrors than any house August had ever appraised, and August had appraised a hotel.

They counted them that afternoon with the grim thoroughness of a man counting teeth in a mouth that kept growing new ones. A gilt-framed oval above the fireplace in the drawing room, its glass clouded at the bottom edge like breath that wouldn't fade. A rectangular pier glass between the dining room windows, so tall and narrow it turned anyone standing before it into a candle. Hand mirrors in the drawers of vanities, their handles worn smooth by hands that no longer existed. A shaving mirror bolted to the cellar wall, which was strange — who shaved in a cellar? — and a small round one set into the paneling of the upstairs corridor at the height of a child's eyes, which was stranger, because there had never been children here. Not according to any record August could find. The house had been built, tenanted, emptied, and re-tenanted in cycles the county archives recorded only as changes of name, and no name had ever come with a family attached.

Seventeen mirrors. August wrote the number down, then crossed it out and wrote *16+*, because they had a nagging feeling about a closet off the blue room, and when they opened the closet there was indeed an eighteenth mirror inside, facing the wall, hung backward, as if the house had decided at some point that it had shown enough.

By mid-afternoon August had developed a private taxonomy of the lag.

The pier glass in the dining room was the worst — or the most honest, depending on how you wanted to think about it, and August very much did not want to think about it. Standing before it, they watched their own arm rise to check their watch and saw the sleeve follow a heartbeat behind, like a dancer who had learned the choreography from a description. The drawing room mirror lagged less, or perhaps August had simply stopped being able to tell. That was the thing about a beat of delay: the mind papered over it. The mind was desperate to paper over it. Every time August turned to look directly, the reflection snapped into perfect synchrony, obedient, innocent, and it was only in the peripheral vision — in that soft, unguarded country at the edge of sight — that the reflection moved like something following rather than something mirroring.

Optics, said the voice in August's head that sounded like reason and was mostly just loneliness wearing a lab coat. Parallax. Refraction through sagged panes. You are tired, you slept badly on a mattress older than your grandmother, you have eaten nothing today but vending machine biscuits, and you are alone in a large house, and alone is a condition the brain treats as emergency. This is what alone does. It makes the furniture watch you back.

That was true, and August knew it was true, and knowing it was true helped exactly as much as knowing the statistics for plane crashes helps during turbulence.

Because here was the thing August kept circling back to, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth: the mirrors didn't all lag equally. Faulty glass failed randomly. Faulty glass rippled, doubled, distorted. It did not *wait*. And yet every reflective surface in Harrow Glen — including, August discovered at dusk, the darkened window of the pantry, including the still black water standing in the courtyard cistern, including the polished brass of a door handle — produced the same effect, each in its own degree, as though they were all tuned instruments playing the same piece slightly flat.

As though they were all listening to the same conductor.

August stood in the pantry with the last of the daylight going amber behind them and watched their reflection in the cistern water hold up its end of a conversation August hadn't started. Then they went downstairs to the kitchen, put the kettle on the camp stove they'd brought, and ate tinned soup standing up, and told themselves the day had been productive.

It had, in a way. They had eighteen mirrors catalogued, three rooms remeasured with discrepancies logged, and a working hypothesis: settling. The whole house was settling, unevenly, on foundations that predated accurate surveying, and the settling had racked the frames and stressed the glass, and stress plus age equaled artifact. It was a good hypothesis. It was the kind of hypothesis you could write in a report, and reports were how August paid for the small flat in the city where nobody waited for them, and the flat was fine, the flat was quiet, the flat was exactly as empty as August had designed it to be, because design was safer than accident.

The kettle screamed. August's reflection in the dark kitchen window did not scream until after August had already flinched.

August did not look at the window again while they washed the pot.

* * *

Night in Harrow Glen arrived the way water arrives in a sinking ship: gradually, then everywhere at once. August had brought lamps, good ones, and set them up in the ground-floor study — a room with one door, two windows, and, crucially, no mirrors, unless you counted the glass-fronted bookcase, and August had covered it with a dust sheet around six o'clock without letting themselves examine why.

They worked. That was the mercy of work; it gave the hands something honest to do while the mind misbehaved. They transcribed measurements. They cross-referenced the deed abstract against the physical footprint of the house and found, for the fourth time, that the interior did not match the exterior — that rooms existed inside whose space, according to the outer walls, should belong to other rooms, or to nothing at all. Eleven years of surveying had taught August that old houses grew like trees, additions grafted onto additions, but Harrow Glen's growth was inward, secretive, a snail's spiral of swallowed space. Somewhere in the middle of the house was a volume of darkness roughly the size of a chapel that no door admitted to and no window lit.

August drew it on the plan as a hatched void and labeled it, in small square letters, *unverified interior*, and felt the label sit in their stomach like a stone.

Around eleven, they allowed themselves the weakness of a walk-through. This was habit, not courage: a final circuit to lock up, lamp in hand, checking latches out of professional compulsion even though they'd already checked them. The house at night was a different animal than the house by day. By day it was merely decayed — peeling paper, buckled floors, the sweet-rot smell of damp plaster. By night the decay acquired intention. Shadows pooled in corners with the patience of things that had nowhere else to be. The floorboards spoke in the language of old houses, random creaks, settling timbers, and August catalogued each sound as it occurred and assigned it a mundane cause and did not notice, would not let themselves notice, that the sounds had begun to arrive in patterns. Two creaks. Pause. Two creaks. Like footsteps learning a rhythm. Like something practicing the shape of walking.

The corridor upstairs was the longest in the house, and August walked it with the lamp held high, telling themselves the story of faulty glass one more time, polishing the hypothesis like a talisman, and it was there — halfway down, passing the small round mirror set at the height of a child's eyes — that the evening finally stopped pretending.

August did not stop walking. That was important, later, to remember: they did not stop. They slowed, the way you slow past a car crash, and in the curve of the little round glass they saw the corridor behind them, lamp-lit, empty — and then not empty, or not quite, because at the far end, where the corridor bent toward the east wing, the reflection showed a shape standing where August had passed thirty seconds earlier. A figure. Tall. Still. Wearing August's coat.

August spun.

The corridor behind them was empty. Lamp-light, peeling paper, a door standing ajar onto darkness. Nothing else. The floorboards held their silence with the smugness of the innocent.

Reflections, August reminded themself, do not show what is behind you. Reflections show what is in front of you. If the mirror showed a figure at the far end of the corridor, then either the mirror was broken — plausible, documented, boring — or the figure had been there and gone in the seconds it took August to turn, which was not plausible, was not documented, and was in no way boring.

They made themselves walk back to the little round mirror and stand in front of it, chin level with the top of its frame, and look.

Their own face looked back. Tired. Thirty-eight years of tired arranged around gray-shot brown hair and a jaw set like a man bracing for bad news, which, in fairness, was August's resting expression and had been since childhood. Behind the reflected face, the reflected corridor stretched away, empty, correct.

"Right," August said aloud, and their voice sounded too loud in the hall, and the reflection's mouth moved with it, perfectly, instantly, inarguably synchronized. "There. Perfectly normal. Synchronized. Textbook."

And because they needed to prove it thoroughly, the way you press a bruise to confirm it's only a bruise, August smiled at the mirror. A quick, deliberate, clinical smile — the kind you'd give a passport photographer.

The reflection smiled back a full second after August had already stopped.

It was not a copy of August's smile. That was the detail that cracked the evening open like an egg. August's smile had been brief, tight, mechanical, gone almost before it arrived. What the mirror showed was slow, and wide, and warm — the smile of someone delighted, someone welcoming, someone who had been waiting a very long time for the person in front of them to finally, finally turn around — and it held. It held while August stood frozen with the lamp shaking in their fist. It held while the flame gutted. It held while August's rational mind threw up explanation after explanation — persistence of vision, fatigue, a trick of the wick, a trick of the glass, a trick of the meat — and discarded each one faster than the last, because there is no optics on earth that manufactures an expression. Glass can warp a face. Glass cannot mean something by it.

The smile ended. The reflection became August again: tired, frozen, lamp trembling. In the glass, the reflected lamp trembled a beat later, like an echo arriving from somewhere deeper than the surface.

August walked back to the study at a pace just short of running, locked the door, sat down among their neat papers and their covered bookcase, and did the thing they always did when the world misbehaved: they reached for a number. Any number. The north wall of the east bedroom, four meters and — and the number wasn't there. They knew they'd written it. They knew their own handwriting, small and square, twin to the notes in their own files going back eleven years. But when they flipped through the clipboard, the page for the east bedroom was blank except for the header, *East Bedchamber*, and beneath it, in their handwriting, in their ink, in their hand, a single unfinished line:

*the mirror keeps my place when I*

Nothing after it. No period. No second sentence. Just the line, ending mid-thought, the way a sentence ends when the hand writing it is interrupted.

August stared at it for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, in the dark upper floors, a floorboard creaked twice, paused, and creaked twice more, patient as a metronome.

They did not sleep so much as lose consciousness in a chair, and they dreamed of nothing, which was somehow worse than dreaming, a black and mirrorless sleep like falling down a stairwell with no bottom, and when they woke gray and stiff-necked in the morning, the first sound was not the birds or the wind or the groan of old timber.

It was knocking. Fists on the front door below, rapid and angry and unmistakably human, and a voice calling up through the boards of a house that had, until this moment, felt entirely, terminally alone:

"Hello? I know someone's in there! Open the door — that's *my grandfather's house!*"

August sat up, heart hammering, and for one disoriented second — one stolen, lagging second — their reflection in the dark study window smiled before they did.
