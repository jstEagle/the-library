## 5. What the Deed Owes

Wren Ashford had learned to read the house the way other people read weather.

There was a pressure behind her sternum when it was pleased, a looseness in her back teeth when it was hungry, and on mornings like this one — gray light leaking through the kitchen window in long, reluctant ribbons — there was a stillness so complete she could hear the wallpaper thinking. She stood at the counter with her grandfather's chipped enamel kettle in both hands, not boiling water, just holding it, because holding something was easier than admitting what she'd seen through the study door an hour ago: a stranger with a surveyor's case and a stranger's careful hands, measuring the front hall as though Harrow Glen were a thing that could be measured.

The kettle ticked as the metal warmed under her grip. She set it down before it could begin to hum. The house liked humming. It collected sounds the way a magpie collected foil, and she had no intention of giving it anything more than it already took.

Her grandfather's chair sat empty at the head of the table. It had been empty for eleven months, four days, and — if you counted the way Wren counted, which was in harvest moons rather than weeks — one moon shy of a full turn. She still made tea for two some mornings. She told herself it was habit. She knew better. Habit didn't set the cup at his place with the handle turned the way he liked, toward his right hand, toward the window where he could watch the orchard go to rot.

"Drink your tea, Grandad," she said quietly, to the cup, to the chair, to whatever part of this house remembered him fondly enough to leave his ghost-warmth in the wood.

The house said nothing. The house never said anything directly. That was the first lesson he'd taught her, when she was seven and full of questions about why they never used the east wing, why the mirrors in the good parlor were covered with sheets every autumn, why he checked the locks twice at night and then a third time without touching them, just looking, the way you look at a dog that's been growling in its sleep.

*It doesn't speak,* he'd said, crouching to her level, his hands rough and warm around hers. *It listens. And everything you finish inside these walls, it keeps.*

She hadn't understood then. She was beginning to understand now, watching through the smudged glass as the stranger — Halloran, the name signed in careful block letters on the letter she'd found tucked in the gate — moved from window to window of the west facade with a tape measure and a notebook, cataloguing her grief like inventory.

Wren poured the second cup anyway. Steam curled up and vanished against the cold air, and for half a heartbeat the shape it made looked like a hand reaching for the rim.

She drank her own tea standing. She left his to cool.

* * *

The deed lived in the tin box under the floorboard beneath the stove, wrapped in oilcloth beside her grandfather's spare glasses, a child's drawing of the house with too many windows, and a key on a leather cord that she wore around her neck and had worn since she was old enough to be trusted with it. Which was to say: never. He'd put it on her the night before he walked into the east wing and did not walk out, pressing it into her palm with fingers gone papery and strange, and he'd said the only instruction that mattered, the one she repeated to herself like a rosary bead worn smooth:

*Never finish anything you start here.*

Not *don't go in*. Not *don't touch*. Never *finish*. Because the house didn't want trespassers or vandals or even victims, not exactly. It wanted completions. A song sung to its last note. A letter written to its signature. A staircase climbed to the top. A sentence ended. Every unfinished thing in Harrow Glen was a held breath, and the house had been holding its breath for two hundred years, waiting for someone careless enough to exhale.

Wren knelt on the cold flagstones and lifted the board. The tin smelled of rust and lavender sachets and underneath both, faintly, of earth — the deep, sweet-rotten earth of the cellar, though the box had never been down there. Some smells traveled. Some things wanted to be found.

She carried the deed into the hall, where the stranger was crouched over their instruments, and dropped it on the floor between them with a slap that echoed longer than it should have.

"You'll want to see what you're appraising," she said.

August Halloran straightened slowly. Up close, they had the look of someone who'd spent years being careful in rooms where nobody noticed them — dust-gray jacket, sleeves rolled with precision, eyes that catalogued before they trusted. Wren recognized the type because she saw it in her own reflection every morning. Loneliness wears a uniform.

"That's the original deed?" Halloran asked, and their voice had the flat professional calm of someone who had already decided not to be unsettled by anything today.

"It's the *current* deed." Wren crouched and unwrapped the oilcloth. "There have been eleven."

* * *

She let them read. She watched them read, because people's faces told the truth a half-second before their mouths caught up, and August Halloran's face went through a whole quiet weather system: skepticism, confusion, the small furrow of someone doing arithmetic that keeps coming out wrong.

The parchment was soft as cloth at the edges, brown as tea-stain, and the ink had faded to the color of dried blood, which Wren supposed it might well be. The script was beautiful. That was the worst part. Whoever had drawn up the first lease had done it with love, with flourishes, with the pride of a man building something meant to outlast him.

"*To the keepers of Harrow Glen, and their heirs, in perpetuity,*" Halloran read aloud, slow. "*In consideration of maintenance of the bounds and continuance of the work begun, the undersigned grant tenure of the whole estate—*" They stopped. Turned the page over, as though the rest might be hiding on the back. "*Tenure ends at the rising of the harvest moon following the seventh year.*"

"Every lease is the same," Wren said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Seven years. Then the moon rises, and the lease ends. Not expires — *ends*, the way a story ends. Look at the signatures."

Halloran looked. Eleven deeds, eleven names, each in a different hand across two centuries: Aldous Merrick, 1809. Thomas Greer, 1830. The Vane family — Elias Vane's own cramped, precise script, 1798, the first of them. Names marching down the years like fence posts, and after each one, in the margin, in the same beautiful hand as the body of the text, a single word: *Settled.*

"What does settled mean?" Halloran asked.

"It means accounted for." Wren heard the bitterness rise in her own throat and didn't swallow it down. This time she let it come. "Except nobody was ever accounted for. I've been to the county archive in Ashcombe. Twice. There are transfer records, tax records, a marriage license for Thomas Greer, a shipping manifest with Aldous Merrick's name on it. You know what there isn't? Death certificates. Obituaries. Burial plots. Wills probated. Eleven owners over two hundred years, and not one of them ever *died*. On paper, they all just — stopped. Walked off the edge of the record."

"They could have left. Sold up, moved on—"

"To where?" Wren stood, and the movement pulled the key on its cord taut against her collarbone, warm from her skin. "Greer had a wife in Ashcombe who placed notices in the paper for six years asking after him. Merrick's brother came looking in 1816 and stayed three days at the inn and then went home and never spoke of it again — the innkeeper's diary's in the archive, I read it, his hands shook writing about the nights here. Nobody leaves, Mr. Halloran. Or rather—" she corrected herself, because precision mattered, because her grandfather had drilled precision into her the way other men drilled scripture, "—nobody leaves *whole*. The ones who ran got out with their skins. What they left behind, the house kept."

Halloran was quiet for a moment. Outside, wind moved through the dead orchard, and the bare branches scraped the western wall in long slow strokes, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"You said eleven deeds," Halloran said finally. "But the ledger in the cellar—"

"You've been in the cellar?" Wren's heart stumbled. "You went down there alone?"

"I survey properties. Cellars are properties." But their eyes flicked away, just slightly, and Wren had spent a lifetime learning to read evasions in a house that specialized in them. Something down there had shown itself to this stranger. Something had already started.

"The founders built it," she said instead. "Four families. The Vanes, the Harrows, the Glens — that's where the name comes from, it's not a place-name, it's a *roster* — and the Corvuses, whose line died out first, or was spent first, I should say. They built the house around something. Brought it here from wherever it was before, fed it, bound it into the walls and foundations, and wrote themselves leases so their descendants would have to stay and keep the binding fed. Seven-year terms. Renewable. Except renewing means staying, and staying means—" she gestured vaguely at the ceiling, the walls, the whole patient architecture of the place, "—this. And every family eventually produced one person who decided seven years was enough, who broke the chain, who left the work unfinished. And the house kept collecting rent anyway. Just not in money."

"In people," Halloran said.

"In people." Wren laughed, short and joyless. "Missing persons, mostly. Ask the village sometime why Ashcombe has a memorial garden with forty-one names and no war ever touched it. Ask them why nobody will drive the north road after dark. They know. They've always known. Their grandfathers helped build the thing and their grandmothers left offerings at the gate every harvest eve until — oh, sixty years ago, maybe, when it became embarrassing. Modern times." She spat the words like seeds. "They didn't stop believing. They just stopped *paying attention*, and they hoped the appetite would follow."

* * *

Halloran sat down slowly on the bottom stair, the deed across their knees, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. The silence of the house pressed in around them, and Wren felt it listening, felt the particular quality of attention that settled over the room when two living people spoke its name aloud. The lamps dimmed a fraction, though the wicks hadn't changed. The fire in the parlor grate, banked hours ago, gave a single soft pop.

"Your grandfather," Halloran said. "He was the tenth keeper?"

"Tenth and eleventh. He renewed twice. Foolish, brave, stubborn man." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, hated the house for hearing it. "He thought if he understood the ritual well enough, he could end it. Rewrite the terms. He spent thirty years studying the founders' ledger. And then last autumn, three days before the harvest moon, he told me he'd figured out what the final line required. He wouldn't tell me what. He kissed my forehead, he gave me the key, and he said—" 

She had to stop. She pressed her thumb hard against the key's teeth until the edges bit.

"He said, *never finish anything you start here, Wren-girl. Promise me. Whatever you find, whoever asks, however right it feels — leave it undone.* And he went into the east wing to do exactly the opposite, because he was going to try to finish it himself, and either he succeeded and paid the price, or he failed and—" She spread her hands. "Eleven months. No body. No death certificate. No account. The house doesn't even have the decency to grieve you properly. It just closes the book."

Halloran looked down at the deed in their lap. When they spoke again, their voice had lost its surveyor's flatness; something underneath had come loose.

"The final line of the ritual. Your grandfather knew what it required."

"I know he was afraid of it in a way he was never afraid of anything else. My grandfather slept with salt under his bed and laughed at thunder. Whatever that last line says, it scared him more than the entity did." Wren crouched so they were eye level, and she searched the stranger's face with the frankness of someone who had run out of time for politeness. "And you've seen it. Haven't you. The ledger. You've read further than I ever managed, because the pages won't hold still for me — they know my blood, they know I'm heir, and they're saving themselves. So tell me, Mr. Halloran. What does the deed owe? What does the *work* owe? What's at the bottom of all this?"

August Halloran opened their mouth—

—and the front door, which Wren had locked that morning with both bolt and prayer, swung inward with a slow ceremonial ease, letting in a gust of October air that smelled of turned earth and ripe apples and something sweeter underneath, something like the inside of a ripe thing, and every mirror in the hall — uncovered, unshrouded, eleven months early — reflected the open doorway a half-beat before the door actually moved.

Wren's hand found the key at her throat.

"Never mind," she said, not taking her eyes off the glass. "I think the house wants to answer for itself."
