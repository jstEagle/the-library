## 7. Doors That Count

Wren woke to the sound of the house breathing.

She had lived at Harrow Glen long enough to know its noises — the tick of cooling pipes in winter, the groan of old joists settling like an arthritic man into a chair, the wind worrying the loose shingle above the east dormer. She knew which sounds meant weather and which meant nothing and which meant *pay attention*. This was none of them. This was a slow, tidal exhalation that came up through the floorboards and through the mattress and through her ribs, as though the whole structure were a lung and she were lying on it.

She lay still in the dark of her grandfather's old room and counted. Four seconds in. Six seconds out. The house had never breathed before. Houses didn't breathe. But then, houses didn't lag their own mirrors either, and they hadn't done that until August Halloran arrived with their laser measure and their sensible boots and their eyes that kept snagging on corners where nothing was — yet.

"Grandda," she said to the ceiling, because she always said it first thing, a habit worn smooth as a stair rail, "I think your house is waking up."

The ceiling said nothing. Ceilings were honest that way. It was everything else in Harrow Glen that lied.

She dressed by feel, pulled her hair back, took the iron key from around her neck and held it a moment in her fist until the cold of it became her own cold, and went out into the hall with the flashlight she kept charged for exactly this kind of night.

The hallway was longer than it should have been.

That was the first thing. Not dramatic, not impossible — just wrong the way a familiar face is wrong when someone has moved the eyebrows half an inch. Wren had walked this corridor ten thousand times. Eleven steps from her door to the landing window, twelve if she dragged her heel on the seventh board out of superstition. Tonight she walked eleven steps and the window was still ahead of her, pale and fogged with October, another four steps off. She stopped. The house stopped with her, in the way it did — not silence but *attention*, the particular hush of something pretending to be architecture.

"Fine," she told it. "Be like that."

She walked the remaining steps. At the window she turned and looked back down the corridor toward her open door, and measured it with her eyes, and it was fifteen steps now instead of eleven. She hadn't heard anything move. No whisper of wallpaper shifting, no creak of boards sliding over boards. The hallway had simply been fifteen steps all along, apparently, and her memory of eleven was the faulty instrument.

Except her memory wasn't faulty. Her memory was the one thing in this house that had never once betrayed her.

* * *

By full morning the arithmetic had gotten worse.

Wren made tea in the kitchen — the kitchen still being where kitchens were, thank God, though the pantry door now opened onto a wall that smelled faintly of wet stone and old apples — and she stood at the counter with her notebook and did what her grandfather had taught her to do when the house misbehaved: she wrote it down. Dates. Times. Measurements. *A record is a weapon,* he used to say, tapping his own ledger with a cracked thumbnail. *You can't fight what you won't count.*

So she counted.

The front hall: yesterday, three doors off it besides the front entrance itself — drawing room, study, stairs. This morning there were two. The drawing room door was gone. Not locked, not boarded, not hidden behind furniture. Gone. Where the doorframe had been there was unbroken plaster, painted the same tired green as the rest of the hall, and if you ran your hand across it you'd swear no door had ever hung there. Except Wren had slept in that drawing room the first month after Grandda vanished, because she couldn't bear her own bed, and she remembered the draft under that door, and the way the moonlight came through the tall windows and lay across the floor like spilled milk.

The service stairs behind the kitchen: gone. A linen cupboard stood in their place, and inside the cupboard were linens she'd never bought, folded with a precision that made her skin crawl, smelling of lavender and something underneath the lavender, something like a cellar.

The back door: present, but when she opened it, the porch beyond was four feet narrower than it had been yesterday, and the yard seemed to have leaned closer, the dead apple tree standing nearer to the house than any tree had a right to stand without being rude about it.

And the windows. That was the part that got her, sitting there with her tea going cold. The east wall of the kitchen had had five windows. She'd washed them herself in spring, all five, cursing the paint flecks. Now there were four, evenly spaced, and the light coming through them fell differently, and the wall between them was seamless, and somewhere in the last nine hours Harrow Glen had quietly subtracted a window from the world and dared her to prove it had ever existed.

She proved it anyway. She went to the exterior wall outside, in the raw wind, and looked up, and there were five windows on the outside of the east wall and four on the inside, and the discrepancy sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

The house was eating itself. Or — no. She stood in the yard with the wind pulling her braid sideways and made herself look at it properly, the way August would look at it, all angles and evidence.

The house wasn't eating itself. The house was *tidying*. Folding itself smaller, room by room, exit by exit, the way a hand closes finger by finger. And hands don't close around things they intend to keep safe. Hands close around things they intend to *hold*.

* * *

August found her at noon in the front hall, standing under the staircase with her notebook open, staring at the wall where the drawing room door used to be.

"You see it too," Wren said, before they'd even finished coming down the steps. Their footsteps had that careful surveyor's rhythm, each tread tested, and she hated how much comfort she took in the sound.

"The hallway upstairs is nine feet longer than my measurements from Tuesday." August's voice was flat, professional, but Wren had learned to hear the strain under it, the sound of a rational mind doing isometric exercises against something irrational. "I re-measured twice. Same tape. Same starting point. Nine feet, give or take."

"Give or take," Wren repeated. "That's the part I hate. It doesn't slam doors or rattle chains. It just... adjusts. Like it's got all the time in the world."

"It does have all the time in the world." August came to stand beside her, close enough that she could smell chalk dust and cheap coffee on them. They touched the green plaster where the door had been, palm flat, the way you'd check a patient for fever. "It's been here two hundred years. We've been here two weeks. From its perspective we're mayflies arguing with a mountain."

"Well, this mayfly's keeping score." Wren flipped her notebook around so August could see the page. She'd drawn the ground floor as it should be, every door marked, and beside it the ground floor as it was this morning, and she'd drawn arrows between them, and the arrows all pointed inward. "Look. Every change goes the same direction. Rooms gone, halls longer, exits fewer. Nothing's been added. Nothing's been opened. It's subtraction, all the way down."

August studied the page for a long moment. When they spoke again, their voice had changed — quieter, the flatness cracking to show something underneath that Wren recognized, because she'd heard it in her grandfather's voice near the end, in the nights he sat up listening to the walls.

"It's counting down."

"That's what I keep coming back to, yeah."

"Not locking us in. If it wanted us trapped it could have taken every door on the first night and we'd never have known the difference — we'd have wandered those lengthening halls until we starved and thought it was our own bad luck." August's finger traced her arrows. "But it's leaving us the front door. Leaving us the choice. It's taking everything else away one piece at a time and watching what we do about it. That's not a cage, Wren. That's a *question*. It's asking how much we'll tolerate before we run. Or—" they stopped.

"Or before we don't run," Wren finished. "Or before we go deeper instead of out." She shut the notebook. Her hands weren't quite steady and she let August see that they weren't; she was done performing steadiness for anyone. "My grandda told me one thing about this house. One instruction, and he made me swear it on his deathbed with his hand so cold I could feel his pulse slowing through his skin. He said: *never finish anything you start here.* Never finish a meal, never finish a song, never finish walking down a hallway — turn around, leave the plate, hum something else. Because finishing is consent, in this place. Finishing is signing."

"And you think the house is finishing something," August said slowly. "Right along with us."

"I think the house has been mid-sentence for two hundred years," Wren said, "and I think it's finally getting to the end of the line."

* * *

She spent the afternoon walking the perimeter, inside and out, and the picture only darkened.

Outbuildings first. The garden shed — fine, still there, though its door now faced the wrong way, hinged on the opposite side, so that opening it felt like reading writing in a mirror. The well — she checked it with a thrown pebble and heard water, real water, a small mercy. The carriage house, though: the carriage house was a smear. Not ruins, not collapsed — simply less *there* than it had been, its outline soft against the sky, its doors grown over with ivy that had not been growing yesterday and that moved, very slightly, in windless air.

Inside, worse. The library had lost its north wall of books — not the books, the *wall*, the shelves replaced by a stretch of bare plaster that hummed when she pressed her ear to it, a hum below hearing, more vibration than sound, like pressing your ear to the flank of something enormous and sleeping. The chapel — Grandda had called it the chapel, though no God had ever been welcome in it — had subtracted its altar and left a rectangle of darker floorboards, and standing in that rectangle Wren felt watched with such specificity, such *personal* attention, that she stepped backward out of it without turning around, the way you back away from a dog you don't trust.

And the mirrors. She made herself check every mirror in the east wing, because August had told her about the lag and she needed to know it with her own eyes. In the gilt mirror at the top of the service stairs — the ones that were now a linen cupboard, except the mirror remained, hanging on plaster that had never been a stairwell, which was its own quiet horror — she raised her right hand and watched her reflection raise its left, and waited, and counted.

One second. Maybe a little more. The reflection followed like a dancer who knew the choreography but resented it.

Then, as she lowered her arm, the reflection lowered its arm a fraction too slowly, and at the bottom of the movement its fingers curled — just slightly, just for a heartbeat — into something that was almost a wave. Almost a beckoning.

Wren put a dish towel over the mirror and did not take it back down.

* * *

Dusk came early, the way it did in October, pooling in the valleys first and then rising like floodwater. Wren lit the lamps in the rooms that still had lamps, following her grandfather's old circuit, and noticed that the circuit was shorter now — two rooms had lost their fixtures entirely, wiring and all, as though electricity had simply given up on them, or been given up.

She found August in the study, surrounded by the previous surveyor's notes — Elias Vane's notes, the ones from 1804, written in a hand so like August's own that both of them had agreed not to talk about it anymore. They had the ledger open too, the founders' ledger from the cellar, its vellum pages covered in that dense, ritualistic script. August looked up when she came in, and the lamplight caught their face, and Wren saw that they hadn't slept. Neither had she. Neither, she suspected, had the house.

"Nine days," August said.

"I know. Full moon's the thirty-first. Grandda's almanac has it circled going back forty years." She sat down across from them, pulled the notebook out, laid it on the desk between them like a card played face-up. "But that's not what I came to say. I've been counting all day, properly counting, and it's not random. The subtractions. There's a pattern."

August leaned forward. "Show me."

"First night after you arrived — that was when the mirrors started lagging, yes? One thing wrong. One." She turned to a fresh page and wrote as she talked, her pencil moving fast. "Two days ago, the pantry door. Three days ago — wait, no, I'm getting ahead. Yesterday: the drawing room door, the service stairs, one window, the carriage house. Four things. Today: the library wall, the chapel altar, two lamp circuits, the garden shed reversed. Eight things." She looked up. "One. Then four. Then eight. It's doubling. It's not tidying, it's not closing its hand — it's *counting*, August. Counting down in the only language it knows, which is rooms. And if it doubles again tomorrow—"

"Sixteen," August said. "And the day after, thirty-two. And the day after that..." They did the math silently, lips moving, and Wren watched the number land on their face like a dropped weight. "The day before the harvest moon, the count would be two hundred fifty-six. And on the night itself—"

"On the night itself it runs out of house to subtract," Wren said. "Or it runs out of *us* to subtract around. Either way, whatever it's been counting toward arrives at zero, and zero is a door too. Just not one with hinges."

The lamp between them flickered. Neither of them reached to steady it. In this house you learned which anomalies to touch and which to leave strictly alone.

August sat back, and for a moment the surveyor's composure slipped entirely, and what was underneath was younger and more frightened and somehow more decent than the composure ever let show. "We could leave," they said. "Tonight. Front door's still there. My car's at the gate. We could be in a motel by midnight and you could sell me the story rights and we'd both live to be old."

Wren laughed — a short, ugly, grateful sound. "You know I can't."

"Do I? Because I've been wondering. You've got a key and a legacy and a dead man's instruction, but legacies aren't chains. Nobody would blame you."

"My grandda blamed himself," Wren said. "Forty years he kept the lights on and the doors shut and never once finished anything, and he still died thinking he'd failed whoever came before him, and whoever came before *that*, all the way back to the ones who built the thing. He died alone in a house that ate him a little more every year, and the last thing he said to me was *never finish anything*, and I swore it, and I've kept that oath for two years by living like a woman crossing a frozen river — small steps, no sudden weight, never stop moving." She closed the notebook. "But the house has stopped respecting the oath, August. It's counting whether I like it or not. And there are two ways to refuse to finish something. One is to walk away forever. The other is to be the one who decides how it ends."

August looked at her for a long time. Outside, the wind rose, and somewhere deep beneath the floorboards — below the cellar, below the thirteen steps, below whatever the house admitted to having — something exhaled, slow and satisfied, like a lung filling for the last time before speech.

"Nine days," August said again, but softly now, and it sounded less like a warning than like a promise being made.

"Nine days," Wren agreed. "And tomorrow it takes sixteen more things. So tonight we figure out what it's taking them *for* — because I've read enough of my grandda's ledger to know this: a countdown isn't a threat. It's an invitation. Something in this house expects guests on the thirty-first, and it's setting the table."

The lamp guttered and steadied. And down the hall, in the drawing room that no longer had a door, faintly, unmistakably, something began to set the table — the bright ring of china against china, plate after plate after plate, patient as arithmetic, counting its way down to none.
