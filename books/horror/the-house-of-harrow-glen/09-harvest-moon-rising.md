## 9. Harvest Moon Rising

Wren counted the days the way her grandfather had taught her to count the stairs: out loud, in the dark, so the house would know she was paying attention.

"Nine," she said to the kitchen ceiling, and the ceiling said nothing back, which was how she knew it was listening.

Nine days until the harvest moon rose fat and orange over the ridge behind Harrow Glen, and everything the house had been holding its breath for came due. She could feel it in the way the floorboards had begun to warm underfoot, faintly, like a sleeping animal's flank. In the way the taps ran a half-degree colder than they should have in September. In the way the mirrors — she'd covered them all with sheets weeks ago, but she still felt them — waited.

The stranger was asleep in the east parlor. She'd given him the parlor because it had no mirrors and only one door, and because it was the room farthest from the cellar, and because if the house decided to take him in the night she wanted to hear it happen. That last part she wasn't proud of. Grief does that. It makes you weigh people in terms of what their loss will cost you.

She put the kettle on and did not light the stove beneath it. The house boiled water on its own these days, when it felt like being generous, and she'd learned not to waste matches on things the house wanted to do itself. You let it think it was hosting you. That was the whole trick of surviving Harrow Glen: you were never a resident, you were a guest, and guests got eaten last.

The kettle sighed. Steam curled up and hung in the air longer than steam should, drawing shapes she refused to look at directly.

*Eight days after tonight,* she corrected herself. *Nine including tonight.* Her grandfather had been precise about this. Precision was armor. The house fed on vagueness the way other houses fed on damp — it got into your loose thinking and rotted it. *Never finish anything you start here,* he'd told her, pressing the iron key into her palm with hands that shook too much for a man of fifty-four. *Don't finish sentences. Don't finish meals. Don't finish—* and he'd stopped there, mid-instruction, either as a lesson or because he'd already forgotten what came next, and she'd never known which frightened her more.

Now he was gone three months, and the instruction was the only inheritance she had left besides the key and the house that had swallowed him.

She carried two cups of tea down the hall, past the wallpaper that rustled when she walked by — not moving, exactly. Rustling. Like paper remembering wind. She knocked on the parlor door with her elbow because her hands were full, and the sound of her knuckles against wood echoed wrong, doubled, as though someone inside had knocked at the same moment from the other side.

"Halloran." She pushed the door open with her shoulder. "Tea. And then you and I are going to have a conversation I've been avoiding for six days, and you're going to hate it."

August Halloran was sitting up on the daybed with his surveyor's kit open beside him like a surgeon's tray, and he looked — Wren noted this with something uncomfortably close to relief — terrible. Gray around the eyes. The kind of tired that sleep doesn't touch because it isn't coming from the body. Six days in the house had done to him what six years should have.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"I slept." He took the tea. His hand was steady; she gave him credit for that. "I just don't think the sleeping counted. I dreamed about measuring things. Corridors. Every time I measured one it came out different, and in the dream that was fine, that was normal, and I kept writing the numbers down and the numbers kept—" He stopped himself. Looked at the tea. "You said a conversation."

"The ledger." She sat across from him on the piano stool, which meant her knees were higher than his, which meant she could look down at him, which was petty of her but she took what advantages the house left available. "You found it in the cellar. Don't lie to me about it — the dust is disturbed down there in a way that isn't mice, and mice don't read Latin."

"It's not Latin. Mostly." August set the cup down without drinking. "It's a surveyor's notation system crossed with something older. I've been translating. Slowly."

"How slowly?"

"Slowly enough that I haven't gotten to whatever's written on the last pages yet." His eyes came up and met hers, and she saw the thing underneath the professional calm, the thing she'd been watching grow in him since the mirror incident: fear with nowhere to put itself. "Wren. What is this house? Not the appraisal answer. The real one."

So she told him.

Not all of it — she didn't have all of it, nobody living had all of it — but more than she'd ever said aloud to anyone, and certainly more than she'd intended to say to a man who'd arrived with a clipboard and a rental car and the word *appraisal* in his mouth like a shield. She told him about the founders, the seven families whose names were carved into the lintel stones, honored dead now, streets named after them in the village below. She told him about the harvest moon clause that appeared in every lease ever signed for Harrow Glen, the one the solicitors always called a formality, the one that ended every tenancy on the same night every twenty-eight years. She told him about the tenants who went unaccounted for. About her grandfather keeping the lights on and the doors shut for thirty-one years, alone, because somebody had to, because the alternative was the house going hungry and walking down into the village to feed itself.

"And the ledger," she finished, "is the leash. Or half of one. My grandfather thought the ritual in it was never completed. The founders started binding something into the walls — something the town gave them, willingly, back when giving things willingly still meant something here — and then the last founder ran, and the binding has been unfinished for two hundred years, and an unfinished binding isn't empty space, Halloran. It's a wound. And wounds want to close."

August was quiet for a long moment. Outside the window, the light was doing something wrong — thickening, ambering, though it was barely past noon. Autumn in the Glen came early and came hungry.

"My measurements," he said finally. "The rooms that don't match themselves. I thought it was settlement. Subsidence. I wrote *structural anomaly* in my report four times before I stopped writing reports."

"What changed your mind?"

He told her about the mirror. About the second of lag, the reflection that smiled after he'd stopped, the one that had kept smiling while he stood there with his heart slamming and his rationality shredding like wet paper. He told it flatly, in surveyor's language, coordinates and observations, and somehow that made it worse, hearing the impossible filed under *anomalies noted.*

"I need to see the ledger again," he said. "All of it. If the ritual's incomplete, I want to know what completing it looks like."

"No."

"Wren—"

"No." She stood up off the piano stool, which put them nearly eye to eye and made her feel marginally less like a child lecturing a schoolmaster. "You don't understand what you're asking. Reading the ritual is fine. Translating it is fine. But *completing* it—" Her voice caught on the word and she hated that, hated that the house probably heard the catch and filed it away somewhere. "My grandfather spent three decades making sure nothing in this house got finished. No sentence, no meal, no song, no task. You know why? Because finishing things here means something different than it means out there. The house collects completions. It's been waiting two hundred years for someone to finish the one thing that would set it free, and everyone who comes through that door is a candidate, and most of them are stupid enough to try."

"Then we're smarter than most." August rose too, and there was something new in his face now, something she hadn't seen in six days of careful professional distance. Stubbornness. Worse — conviction. "Because I've read enough of the ledger to know what happens if nobody finishes it. The moon rises, the lease comes due, and the house takes its rent. From whoever's standing inside. Which is currently you, and me, and nobody else in the world gives a damn."

"That's not—"

"Is anyone coming?" he asked, simply. "Anyone at all? Police, clergy, historians, anyone?"

Wren opened her mouth and closed it. Three months. Three months of letters unanswered, of the constable who drove up the lane once, glanced at the house, and developed a sudden urgent errand elsewhere. Of the vicar who'd told her gently that some properties were best left to God's own management. Of a village that had paid its rent in missing persons for two centuries and had learned to keep its ledgers clean.

"No," she said. "No one's coming."

"Then it's us." He said it like a measurement. Like a fact he'd verified twice. "You know the rituals — you grew up in this house, you know its rules better than anyone alive. And I can read the architecture. That's what I *do*, Wren. A building tells me things. Where it's load-bearing. Where it's lying. This house has been lying to me since the front door, but it can't help leaving the truth in the structure, and I'm starting to learn how it lies." He held out his hand — not to shake, she realized, just open, palm up, an offer with nothing in it. "Nine days. Your knowledge, my reading. That's the pact. Nothing else. No promises we can't keep, no secrets we can't afford."

Wren looked at the hand.

She hadn't touched another person in three months. She'd catalogued this absence the way she catalogued all the others — grandfather's chair unoccupied, phone silent, the particular silence of a house where you're the only heartbeat — and she'd told herself it was discipline, self-sufficiency, the Ashford inheritance. Standing here now, she recognized it for what it was. Loneliness worn so long it had calloused over and mistaken itself for strength.

Her grandfather had been alone in this house for thirty-one years. She'd always assumed it was necessity.

Looking at August Halloran's open hand, she suddenly wondered if it had been choice — if he'd believed, the way she'd believed, that letting anyone else in just gave the house more to take.

"If we do this," she said, "we do it my way. Lights stay on. Doors stay shut. Nothing gets finished — no task, no meal, no conversation — except the ritual, and only the ritual, and only when we're ready. You sleep with salt across your threshold. You don't answer anything that calls you by name after midnight, even if it sounds like me. *Especially* if it sounds like me."

"Agreed."

"And Halloran." She took his hand. His grip was dry and warm and human and it undid something in her chest that she'd welded shut years ago. "If it comes down to the last line of that ritual — the part Elias Vane's notes called *the price* — we walk away. We bar the cellar, we burn the ledger if we have to, and we spend the harvest moon in the village eating bad pub food like ordinary cowardly people. Swear it."

Something moved behind his eyes. Quick, furtive, gone before she could name it — a door closing somewhere in him, the way doors closed in this house.

"I swear," he said.

Later — much later, when she would have cause to remember this exact moment, standing in this amber-wrong noon light with a stranger's hand in hers — she would try to work out whether he'd lied to her then, or whether he'd only lied to himself, and whether there was any real difference in a house like this one. But that was later. Now there was only the grip, and the warmth, and the strange terrifying lightness of having decided, after three months alone, to trust somebody.

"Then we start tonight," she said. "Cellar. Bring your notebook and leave your skepticism — it won't fit through the door."

* * *

They worked by lamplight because the cellar ate electricity. Wren had learned that young: the bulb overhead would glow for a minute, dim, brown, and die, while the oil lamp her grandfather kept on the shelf burned steady as a promise. The house permitted fire down here. Perhaps because fire was old. Perhaps because it liked to watch the shadows the flames made.

The ledger lay open on the trestle table where August had left it, weighted at the corners with four brass washers arranged with surveyor's precision. Up close, in honest light, the pages looked worse than parchment had any right to look — not aged but *drained*, the color of things from which something had been slowly sipped for two hundred years. The ink had gone the brown of dried blood everywhere except the final pages, where it was still black. Wet-looking. Wren made herself stop looking at those pages.

"Read me what you have," she said, and settled onto the cold stone with her own notebook, the one with her grandfather's rituals copied out in her handwriting, each one annotated in his cramped marginal hand: *works, works if you're desperate, DO NOT, works.*

August read. He'd organized the translation into columns — original script, phonetic guesswork, plain English — and his plain English was better than she'd expected from a man who talked like a tax form. The opening invocations. The naming of the seven founders, each name followed by a phrase Wren recognized from the lintel stones. Then the middle sections, the ones that made her skin crawl even secondhand: instructions for the feeding, the tending, the *keeping*. Words for what lived in the walls that she noticed August pronounce carefully, flatly, refusing to give the syllables any weight.

And then, near the end, he stopped.

"Here." His finger rested on a passage where the columns ran short, the English fragmentary. "*...and the binding shall be sealed with a hand given freely, pressed to the threshold stone, and the giver shall be bound to the house as founder, and the house shall be bound to the giver as flesh is bound to bone, world without end, amen.*" He looked up. "That's the gap. Everything after this is commentary — later hands, additions, warnings. The actual sealing rite is missing. Torn out, maybe. Or never written."

"Or written," Wren said quietly, "and taken."

"By whom?"

"By whoever ran." She hugged her knees, lamp shadow swimming huge on the curved wall behind her. "The last founder. Elias Vane's account says seven names on the stone but only six signatures in the town charter. Someone didn't sign. Someone got to the last page of this and decided two hundred years of tenants was a fair price for their own skin."

August was quiet. The lamp flame leaned, though there was no draft, leaning toward the ledger like a listener.

"Two hundred years," he said at last, "and no one's tried to finish it."

"People have tried. People always try. My grandfather's margins—" She flipped her notebook open to a page near the end, where his handwriting deteriorated from careful to frantic. "See? *Attempted 1987, failed. Attempted 1994, failed, lost Thomas Reeve. Attempted 2003—*" She stopped. The entry for 2003 was a single line, and the line was unfinished, the pen stroke trailing off the edge of the paper as though the hand writing it had been lifted away mid-word.

August read it upside down. "*Attempted 2003, the house offered—*"

"Yeah," Wren said. "That's where it ends. That's the year he stopped talking about the ritual entirely. Started saying *never finish anything.*" She closed the notebook. Her hands weren't quite steady and she let them not be, down here in the dark where the house could see honesty was cheaper than performance. "Whatever it offered him, it worked. Thirty-one years of keeping the lights on instead."

The lamp flame straightened. Somewhere above them, through six floors of old timber and older intention, a door closed — softly, deliberately, the way a house closes a door when it wants you to know it has opinions about your conversation.

Wren stood. "We're done for tonight."

"We've barely started the—"

"*Done for tonight.*" She was already gathering her notebook, her lamp, her nerve. "Rule one, Halloran. We don't finish anything. Including conversations the house is interested in. Come on."

They climbed the thirteen steps — she counted them aloud, both of them, together, their voices overlapping on *thirteen* — and she barred the cellar door behind them with the iron rod, and only then did she realize August had gone pale in a way that had nothing to do with the dark.

"What?" she said. "What did you see?"

He was looking at the stairwell behind them, at the banister, at the space where the bottom step met the cellar floor. His mouth worked once before the words came.

"When we counted," he said. "Just now. Going up." He swallowed. "I counted fourteen steps, Wren. I've measured that staircase twice. There were thirteen."

Behind the barred door, in the dark below, something shifted its weight — slow, patient, comfortable — like a guest settling in for a long stay.

Wren put her hand on the cold iron rod and held on, and did not finish the sentence she was thinking.
