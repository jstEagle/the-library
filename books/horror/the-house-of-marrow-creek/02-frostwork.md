## 2. Frostwork

I woke at six-forty to a house that had been busy while I slept.

The frost was on the *inside* of the windows.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only religion I've ever kept. Frost forms on the exterior glass of an unheated building when interior moisture meets a cold pane — that's physics, that's Tuesday, that's fine. But this frost was on the interior surface of the bedroom window, and it wasn't the usual ferning, the random lacework that looks like God doodling on a cold morning. It was lines. Straight ones. Right angles. A grid.

I sat up in my sleeping bag with the particular slowness of a man who does not want to confirm what he is seeing, and then I confirmed it anyway, because I am constitutionally incapable of leaving a measurement unmeasured.

It was a floor plan.

Not a floor plan of anything I recognized — not yet, anyway — but unmistakably a floor plan: rooms rendered in frost-etched outline, walls drawn thin and true, doorways marked with small gaps in the line where a door would interrupt the wall. There were annotations. Tiny characters, delicate as etching, clustered in the corners of each room like dimensions on a surveyor's drawing. I pressed my nose to the glass. Dates. 1911. 1934. 1962. And names, or fragments of names, half-dissolved back into ice: *Hollis*, and something starting with *M*.

My first thought — and I'm not proud of this, but honesty is the whole point of confession, isn't it — my first thought was: *somebody's been here.* Some kid, some local, some bored teenager with a stencil and a spray bottle of glycerin, having fun with the out-of-towner. That thought lasted about four seconds, which is roughly how long it took me to notice there were no footprints in the dust between the bed and the window. The dust in that house was geological. It had strata. Nothing had crossed it but me.

My second thought was condensation. Thermal bridging. Old single-pane glass, moisture from my breath overnight, freezing in patterns influenced by microscopic irregularities in the pane — scratches, mineral deposits, the ghost of some long-peeled sticker. Patterns *look* meaningful; that's pareidolia, the same trick that puts faces in electrical outlets. The human brain is a meaning-making machine running unsupervised, and I've spent forty-one years being embarrassed by mine.

So I did what I always do when the world misbehaves. I photographed it.

Wide shot, detail shots, macro on the annotations. I shot the whole window, then the second window, which bore a different plan — the same footprint, but the rooms rearranged, as if someone had redrawn the house overnight and couldn't decide on a layout. Then I went downstairs, and the frost went with me.

Every interior window in the Hollis house had been drafted overnight. The parlor windows showed what I'd later understand was the ground floor — and here I stopped, camera halfway to my face, because the parlor's frost-plan didn't match the parlor. The room I stood in was smaller than the room on the glass. The hallway on the frost-map ran straight through where the staircase actually dog-legged. It was a floor plan of the house as it wanted to be, or as it had once been, or — and this thought arrived uninvited and stayed like a relative — as it *was*, somewhere I hadn't looked yet.

The kitchen window was the worst. No floor plan there. Just a date, written large across the pane in a hand that trembled slightly at the downstrokes:

**DEC 14**

Today's date.

I told myself it was a coincidence. I told myself it so firmly and at such length that you'd think I was being paid by the telling. December fourteenth is a common date. Houses have no way of knowing calendars. Ice does what ice does.

Then I noticed the small print beneath it, faint as breath: *one per winter.*

I took a picture of that too. In the viewfinder, for just a moment, the words seemed to sharpen, the way text does when it wants to be read. Then they softened again into mere frost, and I stood in a dead woman's kitchen telling myself stories about nucleation sites while my hands shook hard enough to blur the frame.

* * *

The general store in Marrow Creek is called Marrow Creek General Store, because the village apparently exhausted its naming budget sometime around the bell tower. It smells of kerosene, penny candy, and the specific staleness of a place where nothing has been restocked since the Reagan administration. I went in for coffee, batteries, and the reassuring normalcy of a receipt.

The man behind the counter rang up my items without once looking above my collarbone. I said good morning. He said "mm." I asked if the road over Harrow Hill was plowed yet, since I'd need to get my truck closer to the property. He said "mm" again, with the cadence of a man answering questions he'd already answered many times, to people who were never heard from again.

"You get much business out at the Hollis place?" I tried.

The mm acquired a warning note in it. Behind him, a woman stocking shelves — middle-aged, flannel, the posture of someone listening very hard while pretending not to — went still.

"Appraisal work," I offered. "Bank sent me. House has been vacant since—"

"Mm."

I have been stonewalled by homeowners, lawyers, and one memorable county assessor named Doreen, but this was different. This wasn't bureaucratic evasion. This was fear wearing the mask of indifference, and it was communal. Everyone in that store — the counter man, the shelf woman, an old fellow by the wood stove who hadn't turned a page of his newspaper since I walked in — everyone was performing the same act: *there is nothing here, there is nothing here, please stop asking.*

Only one person broke formation.

She was in the corner by the window, small and folded into herself like a letter, drinking tea from a thermos cup. Eighties, maybe. Eyes the pale washed-out blue of old denim. She watched me the way nobody else in the village would — directly, steadily, with something in her face that might have been pity or might have been appraisal, and believe me, I know appraisal when I see it.

"You're staying out there," she said. Not a question.

"I'm surveying the Hollis house, yes. Few days' work."

"Don't stay past dark."

"The dark? Ma'am, I'm sleeping there. Dark is kind of the package deal."

She set down her cup with great care, the way you put down something fragile when your hands aren't steady. "You'll want to leave before it," she said. "Before three-eleven."

"Three-eleven in the morning," I repeated. "Why three-eleven?"

The store had gone quiet enough that I could hear the wood stove ticking. The newspaper man had stopped pretending to read. The counter man stared at the register with the intensity of a man praying to it.

The old woman looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw — skepticism, loneliness, the particular hunger of a man who hasn't been looked at properly in years — made her decide something.

"It keeps books," she said. "That house. My grandmother called it the auditor. It counts everything. What you brought in with you, what you're carrying, what you owe." She paused. "What the town owes."

"That's a colorful bit of local lore."

"It's not lore." Her voice didn't rise at all, which somehow made it worse. "Lore is what you tell strangers to keep them from asking real questions. This is just what happens. Every winter. One entry." She gathered her coat, slow, joint by joint. "Edith Marsh. If you want to know more, don't come to me. Come to me anyway. But not past dark, and not before you've decided whether you'd rather know or sleep."

"That's quite the menu."

"It's the only one on offer." At the door she stopped, her back to me, and said, without turning around: "You signed something to come here. Papers. An engagement letter, maybe. Check who signed the other end."

And she left, bell jangling, into a morning so bright and cold it looked rehearsed.

* * *

I bought the coffee and the batteries. The counter man gave me change without touching my hand. As I left, the woman from the shelves caught up with me on the porch, moving fast for someone in flannel.

"She shouldn't have talked to you," the woman said, low. Not hostile — frightened on Edith's behalf, or maybe on her own. "Edith's family's paid enough. We all have. You do your numbers, you take your pictures, you go home, and everybody stays where they are. That's how it works here."

"How *what* works?"

She looked at me then, really looked, and her face did something complicated — guilt and warning and a terrible tired patience, the look of a person watching someone walk toward a cliff edge they've seen others walk toward.

"Just finish before dark," she said. "Please. For your own sake. Finish the appraisal and go."

* * *

Back at the house, I did what any rational professional would do: I pulled the engagement file from my truck and checked the paperwork, because Edith Marsh had gotten under my skin with that last remark, and because checking paperwork is my version of a security blanket.

The request for appraisal had come through the bank's standard channel. Referred by the estate. Signed authorization, dated fourteen months ago, granting access and commissioning a full structural valuation of the Hollis property.

The signature read **A. Hollis**.

I sat in the cab with the engine running and the heater ticking and told myself it was a descendant. Some cousin, some grandnephew, initials matching, no cause for alarm. Then I looked at the date beside the signature, and then at the copy of the death certificate I'd pulled from the county records the week before, because I'm thorough, thoroughness being both my profession and my only personality trait.

Albert Hollis, last resident of the house, died in 1987.

Fourteen months ago, Albert Hollis had invited me to appraise his home.

I sat there a long time. The engine idled. Somewhere behind me, up in the dark of the attic, something shifted its weight — or the house settled, or the wind moved through a chimney flue, or nothing happened at all, which by then felt like the least likely explanation on the table.

When I finally went inside, the kitchen window had changed.

The date was gone. In its place, written small and neat in the corner of the glass, in frost fine as pencil lead, was a new annotation — a little marginal note, the kind an appraiser makes in the corner of a survey:

*Subject property: occupied. Reassessing.*

And beneath the floor plans on the parlor window, the tiny characters in the room corners had multiplied overnight. New dates. New names. And in the room marked as the master bedroom — *my* room, the room where I'd slept — the frost had added a name of its own.

**V. VASQUEZ**

No date yet. Just the name, waiting, the way a ledger waits for the figure that completes the column.

I photographed it. Of course I did. And then I stood in the failing light with my camera warm against my chest and understood, finally, that I hadn't been documenting evidence.

I'd been helping it keep its books.

Outside, the first snow of the storm was starting to come down, fat and silent, and somewhere over the ridge, though I couldn't possibly have heard it from there, I would swear until my dying day that something began counting.
