## 5. What Was Invited

The letter arrived in my inbox at 6:47 a.m., which should have been my first clue that the universe was taking an unusual interest in my career. Appraisal requests do not come at dawn. Appraisal requests come on Tuesday afternoons, from property managers with the emotional warmth of damp carpet, and they say things like *please advise re: timeline*.

This one said only: *The Hollis house. Final appraisal required. Signed: E. Hollis.*

I'd been sitting in my rental with a thermocoffee and the general, low-grade dread of a man who has spent four nights sleeping in a house that measures itself. I opened the attachment because I am, above all things, a professional. The signature block read:

*Eleanor Hollis, Trustee, Hollis Estate Trust.*

Eleanor Hollis had died in 1984. I knew this because Edith Marsh had told me so over a jar of honey I hadn't asked for, in the tone of a woman describing weather that had already killed someone. I'd checked the county records that same evening, mostly to prove Edith wrong, which is the engine that runs my entire life. She had not been wrong. Eleanor Hollis, born 1901, died 1984, buried in the churchyard that no longer had a church, because the church no longer had a bell.

And yet here was her signature, fresh as paint, requesting an appraisal dated eleven days ago.

I sat with that for a while. I did the thing I always do, which is build a rational scaffold around the impossible and then stand on it like it's a balcony. Executors sign for the dead all the time. Estates outlive their owners; that's the whole point of an estate. Somewhere there was a lawyer, a trust administrator, a very tired paralegal who had typed *E. Hollis* into a template without thinking.

Except.

Except that the letter had been mailed from Marrow Creek. Postmarked the day after I'd arrived. And except that when I zoomed in on the signature — because I photograph everything now, I photograph frost and I photograph mail like a paranoid — the ink hadn't faded. It hadn't dried, either. When I tilted my screen, the pixels of the scanned signature glistened.

I am a surveyor. I deal in facts: boundaries, easements, the difference between what a property claims to be and what it is. And the fact, as of 6:47 that morning, was that a dead woman had invited me into her house, and the house had accepted the invitation on her behalf, and I had walked through the front door like a delivery of groceries.

"You were *summoned*," I said aloud, to the empty kitchen, and the house — I swear this, and I know how it sounds — the house settled. A long wooden sigh, like something rolling over to get comfortable.

* * *

The snow started at noon.

I want to be clear that I checked. I have a weather app like every other functioning adult, and the app said *partly cloudy, high of minus two*, which is the sort of forecast that lets you plan an exit. By one o'clock the sky had the color and texture of an old dishrag and snow was coming down in that horizontal, purposeful way that snow comes down when it has a job to do. By two, the plow I'd seen idling near the store all week was gone, and Main Street was a white throat.

I packed in eleven minutes. I am not proud of how fast I packed. I'd like to tell you I stood in the parlor and confronted the house, demanded answers, delivered a monologue. What I actually did was throw my laptop into my bag with the charger still tethered to the wall, yank the cord out of the outlet, and say "nope" — out loud, twice — to a house that had done nothing but exist at me.

The frost on the dining room window watched me go. I mean the pattern on it. The frost had spent the week mapping the house room by room, floor plans in white lace, and overnight it had added a new wing to the drawing: a small room I had never surveyed, with a single window and a figure in it, drawn in the confident shorthand of a child's crayon. The figure was standing. The figure was waiting.

I did not look at it on my way out. I looked at it the whole way out, the way you look at a dog that's watching you eat.

The truck started on the second try, which felt like a personal insult. Of course it started. Things that run are supposed to run. I backed down the drive, and in the rearview the Hollis house sat in its falling snow like a face behind frosted glass, every window lit with that same white lace, and I could have sworn — could have *sworn* — the pattern had grown a new room since morning.

I turned onto County Road 9 and drove.

* * *

Here is what I know about roads, professionally: they do not loop. A road is a line. It has a beginning, an end, and a legal obligation to connect them in a manner consistent with its posted signage. County Road 9 runs nine miles from Marrow Creek to the junction with Route 6, where there is a gas station, a cell tower, and the rest of my life.

I drove it for what my odometer insisted was eleven miles. The snow thickened. The trees on either side went from bare oak to — and I want to be careful here, because I've had four days to find a rational explanation and I haven't — to pine. Dense, black, wrong pines, standing too close together, in a county where the forestry survey shows no pines for six miles in any direction. The road narrowed. The snow came down so hard the world outside the headlights was a white wall with suggestions in it.

And then the trees opened, and there was a fence. A wrought-iron fence, snow-capped, running along both sides of a gate.

The front gate of the Hollis house.

I sat in the idling truck with my hands at ten and two and laughed. It came out of me in one ugly bark, the laugh of a man whose scaffolding has just collapsed with him on it. I checked the odometer: eleven miles. I checked the clock: forty minutes elapsed. I put the truck in reverse, turned around, and drove the other direction, back toward town, because if the road was a loop, maybe the loop had two exits.

Twenty minutes later I passed a fence. A wrought-iron fence, snow-capped, and a gate, and behind it, the Hollis house, its windows all facing me at once, which is geometrically impossible for a house and yet there it was, arranged like a portrait that had turned on its nail.

I tried the third time with the GPS on. The little blue arrow of me traveled confidently down County Road 9, and then, on the screen, the arrow did a slow, smooth pirouette — not the truck, the *arrow*, the map itself bending around me like water around a stone — and arrived at the gate. The app's voice said, cheerfully, "You have arrived at your destination."

"I have *not*," I told it. "My destination is a gas station with a coffee machine that works."

The snow kept falling. The house kept waiting. And I understood, in the way you understand things when your body gets there before your mind does, that the storm wasn't weather. The storm was a wall. The house hadn't trapped me. The house had simply stopped pretending I could leave, the way a spider doesn't chase the fly — it just waits for the fly to finish believing in the window.

I sat there until the fuel gauge made its argument, then drove the loop one last time, slow, and let it deliver me to the front door like a courtesy shuttle.

* * *

Inside, the house was warm.

That was the worst part, I think. Worse than the loop, worse than the signature. Four days of cold rooms and my breath hanging in the air, and now the radiators ticked like a contented clock and the hall smelled faintly of woodsmoke, and the house was *welcoming* me home. It had never wanted me to go. The cold had been the appraisal. The warmth was the acceptance.

The frost had finished while I was out.

Every window on the ground floor now carried the same drawing, repeated like wallpaper: the house itself, complete, every room labeled in that spidery white script. And at the center of the map, in the small room with the single window — the room that didn't exist in any blueprint, the room the house had been saving — the figure was no longer standing.

It was sitting. At a desk. And beneath it, in letters an inch high, sharp as cut glass:

**RAFAEL VASQUEZ — 3:11 A.M.**

Tomorrow's date.

I stood in the hall with my bag still on my shoulder and read my own name in frost, and the terror I'd been carrying all week — the manageable, professional terror, the terror I'd been filing under *structural anomaly* — turned over and showed me its other face, which was fury. Pure, white, furnace-door fury. Because this wasn't haunting. Haunting is accidental; haunting is a record left spinning in an empty room. This was an *appointment*. This was a dead woman's signature and a looped road and a warm radiator, all of it arranged, all of it *itemized*, because somewhere in this house's arithmetic I was a line entry. A receivable. The annual payment, delivered by mail, signed for by the dead.

They had *hired* me to die here. That was the part that made me laugh again, that ugly bark, in the warm hall, alone. Somewhere in 1911 or 1984 or last Tuesday, this house had learned to use the mail. It had written a job posting. *Wanted: surveyor, detail-oriented, must not believe in ghosts.* And I had answered it, because the pay was decent and because I was lonely in the specific way that makes a man take a job in a town where nobody meets his eyes.

I set my bag down in the hall. I don't know why. It felt like surrender and it felt like staying, and both of those were true.

"You want an appraisal," I said to the staircase. "Fine. I'm a surveyor. It's what I do. But I don't sign off on anything until I've seen the books."

The house settled around me, that long wooden sigh, and I could have sworn it sounded pleased.

In the cellar, where I'd found the ledger two nights ago, a light was on. I hadn't left a light on. The door at the bottom of the stairs stood open, and from the dark below came the smell of cold paper and old iron, and beneath it, faint as memory, the sound of a bell that had been melted down a hundred years ago, clearing its throat.

I went down. Because the frost had dated me for 3:11 tomorrow morning, and it was only 6 p.m., and a man with a deadline should spend his remaining hours doing his job.
