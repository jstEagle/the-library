## 1. The Songs No One Sings Twice

The key still smelled of her grandmother's kitchen — tallow and dried thyme and something older underneath, something like the inside of a church. Juniper Almeida stood on the front step of the house at the end of Old Chapel Road and turned the brass over in her palm until it warmed, as if warmth were permission. The door had not been opened in eleven months. She knew this because she had been counting, the way she counted everything now: days since the funeral, verses collected, silences recorded.

She let herself in.

Dust hung in the hall like a held breath. The light through the landing window fell in a long amber column, thick enough to stir, and motes rose around her ankles as she crossed the boards, reluctant to be disturbed. Everything was where it had always been: the coat hooks with their empty hooks, the umbrella stand with its one broken umbrella, the photograph of her grandfather on the stair wall, smiling his one photographed smile. The house exhaled around her, slow and papery, and Juniper set down her cases — the recorder, the microphones, the laptop, the notebooks in their oilcloth wrapping — and stood a moment in the middle of the hall with her eyes closed, listening to the particular silence of a house that has been waiting.

It was not an empty silence. That was the first thing she would write in her field journal that night, though she did not yet know why. *Empty* silences have a texture like felt. This one had grain. It ran in a direction. If she had been asked to say which direction, she would have said *down*.

But that was for later. For now there was unloading to do, and tea to make in a kitchen that had not been hers for twenty years and had never once stopped being hers, and a village at the end of the lane full of people who had known her before she could walk and who would want, each of them, to touch her elbow and say *you have her hands* and then change the subject.

She had come to do a job. That was what she told herself, and it was mostly true. The Almeida song cycle — nine verses, maybe ten; no one agreed — had been sung in this valley for longer than the parish records went back, and every folklorist who had come sniffing after it had left with nothing but secondhand fragments and a vague sense of having been politely escorted off the premises. But Juniper had grown up here. Juniper had a dead grandmother whose house key fit her hand. And Juniper had heard the songs sung whole exactly once, when she was seven, by women who had since died or gone quiet, and she remembered — this was the strange part, the part she had built a career on refusing to examine — she remembered every note.

* * *

Mrs. Ondrusek was shelling peas on her porch when Juniper came down the lane the next morning, and the old woman's face did the thing faces did in this village: brightened, then banked, like a stove being damped for the night.

"Juniper Almeida," she said. "Look at you. You've her hands."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Because it's true." Mrs. Ondrusek patted the bench beside her, and Juniper sat, and for a while they talked about safe things — the weather, the state of the road, who had married whom — while peas rattled into a bowl like small green applause. Then Juniper took out her notebook, and Mrs. Ondrusek's hands slowed.

"I'm recording the songs," Juniper said gently. "Grandmother's songs. For the archive. You know them better than anyone living."

"Do I." It wasn't a question, and it wasn't agreement either.

"The harvest verse. The one about the chapel bell. I remember you singing it at the well-dressing when I was small. There's a line about—" she closed her eyes— "*the water remembers what the stone forgets.*"

Mrs. Ondrusek had gone very still. A pea rolled off her lap and onto the boards, and neither of them moved to pick it up.

"You shouldn't have come back to that," the old woman said at last, and her voice had changed — flattened out, careful, the voice of someone stepping around ice. "Your grandmother kept those things where they belonged."

"In her head?"

"In her house." Mrs. Ondrusek resumed shelling, briskly, as if the pod had offended her. "And look how that ended."

"She died in her sleep, Mrs. Ondrusek. Peacefully. Everyone said so."

"Everyone says a lot of things." The old woman looked out at the lane, at the long green tunnel of it running toward the chapel ruin, and something moved behind her face like a fish under pond ice. Then, abruptly, as if deciding that refusal itself was too revealing, she began to sing.

Her voice was thin and dry and true. It was the water-remembering verse, all right, and Juniper's pen flew, and her heart ached with the beauty of it, the modal turns that belonged to no scale taught in any conservatory, the way the melody seemed to walk downhill toward some resolution it never reached. Mrs. Ondrusek sang four lines. Five. Six—

On the seventh line, mid-word, she stopped.

Not faltered. Stopped. Her mouth remained open around the shape of a syllable that never came out, and her hands lay flat on her apron, and for three full seconds she stared at the middle distance with an expression Juniper had seen only once before — on the face of a woman in a Romanian village who had begun to answer a question about the dead and then remembered, visibly, physically, that she must not.

"That's how it goes," Mrs. Ondrusek said finally, in a voice like a door closing. "That's all of it anybody needs."

"There are more lines. I've heard more lines."

"No," said Mrs. Ondrusek, "you haven't," and picked up her bowl of peas and went inside, and the screen door banged behind her with a sound horribly like the last note of a verse.

Juniper sat alone on the porch a long moment. In the bowl, abandoned, the shelled peas gleamed like small wet eyes. She wrote in her notebook, beneath the six lines she'd caught: *Subject ceased mid-phrase. Not forgetting — withholding. Or prevented.* And then, because her training demanded honesty even when her nerves objected: *Prevented by what?*

* * *

It went the same way at the Harrow farm, and at the post office, and at the King's Arms, where the landlord's father had supposedly been the finest singer in the parish and the landlord himself turned out to be made entirely of weather talk and beer mats. Six lines. Seven. Never eight. Always mid-word, always the same flinch, the same flat careful voice afterward, the same sudden urgent need to discuss anything else. By late afternoon Juniper had a notebook full of matching fragments and a recorder full of matching silences, and she understood that she was not collecting a song cycle. She was collecting its amputations.

There was one exception, and it frightened her more than the refusals.

At the top of the lane, where Old Chapel Road bent past the ruined chapel itself, an old man named Tomas sat on a bench that faced the chapel wall, as he apparently faced it every day of his life. He was small and neat and brown as a nut, and when Juniper greeted him he looked at her with an attention so complete it was almost physical, the way a dog looks at a stranger carrying something under its coat.

"You're Edith's girl," he said. Not a question.

"Granddaughter."

"You're here for the songs."

"Yes." She hesitated. "You know that."

"I know everything that walks up this road." He patted the bench. She sat. He did not sing, and she did not ask him to, and somehow between those two refusals a conversation happened anyway — sideways, oblique, conducted in the grammar of people who have both survived the same place.

"Your grandmother sang them twice," Tomas said, after a while. "Once for keeping. Once for feeding."

"For feeding what?"

He smiled. It was a kind smile, and it terrified her. "You're a scholar. You'll find out. Scholars always do — that's the trouble with them." He stood, joints cracking like green wood, and looked at the chapel wall a moment longer. Ivy had taken most of it, but the foundation stones showed through, and Juniper saw, with a small cold start, that they were scored with marks. Tallies. Hundreds of them, worn shallow, centuries deep.

"Count your chairs," Tomas said, turning to go. "Every night. And if there's ever one more than there should be — don't sit in it. Don't let anything else sit in it either."

"Tomas—"

"And Juniper." He paused at the bend of the lane without looking back. "Your grandmother left you more than a house. Leave things where she left them. Some doors are shut from the inside."

Then he was gone down the hill, a small neat figure diminishing into the green, and Juniper sat on the bench in the last of the light with her heart going oddly, looking at the tally marks on the chapel stones and thinking, against every instinct of her profession, *I don't want to count these.*

* * *

The house at dusk was different from the house at noon. All houses were, she told herself, unpacking her equipment in the kitchen, running cables, checking levels — all houses changed register when the light went, that was architecture and psychology, nothing more. But she found herself leaving the kitchen door open so she could see the hallway, and leaving the hallway light on so she could see the stairs, and when the refrigerator cycled on with a groan she wrote in her journal, in handwriting less steady than she liked: *Day 1. Six informants. Fragments only. Pattern: cessation at line 7–8, involuntary. One warning re: chairs (see Tomas). Grandmother's role unclear — keeper? Singer? Both?*

Beneath that, after a pause, she added: *The house smells like she just stepped out.*

It did. That was the unbearable thing. Eleven months of shut windows and the air should have gone stale and mineral, cellarish, dead. Instead the kitchen smelled faintly of bread that had been baked recently enough to remember, and under that the tallow-and-thyme of the key, and under *that* — very faint, very far down, the way a river runs under a field — something like candle smoke and cold stone.

Juniper made tea she didn't drink and carried her grandmother's tape collection in from the hall, twelve reels and a shoebox of cassettes, each labeled in that upright schoolroom hand: *Verse I — Keeping. Verse II — Keeping. Verse III — Keeping.* On and on, all the way to *Verse IX — Keeping*, and then, apart from the others, in a box of its own, a single cassette labeled simply:

*Tape Seven. Do not play at night.*

Juniper held it a long time under the kitchen light. Outside, the lane darkened by degrees, and somewhere down the valley a dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

It was not yet night. There were, she estimated, forty minutes of legal daylight left, and she was a folklorist, and folklore was the discipline of doing the frightening thing carefully, with notes.

She fed the cassette into her grandmother's old deck, threaded the connection to her own recorder, and pressed play.

Hiss. Then her grandmother's voice, younger than Juniper remembered it, clear as water over stone, singing the harvest verse — the whole of it, all nine lines, the melody walking downhill toward the resolution it never reached. And reaching it. That was the difference, Juniper realized, sitting forward, pen forgotten. Every fragment the villagers had given her broke off short. This version *arrived*. The final cadence turned where it had always refused to turn, and the effect was like a knot coming undone, like a door—

And then another voice answered.

It came from nowhere on the track — from behind the singer, from below the singer, from the room the microphone was standing in and also from no room at all. It sang the next line. There was no next line. Juniper had transcribed this cycle from three archives and two dissertations and her own seven-year-old memory, and there was no tenth line, and the voice sang it anyway, low and sweet and wrong, a voice like a boy's and like an old man's and like wind finding a bottle, and her grandmother on the tape drew one audible breath —

The tape ended. Mid-word. Mid-breath. The deck's counter read 00:00 and the kitchen was silent except for the refrigerator, except for Juniper's own blood, except for — very faintly, from below the floor, from the direction of the cellar stairs — a single sustained note, fading, like the last of a verse someone else had been singing along.

Juniper did not move for a long time. The light through the window went from amber to ash to blue.

Then she wrote, in a hand she barely recognized as her own:

*Day 1, continued. Tape Seven played at dusk. Anomalous voice, unidentified, sings material absent from all known sources. Recording ends mid-word — deliberately? Damaged?*

And beneath that, because the house was quiet in a way that had grain and direction, and the direction was down:

*I think my grandmother was not archiving these songs.*

*I think she was answering them.*
