## 2. Inventory of a Dead Woman's Kitchen

The key still turned the way it always had — with a small, reluctant click, like a throat clearing before an apology. Juniper stood in the doorway of her grandmother's house and let the smell of it arrive: dust and lavender soap, old wool, the ghost of woodsmoke baked so deep into the plaster that no amount of seasons could burn it out. Forty years of her childhood lived in that smell. Three days of her grief would have to learn to live in it too.

She set her cases down just inside the threshold — the recorder case first, then the box of archival supplies, then the duffel she'd packed in a hurry and repacked twice because she couldn't decide whether mourning required black or simply required clean. The floorboards received her weight with a familiar complaint, one plank near the door that had always announced visitors, and Juniper found herself saying, "It's me, Gran," to an empty hall, because some habits are load-bearing.

No one answered. Of course no one answered. But the house held the words a moment longer than physics strictly required, the way a bell holds its note, and she chose not to think about that.

* * *

She began where a folklorist begins: with an inventory. Her grandmother had been Edith Almeida for eighty-one years, collector of ballads, keeper of the village's singing traditions, a woman whose field recordings were cited in three university syllabi and whose funeral had been attended by nearly everyone in the parish and sung at by almost none of them. That last fact had snagged in Juniper's mind like a burr since the burial. She'd told herself it was grief etiquette, some local custom she'd never learned. But it itched.

The kitchen first. It was the heart of any house on Old Chapel Road, and it had been the heart of this one especially — the room where Juniper had learned harmonies before she'd learned cursive, where her grandmother's hands had taught hers to knead bread and to keep time, one palm flat against the table's edge, tapping out the meter of songs about drowned girls and unfaithful soldiers and a green road that only appeared at certain hours.

The table was still there. Oak, scarred, older than either of them. And around it —

Juniper stopped with her notebook half-open and her pen uncapped.

Chairs. She counted them without meaning to, the way you count stairs in the dark. Six. A mismatched congregation: two ladder-backs from the farmhouse set, a bentwood chair with a cane seat gone soft as an earlobe, a pine kitchen chair painted the faded blue of a robin's egg, and two heavy carven things at the ends that might once have belonged to a church vestry or a magistrate's parlor. They were beautiful, all of them, in the way of objects that had been loved hard and long.

But they didn't match the photographs.

Juniper had spent the previous evening, unable to sleep in the village inn, going through the digital scans she'd made of the family albums years ago for a conference paper. In those photographs — Christmas 1974, a christening in 1981, the blurry golden afternoon of her own eighth birthday — the kitchen table sat under a window with light pouring through, and around it stood four chairs. Four matching chairs, spindle-backed, honey-colored, unmistakably a set. She remembered them. She had eaten off her knees at one of them while her grandmother sang the washing-up song.

Four matching chairs. Not six strangers.

She wrote it down anyway, because that was the discipline: *Dining set — six chairs, non-matching. Family photos (1970s–90s) show four, matched.* Then she underlined nothing, because there was nothing yet to underline, and told herself the obvious explanations — replacements, breakage, the ordinary erosion of households — until the words looked less like sense and more like a bandage over a splinter she couldn't see.

* * *

The cataloging took the morning. She worked the way her grandmother had taught her to work a song: slowly, completely, letting each object give up its name.

The dresser drawers yielded cutlery wrapped in newspaper from 1996; a tin of buttons that had once, mysteriously, contained her lost milk tooth; recipe cards in a hand that went from confident to tremulous across the decades, the ink thinning like a pulse. On the windowsill above the sink, three jars of salt, each one open, each one refilled recently enough that the crystals still glittered. Salt for cooking, she thought. Except that cooks kept salt by the stove, not the window, and cooks did not keep *three*.

Under the back door, when she knelt to check the draft — the house had always whistled in winter — she found a line of salt poured along the threshold, packed into the gap between floorboard and door, dense and deliberate, renewed many times. The wood beneath it was stained faintly white, the way stone goes pale where water has run over it for years. Years of renewing. Decades.

She checked the front door. Salt. The cellar door at the end of the hall — and here her pen hovered — salt, and something else: a horseshoe nailed points-down above the frame, and a sprig of something dried and brown tucked into the crack, rowan by the look of what remained of its berries, though rowan was a highland tree and did not, to Juniper's knowledge, grow anywhere within ten miles of Old Chapel Road. Someone had carried it here. Someone had carried it here *on purpose*, and more than once, given the little drift of old stems swept into the corner like the leavings of a ritual performed so often it had become chore.

Her grandmother had been a folklorist too. Juniper knew all the protective vernacular — iron over doors, bread and salt for thresholds, the red thread, the mirror turned on St. John's Eve. She'd written a paper on apotropaic domestic practice that had been, briefly, moderately read. She knew what salt lines meant the way she knew what a minor key meant: academically, fluently, and without ever having needed one.

But scholars *collect* these practices. They don't *practice* them. Edith Almeida had documented the village's beliefs with a scholar's detachment and a footnote's coolness, and all the while her own doorframes had been garrisoned like a border town expecting trouble.

Juniper sat down on the blue chair — the robin's-egg one, chosen at random, or so she believed at the time — and wrote, in the careful hand she used for field notes:

*Subject's protective practice: extensive, sustained, concealed. Discrepancy between published skepticism and private observance. Question: what was she afraid of?*

Then, after a moment, she crossed out *afraid* and wrote *guarding against*, which was not the same thing, and crossed *that* out too, and left the line blank, because the blank felt more honest.

* * *

The tapes were in the front room, in the oak cabinet that had always been locked when Juniper was a child — locked, she now realized, with the same small brass lock as the cellar door, a matched pair, as if the house had two secrets and only one key between them. The lock hung open now. Her grandmother had stopped locking things, apparently, somewhere in the last year, the way people stop locking things when they know they won't be the one answering the door.

Inside: shoeboxes. Dozens of them, labeled in Edith's upright, unhurried script, each tape dated and titled. *Mrs. Callow — lullabies, partial. Choir practice, St. Brigid's, 1961. The Wren Song — Tomas H., refuses second take.* Juniper lifted them out one by one, reading labels like gravestone inscriptions, and felt the familiar lift of scholarly hunger underneath the grief, the two feelings braided so tightly she could no longer tell which was pulling.

There were forty-one tapes. She counted twice. On the inside lid of the cabinet, taped to the wood where a child wouldn't look and a stranger wouldn't think to, was a slip of paper in her grandmother's hand:

*Play them in order. Play them alone. Do not play Seven after dark.*

Juniper read it four times. It was not signed. It did not need to be; she knew the handwriting better than her own, having inherited its stubborn downstrokes. It was not frightened handwriting. It was *instructing* handwriting, the hand of a woman leaving directions the way you leave directions to the fuse box — practical, unsentimental, assuming the reader will do as they're told because the alternative is standing in the dark.

*Do not play Seven after dark.*

Which meant, of course, that Seven existed. Which meant that whatever was on Seven had required a warning, and warnings of that specificity — *after dark*, not *ever* — were the kind folklorists filed under *taboo narrative structure*: the rule exists to be broken, the prohibition is the plot. Every ballad she'd ever collected worked that way. *Don't look behind you. Don't ask his name. Don't sing it twice.*

Juniper looked at the window. Mid-afternoon light lay across the floorboards in long amber planks, harmless as honey. Hours of daylight left. Plenty of time to finish the inventory, to unpack, to make tea in her grandmother's kettle and stand in her grandmother's kitchen being sad in a manageable, scholarly way.

She put the slip of paper in her notebook, between pages, where it pressed flat and waited.

* * *

By late afternoon she had cataloged the pantry, the parlor, her grandmother's bedroom — where the bed was made with hospital corners so crisp they looked rehearsed, as if Edith had known someone would inspect them — and the small study with its desk and its wall of notebooks. The notebooks alone would take weeks. She saved them deliberately, the way you save the best verse of a song, hoarding the pleasure of it.

It was when she came back through the hall, carrying the last box of tapes to stack by the record player, that she noticed the dining chairs again.

Not all six. Just the blue one.

She had left it pushed in under the table, flush, the way she'd found every chair in the house — her grandmother had been strict about that, chairs tucked in, surfaces clear, a tidiness that in retrospect looked less like neatness than like *positions held*. Now it stood out from the table by perhaps eight inches. Angled, very slightly, toward the door.

Juniper stood in the kitchen doorway with the box in her arms and looked at it for a long time. The house was quiet in the way houses are quiet at that hour, when the day's sounds have packed up but the night's haven't arrived, a held breath between shifts. The light had gone the color of weak tea. Somewhere upstairs a pipe ticked as it cooled, and she clung to that tick like a handrail.

Drafts, she told herself. Old houses breathe. She had cataloged a hundred old houses; she knew their respiratory habits. She set the box down, walked to the chair, and pushed it in until it touched the table's edge, her palm flat on the worn seat, and said aloud, because saying it aloud made it smaller:

"There. Tucked in."

The word *tucked* came out wrong somehow, tender and terrible at once, the way you'd speak of tucking in a child, and she snatched her hand back as if the chair had been warm.

It wasn't warm. Of course it wasn't warm. Wood holds the day's heat; kitchens hold the day's life; there was tea to make and tapes to shelve and a warning on a slip of paper that said *after dark*, and the sun was going down behind the hill in exactly the unhurried way that suns do when you wish they'd hurry.

Juniper made tea. She drank it standing up, watching the six mismatched chairs arrange themselves in her memory beside the four matched ones in the photographs, and the discrepancy sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Two chairs' worth of difference. Someone had removed a set and replaced it with strays — or the strays had come from somewhere, the way strays do, invited in and never sent away.

On the windowsill, the three jars of salt caught the last of the light and threw it back in small white glitters, patient as teeth.

Outside, the lane darkened. Inside, Juniper washed her cup, dried it, and set it on the rack, and only then noticed that the drying rack already held a cup — an old one, chipped at the lip, the one her grandmother had used every morning of Juniper's remembered life — sitting there as if recently washed, as if someone had drunk from it that morning and rinsed it and set it carefully, thoughtfully, in its place.

Edith had died eleven days ago. The vicar had found her. The house had been closed since.

Juniper looked at the cup for a long moment. Then she took it down, washed it again though it was clean, dried it, and put it away in the cupboard, closing the door on it gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping room.

In the front room, forty-one tapes waited in their shoeboxes, and the seventh of them waited hardest. Through the window, the last light drained off the hills, and the house began, board by board and hinge by hinge, to make the sounds it makes when it is alone with someone new.

Juniper did not play Tape Seven that night. But she slept with the notebook open on her chest, and the slip of paper pressing its instructions against her heartbeat, and in the morning — she would remember this later, precisely, the way you remember the exact shape of a step missed in the dark — in the morning, the blue chair was out from the table again.

Closer to the fire.
