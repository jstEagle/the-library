## 5. What the Photographs Remember

The albums lived on the landing shelf, in the order of a life: blue cloth, brown leather, green plastic, and then the newer ones with adhesive pages whose yellowed overlays had begun to peel like sunburned skin. Juniper carried them down two at a time and arranged them on the kitchen table, which she had cleared of tapes and notebooks and the salt canister she'd caught herself reaching for without deciding to.

Morning light came in through the east window the way it always had, slanting and honey-colored, catching the dust motes that never seemed to settle in this house no matter how long it stood empty. She had opened all the windows the night before. She had counted the chairs before bed — six, the correct number — and again on waking, out of a habit she was already forming and did not want to examine. Six. Tomas's voice in her head: *Count them every night, girl. And never sit in the extra one.* But there had been no extra one, and she had felt, absurdly, a flicker of something like disappointment before she felt the fear that should have come first.

She set up the scanner on the sideboard, plugged it into the power strip, and began with the blue album because it was the oldest and therefore, in her professional opinion, the most urgent. Archival work was a race against chemistry. Emulsions faded, silver migrated, glue yellowed and ate its own pictures. Every year she delayed was a year the past lost a little more resolution, a little more of its argument with forgetting.

Her grandmother had been the family's photographer, which meant the albums were not a record of the family so much as a record of what her grandmother had chosen to look at. Juniper had understood this at twenty-two, in a graduate seminar on vernacular photography, and had written a paper about it that her professor called "perceptive" and her grandmother, when Juniper read it aloud to her over the phone, called "a lot of words to say I liked looking at your grandfather."

The first photographs were from the fifties: a young woman in a housedress on the front step of this very house, squinting into sun that had stopped existing before Juniper was born. The porch rail behind her. The road beyond, unpaved then, rutted with cart tracks. Old Chapel Road before it was old in any way that mattered.

Juniper fed the pages into the scanner one by one, watched the images assemble themselves on her laptop screen in their clean, digitized permanence, and let the rhythm of it carry her. This was the part of the work she loved most, the part that had made her a folklorist instead of something sensible: the feeling of handling time directly, of taking something fragile and mortal and giving it a second body that would not decay. Preservation as a form of tenderness. Her whole career was, she realized now, sitting in her dead grandmother's kitchen, an extended conversation with her grandmother about what deserves to be kept.

She was three pages into the blue album when she noticed the chair.

It was in the background of a photograph dated, in her grandmother's upright hand on the album page, *August 1961 — harvest supper*. The picture showed the kitchen table — this table, though then it stood nearer the window — crowded with dishes, and around it the blurred suggestion of people caught mid-laugh, her grandmother young among them with her hair in a kerchief. And behind the crowd, against the wall where the sideboard now stood, there was a chair.

A high-backed chair. Dark wood, taller than the others, its back rising above the heads of the seated guests like a headstone at a crowded churchyard.

Juniper stopped the scanner. She pulled the album page toward her and looked at the original print, a small rectangle of Kodak color gone amber at the edges. She had to hold it close to the window. There — yes. The chair was there in the original too. She had simply never seen it, the way you can read a sentence twenty times and miss the word that doesn't belong, because the eye edits toward sense and a chair at a harvest supper made perfect sense.

Except that it didn't. She knew this table. She knew this kitchen the way you know a room you dreamed in as a child. There had never been a high-backed chair in it. There were six ladder-back chairs with woven rush seats, and her grandmother had re-seated them herself one summer, and Juniper remembered the smell of the soaking rushes, and there had never — not in any photograph she'd ever paged through, not in any story — been a seventh chair with a back like a church pew.

She checked the other pictures on the page. The chair appeared in two of the three. In the third, cropped tighter, it was just out of frame — but the edge of its back was there, a dark vertical sliver at the margin, like something leaning in to watch.

*You're tired,* she told herself. *You didn't sleep. You're finding patterns because pattern-finding is what you do.*

It was a reasonable explanation. She had built a career on reasonable explanations; she could produce them the way other people produced small talk. But her hands, she noticed, had gone cold in the warm kitchen, and she had turned the original print face-down on the table without deciding to, the way you turn a mirror to the wall.

She made tea she didn't drink and kept working, because the work was the only sane thing available, and because stopping would mean thinking.

* * *

The brown leather album covered the seventies. Juniper scanned it page by page, and now she was looking, and looking was its own kind of danger. She found the chair again at a christening — not this family's; a neighbor's baby, held up to the light on the front lawn, the whole village gathered — and again at a funeral gathering on this same porch, the mourners arranged in rows, and the chair standing apart from them at the edge of the frame, empty, its back to the road.

In every picture, it was empty.

That was the detail that made her stop pretending it was tiredness. Not the chair itself — old houses accumulate furniture the way rivers accumulate stones; things drift in from estate sales and dead relatives and no one remembers when. But a chair that appeared in photograph after photograph across decades, always empty, always at the edge, always facing the group — that was not accumulation. That was attendance.

She began a log, because the log was her weapon and her shield, the folklorist's answer to the uncanny: *write it down, give it a number, and it belongs to you instead of you to it.* Date of photograph. Location in frame. Orientation. Empty or occupied. The act of cataloging steadied her hands. The act of cataloging did nothing at all for the cold spreading up from her stomach.

By early afternoon she reached the green plastic album, the eighties, her own decade, her own childhood pressed in glossy squares. Here she was: Juniper at four, on a tricycle on the front path, mouth open around some vanished laugh. Juniper at seven, holding a cat that had been named Biscuit and had scratched her, and her grandmother's hand on her shoulder. Juniper at nine, asleep on the parlor floor during a storm, and her grandmother in the doorway behind her, and —

Juniper leaned into the screen.

Behind her grandmother, in the interior darkness of the parlor, the high-backed chair. Inside the house now. It had been inside the house the whole time she'd been a child in it, and she had never once seen it, had run past that doorway a thousand times, had played on that parlor floor, and the chair had stood in the dark behind her grandmother like a chaperone, like a keeper, like a thing on duty.

She sat back. The kitchen was very quiet. The refrigerator hummed its one note. Outside, a crow said something brief and ugly and was gone.

*It's a photo defect,* she tried. *Lens flare. A printing artifact. The same lab, the same paper, the same chemical ghost recurring across thirty years of different cameras and different films.*

She was a scholar. She knew the literature on photographic anomalies, on pareidolia, on the way the human visual system will build a chair out of shadow the way it builds a face out of a electrical outlet. She had taught a unit on it. She had, she now remembered with a sour twist, used a slide of an alleged "spirit photograph" from 1911 to demonstrate how easily grief makes us see what we need.

But the scans were not grief. The scans were light through a lens onto a sensor, mathematics all the way down, and the chair was in the mathematics.

And then she got to the last page of the green album, and stopped being able to pretend at all.

* * *

The last page held a single photograph, and she knew it. She knew it the way you know your own signature. It had hung for years in the hallway upstairs, in a cheap gold frame: the two of them, her grandmother and her, taken by Tomas one autumn afternoon when Juniper was twelve, the two of them at this very table, peeling apples, both of them laughing at something the photograph had the grace not to record. It was the picture Juniper had chosen, three weeks ago, to put on the front of the funeral program. She had scanned it herself for that. She had looked at it for hours, choosing crops, adjusting contrast. She knew every pixel of it the way you know a scar.

The apples were there. The laughter was there. Her grandmother's hands, spotted and sure, were there.

And the chair was there — but not against the wall, and not in the dark of the parlor, and not at the edge of anything.

It stood at the table. Drawn up to the table, in the gap between her grandmother's chair and the window, in a space Juniper would have sworn was too small for it, its high back rising out of the top of the frame as if it were taller than the room. And on the table in front of it, where the photograph had never shown anything but the apple peels and the paring knife and the bowl, there was a place setting.

A plate. A cup. She could not tell, at this resolution, whether the cup held anything. Whether it was steaming. The pixels would not resolve, and she had the sudden, animal certainty that they would not resolve no matter how good the scanner was, that the photograph was deciding what to show her and had decided this was enough.

She pulled the original from the frame upstairs with shaking hands. The print was the print she remembered. Two people, apples, laughter. No chair. No place setting. She held the original and the scan side by side on the laptop screen and the discrepancy sat between them like a confession: the paper remembered one thing, the light remembered another, and the light was changing its mind.

*The past is being edited,* she thought, and the thought had a taste, iron and cold, like a coin held too long in the mouth. *In real time. Backward. Someone is going through the house's memory and adding a chair.*

She thought of the tapes in the cellar-dark of the pantry, labeled in her grandmother's hand. She thought of Tape Seven, which she had not played again, and would not, not yet, not alone — the singer's voice, and then the other voice answering from underneath the song like something under floorboards, and the tape ending mid-word as if the word had been taken out of the air before it could land. *Leave him room,* said the margin note in the transcription she'd found, and she had read it as sentiment, as grief, as an old woman's tenderness, and now she read it again in the dark behind her eyes and understood it might have been an instruction.

She did the only thing her body knew how to do, the thing her grandmother had done and her grandmother's mother before her, the thing that needed no verse and no binding: she put the kettle on. She stood at the stove while it heated, in the honey light, in the house that was quietly rewriting its own history around an empty chair, and she watched the six ladder-backs against the wall and counted them twice. Six. Still six.

The kettle began to climb toward its whistle.

It was while she stood there, waiting for the water, that she understood the direction of the movement, and understanding it was worse than seeing the chair at all. She went back to the log, back through the scans, and laid them out in order across the screen of her mind like frames of a film: 1961, the chair against the wall, far. The seventies, the chair at the edge of the porches, at the margins, outside. The eighties, the chair inside, in the parlor dark. And now — now — the chair at the table. A place setting. A seat kept.

Fifty years, and the chair had been walking toward the table the whole time. One photograph at a time. The length of a room per generation. And her grandmother had sat at that table peeling apples with her granddaughter, laughing, with that thing drawn up beside her, and had never once, in all the years of letters and phone calls and visits, said a single word about it — which meant either that she couldn't see it, or that she had made a peace with it so complete it looked like blindness, and Juniper did not know which possibility frightened her more.

The kettle screamed. She took it off the heat. In the ringing quiet after, she heard — or thought she heard, and the *or thought* was no longer any protection at all — a sound from the direction of the pantry where the tapes were kept. Small. Domestic. The particular soft scrape of wood on floorboard.

Of a chair, being drawn closer to a table.

She stood in the kitchen with her untouched tea going cold, and made herself a scholar's promise, the kind you make when the alternative is running to the car and driving until the fuel light comes on: she would scan every photograph in the house. She would log every appearance, every position, every change. She would document the thing the way her grandmother had documented the songs, because documentation was the only prayer she knew, and because if the past was being edited, then someone had to keep the original.

She went back to the albums. She worked until the light went amber, then gray, and the log grew to three pages, and the chair appeared in eleven more photographs she had never noticed it in before, always a little nearer, always empty, always patient.

The last scan she made that day was of a snapshot from the back of the green album, loose, unmounted, undated. It showed the kitchen at night. The table, bare. The window black. And the high-backed chair at the table's head — not at the side now, not in a gap, but at the head, where her grandmother had always sat — with its back to the window and its face, if it had a face, toward the room.

On the reverse, in pencil, in her grandmother's handwriting, smaller than any note Juniper had ever seen her write, as if the words had been made to whisper:

*It only needs to be asked once.*
