## 7. Village of Polite Silence

The rain had been falling on Old Chapel Road since Tuesday, a thin, patient rain that seemed less like weather than like the village itself trying to say something and giving up halfway through. Juniper walked down its center with her recorder zipped inside her coat to keep it dry, past houses whose windows were lit but whose curtains were drawn against her specifically. She could feel it — not paranoia, she told herself, though by now she had stopped entirely believing herself — the particular quality of attention a street pays when everyone in it has agreed not to look at you.

She was going to see Agnes Doyle.

Everyone had told her not to. Not in words — no one in this village said anything in words if they could manage a silence instead — but in the small choreography of refusal. When Juniper asked after the choir mistress at the post office, the postmaster's hands went still over her parcel for exactly one second too long. When she asked at the pub, the conversation around her continued at precisely the same volume, which was itself an answer; grief and guilt both lower voices, and this village had learned to keep its voice level the way you keep your hand level near a sleeping dog.

*The songs were sung twice only once,* Tomas had almost said, two nights ago, standing in her grandmother's doorway with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral he couldn't admit to attending. He'd caught himself before the sentence finished. But he had pointed her toward Agnes Doyle with a tilt of his chin, the way you point someone toward a doctor or a grave.

Agnes Doyle had directed the village choir for forty-one years. Her house sat at the crook of Old Chapel Road where it bent toward the old churchyard, a low stone cottage with a garden gone beautifully wild, rosemary escaping its bed and taking over half the path. The door was blue once. Now it was the color of weathered things generally.

Juniper knocked. Waited. Knocked again.

The door opened before the third knock finished, which meant Agnes had been standing behind it, deciding.

"You're the Almeida girl." Agnes was small and straight-backed, silver hair cut short as a hedge, eyes the pale gray of slate after rain. She looked at Juniper the way one looks at a bill that has finally come due. "You have her chin."

"I have her everything, according to the photographs." Juniper kept her voice gentle. "Mrs. Doyle. I'm cataloguing my grandmother's work. The song cycle. I wondered if—"

"No."

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You were going to ask me to sing them." Agnes's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Everyone asks eventually. Come in out of the rain, then, since you'll only stand there dripping at me until I do."

* * *

The cottage smelled of tea and lamp oil and, underneath both, faintly of chalk — forty years of writing hymn numbers on slate boards. Agnes poured tea without asking whether Juniper wanted it, set out biscuits Juniper suspected had been bought for an occasion exactly like this one, and then sat with her own cup untouched, hands folded, a woman braced.

"The cycle has seven songs," Juniper began, because names and numbers were safe ground. "My grandmother recorded them between 1961 and 1974. The singers are anonymized on the tape labels, but the timbre of track three is yours. You were what, nineteen?"

"Eighteen." Something flickered across the old woman's face — vanity, maybe, or its ghost. "I had the high voice then. Everyone said so."

"And the songs themselves. They're extraordinary. Modal structures that don't map onto any regional tradition I can find. The lyrics are fragmentary — deliberately, I think. And every single person I've asked to sing even a bar of them has gone silent mid-verse. Not refused. *Silenced.* There's a difference, and I've been trying to understand it all week."

Agnes turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. Turned it back.

"Your grandmother ever tell you why she stopped recording?"

"She stopped singing them herself, is what I was told. After 1974 there's nothing."

"There's nothing because there was nothing left to do." Agnes looked up, and her slate eyes had gone hard and bright, the way stone goes bright when water runs over it. "You want to know about the songs, girl, and I'll tell you what I told your grandmother, God rest her stubborn heart: they aren't songs. They're stitches. Every verse is a stitch, and the singing is the needle going through, and you do not pull a stitch twice unless you mean to open the wound."

Juniper's recorder lay silent in her bag. She had decided, walking up the path, not to take it out until invited. Some part of her — the folklorist, the archivist, the granddaughter — understood already that this conversation was not data. It was testimony.

"Open the wound," Juniper repeated carefully. "Meaning what, exactly?"

"Meaning that these songs hold something in place. Have always held something in place. Your grandmother knew it. That's why she came here in the first place, though she let everyone believe she came for the folk music." Agnes laughed, a dry sound with no floor under it. "Folk music. As if anything in this valley is only folk music."

"Then sing me one line. One. Not from the cycle — just describe how it felt to sing it."

And Agnes Doyle, who had refused the whole village for fifty years, who had outlasted priests and postmasters and the slow erosion of memory itself, looked at the daughter of her dead friend and found that she was tired. Tired the way walls are tired. Tired the way a held breath is tired.

"It felt," she said slowly, "like setting a table."

* * *

*It felt like setting a table.*

That is what I told her, and I watched her write it down, and I thought: Maren, you clever, terrible woman, even dead you're making me do this. Because the girl has your chin and your patience and that same way of waiting — of letting silence do the asking — and I am seventy-three years old and I have carried this the way you carry water, careful and level and never spilling, and I find tonight that my arms are done.

She doesn't know yet what she's asking for. Let her drink her tea. Let the rain get louder. There is a version of this where I send her home with nothing, and I have lived inside that version for fifty years, and it is cold in there, colder than any cellar, because at least a cellar holds something.

The truth is this: we sang them twice. Once, only once, and never again, and the reason is a boy named Peter Halloran who would be sixty-eight years old now if he were anything at all.

I was eighteen. I had the high voice. It was the night of the harvest supper, and the hall smelled of apples and wet wool, and someone — I will not say who, though God knows I've said it to myself ten thousand times, in the dark, in the voice I use for counting sins — someone dared us. Dared us to sing the seventh song through, and then through again, back to back, no pause for breath between. A dare. That's all it was. Children playing at the edge of a thing their grandmothers had sewn shut generations before, and none of us knowing — none of us *knowing* —

Except Maren knew. Maren Almeida was there, twenty-two years old, home from university with her notebook and her new tape machine, and when we reached the end of the second singing she was already moving, already shouting at us to stop, except we had already stopped, and the stopping was the problem. The song wants to go somewhere when it ends. It needs somewhere to *go*. And a boy stood up from the back bench — Peter Halloran, fifteen years old, freckles, a laugh like a door swinging — and said, clear as a bell across the whole hall:

*I'll go.*

And he did.

* * *

"I'll go," Juniper whispered. "He said 'I'll go.'"

"He said it like he was volunteering to fetch more chairs." Agnes's voice had gone flat, pressed smooth, the voice of someone reciting rather than remembering. "That's the worst of it. He smiled. He picked up his coat. His mother was in the kitchen helping with the washing up. He went out the back door into the dark and the song — the song went with him. We heard it. All of us. Fading down the lane, sung in a voice none of us recognized and all of us knew, going away toward the chapel ruins, and then not fading anymore. Just *gone*, the way a radio station goes when you drive out of range."

The rain filled the pause. Against the window it made a sound like fingers, patient, counting.

"What happened to him?" Juniper asked, though some cold cellar of her mind had already begun assembling the answer from tape seven, from tally marks she hadn't found yet, from a chair in photographs that shouldn't be there.

"Nothing happened to him. That's what no one will say. Nothing *happened*. Things happen to people. This wasn't a thing happening. This was a thing *accepting*." Agnes finally lifted her teacup, drank, set it down with a click that sounded final as a latch. "Your grandmother ran out after him. I ran too. Half the hall did. There was fog off the river that thick you could chew it. We called until dawn. His mother called his name until her voice tore, and then she called it after that anyway, in pieces."

"And the binding? My grandmother—"

"Finished it alone. That same week. She went to whoever taught her — I never knew who, some old woman up-country, some keeper of the stitches — and she learned the last verse, the closing verse, the one that ties the knot. And she sang it. Once. Properly. Alone in the chapel ruins at midnight with the fog coming in off the river, and I know because I followed her, because I was eighteen and terrified and in love with the sound of my own courage." Agnes's hands had begun, very slightly, to shake. "She sang it once and the valley went quiet in a way I've never heard quiet since. Like the whole world holding still while a knot is pulled tight. And when it was done, Maren Almeida opened her mouth to speak and nothing came out. Nothing. For eleven days. And when her voice came back it was different. Lower. Rougher. The voice on those tapes of yours — you've noticed, haven't you? On the early tapes she sings like a girl. On the later ones she sings like gravel learning to be a girl again."

Juniper had noticed. She had filed it under age, under cigarettes, under the ordinary erosion of decades. Her skin crawled now with the recataloguing.

"She paid with her voice," Juniper said. "The binding took it as payment."

"Took something. Took enough." Agnes stood abruptly, went to the window, stood with her back to the room looking out at the rain-dark garden. When she spoke again her voice had changed register — quieter, aimed at the glass rather than at Juniper. "Here is what I need you to understand, girl, before you go digging any further with your little machine. What your grandmother bound that night — she didn't bind Peter Halloran. You don't bind a boy. She bound the thing that takes boys when a song is sung twice and someone says *I'll go*. She bound it with the seventh song closed shut, knotted, finished. And it has stayed bound for fifty years because every person in this village has kept the bargain without ever being told the terms: we sing once. We never sing twice. We never finish what the dare started."

"But the tapes." Juniper's mouth had gone dry. "Her recordings. If the songs are bindings—"

"They're copies of bindings. Knots remembered in wax and magnetic tape." Agnes turned around. Her face in the lamplight was ancient suddenly, carved, a face from a church tympanum. "And a copy of a key opens nothing, child, until someone puts it in a door and turns it. So I'll ask you the question I've been dreading since you knocked, since I saw her chin above your collar. What have you been doing in that house? What have you been *playing*?"

The rain intensified, all at once, as if the sky had been waiting for permission.

Juniper thought of tape seven. Of the voice that answered the singer — a young man's voice, warm, amused, threaded through with static like light through fog. Of the way the tape ended mid-word, hers, her grandmother's, cut off in the middle of a syllable as though a hand had covered her mouth.

"I played them aloud," Juniper said. "All of them. In sequence. In the house."

Agnes Doyle closed her eyes. For a long moment the only sound was the rain, and beneath the rain — so faint Juniper might have invented it, might spend the rest of her life wondering whether she'd invented it — something below the floorboards of the world shifted its weight, politely, the way a guest does in a chair that has grown uncomfortable.

Then the choir mistress opened her eyes and said, in the voice she used for leading hymns, steady and carrying and pitched to reach the back pew:

"Then you had better come with me. There's a verse your grandmother never wrote down, and I am the only one left alive who heard it whole — and if that thing is loose in your house, you'll need every stitch we can find."

* * *

They went out into the rain together, two women and a satchel of tapes, down the crook of Old Chapel Road toward the house with the blue-gray door. Behind them Agnes locked her own door with the ceremony of a woman bolting a stable against something that has already learned handles.

Neither of them saw the light in the upstairs window of the Almeida house, burning yellow and steady, though no one had switched it on.

Neither of them heard, over the rain, the small domestic sounds of a table being set for company.
