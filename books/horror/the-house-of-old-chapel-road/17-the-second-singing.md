## 17. The Second Singing

The house had stopped pretending to be a house.

Juniper noticed it first in the hallway, where the floorboards had begun to slope — gently, almost courteously — toward the kitchen, as if the whole structure were a hand cupped around a table. The doors stood open now, every one of them, even the ones she'd locked. They opened inward, the way a mouth opens to receive. The beds upstairs had made themselves with hospital corners she hadn't taught them. And the table — God, the table — had grown overnight by a full plank's width, its grain running seamless into new wood that still smelled of sap and sawdust, as though the pine had been felled that morning in some forest that did not appear on any map of the county.

She counted the chairs again because counting was the only arithmetic left to her.

Eleven at dawn. Twelve when the light went gray. Thirteen now, at half past ten in the morning, arranged around the elongated table with a precision that felt less like arrangement and more like attendance. The thirteenth was small — a child's chair, spindle-backed, its seat worn pale in the shape of someone who had sat there for years.

It had not been there an hour ago.

Juniper stood in the kitchen doorway with her grandmother's notebook open against her forearm and let herself feel, for exactly ten seconds, the full weight of what she was. Not a folklorist. Not a granddaughter. A hinge. The last living joint between a song and the thing it held shut.

Then she counted to eleven — her own ritual, borrowed from no tradition but grief — and sat down at the table to do the math.

* * *

The math was this:

The binding required blood. She had known it since the night before, since the letter, since the moment she'd read her grandmother's handwriting change from ink to confession: *sung twice by blood, or not sung at all.* The song cycle was a lock, and locks are only ever as strong as the hands that turn them. Edith Almeida had turned hers for fifty-three years, alone in this kitchen, singing verses into the cellar dark the way other women said grace — until her voice gave out, and then she'd recorded them, and then she'd died, and the recordings had kept the seat warm the way a pilot light keeps a room technically warm without warming anyone.

Juniper was blood. That part was simple.

What was not simple was the verse itself.

She spread her grandmother's transcription across the table, weighting the corners with the salt cellar, a tape reel, Tomas's flask, and her own phone — four objects from four different centuries of keeping things down. The final verse ran two lines and stopped. Two lines in Edith's careful hand, and then nothing but margin: *Leave him room.*

Two lines. Juniper had read them perhaps two hundred times since finding them, and each reading had peeled back another layer, the way you might lift wallpaper in an old house and find older paper beneath, and beneath that, plaster, and beneath that, brick, and beneath that — sometimes — something the builders had walled in on purpose.

The first line was an invitation. She was sure of it now. It used the old formulae, the threshold-grammar Tomas had described in his halting way: *come in from the cold, the table is laid, your name is kept.* Every culture she'd ever archived had some version of it — the words you spoke to call a guest across a boundary, words that worked equally well on the living and on whatever the living leave behind. Her grandmother had written it the way you write a letter you know will outlive you.

The second line was the binding. Also certain. Its meter inverted the first line's; its imagery closed every door the first line opened. *Sit and stay, sit and stay, the road behind is water.* It was beautiful and it was a cage, and the two facts did not cancel each other out. That was the thing about the old songs that no journal article had ever captured properly: they were never one thing. They were always both.

And the verse was unfinished. Which meant —

Juniper pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

Which meant that for fifty-three years, the boy in the cellar — Halloran's boy, Tomas's brother, whatever remained of him after 1968 — had been neither called nor bound. Neither invited nor refused. He had been left in the grammar of the conditional, suspended mid-sentence, given a chair but never told whether he was guest or prisoner. *Leave him room,* her grandmother had written, and Juniper understood at last that it had been mercy and cowardice in the same stroke of the pen, love wearing duty's coat, or duty wearing love's — she could no longer tell which, and suspected Edith hadn't been able to either, not at the end.

But here was the arithmetic. Here was the impossible sum.

If she sang the verse completed — if she finished those two lines with a third and fourth of her own making, closing the invitation and sealing the binding — the boy would go where bound things go. Down. Under. Into the dark below the floorboards, into the place the tally marks counted away from, into the company of the thing her grandmother's songs had fed and fenced for half a century. Because that was the secret structure of the whole cycle, the architecture she'd finally mapped at three in the morning with her heart hammering like a fist on a door: the songs did not keep the worse thing *out*. They kept it *fed*. Each verse was a portion. Each singing a meal. And the guest at the table — the polite young man made of pause and echo, stitched together from tape-hiss and her grandmother's voice — was not the hunger.

He was the seal. The stopper. The cork in the throat of the bottle.

Every photograph proved it. In the oldest pictures, the high-backed chair sat far from the table, angled toward the cellar door — a sentinel, not a diner. In the newer ones it had moved closer, yes, drawn by her recordings, by her invitations, by her own careless scholarship played aloud in a house that remembered how to listen. But it had moved closer *to guard*, not to eat. It had been holding the door with its back for fifty-three years, the way you hold a door against wind, the way you hold a door against something coming up the stairs.

Free him — truly free him, send him off into whatever mercy waits outside the cycle — and the door stood open.

Bind him — sing him down into the dark with the rest — and he became food. Or worse, company. The worst things, her grandmother's marginalia suggested in a hand gone suddenly small, *are lonelier than we are, and loneliness makes them larger.*

There was no third option. She checked. She had spent the night checking, cross-referencing Tomas's fragments against the choir mistress's reluctant testimony against three folklore monographs and her own field notes from six countries. Bindings were binary. That was their cruelty and their comfort. You sealed the door or you opened it, and both acts cost exactly what they cost.

Except.

Juniper looked up from the notebook, and the thirteenth chair looked back at her — not literally, chairs don't look, but the house had learned a kind of looking, and she felt it settle on her like dust.

Except that bindings required the verse sung twice. Sung twice *by blood*, Edith had written, and underlined it twice herself, which meant the old woman had understood the doubling as load-bearing, structural, non-negotiable. But why? Juniper had turned the question all night. Folkloric precedent offered answers — the echo as witness, the repetition as contract, the second singing as signature on the first singing's oath. Fine. True, probably. But there was another answer, one the monographs didn't contain because they'd never had to consider it:

The second singing was a choice.

The first time you sang, you might be compelled. Fear could put the words in your mouth; desperation could; a dare could — Tomas's brother had vanished on a dare, on a song sung twice by boys who didn't know what they were signing. But the second time — the second time was never compulsion. The second time, you already knew what the song did. You sang it again anyway. The second singing was where the soul signed. It was the difference between an accident and an act.

Which meant the two singings did not have to be identical.

The thought arrived so quietly she almost missed it, the way important thoughts do — not through the front door but already sitting in the kitchen when you come down in the morning, patient, as if it had always lived here.

The first singing could bind. The second could — 

No. She made herself say it plainly, out loud, to the empty chairs, because vagueness was a luxury the house would exploit: "The first singing closes the lock. The second singing turns the key."

And a key, unlike a lock, could be turned in more than one direction.

* * *

She spent the middle hours of the day preparing the way scholars prepare for fieldwork they may not survive: methodically, obsessively, with a tenderness toward the materials that surprised her every time she caught herself feeling it.

She transcribed the two existing lines onto fresh paper in her own hand, because the binding would need her voice and perhaps her script too — blood was blood, but intention had a handwriting. She tested melodies against the words, humming low so the house wouldn't hear, and found that the tune her grandmother had noted above the fragment fit the first line perfectly and fought the second like a animal fighting a trap, which told her Edith had never managed to make the cage comfortable, and had maybe never wanted to.

She ate. Deliberately, mechanically, standing at the counter with her back to the table, because the guest had taught her that meals in this house were negotiations and she refused to negotiate hungry. The bread was stale. She ate it anyway. Hospitality ran in both directions or it ran nowhere.

At noon she went down to the cellar.

Not to the table — not yet, not while the sun was up and the rules still favored her — but to the top of the stairs, where the light from the kitchen fell in a long gray parallelogram across the dirt floor, and the tally marks on the walls caught it and threw it back dim as fish scales. Thousands of marks. Fifty-three years of days, cut into the stone by a woman who came down here with a knife and a lantern and sang supper to the dark.

"Grandma," Juniper said, and her voice sounded very small and very loud at once, the way voices do in cellars, in churches, in places built to hold sound the way jars hold preserves. "I need you to have been right about this. I need there to have been a reason."

The cellar said nothing, which was its custom, and then — at the edge of hearing, below the floor, below the floor below the floor — something said nothing *louder*. A pressure. A lean. The sensation of a vast attention shifting its weight, the way a sleeping animal shifts toward warmth.

Juniper climbed the stairs backward. She did not decide to do this; her body simply knew the etiquette.

* * *

Tomas came at four, as he had come every day since the confession, carrying flowers he never brought inside and a face he couldn't help bringing everywhere.

"You've figured it out," he said. Not a question. He stood in the yard because she'd asked him to, asked him firmly, after the clocks stopped and the chairs began arriving — asked him the way you ask someone you love to stand clear of a riverbank. "I can see it in you. Your grandmother got that look the last year. Like a woman doing sums she doesn't want the answer to."

"The verse has to be sung twice," Juniper said. "By blood. Once to bind, once to — " She stopped. Started again. "Once more."

"And?"

"And I think the second singing can be different." She wrapped her arms around herself against the evening chill, though the chill was the least of the evening's weapons. "I think the first singing seals him down with the rest of it. And the second — if I sing it right, if I mean it right — I think the second singing can be a release. Not of the seal. Of *him*. Just him. Pulled out of the lock like a splinter, clean, before the wound closes over."

Tomas was quiet for a long time. The flowers drooped in his fist — white ones, she noticed, the color this village used for graves that had no body in them.

"Your grandmother tried something like that," he said finally. "Near the end. When her voice was going. She told me — " His jaw worked. "She told me she'd found a way to let Danny out and keep everything else where it belonged. She said the trick was that the song had to want it too. That the binding wasn't just words, it was — consent. The song had to agree to give him up." He shook his head slowly. "And then her voice went, and she couldn't test it, and she was afraid to try it half-done because a half-finished mercy is just a hole in the wall."

"A recording isn't a voice," Juniper said. "I know that now. That's my fault — mine, the tapes, all of it. But my voice is mine. And the song — " She touched her sternum, where the melody had taken up residence over the past week like a tenant who paid rent in dread. "The song knows me, Tomas. It's been learning me since I got here. I think it's been waiting for someone who'd ask it the right question."

"What's the right question?"

"'Would you rather be a lock,'" Juniper said, "'or a lullaby?'"

Tomas looked at her for a long moment, and then he did something she hadn't seen him do in all the weeks she'd known him: he smiled. It was a terrible smile, cracked down the middle with fifty years of silence, but it was real.

"Danny used to sing," he said. "Badly. All the time. Drove our mother mad." He held out the flowers. "Take these. Put them on the table tonight. He'll know what they mean, even if — " His voice failed, recovered. "Even if it's the last supper."

Juniper took them. White flowers, cold stems, the whole weight of a village's guilt arranged in cellophane.

"Tomas. If this goes wrong — if the second singing opens instead of frees —"

"I know what's under there," he said. "I've known since I was nine years old. Go inside, Juniper. The sun's going down, and whatever you're going to do, do it while you're still the one the house belongs to."

* * *

Sunset came the way it always did now in the house on Old Chapel Road: not as a change of light but as a change of *tenure*. At 6:41 the shadows reversed themselves, flowing uphill, pooling in the corners like water finding its level, and the kitchen became a room that was being lent to her rather than owned by her. The clocks, all thirteen of them — when had there been thirteen? — ticked in unison, which clocks should never do, a sound like a hundred small hearts agreeing on something.

Juniper set the table.

Her hands shook and she let them; steadiness was for people whose work didn't matter. She laid out plates — twelve, plus the child's chair at the thirteenth place, where she put the smallest plate and the white flowers in a jelly glass. She poured water into every cup. She cut bread and left the loaf whole at the center, because some hungers were for bread and some were for the idea of bread, and she'd learned you couldn't tell them apart until you offered both.

At the head of the table she placed her grandmother's notebook, open to the final page. Two lines in faded ink. Margin note: *Leave him room.*

Below them, in her own hand, in ink she'd mixed with a drop of her own blood because the old instructions had insisted and she was past arguing with the old instructions, she had written the completion. Four lines. She'd worked on them for six hours and they were the hardest four lines she'd ever composed, harder than any novel sentence, because they had to do three things at once: close the invitation, honor the guest, and leave a seam — a single deliberate seam, a stitch she could pull — through which a second singing might travel.

A lock with a keyhole shaped like mercy.

She read them over once more. Then she stood at the head of the table, facing the cellar door, and waited for the sun to finish dying.

The guest arrived at dusk, as it always did, politely, between one breath and the next. The high-backed chair was occupied. The young man made of pause and echo sat with his hands folded, and where his face should have been there was a listening — a shape that listening makes when it goes on too long and starts to become a person. Around him, faintly, the ambient hiss of magnetic tape, and threaded through it, unmistakable, her grandmother's voice saying something too soft to parse, over and over, like a prayer worn smooth by use.

Juniper did not look directly at him. She served the bread. She filled his cup. And then she picked up the notebook, and found the melody, and sang the first line — the invitation, the threshold-grammar, *come in from the cold, the table is laid, your name is kept* — and felt the house lean in around her like a congregation.

She sang the second line. The binding. *Sit and stay, sit and stay, the road behind is water.* The cellar door rattled once, softly, like a dog dreaming of running.

And then, before the silence could close, before the house could decide the song was over, Juniper sang her own four lines.

Her voice cracked on the third one. She let it crack. Blood was blood.

The effect was not dramatic. That was the horror and the wonder of it — the house received the new verse the way soil receives seed, silently, totally, with a patience that implied millennia. The clocks stopped ticking in unison and resumed in *counterpoint*, weaving. The table's new wood darkened to match the old. And the figure at the head of the table — the seal, the sentinel, the boy who had been holding a door with his back for fifty-three years — turned his listening face toward her, and in the tape-hiss of him, her grandmother's voice rose clear for the first time, saying the thing she had apparently been saying all along, on every tape, under every verse:

*"Almost. Almost. Hold on, Danny. Almost."*

One singing done. The lock was turning.

Juniper set down the notebook, flexed her blood-stiff fingers, and prepared to learn whether a key, turned twice, opens — or lets go.
