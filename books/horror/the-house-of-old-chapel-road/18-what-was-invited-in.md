## 18. What Was Invited In

The house had stopped pretending.

Juniper felt it the way you feel a fever breaking into something worse — the moment the shivering gives way to a heat that no longer bothers to hide. All evening the rooms had been arranging themselves toward hospitality: doors swinging inward on their own weight, the hallway runner straightening itself as though a maid had passed through, the kitchen table lengthening by inches every time she looked away and looked back. She had counted the chairs at eleven, then twelve, then thirteen, and after thirteen she had stopped counting, because counting had begun to feel like participation.

Now, past one in the morning, she stood in the kitchen with her grandmother's letter open on the table, and the floor beneath her feet was breathing.

Not metaphor. Not nerves. The floorboards — old pine, wide-planked, the same floor she'd run across as a child in her socks — rose and fell in a rhythm slow as sleep, a swell of perhaps a quarter inch, then a settling, then the swell again. Under her bare feet she could feel it in the soles, in the small bones of her ankles. A tide. Something down there inhaling.

*The guest is not the danger,* the letter had told her, in her grandmother's careful hand. *The guest is the cork in the bottle. God forgive me, I tied a boy to the mouth of it.*

Juniper had read the letter four times now. Each time, the same sentence rose out of the page like a hand breaking water: *I did not bind him to keep you safe from him. I bound him to keep you safe from what he was sitting on.*

She looked down at the floor, at the breathing boards, and understood — finally, completely, with the sick clarity of a scholar whose footnotes have turned on her — that the house on Old Chapel Road was not a house at all. It was a lid.

* * *

She had gone back to the photographs because the photographs were the only witnesses she trusted. The albums lay open on the kitchen table now, beside the letter, beside the cold cup of tea she'd poured at midnight and never touched. Her laptop's scanner hummed. She fed it images the way she'd been feeding it images all week — the family at Easter, 1974; her grandmother young and sharp-faced on the porch steps; the Halloran boy in a school portrait, gap-toothed, forever eleven.

The first new scan came up on the screen and Juniper's hands went still on the keyboard.

The high-backed chair was still there. Of course it was — it had been there for days, drifting closer to the table in each successive scan, the past editing itself in real time while she documented it. But now, behind it, at the far edge of the frame, half in the shadow of a doorframe that had not existed in 1974 — there was a second chair.

A plain one. Kitchen wood. Unremarkable, except that it was empty in the specific way that a chair is never empty, the way a chair is empty when someone has just gotten up from it or is about to sit down. The seat looked warm. Juniper could not have said how a photographed chair could look warm, and she was a folklorist, she had spent her life learning the grammar of the uncanny, and still the seat looked warm.

She scanned the next photograph. Easter, 1974. Second chair, closer now, angled toward the table as if drawn up in conversation.

The next. A third chair.

She sat back from the screen. The kitchen was very quiet, except for the floor, breathing. Outside, the wind moved through the elms along Old Chapel Road with a sound like pages turning.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the house, to the chairs, to whatever was listening. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Okay. I see you."

The house did not answer. The house had stopped answering in words hours ago; it had moved on to furniture.

* * *

She made herself think it through the way her grandmother would have wanted her to — methodically, in order, with citations. It was the only prayer she knew how to say.

One: the songs were bindings. Each verse a nail, each repetition a hammer-blow, the whole cycle driven into the cellar like a lid nailed shut. Her grandmother had sung them for fifty years, alone, in a house with salt under every doorframe, and had paid for the singing with her voice, which had thinned to a whisper by the time Juniper was old enough to remember her.

Two: the final verse was left unfinished. Two lines, and then silence, and in the margin, *Leave him room.* Juniper had spent days reading that as cowardice. Then as love. Now she understood it was both, and neither — it was *engineering*. The unfinished verse was a flaw deliberately left in the seal. A pressure valve. A seat kept warm at the table so the thing below would have somewhere to go that wasn't *up*.

Three: her field recordings, played aloud in the house, had constituted an invitation. She had unbound the oldest verse with a speaker and a laptop and the best of scholarly intentions, and the binding had come loose, and something had accepted the invitation — the guest, the young man made of pause and echo, stitched together from tape hiss and her grandmother's voice and fifty years of a boy who never came home.

Four — and this was the one her mind kept sliding off of, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth — four: the guest was not the danger.

The guest was the *seal*.

Her grandmother had not bound the boy to imprison him. She had bound him to *employ* him. To seat him, forever, at the mouth of the cellar, a polite and hungry and endlessly patient young man standing between the world and what was underneath it. *Leave him room* had meant: leave him a place at the table, because the table is the only thing standing between us and the floor.

And Juniper, with her documentation, her unbinding, her careful archival work, had pulled the cork halfway out — and then, in an act of mercy she was already beginning to suspect was the bravest and possibly the most catastrophic thing she would ever do, had invited the guest properly, fed him bread without looking up, refused to name him or thank him, kept the old laws. She had re-seated him. She had, without knowing it, become the second singer of the binding.

But a seal held by one person is not a seal held by fifty years of song. The house knew it. The floor knew it. And the photographs — the photographs were showing her the arithmetic.

Second chair. Third chair.

*Something else is coming to dinner.*

* * *

She went down the cellar stairs at two in the morning because there was no one to stop her and because not going down felt, suddenly, like the more dangerous act. The flashlight trembled only a little. The walls were scored with tally marks — she had counted them days ago, thousands of them, and now she understood they were not a prisoner's marks. They were a *calendar*. Someone had been counting the nights they had kept watch.

The small table below the floorboards still had its place setting. The bread she'd left was gone — not eaten, *gone*, the way sound goes when a tape runs out — and in its place, arranged on the plate with terrible precision, was a handful of wheat. Live wheat, green at the base, smelling of a summer fifty years gone.

She knelt. She put her palm flat against the floor, against the breathing boards, and she felt it answer — a pressure from below, patient, vast, *interested*. Not malevolent. That was the worst of it. It did not feel like hate. It felt like hunger feels when it has waited so long it has become a kind of geology. It felt like an appetite that had been promised a meal in 1968 and had been kept waiting ever since by a boy in a high-backed chair and an old woman's ruined voice.

The floor swelled under her hand. Held. Settled.

"You're welcome," Juniper whispered, and then clapped her hand over her own mouth, because that was the wrong word, that was the word that fed it, that was *thanks*, and the boards under her palm went very still, and somewhere below, in the dark under the dark, something turned over in its sleep like a body adjusting in a too-small bed.

*Not thanks. Never thanks.* She had written the rules on the kitchen wall in grease pencil. She had nearly broken the first one at supper. She would not break it kneeling on a cellar floor at two in the morning.

She climbed the stairs backward, the way you leave the presence of something older than manners.

* * *

In the kitchen, the clocks — every clock in the house, stopped at 3:12 since the night of the invitation — had begun, very softly, to tick again. Not to keep time. To *count down*. She could hear it: a rhythm slightly too fast, slightly too eager, the tick of a kitchen timer, of an egg boiling, of something measuring out the last of a thing.

Sunrise was two hours off.

She scanned one more photograph. Her hands were steady now, in the way of hands that have passed beyond trembling into something colder and more useful. The image rose on the screen: her grandmother, middle-aged, standing in this same kitchen, this same window behind her. And at the table — the high-backed chair, drawn fully up. The second chair, closer. The third chair, no longer at the edge of the frame but *at the table*, its seat warm, and beside the third chair, just entering the frame, the carved front leg of a fourth.

Juniper closed the laptop. She looked at the kitchen wall, at her own grease-pencil rules, and beneath them, in letters large enough to read from the doorway, she wrote:

*THE GUEST IS THE LOCK. THE FLOOR IS THE DOOR. SUNRISE IS TWO HOURS OFF. FINISH THE VERSE.*

And then, underneath, smaller, because it was the truest thing she knew:

*She wasn't keeping him out. She was keeping him seated. And I have to decide, before the light comes, whether mercy is a debt I can afford to pay twice.*

The floor breathed. The clocks counted. Down in the cellar, at a small table below the floorboards, a place was being set for company — and Juniper Almeida picked up her grandmother's transcription of the unfinished verse, two lines in faded ink and fifty years of silence, and began to walk toward the stairs.
