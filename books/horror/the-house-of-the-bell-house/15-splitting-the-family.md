## 15. Splitting the Family

Marisol Vega had learned to count in a house that was no longer a house, and she counted everything now, the way other kids bit their nails.

Three beds in Odette's kitchen loft. Two survivors from her class who still slept whole nights through. One deacon who snored like a foghorn with a chest cold. Eleven names on a piece of paper that would not burn, and nine days left on the calendar Odette kept by the stove, each morning one square crossed out in red pencil with a small, terrible neatness.

She counted because counting made things stay put. Counting was how you knew the world hadn't eaten something while you weren't looking.

Tonight the kitchen smelled like cinnamon and burnt toast, because Theo had tried to make cinnamon toast for everyone and gotten distracted arguing with the runaway teen, Jess, about whether ghosts could get sunburned. ("They're not ghosts," Marisol had said. "They're wardens." "Same thing." "It is *not* the same thing, Jess, oh my god.") The toast sat in a sad black row on the counter, and Odette was scraping it onto a plate anyway, because Odette never threw food away, not even food that had technically become charcoal.

"Carbon," Odette said, setting the plate down, "is what diamonds are made of. Eat your diamonds."

Marisol almost smiled. Almost.

Because under the cinnamon and the burnt toast and the radio playing low, there was the other sound — the one that had started an hour ago, when Ada came back from the sheriff's office with his face gray as dishwater and said, quietly, "We need to talk," and Odette had looked at him and said, "Not in front of the kids," and Ada had said, "They *are* the kids it's about."

So now the adults were in the pantry room with the door shut, which fooled nobody, because the pantry door didn't latch properly and because Mr. Deacon — his name was Father Amos, but he'd asked them, embarrassed, to just call him Amos, and nobody could, so he was Mr. Deacon forever — had left it cracked an inch when he went in with his mug of chicory coffee.

Marisol sat at the kitchen table with her homework open in front of her. Long division. She was very good at long division. She stared at the numbers and listened to the adults decide her life.

* * *

"—I'm telling you, Voss wasn't lying about the roads," Ada's voice, low and fast. "The sheriff all but drew me a map out of town. Highway 1 north, past the cannery, don't stop, don't ask questions. We take the eleven named children tonight, we're in Oregon by Thursday—"

"And then what?" That was Odette, and her voice did the thing it did when she was angry at someone she loved, which was go very quiet and very even, like a knife being laid flat on a table. "We run. Eleven children, two adults, a teenager who steals cars for fun, and a deacon with bad knees. We run where? It knows their names, Ada. It wrote them. You think state lines mean anything to a thing that reaches into sleeping kids' heads and walks them across town?"

"Then we fight it here, is that better? With what? Salt? Prayers? Amos's sermons?" A chair scraped. "Odette, I have watched this town eat its own children for two hundred years and call it luck. I am not going to stand in a kitchen and wait for it to finish the list."

"You won't be standing anywhere if you get eleven exhausted children killed on a highway because you needed to *do something*—"

"I need to keep them *alive*—"

"That's what everybody always says!" And that was Mr. Deacon's voice, cracking up high, and then a silence so complete Marisol could hear the refrigerator hum. When he spoke again it was slower, sadder. "That's what the founding families said. Keep them alive. Keep the town alive. Every generation, somebody doing arithmetic with somebody else's child and calling it love."

"Don't you dare compare us to them," Odette said.

"Somebody has to say it." A pause. "Give it the eleven. That's what it offered. The town prospers, the rest of the children live, the families keep their babies. Eleven against four hundred. I've done the math, God forgive me, I've done the math every night since it spoke through that boy, and eleven against four hundred is the only sum in this whole cursed ledger that comes out merciful."

"You want to hand them over." Ada's voice had gone flat and strange. "You sit at our table, you eat Odette's food, you let Marisol teach you gin rummy, and you want to hand her over."

"I want to save *everybody I can*," Mr. Deacon said, and his voice broke clean through. "That's the whole job. That's been the whole job since I was twenty-two years old and they took my collar away for asking too many questions. You think I don't know what I'm saying? You think I sleep? I do the math because if I don't do the math I will simply lie down on the floor of this pantry and never get up again."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Odette, very quietly: "And you, Ada? What's your math?"

"My math," Ada said, "is that I have failed every child I've ever tried to protect, and I am not doing it again. Not one. Not a single one. If we can't run and we can't fight and we won't feed it, then we find the third thing. There's always a third thing. There has to be."

"The third thing," Odette repeated. She laughed, but it wasn't a laugh, it was more like a cough with bitterness in it. "You sound like a man negotiating with a flood."

* * *

Marisol looked down at her long division.

The numbers had gone blurry. She wiped her eyes with her sleeve, furious at herself, and made herself read the problem again. 4,896 divided by 12. Bring down the 6. How many times does 12 go into 69?

Eleven against four hundred.

Mr. Deacon had said it like it was gentle. Like mercy was a thing you could weigh on a scale, and her brother — no, not her brother, her brother was already gone, already *received*, already a smudge in a ledger nobody would admit existed — like *she* was the remainder. The part left over after the division. The number you write small at the top and try not to look at.

She thought about how Ada had taught her that trick for checking your work — subtract your answer times the divisor from the original number, and whatever's left is your remainder, and remainders are fine, remainders are normal, every division makes one.

Except when the division is children. Then the remainder gets a date written next to it.

Theo slid into the chair across from her. He'd been pretending to organize the board game shelf for the last hour, which meant he'd heard everything too. His eyes were red-rimmed and steady.

"They're talking about us," he whispered.

"I know."

"Like we're groceries."

"I know."

Theo picked at the corner of his workbook. He was nine and he chewed his pencils down to splinters and he hadn't slept a full night since the entity spoke through him, and right now he looked older than Marisol felt, which wasn't fair to either of them.

"Are we gonna run?" he whispered.

"I don't know."

"Would you run? If it was just you?"

Marisol thought about it honestly, because Theo deserved honesty; everyone in this kitchen lied to each other out of love and it was suffocating all of them. "No," she said. "Mama used to say you can't outrun a thing that's already inside the house." She paused. "But I'm not the one deciding. Nobody ever lets the groceries vote."

Theo made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and put his head down on his arms.

In the pantry, the voices had risen again — Ada saying *Oregon*, Odette saying *nowhere is far enough*, Mr. Deacon saying *eleven, eleven, it's eleven or it's everyone* — and underneath all of it, faint as a radio between stations, Marisol heard the other voice. The one that had been living at the edge of her hearing for six days now. It didn't use words anymore. It just hummed, low and patient, like something humming to itself while it waited for a kettle to boil.

It sounded, horribly, a little like the tune Marisol had been teaching Ada last week, the one from her grandmother, the one she'd hummed while shuffling cards. She'd stopped humming it since. She wondered if it knew that. She wondered if it was doing it on purpose, wearing her own song like a stolen coat.

* * *

The pantry door opened at nine-forty.

Marisol knew because she'd been watching the clock over the stove, counting seconds, which was a thing she did now. Ada came out first, jaw tight, sleeves rolled like a man about to wash dishes or start a war. Odette followed, arms crossed. Mr. Deacon came last, holding his cold coffee with both hands like it was the last warm thing in the world.

"Okay," Ada said brightly, in the voice teachers use when the fire drill is not a drill. "Who wants hot chocolate?"

"We heard," Theo said, without lifting his head off the table.

Ada stopped. Marisol watched him do the thing he did — the flicker behind his eyes, the quick recalculation, the teacher realizing mid-sentence that the class already knows. For a second he looked like he might lie anyway. She watched him decide not to.

"Okay," he said again, quieter. He pulled out a chair, turned it around, and sat on it backward, arms folded over the backrest, the way he did when he was about to tell them something true. "Okay. Then you should hear all of it, because you're not cargo and you're not a math problem. You're people, and people get a vote."

"There are eleven of us," said Priya Nair from the doorway of the loft, where three of the named kids had gathered like birds on a wire. Priya was twelve and terrifyingly organized; she'd already made her own roster of the eleven names, laminated, in her mind. "Four of us can't vote. Danny's six."

"Danny's asleep," Ada said. "Danny doesn't need to hear this yet. But yes. Everyone else votes."

So they voted, right there in the kitchen, among the burnt toast and the cinnamon sugar and the red-crossed calendar squares. Run: Ada, Jess, Priya, two others. Stay and find the third thing: Odette, Marisol, Theo. Accept the terms: Mr. Deacon, alone, his hand going up slow and shaking like a man raising it in class knowing the answer will get him beaten.

"It's not a betrayal," he said, to the table, to his hands. "I need you all to understand it isn't. It's triage. It's the oldest arithmetic there is—"

"Stop calling it arithmetic," Marisol said.

Her voice came out louder than she meant. Everyone looked at her. She hadn't planned to speak. Her heart was going like a woodpecker.

"You keep saying it like it's numbers," she said. "Eleven against four hundred. Remainders. Triage. But it's *us*. It's Danny, who lost his tooth yesterday and put it under his pillow even though he knows, he *knows* what's under the school, because he still believes in the tooth fairy, because he's *six*." Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going. "You sit in there and divide us up like homework and then you come out and offer us hot chocolate and a *vote*, like the vote changes what you were doing in there. You were dividing us. All of you. Even you, Ada. Running is just dividing us from the town instead of from each other. Everybody's got a way to make us smaller so it hurts less."

"Marisol—" Odette started.

"No! You don't get to—" She was standing now. She didn't remember standing. "My brother sat in the third row. Nobody voted on him. Nobody even *said his name* after. And now there's a paper with my name on it and the whole grown-up world is in a pantry doing long division with my life, and you want me to be *grateful* you left the door cracked?"

The kitchen was silent. Theo was crying quietly. Mr. Deacon had his face in his hands. Ada looked at her with an expression she couldn't read — grief, maybe, or recognition, like she'd said something he'd been saying to himself for years.

"Kid," Jess said softly, "maybe breathe—"

"I'm not a kid. I'm a remainder." She grabbed her jacket off the back of the chair. "You all keep doing your math. I'm going to go do mine."

"Marisol, no—" Odette was moving, but Marisol was already at the door, and she was fast, faster than a grieving cook with bad knees, faster than a tired teacher, out into the fog that had come up thick off the water like the town exhaling.

* * *

The fog swallowed the streetlights one by one as she ran.

Down Hill Street, past the shuttered cannery, past the church whose bell hadn't rung since the count resumed. Her breath came in white rags. Somewhere behind her she heard a door bang and a voice shouting her name — Ada's voice, hoarse, getting closer or not getting closer, impossible to tell in the fog, sound moved strange here, sound moved like it was wading.

The pit was ahead. She could feel it before she saw it, a cold spot in the air, a silence shaped like a hole. The rain had collapsed the shoring weeks ago and the town had filled it twice and twice it had opened itself again overnight, patient as tide, and now it lay at the edge of the schoolyard like a mouth that had learned to wait.

The humming was louder here. Her grandmother's song, note for note, coming up out of the dark.

Marisol stopped at the edge. Below her, forty feet down, half-buried in wet earth, she could just make out it: the door of blackened oak, iron ring fused solid, sealed from the inside. Sealed by seven children a hundred years ago who loved their brothers and sisters enough to shut themselves in with the hunger. Wardens, Odette called them. First victims. Still children, still waiting.

"Hey," Marisol said, and her voice shook, and she said it anyway. "I know you can hear me. My brother's name was Mateo Vega. Third row. Nobody ever said it again. So I'm saying it. Mateo Vega. Mateo Vega. Mateo—"

The humming stopped.

And then, from below, from behind the door that could only be opened from within, a voice answered — small, and dry, and old as dust, and unmistakably a child's:

"*We know, Marisol. We've been keeping his seat.*"

Behind her, boots pounding through fog, Ada Nakamura shouted her name — and this time it wasn't fear in his voice.

It was recognition. Like he'd heard the voice below too. Like he suddenly understood exactly what kind of payment this door had always taken, and what he was willing to put on the table.
