## 1. The Wrong Twin

The farmhouse appeared out of the corn the way it always had in my memory: too suddenly, as if the road had been holding its breath and finally let it go.

I hadn't been back in twenty years. The GPS on my phone had argued with me for the last ten miles, insisting I turn around, recalculating with the patience of a machine that didn't understand some destinations are chosen against all sense. When the gravel gave way to the long dirt drive, lined with the same crooked fence posts my grandfather had planted before either of us was born, I pulled over and sat with my hands on the wheel and let the engine tick itself cool.

The house was smaller than I remembered. That's what people say about childhood homes, isn't it? A lie we tell ourselves to make the shrinking bearable. But this one really was smaller—or maybe just diminished, the way a face looks diminished in a casket. The white paint had gone gray and chalky, peeling at the corners of the windows like old skin. One shutter hung by a single hinge over the east window, Marisol's window, tilted at an angle that suggested it had been that way for years and no one had cared enough to fix it. The porch light above the front door was on.

It was two o'clock in the afternoon, bright and hot, cicadas sawing through the air like tiny dull blades.

I stared at that light for a long time before I understood what bothered me about it. Then I understood, and decided not to understand. Some things you file away. Some things you refuse to open.

*She left it on for me,* I thought, and then: *No. She died in March. It's July. Lights burn out or get forgotten.* Both thoughts were true. Neither helped.

Marisol had died on a Tuesday, of something the doctors called idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, which is medicine's way of saying *we don't know why her lungs stopped wanting air*. She was thirty-seven. I am thirty-seven. We were twins, which means every birthday I celebrate from now until I die will be half memorial, and I will have to blow out candles knowing she never got to.

I got out of the car. The heat hit me like a wet towel—Kansas in July, thick with the smell of dust and cut hay and, underneath it, something sweeter and wronger, like fruit going bad somewhere out of sight. My heels sank into the dirt. I'd worn city shoes. I'd worn a black dress appropriate for a funeral, though the funeral wasn't until tomorrow, because I didn't own clothes for anything else anymore. Grief had become my wardrobe.

The porch steps creaked under my weight, three of them, each one announcing me. On the second step, someone had chalked a small mark—a circle with a line through it, faded almost to nothing. Rain should have erased it months ago. It hadn't.

I stood on the porch, key in hand—the lawyer had mailed it ahead, along with a folder of documents thick enough to stop a bullet—and found the lock already turned open.

*She knew you'd come,* said a voice in my head that sounded suspiciously like my mother's, dead eleven years now and still editorializing.

*She knew I'd have to come,* I corrected it. Estates don't settle themselves. Debts don't pay themselves off. Sisters don't bury themselves, though God knows Marisol had done everything else alone.

I went inside.

* * *

The house smelled like her.

That's the thing nobody warns you about. You can prepare for the paperwork, the relatives, the casseroles, the terrible small talk of grief. What you can't prepare for is opening a door and being ambushed by a smell—lavender dish soap and woodsmoke and the particular dusty sweetness of an old house in summer—and having your body betray you with recognition before your mind has consented to anything. My knees actually buckled. I caught myself on the doorframe and stood there in the entryway with my eyes shut, breathing my sister in, hating how much I loved it.

"Hello, Ada."

No one said it. The house was empty. But the acoustics of that hallway had always been strange—I used to stand there as a girl and whisper and hear my voice come back from the kitchen a half-second late, like the house was tasting my words before returning them—and for one vertiginous moment I was seven years old again, playing the game where the house talked back.

I opened my eyes. Dust motes turned in the light from the west window. A coat rack held Marisol's denim jacket, sleeves hanging empty. On the hall table, a stack of mail waited, rubber-banded, most of it addressed to *Estate of M. Quintana*, but underneath those, older envelopes, softer with age, addressed to both of us in our grandmother's spidery hand: *To the Quintana girls.*

Both of us. As if Grandmother Elena had never accepted that one of us would leave.

I set down my suitcase. I did not cry. I want that on the record, wherever records are kept: I did not cry, because crying would have meant admitting I belonged here, and I had spent twenty years building a life on the foundation that I did not. New York. An apartment with a view of other apartments. A career editing other people's novels because writing my own felt too much like the stories Marisol and I used to invent on this very porch, and I couldn't risk what they might turn into if I took them seriously.

The kitchen was clean. Too clean, actually—surfaces wiped down, dishes done, a dish towel folded over the oven handle with geometric precision. On the refrigerator, held by a magnet shaped like a strawberry, hung a grocery list in Marisol's slanted hand:

*flour (10 lb)*
*salt*
*candles — the good ones*
*milk, eggs*
*for A. when she comes*

*For A. when she comes.*

I read it four times. The paper trembled slightly, and it took me a moment to realize the trembling was mine.

When had she written this? Her last weeks? Had she known she was dying—known enough to stock a house for a sister who hadn't called since Christmas, who hadn't visited since the funeral-that-wasn't-a-funeral when Mom passed, who had sent money instead of herself because money was easier and money didn't ask questions like *why did you stay* and *why did you never leave*?

I put the list in my purse. I'm not proud of it. It felt like theft, taking evidence of her love without having earned it.

Outside, a car crunched up the drive. Through the window I watched my Aunt Rosa's Buick lurch to a stop, and I straightened my dress and put on the face I'd practiced in airport bathrooms—the composed one, the capable one, the one that says *I'm handling it* so no one asks what it is I'm handling.

Time to be the surviving twin in public.

* * *

The wake was held at the First Methodist hall in town, because the farmhouse was "too far out" for the elderly relatives, which everyone understood to mean *too close to whatever it is about that place that makes us uneasy*. Forty people came. Marisol had lived in this county her whole life; forty people was a poor harvest, and I could feel the room doing arithmetic about it. *The quiet one,* they'd say later. *Kept to herself. Always was odd, after Elena.*

They came to me in a slow procession, offering their hands and their condolences and their carefully averted eyes. I shook hand after hand. Cousin Danny, bald now, kids I didn't know. The pastor, whose name I forgot before he finished saying it. A woman I vaguely recognized as somebody's wife pressed my fingers between both of hers and said, "You're the one who got out," with an admiration that landed on me like a slap dressed as a compliment.

Got out. As if the family were a burning building and I'd leapt from the window while my sister walked back in.

By eight o'clock the punch bowl was drained and the room had thinned to the hardcore: Aunt Rosa presiding over the coffee urn, a few gray-haired men discussing rain like it was scripture. I stood near the folding tables of untouched food, holding a styrofoam cup I had no intention of drinking, performing the role of Grieving Sister with what I hoped was adequate conviction.

That's when I heard it.

Not loudly. That's the thing about whispers—they only need to be loud enough for the person they're aimed at. I was passing the doorway to the kitchen, where a knot of older cousins had gathered, and a woman's voice—Diane, maybe, Danny's wife—slipped out under the clink of spoons:

"—shame is all I'll say. Thirty-seven years she stayed, did right by that house, and where was—"

"Diane." Danny's warning tone.

"I'm just saying what everybody thinks. The wrong one died. That's all anybody's saying. The *wrong one*."

A silence followed, the specific silence of a room full of people listening to see if you heard.

I kept walking. I set my cup down on the table by the door with a steady hand, and I smiled at Aunt Rosa across the room, and I did not go into that kitchen and say the things rising in my throat like floodwater. Because here is what I knew, standing in that church hall in my funeral dress with my sister's grocery list in my purse: Diane was wrong, and Diane was right, and both of those truths were unbearable.

Wrong, because no death is a clerical error, because Marisol had lived a whole interior life none of these people had ever bothered to read, because reducing her to *the one who stayed* was its own kind of obscenity.

Right, because some animal part of me—the part that had been calculating odds since we were born four minutes apart, she first, me second, her leading and me following our entire lives—agreed. Marisol was the better twin. Kinder, steadier, braver in the ways that matter, the kind of brave that doesn't photograph well: staying, enduring, showing up daily for a house and a duty I had run from at eighteen with a scholarship and a suitcase and never once looked back.

Four minutes. That was the margin. Four minutes and one decision, made by a nineteen-year-old with stars in her eyes, and the ledger of our family had balanced itself accordingly ever since.

I found Aunt Rosa by the coffee urn. She was seventy-something and built like a fence post, and she was the only relative all evening who looked me directly in the face.

"You'll stay at the house," she said. Not a question.

"That's the plan."

Her mouth worked around something. She glanced toward the kitchen doorway, then back at me, and what crossed her face wasn't pity exactly. It was closer to the look people give a house they know is condemned.

"Your grandmother had a saying," Rosa said. "About that place. *The Quintanas don't own the farm, mija. The farm owns the Quintanas.*" She patted my arm once, briskly, the way you'd pat a horse you were sending into weather. "Your sister understood that better than anyone. Be gentle with what she left you."

"What did she leave me?"

Rosa looked at me for a long moment. Behind her, the last of the cousins were gathering coats, murmuring their goodbyes to each other and not to me.

"A job," she said finally. "Same job every woman in our family has ever had. Lord rest them."

Then she was gone, and I was alone in a church hall smelling of burnt coffee and cut carnations, holding a dead woman's grocery list, hearing *the wrong one died* replaying in my skull on a loop I couldn't switch off.

* * *

I drove back to the farm in full dark, headlights carving a tunnel out of the black. The corn stood on both sides of the road, taller than the car, moving in a wind I couldn't feel with the windows up—a vast dry rustling, thousands of stalks whispering among themselves, and I had the irrational, skin-crawling certainty that they weren't whispering to each other.

The farmhouse rose out of the dark ahead, and my foot eased off the gas.

The porch light was still on. Of course it was. But as I rolled up the drive I noticed something new, something that hadn't been there that afternoon, and I stopped the car twenty feet out and sat with my heart going hard against my ribs.

There was a shape on the porch steps.

Small. Seated on the bottom step, knees drawn up, perfectly still, in the cone of yellow light. My first thought was a child—whose child, at ten o'clock at night, on a dead woman's porch, miles from any neighbor—and my hand was already reaching for my phone when the shape turned its head and looked at me.

It was too far away to see a face. But I knew the posture. I knew it the way you know a scar.

I used to draw that posture, over and over, at seven years old, in crayon on the backs of wallpaper scraps: a small figure sitting on the porch steps, waiting, patient as furniture. And I had named him, the way children name the things only they can see.

Wren.

"Don't be ridiculous," I said aloud, to the empty car, to myself. My voice sounded thin and young in the dark. "He's not real. He was never real. He was an imaginary friend and imaginary friends don't survive their children."

The figure on the steps did not move. The corn whispered. Somewhere beneath the idling engine, beneath the tires, beneath the packed earth of the drive, I felt—or imagined, I told myself firmly, *imagined*—a low vibration, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

I killed the headlights. In the sudden black, the porch light seemed to brighten, patient and golden, illuminating the small waiting shape like a stage.

Tomorrow I would bury my sister. Tonight, apparently, I was expected.

I put the car in gear and drove the last twenty feet, telling myself the whole time that whatever sat on those steps would be gone by the time I reached the porch—that it was a trick of exhaustion and grief, a shadow, a garden statue, anything.

It wasn't gone.

As I stepped out of the car, the small figure lifted its head, and in a voice like paper turning over, like the house itself remembering how to speak, it said:

"You came back."
