## 8. Return What Was Taken

They had given him the best bed in the village, and that was the worst part.

Ansel lay awake in the Halloway farmhouse listening to the house breathe — floorboards settling, a kettle ticking somewhere below, Wren Halloway's slow footsteps pacing the landing like a shepherd counting a flock that included him. The blanket smelled of lavender and woodsmoke. Someone had left a slice of seed cake on the nightstand, wrapped in a cloth, with a note in a careful hand: *For your strength, returner.*

He'd eaten it. That was the humiliating thing. It had been excellent seed cake.

"You can't be held captive by hospitality," he told the ceiling. "There are rules."

The ceiling did not agree. The half-moon through the window did not agree. Somewhere out past the fields, the bell that did not exist rang a single soft note, the way a mother might tap a spoon against a bowl to say *supper* — and Ansel got up, dressed in the dark, and went to give the tower what it had been asking for.

* * *

St. Brannoc's had been roofless since before the war, its nave a garden of nettles and sheep droppings, its tower the only thing the villagers had bothered to keep standing — and even that they'd kept the way you keep a wasp's nest you can't quite reach: carefully, at arm's length, pretending it isn't there.

The door at the tower's base was not locked. It was worse than locked. It was *polished*, the latch worn bright by generations of hands that had touched it and thought better.

Inside, the stairs spiraled up through a smell of cold stone and old wheat, and Ansel climbed with his recorder in his pocket and his heart going like a cheap metronome. He talked to himself as he climbed, because that was what he did, because silence had become a thing the village could fill with its own voice if he let it.

"Field notes, October the ninth," he murmured. "Investigating the tower of a church that has no bell, in search of a bell that rings. Note for the journal: when the impossible rings, it does not ring *at* you. It rings *for* you. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole horror."

The belfry, when he reached it, was empty in the way a stage is empty before a play. Ropes gone. Beams bare. A round hole in the ceiling where the bell had hung, open to the sky and the swollen half-moon, and beneath it —

Grain.

A heap of it, pale and sweet-smelling, piled in the middle of the belfry floor like something buried and something kept, both at once. Barley, he thought, though the ears were wrong, too long, too golden, golden the way the fields went golden at dusk when the light came from the wrong direction.

Ansel knelt. He was a folklorist; digging was in his blood. He dug with his hands, and the grain was warm — warm as a living thing, warm as a held bird — and three hands' depth down his fingers struck metal.

The clapper.

It came up out of the grain trailing wheat like a drowned man trailing weed: a great iron tongue, longer than his forearm, green with age except where the grain had kept it bright. And wound around it, knotted through the mounting ring, a thread. Not wire. Not twine. A thread of something that caught the moonlight the way a voice catches in a throat — and when Ansel's fingers closed around it, the thread *hummed*.

He knew the hum. He had it on tape. He had it on tape because he had paid a stranger in a county archive forty pounds to copy a 1974 field recording labeled ASHGROVE, M. — HARROW'S BEND, a recording he had told himself he'd ordered for scholarly completeness, a lie so thin you could read a newspaper through it.

His mother's voice. Three seconds of it, on a reel of tape, singing the first line of a song before the recording cut off mid-breath, as if the singer had been *interrupted*.

The thread hummed, and it was that line. It was her line, looped, preserved, kept the way the village kept everything: fed, and waiting.

"Oh," Ansel said. His voice cracked in the middle like a boy's. "Oh, you've had her all along."

The clapper was warm in his hands. The grain rustled though there was no wind. And beneath him, up through the stone, he felt the fields lean in.

* * *

He sat in the belfry until his legs went numb, holding the clapper across his knees like a sleeping child, and he made himself think it through, because thinking was the one tool they hadn't managed to take from him yet.

Wren had told him the shape of it two nights ago, by firelight, in the voice of a woman confessing to a crime she'd inherited: *Each generation gives a voice to the fields, and the fields give back bread. Your mother was the giving for her generation. She sang the counter-verse and broke the rite, and she ran, and she took the giving with her. The land's been starving honest ever since, and starving dishonest too, because the songs got hungry, and hungry songs go looking.*

*The fields have waited fifty years for her blood to come back and finish the verse.*

And now here was her blood, sitting in a belfry with a bell's tongue in his lap and his mother's voice wound round it like a ribbon on a gift.

He took out his recorder. He always thought better with it in his hand; it was his rosary, his compass, his security blanket, and he was not too proud a scholar to admit it.

"Field notes," he said, and his voice steadied. "The clapper of the 1911 bell, buried in grain, bearing a thread of preserved — " he made himself say it — "preserved voice. Mara Ashgrove. My mother. Hypothesis: the bell was melted down, but a bell is not its metal. A bell is its *tongue*. They kept the tongue. They kept the voice with the tongue. The pact doesn't want me to *give* something to the fields. It wants me to give her back."

He stopped. The recorder turned. The tape hissed.

And then, in his own voice, in a cadence that was not his own — flatter, older, patient as weather — the recorder said:

*"And what will it cost him, to give her back?"*

Ansel's thumb found the stop button and pressed it so hard it hurt.

He knew the answer anyway. He had known it since the tapes started filling, since his own memories had begun to go thin as cheap paper — he could no longer remember the exact color of the kitchen in the flat where she'd raised him; he could remember that there *had been* a kitchen, and a color, and that he'd once known it. The village was in his tapes, and his tapes were in his head, and the traffic ran both ways.

The counter-verse was on those tapes. Wren had sung it to him, all of it, the verse Mara had made to break a god's appetite — and to sing it, truly sing it, give it back through his own throat to its owner, he would have to pour into the singing everything the tapes held. Everything the village had poured in. Every scrap of her they'd let him keep: the three recorded seconds, the kitchen's color, the way she'd hummed while cooking, whether she'd hummed while cooking, *whether* —

Whether she'd hummed at all.

That was the trade. That was the whole trade, laid out on the belfry floor in moonlight and barley: her voice to the fields, his memory of her to the dark. The village freed, and him walking out of it with a mother-shaped hole so clean and complete he would not even know what had been taken, because the knowing would go with it.

"Right," said Ansel to the empty tower. "Right. Well. That's — "

He laughed. It came out wrong, wet at the edges.

"That's a terrible deal. I want you to know that. Whoever's listening. That is a *terrible* deal, and my academic supervisor always said I was too generous in the field."

The grain rustled. The thread hummed her three seconds, over and over, patient as a tide.

He thought about the villagers. That was the trap of them — they'd made themselves impossible to hate. Wren, who fed him seed cake and called him *love* and had spent fifty years watching her village's grain come up bitter and her songs come up hungry, and had still, when she confessed, wept more for the fields than for herself. Tam the miller, who left bread on his doorstep with the shy pride of a man courting. The children, who sang the harvest rounds at dusk with their whole hearts because they didn't know the songs were eating, and whose children would inherit the hunger if he walked away.

He thought about his mother, who had run. Who had out-sung a god, briefly, Wren said — *briefly, love, but she did it* — and carried him out of here under her heart, and never once, in all his childhood, had she sung. He'd asked her once, age seven, why she never sang, and she'd gone to the window and looked at the sky for a long time and said, *Some songs you pay for, Ansel. I already paid.*

She had known. All those years, she had known the fields were waiting for her, and she had kept her mouth shut and her son close, and she had paid the price of silence every single day so that the debt would stay hers and not become his.

And the debt had become his anyway, because debts like this don't stay put. They *return*. The whole village sang about it.

"So here's what I think she'd say," Ansel told the clapper, and his voice was almost steady now, the way a rope is steady when you're hanging from it. "I think she'd say: don't you dare trade my voice for your memories, boy, memories are how they get you. And then I think she'd say — because she was my mother, and mothers are contradictory — I think she'd say, *but finish the verse, love. Finish what I started. I couldn't carry the tune all the way. Maybe you can.*"

He stood. His legs were pins and needles; he didn't care.

The thread hummed. The fields leaned in. The half-moon sat in the belfry's round hole like an eye half-open, waiting to open all the way.

"Tomorrow," Ansel said. "Tomorrow night, when you're full. I'll need the moon whole, I think, for what I'm going to sing. And I'll need — "

The tower stairs creaked.

He turned, clapper in his hands, heart in his teeth — and there in the doorway, silhouetted against the stars, stood Wren Halloway in her nightdress and shawl, a lantern in her hand, looking at him with an expression he had never seen on a villager's face before.

She looked *afraid*. Not of him. *For* him.

"Oh, love," she said. "You found her. You found her voice." She came up the last steps slowly, and set the lantern down, and her old eyes were wet and shining. "Then listen to me quick, because tomorrow they'll come for you with bread and music and love, and love is how this village has always fed the field — and you must know the last thing about the counter-verse before you sing it. The thing your mother knew, and I never told you, because I'm a coward, and because I hoped you'd never need it."

She took his hands, and hers were cold as the clapper, and outside, across the dark fields, the bell that did not exist began to toll — faster now, eager, like a heart.

"The verse doesn't break the pact by refusing it," Wren whispered. "It breaks the pact by *paying it*. And the fields will take whatever voice sings it — hers, or yours — and they will not be gentle about the difference."
