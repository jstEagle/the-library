## 6. The Neighbor Who Won't Look at the House

The dog found me before the woman did.

I was standing at the property line that morning, where the Lockridge grass gave way to the Pell driveway, holding my coffee and watching the house the way you watch a sleeping animal — with respect, and from a distance. The dog was a big, gray-muzzled shepherd mix, the kind of animal that has been loved so long it has taken on the shape of its family's patience. It came down the driveway at a trot, ears up, tail making small hopeful circles, and then it stopped.

It stopped as if it had walked into glass.

One moment it was coming toward me, all friendly momentum, and the next its front paws were planted on an invisible seam in the gravel and the rest of its body had decided, unanimously, against proceeding. Its tail stopped. Its ears went back. It looked at me with an expression I recognized from my own work — the look of a creature whose map has just contradicted the territory.

"Brutus," a voice called from the porch next door. "Leave it."

The woman who came down the steps was small and straight-backed, dressed in a cardigan the color of oatmeal despite the warmth of the morning. Her hair was white and pinned with the kind of clips I associate with women who have outlived every argument they ever cared about. She took her time crossing her own yard, and when she reached the dog she did not step past him. She stood beside him at the edge of her property and looked at me across the four feet of gravel that separated us, and something in her face told me that those four feet were not four feet at all.

"You're Marion's granddaughter," she said. Not a question.

"I'm Elodie," I said. "Elodie Lockridge."

"I know who you are." She said it without unkindness, the way you'd say *I know what weather is*. "Ruth Pell." She looked down at Brutus, who was still staring at the ground between them as though the gravel itself had said something rude. "He won't cross," she added, almost apologetically. "Hasn't since 1974. Dogs know better than we do. They always have."

*Since 1974.* The year on the newspaper clipping in my grandmother's study. The year of the man who vanished. I filed it away the way I file everything, which is to say I pretended it was professional habit and not dread.

"I was just looking at the house," I said.

"Don't." Ruth's voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "Don't look at it too long, Miss Lockridge. That's how it starts."

"How what starts?"

She didn't answer. She was looking past me now, at the porch behind me, and her face did something complicated — grief and guilt and a very old, very practiced refusal all moving through it at once like weather fronts. When I turned, the porch was empty. Wren was gone, if Wren had ever been there at all. But the rocking chair was still moving, very slightly, the way a chair moves after someone has just risen from it.

Ruth saw it too. I watched her see it. And then I watched her do the most remarkable thing I had witnessed in thirty years of documenting strange places: she looked away. Deliberately. She turned her head, squared her shoulders, and fixed her eyes on a point somewhere over my left shoulder, at nothing, at the road, at the middle distance, and she did not look back at the house again for the entire length of our conversation.

That was the first thing I understood about Ruth Pell: the not-looking was a discipline. Forty years of practice. You could see the muscle of it in her neck.

"There's a diner in town," she said. "The Kettle. They'll feed you and nobody will ask you questions, because nobody in this town asks questions about that house. It's the one thing we're all agreed on." She hesitated. "Come by this afternoon. If you want to know anything worth knowing, you come to me. But not here. Never here."

"Why never here?"

Brutus whined, low, and pressed himself against Ruth's legs. Above us, on the second floor of my grandmother's house, a curtain shifted in a window I could have sworn had been empty a moment before.

"Because it listens," Ruth said simply. "And because I promised Marion I'd tell you the truth once, and I'd rather do it where the walls can't hear me keep my word."

* * *

The Kettle was the kind of diner that exists in every small town and belongs to no decade in particular. Laminated menus. A pie case. A radio playing something soft and old from a speaker above the register. Ruth sat in the corner booth with her back to the wall — I noticed she'd chosen the seat facing the door, and I noticed further that she'd chosen it so that the window at her shoulder showed only the street, not the ridge where Hollow Creek Road climbed toward the houses at its end.

She ordered tea she didn't drink. I ordered coffee I didn't want. For a while neither of us spoke, and the silence between us had the texture of a held breath.

"You lived here," I said finally. "When I was a child. I remember you. You gave me rhubarb once. From your garden."

"I remember giving you rhubarb." Something moved behind her eyes, quickly buried. "You were a strange little girl. Sweet, but strange. Always writing things down. Your grandmother used to say you'd grow up to be a librarian or a spy." She smiled without any humor in it. "You grew up to be both, I expect."

"What do you mean?"

"You document things," Ruth said. "Marion told me. Maps, photographs, recordings. Twenty years of it, she said. Putting everything on paper so it can't be lost." She wrapped her hands around her teacup but didn't lift it. "You need to stop doing that while you're here. While you're in that house."

"It's what I do," I said. "It's how I understand places."

"That's exactly what I'm afraid of." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped to the register people use in hospitals and churches. "Miss Lockridge. Elodie. I'm going to tell you about 1974, and then you're going to decide whether you believe me, and either way you're going to sell that house and leave, because that is what a sensible woman does with a thing like this."

"And if I'm not sensible?"

"Then you're your grandmother's granddaughter, God help you, and you'll do it the hard way, same as she did."

* * *

What follows is what Ruth Pell told me, in the corner booth of the Kettle, over two hours and one untouched pot of tea. I am setting it down here in order, as best as I received it, because ordering things is how I keep myself from screaming.

In 1974, the Lockridge house was already old, but it was not yet wrong. Ruth was twenty-six that summer, married, living in the house her parents had built next door. Thomas Lockridge was forty-one, a surveyor — I felt that land in my chest like a bruise; the profession came down through the family like eye color — a quiet man who kept bees and played fiddle badly and adored his daughter. His daughter was sick that year. My mother. Ruth wouldn't say with what, only that it was the kind of sick that makes doctors go quiet in hallways and speak in costs.

"Marion had tried everything," Ruth said. "Doctors in three states. Faith healers, which wasn't like her, but a dying child will make a pagan out of anybody. And then in June, something changed. She got hopeful. All at once, overnight hopeful. And I asked her — I was her closest friend, I could ask — I said, Marion, what happened? And she looked at me with this light in her face that scared me worse than the sickness had, and she said, 'We've come to an arrangement.'"

Ruth stopped there. She looked out the window at the ordinary street, at the ordinary afternoon, gathering herself the way you gather a spilled bag of marbles.

"The girl got better," she said. "By July she was up and eating. By August the doctors were using the word *remission* like it was a gift they'd personally delivered. And everyone wanted to celebrate, and Marion let them, and Thomas — Thomas never celebrated. He went quiet that summer. Quiet and thin. Like a man carrying something heavy up a long hill and pretending it's a picnic basket."

"On the last night of September," Ruth said, "Thomas Lockridge walked out his front door at dusk and was never seen again."

She said it flatly, without drama, and somehow that made it worse. No body. No note. His truck stayed in the drive. His boots were by the door — I thought of the boots in the tunnel chamber, the ones I hadn't seen yet, would not see for days, and my hands went cold around my cup. His coat was on its hook. The sheriff's department searched for three weeks. Dragged the creek, walked the ridge, flew a plane low over the county. Nothing. Not a button, not a thread.

"And here's the part you need to hear," Ruth said, and her voice went hard, harder than grief, hard the way things get when they've been rehearsed in silence for fifty years. "The town knew. Not the details. We knew enough. We knew that Marion Lockridge had made some kind of bargain, and we knew what the price had been, and we knew — every one of us knew — and we said nothing. We brought her casseroles. We sat with her at church. We let her bury an empty box and put a stone in the cemetery with Thomas's name on it, and not one person in Hollow Creek ever asked her where he was. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Fifty years of complicity. We fed our silence to that house right along with everything else. Silence is a kind of witness, Elodie. It's the worst kind. It's witness that agrees to lie."

"Why?" I asked. It came out smaller than I intended. "Why would everyone agree to that?"

"Because of what happened to the ones who didn't." Ruth finally lifted her teacup, found it cold, set it down again. "Old Man Dill, in '76. He drank, and drunk men talk, and he started saying at the Legion that Marion Lockridge had killed her husband and buried him under the floorboards. He was going to get a warrant. He told people he was going to bring a crowbar and open up her floors himself." She met my eyes. "Dill walked home from the Legion on a Tuesday night, cutting across the edge of the Lockridge property the way he'd cut across it his whole life, and he never arrived. They found his hat at the tree line. Just the hat. And after that, nobody talked. After that, the whole town learned to look away from that house the way I look away from it now. You train yourself. You keep your eyes on the road. You teach your children the property line the way you teach them about traffic."

Brutus, I thought. The dog at the invisible wall. Fifty years of dogs taught by their mothers' mothers.

"Did you ever ask Marion directly?" I said. "Afterward. In all those years."

"Once." Ruth's composure cracked, just at the seam, just for a second, and underneath it was something so tired and so tender that I had to look down at my own hands. "Maybe ten years ago. We were old women by then, sitting on her porch — on that porch — and I asked her, plain out, what happened to Thomas. And she was quiet a long time. And then she said—" Ruth closed her eyes, reciting it, and I understood she had carried these words for a decade like a stone in her shoe. "She said, 'Ruthie, the house loves us. That's the horror of it. It doesn't hate anyone. It takes what it loves, and it keeps it, and it takes care of it, and it waits for you to love it back.' And then she patted my hand and asked if I wanted more lemonade, and we never spoke of it again, and I let her not speak of it, because I was a coward, and because some part of me — God forgive me — some part of me was afraid that if I pushed, the house would notice me too."

The pie case hummed. The radio murmured. Somewhere in the kitchen a bell rang for an order no one rushed to fill.

"There's one more thing," Ruth said. "And then I'm done, and you'll do what you'll do, because Lockridges always do what they'll do." She reached into her handbag and set something on the table between us: a photograph, creased white with folding, old color gone amber. Two young couples on a porch — the Lockridge porch, before the paint faded. Thomas with his arm around Marion, laughing at something outside the frame. Beside them, a younger man with dark hair and Marion's own smile on a different face, his hand resting on the shoulder of a boy of maybe eight. The boy was grinning gap-toothed, squinting into the sun.

"My husband, God rest him," Ruth said, tapping the younger man. "And that's our sister's boy. Samuel. He was over at the Lockridge place constantly that summer. Him and your mother were thick as ticks." Her finger hovered over the boy and did not quite touch him. "Samuel Pell disappeared three weeks before Thomas did. Same summer. Same road. He was walking home from the Lockridge house at suppertime and just — wasn't anymore. And the terrible thing, the thing I can't get out from under even now, is that when Samuel vanished, Marion's daughter started getting better. Not slowly. Overnight. And when Thomas vanished—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Well. By then we'd all stopped asking what the improvements cost."

I stared at the boy in the photograph. Eight years old, gap-toothed, squinting. Something in me was ringing like a struck wire, some connection assembling itself below the level of thought, in the place where I keep the maps I haven't drawn yet.

A yellow raincoat. A too-wide smile. A friend who had waited thirty years on a porch without aging a single day.

"Ruth," I said carefully. "What was Samuel's last name?"

"Pell. Same as mine, why?" She frowned. "My sister's boy. Why do you ask?"

"No reason," I said, and heard my own voice do the thing voices do when they are lying competently. "Thank you. For telling me."

She left money on the table for a tea she hadn't touched, and at the door she paused with her back to me and said, without turning around: "Sell the house, Elodie. Take whatever's in it that matters and burn the rest, and sell it to somebody from far away, and never come back. That's the advice. That's the advice I gave Marion in 1974, and she kissed my cheek and said, 'But Ruthie, it's such a good house.'" Ruth's shoulders rose and fell once. "It's a hungry house. There's no such thing as a good hungry thing."

Then she pushed through the door into the afternoon, and I watched through the window as she crossed to where Brutus waited at the curb — waiting, I noticed, well short of the diner's window, well short of any line of sight toward the ridge — and the two of them went off together down the street, the woman and the dog, both of them keeping their eyes forward, both of them trained by half a century never to look back.

* * *

I sat in the booth a long time after she was gone, and I did the thing I always do, the thing Ruth had begged me not to do: I documented.

I wrote until the waitress refilled my cup twice and the light through the window went from gold to gray. I wrote down 1974 and June and *we've come to an arrangement*. I wrote down Thomas, boots by the door, coat on its hook. I wrote down Samuel Pell, eight years old, gap-toothed, last seen walking home from the Lockridge house. I wrote down *the house loves us, that's the horror of it.*

And then, at the bottom of the page, I drew the thing I had been refusing to draw since Ruth first laid the photograph on the table. A small figure in a yellow raincoat. A smile drawn too wide. And beside it, in my careful surveyor's lettering, a question I had already answered:

*Wren — b. ?*

*If Samuel Pell disappeared in 1974, and Wren has not aged in thirty years, then what did I play with as a child?*

I knew the answer. I had known it since the porch, since the too-wide smile, since the words *you came back to finish it.* Some part of me had known it the way the town knew about Thomas — completely, and in silence, and with the specific cowardice of someone who loves the thing they should be running from.

Because here is what I had not written down, what I had never written down, what lived only in the oldest room of my memory: when I was seven years old, Wren told me about the tunnels under the house. Wren drew them for me in the dirt with a stick. Wren knew every turn of them, every chamber, every throat.

An eight-year-old boy had gone into those tunnels in 1974 and never come out.

And something wearing his absence had spent thirty years being my best friend.

I closed the notebook. Outside, the first rain of the evening had begun to fall, and far up the ridge, barely visible through it, a light had come on in the upstairs window of my grandmother's house — though I had left every lamp in it dark.

It was not the porch light. It was the window of the locked study.

And it was on.
