## 12. Phone Call from a Dead Line

Sleep had become a negotiation.

Elodie had learned, over the years, to treat it like a border crossing — you surrendered your documents, your name, your weapons, and you hoped the country on the other side would let you through. In the Hollow Creek house, the negotiation had grown more complicated. She slept with the lamp on now, and with the ledger open on the nightstand like a shield, and with her recorder running on the dresser, its red eye a small patient wound in the dark. She told herself the recorder was for documentation. She told herself a great many things these days, in the flat, reasonable voice she used for things she did not believe.

The house had been quiet for three nights. Too quiet, in the way that a held breath is quiet. The floors did not shift. The pantry hatch, which she had nailed shut with a two-by-four and six-inch spikes, did not creak. Even the wallpaper in the upstairs hall — the pattern of small climbing roses that she had begun, in her worst hours, to suspect were arranged in the shape of hands — stayed perfectly still.

On the third night, at three o'clock in the morning, the telephone rang.

Elodie was not asleep. She was lying on top of the covers with her boots on, which had become her compromise with the house: ready to run, pretending to rest. The sound went through her like a fishhook through water — a single bright jerk, and then she was reeling toward it before her mind had agreed to anything.

The landline. The rotary telephone in the downstairs hall, cream-colored, its cord a coiled yellow intestine. She had found it on her first day, lifted the receiver out of habit, and heard the flat arterial silence of a dead line. No dial tone. She had checked with her phone — no service registered to the property, no account, nothing. The line had been cut at the pole years ago, Ruth Pell said, after Marion Lockridge stopped paying for it and the company stopped asking. *Nobody calls that house,* Ruth had said, and had not elaborated, and Elodie had not asked, because by then she had learned the shape of the silences in this town and how they were load-bearing.

It rang again.

The sound was wrong in a way she couldn't immediately name, and then she could: it rang with a bell, an old mechanical bell, a bright brass shivering — but the phone on the hall table was not a bell phone. It was the later model, the one with the electronic warble. The bell belonged to the phone her grandmother had kept when Elodie was a child, the heavy black one with the finger-wheel, the one Elodie had been forbidden to touch because *the calls that come to this house are not for children.*

She went down the stairs the way you wade into cold water. Each step she took, the ringing seemed to wait for her, patient as a held note. The hall was dark except for the light over the stairs, and the telephone sat on its little table like a sleeping animal that had learned to imitate furniture.

It rang a third time.

She picked it up.

She did not say hello. Some instinct, old and cold and older than she was, told her that greeting it would be an invitation, that the first word spoken into that receiver would belong to her and could be kept. So she said nothing. She stood in the dark hall with the receiver against her ear and listened to the connection open like a throat.

Static first. The static of a long distance, of weather happening somewhere else, of a wire stretched across years. And beneath the static — breathing. Slow. Familiar. The particular rhythm of a woman who had smoked for forty years and then stopped, abruptly, in 1989, and whose lungs had never quite forgiven her.

"Grandma?" Elodie said, and hated herself for it, and heard the word come out of her sounding nine years old.

The breathing stopped. The static arranged itself, grain by grain, into a voice.

*"*— entry for the fourteenth of June, nineteen ninety-four,*"* Marion Lockridge said.

Her voice was exactly as Elodie remembered it, which was the first impossible thing, and it was coming through a line that had been dead for a decade, which was the second, and it did not sound like a recording, which was the third and worst. A recording has a room inside it. A recording carries the acoustics of wherever it was made, the faint box of the past around the voice. This voice had no room. It was close. It was closer than the receiver. It was the distance of a mouth.

*"Item: one girl, Elodie Marion Lockridge, age nine, resident, blood line direct. Disposition: deferred. Note —"* the voice paused, and in the pause Elodie heard the sound of a page turning, dry and deliberate, *"— note: she draws the tunnels. She has named the mouth. The house is fond of her. Fondness is not forgiveness. Payment postponed to majority, interest accruing."*

"Stop," Elodie said. "Stop it. You're dead. This isn't — you're dead, and this line is dead, and I know what a recording sounds like, I've spent my whole life listening to recordings, and this isn't one."

The voice continued, gentle, unhurried, in the tone Marion Lockridge had used for reading recipes aloud, for reciting psalms, for telling Elodie about the weather that was coming three days before it came.

*"Entry for the second of November, nineteen ninety-four. Item: one girl, as above. The house asked for her. The house asks every year now. It likes her drawings. It says she sees it truly, and a thing seen truly is a thing half-taken. I told it no. I told it no, and it laughed under the floors, and it said, then we will wait, Marion. We are very good at waiting. We have a girl in the ground who has waited longer than you have been alive."*

Elodie's free hand had found the wall. She pressed her palm flat against the wallpaper, against the small climbing roses, needing the proof of something solid. The paper was cool. The paper was breathing. She took her hand away.

*"Entry for the spring of ninety-five. The girl has gone back to the city. The house sulks. It has begun leaving the porch light on. It knows she will come back. Everything comes back to a house that loved it first. Note to self: when she comes back, tell her nothing. Let her be a child as long as the world allows. The debt is mine. I will keep it mine as long as I can hold the pen."*

"Grandma." Elodie's voice cracked down the middle. "Grandma, I'm here. I came back. I found the ledger, I found the — the feedings, all of it, I know what you did, I know about Thomas, I know about the bargain. Just — talk to me. Not the ledger. Talk to *me.*"

For a moment, only static. Then, quieter, the voice came off the page. It wavered. It was, for three seconds, an old woman's voice and not the ledger's.

*"Elodie. Baby girl. Don't be in the hall."*

The line went to a dial tone — a real dial tone, round and humming, the sound of a living line, and it was somehow more obscene than everything before it — and then the dial tone said, in her grandmother's voice, in the ledger's cadence:

*"The debt transferred with the deed."*

The line went dead. Truly dead this time — she could hear the difference, the flat nothing of a severed wire, the silence of a thing that had never been connected at all. She stood in the dark hall holding the receiver against her ear long after there was nothing to hear, the way you keep holding someone's hand after they've stopped holding back.

* * *

She did not sleep again. She did not try.

At four she made coffee in the kitchen and drank it standing up, facing the window over the sink, watching the yard resolve itself out of the dark in gray increments. The porch light was on. It was always on now. She had unscrewed the bulb on her second day; it had been burning when she came down the next morning. She had unscrewed it again, and then taken the whole fixture down, and the light had simply been *on*, a warm yellow glow with no source, falling on the boards of the porch where Wren sat sometimes in the evenings with his knees drawn up and his too-wide smile, watching the road as if expecting someone.

At five she went to the study. The lock on the door had been forced — by her, on the fourth day, with a screwdriver and a fury she hadn't known she was carrying — and she had not closed it since. She couldn't. Closing it felt like agreeing to forget what was inside.

The ledger lay open on the desk where she'd left it, weighted with a paperweight her grandmother had owned, a brass hand holding a glass sphere. Elodie had spent two days with the ledger after forcing the door, photographing every page, transcribing every entry, building a timeline in her notebook with the same rigor she'd once used for storm tunnels. She knew it by heart now, the way you know a wound by heart. But she turned to June 1994 anyway, because the voice had recited it, and she needed to see the words in her grandmother's own hand.

They were there. All of them. *Item: one girl.* The dates matched. The phrasing matched. Even the pause — the place where the voice had stopped, where a page had turned — corresponded to the bottom of the page, where the ink changed weight, where Marion had set down her pen and picked it up again.

But there was one thing in the ledger that had not been there when Elodie had photographed it four days ago.

Below the final entry — below the entry with her own childhood name in it, the entry that had felt like a knife going in and had not yet come out — a new line had been written. The ink was fresh. It was still, faintly, wet at the edges, the way ink is when the paper has been holding it for only hours.

*Received: one call, placed. Acknowledgment of transfer. The girl has been told.*

Elodie stood in the study with the ledger under the desk lamp and felt the grief in her chest turn over, the way a fish turns over when the water changes. It had been grief since the funeral — since before the funeral, since the phone call from the hospice, since the word *died* had entered her life and rearranged its furniture. She had been carrying it like a weight carried in both arms, heavy but honest, heavy but hers.

Now she looked at it and saw that it had been handled. That something had picked it up and turned it and set it down facing the direction it wanted her to walk.

The inheritance notice. She thought of it now with a cold new attention. It had arrived in Detroit eleven days after the death — fast, efficient, oddly eager for a probate process in a county with one traffic light. The letter had been typed, professional, unremarkable, except that it had named her as sole heir with no mention of other kin, and her mother — Marion's daughter, the sick child of 1974, the life that had been bought with Thomas's disappearance — had not been mentioned at all, as if the lawyer had known, as if the lawyer had understood that the deed did not pass through the daughter, that the daughter had never been part of the transaction, that the transaction had always had only two parties and one of them was the ground.

The house had chosen her. Not the family. *Her.* The girl who drew the tunnels. The girl who named the mouth. The one who saw it truly.

And a thing seen truly, the ledger said, is a thing half-taken.

She sat down in her grandmother's chair, and the chair received her with a creak of settled leather, and she put her face in her hands and let herself, for exactly one minute by the study clock, be a woman crying at her dead grandmother's desk. She gave it the full minute. She had learned that from the mapping: you do not pretend the chasm isn't there; you measure it, and then you build.

When the minute was over she took out her notebook and wrote, in pencil, because pencil was for things she might have to revise:

*The inheritance was not a gift. It was a summons. The house sent for me the way you send for a witness — no. The way you send for a meal. It has been setting a place for me since 1994. Grandma kept it waiting. Grandma is dead, and the waiting is over, and the invitation is in my name now, written in ink that dries only when I've read it.*

She looked at the last line and underlined it.

*Ink that dries only when I've read it.* She was beginning to understand the grammar of the place. The house did not move when she watched it. The house did not write when she wasn't looking — no. The house wrote *because* she was looking. Her attention was not a defense. It was a delivery system. Every photograph she took, every page she transcribed, every careful measurement she recorded was a letter the house could read over her shoulder, and the house was learning her handwriting, and last night it had used her grandmother's voice to sign something.

She had thought documentation was a prayer. She had built her whole life on the idea that a thing recorded was a thing saved.

Standing in her grandmother's study, in the gray before dawn, with the ledger open and the new ink drying, Elodie felt the idea crack down its long axis for the first time. Because a prayer goes up, toward something that listens out of love. This went down. Everything here went down. And what listened beneath the floors did not love her — it *cherished* her, which was worse, which was the word the house would have chosen, the word it kept in the chamber below with the baby shoes and the watch, the word it had been keeping warm for thirty years.

*Fondness is not forgiveness,* the ledger said. And: *a thing seen truly is a thing half-taken.*

She closed the ledger. She carried it out of the study and down the hall, and as she passed the telephone table she stopped, because the receiver was off the hook. She had left it on the hook — she was almost sure, she was professionally almost sure — and it lay on the hall table now like a tongue lolling out of a mouth, and from the earpiece, very faint, came not a voice but a sound she recognized from her childhood, from the summers here, from the nights when the floors had murmured under her sleeping bag:

Humming.

A child's tune. Simple, looping, patient. The kind of thing a child hums when it is waiting for its mother to come back into the room.

Elodie set the ledger down on the table, picked up the receiver, and hung it up with a hand that did not entirely shake. Then she stood in the hall and listened to the house settle around her — the small, satisfied sounds of a structure that had gotten what it wanted, that had delivered its message, that had all the time in the world.

Outside, the sky was going pale at the edges. The porch light was still on. And on the porch, in the first gray light, small and still and smiling, sat the yellow raincoat, waiting for her to come out, waiting to ask her — as it asked every morning now, with that wide, fond, terrible tenderness — whether she had slept well.

She had one thought as she watched it through the glass, and the thought was cold and clear and hers:

Her grandmother had spent fifty years keeping the house's hands off her. And the first thing the house had done with her inheritance was use her dead grandmother's voice to tell her the debt was due.

She picked up the ledger again and went to find Ruth Pell. There were questions the dead couldn't answer, and she was done asking them.
