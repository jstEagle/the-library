## 14. Marion's Confession, Part One

The study smelled of dust and cedar and something older underneath both — the smell of a room that had been closed on purpose, the way you close a jar.

Elodie sat on the floor with her back against the desk, the forced lock's brass shards still glittering in the lamplight like dead insects. She had meant to sleep. She had meant to do anything but sit in this room another minute, but her body had carried her here anyway, the way water carries a leaf toward the drain. The ledger lay open beside her, its last entry still burning in her mind like a brand:

*E. Lockridge, b. 1984. Held in reserve.*

Held in reserve. As if she were a canning jar. As if she were fruit put up against winter.

She had been sitting there for perhaps an hour when she noticed the drawer.

It was the bottom one, the shallow false-front kind that old desks sometimes hid, and it had sprung loose when she'd wrenched the main lock. Inside: a single cassette tape in a paper sleeve, unlabeled except for a date written in her grandmother's small, upright hand — *March 1998* — and beneath that, two words:

*For Elodie.*

Her own name, in Marion's handwriting, should not have hurt the way it did. She had been reading that name all day, in the ledger, in the margins of books, in the scratched-out photographs. But seeing it here — *for* her, addressed to her, intended across all those years — made her chest tighten around something hot and swallowed-whole.

The tape deck was on the shelf above the desk, an old silver Sony with a cracked cassette door, and when Elodie pressed PLAY the machine made a sound like a long-held breath finally released.

Static. Then a click. Then her grandmother's voice, closer than a ghost, closer than memory, filling the study as if Marion herself were kneeling on the other side of the desk.

"If you're hearing this," the voice said, "then I'm dead, and you came back anyway, and I'm sorry. God help me, Elodie, I'm sorry for all of it."

Elodie's hand froze over the STOP button. She didn't press it.

"I don't know how to say any of this," Marion continued, "so I'm just going to say it the way I've said it to myself every night for twenty-four years, lying in bed, listening to the house settle. The way I've confessed it to no one. You're my priest now. Isn't that a terrible thing to make of a child. Isn't that a terrible thing to have done."

A pause. On the recording, somewhere far away, a door closed — or the house breathed; by now Elodie could no longer tell the difference.

"Your grandfather didn't run off." The words came out flat, rehearsed, worn smooth from decades of internal repetition. "I know what the town thinks. I know what your mother thinks, what she was told. He didn't leave us. I gave him to the house."

The lamp flickered. Elodie told herself it was the wiring.

"You were three years old," Marion said. "You won't remember the winter of '74, thank God, but I remember every hour of it. Your mother — my Lila — she had the fever. Scarlet fever, the doctors said, though they said it strangely, like men naming a weather system they didn't understand. It went into her blood. By February she was sleeping nineteen hours a day and waking confused, calling for people who weren't in the room. Dr. Amos at the county hospital used the word *septic* and then he used the word *comfort* and then he stopped looking me in the eye, because that's what doctors do when they've already written the date."

Elodie realized she had pulled her knees up to her chest. She was thirty-nine years old and she was curled on the floor of a haunted study like a child at story time, and she could not make herself move.

"I wasn't raised religious," Marion said. "My people were practical. Farmers, machinists. We believed in seed rotation and tire pressure. So when the house spoke to me, I didn't have a theology for it. I didn't even have a word for it. I want you to understand that, Elodie. I want you to understand that I was not a woman who believed in magic. I was a woman whose daughter was dying, and something in the walls offered me a trade, and I was too tired and too frightened and too much in love with my child to ask the right questions."

Another pause. When the voice resumed, it was quieter, as if Marion had leaned toward the microphone.

"It started with the sounds. That February, the house began making noises under the floors. Not settling — settling is random, settling is tired wood. These were rhythmic. A slow peristalsis, if I'd known the word then. A swallowing sound. And warm air coming up through the gaps in the floorboards, air that smelled like bread baking, like a kitchen in a house where someone loved you. Do you understand how cruel that is? How precise? It smelled like safety. It smelled like my mother's kitchen, and my mother had been dead eleven years."

Elodie's skin crawled. She thought of the second chamber, the preserved belongings, Thomas's watch ticking in its velvet dark. *The house keeps what it loves.* And it loved with a connoisseur's precision. It knew exactly which smells opened a grieving woman like a key opens a cheap lock.

"On the third night," Marion said, "I got down on the kitchen floor and I put my ear to the boards, and I heard it breathing, and I heard — under the breathing — voices. Small ones. And I know, I *know*, how that sounds. I'd been sleeping four hours a night for six weeks. But I pressed my mouth to the gap between the boards and I said, out loud, like a fool, like a woman praying to a furnace: *Can you help her?*

"And the house said yes."

The tape hissed. In the silence, Elodie became aware of the study around her — the wall of defaced photographs, the ledger, the window where the curtain hung perfectly still despite the draft she could feel moving along the floor. The house was listening to its own confession. Of course it was. It had probably been waiting fifty years for someone to play this tape. Maybe it had kept the tape safe the way it kept everything else. Maybe this room was less a study than a reliquary, and Marion's voice was the relic, and Elodie was the pilgrim who had finally come.

"It didn't speak in words, not at first," Marion said. "It showed me. I closed my eyes and I saw Lila well. I saw her at nine, at fifteen, at her wedding — your father was there, young, ridiculous haircut — I saw her holding a baby with your eyes. The whole life, Elodie. The whole unwritten life, shown to me in the space of a breath, the way you'd show a starving woman a table set for a feast. And then the voice — and by then it was a voice, it was inside my skull, it was gentle, that's the thing I need you to believe, it was *gentle* — the voice said: *One for one. A life for a life. Give me what you love second-best, and keep what you love best.*

"Second-best." A sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter could rust. "That's how it phrased it. Like a butcher explaining cuts of meat. And I lay on that cold floor with my daughter dying upstairs, and I thought: Thomas would understand. I actually thought that. I thought, Thomas loves her almost as much as I do, Thomas would trade himself, Thomas would *insist*. And maybe he would have. I never asked him. That's the sin I can't get around, Elodie. Not the bargain itself. That I never gave him the chance to refuse it."

Elodie pressed her palm flat against the floorboards. They were warm. Slightly, faintly warm, like the flank of a sleeping animal, and she snatched her hand back and wiped it on her jeans as if warmth were contagion.

"I didn't say yes out loud," Marion said. "I want that on the record, whatever record this is. I never said the word *yes*. I said — I said, *take whatever you need.* I said, *do what you have to.* I was crying into the floorboards and I said, *just let her live.* And the house took that. It took the shape of my grief and it poured itself into it, and it considered itself answered. Considered itself *contracted*. Because that's what it does. It doesn't trick you, exactly. It just holds the door open while you walk through it yourself, and then it counts the steps as consent."

On the tape, a chair creaked. Marion shifting position, twenty-six years ago, in this same room.

"Thomas died on March fourth, 1974. Officially. The official version is that he drove to Canton for parts and never arrived. His truck was found — I paid a man to abandon his truck near the reservoir, and I have regretted that lie so long it's grown roots in me. What actually happened: I sent him to the pantry for a jar of preserves. I sent him. I knew, by then, I knew what the house wanted, and I sent him anyway, and I stood in the kitchen and listened to the floor open him like a mouth."

Elodie's stomach turned over. She thought of the hatch behind the pantry. She thought of Tunnel One, and the portraits wallpapering the chamber, and how many mouths a house could have.

"And Lila woke up the next morning asking for breakfast," Marion said simply. "The fever broke overnight. The doctors called it a miracle. I called it nothing. I never called it anything. You don't name a thing like that; naming is how it gets a hold on you, and it already had the only hold it needed. It had my signature in everything but ink."

The voice grew quieter. Older-sounding, though it was the same recording — as if the years were compressing, all of them funneling down into these final minutes of magnetic tape.

"But here's what I learned, and learn it fast, girl, because you're standing in my kitchen and the floorboards are warm under your feet whether you've noticed yet or not: the house doesn't consider a debt *paid*. It considers a debt *opened*. Thomas bought Lila's life, fine. But then the house decided that a life saved accrues interest. Every year Lila lived, the balance grew. Every year after, when your mother left and it was just me and the house and the quiet — every year I paid. Small things, at first. Things I could pretend were superstition. A cup of milk poured down the pantry drain. My garden's first tomato. Then the Hendersons' boy who used to cut through our property — he didn't disappear because of me, I don't think, I *don't think* — but I heard the floor swallow that night too, and I wrote the date in the ledger, and I didn't call anyone, and that is also a payment. Silence is currency here. Attention is currency. Everything is currency. The house is rich, Elodie. The house has been getting richer for fifty years, and I am the account that kept it solvent."

Elodie looked at the ledger beside her. All those entries. Dates, names, offerings. Fifty years of bookkeeping for a predator, kept in a careful hand, in a locked room, by a woman who had once been desperate enough to feed it and then dutiful enough to keep feeding it forever.

"I tried to end it twice," Marion said. "1979, I packed the car and we drove as far as Zanesville before I turned around, because Lila had developed a cough that wouldn't quit and I couldn't stop thinking: *interest*. And 1988, the year you were born —" Elodie's breath caught — "I stood in the kitchen with a hammer and I meant to pry up the hearthstones, I meant to find whatever was down there and burn it, and the house showed me your face. Your infant face, asleep. And it said, in that gentle voice, *she has such a long life ahead of her,* and I put the hammer down, and I went back to paying."

A long silence on the tape. Long enough that Elodie checked the counter — the reels were still turning.

"The last thing I'll say is this," Marion said finally, and her voice changed — dropped its confessional steadiness and became, suddenly, terribly, the voice of an old woman talking to a child. "You were four years old the summer you met Wren. I watched you on that porch, talking to empty air, laughing at nothing, and I told myself it was imaginary friends, it's healthy, the doctors say it's healthy. And then I found your drawings. The yellow coat. The smile too wide. And I went down to the pantry and I put my ear to the floor and I asked the house, *what did you give my granddaughter,* and the house laughed. It laughed through every board in the kitchen at once, Elodie, and it said: *I didn't give her anything. I lent her a friend. She'll bring the friend back when she's ready to stay.*

"So I kept you away. Twenty years of Sundays I invented reasons you couldn't visit. And it worked, mostly, and then I died, and the lawyers did what lawyers do, and here you are. Here you are with the deed in your pocket and the porch light on and Wren waiting on the step like a dog that never doubted you'd come home."

The tape ran on into hiss. Elodie sat motionless, tears running freely now, not grieving — past grieving — cataloguing. That was what she did. That was the only prayer she knew. Her grandmother had loved her enough to keep her away for twenty years and had died leaving her the debt anyway, because love, in this house, was just another word for collateral.

Then, near the end of side A, Marion's voice returned one last time, low and fast, as if someone might be listening. As if someone *were* listening.

"There's a second tape. Side B of this one won't play — I broke it myself, and I'm not sorry. The second tape tells you how to finish it. Where I hid it, I won't say on this recording. The house hears everything spoken aloud in its rooms. Find the second tape, Elodie. And whatever you do —"

The tape warbled. For half a second, beneath Marion's voice, Elodie heard something else: a low, wet, rhythmic sound, like swallowing, like the house leaning in.

"— don't trust the yellow coat."

Click. The reel spun out into silence.

Elodie sat in the dark of the study for a long time, the dead tape warm in her hands, the ledger open beside her, the floorboards faintly warm beneath her knees. Somewhere below her, deep in the throat of the house, something shifted its weight.

And from the hallway, cheerful and unhurried, came the sound of small footsteps — and a voice singing a song Elodie hadn't heard since 1994, getting closer.

* * *

She was on her feet with the tape clutched to her chest when the footsteps stopped outside the study door. No knock. No shadow under the door. Just the pause of something polite, something patient, something that had all night and all decade and all century.

"Elodie?" Wren's voice, bright as a bell over a grave. "Supper's ready. Grandma made too much again."

Through the door, Elodie could smell it — bread baking, and under the bread, faintly, the smell of a kitchen where someone loved you.

Her grandmother's recipe. Her grandmother's kitchen. A meal that could not exist, cooked by hands that were ash, offered by a mouth that was borrowed.

Elodie looked down at the broken tape in her shaking hands, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the second tape was the only thing in the world the house was afraid of —

— and that she had just heard its owner say, out loud, in these walls, that it existed.
