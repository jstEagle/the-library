## 15. Wren Asks a Favor

I have been the house's mouth for fifty years, and in all that time I have learned one thing about mouths: they are made for asking.

The house asks through me. It asks for salt, for blood, for the small warm offerings that keep its appetite banked like coals. It asks through the creak of stairs and the drip of faucets and the way the porch light buzzes at dusk. But tonight I am not asking for the house. Tonight I am asking for myself, and I do not know how to do it, because I have never been anything but the house's mouth, and a mouth that speaks for itself is a mouth learning a new language.

She is in the kitchen with her camera balanced on a stack of field notebooks, photographing the pantry wall where the hatch hides behind a shelf of dust-canned peaches. The flash pops. Pop. Pop. Each burst of light is a small act of mercy she doesn't know she's performing — every photograph pins the house down like a moth on corkboard, holds it still, holds it *here*, keeps it from sliding sideways into the places it goes when no one looks. She figured that out two nights ago. I watched her figure it out. I watched her face change in the dark of the hallway, the flashlight under her chin, when she understood that the walls held still for the lens the way a dog holds still for a hand it fears will strike or pet, and it cannot tell which.

Elodie Lockridge. She smells like cold air and film chemicals and the particular iron tang of grief that hasn't been eaten yet. The house wants her. I can feel it wanting her the way I can feel weather coming in my teeth — which are my teeth now, though they weren't always, though there was a boy once who had smaller ones, crooked ones, a boy who bit into a Honeycrisp apple on an October afternoon in 1974 and never finished it.

But that's later. That's the part I have to make myself say out loud, and mouths are cowards.

* * *

I come to her the way I always come to her: through the door that isn't a door, the one between the kitchen and the back hall that only opens when the house permits it, which is always, because the house likes us together. The house thinks I am softening her up. The house is not wrong, exactly. But the house also thought Thomas would go quietly, and the house has been wrong before, and it forgets its wrongs the way it forgets everything it swallows — completely, and without indigestion.

"Elodie."

She doesn't jump anymore. That's new. Three weeks ago she jumped; two weeks ago she flinched; last week she turned slowly, camera first. Now she just turns, and the yellow raincoat I wear — my raincoat, the one I died in, the one the house remade around me out of memory and vinyl and appetite — drips a little on the linoleum, because it always drips, even indoors, even in summer, even when it hasn't rained. The dripping is the house's joke. Or maybe it's mine. After fifty years I can't always tell where the house ends and I begin, and that is the thing I need to tell her, and I am circling it like a fly at a window.

"You're doing the pantry again," I say.

"The hatch moves." She taps the shelf. "Half an inch left since Tuesday. If I don't photograph it every day I lose the reference points." She says it flatly, professionally, but I see what's underneath, because I have spent five decades learning to read the faces of people who are trying very hard not to be afraid: underneath the surveyor's calm is a girl who used to sit on this same floor with crayons, drawing tunnels on butcher paper while I told her where the walls went.

"You were good at this even then," I say. "Mapping things."

"I had a good teacher." A pause. Her eyes go to my smile, which is too wide, which has always been too wide, which the house gave me along with the coat and the name. "You told me where everything went. You never told me why you knew."

Ah. There it is. The question under all her questions, surfacing at last like a body in a flooded quarry. I have been waiting for it the way the house waits at the bottom of the throat — patiently, hungrily, with something that is almost love.

"Why *do* you know, Wren?"

* * *

Here is what I remember of Samuel Pell, and I remember it the way you remember a book you read as a child: vividly, and in the wrong order, and with the ending missing.

I remember my mother's kitchen smelled of bread and bleach. I remember Ruth — Aunt Ruth then, just Ruth, just the woman next door who let me wind her garden hose into perfect coils because I liked how the circles nested — I remember Ruth paid me a nickel once to recite the alphabet backwards and I did it and she laughed so hard she had to sit down on her own porch step. I remember the Lockridge place was warm. Everyone said so. Before, people said the Lockridge house was the warmest house in Hollow Creek, and children were welcome in it, and Marion Lockridge kept a jar of peppermints on the hall table at exactly the height of a child's reaching hand.

I remember October 1974. I remember cutting across the Lockridges' back field because it was faster than the road, and the light going strange and honeyed the way it does before snow, and the back door standing open, which it shouldn't have been, and the house being quiet in a way houses aren't, a listening quiet, a held-breath quiet.

I remember the floor was warm.

That's the detail I return to. Not the sound — there was a sound, a sound like a throat clearing below the world, and I have never found language for it and I have had fifty years to try. Not the falling, which took longer than falling should and felt less like falling than like being *poured*. The warmth. The floorboards under my sneakers were warm as skin, warm as the inside of a cheek, and some animal part of me understood before my mind did, and my mind caught up one syllable at a time: *the house is hungry, the house has always been hungry, the house was hungry before it was a house.*

And then there was a long dark that was not sleep, and then there was a yellow raincoat, and a name whispered into me by something vast and patient that needed a messenger the way a king needs a herald, and the name was Wren, and the boy named Samuel Pell was folded up small inside it like a letter no one would ever mail.

I have been the house's mouth ever since. I carried messages to Marion Lockridge for fifty years. *Feed me. Feed me. The debt comes due. Feed me.* And once — only once, in 1994, when a small dark-haired girl sat on this kitchen floor drawing tunnels — I slipped my leash. Once, for one summer, I was not the house's mouth. I was a girl's friend. She called me imaginary and I let her, because *imaginary* was the kindest word anyone had used for me since 1974, and because a child's imagination is the one country the house cannot annex.

Then she grew up and left, and I went back to being a mouth.

Until the funeral. Until the porch. Until she came walking up the drive at dusk with her grandmother's jaw set in her face and her whole life packed into archival boxes, and I heard myself say *you came back to finish it,* and I did not know, saying it, whether I spoke for the house or for the boy.

I still don't. That's the truth of it. That's the terrible hinge of everything: I don't know whose favor I'm asking tonight.

* * *

"Sit down," I say. "Please. I want to show you something, and standing up isn't right for it."

She sits. She sits at the kitchen table, the one with the burn ring from a pan Marion ruined in 1981, and she folds her hands the way her grandmother used to fold hers in church, and I stand in the middle of the kitchen dripping rain that isn't falling anywhere else, and I try to find the beginning.

"I was real," I say.

Her breath does something small and complicated.

"Not imaginary. Not a game you played. I lived three houses down. My aunt was Ruth Pell — Ruth, the neighbor, the one who won't look at this house. I cut across your grandmother's field on the fourth of October, 1974, and the floor was warm, and—" My voice does something I don't authorize. It cracks. Fifty years of carrying other voices, and mine cracks like a twelve-year-old's. "And the house took me. First. Before Thomas. Before anyone. I was the first payment on a bargain I never heard spoken. Marion traded a life to save your mother's, and the house reached out and took the nearest life, and the nearest life was mine."

The refrigerator hums. The house is listening. Of course the house is listening; the house listens through every nail and joist, and I can feel its attention pressing against the inside of my skull like a thumb against an egg.

"And the house made you Wren," Elodie says quietly. "Made you its—"

"Mouth. Messenger. Errand boy." I smile, because smiling is what the coat makes me do when the truth gets close, and I hate it, I hate that my grief arrives wearing this grin, I hate that she will never once see me grieve with a straight face. "It needed hands it could send upstairs. It needed a voice that sounded like a child, because children trust children, and it needed someone to teach little girls to draw maps of its stomach. So it dressed me in the coat I drowned in and it named me after the bird that nests in walls, and it sent me up to befriend the granddaughter of the woman who owed it."

"Samuel," she says.

My name. In her mouth. I have not heard it in fifty years and it goes through me like a splinter going in — pain first, then the strange relief of something finally where it belongs.

"Say it again," I say, and hate how much I sound like begging, and say it anyway.

"Samuel."

* * *

What happens next is the favor, and I have to be careful, because the house is listening, and because the favor is a trap, and I know it's a trap, and I'm springing it anyway, and the cruelest part — crueler than the coat, crueler than the grin — is that I don't fully know whether I'm trapping her or trapping myself.

"I need you to document me," I say.

She blinks. Of all the sentences I might have said, that one plainly wasn't in her ledger.

"Photograph me. Record my voice. Write down what I tell you — my name, my dates, my aunt's garden hose, the nickel, the apple I never finished. Everything. Put me in your archive with your storm drains and your forgotten tunnels. File me somewhere permanent."

"Wren—"

"*Samuel.* Samuel Pell, born March ninth, 1962, taken October fourth, 1974. Son of Alma Pell. Nephew of Ruth. I liked apples and I could say the alphabet backwards and I wound Mrs. Pell's hose into coils because I liked how the circles fit inside each other." The words come fast now, fifty years fast, a dam going. "Because here is the thing nobody tells you about being eaten, Elodie: the house keeps your body but it eats your *witness*. Nobody remembers Samuel. Ruth's sister Alma stopped setting a place at the table within a year — not because she forgot, but because the house smoothed him out of her, wore the memory down like water wears stone, until one day she set three plates instead of four and couldn't say why it felt like a confession. The town forgot. The records forgot. There's no grave. There's no photograph — the house got into the album and blurred me like a face in a police report. For fifty years I have existed only as a mouth, and a mouth is nothing, a mouth is just a hole that talks."

I stop. The raincoat drips. Somewhere above us a door eases itself shut, gently, the way the house does when it wants you to know it's paying attention.

"When you draw a map," I say, softer, "the thing mapped stays put. You taught me that without knowing it, back in '94, when you drew the tunnels and for one whole summer the walls behaved. Witness is the only thing that makes me real. Your camera, your notebooks, your voice saying my name into a microphone — that's the only resurrection on offer in this house, and I am asking you for it. Document me. Please. Before the house takes you too, and there's no one left who ever knew I was a boy."

* * *

She is quiet for a long time. The kind of quiet I've learned to fear, because the house fills silences, seeps into them, starts rearranging the furniture of your thoughts while you're not looking. But this silence is hers. I can tell because it's warm.

"You understand what you're asking," she says finally. "If I put you in the archive, the archive becomes — it's already a leash, Wren. I've seen it. The house follows the documentation. Whatever I record, it can reach."

"I know."

"If I write your name down, I'm writing a hook into you. Anything witnessed, it owns. That's the rule we've both been dancing around for weeks."

"I know." And I do know. Better than she does. That's the trap, you see — hers and mine both. To witness me is to bind me to the house's attention forever. To refuse is to leave me nothing but the hunger and the grin and the dripping coat until the end of time. Either way the house wins something. I am asking her to choose which something, and I am asking because the alternative to being bound is being erased, and I have been half-erased for fifty years and I cannot bear the other half.

But there is more in it than that, and this is the part I can barely look at, the part I hold up to the light and turn like the nickel Ruth once gave me: if she documents me, I get to exist. Really exist. In ink, in silver halide, in the magnetic ghost of tape. And if I exist — if somewhere outside these walls there is proof that Samuel Pell was a boy who liked apples — then maybe, someday, someone finds the page. Maybe someone reads it aloud. Maybe being witnessed by strangers is a door, and doors go both ways, and the house has never once considered that the mouth it made might learn to speak a word the house didn't put there.

Or maybe I'm just a lonely dead thing asking a living woman to look at me, and dressing the loneliness up in strategy because the coat won't let me cry.

Both things are true. That's the worst of it. Both things are true at once, and I cannot separate the tenderness from the trap any more than I can separate the raincoat from the boy.

"All right," Elodie says.

I look up. The grin is on my face but for once it doesn't matter, because my eyes are doing their own work, and fifty years of unwept tears have to go somewhere, and apparently they go nowhere, because the house doesn't let me, and so they just sit behind my eyes like rain behind cloud.

"All right," she says again, steadier, pulling a cassette recorder from her bag, the good one, the Sony with the foam windscreen bitten by a cap in some Detroit tunnel years ago. She sets it on the table between the burn ring and the salt. She presses record. The little wheels turn. "State your name for the record."

"Samuel Pell." My voice comes out steady. Steadier than it has any right to. "Born March ninth, 1962. Taken October fourth, 1974. Friend of Elodie Lockridge, who drew me maps when I was a monster, and is drawing them still."

She turns the camera toward me and the flash pops, and for one white instant I am fixed, pinned, *kept* — a boy in a yellow coat in a kitchen in Hollow Creek, witnessed, real, archived against forgetting.

And overhead, faint as a settling beam, I hear the house shift its weight, and begin, very softly, to plan.

It heard the name. It heard the tape turning. And tomorrow morning her boots will be cleaned and her bed turned down and breakfast waiting warm, because the house has decided that the way to keep a cartographer is to make her feel at home — and I am the one who taught it, long ago, that the surest trap ever built is a kindness.
