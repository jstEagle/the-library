## 20. What's Buried Under the Hearth

The hearthstones had been laid by someone who never wanted them lifted.

I understood this before I understood anything else about them — before I knelt, before I ran my fingers along their seams, before I went out to the truck for the pry bar and the mason's chisel I'd bought in Detroit a decade ago and never once used. The stones told me in the way stone tells things: through weight, through fit, through the particular silence of a thing that has been set down with intention. Each slab was broad as a coffin lid, flush-edged, mortared with a line so fine it might have been drawn rather than poured. No gaps. No purchase. Whoever built this hearth had built it the way you build a lid.

And yet Ruth had said *the summer it changed*, and Marion's first cassette had said *the hearth*, and Wren — standing in the pantry doorway that morning, hands folded over its yellow raincoat like a child at a funeral — had said nothing at all, which was its own kind of confirmation.

"You don't have to do this," Wren said now from the kitchen doorway. Its voice was still that impossible thing, a child's voice with too many winters folded into it. "You could sell. You could drive away today and be sad about it for a while and then be okay."

"Could I?" I didn't look up. I was on my knees at the edge of the firebox, working a putty knife into the seam between the first stone and the surrounding brickwork. The blade slid in half an inch and stopped, as if the mortar had teeth.

"No," Wren admitted. "But I'm supposed to say it. It likes when someone says it out loud. It counts the times nobody tries to stop you." A pause. "There haven't been many."

*It.* Not *we*. I filed that away. Whatever Wren was, whatever it had been made into, it was drawing a line between itself and the house, and lines drawn by frightened children are the truest maps we get.

The morning was cold enough that my breath showed indoors. The furnace had been dead since before I arrived; the house kept itself warm in ways I'd stopped trying to explain, warm in the rooms where I slept and cold everywhere else, warm like a held breath. But the hearth exhaled cold. Kneeling there, I could feel it rising between the stones the way cold rises off a lake at dusk — not draft, exactly. Exhalation. Something below breathing out through the seams of its own floor.

I worked the putty knife deeper. Grit rained onto my wrist. Somewhere above me, in the walls, the house made a sound I had learned to read as attention: a settling, a creak, the long wooden groan of a structure shifting its weight the way a large animal shifts when something small moves near its flank.

"I know," I murmured, to the house, to no one. "I know you're watching."

The mortar gave all at once, with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the first stone rocked loose under my hands.

* * *

It took me four hours to lift three stones.

Not because they were heavy — though they were, each one requiring the pry bar and a fulcrum of scrap two-by-four and my whole bodyweight leaning into the handle until my shoulders screamed — but because they were heavy *deliberately*. Underneath the top slabs were more slabs. Then flagstone. Then a layer of what I first took to be rubble and recognized, brushing the dust away, as broken crockery: the shattered remains of dishes, dozens of pieces, blue-and-white willow pattern gone brown with age, pressed flat into the subfloor like a mosaic. Someone had buried a household's worth of plates here. Someone had wanted the breaking to be permanent.

Wren watched from the doorway the whole time. It didn't help. It didn't leave, either. When I paused to drink water, it spoke without being asked, in the tone of a child reciting something learned by rote.

"The original owners were called Aldous. Before that it was just land. He built the house in 1911 for his wife, Constance, and their daughter." A pause, long enough that I thought it had finished. "The daughter liked to dig."

My hands went still on the pry bar.

"Dug holes all over the yard," Wren continued, watching me with those wide-set eyes that never blinked quite right. "Dug under the porch. Dug until her father filled every hole and scolded her and she cried and dug again. She wasn't looking for anything. She just liked the going-down." Another pause. "That's what he couldn't forgive, afterward. That he'd taught her the ground was safe."

"What was her name?" I asked, though some part of me already knew I shouldn't.

Wren smiled. The smile was the same one it had worn on the porch the day I arrived, the same too-wide arrangement I had loved when I was seven and feared now, and I realized I had never once seen it use that smile for anything except covering something else.

"That's the thing," it said softly. "Nobody wrote it down."

* * *

By late afternoon the light through the kitchen windows had gone the color of weak tea, and I had cleared a space roughly three feet square down to the packed earth beneath the hearth — except it wasn't packed earth. Earth packs unevenly. Earth has roots in it, pebbles, the casual disorder of ground left alone. This was smooth. This was tamped and leveled and finished, worked with care, the way you finish a bed you expect someone to lie in for a long time.

And it was stained.

Not blood-dark, not the dramatic rust I might have imagined. It was a soft discoloration, a shadow soaked deep into the soil, roughly the size and shape of a body curled on its side. Small. The size of a child of six or eight, knees drawn up, the way children sleep when they are trying to take up as little room as possible.

I knelt at the edge of it for a long time. The cold came up through my jeans, through my knees, into the bones of my legs, and I let it, because moving felt like a kind of consent and staying felt like a kind of prayer, and documentation is a form of prayer but so is hesitation. My camera sat in its bag three feet away. I did not reach for it. Some part of me — the part that had spent twenty years turning other people's forgotten places into records, the part that believed a thing seen clearly is a thing half-saved — understood that whatever was under these stones had been seen deliberately, ritually, and covered up on purpose, and that my lens was not the instrument this moment required.

"Elodie." Wren's voice, very quiet. "It's going to want you to hurry."

"Hurry?"

"When you find it. It gets excited. It's been waiting a long time for someone to dig, and it doesn't know the difference between excitement and hunger anymore. They got mixed up in it a long time ago." Wren's hands twisted in the fabric of the raincoat. "So if your hands start shaking, that's not only you."

I looked at my hands. They were steady. They were steady in the way that a struck bell is steady in the instant after the strike, holding its shape around a violence that hasn't finished happening.

I picked up the hand trowel instead of the camera, and began to dig.

* * *

The soil came away in layers, and the layers were wrong.

Topsoil, fine. Beneath it, ash — a stratum of gray-white ash perhaps an inch thick, uniform, deliberate, the residue of a fire burned hot and complete and then raked level. Beneath the ash, a layer of dried flowers. I recognized them with a lurch: yarrow, rue, rosemary. Funeral herbs. Old-world herbs, the kind you'd lay on a grave if you were the kind of family that remembered why graves got laid with them. They crumbled at my touch into a scent that was half garden, half incense, and underneath the scent something sweeter and worse, the smell of a room where milk has soured inside the walls.

Beneath the flowers, the earth turned dark again, and rich, and I stopped needing the trowel, because the earth here was loose. Turned earth. Dug earth. Earth that had been opened and closed by human hands, and opened recently — recently in the way geology means nothing and grief means everything — and I understood that I was not excavating a burial. I was excavating a reburial. This spot had been dug and filled more than once. The house had tended this grave the way you tend a wound you can't stop touching.

My hands found cloth first. Wool, felted nearly to felt by decades of damp, but intact — a hem, a collar, the ghost of pinstripes. A dress. A child's dress, small enough to fold into a shoebox, and I lifted it with both hands the way you lift a bird with a broken wing, and beneath it the soil was pale, and the pale soil had a shape.

I am a surveyor. I have mapped storm tunnels full of things I won't describe to my colleagues, and I have never once vomited at a site, and I am telling you this so you understand what it cost me to keep my hands moving when the shape resolved. Because it was small. Because it was curled on its side, knees drawn up, taking up as little room as possible, exactly as the stain had promised. Because the skull was delicate as a teacup and the eye sockets held fifty years of dark like two cups of it, brimful, unspilled. And because the hands —

The hands were folded.

Folded at the breastbone, one atop the other, in the posture of the composed dead, the prepared dead, the dead someone loved enough to arrange. And clasped between those small fingers, gripped the way a child grips a ticket she's afraid of dropping, was a rectangle of stiff paper, wax-sealed along one folded edge, the wax stamped with a crest I didn't recognize and would spend weeks failing to research because no record of it would survive anywhere but here.

A deed.

I knew it was a deed the way you know a face across a crowded station. The dimensions of legal paper. The formal crease. Even before I eased it free — even before the fingers, dry as twigs, released it with a whisper of grit that sounded horrifyingly like cooperation — I knew I was holding the founding document of this place. Land grant or title or covenant, ink faded to the brown of old tea, and I did not read it then. I want that recorded. I did not read it kneeling over a child's grave with my heart hammering like something trying to get out of a locked room. I folded it into my coat, against my chest, and I sat back on my heels in the cold breath of the open hearth, and I looked at what I had uncovered, and the house looked back.

Every surface in the kitchen was warm now. Every surface. The windows fogged gently from the inside. The floorboards under my knees had the soft give of a living thing's flank. And through the walls, through the floors, up from the throat of the tunnels I had mapped and would map again, came a sound I had heard once before, in the dark of Tunnel One, and had told myself was water:

A long, shuddering inhalation. Drawn out. Savoring.

The house was smelling what I had uncovered. The house was smelling its own beginning.

"It's happy," Wren whispered from the doorway. It had come closer without my noticing; it stood at the edge of the hearth now, yellow coat bright against the gloom, and its face wore an expression I had never seen on it before and hope never to see again: reverence. "It's been so hungry for so long that it forgot this feeling. Being full isn't eating. Being full is remembering why you started." It crouched slowly, keeping its distance from the small pale shape, and its eyes moved over the folded hands, the woolen dress, with something that in any other face I would have called tenderness. "She was first," Wren said. "Before the bargain, before Thomas, before me. Everything after is just interest on her."

"Wren." My voice came out sanded down to almost nothing. "What happened to her?"

"She went down." Simple. Final. The way children state unbearable things. "Down where the ground was soft. Down where she liked to go. And the ground —" Wren's head tilted, listening to something beneath us, something vast and slow turning over in its sleep of appetite. "The ground had gotten a taste for company. Her father dug for nine days. He found the dress. He found the deed she'd taken from his desk, because she'd seen him press his seal into it and she wanted to hold something important while she went down into the dark, the way children carry a blanket." Wren looked up at me. "He never found her. So he built the hearth over the place where the digging stopped, and he laid the stones like a lid, and he fed the fire every night for the rest of his life, and the house learned from him. It learned that love is a thing you bury and keep feeding."

The words went into me like cold water into a crack. I thought of the ledger upstairs, decades of entries, dates and names and offerings. I thought of Marion paying interest for fifty years. I thought of the chamber wallpapered with portraits, the preserved belongings, Thomas's watch ticking somewhere in the dark for a woman who never came back for it. All of it — the whole architecture of appetite, the bargains and feedings and the patient digestion of everyone this family had ever loved — all of it grown from this: a father's grief, poured like mortar, set like a lid.

The house hadn't been born hungry. It had been taught.

And it had been a good student.

I reached for my camera at last. My hands were shaking now, exactly as Wren had promised, shaking with something that was not entirely mine, and I framed the small grave in the viewfinder — the folded hands, empty now, the woolen dress, the dark stain of a body-shaped absence in the earth — and I photographed it the way I photograph everything, which is to say the way I pray, which is to say: *let this be witnessed, let this count, let this have happened to someone.*

The shutter clicked.

The house flinched. I felt it through the floor — a contraction, a ripple running outward from the hearth through the foundations, the whole structure tightening around its secret like a fist closing. The warmth vanished. The windows cleared. In the sudden cold, my breath hung in the air like something summoned.

And far below — far below the grave, below the ash and the herbs and the turned earth, down past the depth of any foundation, down where my childhood maps ended in pencil marks that trailed off into nothing because even at eleven I had known better than to draw what lived down there — something answered the flash of witness with a sound.

Not the voices of the taken. Not the pleading chorus of the throat. This was older. This was single. A child's voice, thin as wire, coming up through fifty years of packed dark, and it was not screaming, and it was not weeping.

It was humming. A lullaby, half-remembered, the melody dissolving every few bars into silence and beginning again, patient as erosion, the song of something that had been singing to itself in the dark for so long it had forgotten there was ever anyone meant to hear it.

Wren stood up very fast. I had never seen it move quickly. It backed against the doorframe, and its too-wide smile was gone, and what was underneath the smile was the oldest fear there is, the fear of the thing that came before you.

"She never sang before," it whispered. "In all the years I've been here. She never made a sound."

The humming rose a little. Sweetened. And beneath the melody, almost lost in it, almost nothing, I heard — or I dreamed I heard, and I would spend the rest of this story unable to decide which — a word.

My name.

Said the way you say the name of someone you've been waiting for.
