## 36. Samuel Pell, In Memoriam

The Pell family history was not hard to find. That was the first strange thing about it — how easy it was, after everything else had been so difficult.

I had spent three weeks in the county records office in Ashford, sitting under fluorescent lights that hummed like trapped insects, pulling deed books and census rolls and burial registers. The clerks knew me by then. The older one, a woman named Dot with reading glasses on a beaded chain, brought me coffee without being asked and once said, "You're the fourth person to research that family, you know. But the first one who didn't want to find something."

She was right. I didn't want to find anything. I wanted to give something back, and to do that I needed only enough — names, dates, a paper trail thin as communion wafer but real.

The Pells had been in Hollow Creek since before it was Hollow Creek. They'd come up from the Carolinas in 1849, farmers and stonemasons, and their name appeared in the cemetery register like a heartbeat: Pell, Pell, Pell, generation after generation, plot after plot along the eastern fence where the ground drained well and the morning sun came first. Ruth's parents. Ruth's grandparents. And there, in the row of Ruth's own generation, a gap.

Samuel Pell. Born March 3rd, 1966. No death date. In the register, beside his name, someone had written *lost* and then, apparently unable to bear even that much, had pressed so hard with the pen that the word tore through the page.

*Lost.* Not dead. Not buried. Lost, like a glove. Like a key. Like something that might still turn up if you looked in the right coat pocket.

I sat with that torn page for a long time. Around me the records office went about its business — a man arguing about a property line, a young couple applying for a marriage license, laughing at something on a phone. Life, conducting itself loudly and carelessly over the top of a boy who had been lost for fifty years because a house across town had been hungry, and a woman desperate enough to feed it.

I thought about what Wren had begged me for, in the last hour of the house's life. *Write it down. Write my name down before it takes me, so that someone, somewhere, will know he existed.*

I had written it down. And then the fire had taken the writing, and the forgetting had taken everything else, and Samuel Pell had gone back to being a torn word in a county register — except that now there was no one left who remembered why the word hurt.

Except me. I was the only witness left, and I was a broken one: I remembered a yellow raincoat and a too-wide smile, but when I tried to remember the boy underneath them, my memory slid off like rain off oilcloth. I knew his name the way you know a word in a language you used to speak. I could spell it. I could not feel it.

That was the house's last cruelty still working itself out in me, I think. It had starved itself of witness, and the price was that no one would remember — but I had stood at the hearth when it happened. I had paid the price from inside the transaction. So I kept these splinters: the name, the dates, the shape of what was owed. Not memory exactly. Debt. Debt survives forgetting. Maybe debt is the only thing that does.

* * *

Ruth Pell lived in a narrow white house on Corbett Street with a ramped porch and a garden gone pleasantly wild. I had knocked on her door twice since the fire — once to return her sister's locket, which I'd carried out of the second chamber wrapped in a handkerchief like a relic, and once just to sit with her while she told me, again, the story of 1974, because telling it seemed to ease something in her even though she no longer understood why she needed to tell it.

She didn't know me. That was the arrangement I had accepted without ever agreeing to it. She knew there was a woman who came by, a nice woman, a surveyor or engineer of some kind, quiet, good listener. She called me "hon" and gave me lemon drops from a cut-glass dish and told me the story of Marion Lockridge and the sick daughter and the bargain at the hearth as though it were local folklore, something she'd heard from her own grandmother, something that had happened to other people a long time ago.

"Thomas Lockridge," she'd say, shaking her head. "Vanished clean off the face of the earth, and Marion never shed a tear in public. Folks said that was suspicious. I always thought—" and here she would pause, frown, press her fingers to her temple like a woman reaching for a word on the tip of her tongue, "—I always thought somebody ought to have asked her harder questions. Somebody ought to have *helped* her."

"You did help her," I said once. "As much as anyone could."

"Did I?" She looked genuinely surprised, and then pleased, and then confused again, and I poured us both more tea and let the moment pass.

Her sister's locket sat on her mantel now. She didn't know where it had come from. She'd told me she found it in a drawer and couldn't imagine how it got there, since she'd thought it lost fifty years ago, and she'd cried holding it and couldn't say why, and finally she'd put it out where she could see it every day. "Some things," she told me, "you don't need the whole story of. You just need to know they came home."

I held onto that. Some things you don't need the whole story of.

* * *

The stonecutter worked out of a shop on Route 9, a low cinderblock building with samples leaning against the fence like headstones waiting patiently for their people. His name was Albrecht. He was sixty-ish, forearms like bridge cable, and he had cut stones for that county for forty years.

"What are we doing?" he asked, when I laid out the order. Granite, modest size, standard cemetery regulations. He read the inscription draft I'd written on a sheet of legal pad paper and his eyebrows climbed.

"SAMUEL D. PELL," it read. "1966 – 1974. LOST TO US, NOT FORGOTTEN. BELOVED SON OF MARGARET AND DALE PELL. NEPHEW OF RUTH."

"'Lost,'" he repeated. "You don't see that on stones. Usually it's 'died' or 'passed' or some poem about gates and gardens." He tapped the paper. "And no cause. No 'drowned,' no 'killed.' Just lost."

"That's accurate," I said.

He studied me over the counter with the frank appraisal of a man who deals in grief professionally and has stopped being fooled by its costumes. "Family order?"

"The family doesn't know yet."

He set the paper down. "Ma'am, I'm going to ask this straight because it saves time. Is this a scam? Because I've had people try to sell plots back to grieving families, and I've had folks order stones for grudges, putting lies in granite. If this is some kind of—"

"It's restitution," I said.

The word came out of me with more force than I intended, and it did something to the air between us. Albrecht looked at me a long moment. Whatever he saw made him nod slowly, the way men nod when they've decided not to ask a second question.

"Restitution," he said. "All right. I can cut that." He turned the paper back around and picked up his pencil. "You want the dates right? Born '66. When'd you want for the second date?"

"1974," I said. "August. The fourteenth."

"How do you know the exact day, if he was lost?"

Because I had heard his voice in the throat of a house that no longer exists, I almost said. Because a thing wearing his face and a yellow raincoat told me everything it remembered in the last minutes it had, and August 14th was the day the hunger started, and the hunger started with him.

"I did my research," I said.

* * *

It took six weeks for the stone. I drove up from Detroit twice in that span — once to pay the balance, once to visit the cemetery and stand at the empty spot along the eastern fence where the stone would go, between Margaret and Dale Pell and a weathered angel marking some Pell infant from the 1890s whose name the lichen had eaten.

The cemetery sits on a rise above the creek, and from it you can see, if you know where to look, the roofline of my grandmother's house — sold now, renovated, its windows new and double-paned, its porch repainted a cheerful gray. From here it looked like any house. From here it had probably always looked like any house. That was the horror of it, I was learning: the monstrous is almost never visible from a distance. You have to go inside. You have to be invited.

I brought a folding chair the second time and sat by the empty grave plot for an afternoon, doing nothing but being present, which is its own kind of documentation — the kind that leaves no record. A red-tailed hawk worked the treeline. Somewhere below, the creek made its small argument with the rocks. I tried to remember Wren — the real Wren, the friend, the thing that had held the hearthstones open for me knowing it was choosing its own ending — and what came instead was a boy's voice saying *thank you* in a tone I couldn't hold onto, like water through fingers.

So I spoke aloud, to the grass and the hawk and nobody. "You're getting a stone," I said. "Your aunt is going to cry at it, and she won't know why, and that's the best I can do. I'm sorry it isn't more. I'm sorry it isn't remembering. But a stone is a kind of memory that doesn't need a mind to hold it. People will read your name for a hundred years. Strangers. Kids cutting through the cemetery. Someone will rub the letters with their thumb the way people do, and for half a second you'll exist in a stranger's attention, and that's more than the house ever let you have."

The hawk screamed once, far off, and I chose to take it for acknowledgment.

* * *

The stone was set on a Tuesday in late October. Albrecht's crew placed it in the morning; I arrived at noon with a level and checked their work, which was perfect, and then spent an hour cleaning mortar smears off the base with a toothbrush and a bucket of water, kneeling on the cold grass like the gravedigger I was, in my way.

The granite was gray-black, shot through with faint mica that caught the light when you moved. The letters were deep-cut, meant to hold paint and weather and time:

**SAMUEL D. PELL**
**1966 – 1974**
***LOST TO US, NOT FORGOTTEN***

Below, smaller: *Beloved son. Beloved nephew. This stone stands where love ran out of places to put itself.*

Albrecht had questioned that last line too. "Bit unusual," he'd said. "Most families want scripture."

"He wasn't most families," I said, and Albrecht, who had decided weeks ago not to ask second questions, wrote it down.

I planted bulbs around the base — daffodils, because they come up every year whether anyone watches or not, which felt like the right theology. I left a small jar of river stones at the foot of the stone, the way children do, because somewhere under fifty years of yellow raincoat there had been a boy who collected pretty rocks, and Wren had told me that too, in the end, and it was one of the few things about him that had survived the forgetting intact. Some facts are too simple to erase.

Then I went to Corbett Street, because I had decided — after long argument with myself — that Ruth should be there when she found out. Not told. Found. Telling her would mean explaining, and explaining was impossible, and some things you don't need the whole story of.

I knocked. She answered in a cardigan the color of persimmons, delighted, flustered, insisting I come in, had I eaten, there's soup. I let her fuss. Then I said, "Ruth, I need to show you something. It's a short drive. Will you come?"

She came. Of course she came; she was the sort of person who came, which was the whole tragedy of her — the one who noticed, the one who would have helped, the one the town's silence had used as its instrument without her ever knowing.

We drove with the windows cracked despite the cold. She talked about her garden going dormant, about the locket on her mantel, about a dream she'd had — "a funny one, hon, I dreamed about a little boy in a yellow slicker, standing on a porch, and I woke up crying and I don't know whose boy he was, but I loved him, isn't that strange? Isn't that the strangest thing? Loving somebody in a dream and then missing them all day?"

"That's not strange," I said, and my voice did something I didn't authorize, cracked down the middle, and I covered it with a cough and turned onto Cemetery Road.

* * *

The cemetery in late October was all copper and rust, the maples letting go in slow spirals, the light coming in low and gold and forgiving. I parked at the gate and walked her in, my hand under her elbow on the uneven path, past the Pells' generations, past the angel with the eaten name, to the eastern fence.

To the stone.

Ruth stopped walking about ten feet away. I felt it through my arm — the small arrest of her body, the way her breath changed gear.

"Oh," she said. Nothing else. Just *oh*, in a voice I had last heard from myself, alone, in a motel room, listening to a recording of my own voice that wasn't mine.

She approached the way you approach a sleeping animal. She knelt — seventy-eight years old and she knelt on frozen ground without hesitation, the way you kneel at a crib — and she put both hands flat on the granite, on her nephew's name, and she began to weep.

Not politely. Not the tidy mourning of a funeral. This was the weeping of a body that has been carrying something for fifty years and has finally, without warning, been allowed to set it down. It came out of her in waves, her shoulders heaving, her forehead eventually resting against the cold stone, her hands moving on the carved letters the way you'd touch a child's hair.

I knelt beside her. I didn't touch her. Some griefs you have to let stand alone or they close up like a fist.

"Why am I—" she managed, lifting her head, her face wrecked and shining. "Hon, why am I crying like this? I don't— Sam. Sammy. Why do I know that name? Who told me that name?" Her thumb traced the *S*, the *A*, the *M*. "I had a dream about him. Yellow slicker. Standing on a porch. I woke up crying and I've been crying inside all week and I thought it was just age, they tell you it's just age—"

"His mother was your sister," I said gently. "Margaret."

"Margaret." The name hit her like a wave hitting a piling. "Margaret. Maggie. God, Maggie. We were— we were close, we were *close*, why did we stop— why did we stop talking, what happened, what happened in—" and then she stopped, because the stone answered her. The dates answered her. 1966. 1974. *Lost.*

"August," she whispered. "August of '74. Something happened in August of '74 and nobody would say what and Maggie stopped speaking to half the town and Dale took to drinking and I—" She looked at me, and her eyes were fierce through the tears, the eyes of the woman who had warned me to sell and leave, the woman whose dog wouldn't cross the property line, the woman who had known in her bones for fifty years that something was wrong and been talked out of it by everyone she trusted. "Did you do this? Did you put this stone here?"

"Yes."

"Who are you?" she asked, and it wasn't suspicion. It was wonder. It was the question of a woman standing in front of a miracle she couldn't file anywhere. "Why does my heart know you? I look at you and I want to feed you soup and tell you things I've never told anybody. Who *are* you?"

"I'm someone who owes your family," I said. "That's all you need to know. Some things you don't need the whole story of."

She stared at me. And then, horribly and wonderfully, she laughed — a wet, broken laugh — and said, "That's what Maggie used to say. Word for word. *Some things you don't need the whole story of.* She said it the last time I ever saw her, on her porch, and she was looking at me like I was the one who needed protecting." Ruth shook her head, pressing her palm flat against the stone one more time. "Sammy," she said to the granite. "Sammy, I'm sorry. I don't know what I'm sorry for, but I've been sorry for fifty years and now I know who I'm sorry *to*."

The wind came through the maples then, all at once, a long exhale, and the last of the copper leaves came down around us like something released. On the eastern fence, a strand of wire hummed a single low note and went quiet.

Ruth stayed until the cold drove her out — nearly two hours, talking to the stone, telling it about the farm, about Maggie's terrible pies, about a summer when the boys built a dam in the creek and Sammy got soaked to the ribs and lied about it with a gap-toothed grin. Fragments surfacing. Fifty years of silt shifting, letting a few bright things rise. She couldn't hold any of it together — the timeline kept dissolving, faces detaching from names — but the fragments themselves were whole, and true, and hers.

Before we left she picked up one of the river stones from my jar, turned it over in her fingers, and put it in her cardigan pocket. "For my mantel," she said. "Next to the locket." Then, puzzled at herself, fond of me beyond reason: "Come Sunday. There'll be soup. There's always soup."

* * *

I drove back toward Detroit that evening with the windows down and the cold pouring in, because I wanted to feel it, all of it, the full price and the full purchase. Behind me the stone stood in the darkening cemetery with a boy's name cut into it, and strangers would read that name tomorrow, and next year, and in fifty years, and none of them would know about the house or the hearth or the throat or the yellow raincoat, and that was the point. That was the entire point. Witness didn't have to be complete to be real. A name on granite is a candle in a window: it doesn't explain the dark. It just refuses to agree with it.

At the bottom of Cemetery Road I stopped at the mailbox by the church and checked my phone out of habit, and there was an email from the historical society — automated, some mailing list I'd joined during the research. *Upcoming: Our Annual Walking Tour of Historic Hollow Creek Homes,* it said. *This year featuring the recently restored Lockridge House, c. 1902.*

I stared at it for a long time in the blue dusk.

Restored. Featured. Open to the public. Strangers walking those rooms with pamphlets in their hands, admiring the original millwork, the generous hearth.

The hearth.

I sat in the idling car while the light died, and I thought about a house that had learned, above all things, how to wait — and about porch lights that turn themselves on at dusk, and about appetite, and about how the surest way to feed something is to make people come and look at it.

The tour was scheduled for the first Saturday of December.

I had three weeks.
