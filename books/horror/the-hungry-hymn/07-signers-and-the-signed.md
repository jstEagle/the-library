## 7. Signers and the Signed

The summons came by way of a child who did not exist.

Odile found her standing at the undertaker's door at first light, a girl of perhaps nine in a gray wool coat buttoned to the throat, her breath steaming in the cold. She had no name Odile could recall, though the girl spoke as if they were old acquaintances. *Mother Casque asks for the coffin-maker and the brother. Before the bell rings. Before the dusk.* Then she turned and walked up the crooked lane toward the church hill, and Odile stood in the doorway a long while, trying to remember where the girl lived, which house, which family, and finding nothing there. Not a blank, exactly. A smooth place, like a patch of ice over deep water. Her mind slid off it every time she reached.

She had lived in Vesper's Hollow forty-one years. She had buried half the village with her own hands. She knew every name, every bloodline, every grudge and kindness passed down like furniture. And she could not tell this child's face from the faces of a hundred children she had never seen, because it was the same face, or no face, or a face the land had loaned out the way it loaned everything — at interest, and never returned whole.

She went to find Isandro.

* * *

He was in the kitchen of the old house, and he did not look well. He had not looked well since the ledger, since the papers with their waiting line and their patient, patient blank. He sat at the table with Anselm's burned journal open before him and his hands flat on either side of it, as if the book might bolt.

"The Casque woman," Odile said. "She wants us. This morning."

Isandro looked up. In the gray kitchen light his eyes were the color of water left standing too long. "I dreamed about her," he said. "I dreamed she was counting us. Like coins."

"She's the last signer. Whatever's wrong with this town, it runs through her."

"Then let's run through her back." He closed the journal. His hands were steady. Odile had learned to distrust that steadiness; grief that didn't shake was grief being held somewhere, and held things always got out.

They walked up through the village together. The morning was bright and wrong — the light came down too clean, too evenly, as if the sun were a lamp someone had hung there and forgotten to dim. The streets were empty. Not quiet: empty. Doors stood open on cold hearths. A kettle sat on a step, still steaming. Somewhere a radio played to a kitchen no one was in, a hymn, the same four bars over and over, and Odile realized with a lurch that it was not a recording skipping. It was the singer. Whoever was in that house — or had been — could not get past the fourth bar, because the fifth bar was gone from them, and the hymn went on and on in a voice growing hoarse, trying to find the rest of a song it had once known whole.

Isandro stopped walking. "Odile."

"I hear it."

"Is that what it's like? When they take someone?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. They both heard the hoarse voice circling the same four bars, patient as water, and they walked faster.

* * *

Mother Casque's house sat at the crest of the hill above the church, the oldest house in the Hollow, timber black with a century of weather, its windows small and deep-set like the eyes of something patient. The door opened before they knocked.

She was smaller than Odile remembered, and Odile remembered her tall. Once she had filled doorways, a woman with a voice that could carry across a harvest field. Now she seemed folded, compressed, a letter written large and then folded down and down until only the creases showed. Her eyes were the worst of it. They were not blind and not seeing. They were hollowed, the way a tree is hollowed — still standing, still wearing bark, but the living wood long since gone out of the center.

"Come in," she said. "I've been dying slowly for sixty years, and I'd like to get the arithmetic done before the bell."

The parlor smelled of dust and lamp oil and, beneath it, something older — the same smell that rose from the scorched beam in the church, the smell of the fire in 1911, the smell of a thing burned but not destroyed. On the table in the center of the room lay the ledger, and beside the ledger lay a pen. A plain steel nib in a black holder, worn smooth where a hundred years of hands had gripped it.

"Sit," said Mother Casque. "Or stand. It doesn't matter. Nothing matters except what I'm about to tell you, and you'll wish it didn't, but wishes were never part of the covenant."

Isandro sat. Odile remained standing, and the old woman's hollowed eyes found her with something like approval.

"The undertaker stands. Good. Someone should. Sit down long enough in this town, coffin-maker, and it starts counting you as furniture."

"What is the price?" Isandro said. No preamble. Odile could see he'd been carrying the question since the ledger, since the waiting line, since the blank, and it had worn him smooth in places, the way water wears stone.

Mother Casque folded her hands. They were beautiful hands, Odile noticed. Unlined. A dead woman's hands on a living woman's wrists.

"You've seen the forgetting," the old woman said. "The names going. The recipes. The dead going out of the graves of memory. You've seen it and you've thought: the land is starving, the land is eating, the land takes what it takes, indiscriminate, like famine." She shook her head, a small precise motion. "No. The land takes nothing indiscriminate. The covenant is a contract, and contracts have terms, and I am the last living party to the terms, so hear them."

She leaned forward. The lamp flame bent toward her, Odile would swear it afterward, bent toward her like a listener.

"Every generation, the covenant must be renewed. A signature, freely given, by one of the founders' blood or one the founders' blood has chosen. And the signature is not ink. The signature is a person. One villager, chosen by the signing hand, is given to the land entire. Not killed — nothing so merciful. *Erased.* Out of every memory but the land's. Their name out of the stones. Their face out of the photographs — look at the old pictures sometime, look at the gaps, the spaces where someone stood and the grass grows wrong. Their voice out of the hymns. Their four bars out of the song." Her eyes went, for one flicker, toward the window, toward the village below, toward the hoarse voice that could not find its fifth bar. "The land keeps them. The land remembers them perfectly. The land has all of them, every one, down the centuries, walking its hills in the dark where no one will ever miss them because no one can remember there was anything to miss. And the village lives. The crops come in. The fever passes over. The mine doesn't collapse. The Hollow survives, and survives, and survives, on people no one knows it's eating."

The silence after was very long. Somewhere below, faintly, the hoarse voice found the fourth bar again and began to circle it.

"How many," Odile said. Her voice came out flat, undertaker's voice, the voice she used over open graves.

"Since 1911? Eleven. Before that, more. The ledger's earlier pages are in a hand that isn't ink." Mother Casque smiled, and the smile was the most terrible thing Odile had seen in forty-one years of laying out the dead, because it was warm. It was genuinely warm. "You want to know the worst of it, brother of the dead man? The worst of it is that it works. Sixty years I've signed, and the Hollow has never known a hungry winter. Every child fed. Every fever broken. I have watched this village flourish like a garden, and I have known, every hour of every day, what the watering was." She spread her beautiful dead hands. "And I would do it again. That's the worst of it. That's what I need you to understand before you take the pen. Not that the covenant is monstrous. That it is *reasonable.* It will seem reasonable. It will seem like arithmetic. One, against all. And the one will be someone you can bear to lose, because the land will show you someone you can bear to lose, it is very helpful that way, it has had centuries of practice."

Isandro had gone very still. "You summoned us," he said. "Why?"

"Because I'm dying, and the covenant doesn't lapse. It *transfers.* If no hand signs, the land doesn't starve patiently. The land starves *impersonally.* It will take and take and take until it has the village entire, and it will not be gentle, and it will not be one at a time, and there will be no arithmetic left to do because all the numbers will be gone." She turned her hollowed eyes on Isandro, and Odile saw the temptation arrive in the room like a change in the air pressure, like weather. "You are the founders' blood. The last of it, now. The papers wait for you. And here is what no signer has ever had before, brother: here is what I never had."

She pushed the pen across the table. It moved an inch and stopped, precisely, in front of his hand.

"Choice. I was chosen. My mother signed me over before I had a name, did you know that? I have been the covenant since I was a child in this house, and I have never once been asked." The warmth in her voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture in old porcelain, and Odile understood suddenly that the hollowness in the woman's eyes was not age. It was the shape of a person worn thin by holding a thing alone for sixty years. "You will be asked. Sign, and choose, and the Hollow lives, and you will spend the rest of your life as I have spent mine — fed, honored, safe, and *hollow,* with the arithmetic running in your head every night, one against all, one against all, and the one always someone you've decided not to remember. Or don't sign. And watch what a starving land does when it stops pretending to be a village."

Isandro looked at the pen. Odile watched his face and saw, with an undertaker's eye for the moment the breath leaves a body, the exact moment the temptation entered him. Not greed. Never greed. It was worse than greed. It was *relief.* She saw it in the set of his shoulders, the way a man stands differently when he has been given permission to put down something he has carried his whole life. All those years hiding behind his brother's name, all those years being no one — and here, at last, a thing he could be. A signer. A keeper. A man whose signature kept children fed and fevers broken. A man who mattered, purchased at a price he could tell himself was arithmetic.

"Isandro," Odile said quietly.

He didn't look at her.

"The land will show you someone you can bear to lose," Mother Casque said, soft as a hymn. "It has already begun. Think of who you've already begun not to think of. Think of the empty chair in your grief. Think of who died in that coffin, brother, and who didn't, and which of those two the land would rather have."

The lamp flame stood straight. The hoarse voice below found the fourth bar. And Isandro reached out — not for the pen, Odile saw, not yet, but toward it, the way a drowning man reaches for a rope he knows is tied to an anchor — and Mother Casque smiled her warm, terrible smile, and said:

"Good. Now let me show you the room where the founders sang, and what became of the last man who tried to sing the verse *his* way."

She rose, unfolding like something that had been folded too long, and led them down the hall toward the dark at the back of the house, where the walls, faintly, unmistakably, were already humming.
