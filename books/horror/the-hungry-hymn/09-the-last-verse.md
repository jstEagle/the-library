## 9. The Last Verse

The bell rang at dusk, and Isandro walked toward it.

He had spent his whole life walking away from things—away from Vesper's Hollow, away from Anselm's shadow, away from every mirror that showed him a face he had learned to call someone else's. But tonight the road ran only one direction, and he let it carry him, past the shuttered bakery where Mrs. Aldous had forgotten her own bread, past the well where children's names had been scratched and then smoothed over by hands that no longer remembered why. The village moved around him in a slow, silent procession, drawn like iron filings to a magnet buried under the churchyard.

Odile walked beside him. She had not spoken since Mother Casque's house, since the old woman had pressed the pen into his palm with fingers like dried riverbeds and said, *It was always meant to be you.* The pen was cold in his pocket, heavier than any object had a right to be, as though it were not brass and bone at all but a small dense piece of the land itself.

"You could still run," Odile said finally. Her voice was rough, scraped thin.

"I've been running for forty years." He watched the church rise against the bruised sky, its tower black, its windows lit from within by a light that had no source. "I'd like to see what happens when I stop."

The doors stood open. Of course they did. They had always stood open; he understood that now, understood that every locked door in his childhood house, every sealed room, every silence Anselm kept, had been this door, waiting at the end of all the others.

Inside, the founders' pews were full. Not with the living—or not only with the living. The villagers who had come at the bell's calling sat among shapes that flickered when he looked at them directly: men in nineteenth-century coats, women in shawls gone gray with age, faces worn smooth as river stones, features eroded not by time but by *use*, the way a stair hollows under centuries of feet. The signers. All of them. Every generation that had fed itself to the ground so the ground would let them stay.

At the front, beneath the empty bell tower, stood a figure wearing his brother's body.

It turned when he entered. It had Anselm's height, Anselm's way of holding one shoulder slightly lower, and when it smiled, the smile was Anselm's smile, the one that had always meant *I know something you don't*. But the eyes were wrong—not empty, exactly, but *full*, filled to the brim with something dark and patient, like soil seen through water.

"Brother," it said, and the whole room breathed in.

Isandro's chest seized. That was the cruelty of it, he thought. Not the horror. The *accuracy*. Forty years of longing, and the land had studied him long enough to know exactly which voice would open him like a lock.

"You're not him," Isandro said.

"I'm what remembers him." The thing spread its hands—the wrong hands, the unscarred hands, the hands of the stranger in the coffin. "He came back to finish the hymn. He faltered at the last verse. He loved you too much to spend you." Its head tilted. "I do not love you at all. I find it makes me more honest."

"Then sing your own hymn," Isandro said. "Leave them out of it."

"The hymn requires a name." The figure gestured, and the ledger lay open on the altar, pages breathing faintly, the blank line at the bottom of the covenant pale as an unfed mouth. Beside it, the pen waited in a dish of earth. "And a payment. The village has paid in memory for two hundred years. There is so little left, Isandro. Look at them." He looked. Mrs. Aldous sat with her hands folded, mouthing nothing. The baker's son stared at the ceiling as if it were a sky he'd never seen before. "One more renewal and there will be nothing left to renew *with*. Unless—" the smile widened, gentle, terrible, "—unless someone pays in something fresher. Someone who has been hoarding. Someone who has spent his whole life keeping everything inside."

The humming started low in the walls, in the floor, in the bones of the tower. The Hungry Hymn, rising toward its final verse, the verse that had never been sung, the verse that needed a signature to unlock it. The villagers began to sway. Odile gripped his arm, her nails biting through his sleeve.

"Don't," she whispered. "There has to be another way. We'll burn the ledger. We'll salt the ground. We'll—"

"There isn't another way." He said it calmly, and was surprised by the calm. "There never was. Anselm knew. Mother Casque knew. Everyone who ever signed knew. There's only the price, and who pays it." He drew the pen from his pocket. "They've been paying in other people's memories. In erased neighbors, in forgotten dead. I'm going to pay in mine."

* * *

He walked down the aisle alone. Odile did not follow; whether she was held back or held herself, he never learned. The pews of the dead did not turn to watch him, but he felt their attention the way you feel weather through a wall.

At the altar, the ledger waited. Up close, the ink of the old signatures seemed to move, each name a small dark animal curled in sleep. He read them without meaning to—Casque, Casque, Maren, Aldous, Lockridge—and stopped at the last one. Anselm's hand. His brother had signed after all. Had come home, had opened the book, had written his name—

—and then struck it through. One clean line, dragged hard enough to tear the page. Below it, in the same furious script: *Find another way or find no way.*

So he hadn't faltered. He had refused. And the refusal had killed him, or the land had collected on the debt some other way, and the body in the coffin—the stranger's body, the substitute—suddenly made a horrible new sense. The land took a body when it couldn't take a soul. It had been showing Isandro the receipt all week.

"I loved him," the thing said beside him, reading his stillness. "In my way. I gave him a choice."

"He chose me." Isandro picked up the pen. It warmed in his grip like something waking. "That's the part you'll never understand. Everything in this town runs on being chosen by the land. He chose *me* instead."

He set the nib to the blank line.

The hymn swelled. The walls themselves seemed to lean in. Somewhere above, in the tower, the bell that had been melted down in 1911 gathered itself to ring, and he felt the sound before it came, a pressure behind his eyes, a taste of copper and loam.

*Say the name,* the land said, in every voice it had ever eaten. *Say the name and be finished.*

"My name," Isandro said, and his voice cracked and steadied, "is Isandro Lockridge. Not Anselm. Not the twin. Not the spare. Mine."

He signed.

The moment the ink touched the page, the memories rose in him—not fading, but *rising*, called up like water from a deep well, and he understood that this was the payment gathering itself, that the land was already drinking. So he drank first. He took one long last look at everything.

The summer he was six, hiding under the porch with Anselm while their parents shouted about money, the two of them pressed shoulder to shoulder in the dust, sharing a single stolen plum, and Anselm whispering, *when we're grown we'll live somewhere the ground doesn't want anything.* The night their mother died, when Anselm had held his hand through the funeral because Isandro couldn't stop shaking. The apartment in the city, the years of answering to his brother's name on leases and letters and telephone calls, hiding inside Anselm like a hermit crab, safe and suffocating. The letter that brought him home. The coffin. The wrong hands. Odile's steady voice saying *we'll wait, we'll get it right.*

He had hoarded all of it, every scrap, terrified of losing anything, and now he opened his hands.

"I offer the verse myself," he said, and began to sing.

His voice was poor. It didn't matter. The final verse of the Hungry Hymn had waited two hundred years for a voice, and it took his, bent it, carried it up into the rafters where the dead leaned forward to hear. But he did not sing the words the walls offered him. He sang them changed. Where the hymn demanded *the village remembers*, he sang *I remember*. Where it demanded *the land is fed*, he sang *let the land be fed from me alone*. Where it demanded souls, names, generations, he sang the only currency he truly owned: the porch, the plum, the shaking hand, the borrowed name, the grief, the hiding, the long cowardice and the one brave hour of it.

*I pay in my own childhood. I pay in my own grief. Take the remembering and leave the remembered.*

The hymn fought him. The walls screamed in harmonies. The villagers swayed harder, and the thing wearing Anselm lunged for the ledger—but the signature was already drying, and a signature freely given, freely named, freely *priced*, bound even the binder. The land had rules. It had always had rules. That was the horror and that, tonight, was the mercy.

Above them, the bell swung.

He heard it gather—one great indrawn breath of bronze—and felt the strike begin somewhere in the roots of the tower, traveling up through stone and rope and metal, the first stroke of a peal that should have rung until dawn, sealing the covenant, feeding the ground, erasing and erasing and erasing.

The bell cracked mid-stroke.

The sound it made was not a ring but a *wound*—a single broken note, half bronze and half silence, that fell through the church like an axe through ice. Cracks spidered across the tower walls. Dust rained from beams scorched black a century ago. The pews of the dead shuddered, thinned, and went out like candles, and the villagers sagged in their seats, gasping, weeping, clutching at names rising back into them like swallowed birds returning to the throat.

The thing wearing Anselm's body looked down at its hands. They were coming apart, gently, grain by grain, becoming soil, becoming the ordinary honest dirt of the churchyard.

"You rewrote it," it said, and there was something in its voice that might have been wonder, in another creature. "No one has ever rewritten it."

"He did," Isandro said. "He just ran out of time to finish."

The last of it fell. Where it had stood, a scatter of dark earth on the altar stones, and nothing else.

Isandro's knees buckled. Odile caught him—she was there suddenly, arms around him, real and warm and smelling of lye and lamp oil—and he clung to her the way he had once clung to his brother's name.

"It's done," she was saying, into his hair, over and over. "It's done, it's done."

But he was looking at his own hands, turning them over, searching them the way he had searched the corpse's hands a lifetime ago, a week ago. Because the payment had begun. He could feel it, a soft erosion starting at his earliest edges—the porch going dim, the plum losing its taste, his mother's face dissolving like sugar in rain—and he understood, with a calm that frightened him more than any bell, that he had bought a village with the only self he had ever owned.

"Odile," he said. His voice sounded very young. "Who am I? Quickly. Before I have to ask again."

She took his face in her work-roughened hands and told him.
