## 6. The Founders' Ledger

The church of St. Aldhelm's had been deconsecrated before I was born, which is the sort of joke Hollowmere would make if Hollowmere had a sense of humor. It stood at the village's eastern edge with its roof caved in on one side and its bell still hanging, mute, in the tower — a bell nobody would ring, though nobody could tell me why when I'd asked, seven years ago, with my recorder held up like a tourist.

Now I knew why. Now I knew a great many whys, and every one of them had teeth.

The vestry archive was behind a door in the north wall, and the door was locked, and the key had lived for as long as anyone remembered in the vicarage — except there hadn't been a vicar in Hollowmere since 1963, and the vicarage had been a holiday let since the nineties. I'd always assumed the key was lost. I'd always assumed a lot of things.

It was hanging on a nail inside the porch, exactly where a key would hang if someone had expected me to need it.

I stood in the porch for a long moment with the rain coming down behind me — the same bruised, wrong rain that had been falling since the sky forgot how to be night — and I thought about that nail. About intention. About the difference between a village that loses its key and a village that leaves it out for the folklorist, the way you leave out milk for something that isn't a cat.

"Petra?" Tam's voice, from the doorway, breathless. The boy had run the whole way from the pub, which was half a mile, which told me everything about how the night was going back there. "Bess says — she says if you're going to the church, you shouldn't go alone. She says that's rule one. She didn't say what rules two through whatever are."

"Rule one is good rules," I said. "Come on, then. You can carry the torch."

He did, eagerly, the way teenagers carry anything — like a weapon, like a responsibility, like a thing they've been finally, finally trusted with. He was fifteen and he'd watched the dead speak out of his neighbor's mouth six hours ago, and he had decided, with the terrible adaptability of the young, that the correct response was to be useful. I envied him that. I had responded by becoming very still and very academic, which is my version of screaming.

The vestry smelled of paper and mouse and time. Shelves ran floor to ceiling, and the shelves were full of the ordinary sediment of a small parish: registers, minutes, tithes, a box marked FLOWER SHOW 1974–1981 that I genuinely, in that moment, would have traded my left hand for. Tam swept his torch across them.

"What are we looking for?"

"Anything old," I said. "Anything that isn't a flower show."

It was Tam who found it — of course it was, because the universe has a sense of humor even if Hollowmere doesn't — wedged flat at the back of the lowest shelf, behind a ledger of pew rentals. A book bound in leather gone the color of tea, with no title on the spine, and when I lifted it, it was heavier than it had any right to be. Not mysteriously heavier. Just heavy, the way a thing is heavy when a great many people have held it and meant it.

The first page read: *Being a true account of the bargain made at Hollowmere, in the year of our founding, by those who invited It, that It might sleep, and we might live.*

"Well," I said. My voice came out very level, which I noted with detached professional interest. "There's a sentence you don't get in the county records office."

* * *

I read it aloud. I don't know why — habit, maybe. Seven years of recording, seven years of believing that a thing said aloud is a thing preserved, a thing you can go back to. Some habits are load-bearing. Tam sat on an upturned crate with the torch held steady and listened the way he'd listened to everything all night: completely, without blinking, like a boy memorizing how to be brave.

The founders' hands were different — you could see where one account ended and another began, the ink changing, the spelling wandering — but the bargain was consistent, and it was simple, and it was monstrous.

They had come to the moor intending to farm it. The moor had other ideas, or rather the moor had *occupants*, and the occupants were hungry — not for blood, not for flesh, the ledger was very clear and almost apologetic on this point. It fed on *remembering*. On the act of it. On the warm interior weight of a life held in a mind. It had eaten the memory of the land itself, the ledger said, which was why nothing grew well and why the mist came down at noon like a lid.

They could not kill it. They could not leave — those who tried, the ledger noted with a brevity that hid a horror, *did not arrive anywhere*. So they made it an offer. They would feed it, willingly and on schedule, their memories — not everything, never everything, but the surplus, the griefs and the dead years and the faces they could bear to lose. And in exchange they would sing to it. Songs to make the eating gentle. Songs to keep it sleeping.

"The songs," I said, and had to stop, and start again. "The songs were never warnings."

"They were lullabies," Tam said. He said it flatly, the way you say something once you've stopped being able to be shocked by it. "Maren said. Last night."

Maren had said. Maren, who had stopped at the seventh verse with her hands shaking, because the seventh verse wasn't a verse, it was a *dose*, and she'd nearly fed it something fresh.

I turned pages. The names of the invited ran down the inner cover in a column: Underhill. Voss. Merrick. Okafor. Names I knew — names that were still, against all odds, walking around this village. Dara Okafor had broken a door that his own ancestors had sealed, and the ledger didn't know that yet, but it would.

And then the final page.

It was written in a different hand — later, I judged, the ink browner, the nib finer — and it was headed *Of the Re-Invitation*, and I read it once in silence and then, because I am who I am, once aloud, and I wish to God I hadn't, because Tam was listening the way he listened to everything.

*When the seal is broken and It wakes and will not be sung down, there remains one remedy. A living singer must stand at the threshold and offer not the surplus but the store — the whole of what they are, willingly, in song. It cannot take what is not given. It will not refuse what is given whole. The singer will walk out at dawn empty, and the door will be sealed from the inside, and the bargain will hold for another age, or until some fool with a shovel —*

The rest was water damage. I sat with the water damage for a while.

"Empty," Tam said.

"It means the memories," I said. "It means — you give it your remembering. Not your skills, not your — " I stopped, because I was lying, and I was aware I was lying, and Tam was aware, because fifteen-year-olds can smell a lie the way sharks smell blood. "I don't know exactly what it means. The ledger doesn't itemize."

"It means you'd forget," Tam said. "You'd forget us."

The torch was steady in his hand. His voice was steady. Everything about him was steady except his eyes, which had gone bright and furious, and I understood suddenly that this boy — who had lost, in one night, the ordinary assumption that the world was safe, who had watched his dead grandfather's voice come out of the postman's mouth — had decided somewhere in the last few hours that I was one of the fixed points. That I was load-bearing. And here I was reading him a document that said the load-bearing wall was for sale.

"Tam."

"Don't." He stood up. The crate scraped. "Don't do the voice. The reasonable voice. The one you do when you're about to do something stupid for a good reason."

"That's a very specific accusation."

"Maren does it too," he said. "Everyone old does it. You get a face you make." He set the torch down on the crate so its beam fell across the ledger, across the water-damaged final line, across the word *empty*. "Bess held the door last night. She — " and here his steadiness finally cracked, one hairline fracture down the middle, "— Bess is *gone*, and you're sitting here finding a book that says the way to fix everything is for you to stop existing, and you've got the face on already."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"You've agreed to everything," he said. "You agreed when you picked the book up. I could see it."

I wanted to tell him he was wrong. I wanted to tell him that a folklorist knows better than anyone that a text is not a fate, that documents record choices rather than compel them, that the ledger was one more song in the archive and songs mean nothing until someone sings them.

I wanted to tell him that, and I couldn't, because my hand was resting on the final page, and I had not moved it.

* * *

We carried the ledger back through the rain, Tam walking close enough that our shoulders touched, which neither of us acknowledged. Behind us the church bell hung in its tower, and I found myself thinking — with the calm, clinical part of my mind that never stops taking notes, the part that will be taking notes at my own funeral — that a bell is a kind of song too. That everything in this village was a kind of song. That the question was never whether the singing would continue.

The question was who would be left, afterward, to remember what the songs had been for.

The pub lights were visible down the lane, one warm square in all that bruised grey, and standing in the doorway of it, backlit, arms folded, was Maren Voss — ninety-one years old, complicit for a lifetime, waiting for us with the expression of a woman who already knew what was in the book.

Because of course she did. Her family had kept it.

"You've read the last page," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Maren—"

"Then you know what I've known since I was six years old," she said, and stepped aside to let us in, and out of the dark behind her, faint and patient, something beneath the moor began — very softly, in a voice I had recorded myself, seven years ago, in this very village — to sing my name.
