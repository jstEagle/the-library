## 9. The Re-Invitation

The false dawn was the color of a bruise that had stopped apologizing for itself.

I stood in the churchyard with the ledger under my arm and my breath coming out in little white flags of surrender, and I thought: seven years of fieldwork, two degrees, one very patient thesis advisor, and it has all come down to this — me, singing in a graveyard at an hour that refuses to be morning, to something that eats memories the way I eat biscuits when I'm nervous. Which is to say: completely, and without dignity.

The others stood behind me. Maren had insisted on that arrangement. "It needs to see your face," she'd said, "but we need to see yours too." Tam held the ledger open to the final page because my hands had developed a tremor I refused to acknowledge on the record. Dara stood slightly apart, arms crossed like a man guarding his own guilt from pickpockets. And Bess's absence stood with us too, the way absences do — a shape cut out of the group, cold air rushing into it.

The thing beneath the moor had been singing all night. Not its own voice — mine. My recordings, played back through the mouths of the dead, through the walls of the inn, through the ground itself if you pressed your ear flat enough to listen. It knew every verse I'd ever collected. It had tasted them through seven years of tape and paper and my own careless, delighted attention.

That was the horror of it, and also — Maren had helped me understand this in the small hours, her hands wrapped around tea she never drank — that was the opening. You cannot seal a door with something the thing on the other side doesn't want.

"Petra," Tam said quietly. "Page says you have to mean it."

"I know what the page says."

"Just checking. You've got your academic face on. The one where you're about to do something reckless and call it methodology."

I almost laughed. That would have ruined everything, so I swallowed it and looked out over the churchyard wall, past the leaning stones, to where the moor rose black against the bruised sky. The barrow was a dark molar in that jawline. Somewhere inside it, behind a door sealed from the inside by people three centuries dead who had thought themselves very clever, something old was waiting to be sung to sleep again.

Or waiting to be fed. The page didn't distinguish. The founders' handwriting got shakier toward the end, as though the ink itself had misgivings.

*Let a living singer stand at the threshold and offer freely what the dead could only surrender,* the ledger read. *Memory given is a lock. Memory taken is only a meal.*

Maren had translated that for me twice, and both times her voice had cracked on the same word. *Freely.* The founders had fed it scraps — a name here, a summer there, the small forgettings of a village that agreed not to notice what it lost. Lullabies were just the spoon. But a whole life, offered whole, willingly — that wasn't a meal. That was a bolt sliding home.

Seven years of Hollowmere lived in my head. Every song, every informant, every harvest supper and funeral wake and argument about whose grandmother had mangled which verse worse. Maren's kitchen. Dara swearing at his radar equipment. Tam teaching me to skip stones badly. Bess pulling me a pint and calling me "our scholar" like it was an affectionate insult.

All of it, on the table.

"Petra." Maren's hand found my shoulder. Her fingers were ice; hers always were, even before any of this. "You don't have to—"

"Yes, I do." I turned and looked at them, all three of them, trying to take a photograph no camera could hold. Trying to memorize the memorizing. "And when I don't know your names tomorrow, you're going to tell me anyway. All of you. Even the embarrassing parts. *Especially* the embarrassing parts."

Dara made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Tam was crying and pretending the wind was responsible, which was unconvincing, as there was no wind.

I faced the moor.

*

Here is what nobody tells you about singing to a god: your body knows how before your mind consents. The first verse came up out of me like water finding a crack — the harvest song, the one Maren had stopped at verse seven with shaking hands, and I did not stop. Verse seven. Eight. Nine. The verses about the guest beneath the hill, the guest who must be sung full so he does not go walking, the verses the village sang once and never twice because twice was an invitation and once was only maintenance.

My voice carried strangely in the false light. It sounded younger than me. It sounded, in places, like voices I had recorded and archived and filed away — Maren's mother, old Aldous Crane, the whole choir of the dead that Hollowmere had been feeding to the dark for three hundred years, all of them rising through my throat now, taking their turn.

The moor answered.

Not with sound at first. With *attention*. I felt it the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder — a pressure against the back of the skull, vast and curious and terribly, terribly pleased. The bruised sky leaned closer. The grass bent flat in a circle around me though nothing moved. And then, from everywhere — the stones, the soil, the space between my own heartbeats — it began to sing back.

It used my voice. Of course it did. It had eaten enough of me by now to wear me like a coat, and hearing myself harmonize with myself across a churchyard should have been the most horrifying thing I'd ever experienced, except that it wasn't. It was *lonely*. That was the thing I hadn't understood until this moment, standing in the open with my whole life in my mouth: it wasn't hunger the way a wolf is hungry. It was hunger the way an empty house is hungry. Everything it had ever taken, it had taken because the alternative was the silence underneath the world, and it had been down there since before anyone thought to sing, and it remembered the silence better than anything else.

*I know you,* it sang, in my voice, in Maren's, in Bess's drowned brother's, in a hundred voices layered like sediment. *You kept us. Seven years you kept us in your little machine, your little papers. No one else kept us.*

"I'm not keeping you," I said, and my voice only shook a normal human amount. "I'm giving you back. All of it. Every song, every face, every hour I spent in this village. Freely. As a seal. Sleep on it. Sleep *inside* it."

The attention pressed harder. Curious. Testing. Somewhere in the pressure I felt it turn my memories over like a merchant appraising goods — the archive, the tapes, the notebook margins full of my terrible shorthand — and I felt it find what it had always found there, which was everything. I was the richest meal this thing had ever been offered, and I was offering myself as a *door hinge*, and the sheer audacity of it hung in the air between us.

*You will forget,* it said, and for the first time it used no stolen voice at all. It used its own, which sounded like the moment before an avalanche, like geology finally admitting it had opinions. *All of it. That is the price of the lock. A seal must be seamless. There can be no thread left loose to pull.*

"I know."

*You will walk out of this village tomorrow and it will mean nothing to you. They will mean nothing to you.* Something moved in the pressure — almost regret, or the shape regret leaves when it fossilizes. *They will remember you. You will not remember them. Is that the bargain?*

Behind me, Tam made a small broken noise. Maren said, very steadily, "Take the deal, you stubborn fool," and I heard Dara murmur amen.

"That's the bargain," I said.

And then, because I am who I am — a folklorist to the marrow, incapable of leaving a source unrecorded even at the end of the world — I added: "But I want it noted for the record that they'll teach me. They promised. So you won't actually get all of it. Some of it they'll give back. You're sealing a door with a book that gets reprinted."

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats.

Then the thing beneath the moor *laughed*, and it sounded exactly like me, and I have decided never to think about that again.

*Accepted,* it said. *Sing.*

*

I sang the rest.

Every verse. Every variant. The lullabies and the warnings and the songs that were both, the ones Maren's mother had hummed, the ones Aldous Crane had sworn were nonsense, the ones I'd found scratched into beam-ends and gravestones and the flyleaf of a Bible older than the church. I sang until my throat tore and my knees gave and Tam and Dara caught an arm each and held me upright, and still the verses came, because they weren't only mine anymore — the dead were singing through me, all of them, the whole fed choir of Hollowmere, pouring three centuries of memory through one exhausted folklorist like light through a lens.

And the entity drank it. I felt it drink. Felt my own life leave me in long warm pulls — Maren's kitchen going first, then the pub, then the moor walks, then faces softening into shapes, shapes into smudges, smudges into nothing — and with each pull the pressure around me eased, the sky lifted a shade, and the door beneath the barrow swung shut the way doors swing shut in dreams: slowly, inevitably, and from the inside.

The last thing I felt before the feeling ended was gratitude. Its, or mine, or everyone's. It's hard to file those things separately when you're being filed yourself.

Then the sun came up — the real sun, rude and ordinary and gold — and I was kneeling in a churchyard with wet cheeks and a sore throat and no idea why, surrounded by three strangers who were looking at me like I was the most important thing in the world.

"Hello," said the old woman, smiling through tears. "Welcome back. Well—" she glanced at the others, and her smile went wry and brave and familiar in a way that snagged on something deep in me and found nothing to hold onto. "Well. Welcome, anyway. Let's start at the beginning."
