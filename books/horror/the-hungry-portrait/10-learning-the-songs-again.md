## 10. Learning the Songs Again

The first thing I knew was birdsong, and the second thing I knew was that I didn't know where I was.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only tool I've ever had. It wasn't confusion. Confusion is a fog you can wait out. This was a clean absence, like walking into a room and finding the walls freshly painted — no pictures, no hooks, no nail holes. A room built yesterday, waiting for furniture.

I was in a bed under a sloped ceiling. There was a notebook on the nightstand, a good one, leather-soft with handling, and my handwriting filled it — but when I read it, it read like a stranger's diary. *Field notes, Hollowmere.* The name meant nothing. The handwriting was mine; the life inside it wasn't.

There was a photograph tucked into the cover. An older woman with white braids and a face like weathered oak, mid-laugh, caught off guard by whoever held the camera. On the back, in my hand: *Maren Voss, harvest songs, June.*

I sat there for a long time holding a picture of someone I loved, because you could tell it was love, even without the memory of it. Love leaves evidence. Memory just leaves records.

The door opened, and the woman from the photograph looked in at me, and her face did something complicated — relief and grief sharing a single expression, like two people trying to look through one keyhole.

"There she is," she said softly. "Or there you are. Sorry. We're still working out the pronouns of it."

"Who are you?" I asked. It came out gentle, which surprised me. Apparently even blank, I had manners.

"Maren," she said. "Maren Voss." She waited, watching my eyes for any flicker. I gave her nothing, and something in her settled rather than sank, as if she'd rehearsed this particular disappointment until it fit her like an old coat. "You don't know me."

"No. Should I?"

"Seven years' worth," she said. Then, briskly, before the words could grow teeth: "But we'll get to that. Are you hungry? You're always hungry in the mornings. That hasn't changed, thank God. Some things survive the crossing."

"The crossing," I repeated.

"Breakfast first," said Maren Voss, who was clearly a woman who believed dread kept better on a full stomach. "Dread's thin gruel on an empty one. My mother said that. Well — someone's mother said that. Mine, I think. I still have mine." Her voice caught on the last word, just slightly, and smoothed itself over. "That's the deal, pet. You'll see. Come down when you can walk. Take your time. We've got nothing but time now — that was rather the point."

* * *

The kitchen smelled of bread and woodsmoke, and three people turned toward me when I came in, with the careful brightness of people who have practiced not staring.

I knew none of them. I knew all of them, in the way you know a song from its first bar without knowing its name.

A tall man with foreman's shoulders and a healing scar along his jaw stood up too fast, then made himself sit back down, folding his big hands like a schoolboy. "Dara," he said. "Dara Okafor. I dug the hole. Metaphorically and — well. Also literally." He winced at himself. "I'm better at briefings than introductions."

A teenager slouched against the counter, all elbows and defiance, watching me with the fierce attention of someone guarding something fragile. "Tam," they said. "I'm Tam. You taught me to record properly. Gain staging. You said my first recordings sounded like 'a bee trapped in a hymnal.'" A pause. "You laughed after. In case that matters. You were laughing."

"It matters," I said, and meant it, though I couldn't have said why.

And Maren set a bowl of porridge in front of me, sat down opposite, folded her hands, and said: "Ask us anything. That's the rule we agreed on. Anything except *what happened*, because that one takes the whole day, and Dara makes a terrible job of the middle part — he goes grey and starts apologizing to the furniture."

"I apologized once," Dara muttered.

"To a chair," said Tam.

"It was a *good* chair."

I looked around the table at these strangers wearing familiarity like borrowed coats, and I asked the only question that seemed load-bearing. "Why do I remember nothing? Not just this village. Nothing since—" I stopped. The edge of the absence had a shape. "How far back is gone?"

Maren's hands tightened around each other. "Seven years," she said. "Give or take. Everything from the day you arrived here." She held up a hand before I could speak. "Before you ask — yes, it was worth it. Yes, you chose it. No, we didn't let you, exactly, but we didn't stop you either, and I'll answer for that sin at whatever altar takes complaints. And yes—" here her voice went quiet and steady, the voice of a woman who had decided long ago how this sentence would go, "—we are going to give it all back. Every day of it. However long it takes. Because you wrote it down, pet. Seven years of notebooks, tapes, and a head full of songs, and you left instructions. You always left instructions. Even for this. *Especially* for this."

* * *

They started, as instructed by my past self, with the songs.

Not the story — the story could wait, Maren said, the story needed daylight and strong tea and possibly a priest, though the vicarage stood empty now and would stay that way. The songs first. Because the songs, my own notes explained in my own handwriting, were the fastest road back. *Memory lives in melody longer than it lives in language,* I had written. *Sing them the archive.*

So that first morning, Maren pushed back her chair, stood in her own kitchen like a woman standing at a lectern, and sang.

It was a harvest song. I recognized it the way you recognize your own name called across a field — not the words, which meant nothing, but the fact of being addressed. Something behind my sternum leaned toward it. The melody walked up through the verses, verse after verse, and I counted without meaning to, some buried archivist in me ticking them off — five, six, seven —

She stopped at seven.

Then she took a breath, and started again from the beginning, and sang the whole thing through twice, all the way to the end, and the room did not change temperature, and nobody flinched, and Tam grinned like sunrise, and Dara let out a breath he'd apparently been holding for weeks.

"You never sing anything twice," I said. I didn't know why I said it. The knowledge arrived from nowhere, fully formed, like a stone surfacing in a ploughed field.

"We used to have our reasons," Maren said. She sat back down and took my hand across the table, her grip dry and strong. "We don't anymore. That's the short version of everything, pet. The long version has a door in it, and a ledger, and the worst night this village ever spent, and a woman who sang a hunger to sleep with her own life's memories as the price of admission. But the short version is: we don't have reasons anymore. We have a sunrise. Watch."

She drew back the curtain, and there it was — ordinary, gilded, unremarkable, climbing over the moor like it owned the place. I felt the whole table relax toward it, four people leaning into warmth the way plants lean into light, and I understood that they had been starved of it, recently, in some way none of them would explain yet.

"Now," Maren said, turning back to me, businesslike, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand as if tears were simply housework, "your notes say we start with the sea shanties, because you found me singing those my first week and you said they reminded you of home, though you refused to say what home was, which we've all agreed was very rude of you. So." She cleared her throat. "Question one. Does any of this feel familiar?" And she sang.

* * *

We worked through the morning, and the days after, and the weeks. They came at me in pieces, gently, the way you feed someone after a long illness — a spoonful at a time, nothing rich enough to shock the system.

Dara brought me to the moor and showed me the barrow, now sealed again, turf laid neat as a bandage, and told me about the door with his hands shaking and his voice level, and stopped twice, and finished anyway, because he said I'd once told him that finishing was the whole discipline. He showed me the excavation photographs. In every one, I stood at the edge of the trench with my recorder raised, and in every one I was frowning, and he said, "You always frowned at it. Right up until the end. You were the only one who never thought it was geology."

Tam played me my own tapes — hours of them, my voice asking questions in a younger accent of confidence, villagers singing verses they'd sworn never to repeat. I listened to myself interview ghosts and felt nothing, and then Tam played one where I laughed — really laughed, helpless, at something a child had sung wrong — and grief went through me like a current through wire. Grief for what? I couldn't say. But Tam watched my face and nodded slowly, like a scientist confirming a result. "That's the one," they said. "Bess's daughter. Bess recorded it the week before—" They stopped. Swallowed. "Bess was ours," they said instead. "You'll meet her in the tapes. She'd have liked this part. She liked teaching things back to people."

And Maren sang, every day, twice through, everything twice, fearless, while I sat with my notebook open and my pen moving, re-collecting my own collected life one verse at a time.

Some of it came back. Not as memory — as recognition. A lane behind the church that made my feet turn left without consulting me. The exact weight of Dara's flask, handed to me at the right moment as if my body remembered thirst. Tam's laugh, arriving from two rooms away, making me smile before I knew whose it was. The past returning not as a story but as a habit, which is, I suspect, how it lives in everyone anyway.

But the center stayed dark. Whatever I had done at the end — and they circled it carefully, always, a family walking soft around a grave — whatever bargain I had struck, whatever I had fed and whatever it had cost, that was gone entirely. Sealed, Maren said, like the door. Paid out, spent, eaten. I had bought a sunrise with seven years of myself, and the receipt had burned with the purchase.

Some nights I woke at three a.m. in that strange painted room and mourned a woman I had never met: the one who lived in the notebooks, who had mentors and archives and a village full of people who loved her, who had walked out of something terrible at dawn carrying nothing. I missed her the way you miss the dead — completely, uselessly, with love.

And every morning, Maren knocked, and brought tea, and said, "Right, pet. Where were we," and we began again.

* * *

On the first of the month — I know because Tam marked the calendar with a small triumphant cross — I sang back.

It was the harvest song, the one Maren had opened with, and I got through all nine verses without the notebook, my voice finding turns in the melody I hadn't known I knew, ornaments rising out of me like bubbles out of deep water. When I finished, the kitchen was very quiet.

"Twice," Maren whispered. "Sing it twice."

So I did. Twice through, all nine verses, and the second time my voice cracked on the seventh — cracked, and carried on, and nothing in the walls answered, and nothing beneath the floor stirred, and the birds outside went on being birds.

Dara cried a little and pretended it was the steam from his mug. Tam whooped loud enough to startle crows off the roof. And Maren took both my hands and pressed them together between hers like something being kept warm, and said, "There you are. There's some of you. Bit by bit. That's how we'll do it — bit by bit."

Later, alone, I opened the oldest notebook, the one from my first week, and read the note I'd written in the margin of the very first page, in handwriting I was only just beginning to recognize as mine:

*They won't sing anything twice. Find out why.*

Underneath, in fresher ink — added, I realized, sometime in the last few weeks, in a hand steadier than mine had ever been — Maren had written a reply:

*Found out. Cost everything. Worth it. Now learn it again, pet. We'll teach you. We've nothing but time, and we remember you enough for both of us.*

I closed the book and went downstairs toward the voices, toward the tea and the second verses and the people who were patiently becoming my family for the second time, and on the stairs I stopped, because from the kitchen below, faint and unhurried, I heard them starting the shanties without me — and my feet, all on their own, already knew the way down.
