## 5. What Wren Knows

The voice starts on a Tuesday, which Wren thinks is funny later, because Tuesdays are spelling days and the voice is very good at spelling. It spells her name first. *W-R-E-N.* Like a bird, it says. Like a small bird in a big house.

She is lying in bed with the covers pulled up to her chin because October has gotten into the walls of the Merrow house and made them cold in a way that blankets don't fix. The radiator under her window ticks like something learning to talk. Dad says the house is old and old houses make noises, and Mom says the same thing but slower, like she's trying to convince herself while she's saying it. Wren is nine and three-quarters, which means she is old enough to know when adults are doing that thing where they answer the question they wish you'd asked instead of the one you did.

*Wren,* says the voice, from upstairs. Not mean. Not whispery-scary like in movies. Kind, actually. That's the part she can't explain to anybody, so she doesn't try. It sounds like a grandmother in a commercial, or a librarian who really likes you, or the lady at the bakery who saves you the end piece of the cinnamon bread because she knows it's your favorite.

"I'm here," Wren says, into her pillow, very quietly, because if she says it too loud then it becomes a real conversation and real conversations have consequences.

*You're here,* the voice agrees, pleased. *I've been counting.*

"Counting what?"

*Everything worth keeping.*

She lies there and listens to the radiator tick and thinks about that. Everything worth keeping. She looks around her room in the dark — the boxes she hasn't unpacked, the moon through curtains that aren't hers yet, the cardboard box of books stacked by the door. Nothing feels kept yet. Nothing feels like hers except maybe the stuffed rabbit, Button, who came with her from the apartment and therefore doesn't count as belonging to the house.

Upstairs, faintly, something counts. Not words now. Just numbers, soft as someone tapping a pencil. One. Two. Three. It gets to eleven and stops, and the stopping is somehow louder than the counting, like a held breath.

Wren pulls Button closer and does not ask what comes after eleven.

* * *

At school she sits in the third row of her dad's classroom, because her school got consolidated last year and now the fifth graders from Hollow Creek come to the building where he teaches, which everyone says is temporary but nobody acts like it's temporary. There's a plaque by the door with names on it, and sometimes kids touch it when they walk past, quick, like petting a dog that might bite.

A girl named Priya Raghavan sits next to her at lunch and asks if she's the new teacher's daughter and Wren says yes and Priya nods like this explains everything about Wren, which maybe it does.

"You live out past the church?" Priya asks. "On the old road?"

"The Merrow house."

Priya's sandwich stops halfway to her mouth. Then it keeps going, and she chews, and she says, "Cool," in a voice that means the opposite of cool, and after that Priya always seems to have somewhere else to sit.

Wren doesn't mind. Wren has the voice.

That night the voice teaches her things. It teaches her a skipping rhyme the girls in Hollow Creek used to sing in 1954, before anyone she knows was born. It teaches her how to whistle through a blade of grass. It tells her what her grandmother's kitchen looked like — yellow wallpaper with little blue birds, a jar of buttons on the windowsill — and when Wren checks the attic boxes the next afternoon, there is a jar of buttons, dusty and heavy, exactly like the voice said.

"You knew her," Wren says that night, sitting up in bed, talking toward the ceiling because the voice lives up there, or comes from up there, or *is* up there; she's not sure those are different things in this house.

*I kept her,* the voice says. *The way I keep all of you.*

"What does that mean?"

*It means you're mine to look after. All the children of this house. I've never lost one yet.*

Something cold walks down Wren's spine, but she makes herself ask anyway, because that's the rule her dad taught her: if a question scares you, it's probably the important kind. "The other kids. On the plaque at school. Did you keep them too?"

The house is quiet for so long that she almost falls asleep waiting. When the voice comes back it sounds different — softer, sadder, or pretending to be, and Wren is starting to learn the difference between the voice's feelings and its costumes.

*Safe in the book,* it says. *They're all safe in the book. Nobody can ever hurt them again. Wouldn't you like to be safe in the book, Wren?*

"With the other kids?"

*With all of them. Together. Forever is much less lonely with company.*

And here is the terrible thing, the thing Wren will carry even after everything, the thing she'll never quite forgive herself for: some part of her wants to say yes. Because forever sounds long, and third grade at her old school had a girl named Madison who made her cry every recess for four months, and being safe in a book with other kids who were also safe sounded — for one second, one whole second — like a good deal.

Then she thinks about her mom's laugh, which sounds like a screen door slamming in summer. She thinks about her dad reading aloud at breakfast in different voices for every character. She thinks about how Button's ear is coming unstitched and only Mom knows how to fix it right.

"No thank you," Wren says politely, because her mother raised her to be polite even to grandmothers in commercials who live in ceilings. "Not tonight."

*Soon, though,* the voice says, and it isn't a question, and it isn't unkind either, and somehow that's worse than both. *You're nearly ten. Ten is my favorite number.*

* * *

After that, Wren answers it.

She doesn't tell herself she's answering it. She tells herself she's being friendly, the way you're friendly to the checkout lady, the way you're friendly to Mrs. Hollis next door who talks to her mailboxes. You don't have to believe someone is real to be polite to them. Politeness is free.

But at night, when the counting starts — and it starts earlier now, eight-thirty, then eight-fifteen, working backward through the evening like water finding cracks — Wren lies in the dark and calls back numbers. "Twelve," she'll say, when it reaches eleven. "Thirteen."

The voice loves this. The voice goes quiet with happiness, the way a dog goes quiet when you finally throw the stick.

*Yes,* it breathes. *Yes, that's it. You do it perfectly. Do you know why you're so good at it?*

"Why?"

*Because it's yours. The count. It's been waiting for you since before you were born. Your great-great-something started it and your grandmother carried it and now it's rolled all the way down the years like a marble, right to you, right to this room, right to this night.*

"That doesn't sound fair," Wren says.

*Fair,* the voice repeats, tasting the word. *Fair is a thing children say. I'm not unfair, Wren. I'm just hungry. Those are different.*

She should tell someone. She knows she should tell someone. She practices it in the bathroom mirror sometimes, making her face serious: *Mom, there's a voice in the house and it knows Grandma's name and it says I belong in a book.* But every time she gets close to saying it out loud, two things stop her.

First: grown-ups fix problems by making them not exist. If she tells Dad, he'll say *imaginary friend* in his relieved voice, and then he'll smile too hard at dinner, and then he'll start checking on her at night with the hall light on, and the whole family will bend itself around a story everyone agrees to believe, and the voice will still be there, underneath, counting, and now Wren will be alone with it AND with the lie.

Second — and she couldn't explain this to anyone, wouldn't know how to start — telling feels like giving it away. The voice told her once: *What's spoken can be taken. What's shared belongs to everyone. Keep me just yours and I stay just yours.* And Wren, who lost her best friend to a move two years ago and never wrote to her even once, understands keeping things. Some things get smaller when you show them. Some things need the dark.

So she keeps it.

She keeps it on Thursday nights when the counting climbs higher than usual, into the twenties and thirties, fast, excited, like the voice is showing off. She keeps it when she finds her own name written in dust on the upstairs hallway floor, written in careful child-letters, though no child has walked up there in socks all week. She keeps it when the mirror in the hallway shows her walking a half-step behind where she actually is, so that she learns to close her eyes going past, and pretends the flutter in her chest is just a game she's playing with herself.

She keeps it most of all on Sunday, when the voice says, *Ask your father about the ledger,* and Wren, who has never heard the word ledger in her life, goes downstairs and finds Dad at the kitchen table with an old brown book open in front of him, his face gray as oatmeal, and she stands in the doorway and watches him read names like they're debts, and she says nothing, and goes back up, and answers the count.

*Forty-one,* says the voice.

"Forty-two," says Wren.

*Good girl. Almost halfway. Do you want to hear a secret?*

"Okay."

*Your father found the book today. He's going to come up those stairs soon, and stand outside your door, and listen. And when he hears us talking, he's going to decide we're pretend. He's going to choose that, on purpose, with his whole heart, because the truth would make him do something, and doing something costs more than he has.*

"That's mean," Wren says.

*It's kind,* the voice says. *It's the kindest lie a father can tell. Now hush. Count with me.*

* * *

Jonah Sandoval comes up the stairs on Sunday night at 8:47, barefoot so the boards won't announce him, carrying a mug of chamomile tea he has no intention of drinking. He has spent the day with the ledger and it has rearranged something fundamental in him, the way a car accident rearranges furniture, everything familiar shoved against the walls. Eleven names. Eleven lines struck through in a hand that started firm and ended shaking. A twelfth line, blank, patient as a held breath.

He is standing outside Wren's door to check on her — that's what he tells himself, checking, a normal paternal errand — when he hears his daughter's voice through the wood, low and conversational and utterly awake.

"Forty-three," Wren says.

A pause. Long enough for Jonah's heart to perform a small, cold somersault.

"Forty-four," Wren says again, answering. "Okay. Yes. Goodnight."

Silence. Then, muffled, sweet, ordinary: "Love you too."

Jonah stands in the hallway with his tea going cold and his blood going colder, and every hair on his arms stands up and salutes the dark at the end of the corridor, where the upstairs room waits behind its closed door with its forty-four counted whatever-they-were.

He could open the door. He could go in, sit on the edge of her bed, and ask. He could kneel down in the dark and say, *Wrenny, sweetheart, who were you talking to?* and watch her face and follow wherever her face led him.

Instead he does the arithmetic of a frightened man. He runs the possibilities the way you'd run possible causes for a noise in an engine, looking for the cheapest one. Imaginary friend: cheap, common, developmental, fine. Kids talk to people who aren't there; the books say it's healthy, a sign of creativity, a practice run for empathy. She's nine, she moved across the state, she left her school and her friends, of course she invented a companion. Of course. Children in empty houses do it all the time. There is probably a study.

The alternative possibility stands at the end of the hallway wearing the shape of a closed door, and it has a ledger in it with his daughter's name on a blank line, and Jonah Sandoval, fifth-grade teacher, man of worksheets and patience and reasonable accommodations, looks at that door and chooses.

He chooses the imaginary friend.

He chooses it the way you choose to step over a hole in the stairs instead of fixing it — not because you don't see the hole, but because fixing it means opening the floor, and opening the floor means finding out what's underneath, and underneath might be anything. He chooses it because his grandmother chose keeping the count over facing whatever needed counting, because the first Merrow chose building a room over facing whatever needed a room, because cowardice, he is beginning to understand, is the family inheritance, passed down more reliably than the house, and he is the current holder of the estate.

He drinks the cold tea standing in the hallway. He rehearses casualness. Then he opens Wren's door and puts his head in and says, "Hey, kiddo. You awake?"

"Yeah." She's sitting up, Button in her lap, and in the streetlight through the curtains her face is calm, watchful, older than nine. "Hi, Dad."

"Thought I heard you talking."

There it is. He watches her weigh it — he actually watches it happen, a flicker of something moving behind her eyes like a fish turning in shallow water — and he prays, to nothing, to the ceiling, to the yellowed wallpaper in the attic, that she'll give him the easy answer, because he has already decided to accept the easy answer, and if she gives him a hard one he does not know what he'll do, and that not-knowing frightens him more than anything in the ledger.

"My friend," Wren says. "The one I made up. From the move."

"The imaginary friend," Jonah says, with relief so vast it echoes.

"Yeah." She smiles at him. It's a good smile. It's a nine-year-old's smile, gap-toothed in back, warm as toast. "She's teaching me a counting game."

"A counting game," he repeats, like a man signing a contract without reading it.

"She says I'm really good at it." Wren settles down under her covers, pulling Button close. "Dad? Can I ask you something?"

"Anything."

"If somebody keeps you somewhere safe, but you didn't pick to go there — is that still being kept, or is that something else?"

The question lands in the room like a dropped glass. Jonah hears it whole, hears every edge of it, hears the shape of the ledger inside it, and he opens his mouth to answer honestly, to sit down on the bed and say *Wrenny, tell me about your friend, tell me everything, right now, leave nothing out* —

— and instead he says, "I think that's called being loved, kiddo. Sometimes grown-ups keep you safe whether you picked it or not."

Wren looks at him for a long moment. In the dark her eyes are very steady.

"Okay," she says. "G'night, Dad."

He kisses her forehead. Her skin is cold, cold as the hallway, cold as the room at the end of it, and he tells himself it's just October getting into the walls.

In the doorway he pauses. "Nightlight on or off?"

"On," Wren says. Then, quieter, almost to herself, in a voice that will follow him down the stairs and into his dreams and lodge there like a splinter: "She likes to see me."

Jonah turns on the nightlight, closes the door softly, and walks back down the hall past the closed door at the end, and as he passes it — just for a second, just long enough to doubt his own ears — he is almost certain he hears, from inside, patient and pleased and kind:

*Forty-five.*

Downstairs, Dana asks if Wren's okay, and Jonah says she's fine, she's got an imaginary friend, it's totally normal, the books say it's healthy, and Dana smiles with relief and says that's a relief, and they stand together in their warm kitchen believing a comfortable thing very hard, both of them, each for their own reasons, while overhead — soft as a pencil tap, patient as winter — the house counts on.

*Forty-six. Forty-seven.*

Almost halfway, it had said.

Wren lies in the dark with her eyes open, listening to it climb, and wonders — with the clear, unsentimental honesty of children, which adults spend their whole lives unlearning — how high a number has to get before somebody finally comes upstairs and makes it stop.
