## 10. The Ledger's Arithmetic

The ledger did not want to be read at night.

Jonah understood this the way he understood the weight of a sleeping child in his arms—wordlessly, in the body, in the small animal brain that predated language and would outlast it. He had carried the book down from the attic at dusk, meaning to open it over coffee, meaning to treat it like a document, like a puzzle, like anything other than what it was. And then the sun had gone, and the lamps had come on, and the book had sat on the kitchen table between the fruit bowl and the salt like a guest who had not been invited but could not be asked to leave.

He made the coffee anyway. Ritual against dread. The kettle's whistle sounded too much like something distant and thin calling a name, so he took it off the heat early and poured the water dark and drank it too fast and burned his tongue, and the small ordinary pain of it was almost a comfort.

Dana had taken Wren's mattress into their room. Neither of them had discussed it; the arrangement had simply appeared, the way ice appears on a window, and neither of them had remarked on it, the way you don't remark on ice. Wren slept now between her parents like a comma in a sentence that did not want to end. Through the ceiling, the upstairs room—*the* room, the one whose door he had not opened, whose doorknob he had polished around for weeks with a rag and a coward's diligence—kept its silence. But it was the silence of held breath. He had begun to know the difference.

He sat down at the table. He opened the ledger.

The paper was the color of old teeth. The binding was leather gone soft as skin, cracked at the spine like a knuckle. His grandmother's handwriting filled the early pages in a copperplate so precise it looked printed—dates, amounts, small domestic notations that meant nothing: *flour, two sacks. mended the east shutter. called on Mrs. Aldous re: the fence.* A woman's life, itemized. A woman keeping accounts because keeping accounts was the only thing standing between her family and the dark.

He turned pages. The entries thinned. The handwriting changed—looser after 1962, the loops tired, the pen pressing harder as though she were trying to write her way through the paper into something solid on the other side. And then, on a page dated in a spring he could not parse at first because the numbers made no sense, he found the arithmetic.

She had drawn it as a table. Columns ruled in pencil, straight as a fence line. Across the top: *name. year. paid.*

Eleven rows.

He read the first one and his hands went cold around the warm mug.

*Elias Thorne, 1931. Paid: twenty years.*

He read the second. *Margaret Aldous, 1951. Paid: twenty years.*

The gap between the dates was twenty years. He counted it twice, then a third time with his finger under the numbers like a schoolboy, because the arithmetic was so clean it refused to be believed. Elias Thorne taken in 1931. Margaret Aldous taken in 1951. Twenty years exactly.

He read down the column of years. 1951. 1971. 1979.

His breath stopped.

1979 was not twenty years after 1971. It was eight. He stared at the row—*Thomas Vane, 1979*—and then at the entry beneath it, written in a hand that had begun to shake, the letters leaning like fence posts in wind:

*The room is hungry early. It does not wait. It does not wait. I have fed it out of turn and it has accepted, but it will not forget the taste of being fed ahead of schedule. Twenty years is what a name buys. Eight is what my failure costs. God forgive me, the arithmetic is not mine to choose.*

Jonah set the mug down because his hands had begun to tremble and he did not want to hear the ceramic chattering against the saucer. The sound would have been too small and too human for the room.

He understood it then, or he understood the shape of it, the way you understand the shape of a thing moving behind frosted glass. Each crossed-out name was not a murder. It was a payment. A tithe. Twenty years of quiet, purchased with a child, and the house—God, the house—had kept its side of it. Had gone quiet. Had let generations of Merrows live and sleep and grow old inside its walls while somewhere upstairs a door stayed shut and a hunger stayed fed.

And his grandmother—Edith Merrow, the woman he had never met, whose face he knew only from one photograph in the hall, a thin woman with a mouth that looked like it had been holding words back for decades—had inherited the debt. Had kept the count. Had fed it when the schedule demanded, and once, in 1979, when the schedule broke, had fed it early, out of turn, and had spent the rest of her life paying for that failure in whatever currency a woman like her possessed.

He turned to the last written page.

The final entry was dated three weeks before her death. The handwriting was very small now, economized, a woman rationing her strength the way she had rationed everything else. He read it standing up, because sitting suddenly felt like a posture for people with more time than he had.

*The count is not a hunger only. It is a schedule. The room eats on the calendar the way the town eats on harvest. A name buys twenty years of quiet, and quiet is all the town has ever asked of us, and all we have ever been able to sell. I am dying. The count does not die. It will resume at the next due date, and the due date is measured from the last feeding, and the last feeding was Thomas Vane, 1979, and I was meant to feed it again in 1999 and I did not, because I was old and I told myself the room had forgotten, and a woman my age is very good at telling herself things.*

*It has not forgotten. It is patient the way winter is patient. The count resumes at midnight on the inheritor's child's tenth birthday. I am sorry. I am sorry. I did not know who would inherit. I only knew someone would, because blood does not end, it only travels.*

*To the inheritor: I am sorry. I kept the count as long as I could. The blank line is yours. God keep you from filling it.*

Jonah read it four times.

The fourth time, his lips moved, and he heard himself whisper the date, and the date was not a date anymore. It was a countdown.

Wren's birthday was in six weeks.

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat at the kitchen table while the house ticked and settled around him, doing the thing houses do at night, and he did the arithmetic again and again because the arithmetic was the only thing in the world that still obeyed rules.

Thomas Vane, 1979. The last feeding. Twenty years from that should have been 1999. His grandmother had died in 1998, and the feeding had not come, and the house had waited—had held its hunger like a held note—for a quarter of a century, because the covenant was broken not by malice but by an old woman's death and an empty chair. And now the inheritor had come. Blood had returned to the bloodline's house. And the room had done what patient things do when the debt comes due.

It had set a date.

He got up and walked through the dark house without turning on lights, because he did not want to see the mirrors, and because some animal part of him believed that if he moved quietly enough the house would not notice he was awake. He stood in the hallway below the stairs. Above him, the upstairs landing was a black rectangle. The door at the end of it was shut. It had been shut since they arrived. He had told himself it was a spare room, a storage room, nothing.

He thought about Thomas Vane. A boy who had turned ten in 1979 in a house on this street, and had walked into a room, or been walked into it, and had become a line in a ledger. *Paid: twenty years.* Twenty years of quiet for the whole town, bought at the price of one child's entire future, and everyone had agreed to the exchange, and no one had called it what it was.

And his grandmother had fed it early that year. Out of turn. *It will not forget the taste of being fed ahead of schedule.* What did that mean? What did a hunger learn from an early meal, except that the schedule was soft, that the keepers could be hurried, that patience could be rewarded with impatience? He thought of Wren at the breakfast table that morning, syrup at the corner of her mouth, laughing at something on her spoon, and he thought: *she has been in this house for five weeks, and the room has had five weeks to learn the sound of her.*

He went back to the table. He opened the ledger to the twelfth line.

The blank one. The one addressed to *the inheritor.*

The paper there was different. He had noticed it before and told himself he hadn't: the blank line sat on a page that was faintly, subtly warmer to the touch than the pages around it, like a stone that had been sitting in sun. And the ruled line itself—the pencil line his grandmother had drawn for a name that was never written—seemed to Jonah, in the small hours, to be very slightly darker than the other ruled lines on the page. As though the pencil had wanted to press harder. As though the line itself were an appetite in miniature.

He put his palm flat on the page, over the blank line, the way you'd check a forehead for fever.

The paper was warm.

He snatched his hand back and sat there in the dark with his heart going like a fist on a door, and upstairs—or he would swear this later, and swear it sober, in daylight—something shifted. Not a footstep. Not a voice. A settling, the way a large animal settles, the way weight redistributes when a thing that has been lying still for a long time decides it might soon need to stand.

Six weeks.

* * *

Morning came gray and thin. Dana found him at the table with the ledger open and his eyes like two bruises, and she stood in the doorway in her robe with her arms crossed against the cold, and he watched her take in the scene—the book, the mug gone cold and skinned over with film, the state of him—and he watched her decide, visibly, deliberately, to be the reasonable one.

"You didn't sleep."

"No."

"Jonah." She came and sat across from him and put her hand flat on the table, not touching the book, an inch of oak between her skin and the pages, and he noticed that too—noticed that her hand had found its distance without being told. "Whatever you found, you tell me. No more of this. No more of you going pale and quiet and saying *it's nothing, Dana, go back to bed.* We're past that. We've been past that since the mirror."

So he told her. He walked her through it the way he'd walked himself through it—Elias Thorne, the twenty-year columns, the clean terrible arithmetic, the 1979 entry, the last page, the date. He heard his own voice go flat and procedural, a man reading a weather report for a storm that was going to hit his own house, and he watched Dana's face as the numbers landed on her. He watched her do what he had done: count the years, arrive at the sum, and then arrive, a half-second later, at the part that mattered.

"Her birthday," Dana said.

"Six weeks. Midnight. The count resumes."

"Resumes." Dana said the word like she was testing it for rot. "As in—it's been going on. All this time. The counting Wren hears. It's been—what, warming up?"

"I don't know."

"You don't know." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. When she took them away, her face was the face he had fallen in love with twenty years ago in a seminar on narrative structure, except that now it was the face of a woman doing the same arithmetic he had done, and arriving at the same sum, and refusing it. "Six weeks. We have six weeks to figure out how to unmake a—a debt, a covenant, a *thing* that's been fed children since 1931. Six weeks, and we don't even know what it is. We don't know what your grandmother actually agreed to. We don't know if there's any way out of it at all, or if the only choices are which name goes on the line."

"I know someone who might know," Jonah said. The words came out of him before he had decided to say them, and once they were out he understood they had been waiting in him for days. "Ruth Okonkwo. She told me to stop asking. She knew things, Dana. The way she said it—*stop asking*—that's not a person who's guessing. That's a person who's paid for the answer."

Dana looked at him for a long moment. Outside the window, the gray light was strengthening, and it fell across the ledger, across the eleven crossed-out names, and made the ink look fresh.

"Then go ask her," she said. "And Jonah—" she put her hand over his, finally, over the warm page, and did not flinch, and he loved her for it and was afraid for her because of it, "—whatever she tells you, whatever the price of knowing is, you come home and you tell me. All of it. We are not doing what this town did. We are not being the people who choose not to know."

He nodded. He closed the ledger, and the cover met the pages with a soft sound, like a mouth closing.

Upstairs, faintly, through the floor, through the joists and the plaster and the years, he heard Wren laugh at something—she was awake, she was in her parents' bed, she was telling Dana's pillow some elaborate story about a dream—and the sound went through him like a splinter going in, because it was such an ordinary sound, such a cheap and common miracle, a ten-year-old laughing in the morning, and because somewhere above her, patient as winter, something had already begun to count the days.

Six weeks.

The ledger sat on the table between them, closed, warm, and waiting.
