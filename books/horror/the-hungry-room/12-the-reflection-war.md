## 12. The Reflection War

The mirror in the hallway stopped being mine on a Tuesday.

I know it was Tuesday because that was spelling-test day, and I was standing in the hallway practicing *necessary* under my breath — *one c, two s's, like a little dress* — when I saw my reflection mouth a different word. Not *necessary*. Something longer. Something with too many teeth in it.

I stopped. The reflection kept going for half a second, like a video that hadn't been told to pause, and then it stopped too, and it looked at me, and it smiled.

I didn't smile.

That was the first time. After that it got worse, the way everything in this house gets worse, slow and patient, like water finding the crack in a foundation.

* * *

Mom says I have a good imagination. She says it the way other moms say *bless you* — automatic, kind, a little worn out. Dad says it too, but his voice does a thing when he says it, a little wobble, like a plate spinning on a stick that's about to tip. I've decided not to tell them things anymore. Not because they won't believe me. Because they *will*, and then they'll have to do something, and the something they do might be worse than the nothing we're all pretending is fine.

Here is what I know, and I'm writing it down so it stays true even when the mirrors say it doesn't:

My name is Wren Sandoval. I am nine. I turn ten in forty-one days, and the voice upstairs knows that number better than I do.

The voice is kind. That's the part nobody would believe. It doesn't growl or whisper like in movies. It sounds like a librarian reading to the little kids, that soft even voice that makes you feel like everything is going to be okay even if the book is sad. It counts. It's been counting since before we moved in, I think, counting for years and years in an empty house, waiting for someone to count *to*.

*Eighty-one,* it says at night, through the floor, through the walls, through the pillow if I press it over my ears. *Eighty-two. You're doing so well, Wren. Almost there.*

Almost where, I asked it once. Just once, in my head, because I didn't want to say it out loud where Dad could hear through my door.

*To the book,* it said. *All the children are safe in the book. Wouldn't you like to be safe?*

And here's the terrible thing, the thing I'll never tell anyone: for one second, I wanted to. Because safe sounded nice. Safe sounded like being tucked in and never having to be scared of anything again, not the dark, not school, not the way Mom and Dad look at each other now like two people reading the same bad letter at the same time.

Then I remembered the eleven desks at Dad's school. Eleven desks with nobody in them. Eleven names on a plaque that everybody in town walks past like it's a fire extinguisher, something on the wall you're supposed to know about but never look at.

Safe like that, I thought. Safe in a book like that.

No thank you, I told the voice. And it laughed, not mean, just *pleased*, like I'd said something clever, like I was a good student. *We'll see,* it said. *Eighty-three. Eighty-four.*

* * *

The reflection problem got worse on Thursday.

I was brushing my teeth in the bathroom — the little bathroom downstairs, because Mom had put a towel over the big mirror upstairs, one of her "drafts," she called it, like the house was cold instead of *wrong* — and I looked up, and my reflection was looking at me before I looked at it. Do you understand? I looked up and it was *already* looking. It had been waiting.

It didn't brush its teeth. It held up its hand — my hand, with the scab on the knuckle from the playground, so I knew it was supposed to be mine — and it pressed its palm flat against the glass. From the inside.

I ran. I left the water running and the toothbrush in the sink and I ran to my room and I didn't tell anybody, and that night the counting went up to sixty-one and the voice said, *Your reflection is lonely, Wren. It only wants to show you the book. Come look. Come stand where it's standing. It's warm in the book. It's safe in the book.*

I put my face under the blanket and did multiplication tables in my head until I fell asleep. Sevens are the best. They're too hard to be scared at the same time.

* * *

Friday morning I got the idea with the drawings.

It came from Mom, sort of. She'd taped paper over the bathroom mirror after the towel thing, and when I asked why, she said, "So it can't watch you," and then she did her spinning-plate laugh like it was a joke, but her hands were shaking when she tore the tape.

So it can't *watch* you. Okay. Okay, Mom. I can do that.

I spent all of art period — Ms. Alvarez doesn't mind, she thinks I'm going through an "intensive phase" — drawing the family. Four of us, even though there's only three, because in my pictures I always draw the extra one standing behind us, the one that's in the mirrors. I draw it gray. I draw it with its mouth open. If you draw a thing, my grandma — not the Merrow grandma, I never met her, but my other grandma, the nice one in Sacramento who smells like oranges — used to say, if you draw a thing you get to decide what it looks like. You get to be the boss of it.

So I drew us. Mom, Dad, me. And behind us, the gray one, and I drew a door on its chest, and I drew the door locked, and I drew a key, and I drew the key swallowed by a bird, because you can't unlock a door that's been eaten.

I taped the drawings over every mirror in the house. The hallway. The bathroom. The little round one in the entryway that shows only your face, which is the worst one, because the face one is where it smiles first. Dad's shaving mirror. Even the dark window in the stairwell, because at night the window is a mirror too, and I'd seen it there, my own face floating in the glass with the yard behind it all wrong.

Forty-one pieces of tape. I counted. I'm good at counting too, now. Everybody in this house is good at counting.

Dad found the drawings that evening. He stood in the hallway in front of the one over the big mirror — the family with the gray thing behind them — for a long time. So long I thought maybe he'd fallen asleep standing up, the way grown-ups sometimes almost do.

"Wren," he said. "Sweetheart. Come here a second."

I came. I always come. That's the rule when your dad says *come here a second*; you go, even if the second is going to be terrible.

"Did you draw these?"

"Yes."

"Why is there a fourth person?"

I looked at the drawing. The gray one's mouth in the picture was open wider than I remembered drawing it. I decided not to think about that. "That's the one in the mirrors," I said.

He crouched down to my level. His eyes were doing the thing where they're wet but he won't let them be. "There's no one in the mirrors, Wren."

"I know," I said. "That's why I covered them."

He didn't have anything to say to that. I watched him try. His mouth opened and closed like a fish, and then he hugged me, hard, too hard, the hug of a dad who's holding on to the wrong thing, and over his shoulder I saw the covered mirror, my drawing taped to the glass, and behind the paper — I could just see the edges, just the sliver where the tape hadn't caught — the glass was dark. Not reflecting-dark. *Looking*-dark.

*Eighty-eight,* said the voice, from everywhere, from the sliver, from the hallway, from under the floor where old houses keep their secrets. *Eighty-nine. The paper is very nice, Wren. But paper isn't glass. And glass isn't the book.*

* * *

Saturday I stopped looking at anything shiny.

This is harder than it sounds. The world is full of mirrors if you think about it. Windows. The toaster. Mom's good spoons. The TV when it's off, which is a big black mirror that sits in the living room like a sleeping eye. Water. Water is the worst, because water moves, and the thing in the water moves *with* it, and on Saturday morning I washed my hands and looked down and my reflection was washing its hands a full second after me, splash for splash but late, always late, like it was learning me from a recording, like it was practicing being Wren for something.

For what? For the book. For the day it gets to be the Wren that goes on living in the house, and I go in the book.

So: no water. I washed my hands with my eyes shut, which Mom thought was a game and played along, and Dad thought was a symptom and wrote something in his notebook, the little green one he thinks I don't know about. I drank from the fountain at school with my eyes on the ceiling tiles. I stopped looking at the TV, the spoons, the dark of the stairwell window. I learned the geography of the house by its matte things: the carpet, the ceiling, the backs of my hands.

Mom noticed first. Moms always notice first; they just don't always say it first.

"Sweetheart, you need to look at people when they talk to you."

"I look at your chin," I said. "Chins are fine. Chins aren't shiny."

She laughed, and then she didn't, and then she sat down on my bed and asked me, very gently, the way you ask a question you already know the answer to and are hoping to be lied to: "Wren. What do you see when you look in the mirrors?"

And I almost told her. I opened my mouth and the whole thing was right there — the hand on the glass, the late water, the word with too many teeth — and then I heard the voice, faint, from upstairs, from behind the closed door at the end of the hall, the room nobody opens, the room that counts:

*Ninety-one.*

And I understood something. I understood that the voice wanted me to tell. It *wanted* it. It wanted me to say it out loud in this house, where the walls are ears, because saying a thing out loud in this house is like signing it. Dad says words are just words. But the house doesn't think so. The house thinks words are *doors*.

So I lied to my mother. First real lie of my life, and I made it a good one.

"I see me," I said. "Just me. I'm just tired of my face."

She hugged me and said I was the bravest kid she knew, which was funny, because I was being the biggest coward, and also the smartest, and nobody in this family knows the difference anymore.

* * *

Sunday night the reflection stopped pretending.

I got up for water at midnight — not to drink, just to hold the glass in the dark kitchen, because holding it with my eyes shut felt like winning — and when I passed the entryway, the little round mirror, I heard my own voice say my name.

"Wren."

Not the counting voice. *My* voice. Out of the mirror. My drawing was still taped over it, forty-one pieces of tape, and the voice came through the paper anyway, muffled, like a kid talking through a pillow.

"Wren. Look. Just look. I have something to show you."

"Go away," I whispered. My chin was doing the crying wobble and I made it stop. *Don't let me be a baby about it,* I told myself. Babies get taken. Babies get *kept safe*.

"It's your mom," the voice said. My voice, saying it, which is how I knew it was lying, because my voice would never say a thing like that, my voice knows better. "She's standing in the book right now. She's waiting for you. She said she'd only wait so long."

"My mom is asleep," I said. "I checked."

A pause. Then the voice dropped the disguise, all at once, like a coat falling off, and it was the librarian voice again, warm and patient and bottomless.

*You're clever,* it said. *The other children weren't clever. That's why they're in the book and you're still out here. Ninety-four. Ninety-five. But clever only delays, Wren. It doesn't cancel. The count doesn't care how brave you are. The count only cares that it finishes. Ninety-six.*

And then — this is the part I'll remember when I'm old, if I get to be old — the paper over the mirror moved. Not blew. Not shifted. *Bulged*, outward, in the middle, slow, the way a balloon bulges when you press a finger behind it. Like something on the other side of the glass was leaning its face against the back of my drawing. My drawing of the family with the locked door and the eaten key.

I went back to bed. I didn't run. I want that written down too: I walked, one foot and then the other, past the bulging paper, past the whispering, and I got in bed, and I pulled the covers to my chin, and I did my sevens until the counting faded toward morning.

*Ninety-nine,* it said, just before I slept. *Almost a hundred, Wren. Nobody's ever made it to a hundred out here. Be the first. Or be in the book. One of those things is going to happen.*

* * *

Monday morning I heard Dad on the phone in the kitchen, before anyone was up, his voice low and rough the way it's been getting.

"I don't care if it's a school night," he said. "I need to talk to her. Ruth. *Ruth.* You were there. You know what happened in this town, and I think — I think my daughter knows something too, and she's not telling us, and I need to know what you know."

A long pause. Through the door I watched his back, his chin, the matte back of his head, safe to look at.

"Then tell me what your mother did," he said. "Tell me what was traded."

I went back upstairs and got my green crayon and the last clean page of my drawing pad, and I drew the kitchen, and Dad on the phone, and me in the doorway, and behind all of us, gray, mouth open, the one from the mirrors.

Then I drew the book. The book the voice keeps talking about. And I drew what I think is inside it, which is eleven desks in a row, and eleven kids sitting at them, very still, all facing forward, all waiting.

And at the front of the room, in my drawing, I drew a twelfth desk. Empty. With a name tag.

I didn't write a name on it. I'm not stupid. You don't sign things in this house.

But the voice knew anyway. That night, from behind the closed door, soft as a lullaby, it counted *one hundred* — and then, for the first time, it didn't stop. It kept going, past the number it had never passed, climbing slow and happy into the dark, and under the counting, almost too quiet to hear, it was saying my name.
