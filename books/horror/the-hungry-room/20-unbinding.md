## 20. Unbinding

**Jonah**

The door at the top of the stairs was open, and it had never been open.

Jonah stood in the hallway with the ledger pressed against his chest like a shield that had never once worked, and he understood, in the marrow-deep way you understand things in the last minutes of a life's old shape, that the house had been holding its breath for forty-six years and was now exhaling. The air that came down the stairwell smelled of cold earth and old paper and something sweeter underneath, something like the inside of a violin, like varnish and grief.

Behind him, Dana held Wren by both shoulders. He didn't look back. If he looked back he would see his daughter's face, and he had spent six weeks learning that his courage lived entirely in not looking.

"Say it loud," Wren said behind him. Her voice was small and steady, the voice she used for spelling tests. "The room likes quiet. Don't be nice about it."

He almost laughed. He almost wept. He climbed the stairs.

The room at the end of the hall — the one that had been locked since 1979, the one whose key his grandmother had worn on a chain around her neck until the day she died — stood with its door wide onto darkness. Not the darkness of an unlit space. The darkness of a mouth. The wallpaper inside was patterned with tiny birds, and in the light of the flashlight he could see that every bird faced inward, toward the center of the room, as if they'd been trying to warn each other for a century.

He stepped over the threshold.

The counting was already going. It had been going all night, climbing through the nineties while Wren sat downstairs with her eyes squeezed shut and her hands over her ears, refusing, refusing, refusing. Now the numbers came from everywhere — from the walls, from under the floorboards, from the mirror bolted inside the closet door, which showed him not himself but a tall thin absence wearing his posture.

*One hundred four,* said the room, in a voice like a kind teacher, like a patient parent, like something that had learned tenderness the way a trap learns the shape of the animal.

Jonah opened the ledger. The pages fell where they always fell: eleven names crossed out in his grandmother's careful hand, and beneath them the twelfth line, blank, waiting, addressed in faded ink to *the inheritor.*

"I'm here," he said. "I'm the inheritor. And I'm renouncing."

*One hundred five.*

"This house was built by a coward," Jonah said, louder, because the walls began to hum, because the birds on the wallpaper seemed to shiver. "Elias Merrow found something hungry and instead of facing it, he gave it a room. He gave it children. He made his fear into architecture and called it keeping safe—"

The door slammed shut behind him.

The dark went total, and then the mirrors lit — every reflective surface in the room glowing faintly, silver-white, and in them Jonah saw his family. Dana at the kitchen table, head down, asleep or worse. Wren standing at the foot of the stairs, looking up at him with an expression of such perfect, terrible patience that his heart stopped and restarted wrong.

*One hundred nine.* His grandmother's voice now, coming out of the closet mirror, out of 1979, out of the grave. *Jonah. Sweetheart. You don't have to do this. I kept the count so you'd never have to know it existed. Let me keep it a little longer. Let me be the coward. It's what I'm for.*

"You're dead," Jonah said, and his voice cracked down the middle. "You've been dead forty years, and it's been wearing you like a coat."

*It kept me,* she said, and there was such longing in it, such human, awful longing. *It keeps them all. Would you take even that away?*

"Yes," Jonah said. "That's exactly what I'm going to do."

And upstairs, in the dark, alone, he began to say the words Ruth Okonkwo's mother had written down in a shaking hand half a century ago: *I, Jonah Sandoval, of the blood of Merrow, refuse the inheritance. I refuse the count. I refuse the covenant sealed in love and cowardice. This debt is not mine and I will not pay it and I will not let my child pay it—*

The room screamed.

Not metaphorically. The walls screamed, the floor screamed, the mirrors screamed in eleven small voices at once, and the temperature dropped so fast his breath came out in white ribbons. Something vast turned over in the dark beneath the floor — something that had been sleeping curled around the foundations since before the town had a name — and it woke up hungry, and it woke up free, and it woke up looking for the nearest blood.

Which was him.

* * *

**Dana**

The hearth fire was dying when Dana knelt in front of it with the ledger's loose pages in her arms, and Wren knelt beside her with the box of matches, and neither of them looked at the windows, where their own reflections were pressing against the glass like children late for something.

Ruth's notes lay open on the rug, transcribed in Dana's own handwriting because her hands shook too badly to trust with the original:

*The room is the hunger, but the ledger is the count. Burn the count and the hunger has nothing left to hold. But it must be burning while the renunciation is spoken — the two acts are one act. Neither works alone. That is why no one has ever done it. No one has ever been brave enough to do both halves at once, in different rooms, trusting each other absolutely.*

Trusting each other absolutely. Dana thought about the mirror lag, the five seconds, the footage of reflections mouthing numbers. She thought about Jonah hiding what he knew to protect her, and her hiding this plan to protect him, and how love kept curdling into secrecy whenever it got scared enough.

Not tonight. Tonight they did it in the open, split apart like a wishbone, each holding half the miracle.

"Mom." Wren struck a match. The flame stood perfectly still in the draftless room, which was wrong, everything was wrong, the whole house was holding still to listen. "What if it doesn't work?"

"Then it doesn't work," Dana said, "and we'll have been brave together anyway, and I will spend whatever time we get being proud of you. Strike it."

Wren struck it against the box and touched it to the first page, the one with Elias Merrow's name at the top in ink gone brown as dried blood.

The paper caught — and the house caught with it.

Every light in Hollow Creek flickered. Down the street, dogs began to howl, and in the Merrow house the mirrors in every room bloomed silver and began, all of them, all at once, to scream.

*One hundred eighteen,* said the walls. *One hundred nineteen.*

"It knows," Wren whispered. "Mom, it knows what we're doing."

"Good," Dana said, feeding page after page into the hearth, watching eleven names curl and blacken, watching twenty years of quiet burn down to ash with each one. "Let it know. Let it be afraid for once. It's had a hundred and fifty years of everyone else being afraid."

The fire roared up green, then white, then a color Dana had no name for, and the heat coming off it was cold, and the smoke rose straight down into the chimney instead of up, and none of that mattered, none of it mattered, because the names were burning, and the count was losing its place.

*One hundred twenty-one. One hundred twenty-two. One hundred—*

The number stuttered.

*One hundred twen—*

The room upstairs shrieked like a kettle, like a train braking, like a woman in 1979 watching her grandchild's name begin to write itself.

"Wren," Dana said, taking her daughter's hand and putting the next page into it, making her burn it herself, making it hers, "say goodbye to your reflection."

* * *

**Wren**

Wren had spent six weeks not looking at herself, and now, in the last minute, she looked.

The hallway mirror had fallen off the wall and lay face-up on the floor, and in it she saw the eleven children. Not ghosts, exactly. Held things. They stood in a row behind her reflected self, close together the way kids stand in a class photo, and they were all looking at her, and the smallest one — a boy with a cowlick, from 1953, from the plaque at school — raised his hand like he had a question.

She couldn't hear him. But she knew what he was asking. *Is it over? Are we done counting?*

"Almost," Wren said out loud, and her breath made fog on the mirror's surface, and through the fog she watched the numbers in the glass — floating there now, everywhere, in the corners, in the grain of the wood — start to come apart. *One hundred thirty* became *one hundred three-ty* became *one hun* became nothing, just smoke written in a language being forgotten.

Her father's voice came through the ceiling, ragged and human and magnificent: *"—and I will not let my child pay it, and I will not pay it, and I REFUSE—"*

The mirror cracked from corner to corner.

Through the crack, Wren saw the room upstairs — saw it truly for the first time, saw the hunger uncoiling off the bed and out of the walls, a shape like smoke, like water going down a drain in reverse, like every unfinished lullaby at once — and she saw it turn, all of its attention, all of its terrible patience, toward the nearest blood.

Toward Daddy.

"No," Wren said. She picked up the biggest piece of broken mirror — it weighed nothing, it weighed everything — and she ran for the stairs, because the notes had said the two acts were one act, and her mother was burning the count, and someone had to make sure the hunger heard the ending.

* * *

**Jonah**

It came for him as a coldness with teeth.

There was no other way to hold it in a mind shaped like a mind. The unbound hunger poured up through the floorboards of the little room and wrapped around him, and it was not angry — anger would have been easier — it was *grateful*, the way a starving man is grateful, the way a flood is grateful for the valley, and it began to drink.

He felt it take his warmth first. Then his weight. Then, horribly, his face — he watched his own expression drain out of him like color out of a photograph, and in the closet mirror the thing wearing his posture smiled with his smile and stepped forward out of the silver—

And stopped.

Because below, in the hearth, the last page caught fire. The blank page. The twelfth line. The inheritor's line, burning blank, burning unwritten, and the count hit its final number —

*One hundred forty-four*

— and lost its voice mid-word, and the silence that followed was the loudest sound Jonah had ever heard.

The hunger convulsed. Without the count it had no shape; without the ledger it had no leash and no anchor and no *name*, and it thrashed through the room like a storm trapped in a bottle, and Jonah, kneeling on the floor with most of himself already drunk away, did the only thing left.

He opened his arms.

"Come on, then," he whispered. "You want blood? I'm the blood. Take me instead of the sentence. Take me instead of the book. But you don't get her. You don't get any of them. Ever again."

The door burst open and Wren stood in it, silhouetted against the hall light, holding a shard of mirror like a sword, and behind her came Dana, and behind Dana came the smell of woodsmoke and the sound of the last of the ledger turning to ash, and the hunger — the great gray starving centuries-old hunger — looked at all three of them, the whole family, the whole impossible stubborn loving family, and understood that it had lost.

It collapsed inward.

The room folded like a hand closing. The mirrors went first — every one in the house shattering in sequence, top floor to bottom, a long rippling crack of applause — and then the holding-places, the somewhere the eleven children had been kept, opening like doors, releasing like breath. Jonah felt it pass through him on its way down, down, down, into the earth, into whatever waits underneath houses and towns and histories, and it took payment as it went, because everything in Hollow Creek took payment.

It took his reflection.

He felt it leave — a coolness sliding off his skin like a coat removed by gentle hands — and he heard it shatter somewhere far below with all the other mirrors, and then the room was just a room. Wallpaper with little birds. A window showing ordinary night. Dust.

Jonah Sandoval lay on the floor of the hungry room, alive, emptied, and his daughter threw her arms around him, and his wife knelt down and held them both, and upstairs in the ashes of the ledger the eleventh name finished curling into nothing, and somewhere across town, in eleven houses, eleven clocks that had run slow for decades suddenly, quietly, began keeping perfect time.

In the hallway below, the last shard of mirror lay face-up on the floorboards, reflecting the staircase, the wall, the front door.

Reflecting everything.

Except him.
