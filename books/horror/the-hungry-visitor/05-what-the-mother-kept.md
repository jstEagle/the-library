## 5. What the Mother Kept

The phone found me at 6:47 in the evening, which was suspicious in itself, because my mother called on Sundays at two o'clock and never deviated. Thirty years of Sundays at two. She had once scheduled a root canal around it. So when my phone lit up with her name on a Thursday evening while I stood in the kitchen of the Holloway house eating a vending-machine dinner over the sink, I felt the way you feel when a clock chimes thirteen — not that something is wrong with the time, but that something is wrong with everything.

I answered on the fourth ring, which is what you do when you want to seem casual and also need three seconds to become casual.

"Hi, Mom."

"Theo." Her voice did something I'd never heard it do. It cracked. Not the polite crack of an aging throat clearing itself, but a structural failure, like ice giving way underfoot. "Theo, are you still there? At that house."

"I'm finishing up the appraisal. Couple more days."

"Leave tonight."

"Mom—"

"Leave *tonight*, Theo." And then she said the thing. The thing she had not said aloud since I was nine years old, standing dripping on the bank of Miller's Creek in a too-big funeral suit, watching men lower a small white box into the ground.

She said, "Mei would not have wanted you in that house."

The vending machine pretzel went dry in my mouth. I set it down on the counter next to my clipboard, which now lived on every horizontal surface of this house like a security blanket with a clipboard's self-esteem.

"What did you say?"

"You heard me." Her voice had steadied into something worse than cracking: it had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone walking across a frozen pond who has just heard the first sound of splitting. "You heard me perfectly well."

I looked out the kitchen window at the yard going blue with dusk. Somewhere behind me, deeper in the house, I could have sworn I heard humming. But then, I'd been hearing humming all day, hadn't I. Humming and measuring tape retracting when nobody touched it, and footsteps upstairs in rooms I hadn't walked through yet, and once — I told myself I imagined this — a child's laugh from inside a wall, muffled and delighted, like someone playing hide-and-seek with the drywall.

"Mom," I said carefully, "you haven't said her name in thirty years."

Silence. Long enough that I checked whether the call had dropped. The little timer kept counting. On the windowsill, the frost I'd noticed that morning — in July, mind you, frost in July — had grown another fern of crystals overnight, and I made a note to mention moisture intrusion in the report. Section four. Water damage. There was always a section for water damage. There was no section for whatever this was.

"I said her name," my mother finally said, "because I am asking you to do one thing for me in your entire life, and I have never asked you for anything, Theo, not once, not even when your father left, not even when—" She stopped. Restarted. "Please. Get in your car. Drive to the end of the street. Call me back from there and tell me you're safe, and I will never bring it up again. We can go back to normal."

Normal. Our normal. Our normal was Sunday calls at two o'clock in which we discussed her garden, my work, the weather, and the price of things, in that order, forever, a carousel that never stopped turning because neither of us ever reached for the brass ring marked *Before*. Our normal was a house where the third bedroom had been converted to a sewing room within a month of the funeral, so fast it was almost impressive, as if grief were a stain and bleach were a substitute for mourning. Our normal was my mother never once saying her daughter's name, and me never once asking why, and both of us pretending this silence was peace rather than a held breath thirty years long.

And here she was, exhaling.

"Why now?" I asked. "What do you know about this house?"

Another silence, but a different texture. This one had weight and shape, like something being moved carefully across a floor.

"It's the Holloway house," she said, as if that explained anything.

"It's a condemned property with good bones and a motivated buyer. It's a job, Mom. I've done a hundred of them."

"Not this one." A breath. "Theo, listen to me. When you were small — before — you used to talk about a friend. An imaginary friend. Wren."

The name landed in my chest like a dropped piano. I gripped the edge of the sink.

"That was a long time ago."

"What did Wren look like?"

"Mom, why are you—"

"*What did Wren look like?*"

I closed my eyes. And there it was, unbidden, undimmed by three decades: a small figure on the porch steps of my childhood, legs swinging, feet never quite touching the boards. Hair the color of creek water over stones. Eyes I could never quite fix on directly, the way you can't look straight at a star. A smile that was always slightly too pleased about something.

"Small," I said. "Smaller than me. Pale. Kind of... washed out, like a photograph left in the sun. Why?"

My mother made a sound I had no category for. Not a sob. Something older than sobbing. A sound like a door opening after thirty years of rust.

"Because I saw him too," she whispered.

The refrigerator in the Holloway kitchen hummed. Or the walls hummed. Or something hummed. In this house I was losing my ability to attribute sounds to appliances.

"When you were six," she said, "you came downstairs one night and told me Wren was hungry and couldn't come inside until somebody sang to him. And I laughed, because you were six, and children say things. But Theo — I had already seen him. Standing at the edge of the yard. Watching the house. Watching *you*. For weeks. I told myself it was a neighbor child. I told myself a lot of things." Her breath shuddered out of her. "Your grandmother grew up four streets from here. She wouldn't come past the property line. She said the Holloways sang. That's all she ever said about them, and she said it the way people talk about the dead. *They sang.*"

"Mom, this is—"

"Don't." Sharp now. "Don't you dare be reasonable with me. I have been reasonable for thirty years. I have been so reasonable that I buried my own daughter without saying her name, and if being unreasonable for five minutes saves my son, then I will be unreasonable until I die."

There it was again. Mei. Twice in one phone call. More mentions than the previous three decades combined. Each time she said it, something in the house seemed to lean closer. I'm aware of how that sounds. I was standing in a condemned house at dusk talking to my mother about an imaginary friend, and the rational part of me — the part that measured hallways and noted sagging porches and wrote *moisture intrusion, section four* — that part was screaming that I was tired, that I was dehydrated, that vending-machine dinners are not food, that grief and suggestion and bad lighting can do wonders.

But the rational part of me had also counted eleven doors in a house whose blueprints showed ten, and had watched a hallway grow four feet longer between measurements taken twenty minutes apart, and had heard my own childhood song coming off the porch in a voice that had not aged a single day since 1994.

Rationality, I've found, is less a fortress than a scaffolding. You keep building it, and the building underneath keeps doing what it does.

"Tell me what happened," I said. "That night. At the creek. Tell me again."

"We've been over it a thousand times—"

"Indulge me."

A pause. Then, recitation-voice, the version worn smooth by repetition: "You and Mei went down to Miller's Creek after supper. You came back alone, soaked, hysterical. The police searched. They found her downstream two days later. It was an accident. The bank was slick. She slipped." Beat. "It was an accident, Theo."

She said it twice. People only repeat themselves when they're trying to convince someone, and usually that someone is themselves.

"That's the story," I said.

"That's the truth."

"That's the story, Mom. I was nine. My entire knowledge of that night comes from you and from a police report I wasn't allowed to read. So when you say truth, what I hear is: that's what we decided."

"Theo—"

"Because here's what I remember." My voice came out low and strange, and I let it. "I remember being at the creek. I remember singing. Wren and I had this song, and we always stopped before the last verse, and that day I don't know why but I kept going, or I wanted to, and Mei — Mom, I think Mei followed us. I think she was there. And I have spent thirty years with a hole in that memory shaped exactly like my sister, and you have spent thirty years making sure nobody ever looked at the hole."

The line went so quiet I could hear her breathing, shallow and fast, and beneath it — I swear this, I swear it on my clipboard and my professional reputation — beneath it, faintly, from somewhere in the Holloway house, a second breathing. Small. Patient. Keeping time with hers.

"You were a child," my mother said at last, barely audible. "You were a baby. Whatever happened, it was not yours to carry."

"Then whose was it?"

Silence.

"Whose was it, Mom?"

"Come home," she said, and her voice broke clean through, and I heard thirty years land in it all at once, all the Sundays at two o'clock, all the names unsaid, all the sewing room bleach. "Please. Come home and we'll figure out what to carry together. Just don't finish whatever you started in that house. Don't sing anything. Do you hear me? Don't—"

She stopped herself. Too late. Way too late. You can't unring a bell and you can't unsay a warning, and *don't sing anything* is not a sentence a mother arrives at naturally. That is a sentence you learn. That is a sentence someone taught her, or something showed her, or some night thirty years ago she stood at a window and watched and understood exactly what her son's little song was for and what it cost to leave it unfinished.

"How do you know about the singing?" I asked softly.

The breathing on the line quickened. The breathing in the hallway matched it.

"Mrs. Okafor still lives across the street," my mother said finally, each word placed like a foot on thin ice. "If you won't leave, talk to her. She knew the Holloways. She knew..." A swallow. "She knew Mei. Better than I let anyone know, back then. She tried to tell me things, after. I didn't listen. I chose not to listen, Theo, and I have paid for it every single day in a currency I can't describe, and I would pay it forever rather than watch you—"

Her voice dissolved. Not into static. Into weeping, quiet and terrible, the sound of a woman who had done her crying exclusively in private for three decades and was now doing it in front of her son because the alternative was worse.

"Mom." I pressed my forehead against the cool window glass. The frost ferned right up to my skin, delicate as lace, cold as a confession. "I'm going to stay. I have to stay. I think... I think she's here. I think part of her has been here the whole time, waiting for somebody to come back and finish listening."

"Theo, no—"

"If I'm wrong, I lose a couple of days and my dignity. If you're right — if either of us is right — then leaving means leaving her in the walls alone for another thirty years. And I can't do that. I did it once already by accident. I'm not doing it twice on purpose."

She cried for a while. I let her. Outside, the last light drained off the yard, and the porch light — which had no bulb; I'd checked; I'd written it up in section seven, electrical — the porch light came on anyway, soft and yellow, illuminating the empty steps where earlier a small pale figure had sat swinging legs that never touched the boards.

"The last verse," my mother whispered, when she could speak. "Theo, whatever you do. However it ends. Don't sing it with them."

"I know, Mom."

"You don't know. You can't know. But Mrs. Okafor might. Go see her. Tomorrow. First light." A wet, broken laugh. "Listen to me. Sending my son to the neighbors like he's nine years old again."

"Some things you never stop doing," I said.

"No," she agreed. "Some things you never stop." And then, so quietly I almost missed it, almost lost it under the hum of the refrigerator and the hum of the walls and the hum of the thing on the stairs that was definitely, definitely humming our song: "I loved her, Theo. I need you to know that, whatever else. Whatever I did or didn't do. I loved her every day I didn't say her name."

"I know," I said. And then, because loyalty is a strange animal and it was straining hard against its leash: "Say it again."

A long breath. Then, steady, deliberate, a woman setting down something heavy she'd carried so long her hands had forgotten their shape:

"Mei. My daughter Mei."

Behind me, at the top of the dark staircase, the humming stopped.

In its place, very faint, from deep inside the walls — a child's voice, rusty as an unused hinge, trying out a single word like someone testing whether the water's warm:

"...Mama?"

I hung up the phone with shaking hands and stood alone in the kitchen of the Holloway house, and I made a decision that would have horrified the man who'd driven out here that morning with his clipboard and his section-four moisture notes.

Tomorrow, first light, I was going across the street.

But tonight — tonight I was going to stand at the bottom of those stairs, and I was going to say my sister's name out loud, and I was going to find out what answered.
