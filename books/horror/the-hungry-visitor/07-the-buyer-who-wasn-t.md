## 7. The Buyer Who Wasn't

I woke up on the Holloway house's parlor floor with my neck at an angle that would've cost me four hundred dollars at a chiropractor and a frost-print of my own hand on the window beside me, as if I'd been sleepwalking and pressing my palms against the glass all night, taking attendance.

I didn't remember lying down. That was the part I kept circling back to while I sat up, cracked my vertebrae like knuckles, and told myself a series of increasingly confident lies. *You were tired. You've been working double shifts. Appraisals are sedentary work but the hours are brutal.* The lies had the smooth professional texture of a cover letter. I'd gotten good at them in the last twenty-four hours, the way you get good at anything with enough repetition.

The clipboard was still on the coffee table where I'd left it. Eleven rooms measured. Eleven rooms that did not agree with the exterior of the house on basic questions of size, the way a witness doesn't agree with the security footage.

I checked my phone. Two bars, which felt generous. A text from Dana at the office, sent at 6:14 a.m.: *Buyer's agent coming at 9. Marisol Vega, Vega Property Group. Play nice, he wants to close before winter. Also why are all your timestamps from inside the house after 8pm, did you sleep there??*

I typed back: *Oversaw overnight moisture readings. Diligence.*

Then I deleted it, because even my lies were developing a sense of shame, and typed: *Yes. Report to follow.*

* * *

Marisol Vega arrived at 9:02 in a white SUV so clean it looked like it had never encountered weather, which in this county was either a miracle or a lie. She got out wearing a camel coat and a smile that had been fitted professionally, and she shook my hand with a grip that was warm and dry and entirely unremarkable, and I remember thinking, with the clarity of a man who has already lost an argument with himself: *nothing about this woman is going to be remarkable, and that is the remarkable thing.*

"Mr. Zhou." Her voice was bright, pleasant, the voice of someone narrating an open house to themselves. "Sorry about the early call. Mr. Ashford wants to move fast. These properties, you know how it is — you have to catch them before they get ideas."

"Get ideas," I repeated.

"You know. Sit on the market. Start telling stories about themselves." She laughed, a small polished laugh, and walked past me onto the porch without looking at it, which — after last night — struck me as either tremendous discipline or tremendous ignorance. The porch boards had been humming faintly when I opened the door. Not the song. Just a hum, like a refrigerator with opinions.

"Eleven rooms?" she asked, producing her own tablet.

"Twelve," I said, before I could stop myself.

"Listing says eleven."

"The listing is wrong."

She smiled at me the way you smile at a man who insists the elevator is haunted, and we went inside.

* * *

Here is what I noticed, and I want to be fair to myself here, because for about forty minutes I noticed everything and did nothing, which is the Zhou family specialty:

Marisol Vega walked through the Holloway house the way water walks through a pipe. She went room to room, tapping her tablet, saying things like "load-bearing wall comes out here" and "kitchen's a gut job but the bones are good" and "oh, the light in this one," and the house let her. That's the phrase my mind kept offering, unhelpfully: *the house let her.* The hallways that had stretched for me stayed obediently short. The doors stayed at a civil number. The hum in the walls dropped so low I could almost pretend it was the furnace, if the furnace had been installed in 1890 and fed on something other than oil.

She didn't shiver. The house was fifty degrees inside and she didn't shiver once. Her breath didn't fog. I watched for it — I want that on the record — I stood behind her in Mei's old room, which the listing called Bedroom Three, and I watched her mouth for the little ghost of breath that every living person makes in a cold room, and there was nothing. Her lips just moved, saying "primary bedroom, en-suite potential," and the air between her and me stayed perfectly, photographically still.

"You're not cold?" I asked.

"I run warm," she said, and smiled, and her teeth were very even, and I understood in the way you understand a word you've stared at too long — technically, completely, without it meaning anything — that she was the coldest thing in the house.

"Mr. Ashford is thinking a six-month flip," she continued, drifting down the hallway. "Cosmetic on the front rooms, full demo in the back. He's done four like this. They turn over so fast you barely—" She stopped at the thirteenth door.

I should explain. There were eleven doors when I'd counted at noon yesterday. Twelve by evening. Thirteen sometime in the dark, each new one opening onto my childhood bedroom, with its water-stained ceiling and its window facing the creek, and I had stopped counting out loud because I'd noticed the house seemed to count *with* me, adding one for every number I failed to say.

The thirteenth door stood at the end of the hall where no door had any architectural right to be, and it was open, and behind it was not my childhood bedroom. Behind it was a stairwell going down into a dark that had texture, the way deep water has texture.

Marisol looked at it and said, "Basement access, nice," and walked in.

"Ma'am," I said. "That's not on the plans."

"Neither's the profit margin, but here we are." She descended four steps, five, her camel coat drinking the dark, and turned back to me with the smile. "You coming?"

And here's the thing — some animal part of me, some part that predates language and appraisals, took one look at that stairwell and said *if you follow her down there, you will be following her down there for a very long time.* So I said, "I'll finish the exterior measurements," which is what a coward says in professional dialect, and she said, "Sure, whatever," and kept going down, and I stood in the hallway listening to her heels on the stairs — tap, tap, tap — getting fainter and fainter, and then not fainter, exactly, but *flatter*, like the sound of heels on stairs being played through a speaker in another room, and then like a memory of heels on stairs, and then nothing at all.

I gave it ninety seconds. Then I went to the top of the stairwell and called down, "Ms. Vega?"

The dark said nothing. The dark had a smell, though — creek water and cold stone and something faintly sweet, like flowers that had been underwater a long time.

"Ms. Vega, I'm going to pull the exterior measurements."

Nothing. I told myself she'd found a back way out, a cellar door, that she was already in the yard inspecting the foundation, that people who flip houses for a living know exits the way I know door counts. I went out the front and around the side of the house with my tape measure like a man performing normalcy for an audience of one very patient building.

The yard was empty. No white SUV. No Marisol Vega. No cellar door. The grass showed no footprints, and the frost — which had come early and hard this year, the frost that had been writing names on the windows all night — lay over the lawn unbroken from the porch to the tree line, a clean white page.

I stood there with my tape measure extended to nothing and did the math I'd been avoiding. A woman had walked into a house. A woman had not walked out. Her car was gone from the driveway, which meant either she'd left — impossible, I'd have seen it, the driveway is thirty feet from where I stood — or it had never been there at all, which raised a question about what exactly had shaken my hand at 9:02 with its warm dry grip.

Behind me, from inside the house, faint and contented, the walls hummed the first two verses of a song I knew.

* * *

I went back inside because — and I've thought about this — because there was nowhere else to go. That's the honest version. Not bravery, not investigation. The driveway felt like a stage set and the road felt like a suggestion and my car sat at the curb where I'd parked it, and when I got in it and turned the key, the engine turned over fine and the radio played fine and the car simply declined to go faster than a walking pace no matter what I asked of it, so I drove it back into the driveway at four miles an hour like a man returning a rental he'd been talked out of, and I sat there, and then I went inside, because inside was where my paperwork was.

My paperwork. God. Let me tell you about my paperwork.

The clipboard was on the coffee table. Under it, my folder — the standard appraisal packet, the one I'd printed at the office, the one with my letterhead and the property photos and the signature line at the bottom reading *Appraiser: Theo Zhou* above a line of crisp, empty, professional white space.

Except it wasn't empty.

My signature sat there. My actual signature — the lazy *Z*, the *T* I never cross, the little downward flick at the end like a man in a hurry — rendered in blue ink, dated yesterday, notarized even, with a notary stamp I did not own and a notary name I did not know: *R. Holloway.*

I sat down on the parlor floor — I seemed to be doing my best work down there lately — and went through the rest of the folder page by page with the care of a bomb technician.

Page two: my measurements, all of them, corrected. Every room now agreed with every other room and with the exterior. The house, on paper, was exactly as big as the house. It was the most boring, accurate, defensible appraisal I had ever seen, and it was in my handwriting, and I had not written it.

Page three: a comparables list. Four nearby sales, all closed, all plausible — except I knew 14 Larkspur had been on the market for two years, and I knew the Hollis place had burned down in March, and I knew this because I'd pulled the comps myself, and the comps I'd pulled said *no comparable sales. Property is an outlier.* The house had done my job for me and gotten a different answer, the way a student copies a smarter kid's homework and changes the name.

Page four was a photograph of the house taken from the street, the standard hero shot. In the upstairs window, second from the left, a small figure stood watching the camera. The figure was out of focus, the way things in motion are out of focus, though everything else in the photograph was sharp as a blade.

I knew the shape of that figure the way you know the shape of your own hand. I had invented it when I was six years old, out of loneliness and an overactive ear for rhyme, and I had abandoned it at nine, on the night of the creek, and it had not aged a day, because it was not the kind of thing that ages. It was the kind of thing that *waits*.

"Wren," I said, to the empty parlor.

The hum in the walls stopped. That was worse. The silence had a listening quality to it, the silence of a held breath, of an animal that has heard its name and is deciding whether to come.

"I need you to tell me what happened to the woman."

Nothing. Then, from the top of the basement stairwell — the stairwell Marisol Vega had descended and not ascended — footsteps. Small, light, unhurried. Not heels. Bare feet, or feet that had decided to sound like bare feet because bare feet are what children have and what I would trust.

Wren stood in the doorway of the parlor, and I want to describe this accurately, because accuracy matters now: Wren looked exactly as Wren had always looked. Small. Seven or eight, forever. A face like a sketch that had been gone over too many times in the same places — the eyes a little too dark, the smile a little too wide, the whole thing *almost* right in the way a word stops looking right. Wren wore the same gray sweater I'd imagined onto it thirty years ago, and the sweater was not worn, not faded, not moth-eaten, because time, as established, did not apply.

"You signed," Wren said.

"I didn't sign anything."

"You signed." Wren came into the room and sat on the piano bench, the way Wren used to sit, hands in lap, patient as furniture. "The house just wrote it down for you. It does that. It's helpful." Wren said *helpful* the way you'd say *hungry*.

"I'm leaving," I said. I stood up. My knees objected. "I'm going to get in my car and drive to the office and file a report, and the police are going to come out here, and—"

"And say what?" Wren asked, and it wasn't cruel. That was the horror of it. It was gentle. It was the tone Wren had used to talk me out of nightmares when I was seven, the reasonable, loving, slightly tired voice of a best friend explaining the obvious. "That the house has thirteen doors? That the lady walked downstairs and didn't come back up? Theo. You signed the appraisal. Your name is on the house now. It's been on the house since you were nine." A tilt of the head, birdlike, tender. "You just didn't know what kind of paper it was written on."

The frost on the parlor window had grown new letters overnight. I didn't want to read them. I read them. Ferning across the glass in that spidery, botanical hand, beneath the dozens of names it had already collected, one new entry, fresh, the frost still bright and wet around it:

*M. VEGA.*

"You can't keep people," I said. My voice did something embarrassing in the middle of the sentence.

"We don't keep anyone," Wren said. "They stay. There's a difference. You'll see." And then, because Wren had always known exactly how to gut me, exactly where the soft place was, it smiled the old smile — my smile, the one I'd made up, the one I'd needed — and said, "Besides. You're not going anywhere either. You have a verse to finish."

The walls took up the hum again, soft and satisfied, and out of the basement stairwell, from very far down, faint as a radio in another apartment, a woman's voice joined in — Marisol Vega, or the shape the house had made of her — harmonizing on the second verse, note-perfect, cheerful, and never once coming up for air.

I looked down at the appraisal in my hands. My signature. My name.

The house had not trapped me last night, or this morning, or when I was nine.

It had just been waiting for me to sign on the dotted line myself — and now, apparently, I had.

Wren patted the piano bench beside it. "Lesson time," it said.
