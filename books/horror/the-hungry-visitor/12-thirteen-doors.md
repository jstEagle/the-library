## 12. Thirteen Doors

I had counted the doors eleven times before I understood that counting them was the problem.

That's the thing nobody tells you about haunted houses—or appraised houses, or whatever taxonomy you prefer for a structure that has opinions about you. The rational mind wants to inventory. It wants lists. It wants the comforting arithmetic of *eleven doors in an eleven-door house*, because as long as the numbers behave, the world behaves, and as long as the world behaves, you are a professional man with a clipboard and not a nine-year-old boy standing in a dark hallway in his socks.

The numbers had stopped behaving around sunset.

I'd spent the day doing what I told myself was work: measuring rooms I already knew were wrong, photographing windows that photographed back wrong, writing notes in the margin of my appraisal form in increasingly small handwriting, like shrinking letters could contain what I was seeing. *Load-bearing walls non-standard.* *Interior dimensions exceed exterior footprint by approx. 4%.* *Recommend structural engineer.* As if a structural engineer could fix this. As if anyone could look at this house and say, ah yes, the joists are rotten, that's your problem right there, and not: the joists are fine, it's the *memory* that's load-bearing.

At dusk I ate half a sandwich I didn't taste. At full dark, I heard the first door open.

Not the creak—I'd catalogued the creaks by then, they were almost friendly, a house clearing its throat. This was the sound after the creak. The sound of a door swinging wide on purpose. An invitation has a different acoustics than an accident. Anyone who's ever heard their front door open at 2 a.m. knows this in their spine.

I stood in the upstairs hallway with my phone flashlight—because of course I hadn't bought a real flashlight, because real flashlights are for people who expect to need them—and looked down the corridor.

All thirteen doors stood open.

*Thirteen.* I want to be clear that there were eleven doors when I arrived. There were twelve by the second day, which I had filed under *stress and poor lighting*. Thirteen required no filing. Thirteen sat up and introduced itself.

And every one of them was open, and every one of them was lit from inside—not electric light, nothing so honest, but a warm amber glow like the lamp light of 1994, like the specific yellow of a house where someone is always downstairs playing piano, and the glow spilled out across the hallway floor in long trapezoids, thirteen of them, overlapping, so that walking the hall meant wading through light.

Behind each door, faintly, something was moving.

* * *

Here is what I should have done. I should have walked out. I had established, over the previous chapters of my life, that leaving was complicated—the paperwork signed itself, the agent without footprints, the sense that the property line extended further than the deed admitted—but I should have *tried*. Effort counts for something, even doomed effort. My mother would have said that. My mother said a lot of things about effort while never once saying her daughter's name.

Instead, I did what I have done my whole life when cornered by the past: I opened the nearest door and looked inside.

It was my bedroom. Not the bedroom as it existed now—stripped, water-stained, smelling of mouse and mildew—but my bedroom as it was. Rocket Ship wallpaper. The glow-in-the-dark stars on the ceiling arranged in no constellation known to science, because Wren had helped me place them and Wren held that the sky was more interesting with some improvisation. My bed with the spaceship comforter. And on the bed, small, gap-toothed, wearing pajamas with dinosaurs on them, was me.

Eight years old. Laughing at something.

I stood in the doorway and watched myself be happy, which is a particular kind of violence no one warns you about.

Little Theo was talking to the empty air beside him, talking with his whole body, arms going, and I knew—I knew with a sick drop of the stomach—who he was talking to, because I remembered the conversation. It was the night we invented the middle verse of the song, the one about the wagon with the broken wheel that still rolled downhill if you believed in it hard enough. He laughed and fell backward onto the bed and the empty air beside him seemed to laugh too, the way air does when you're eight and the world hasn't taught you its rules yet.

Then the scene did what scenes do in dreams. It cut.

Cut to the creek.

The same room, the same wallpaper, the same bed—but now the walls were running with water. Not leaking. *Running*, the way film runs through a projector, and in the water I could see the creek behind the Holloway house, brown and fast with spring melt, and little Theo was standing on the bank in the memory, or the memory was standing on the bank in the room, and he was calling a name, and the name was—

I shut the door.

My hands were shaking. I made a note of that, clinically, the way I'd been making notes all week: *subject experiencing tachycardia and tremor; recommend removal from premises.* Subject being me. Premises being a house that was eating its own history and serving it back room by room.

There were twelve doors left.

* * *

I told myself I would walk past them. I got exactly one door past the first before I opened the second, because here is the other thing about being cornered by your own history: part of you wants out, and part of you has been waiting thirty years for exactly this, and the second part is stronger, because grief is patient in a way survival instinct isn't. Survival instinct gets tired. Grief will wait in a doorway forever.

Door two: age six. The kitchen, rebuilt in miniature inside the bedroom-sized space beyond the frame—no wait. That's not right. Let me get it straight, because getting it straight is all I have.

Each door opened onto a year. Not a room—a *year*. Door two was 1992, and beyond it was our old kitchen in the house we lived in before, the one two towns over, with the avocado fridge, and my mother was at the counter rolling dumpling wrappers with Mei perched on the stool beside her stealing scraps of dough, and Mei was four and solemn about her dough, shaping it with terrible concentration into things she insisted were turtles, and my mother was laughing, actually laughing, head back, and I stood in the doorway of a condemned house watching my mother laugh and felt something crack in my chest along a seam I hadn't known was there.

Then the water came. Through the kitchen window, rising, and the dough-turtles floated, and Mei's stool drifted, and my mother's laughter stretched and warped downward in pitch until it was the sound the creek makes under ice.

Door three: 1990. The hospital corridor where I was born, except I was somehow also five in it, holding my father's hand, and at the end of the corridor was the creek.

Door four: 1993. Backyard birthday party, seven candles, everyone singing—singing, God, even the party guests were singing—and the smoke from the candles formed shapes, and the shapes were ripples.

Door five. Door six. I stopped keeping careful track somewhere around door nine, and I'll tell you why: because by then I had confirmed the pattern, and the pattern was simple, and simple patterns are the worst ones. Every year of my childhood was in there. Every good one. And every single one of them ended at the creek.

Birthday: creek. First day of school: creek. Christmas morning, 1991, the wagon—that wagon, the rusted hulk I'd find later in the dried creek bed, but new then, red, gleaming, the best morning of my life—Mei and I pulling it through snow, and the snow becoming water, and the water carrying the sled—wagon—away, and my sister chasing it, always chasing it, always reaching—

They all ended the same way. Thirty years of my life, curated by something that had studied me the way I study houses, and it had found the load-bearing wall, and the load-bearing wall was a girl in the water, and everything else in my childhood was built on top of her.

I was crying by door ten. I note this without embarrassment, or with the embarrassment that doesn't change anything. A man alone in a haunted hallway cries the way a tree falls in an empty forest; the question of whether it matters is academic.

* * *

Somewhere around door eleven I noticed the frost.

It had been outside, before. On the windows, spelling names—MEI among them, MEI in ferning white capitals, the name arriving on the glass like a guest who'd been waiting on the porch. But frost outside is weather. Frost is arguable. What was on the interior walls now was not arguable.

It had come in through the corners first, I think—I hadn't watched it happen, it worked the way rot works, invisibly, structurally. Now it fanned across the hallway wallpaper in great white fronds, and the fronds were letters, and the letters were moving. Writing faster than they had on the glass. Names, dozens of names, generations of names, and between the names, smaller script, like annotations, like marginalia in a ledger:

*paid in full*
*partial payment*
*arrears*

Arrears. The house was doing bookkeeping. The house had been doing bookkeeping the whole time, and I was reading the accounts receivable of the dead.

I put my hand against the wall—it was cold enough to burn—and the frost wrote under my palm, quick as a teller counting bills, and I read what it wrote and wished I hadn't:

*one verse outstanding*

One verse outstanding. Thirty years of interest accruing.

* * *

Door twelve was last night. Door twelve was me, forty-one years old, arriving with a clipboard, and I watched myself get out of the car and thought, distantly, *I look tired*, and then the scene cut to the creek, because of course it did, because everything cuts to the creek, and in this version I saw something I hadn't seen from the porch: the figure on the bank wasn't me. It was smaller. It had a red coat.

I closed door twelve very gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

Which left thirteen.

The thirteenth door was at the end of the hall, and it was different. The others had glowed with lamplight, with the amber of ordinary evenings. Door thirteen glowed blue. Water-light. The light that comes up through ice, or through the surface of a pond when you're looking down into it, and I stood in front of it for a long time—minutes, maybe longer, time was behaving like the doors by then—and I did not open it.

I want that on the record. Whatever happens after this, whatever the next chapter of this disaster contains: I did not open door thirteen. It opened itself.

And there was no bedroom behind it. No year. Just the creek, real-size, real-sound, running through the frame of a door in a hallway of a house that isn't anywhere near a creek, and on the far bank, small, in a red coat, dripping, patient in the way only the drowned are patient, stood my sister.

She didn't speak. She didn't wave. She just looked at me with the expression of someone who has been trying to hand you something for thirty years and is willing to wait a little longer, but not indefinitely, and down the hallway behind me the frost surged up the walls like a tide coming in, writing faster now, names blurring into columns into ledgers into a single repeated word, and the word was my name, and the chorus in the walls—all those voices I'd been pretending were pipes settling—drew breath to sing.

Mei opened her mouth.

And behind me, at the top of the stairs, small and unchanged and humming, Wren said: "Now we can finish it."
