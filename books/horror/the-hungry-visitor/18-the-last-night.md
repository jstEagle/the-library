## 18. The Last Night

The first door closed at sundown, and I want to be honest with you: I didn't notice.

I'd spent the day in the parlor with the piano and Wren's humming, running the third verse over and over in my head like a man rehearsing a confession. Mei's words sat in me now the way a swallowed stone sits—cold, specific, permanent. *The water took my hands but not my song.* I could recite them in my sleep. I suspected I would.

So when the front door swung shut behind me as I carried a glass of water from the kitchen, I registered it as weather. Old houses settle. Old houses have opinions about drafts. I set the glass down on the piano lid, next to the appraisal clipboard I hadn't touched in three days, and told myself the draft theory with all the conviction of a man telling himself he'll start going to the gym on Monday.

The second door was the one to the study. It clicked shut while I was looking directly at it.

Not slammed. Not creaked. It moved with the unhurried courtesy of a maître d' seating a guest, and then the lock—a deadbolt I had personally noted as rusted open during my initial walkthrough—threw itself with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"Okay," I said, to no one, because that's what I do now apparently. I narrate to empty rooms like a nature documentary host whose crew has abandoned him. "Okay."

Wren was sitting cross-legged on the piano bench when I turned around. Small, patient, wearing the same striped shirt it had worn every day since 1994, since before 1994, since whenever a lonely nine-year-old invents a friend out of loneliness and spare parts. Its face did the thing I'd never been able to describe to my mother when she asked why I wouldn't stop talking to nobody—the thing where it looked like a child's drawing of a face rather than a face. Eyes too simple to lie with. Mouth a single confident line.

"They're closing," Wren said. Not sorry. Not gloating. Just informing, the way you'd tell someone it was raining.

"How many are left?"

"Count them."

And God help me, I did. I walked the downstairs like an appraiser one last time—old habits, old scaffolding—and found the kitchen door shut, the pantry shut, the cellar shut, the coat closet shut, the back porch door shut. Upstairs, through the ceiling, I heard four more soft clicks in sequence, like a hand counting down fingers. Thirteen doors. I'd counted thirteen for days now, and the number had stopped being wrong somewhere around the time everything else did.

"One left," Wren said.

"The parlor."

"You're standing in it."

* * *

Here is what I should tell you about panic, since I've become something of an expert: it doesn't feel like screaming. Screaming is what panic does at parties. Real panic is quiet. It's the stillness of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't want to finish. I stood in the parlor of a house that had eaten at least forty people—I'd read their names in the frost, ferned across the glass like pressed flowers—and I calculated the square footage of the room, the load-bearing walls, the window over the sink that I had broken out on day two with a fireplace poker only to find the boards outside had regrown by morning, thicker, apologetic, like scar tissue.

Then I sat down on the floor across from Wren, because if the house wanted to seal me in with the thing wearing my best friend's face, the least I could do was get comfortable.

"You knew this was coming," I said.

"I hoped." Wren picked at a loose thread on its knee. The thread didn't come loose. Nothing in this house ever came loose; things only ever came *back*. "You've been learning the verse. You're almost ready. And when you're ready—" It looked up, and its simple eyes were wet, which should not have been possible, which I would swear in any court was impossible, and which broke something in me anyway. "When you're ready, we can sing it together. Like we always planned."

"There was never a plan, Wren. There was a song we didn't finish because you said the ending wasn't ours yet."

"It wasn't ours then." The thread twisted around its small finger, once, twice. "It is now. That's what I've been trying to tell you for thirty years, Theo. The ending was always going to arrive. Songs end. Houses end. People end. The question is who's holding your hand when it happens."

Outside, the light went from amber to bruise. The frost had started early tonight. I could hear it—not see it, *hear* it—a fine crystalline whisper against the boarded windows, like sugar pouring, like static, like applause held at a distance.

* * *

I want to explain the bargain properly, because I've had time to think about how good it was, and how much I hated myself for how good it was.

Wren laid it out the way we used to lay out games on the bedroom floor, with total seriousness and total generosity. Sing the verse together—its melody, my voice, harmony, the way we'd always meant to. And in exchange: everything. No more hunger. No more frost writing names. The chorus released—all of them, every name on the windows, every fragment singing in the walls. Mei's note, the one she'd been holding under the waterline of the world for thirty years, finally allowed to resolve.

"And you?" I asked. "What happens to you?"

"I get to stay." Wren smiled, and the smile was nine years old and perfect and I felt it land in my chest like a hook finding meat. "We both stay. Friends again. Forever isn't a trick word here, Theo. It's just accurate. This house doesn't end. We've been over this."

"And if I do what I planned instead? Speak the words, keep my voice out of the melody?"

Wren's face did something I'd never seen it do. It aged. Not into an adult—into nothing. A blankness where the drawing used to be, and beneath the blankness, briefly, something vast and patient and terribly hungry looked out at me from inside my oldest friend, and then the small face came back, and the small face was sad.

"Then the house starves," Wren said quietly. "And starving houses don't let go gently. And I—" it spread its small hands, "—I don't know what I am without it. That's the truth. That's the whole ugly truth. Maybe I'm nothing. Maybe I'm just the appetite wearing a boy, and when the appetite dies, so do I."

"So the bargain is: everyone goes free except you."

"Everyone goes free except us." It crawled closer across the floorboards, and I let it, God forgive me, I let it, because its knee bumped mine and the contact was warm and real and I had not been touched by anything warm in this house in six days. "Theo. Look at me. Look at what's outside those windows. Forty names. Forty people who finished songs alone, or tried to leave, or lied to themselves right up until the frost reached their throats. You could be the one who does it differently. You could be the one who ends it *kindly*."

The frost was climbing the inside of the windows now. I watched it fern up the glass in real time, faster than last night, faster than the night before, letters resolving out of the patterns like faces out of clouds. Names first—the chorus checking in, roll call, attendance taken in ice. Then, below them, larger, deliberate, letter by letter across the bottom pane:

T-H-E-O

"It knows your name," Wren said softly. "It's known your name since 1994. It's been so patient with you, Theo. It waited for you to grow up. It waited for you to come back. Do you understand? Of all the houses, of all the children—it chose the one who stopped singing. Because a finished song is a meal, but an unfinished one—" its voice dropped to something reverent, "—an unfinished one is a *banquet*."

* * *

Night came down like a lid.

I don't know how else to put it. There was no gradual darkening; there was dusk, and then there was a sound like a great book closing, and the parlor was lit only by the frost-glow off the windows, blue-white, cold as a morgue drawer. The piano sat in the middle of it all, black and glossy, reflecting a room that had one more person in it than the room actually contained. In the reflection, just over my shoulder, stood a girl of eleven with wet hair.

I didn't turn around. If I turned around and she wasn't there, I couldn't have survived it. If I turned around and she *was*—

In the reflection, her mouth moved. No sound. But I've spent enough nights in this house to lip-read grief.

*Sing it,* she said. *Please. I'm so tired.*

"That's not her," I said aloud, and my voice cracked in the middle like a dropped plate. "That's the house using her."

"Does it matter?" Wren asked, and it wasn't cruel. That was the worst part. It sounded genuinely curious, the way we used to ask each other whether it mattered if the game was pretend as long as we were playing it. "If it looks like her, sounds like her, wants what she wants—isn't that her? Isn't that what a person is? Theo, you've been alone for thirty years. Don't tell me you haven't noticed. The apartment. The takeout containers. The voicemails you don't delete because they're the only voices you hear some weeks. I know you. I'm *made* of knowing you. And I am telling you that you can walk out of here tomorrow with the truth and the dawn and your mother's forgiveness, and you will go home to an empty apartment and eat standing up over the sink, and no one will ever hum with you again."

The frost reached the ceiling.

I heard it happen—a crackle overhead like the whole sky of the room icing over—and when I looked up, the plaster was blooming white, ferns spreading in slow motion, and among the ferns, letters again, huge now, unhurried, certain:

THEO

THEO

THEO

Repeating across the ceiling like wallpaper. Like ownership. Like a name stitched into a coat.

"She's tired," Wren said. "She has been so tired for so long. One verse, Theo. Thirty seconds. You sing the tune, I'll carry the harmony, and she gets to rest. Isn't that worth more than your principle? What is a principle even *for*, if not for moments like this?"

And here is the thing I will carry out of this house along with everything else, the thing I'll set down someday on my mother's porch and never fully pick back up: for a long moment—one breath, two, five—I wanted to say yes.

Not because I believed the bargain. Some part of me had understood since the doors began closing that Wren's promises were the house's promises, and the house's promises were frost, beautiful and melting and binding nothing. I wanted to say yes because Wren was right about the apartment. About the takeout containers. About the voicemails. I am forty-one years old and the last person who loved me without conditions was invented, and the second-to-last drowned reaching for me in a creek, and I have built an entire adult life out of measuring rooms correctly because rooms, measured correctly, never ask you for a verse.

Loneliness doesn't break you dramatically. It negotiates. It sits beside you on the floor of a sealed parlor, wearing a striped shirt, warm at the knee, and offers you forever at a price you can almost convince yourself is charity.

I looked at the reflection in the piano's gloss. The girl with the wet hair was still there. Still mouthing. But slower now, and I made myself actually read it this time, made myself look past what I feared she'd say to what she was actually saying, syllable by syllable, the way I'd learned Mei's diary, the way I'd learned this whole cursed house: patiently, refusing to flinch.

*Don't sing with it,* she mouthed. *Sing it TO me.*

To her. Not with it. The distinction was the whole world. With was harmony, was joining, was the chorus, was the meal. To was a message sent across a border. To was a letter, not a handshake.

"Wren," I said.

Its face lifted. Hope on it, plain and terrible, a child's hope, and I understood suddenly and completely that the house hadn't just grown fat on families. It had grown fat on *this*—on the exact moment when a grieving person decides the trap is a gift. This was the flavor it liked best.

"If we do this," I said slowly, "we do it my way. I speak the words. You hum the melody. I don't join the harmony. That's the only version I'll agree to."

Wren went very still.

"That's not singing together," it said.

"It's finishing the song. Which is what you said you wanted."

"That's not—" The small face flickered, and underneath it, for half a heartbeat, the vast hungry patience showed through again, and I watched it calculate, watched the appetite weigh a partial meal against no meal at all, watched it decide that a bird in the hand—even a bird that refused to harmonize—was worth the closing of the fist. "Fine," Wren said. "Fine. Sit at the piano. We'll do it your way."

I crossed the room and sat on the bench, and the wood was cold through my clothes, and above me the ceiling spelled THEO THEO THEO in letters that were still growing, still spreading toward the corners, patient as winter.

Wren climbed up beside me on the bench, small and warm and humming the first notes of the melody under its breath, warming up, the way it used to on the stairs in 1994, and I put my hands in my lap and thought about Mei's handwriting, the loop of her g's, the way she dotted her i's with little circles, and I opened my mouth to speak the first word of the last verse.

The doors upstairs, all twelve of them, unlocked at once.

Not opened. Unlocked. A sound like permission, like a held breath releasing, and from behind every closed door came the chorus—forty voices, forty fragments, verses one and two rising through the floorboards in ragged, longing harmony—and beneath them all, faint as a radio between stations, a girl's voice holding a note no child should hold for thirty years.

Mei's note bent. Wavered. Began, at last, after three decades underwater, to move toward resolution.

Wren leaned close, its breath sweet and cold against my ear, and whispered: "Whenever you're ready, Theo. She's waited this long. But the house—" and here its voice caught, or performed catching, and I have honestly never known the difference, "—the house is *so* hungry tonight."

Above us, the frost on the ceiling crept another inch toward the corners of the room, spelling my name in letters the size of coffins, and I spoke the first word of the verse into the dark.
