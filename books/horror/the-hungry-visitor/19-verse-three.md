## 19. Verse Three

The last door closed at 11:47 p.m. I know because I was watching the frost write the time on the window when it happened—no, that's not true, the frost doesn't write numbers, only names, and I only know the time because my phone died at 11:46 and the screen went black like a small polite funeral.

Thirteen doors, all shut. The parlor holding me and Wren and a piano that hadn't been tuned since the Clinton administration. The frost had reached the ceiling sometime after midnight, ferning across the plaster in slow white branches, and in the center of it, letter by letter, in handwriting I recognized because it used to be mine: T-H-E-O.

"Cozy," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt, which is either courage or shock, and honestly at this point I've stopped keeping score.

Wren sat on the piano bench. Of course Wren sat on the piano bench. Thirty years old and still wearing corduroys and a cowlick, still small enough that its feet didn't reach the floor, swinging them now in a rhythm I knew down to my bones—the rhythm of verse one.

"You could sit with me," Wren said. "Like we used to."

"I could also set myself on fire," I said. "Lots of options tonight."

"You're scared." Not cruel. Almost gentle. That was always the worst part of Wren—it never needed to be cruel, because it had me memorized. "That's okay. I was scared too, the first hundred years. Before there were songs."

I looked at the frost instead of at it. THEO, finished now, and beneath it the letters kept going, smaller, filling in like a form being completed by an invisible clerk. I didn't read what they said. Some part of me understood that reading them would be signing them.

"Here's what happens now," Wren said, and patted the bench. "You sing with me. All three verses. The whole song, start to finish, the way it was always supposed to go. And then you don't have to be alone anymore. None of us do. That's all the house ever wanted, Theo. A finished song. It's been so *hungry*."

"And if I don't?"

Wren smiled, and for one flickering second the smile was too wide, the teeth too many, the face a mask worn badly over something with no face at all—and then it was my friend again, patient as weather. "Then you stay anyway. Everyone stays. Singing just makes it nicer."

There it was. The whole con, laid out flat. The house hadn't needed my voice; it had wanted my *yes*. Mei's voice had been taken. Mine, it wanted given.

Well. I've spent thirty years giving people what they want to avoid the hard thing. My mother wanted silence, so I gave her silence. The buyer wanted a flip, so I gave him paperwork. Wren wanted a duet.

I walked to the piano.

* * *

The bench creaked under me like it was pleased. Wren scooted over, corduroys whispering, close enough that I could smell it—that smell from 1994, cut grass and pencil shavings and something underneath like rain on hot stone. It hummed the opening notes of verse one, and the walls took it up, dozens of voices, faint as radio through plaster, every name I'd read in the frost singing along in fragments, harmonies worn smooth by decades of practice.

I put my hands on the keys but didn't press them. This was the plan—or the closest thing to a plan I'd ever had, which is to say it was a decision wearing a plan's clothes.

The rule, the one thing I'd learned in three nights of this house: those who sing it become part of the chorus. Every voice in these walls had sung. That was the hook, the trap, the fine print. The house collected singers the way my mother collected apologies she never delivered.

But a melody isn't a voice. And words spoken aren't words sung.

Mei's diary lay open on top of the piano, where I'd left it, her handwriting marching across the page in careful nine-year-old cursive:

*I know how it ends. I heard it in the water. Tell Theo the last verse goes like this—*

And below, the words. Just the words. No notes. The melody lived somewhere else—in Wren's humming, in the walls, in the place where my sister had held a note for thirty years that no child should have to hold for thirty seconds.

"You'll need me to teach you," Wren said softly. "You can't do it without me. You never could finish anything without me."

"That's the truest thing you've said all week," I said. "Hum."

* * *

It hummed.

Verse one, the one we invented on the back porch in June, about a fox who couldn't sleep. Verse two, the one about the fox crossing the frozen creek, which I'd thought was just a fox, just a creek, just a game, and which I now understood had been a rehearsal the whole time—the house writing itself into us through us, patient as frost.

And then Wren paused, exactly where the pause had always been, the gap where the ending wasn't theirs yet, and looked up at me with eyes that were doing their best to be kind and almost managing it.

"Together," it said. "On three."

"No," I said. "You're on your own. So am I."

And I spoke.

Not sang. *Spoke.* My voice, flat and plain and thirty-eight years old and cracking in the middle, saying Mei's words over Wren's melody the way you'd read a letter at a funeral—no music in my throat, all the music carried by the thing beside me, our two sounds running parallel and never touching, never joining, never becoming the harmony the house was starving for.

*"Come home before the water knows your name,"*

The chorus faltered.

It was small at first—a hitch in the wall-voices, like a needle skipping—and then Wren's humming wobbled, because the melody was reaching for my voice and finding nothing to hold onto. The house had spent thirty years building toward a duet. It got a man reading his dead sister's diary out loud in an untuned parlor, and there was nothing in that for it to eat.

*"the ice remembers every footstep, the creek keeps what it's given,"*

The frost cracked.

A sound like a lake letting go in spring—one sharp report from the ceiling, then another, then a long splintering run across the room as the white ferns fractured and fell, drifting down around us like the world's coldest confetti. Where each flake landed, it didn't melt. It *unwrote*, fading off the floorboards like breath off glass.

*"come home, come home, the lantern's lit, the door was never locked—"*

Wren stopped humming.

The silence that followed was worse than any scream I'd heard in that house, and I'd heard plenty. Because in the silence I could hear what the chorus had been covering: a single high note, thin as wire, coming from inside the walls—from everywhere—from a note held so long it had gone past music into something else, a held breath, a hand reaching, my sister's voice doing the only thing it had been able to do for thirty years, which was wait.

"Oh no you don't," Wren whispered, and its face was coming apart now, the corduroy boy peeling away like wallpaper, and underneath—

Underneath was every singer. Faces pressed forward through its skin like faces through frosted glass, mouths open, mid-verse, mid-word, mid-life. An old man's jaw. A girl's chin. A woman whose mouth shaped a name over and over. They weren't screaming yet. They were *singing*, all of them, verses one and two, faster and faster, a round that couldn't resolve, and Wren stood in the middle of them like a conductor made of stolen instruments.

"*Finish it,*" it said, in all their voices at once, and the word came out as a chord. "*Finish it and they rest. Finish it and YOU rest. You think speaking is enough? You think you can walk out of here with the song half-done AGAIN? That's what brought you back, Theo. That's what the house IS. Unfinished things. You're one of us either way—you just get to choose whether you're a singer or a wall.*"

And God help me, for one full second, I believed it. The logic was beautiful. It had always been beautiful. That's what hunger does when it gets old enough—it learns to argue.

I looked at the diary. At the last line, the one I'd read forty times and never let myself feel:

*tell Theo i'm not mad. tell him the water was cold and then it wasn't.*

She wasn't mad. She'd gone into the creek reaching for me, slipped, and her last thought on the way under had been a *verse*—and then, apparently, absolution. My sister had spent her final nine-year-old act composing a gift for the brother who'd led her to the bank and walked away.

So no. I wasn't going to give the house my voice. But I'd be damned if I gave it my hesitation either.

I spoke the rest.

* * *

*"The fox went home, the fox went home, though home was only a word,"*

My voice broke on *word* and I kept going, because grief given language doesn't need to be pretty, it needs to be *true*.

*"and the creek went on being a creek, and the winter went on being winter,"*

A crack ran up the far wall, floor to ceiling, and behind the plaster something sighed—a long exhalation, decades deep, like a house finally setting down a weight it had forgotten it was carrying.

*"and the girl went home too, in the way that girls go home when the going is water,"*

Every door in the parlor shuddered on its hinges. The chorus was unraveling—I could hear it, voices dropping out one by one, not dying, *leaving*, each one stepping off the note it had held like a passenger stepping off a train that had finally, finally reached a station.

*"so sing her home, sing her home, the lantern's lit, the door was never locked,"*

Wren screamed.

I want to describe it accurately, because accuracy matters now, it's all I have. It did not scream with one voice. It screamed with all of them—every name in the frost, every fragment in the walls, thirty years of finished and unfinished songs tearing loose at once, a chord of the stolen made suddenly unstealable, and the sound wasn't rage. Underneath everything, under the fury and the static and the many-mouthed roar, it sounded like a stomach growling at an empty plate. Like hunger discovering, at the very end, that it had been eating echoes.

The windows blew inward—not the glass, just the frost, a white detonation of it, and through the empty panes came air that smelled like actual night, actual October, wet leaves and distance and a world with no opinions about my childhood.

Wren was on its knees in the middle of the parlor, flickering, the faces sinking back into it or out of it, I couldn't tell which, its small hands pressed to its small ears.

"*You'll be alone,*" it said, and now it was just one voice, just a boy's voice, just mine from 1994, and it was crying. "*You'll be alone again. I was the only one who stayed.*"

"I know," I said. And I meant it, and it hurt, and I said it anyway. "You stayed. But you stayed the way a hook stays."

The last line of the verse was short. Mei had written it small, almost shy, at the bottom of the page:

*the end is just the door being open.*

I spoke it.

The front door opened.

Not burst—opened, unhurried, onto a porch silvered with pre-dawn, and beyond it a yard where the frost was retreating like a tide going out, and beyond that a road, and beyond that everything, the whole unfinished ordinary world, hungry in its own way and none of it interested in my voice.

Behind me, in the settling dust of the fallen frost, I heard something small begin to hum—verse one, just the first four notes, the way a child hums when it's trying to comfort itself.

I didn't turn around. If I turned around, I'd stay. We both knew it. It was the one bargain left that we understood.

"The appraisal comes tomorrow," I told the empty parlor, and my voice only shook a little. "Fair warning. I'm listing this place at zero."

The humming stopped.

And from the walls—not the chorus, not the many, just one note, one voice, high and clear and finally, *finally* resolving—came the sound of my sister finishing her song, thirty years late, right on time.
