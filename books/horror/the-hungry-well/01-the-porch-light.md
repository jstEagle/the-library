## 1. The Porch Light

The Holloway Well House had been condemned twice in Sebastian Iversen's lifetime, which he felt was one more time than most buildings earned. The first condemnation came when he was nine and too busy inventing friends to read the notices stapled to the door. The second was last month, and it was the reason he was standing at the end of Kestrel Lane at four in the afternoon with a camera bag on his shoulder and a municipal survey rolled up under his arm like a baton he'd forgotten how to pass.

Twenty years away from Halloway Bend—no relation to the house, though the town had never appreciated the distinction—and this was what brought him back: a contract job photographing condemned structures for the county's demolition docket. Forty-one properties. He'd done thirty-nine. He'd saved the well house for last the way you save the phone call you don't want to make.

*It's just a building,* he told himself, walking up the cracked apron of driveway. *It's a building that happens to have eaten a decade of your childhood nightmares, but structurally speaking, it's just a building.*

The house sat where it had always sat, at the crook of the lane where the pavement gave up and became gravel, backing onto the wooded rise that everyone in town called the Well Lot without ever explaining why. It was a narrow two-story thing, clapboard gone gray as driftwood, its windows boarded by the city after the fire—no, not a fire, Sebastian corrected himself, there had been no fire, there had been nothing, which was somehow worse. The boards over the windows were new-ish, county-issued plywood stamped in orange spray paint: **NO ENTRY — STRUCTURAL HAZARD**. Someone had added, in ballpoint, smaller: *or else.* Teenagers, probably. Teenagers loved a joke they didn't understand.

He set up his first shot from the sidewalk, out of professional habit. Wide establishing frame, late light raking across the facade so the clapboard showed every scar. His firm wanted documentation, not art, but Sebastian had never quite managed to take an ugly picture of an ugly thing. There was always something in the composition that insisted on being seen properly—the sag of a porch roof like a tired shoulder, the way ivy climbed a downspout with genuine ambition. Buildings deserved witnesses too. That was the whole philosophy of his career, if you could call surveying a career and not just a very elaborate way of avoiding people.

He worked the perimeter. East elevation, south elevation, the collapsed chimney. The grass around the foundation grew in strange waves, taller near the walls, as if the soil itself were better fed close to the house. He noted it without noting it—the surveyor's curse, cataloguing everything, understanding nothing.

Then he came around the north side, into the shadow of the Well Lot trees, and stopped.

There was a porch light on.

Not glowing—*on*, the fixture's glass yellowed with decades of moths, but lit from within by a warm, steady bulb-light that had no business existing in a house with no power, no meter, no legal occupant since 2004. Sebastian stood very still and did the rational things. He checked his watch: 4:47 p.m., full daylight behind him, so it wasn't even doing the dramatic dusk thing. He looked for a generator, a solar panel, an extension cord snaking toward a neighbor's outlet. Nothing. The cord of his own thoughts kept coming back frayed.

*Condemned houses don't turn their lights on.*

He knew that. He had written reports saying essentially that, in language like "no evidence of active utility service." And yet.

And yet some older part of him, some eight-year-old archivist buried under twenty years of CAD software and load calculations, stirred and said: *You know why the light is on.*

Sebastian walked up the porch steps because the alternative was walking back down the lane, and he had driven six hours, and he was, whatever else could be said of him, a man who finished his site visits.

The porch boards announced him with the same three-note complaint he remembered—*creek, groan, tick*—and he hated that he remembered it, hated the warm flood of familiarity, because familiarity with a condemned house was exactly the kind of intimacy that got surveyors written up. At the top of the steps he raised his camera more to have something to do with his hands than out of any hope of documentation, and looked through the viewfinder at the front door.

That was when he saw the boy.

He was sitting on the top step, off to the left, knees drawn up, chin on his wrists, in the exact posture of every child ever assigned to wait somewhere boring. Small. Maybe eight. Dark hair cut badly in the way hair is cut by a parent with kitchen scissors and good intentions. A striped shirt, faded to the color of old candy. Bare feet, filthy, entirely unbothered.

Sebastian lowered the camera. The boy remained. That was the problem. In the viewfinder or out of it, in peripheral vision or dead center, the boy remained, solid as the railing, casting a small gray shadow along the porch boards that matched the angle of the late sun precisely, as if the sun had agreed to cooperate.

"Okay," Sebastian said aloud, because he was alone on a condemned porch and dignity had already left the premises. "Okay."

"You say that a lot," the boy said. "You used to say it into your pillow. *Okay, okay, okay.* Like you were testing whether the word still worked." He turned his head, and his face was—

Sebastian's body made a decision before his mind did. It stepped back. It put the railing between them. It did all the sensible primate things while his higher functions stood in the corner of himself, embarrassed.

Because he knew that face. Not from memory—from *invention*. He had built it, piece by piece, in the autumn of 1998, out of the parts of other faces: the wide-set eyes of a kid from school named Danny Okafor, the gap-toothed grin of a cartoon fox, the general air of someone who found the world slightly funnier than it deserved. He had needed someone to explain the sounds in the walls—the scratching, the slow dragging weight, the nights when the house breathed through its pipes—and a boy who lived in the walls and wasn't scary, who was *funny*, who scratched back sometimes just to say hello, had been the only explanation an eight-year-old could survive on.

His name was Wren. Sebastian had chosen it from a bird book because wrens were small and loud and lived in holes in things.

"Wren," he said now, and his voice came out wrong, flattened, like a recording of himself played through a wall.

"Hi, Seb." Wren smiled the invented smile, gap and all. "You got tall. You always wanted to be tall. You practiced standing up straight against your bedroom door. You marked it in pencil. Your mom found the marks and thought you were measuring yourself for a growth chart and she cried a little, happy crying, because her dad had been tall."

"Stop," Sebastian said. Not because it hurt. Because it *worked*. Every word landed in a slot he hadn't known was empty, and the fit was perfect, and perfect fit was the most frightening thing he had ever felt. A stranger couldn't know these things. A stranger with research couldn't know the pencil marks, the happy crying, the doorframe. This wasn't information. This was inventory.

"I'm not supposed to tell you things yet," Wren said, apologetic, folding his arms on his knees. "There are rules. There were always rules; you just didn't know you'd agreed to them. Nobody does. That's kind of the whole—" He stopped himself, visibly, the way a child stops a sentence when an adult enters the room. "I'm not supposed to. Sorry."

"How are you here?" Sebastian asked. He gripped the railing. The wood was real under his hand, sun-warmed, splintering at the edge exactly where it had always splintered. "You weren't real. I made you up. That's what imaginary means. You stop when you grow up. That's the deal."

"Yeah, everybody thinks that's the deal." Wren picked at a splinter of his own, worrying it loose from the step. "But think about it, Seb. Think about it like a survey. You're good at those. Somebody mapped you a friend right when you needed one. Somebody put him exactly where the sounds were. What kind of imaginary friend shows up *on schedule*?" He looked up, and his eyes were Danny Okafor's eyes, and they were kind, and that was the worst part. "I waited. Twenty years, give or take. I'm really bad at time. But I didn't age either, so we match."

Sebastian became aware that he was shaking his head slowly, the universal gesture of a man declining a fact. "People don't—children don't see things that are real. That's the whole clinical literature. Imaginary companions are a normal stage of—"

"—of development, yeah, I've heard you rehearse that speech. You gave it to yourself about forty times on the drive here. Out loud, in the car, at a rest stop outside Marion." Wren winced sympathetically. "You also bought gas station coffee and immediately regretted it."

Sebastian's hand went, absurdly, to the cup in his bag's side pocket. Still half full. Still regrettable.

The light above the door flickered once—not failing, he realized, but *pulsing*, like a breath, like something inhaling through the filament—and Wren glanced up at it with an expression that was not fear exactly, but the careful blankness of a child listening for footsteps overhead.

"You should know two things before you go in," Wren said quietly. "One: don't sign anything. Anything at all. If someone puts paper in front of you tomorrow, even official-looking paper, especially official-looking paper—you don't hold the pen."

"And two?"

Wren stood. Up close he smelled like nothing at all, which was its own kind of wrong; children smell like something, dirt or sugar or shampoo, and this boy smelled like the space where a smell should be. He nodded past Sebastian, down the lane, toward the gravel and the treeline going gold in the lowering sun.

"Two: you're not the only one who got a letter about this house. There's a man been asking about you in town. Very polite. Very neat. Says he's the buyer's representative." Wren's smile came back, the invented grin, deployed now like a shield. "He asked me if I'd seen you. Which is funny."

"Why is that funny?"

"Because nobody's supposed to be able to see me anymore, Seb." The porch light pulsed again, longer this time, and the shadows on the boards leaned east, all together, like grass under wind. "Only you ever could. So either he's lying to the whole town—or he's not a person, and he can see me fine."

Behind the boarded windows of the Holloway Well House, something shifted its weight, slow and patient, from one foot to the other.

Wren sat back down on the step, folded his hands, and resumed the posture of a child waiting for someone to come home.

"You've got until sunrise," he said. "Might want to start with the basement. Bring your flashlight. And Seb—" he looked up, gap-toothed, eight years old forever, "—the jars are real. I'm sorry. I couldn't stop them labeling your mom's."

* * *

Sebastian did not go into the basement that evening. He told himself it was because the light was failing, which was true, and because the county required exterior documentation first, which was also true, and both truths were small enough to hide behind. He photographed the north elevation with hands that would not fully steady, packed his gear, and walked backward down the driveway the whole way, because turning around felt like agreement.

At the fence line he stopped and looked back once. The porch light burned steady. The boy sat on the step, watching him go, and lifted one small hand—not waving, Sebastian decided. Counting. Holding up fingers, one through five, then starting over.

Like a countdown he already knew the total of.

In the car, Sebastian sat with the engine off and pulled up the property file on his laptop. Parcel number, deed history, condemnation orders, all of it clean, all of it ordinary, all of it exactly as boring as paperwork should be. He scrolled to the scanned signature page of the original 1987 transfer and stopped.

There, in the seller's line, in ink gone brown with age, was a signature he recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting in a mirror: slightly wrong, slightly reversed, familiar anyway.

His father's name. Dated March 3rd, 1987.

Three days before Sebastian was born.

Below it, in the witness line, in a different hand—a thin, careful, old-fashioned script—he found a name he had never seen written anywhere, and which nevertheless made the hairs on his arms rise like the hackles of something small and cornered:

*Elias Merrick Vane.*

The porch light, half a mile back up the lane, went out.
