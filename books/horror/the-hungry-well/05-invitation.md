## 5. Invitation

The county archive occupied the basement of the Hall of Records, which struck me as either a joke or a threat: all the paperwork that proved your life had happened, stored below the water table like something that could be drowned.

I'd been coming here since I was twenty-three and newly licensed, back when I thought municipal records were boring in the way arithmetic is boring—reliable, indifferent, incapable of lying to you. Twenty years of surveying had cured me of most of my illusions about the world, but I'd kept that one. Records didn't lie. They just sat there, patient as sediment, telling you where the easements were.

Then I'd found the Holloway Well House missing its basement on three separate surveys, and the patience started looking less like indifference and more like discipline.

"Basement closes at four," said the archivist, a woman named Dot with reading glasses on a chain and the posture of someone who had personally outlasted every mayor since 1987. "And don't put coffee on the documents."

"No coffee," I said. "Just questions."

"Worse." She waved me back anyway.

* * *

The deed room smelled of dust and toner, the two smells of official memory. I pulled the parcel file for the well house—parcel 1147-C, though the C suffix appeared nowhere on any map I'd ever seen, which should have been my first clue years ago—and found it thinner than it had any right to be.

A property that old should have been fat with paper. Surveys, liens, easement disputes, the slow bureaucratic sediment of a century and a half of people arguing about boundaries. Instead: the original grant from 1891, a tax certificate from 1934, the 1998 waiver with my father's signature, and the current sale packet Marisol Vega was shepherding through escrow. Four documents. A hundred and thirty years, summarized in four pieces of paper, like a eulogy delivered by someone who barely knew the deceased.

But there was a fifth folder. It wasn't filed under the parcel number. It was filed under *Holloway*, cross-referenced in the card catalog with a handwritten note in faded ink: *See also — Covenant, Original.*

Someone had wanted this found. Or—and the thought arrived cold and unwelcome—someone had wanted it findable *only by someone who already knew to look.*

The folder held a single document, eight pages, written in a copperplate hand so even it looked printed. The header read:

**COVENANT AND AGREEMENT OF INVITATION**

*Made this 14th day of October, 1891, between the parties hereinafter named, concerning the dwelling known as the Holloway Well House and the tenant thereof.*

*Tenant thereof.* I said the phrase aloud just to hear how absurd it sounded. Houses have tenants. Wells have water. What did a well house have?

I read. And the rational adult in me—the one who calculated load tolerances and slope grades and had never once, not even as a child, actually believed in Wren—began quietly leaving the room.

*The undersigned Owner does hereby extend Invitation to the Tenant of the Well, who hath kept the waters and the children of this place since before the laying of these stones, and doth agree to the following terms:*

*First: The Tenant shall keep safe the memories of the household, that they be not lost to grief, to age, nor to sorrow.*

*Second: In consideration thereof, the Owner shall feed the Tenant such memories as the Tenant requires, freely given, that the Tenant may continue its watch.*

*Third: The Owner shall speak of the tunnels beneath to no one living, nor record them in any book, map, or instrument, that the Tenant's rest be undisturbed.*

*Fourth: The Tenant shall suffer no child of the household to come to harm while the Invitation stands.*

*Fifth: This Covenant binds the land and passes with the deed, unto each holder thereof, until broken by acknowledgment or abandoned by default.*

There were signatures. Eleven of them, running down the last page like a guest book at a funeral nobody survived. Dates beside each one. And next to each date, in a different hand—a smaller, wetter-looking script, the ink brown rather than black—was a single word:

*Accepted.*

*Accepted. Accepted. Accepted.*

Eleven owners. I cross-checked against what I knew of the property's history, the little of it that remained. Every signature belonged to a head of household. Every household had ended within a year of signing—sold, abandoned, or simply stopped appearing in the census, the way the Aldermans had when I was six and their swing set rusted in the yard for a decade because no one came to take it down.

My father's signature was the eleventh. Dated March 3rd, 1998. Three weeks before we moved out.

*Accepted,* said the brown ink.

* * *

I sat with the covenant for a long time. The fluorescent lights hummed their one note. Somewhere above me, the Hall of Records conducted its Tuesday business, citizens certifying births and deaths and marriages, the whole ledger of a human life, none of them knowing that forty feet below their feet a document described an economy of memory—memories paid out, memories kept safe, memories *fed*—

Fed. Like feeding a dog. Like feeding a fire.

Like feeding a child.

That word kept snagging me. Not *surrendered*. Not *paid*. Fed. As if the thing in the well needed them. As if keeping memories safe and eating memories were, in this arrangement, the same act—which meant every promise of protection was also a promise of consumption, and no one signing had understood the difference until it was too late to matter.

I thought about my mother, in her last lucid year, asking me why she couldn't remember her own wedding. Not forgetting the way the old forget, gradually, edges first. Gone. A hole with clean sides, like a core sample. Her doctor called it early dementia. My father called it tiredness. I had called it nothing, because I was thirty-one and busy and it was easier to call it nothing.

She hadn't lost those memories. She'd *fed* them. Or my father had fed them for her, paying down the household account, keeping us safe, keeping us silent, one wedding and one Christmas and one first kiss at a time.

And I had grown up in that house comfortable. That was the part that made my hands unsteady on the folder. Whatever scratched in our walls had never once scratched at *me*. Eight years old, hearing things move behind the plaster, and I'd never been afraid—not really, not the way kids are supposed to be afraid of the sounds in the walls. Because Wren was there. Because Wren explained everything. Because my parents' fear had been purchased in advance, and the currency was my mother's past, and I had slept soundly on top of the transaction.

*Third: The Owner shall speak of the tunnels beneath to no one living, nor record them in any book, map, or instrument.*

There it was. The reason my own firm's archives had holes in them shaped exactly like a basement. Not redacted by any clerk. Redacted by covenant. Every surveyor who'd touched that property had signed something, somewhere, or inherited the silence along with the job, and the maps had healed over the tunnels like skin over a wound, generation after generation, smooth and blank and wrong.

Except mine. Except the ones I'd drawn last week, which existed now, which I'd photographed, which were sitting in my truck in digital form waiting to become a problem for somebody.

Good, I thought, and then immediately: *oh God, what did I just invite.*

* * *

It was the fourth clause that finally broke me open, though. I kept circling back to it, reading it the way you probe a sore tooth.

*The Tenant shall suffer no child of the household to come to harm while the Invitation stands.*

No child of the household. But the Iversens had taken in a lodger in 1987. I remembered him in fragments—fragments, God help me, actual fragments, the word taking on new weight by the minute. A young man. Boarding in the back room off the kitchen. He'd fixed my bike chain once. He had a name.

I could not retrieve the name.

I tried. Sitting in that basement, sweating under the fluorescents, I reached for it the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, and there was nothing there. Not fog, not blur. Nothing. A shape where a name should be, like the parcel file—four documents where fifty should be. Someone had cored it out of me too, and I'd never noticed the hole because I'd never gone looking, because who goes looking for a lodger from 1987?

Except the covenant said children of the household. A lodger wasn't a child of the household. A lodger was a stranger sleeping under the same roof as an eight-year-old boy and whatever lived in his walls, protected by a contract that covered only family.

I pulled the 1987 visitor log—I'd seen it referenced in Marisol's email, her careful lawyerly phrasing: *your name appears in a visitor log dated August 1987; perhaps you could explain?* The log itself lived in a bound volume on the shelf, and there I was, aged eight, *Sebastian Iversen, accompanied by mother*, visiting the county office on some errand I couldn't remember either. And two lines above my entry, dated June 14th, 1987:

*Ilya Voss, resident of 1147-C, inquiry re: tenancy rights.*

Tenancy rights. He'd come to the Hall of Records to ask about his own legal standing in that house. Two weeks later, per the fragmentary file, he was gone. No forwarding address. No police report. No missing persons filing, which for a man with no local family might have made sense—or might have meant that everyone who would have filed one had already forgotten he existed.

Everyone except whoever wrote his name in this log. Paper remembers. That was the whole religion of this building. People forget; paper doesn't. Which is why the covenant forbade recording the tunnels, why the maps healed over, why the archive felt less like a library and more like a battlefield where the only weapon that worked was omission.

Ilya Voss. I said it silently, then aloud, barely a breath, testing whether the name weighed anything.

It weighed. Saying it felt like setting something down that I'd been carrying without knowing—no, not carrying. Something that had been carried *for* me, stored somewhere cool and dark, in a jar with a label.

The shelves. Wren leading me down through the dark, and the shelves, and the jars, and I hadn't let myself think about it since, because thinking about it meant admitting I'd been down there, and admitting I'd been down there meant admitting Wren was real, and I had built twenty years of adult sanity on top of that refusal like a house built on a filled-in well.

Jars with labels. Labels in handwriting I almost recognized.

I copied the covenant by hand—all eight pages, my writing getting worse as it went—and photographed the log entry twice. Then I sat for a moment with my father's signature in front of me, March 3rd, 1998, and tried to imagine the conversation. My mother crying, maybe. My father hunched over this same table, or one like it, pen in hand, being told the terms by someone whose face I couldn't picture because there was no one to picture. *Sign, and your children will be safe. Sign, and forget you signed.* Had he known what he was paying with? Had he chosen which memories to feed, or had the thing in the well chosen for him, taking the wedding first, the way creditors take the best collateral?

He died still not knowing my middle name. I'd always assumed that was the stroke.

The lights hummed. Dot called down the stairwell that it was five minutes to closing, and her voice echoed strangely, doubling off the concrete, and for one vertiginous second I heard it doubled again—a third voice, younger, repeating her words a half-beat late, the way a child repeats what they overhear through a wall.

*Five minutes. Five minutes.*

I know that habit, I thought. I invented a friend with that habit.

I packed up the copies, returned the folder to its slot, and stood a moment with my hand flat on the drawer, on the paper inside it, on eleven signatures and eleven acceptances and one name—*Ilya Voss*—that the covenant's fourth clause had failed to cover.

The thing in the well had been fed for a hundred and thirty years. It had been thanked never. Named never. And somewhere under the Holloway Well House, in the dark where the water remembered, it was still keeping its side of the deal—still watching the children, still holding the memories, still waiting for an owner who would say its name.

My phone buzzed. Marisol Vega.

"Sebastian." Her voice was carefully level, the levelness of someone standing next to something they can't explain. "You need to get down here. There's a man in my office buying your house, and he says escrow closes at sunrise." A pause. "And Sebastian—he asked for you by name. Both your names."

I looked at the covenant copy in my hand.

"The name I use," I said slowly, "or the one I was born with?"

Another pause, longer.

"How did you know he used two?"
