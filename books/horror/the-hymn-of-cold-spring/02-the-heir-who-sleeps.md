## 2. The Heir Who Sleeps

Marcus Thorne had learned to sleep the way other men learned to hold their breath underwater — out of necessity, and never quite long enough.

It began, or he noticed it beginning, in the weeks after his grandmother's funeral. The sun would go down over the ridge behind Cold Spring House, and something in him would simply... fold. Not tiredness. Tiredness was honest; it had a cause you could name, a remedy you could buy at any pharmacy. This was different. This was a tide. At 6:14 in the evening, while pouring a glass of water or halfway through a sentence of correspondence, he would feel the house exhale against the back of his neck, warm and sweet as milk, and by 6:20 he would be asleep wherever he stood — in the kitchen chair, on the stairs, once standing upright in the hallway with his forehead resting against the wallpaper, dreaming of nothing, which was itself unusual, because Marcus had dreamed every night of his life until then.

He woke each morning with chalk-pale fingers and the taste of cold iron at the back of his throat, and he told himself it was grief. Grief did strange things to the body. Everyone said so. The doctor in town — young, brisk, fresh from the city — ran bloodwork and found nothing and prescribed sleep hygiene, which was rather like prescribing good posture to a man whose spine was being slowly bent by an invisible hand.

So when the pest controller arrived on a gray Tuesday in late June, Marcus had been awake for eleven hours and could feel the edges of himself already softening, like paper left out in the damp.

* * *

Elodie Voss was not what Marcus had expected from the phone call. He'd imagined someone older, perhaps, or rougher — but the man who came up the drive with a steel case in one hand and a clipboard under his arm was somewhere in his forties, lean, sun-browned, with the particular stillness of a person who spent his working life waiting for things to move. His eyes went everywhere before they went to Marcus. They catalogued the sagging porch rail, the ivy eating the north wall, the windows with their curtains drawn against a heat that hadn't yet arrived, and they did it the way an accountant reads a ledger: without judgment, without mercy, totaling everything up.

"Mr. Thorne." He didn't offer a hand until Marcus offered his first, and even then the handshake was brief, professional, dry. "You said on the phone there's activity in the walls."

"There's—" Marcus stopped. Started again. He had rehearsed this conversation three times that morning and every rehearsal had collapsed at exactly this point, because there was no honest way to say *there are hands pressed against the inside of my pantry wall* without saying anything else. "There are signs of infestation. Rodents, I assumed. I'm not — I don't know much about this sort of thing."

"Most people don't." Elodie set the case down on the porch boards and opened it, and Marcus watched with a strange, distant gratitude as the man's attention slid off him and onto the instruments: snap traps, glue boards, a UV flashlight, small labeled vials. Things with weight and purpose. Things that could be held. "Walk me through what you've seen. Droppings? Gnaw marks? Noise?"

"Noise," Marcus said. "At night. And..." He hesitated, and the hesitation cost him something, because Elodie's eyes came back up and settled on his face with the patience of a man who had been lied to by clients for twenty years and had developed an ear for the specific frequency of it. "There's dust," Marcus finished. "In the pantry. It gets disturbed."

"Dust gets disturbed in old houses. Air moves through them like lungs."

"Not like this."

The words were out before he could stop them, and they hung between the two men on the porch, too heavy for the morning air. A crow called somewhere back in the birches. Elodie didn't ask what he meant. That was the worst part, looking back on it later — he didn't ask, just nodded once, slow, and picked up his case.

"Show me the pantry."

* * *

Marcus led him through the house he had grown up in and had sworn, at eighteen, never to return to, and which he had returned to anyway at thirty-one because his grandmother's will had been very clear and his father had been very persuasive and the estate — the land, the timber rights, the spring — had seemed, from the distance of the city, like salvation. He had sold parcels of it within the year. Meadow lots to a developer with a laugh like a braying donkey. The east woods to a logging concern. Every sale had felt, at the signing, like setting down a weight he'd carried since childhood, and every night after had felt like the house noticing.

That was the guilt he carried now, walking these halls with a stranger's footsteps echoing behind him: not that he'd inherited the place, but that he'd begun dismantling it. Because his grandmother had made him promise. He'd been nine years old, sitting on the edge of her bed while she braided her white hair, and she had said, in the same tone she used for telling him to eat his greens, *"Whatever they offer you for the land, Marcus, the house stays. And the spring stays. You'll understand why when you're older, and I pray you won't understand until you have to."*

He had understood nothing. He had signed four deeds.

And now the pantry door stood open ahead of him, and beyond it the flour bin sat with its lid slightly ajar, and the fine white dust of the shelf lay disturbed in patterns he had spent two weeks refusing to look at directly.

Elodie crouched at the threshold. He didn't touch anything at first. He looked, the way Marcus had been unable to make himself look, and the silence coming off the man had a texture to it, dense and listening.

"Well," Elodie said finally. His voice had changed. Flattened. "Those aren't mice."

The prints were pressed into the flour dust on the third shelf, five-fingered, unmistakably hands — but wrong in every particular a hand could be wrong. Too large, spanning nearly the width of the shelf. Too many creases, joints suggested where joints shouldn't be, knuckles stacked like river stones. And they faced outward. Pressed outward, from the plaster, as if whatever had made them had been on the *other side* of the wall and had pushed its palms flat against the inside of the world.

"They were facing the other way last week," Marcus heard himself say. His voice sounded far away to him, calm in the way of men standing on high ledges. "I turned the flour bin around. I thought — I don't know what I thought."

Elodie took photographs. The shutter clicked six times, seven, and each click sounded obscenely loud in the stillness. Then he stood, and turned, and looked at Marcus with an expression Marcus couldn't read — not disbelief, which would have been a comfort, and not belief either, which would have been a catastrophe. Something between. Something being weighed.

"How long has your family lived here?" Elodie asked.

"Two hundred years. Give or take."

"And problems like this. How long?"

Marcus's mouth opened. Closed. Somewhere below them — below the floor, below the cellar, below the stone lip of the old spring that the town was named for and that no one in the town spoke of anymore — he felt, or imagined he felt, a faint coolness rising through the soles of his shoes, patient as groundwater.

"My grandmother handled things," he said. "Around the house. She was very... capable."

"I see." Elodie wrote something on his clipboard. Marcus craned and caught only two words upside down: *prior management.* "Mr. Thorne, I'm going to be direct with you, because it saves us both time. Whatever's in your walls, it isn't rats, and it isn't squirrels, and it isn't anything I can bill you for clearing out in an afternoon. If you want me here, I want a season. Full contract. Traps, monitors, documentation. And I want you to tell me everything you know about this house, starting with why your grandmother needed 'handling' done, and ending with why you flinched just now when I mentioned her."

The afternoon light through the pantry window had gone strange and yellow, thick as broth. Marcus realized he was swaying slightly on his feet, that the tide was early today, that the house wanted him asleep and was reaching up through the floorboards to pull him down into its warm dark pocket. He gripped the doorframe.

"A season," he repeated.

"Paid monthly. I'll need access to all rooms. Including," Elodie added, glancing past Marcus's shoulder, down the hall, toward the low door at the back of the kitchen that Marcus had not pointed out and had hoped would not be noticed, "wherever that draft is coming from."

* * *

They signed the contract at the kitchen table at four o'clock, and by half past five Marcus could barely keep his eyes open.

He fought it in front of Elodie, who was unpacking equipment in the parlor with the methodical sounds of a man building a small fortress. Marcus washed his face. He drank coffee gone bitter and cold. He walked the length of the upstairs hall twice. And all the while the drowsiness rose in him like floodwater, sweet and absolute, carrying on its surface fragments of a melody he hadn't consciously heard in twenty years — his grandmother's voice, low and unhurried, singing in the room next door, the lullaby she'd sung every night of his childhood and had forbidden him, with sudden ferocity, ever to sing past the second verse.

*Hush now, the spring runs deep and cold—*

He woke at dawn on the parlor sofa, having no memory of lying down, with a blanket over him that he hadn't pulled, and Elodie Voss watching him from the doorway with a cup of tea going cold in his hand and an expression that had, overnight, lost all its professional neutrality.

"You were talking in your sleep," Elodie said.

Marcus's heart stumbled. "Was I."

"For about an hour. Same phrase, over and over." The pest controller set the cup down on the mantel. Outside, the summer morning was bright and ordinary and utterly unconvincing. "You said, *she promised it wouldn't be hungry again until I was grown.*"

The words landed in the quiet kitchen like stones dropped down a well. Marcus listened to them fall and fall and heard no bottom.

Down the hall, beneath the floor, beneath the foundations, the spring waited — black, still, and patient as it had been for two hundred years — and somewhere in the walls, in the flour dust, in the space between sleeping and waking, something that wore the shape of hands turned its many-jointed palms outward, and pressed gently against the inside of the morning.
