## 10. The Neighbors Remember

The village of Cold Spring had one road, two pubs, and a church whose graveyard leaned downhill like a congregation slowly giving up on the sermon. Elodie drove through it at nine in the morning with a clipboard on the passenger seat and a cover story he'd rehearsed in the truck: insurance survey. Water damage claims. Nothing to do with songs or springs or handprints pressed into flour from the inside of a wall.

He'd told himself the lie all the way down the hill, and by the time he parked outside the post office he'd almost made himself believe it, which was, he was learning, how belief worked. Not a door you opened. A door that opened in you when you stopped leaning against it.

The first three doors gave him nothing but tea he didn't want and opinions about drainage he didn't need. The fourth gave him a name.

"Thorne?" The woman — Mrs. Aldous, eighty if she was a day, cardigan the color of wet slate — said the name the way people say the name of a dog that bit them once. "You're doing work up at the house, then."

"Clearing an infestation," Elodie said. "Routine."

She looked at him for a long moment over her spectacles, and he watched her decide something. He'd seen that look before, on farmers deciding whether to tell the new vet about the thing in the back field. It was the look of a person weighing whether the truth would do more damage spoken or kept.

"Mice," she said at last, "isn't a word they use about that house."

*They.* Elodie's pen hovered. "Who's *they*, Mrs. Aldous?"

"Everyone who's lived here longer than you've been alive." She poured herself tea she didn't offer to share. "Sit down, Mr.—"

"Voss."

"Mr. Voss. And I'll tell you what my mother told me, and hers told her, and you can decide whether it's worth your wage."

---

It took four hours and six houses. By noon Elodie had filled eleven pages of his clipboard, and the cover story lay dead somewhere behind him on the single road, run over so many times it no longer resembled anything.

The pattern assembled itself the way pest patterns always did: not from any one account but from the overlap. He drew it that evening in the truck cab, dates on a line, and when he stepped back from it his own handwriting seemed to flinch.

Every fifty years. 1821. 1871. 1921. 1971. Four renewals, four winters, and around each date the same cluster of anomalies, reported in different voices across a century and a half.

The winter after each renewal was gentle. That was the first thing. Parish records, almanac clippings, old men's memories all agreed: the cold never came to Cold Spring the year the song was sung. Lambs in February. Apple blossom in March. One farmer's grandfather had sworn the frost that year "went round the valley like water round a stone." Fifty years of soft weather, bought wholesale.

And the second thing — the thing that made Elodie sit very still in the cab with the engine ticking — was what happened to everyone else.

The neighbors sickened. Not dramatically. Not all at once. But in the years following every renewal, the parish burial register thickened. Coughs that became pneumonia that became graves. Children who failed to thrive. Old people who simply faded, as though something had been drawn off them in their sleep, thin and slow, like broth skimmed from a pot. In the twelve months after the 1921 renewal, the village buried nineteen souls. The year before, it had buried four.

And in every one of those years, the Thornes thrived. Births, marriages, harvests, profit. Grandmother Vesper Thorne, born the year of the 1921 renewal, had lived to ninety-four while half her christening cohort died before sixty.

Elodie had spent thirty years reading signs for a living. Rat runs, droppings, grease marks along skirting boards — evidence of appetite, of territory, of something feeding where it shouldn't. He knew what he was looking at now, laid out in his own cramped handwriting across eleven pages.

It wasn't an infestation in the house.

The house was the feeder. The village was the trough.

---

Mrs. Aldous had saved the worst for last, the way old women do, because they have learned that bad news keeps better at the bottom of the pot.

"You'll want to know about the Holdout," she said, when he stood to leave. Her hands were folded in her lap like something closed for the season. "Everyone asks eventually, once they start asking at all. There was a man — not a Thorne. A farmer. Josiah Crake. This would be the early seventies, after the last renewal. His wife had died that winter — one of the sick ones, one of the *taken*, we say, though we don't say it loud. And Josiah worked out the same arithmetic you're working out right now, Mr. Voss. I could see it working behind your eyes since you sat down."

Elodie didn't deny it. Denial had gotten heavy lately, like carrying wet sand.

"He went up to the house," Mrs. Aldous continued. "March it was. Told Marcus's father — Reginald, it was then — told him he'd go to the papers, to the church, to anyone who'd listen, unless the singing stopped. Unless the bargain was broken. And Reginald Thorne just looked at him, Mrs. Aldous said — my mother was there, she'd gone along as witness, brave foolish woman — and said, *'The house hears everything, Josiah. Be careful what you let it hear you want.'*"

"And?"

"And Josiah walked home." She looked out the window at her neat, frostless garden. "And in June — June, Mr. Voss, the month the hay is cut — there came a night so cold the pond froze white to its middle. Just his farm. You could stand at his gate and see the fields either side green as May. They found him in his kitchen, sitting upright at his table, frozen through, kettle boiled dry on the stove. Frost on the *inside* of the windows, written in patterns like ferns. Like feathers. Like—" she paused, choosing, "—like writing."

Elodie's mouth had gone dry. He thought of the tally marks scratched around the wellhead. He thought of the branching lines he hadn't seen yet but already, somehow, dreaded.

"The coroner called it exposure," Mrs. Aldous said. "In June. And nobody argued, because arguing requires believing, and believing requires—" she smiled, small and terrible, "—well. You know what it requires. You're doing it right now, sitting in your van writing it all down so it stays true even when you wish it wouldn't."

---

He drove back up the hill with the windows down and July pouring warm air over him, and still he shivered.

The arithmetic would not stop running. Every fifty years, one throat sang, and one family banked decades of life, and the whole valley paid the interest in coughs and coffins. Gentle winters for the singer. Slow siphoning for everyone else. And for anyone who tried to end it — June frost, indoor ice, a kettle boiled dry beside a frozen man.

Which meant the question he'd driven down the hill to avoid arrived at last and sat down in the passenger seat where the clipboard used to be.

*Who do you warn?*

If he took the ledger of it to the village — the dates, the death counts, the pattern — he'd be Josiah Crake by August. Worse: he'd be the reason Marcus Thorne got hurried. Because that was the other edge of the blade, the one he kept cutting himself on whenever he turned the problem over. The thing below the spring wanted the song renewed. A frightened village hammering on Cold Spring House's door, demanding answers, demanding an ending — that might be exactly the pressure that pushed Marcus toward the hatch, toward the melody, toward the easy mercy of the bargain. Fear was the thing's favorite lever. It had been pulling that lever for two hundred years.

But silence was its lever too. Silence was how it had farmed this valley for generations — a community trained not to believe, not to speak, not to compare notes across the fence lines. Mrs. Aldous knew. Half the old ones knew. And they had all chosen their gardens and their quiet and their not-saying, and the register had thickened anyway, decade after decade, and none of them had frozen in June because none of them had threatened the arrangement.

Elodie pulled the truck onto the verge above the valley and looked down at Cold Spring village, smoke rising straight from chimneys in the windless afternoon, the churchyard leaning downhill, the whole place soft and green and being drunk from, sip by sip, by something under a floorboard forty feet below the house on the hill.

Warn them, and maybe kill Marcus faster. Stay silent, and become part of the machinery — another pair of hands keeping the trough full and the trough-keepers calm.

There was a third option. It had been forming in him since the tapes, since the hatch, since the voice had frosted his breath in July, and he hadn't let himself look at it directly because looking at it meant admitting what it would cost.

The bargain ran on the song. The song was the contract, sung aloud, renewed in a throat. Which meant the contract could be read. Studied. Taken apart the way he'd taken apart every infestation he'd ever faced — find the entry point, find the food source, find the thing that made the walls habitable, and close it.

He needed the whole hymn. All of it, word for word, note for note — including whatever came after the verse Vesper Thorne had forbidden her grandson to pass. If the song was a leash, he wanted to see the other end of it. And if there was a verse that didn't feed the thing but bound it, ended it, unmade the bargain —

That was a thought with teeth, and it bit him even as he held it, because he heard again the low voice on the tape, pausing expectantly whenever Marcus stirred, patient as interest accruing. Things that had eaten for two hundred years did not leave the leash-end lying where a pest controller could reach it.

Unless they wanted him to find it.

He put the truck in gear and drove up toward the house, and the last of his disbelief went out of him somewhere on that road, quietly, without ceremony, the way a fever breaks. He was not going to warn the village today. Today he was going to do what he'd always done when the infestation was bigger than one property: map the whole nest.

Marcus met him in the hall, gray-faced, sleep-rumpled at two in the afternoon, and Elodie saw the chalk-pale skin at his wrists and felt the clock start ticking somewhere behind his sternum.

"We need to talk about your grandmother's sheet music," Elodie said. "All of it. And Marcus — we need to talk about what comes after the second verse."

Somewhere beneath the floor, faint as a draft, something began — very softly — to hum.
