## 11. Verse by Verse

The kitchen of Cold Spring House had become Elodie's war room, though he would never have used the word. War implied belief in an enemy. He preferred *case file*. He preferred the language of invoices and evidence bags, of timestamps and chain of custody. But at three in the morning, with the ledger open beside a laptop playing back forty hours of audio, even he had to admit the kitchen looked less like an office than like the aftermath of something — papers fanned across the scarred oak table, coffee gone cold in three mugs arranged at intervals like little sentinels, and the window above the sink fogged from the inside despite the July heat.

He worked by lamplight because the overhead fixture buzzed at a frequency that made the recordings harder to hear. That was his official reason. His unofficial reason, which he did not examine, was that the dark beyond the windows felt attentive lately, and smaller pools of light seemed to hold it off. A superstition. He noted it, filed it under *irrational responses, subject's own*, and kept working.

The ledger lay open at its most recent pages, Marguerite Thorne's spidery hand marching down columns: dates on the left, verses on the right, and in a third column, in a different ink each time, the payments. *Two years.* *Seven years.* *A winter without sickness in the village.* The entries went back further than the paper did; she had copied the older ones herself from some earlier book, and those transcriptions were written in a hand that shook, as if copying them had cost her something each time.

Elodie cross-referenced everything against the audio.

It had taken him two nights to realize what he was hearing. The lullaby on the recordings was not random humming. It was structured — three verses, each eight lines, each ending on a falling phrase that resolved like a door closing. Marcus's grandmother's voice, decades old, preserved on a cassette found in the attic player, sang all three cleanly. And beneath her voice, so low it took headphones and patience and a kind of stubbornness Elodie had in abundance, another sound followed the melody. Not singing. *Answering.* A beat behind, a register below, the way an echo answers if the echo were thinking about what it said.

He transcribed the words first. That was the easy part, or should have been. The dialect was old — older than the house, older than the town — but English underneath, worn smooth like stones in a streambed. He wrote the verses out longhand because typing them felt wrong, felt too much like giving the thing a clean copy. Three complete verses filled one page. Then the fourth verse began, and the recording degraded into scratches, tape hiss, a sound like fingernails drawn across the shell of the cassette itself, from the inside.

The fourth verse had four lines. He could recover perhaps six words of it. The rest was damage.

*Damage,* he wrote in the margin, and then, because honesty had become a habit he couldn't shake, crossed it out and wrote *erasure.*

He sat back. The lamp hummed. Somewhere above him, in the sealed rooms where they'd shut away anything the voice might whisper through, the house ticked and settled like any old house, like nothing at all.

Elodie Voss had spent thirty-one years refusing to believe in things he couldn't trap. But he had learned, over the past weeks, that there was a category worse than believing: *testing.* Testing required no faith. Testing required only method. And method said that if the hymn was a mechanism — if it opened something the way a key opens a lock — then you didn't understand the lock until you'd heard it turn.

He told himself he only wanted to verify the transcription. To confirm the melody matched the sheet music framed in the hallway, which it did, note for note, except for one flat in the second verse that Marguerite had corrected in pencil. He told himself the spring wouldn't answer to him anyway. It wanted a Thorne. It had always wanted a Thorne. He was nobody, a contractor, a man with a van and a laminated price list.

He cleared his throat, which was dry, and sang the first verse alone in the kitchen of Cold Spring House at half past three in the morning.

His voice was not good. He knew this the way he knew his shoe size — a neutral fact. The notes came out thin and slightly flat, and the old words felt strange in his mouth, consonants like gravel. Eight lines. Twenty seconds, maybe less.

The temperature dropped before he finished the last line.

Not gradually. Not like a draft. Like a hand closing. His breath came out white in front of his face, and the coffee in the nearest mug — cold already, but liquid — crackled as a skin of ice formed across it with a sound like tiny knuckles rapping glass. And beneath the floor, beneath the kitchen, beneath the sealed hatch and the black still water of the spring, something *moved.* Not footsteps. Not the many-jointed dragging he'd heard on the tapes. This was a settling, a vast adjustment of weight, the way a sleeping animal shifts when it hears its name.

Then the humming began.

It picked up the melody exactly where his voice had stopped, continuing into the second verse — the verse he hadn't sung — and it sounded *pleased.* There was no other word for it. The low answering voice from the recordings, unspooled now into the open air of the kitchen, hummed with a warmth that was worse than menace, that was worse than anything, because it was the warmth of recognition. Of welcome. Of a host delighted that the guest has finally sat down at the table.

Elodie's hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a clinical detachment that was itself beginning to fray. The frost crept up the inside of the window above the sink in branching lines — quick, eager, matching nothing yet, or matching everything — and he understood with total, wordless clarity that the thing below had been waiting fifty years for someone to sing, and it did not care whose throat the song came from, and he had just taught it his voice.

He stopped humming along with it. He hadn't realized he was humming along with it.

The silence after he stopped was enormous. The frost on the window held for a moment, trembling at the edges of its branches, then retreated slowly, reluctantly, like breath fading off glass. The temperature climbed. The ice in the mug sighed and softened.

Elodie sat down. He noticed he had been standing without remembering standing. He wrote, in a hand far steadier than he felt: *Subject responds to vocalization within 2–4 seconds regardless of singer. Response is positive. Response is encouraging. Do not sing again alone.*

Then, because the night was long and the fear had burned through him down to something harder, he added: *Do not sing again at all, unless together.*

* * *

Marcus woke at noon to sunlight through curtains he didn't remember closing and a silence in the house that had texture to it, a held-breath quality he'd learned to dread. For a moment he lay still, cataloguing himself the way he'd learned to: hands — check, chalk-pale but his own; memory of the night — check, fragmented, dreams of his grandmother's kitchen and a melody threading through it like wire; the pull toward sleep — present but distant, mercifully, in daylight.

Then he heard the coughing.

It came from the kitchen, deep and ragged, the cough of a man trying to clear something out of his chest that wasn't phlegm. Marcus came down the stairs two at a time and stopped in the doorway.

Elodie sat at the table looking like a man who had been exhumed. Gray-faced, gray-lipped, eyes red-rimmed and sunk back in his skull, a blanket around his shoulders that Marcus was fairly sure Elodie would deny ever having pulled around himself. The table was buried under paper. The laptop sat open, its screen dark, its headphone jack still holding one white earbud. And Elodie's voice, when he tried to speak, came out as a ruin.

"Water," he rasped, or approximated it, and Marcus poured him a glass from the tap, and Elodie drank it down and coughed again, wet and awful, and when he looked up there was something in his face Marcus had never seen there before. Not fear, exactly. Fear he'd seen, the night of the hatch, the night of the frozen breath in July. This was past fear. This was the look of a man who had gone looking for the edge of a cliff in the dark and found it with his foot.

"You sang." Marcus's own voice cracked on it. He didn't know how he knew. He just knew, the way you know a room has been cried in.

Elodie's jaw worked. "Needed to verify," he managed, and the hoarseness shredded the words into gravel. "Transcription. Had to confirm the melody matched."

"And?"

Elodie reached out with a trembling hand and turned the laptop around. On the screen, paused, was an audio file timestamped 3:31 a.m. He pressed play.

His own voice, thin and flat and human, sang the first verse. Marcus's skin crawled at the words — his grandmother's words, forbidden past the second verse, and here was this stubborn, impossible man singing them into the dark like a dare. And then the recording caught it: the drop in temperature rendered as a faint crackle on the mic, the creak of the house adjusting, and then the humming. Low. Warm. *Delighted.* It continued into the second verse, patient as tide, and under it, barely audible, the sound of Elodie's own breathing going ragged, and under that, almost lost, a sound Marcus recognized from his worst nights: the many-jointed thing moving close beneath the floorboards, directly below the table, directly below the chair where Elodie sat.

Marcus stopped the playback. His hand was shaking. "It answered you."

"It answered me." Elodie's ruined voice made the sentence sound like a confession. "Four seconds. I sang eight lines and it picked up the melody like we'd rehearsed." He pulled a page across the table — the transcription, three verses in careful print, the fourth trailing off into ellipses and question marks. "Three verses complete. Fourth verse is mostly destroyed. Tape damage, or deliberate. Six words recoverable, maybe." He tapped the scratched-out margin note. "And it doesn't care who sings. That was the theory I was testing. The ledger says Thorne, generation after generation, but the theory says the contract clause is about the song, not the blood." He looked up, and his eyes were fever-bright in the gray face. "I'm not a Thorne, Marcus. And it sang back to me like I was family."

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, a bird called once and thought better of it.

"Your voice," Marcus said. "What did it cost you? Because I've read the ledger now, Elodie. I know what the payments are. I know what she got, and I know what it took."

Elodie was silent a moment. When he answered, the hoarseness made it worse, made it true. "Don't know yet. Voice is wrecked. Feel like I ran a marathon underwater." He flexed his fingers, examined them. "Or maybe the payment isn't taken up front. Maybe it's invoiced." He attempted a smile; it didn't survive the trip. "Either way. Now we know the mechanism works. Now we know the fourth verse exists somewhere outside that tape. Hymnals, parish records, the county archive. Somebody struck it out once — you can hear the erasure in the recording, it's deliberate, somebody in the last century decided the song needed to be three verses forever." He pushed the page toward Marcus. "Which means somebody knew there was a way to end it. And wrote it down. And then buried it."

Marcus took the page. The three verses stared up at him in Elodie's precise print, and even unsung, even silent on the paper, he could feel them pulling at something behind his sternum, a tuning-fork hum in the bones. He looked at the fourth verse's broken line, the six recovered words sitting orphaned among the gaps, and felt the house lean in around them, listening.

"We do this together," Marcus said. "No more solo tests. You sang alone in my kitchen at three in the morning and the thing under my house tried to adopt you. Next time, we're both in the room, and one of us keeps the other anchored. Deal?"

Elodie looked at him for a long moment — the skeptic, the trapper, the man who billed the world to keep it at arm's length — and nodded, slow, like the motion cost him.

"Together," he rasped. Then he coughed, and winced, and added, in a whisper that was almost his old dry tone: "But I'm billing you for the throat specialist."

Outside the window, high on the glass, unnoticed by either of them, a single line of frost drew itself and faded — quick, exploratory, like a finger testing whether the pane remembered it.

Five weeks to first frost. And somewhere in the county archive, three missing words waited in the dark.
