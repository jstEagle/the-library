## 16. Father's Bargain

The car came up the drive at dusk, a long silver thing that had never once been rained on, and Marcus knew before it stopped who sat behind the wheel.

He had not seen his father in four years. He had told himself the distance was ordinary — the ordinary estrangement of a son who wanted the house sold and a father who would sooner have sold the son — but standing now on the front steps of Cold Spring House, watching the driver's door open with the slow deliberation of a man who had never hurried for anything, Marcus felt the truth of it settle into his bones like the cold coming off the spring. His father had not come for a reconciliation. His father had come because the frost had started writing on the windows, and somewhere in whatever network of old men and older debts the Thornes kept, the news had traveled.

Edmund Thorne climbed the steps without offering his hand. He was seventy years old and looked fifty, and he had always looked fifty, and Marcus had spent his whole childhood assuming this was what wealth did — smoothed a man, preserved him like wax fruit — before he understood that it was not the wealth at all.

"You look terrible," Edmund said.

"You look exactly the same," Marcus said. "That's worse."

Something flickered behind his father's eyes — not guilt, Marcus realized. Recognition. The flicker of a man who has been caught and has already decided not to care.

"Is the other one here? The exterminator." His father said the word the way he said the names of towns he drove through without stopping.

"Elodie. And he's not an exterminator, he's—"

"He's whatever I need him to be elsewhere for the next few hours." Edmund stepped past him into the hall, and the house — Marcus would have sworn it, later, would have sworn it on anything — seemed to lean toward him. The lullaby was playing in the parlor, as it always played now, from devices no one had turned on, and his father paused in the doorway to listen with the expression of a man hearing a hymn in his childhood church. "It remembers me," Edmund said softly. "Good."

"That's not a good thing."

"It's the only good thing this family has ever had, and you'd know that if you'd been raised properly instead of sentimentally." His father turned. In the lamplight his face was smooth as the inside of a shell. "Where is the hatch?"

* * *

Marcus had not wanted to tell him. That was the whole of his strategy, if it could be called a strategy — to stand between his father and the sealed room above the spring the way his grandmother had once stood between Marcus and the well itself, her hand flat against his chest, her voice gentle and absolute: *never look down, darling, the water looks back.*

But the house wanted his father to find it. That was the thing he could not explain to Elodie afterward, the thing that made Elodie go gray and start checking the seals on the hatch with a torque wrench, as if physics were the problem. The house wanted it. Doors that had been locked stood open. The draft that always breathed up from beneath the floor had changed direction, pulling now, drawing the warm air of the house down into the dark like a slow inhale. And when Marcus stood in the corridor outside the spring room and refused to move, his father simply walked past him, and the corridor did not resist, and Marcus understood that he had never once been the one standing guard. He had been the one being kept.

The hatch was open when they reached it.

Elodie's padlock — the good one, the one Elodie had bought with the grim satisfaction of a man spending someone else's money on a problem he didn't believe in — hung from the hasp, unlocked, its shackle resting closed as if in courtesy. The candles Elodie kept down there for light were already lit. The cold came up out of the floor in a column, and Marcus's breath bloomed white in July, and below, through the square of the hatch, the spring lay black and still and patient as an eye.

His father looked down into it with something that was almost tenderness.

"Fifty years," Edmund said. "She renewed it in '74. I sat on those stairs when I was your age and listened to her sing, and I swore I'd never do it. I swore I'd let it starve." He smiled, and it was the smile Marcus had loved as a boy, the one that had made him feel chosen. "Then I watched her live to ninety-four while my brother died at sixty-one of nothing at all. Just nothing. Just the ordinary cold of the world, and no one singing against it." He began, unhurried, to remove his coat. He folded it over the rail of the old chair by the wall, the chair with the tally marks worn into its arm by generations of waiting hands. "I've had fifty years to change my mind, Marcus. I changed it around year ten."

"She's dead," Marcus said. "The thing below — whatever it is — it took her in the end. Elodie found the ledger. She paid and paid and it took her anyway."

"It took her at ninety-four, in her sleep, warm, with the winter that year the mildest in a century." His father's voice was patient, the patience of a man correcting a child's arithmetic. "You call that taking. I call it terms honored." He rolled up his sleeves, and his forearms were smooth, unspotted, the arms of a man who had never worked or weathered anything. "The renewal takes a Thorne. It doesn't care which. Your grandmother's notes are clear enough — I've had them since the funeral. And you've been moping around this house for months letting that pest man run his little traps while the frost counts down on the glass, so I've come to do what you were too soft to do."

"You don't know what it costs."

"I know exactly what it costs. I've read every page of that ledger, including the parts your grandmother thought she'd hidden." He stepped to the edge of the hatch. Below, the water did not move, and yet Marcus felt it attend — felt the whole cold weight of the thing beneath the town turn its attention upward like a flower finding the sun. "It costs a song. It has always cost only a song. You've let the exterminator's fear make a monster of it."

"Elodie's fear is the only sane thing in this house."

"Then it's a good thing," his father said, "that he isn't here."

And Marcus understood, with the slow horror of a dream where the stairs keep arriving, that this was why the doors had opened. Why the padlock sat unlocked. Why the draft pulled downward. His father had not come to the house. The house had gone out to meet him, and had arranged everything, and had cleared the room of everyone whose voice might complicate the transaction.

"Father," Marcus said. "Listen to me. The ledger isn't what you think. The payments — she wrote the dates, she wrote the verses, but Elodie worked out the arithmetic, and the years she gained, they weren't *added*, they were—"

But Edmund Thorne had already begun to sing.

* * *

It was the lullaby. Of course it was the lullaby — the same slow minor melody that drifted through the house at all hours now, the one his grandmother had hummed over the sink, the one she had forbidden him past the second verse. His father's voice was beautiful. That was the unbearable part. It was a trained voice, a voice that had been kept like the house itself, polished and unused, and it filled the spring room the way cold water fills a glass, from the bottom up.

Marcus stood at the top of the hatch steps and felt the song move through him like a current through wet rope. His lips ached to follow. His lungs ached. The melody was not a thing he was hearing; it was a thing he was *remembering*, verse by verse, a thing that had been folded into him before language, and the third verse rose in his throat unbidden and he bit down on it, hard, tasting blood, holding the rail with both hands while his father's voice poured down into the dark.

*Hush now, the spring is sleeping,*

*hush now, the frost is near,*

*give it your warm years keeping,*

*and it will keep you here—*

The water answered.

Not with sound. Marcus would spend the rest of his life failing to describe what it answered with — a pressure, a rising, the sensation of a vast attention coming up the shaft of the well like a diver surfacing, and the candles all bent sideways though there was no wind, and the temperature dropped so fast that frost bloomed across the inside of the hatch boards in feathering white branches, and his father's voice climbed to meet it, joyful now, triumphant, a man closing a contract he believed he had read to the last line.

*Third verse, and the bargain's sealed,*

*the winter soft, the harvest healed,*

*a Thorne above, a thirst below,*

*and fifty years shall sweetly flow—*

Marcus saw it happen.

He would never be able to unsee it, and he knew that too, in the moment, with the terrible double vision of catastrophe: he saw it and understood he was watching it and would carry the watching forever. His father's hands, gripping the hatch edge, went pale — not bloodless pale but *chalk* pale, the pale of Marcus's own sleepwalking hands, the pale the house gave things it was handling — and then the hands aged. Not wrinkled. *Aged.* The skin thinned and spotted and loosened over the tendons like paper over wire, and the hair at his father's temples went white in a spreading tide, and his spine curved, and his beautiful trained voice climbed and climbed and then cracked down into something old and ruined, and he kept singing, he couldn't stop, the melody dragging out of him now like thread from a spool, faster, the thing below taking the song at its own pace and taking the man at the same rate.

"Father—" Marcus was down the steps, he didn't remember descending, his knees hit the stone beside the hatch and he grabbed his father's shoulders and they felt wrong, light, the shoulders of a coat on a hanger, and Edmund Thorne turned his face to his son and Marcus did not know him. Seventy years fell on him in the space of a verse. Eighty. His eyes sank into their sockets and went the color of dirty ice, and still the voice came, and Marcus realized the song was no longer coming *from* him — it was coming *through* him, the thing below singing its own renewal with his father's ruined throat as the instrument, and the payment running backward, the years being drawn out of him not added to him, decades pouring down into the black water like sand through a torn bag.

"You said—" his father gasped, between notes, and his voice was an old man's voice, a dying man's, "you *said*—"

The song stopped.

Mid-verse. Mid-word. The silence that followed had texture, weight, the silence of a held breath, and in it Marcus held his father, who weighed almost nothing now, who was ninety-something and shrinking, whose skin had gone translucent at the temples so Marcus could see the faint pulse of a heart working far too hard.

"It changed the terms," Marcus whispered, to the water, to the dark, to whatever listened. "Why did it change the terms?"

And his father began to laugh, or weep, or both, a thin cracked sound, and clutched Marcus's wrist with fingers like a bird's feet, and said, in a voice that had gone from beautiful to broken in the length of a song:

"The cold. Oh — oh, Marcus, it's under *everything* — under the town, under the ground, under the *years* — it was never fed, we were never *paying* it, we were *lending* — it lends the cold and it calls the warmth home and I gave it — I gave it *everything I'd borrowed* —"

His father looked at him then with eyes that were suddenly, horribly clear, and said the thing that Marcus would carry beside the image of the aging hands for the rest of his life:

"It was never a bargain, boy. It was a *loan*. And the interest—" he shuddered, and the shudder ran through him like a frost through glass, branching, "—the interest is *always* collected in the end. Your grandmother knew. That's why the ledger's in the attic and not the bank. That's why she taught you the first two verses and no more. She was keeping you *ignorant* because ignorance was the only inheritance that wouldn't cost you."

"Then why did you come?" Marcus heard his own voice crack. "If you knew — why sing it?"

Edmund Thorne smiled with the ruined mouth of a man of ninety, and in the smile was the father Marcus had loved, and the stranger who had raised him, and they were the same man, and had always been.

"Because I'm seventy years old," he said, "and I'd have been dead at fifty without it, and I'd do it again, and *that*, Marcus, is what it buys — not years. *Certainty.* The end of doubt. It lets you stop being afraid of the cold, right up until—" His hand tightened on Marcus's wrist. His eyes were going glassy, the pupils widening, fixing on something far below the floor, below the foundations, below, perhaps, the world. "Right up until it asks for the principal. Tell the exterminator—" a breath, rattling, cold, "—tell him the fourth verse is real. She knew. It's in the *margins*. She wrote it backwards so she couldn't—"

He stopped.

Not died — Marcus would learn the difference in the weeks after — but *stopped*, the way a clock stops, the way a song stops mid-phrase, and the frost on the hatch boards bloomed outward in one slow perfect spiral, and the spring below gave off no sound at all, and the cold in the room settled down over the two of them like snow.

Marcus held his father in the dark above the black water and understood, finally, with a clarity that felt like a wound opening: the price had never been fair. Not for his grandmother. Not for his father. Not for any Thorne who had ever stood at this hatch and traded warmth for warmth and called it a bargain because the alternative was to call it what it was.

It had been a debt. Passed down like a title. And now his father had defaulted, and the debt had come home, and Marcus sat on the cold stone with the dying man in his arms and the lullaby — soft, patient, from somewhere upstairs, from every device in the house at once — began to play the fourth verse's melody for the first time.

Except the fourth verse had no melody. He had never heard one. Elodie's transcription ended in scratches.

This melody was new.

And it was teaching itself to him, note by note, as his father's breath slowed, and somewhere above, the front door opened, and Elodie's voice came down the corridor, hoarse and urgent, calling his name.
