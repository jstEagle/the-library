## 22. Teaching the First Line

The porch of Cold Spring House had been Marcus's grandmother's favorite place in the world, though he had never understood why until this autumn. It faced east, toward the orchard gone soft and overgrown, and in the mornings the light came through the old maples in long amber blades that lay across the floorboards like something spilled and drying. She had kept a rocking chair out here, and a tin of peppermints, and a patience he had mistaken for idleness. Now he sat where she used to sit, in a chair he'd bought at the hardware store because hers had finally given out, and across from him, on the top step, sat Elodie Voss with his arms crossed over his knees and his face arranged into the expression of a man being asked to do something professionally beneath him.

"You want me to memorize it," Elodie said.

"I want you to *know* it."

"Those are different words for the same thing."

"No," Marcus said. "They're not. Not anymore."

It was the third week of October, six weeks after the frost had retreated from the glass like a tide reversing, five weeks after they had buried his father in the churchyard at Cold Spring under a sky so clear and cold it seemed polished. The house was warm. That was the strangest part, still — walking through its rooms and feeling warmth come up through the floorboards like the house itself had a pulse again, like the wound beneath the spring had scabbed over rather than simply been ignored. The traps stayed empty. The monitors recorded nothing but mice in the walls, ordinary and mortal and welcome, and once — only once, but Marcus had heard it too, standing in the kitchen doorway at three in the morning while Elodie played it back four times — a single held breath beneath the floor, long and low and patient as a man waiting behind a door he has agreed not to open.

But today was not about the breath beneath the floor. Today was about the song.

Marcus had thought about how to do this for weeks. He had written it down and burned the paper. He had tried saying it silently to himself in the shower and felt the water go cold around his shoulders, the house listening even now, even kindly, even *waiting*. He had decided, finally, that there was no safe way. There was only a shared way. A thing carried by one throat could be taken by one throat. A thing held by two was a vigil, and a vigil could not be seduced, because a vigil was not a hunger — it was a promise made between people who stayed awake on purpose.

"Say it back to me first," he said. "What I told you last week. Why we're doing this."

Elodie rolled his eyes with the theatrical weariness of a man who had spent thirty years explaining to clients why their ghost problem was actually raccoons. "Because your family treated the hymn like a title deed. One bloodline, one singer, one generation at a time, each one holding it alone so it could be leveraged against them." He recited it flatly, without inflection, the way he read trap specifications aloud. "And because the last time it lived in one throat, that throat nearly swallowed you whole. So we break the monopoly. We make it common property. Two people knowing it means neither one can be cornered by it alone."

"And?"

Elodie's jaw worked. He looked out at the orchard, at the light coming down through the maples, and when he spoke again his voice had lost its professional flatness. "And because I spent forty years believing nothing existed until I could trap it, tag it, or bill it. And then I spent one July learning otherwise." He turned back. "I don't believe in it any more gently than I did. I just believe in it now. There's a difference, and the difference is that I'd rather know the shape of the thing than pretend it doesn't have one."

"That's all I'm asking," Marcus said. "Not faith. Just knowledge. Held together."

He began to teach.

* * *

Elodie had expected it to be harder than it was, and it was worse than hard.

Not the words themselves. The words were simple — an old dialect, yes, the same archaic English the voice below the spring had spoken into his microphones that first terrible night, but simple, almost childlike, built of short lines that repeated with small variations the way a mother repeats herself to a child who keeps waking. *Low in the water, low in the stone, / what sleeps beneath us is never alone.* That was the opening couplet. He wrote it in his notebook and immediately crossed it out, because writing it down felt like baiting a trap with himself as the lure, and Marcus had nodded grimly when he said so. "She never wrote it either," Marcus said. "There's no copy anywhere. That's the point. It only lives in people who choose to carry it."

So they said it aloud, on the porch, in daylight, with coffee going cold between them, and Elodie learned it the way he had learned everything in his life: by repetition, by attention, by refusing to be impressed.

That lasted until the fourth repetition.

On the fourth time through the opening verse, the air changed. Nothing dramatic — no drop in temperature, no frost on the railings, none of the theater the house had performed in July. It was subtler than that. It was that the words began to fit his mouth better than they should have. His tongue found the old vowels without stumbling. The rhythm settled into his chest like a key settling into a lock it had been cut for, and somewhere behind his sternum he felt a faint, horrible *rightness*, the sensation of a sentence completing itself, and he understood with total clarity that this was how it worked — this was how it had worked on Marcus's grandmother for ninety-four years, this was how it had worked on his father, this was how it had very nearly worked on Elodie himself in the archive and later at the kitchen table with the pen moving in his hand like something piloted. The song didn't force you. It *fit*. It waited to be wanted.

He stopped mid-line. Set his coffee down with exaggerated care, because his hands wanted to shake and he refused to let them.

"It's trying to be comfortable," he said.

Marcus had gone pale. "You felt it."

"I felt it *fitting*." Elodie looked at his own mouth as if it belonged to someone else. "Like a shoe half a size wrong that you decide not to notice. That's the mechanism, isn't it? That's the whole trick. Not compulsion. Comfort."

"Not compulsion. Comfort." Marcus repeated it slowly, tasting it, and Elodie watched him arrive at the same place he had: that every Thorne for two hundred years had told themselves they were bound, obligated, duty-bound, when the truth was simpler and crueler. The song had simply made itself easy to carry, and no one had ever asked why carrying it felt so much like being carried.

"So we say it ugly," Elodie said.

"What?"

"We say it *ugly*. Deliberately. Wrong rhythm, flat voice, my accent, your stumbles. If it wants to fit, we keep it ill-fitting. A coat that never quite sits right on either of us." He picked the coffee back up. "My mother sang in a choir for forty years. You know what she taught me about hymns? The dangerous ones are the beautiful ones. The ones that give you chills. Chills are just your body agreeing to something your mind hasn't voted on yet."

Marcus stared at him, and then — for the first time since the burial, maybe for the first time since the dawn he had stood at the black spring and sung the unbinding with Elodie's flat, unsung voice reading the fourth verse over him like a rope thrown to a drowning man — he laughed. It came out cracked and startled, a laugh surprised out of hiding, and it broke something loose in the air between them that had been taut for weeks.

"Ugly," Marcus said. "Grandmother would have fainted."

"Your grandmother," Elodie said, "kept a ledger of purchased decades and let her neighbors sicken every fifty winters. Her opinion of proper singing is noted and disregarded."

The laugh died, but gentler things replaced it. They went back to work, and this time they worked the way Elodie insisted: deliberately graceless, Elodie mangling the vowels with his flat midlands consonants, Marcus exaggerating his own hesitations, both of them stopping whenever the words began to feel easy, breaking the rhythm on purpose, arguing about the weather in the middle of verses. It was the least reverent recitation the hymn had received in two centuries, and Elodie suspected — though he would not have sworn to it, because he had learned the limits of what he would swear to — that somewhere beneath the floor, the patient breath listened to its own song being handled like secondhand furniture, and found it unbearable, and that this, precisely this, was the point.

By late afternoon, Elodie knew the first verse. All of it. Cold, in his mouth, ill-fitting, his.

"There," Marcus said quietly. "Now it's not an inheritance. Now it's a watch schedule."

* * *

They ate dinner in the kitchen — actual dinner, cooked, eaten at the table, a domesticity that still astonished Elodie when he caught himself inside it — and afterward they carried their chairs out to the porch because the evening was mild for October and neither of them wanted to go inside yet. This had become the shape of their days without either of them announcing it. Elodie had packed his equipment twice. Both times he had unpacked it. The second time, Marcus had watched him do it from the doorway and said nothing, and the nothing had said enough, and Elodie had put the monitors back on the shelf in the pantry — the same pantry where he'd found those prints in the dust, too large, too many joints, pressed outward from behind the wall — and that had been that.

The stars came out. Somewhere down the valley a dog barked twice and gave up. The house breathed warmth at their backs like a sleeping animal that had decided, provisionally, to be tame.

"Do you hear it?" Marcus asked eventually. "Still. At night."

"Once a night. The breath." Elodie turned his cup in his hands. "Same time, give or take. Three-something. Long inhale, longer hold. Then nothing." He paused. "It's not threatening. I've listened to enough threatening sounds to know the difference. It's..." He searched for the word and hated the one he found. "*Attentive.* Like it's taking attendance."

"Waiting for us to miss a night."

"Or waiting to see if we mean it." Elodie shrugged. "Could be either. Could be both. I've stopped pretending I know what's under there. I know what it offered me. Years, certainty, the end of doubt — doubt, specifically, like it knew exactly which of my organs to reach for. And I know it took your father apart in eleven minutes when he tried to steal what wasn't freely his." He looked over. "I know it honors terms. That's the most frightening thing about it, if you want the truth. It's *lawful*. Everything horrible I've ever dealt with was just hungry. This one keeps contracts."

"Then we keep ours."

"We don't have one. That's the whole design." Elodie set the cup down. "No renewal, no payment, no singer. Just two men who know a song and refuse to sing it, forever, and one thing underneath that has to respect the refusal because the refusal is free. That's what the fourth verse bought us. Not silence. *Consent*, withdrawn permanently." He rubbed his eyes. "Though I'll tell you what keeps me up, and it's not the breath."

"What?"

"Someday one of us will be old, or sick, or grieving, or lonely at three in the morning, and the song will be right there, fitting our mouth like it was measured for us. And the other one might be dead, or moved away, or angry." He said it plainly, because he had promised himself he would never again manage Marcus with omissions; omission was the family disease, and he was not a Thorne. "A vigil only works if someone's always awake. So we build it so someone always is. That's the rest of the design. Not just two throats. A chain. Whoever knows it teaches it — freely, chosen, no blood required. Your grandmother hoarded it and it ate her family for two hundred years. We give it away and it starves."

Marcus was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "You realize what you're describing. You're describing teaching it to strangers. To whoever comes after us. To—"

"To anyone who chooses it. Which is the difference between a burden and a vigil. Nobody volunteers for a burden." Elodie glanced sideways at him. "Starting with you. Say it back."

And Marcus, heir of Cold Spring, last of the line that had fed the spring for two centuries, said the opening verse back to the man who had come to kill an infestation and stayed to keep a watch — said it flat and ugly and ill-fitting, in the dark, on the porch, with the frost still weeks away and the windows clear as clean water — and the words were only words, and the night was only night, and the house around them was warm.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, past the hour when the breath beneath the floor rose and held and released, which both of them had heard through the open window and acknowledged with nothing more than a glance — Elodie stood at the porch rail smoking the cigarette he allowed himself one per season, and Marcus stood beside him not smoking, and the valley lay silver and still below them.

"One more thing," Marcus said. "Before winter."

"Mm."

"When you teach it to someone someday. However many years from now. Whoever it is." Marcus looked straight ahead, at the dark orchard, at the east where the light would come from. "Tell them the last part. Tell them it isn't a spell and it isn't a shield and it won't save anybody. Tell them it's just a way of staying awake together. That's all it ever should have been. A lullaby somebody corrupted into a contract, and us corrupting it back."

"A lullaby," Elodie said. "For the thing underneath."

"For *us*. So we don't fall asleep alone." Marcus smiled, tired and real. "Grandmother had it backwards her whole life. It was never for keeping it fed. It was for keeping each other company during the watch. She just couldn't imagine sharing it, so it curdled."

Elodie considered this with the gravity he gave to evidence. Then he ground out the cigarette and said, "First lesson's tomorrow, then. There's a woman at the archive — the one who pulled the hymnal fragment for me. Sharp as a tack, doesn't scare easy, asked better questions about the fourth verse than I did." He paused. "And she makes a decent pot of tea, which matters, because vigils run on tea."

"You're recruiting already."

"I'm building a rota." Elodie headed for the door, then stopped, one hand on the frame, and looked back at the dark valley, at the house behind him breathing its slow warmth, at the floor beneath them all and the lawful, patient, attentive thing that slept there — that would always sleep there, that they had not ended but only renegotiated, that would listen every night at three for as long as the house stood. "Goodnight," he said to it, clearly, conversationally, the way you speak to a neighbor whose fence line you've finally agreed upon. "We're awake. Both of us. Write it down."

Beneath the floor, in the black and perfectly still water, something that honored terms held its silence — and if it was not quite peace, it was, for the first time in two hundred years, *agreed*.

Marcus turned off the porch light. In the dark, side by side, they went in to keep the watch.
