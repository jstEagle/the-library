## 9. The Refilling

Wendell woke at 3:14 a.m. to the sound of water where there should have been none.

She lay still in her grandmother's bed, listening, the way you listen when your body knows something before your mind consents to. The house was quiet—no pipes running, no rain against the roof, the night outside dry as chalk. But beneath the floorboards, beneath the crawlspace and the clay and the limestone the whole town sat on like a lid on a pot, she could hear it: a low, wet shifting, the sound of a great throat swallowing.

She got up. She didn't bother with shoes.

The old town well stood at the corner of Mill and Chapel streets, a stone ring waist-high, capped with an iron grate that had rusted into lacework sometime before Wendell was born. As a child she'd been told never to look down it, which had of course been the only instruction she ever obeyed, because obedience through terror was the one language children speak fluently.

Now she crouched beside it in the dark and shone her flashlight through the grate.

The water was six feet below the rim. She knew this because she'd measured it on her second day in town, out of pure professional habit—six feet down, black, stagnant, smelling of iron and old pennies. Her laser mapper had logged the depth twice more since. Six feet. Consistent. Boring. A dead well is a boring thing, and boring things were the currency of her sanity.

The water now sat eighteen inches below the rim.

It hadn't rained in eleven days.

Wendell pressed her palm flat against the stone ring. It was warm. Not sun-warm—the night air was cold enough to fog her breath—but warm the way skin is warm, warm the way a thing is warm when it has recently been alive or is about to be. And from somewhere below the waterline came a sound she would spend the rest of her life trying to unhear: a slow, rhythmic lapping, patient as breathing, as though something down there were tasting the rising water and finding it good.

Behind her, gravel crunched.

Hollis stood at the edge of the flashlight's reach, hands in the pockets of his overalls, mud stains exactly where they'd always been—left knee, right hip, a smear across the chest like a failed attempt at wings. He looked the way he always looked, which was to say he looked nine years old and forty years wrong.

"You shouldn't be out here," he said.

"Neither should you." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Nobody should be out here. That's rather the point of three in the morning."

"It's not three in the morning everywhere." He said things like that sometimes—small wrongnesses delivered with the flat calm of a child reciting times tables—and she had learned, mostly, to let them slide past her like stones skipped off water. "Come away from the well, Wendell."

"The water rose twelve feet overnight."

"I know."

"Wells don't do that."

"This one does." He took a step closer, and his bare feet made no sound on the gravel, which she noted and filed and refused to think about. "It's refilling early."

"Early according to what?"

He looked at her then, and there was something in his face she'd never seen in all the weeks since the porch—not patience, not that faintly amused watchfulness, but something older and sadder, the expression of someone watching weather come in over a horizon they can't warn anyone about.

"According to everything," he said. "Go home, Wendell. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow's going to want all of you."

* * *

By morning, the sinkhole behind the barn had changed its breath.

She'd been logging it daily since that first afternoon—a data series titled, with the dry humor she used as armor, *ANOMALOUS AIRFLOW, STRUCTURE 2*. Cool air exhaled for roughly ninety seconds, then drawn back in for sixty, cycle repeating with a regularity no natural karst system should manage. Forty-seven degrees Fahrenheit. Dry. Smelling faintly of stone and deep water.

At 7 a.m., standing at the fence line with her thermal probe, she watched the sinkhole exhale and saw vapor curl up out of the dark like breath on a winter morning.

Warm. Wet. Fifty-eight degrees and climbing, humid as a mouth.

She knelt at the edge, careful to keep her weight distributed across the plywood sheet she'd laid over the soft ground, and held the probe out over the void. Below her, the tunnel dropped into darkness, its carved grooves catching the morning light in thin bright lines—staff lines, she'd thought, though she'd told herself it was pareidolia, the human brain's desperate talent for finding meaning in random grooves. The air rising past her face carried a smell she couldn't place until suddenly she could: it smelled like the inside of the chapel. Candle wax and hymnals and old wood polish, rising out of the earth as if the church had grown roots and they went all the way down.

Her phone buzzed in her pocket. Then buzzed again. Then again, four times in quick succession, which in Harrow Glen constituted a state of emergency.

The first message was from the feed store: *You seeing this?*

The second was a photo, grainy, taken at dawn: the Hendersons' pasture, where six cattle lay dead in a perfect line, every one of them facing the barn. Not scattered. Not fallen where they stood. Arranged. Their bodies pointed toward the Henderson barn like compass needles, like a congregation kneeling toward an altar, and the grass around them was flattened in radiating lines, as if something had passed over them in waves.

The third message was from Deacon Pruitt, and it contained only an address and two words:

*Come now.*

* * *

Pruitt's house sat at the end of Lamentation Road—a name Wendell had assumed was local color until she'd checked the plat maps and found that half the roads in Harrow Glen had names like sermons. Inside, the house smelled of beeswax and coffee and something else, something underneath, like the air after lightning.

His wife's photograph still hung in the hallway. Ruth Pruitt, 1941–1985, her smile kind and her eyes already clouded, even then, like water with too much lime in it.

"She's been down there forty years," Pruitt said without preamble, setting two cups of coffee on the table though his hands shook so badly the cups chattered against the saucers. "And last night I dreamed I heard her singing again. First time in twenty years. You know what that means?"

"That you miss her."

"That the well's waking up." He sat down heavily across from her. His eyes were bad—clouded, like the photograph, like every old singer's eyes in this town—but they fixed on her with terrible precision. "The refill comes every hundred years, girl. My grandfather helped sing it back down in '24. My father in... well. It comes when it comes, and the choir sings it back to sleep, and we go on. But it's early. A hundred years isn't up. It's not supposed to rise for another decade, and instead—" He gestured vaguely eastward, toward the center of town, toward the well, toward the barn behind her grandmother's house. "Instead it's coming up like water in a basement."

"Why?" The question came out harder than she intended. "What changed?"

Pruitt was quiet for a long moment. The coffee steamed between them, untouched.

"You did," he said finally.

The word landed in her chest like a dropped stone.

"I've been doing this arithmetic for weeks," he said. "Trying to make it come out different. But the timeline doesn't lie. The water started rising the week you drove into town. The sinkhole started breathing warmer the day you surveyed it. Those cattle—" his voice caught, steadied, "—those cattle died the night after you recorded the tunnels. You've got instruments, Wendell. You've got machines that listen. Do you understand what it means, that something down there has spent a hundred years listening to us sing, and then one day hears a *recording*? Hears its own hymn played back by a machine that doesn't believe in it?"

"That's absurd. Sound is sound. A recording can't—"

"A recording is a promise," Pruitt said. "It says: this will happen again, whether you will it or not. It heard its own voice come from above instead of below, and it woke up thinking the bargain was broken. Thinking the choir had stopped and started mocking it." He rubbed his clouded eyes with a knuckle. "It heard you coming, child. From the moment you crossed the county line with all those antennas and lasers, it heard you coming, and it started climbing."

Wendell stood up so fast her chair skidded. She walked to the window because her legs wanted to walk and her face wanted to hide, and outside the glass the morning lay over Harrow Glen like a clean sheet over something dead.

"I'm a surveyor," she said. "I map holes in the ground. I don't wake anything up. There is no *it*, Mr. Pruitt. There's an aquifer behaving strangely, and a town with a folk tradition, and grief, and confirmation bias, and—"

"And two singers who collapsed last night."

She turned.

"Mid-verse," Pruitt said. "At the Wednesday gathering. Edna Marsh and Tom Beckett. Edna's seventy-one; they say her heart gave out. Tom's fifty-three, healthy as an ox, and they're calling it a stroke. But I was there, Wendell. I was singing bass, and I watched them go down, and here's what nobody else will say out loud: their mouths kept moving. After their hearts stopped, after the doctor knelt on them and worked them, their mouths kept moving. Shaping the words. Keeping time." His hands were shaking again, worse. "They weren't dying, girl. They were being *collected*. Called down early because the well needs voices and the choir's too small and getting smaller, and it knows it, and it's impatient."

Outside, far off, almost too faint to hear, someone was singing. One voice, wavering, coming from the direction of the chapel—though it wasn't Wednesday, and it wasn't evening, and there was no reason under heaven for anyone to be in that whitewashed building at ten in the morning singing a song meant only for the dark.

Pruitt heard it too. He closed his eyes.

"They're starting early," he whispered. "They're all starting early. Singing in shifts now, around the clock, trying to hold it down with fewer and fewer throats." He opened his eyes and looked at her, and Wendell saw that beneath the fear in his face was something worse: apology. "I begged you to learn the hymn, and you wouldn't. I understand why—I do. Believing is a door, and once it opens you can't close it. But the water's coming up faster now, and every voice we lose makes it come faster, and you—" he spread his trembling hands, "—you're the reason, and you may also be the only cure. That's the shape of it. I'm sorry. God help me, I'm so sorry."

* * *

She walked home the long way, past the chapel, because some part of her needed to see it with her own eyes.

The doors stood open. Inside, nine people sat in the pews—nine, where forty had sung last Sunday—and their voices filled the little building with the three verses she now knew by heart whether she wanted to or not. An elderly woman led them, her clouded eyes fixed on nothing. Beside her, a man in his thirties sang with his head bowed, sweat shining on his forehead though the morning was cool. They sang the first verse, and the second, and the third, and then began again at the first without pause, without breath, without any sign of stopping, and Wendell stood in the doorway and understood that she was looking at people who had become, in the space of a single night, a bucket brigade passing water hand to hand against a fire that never slept.

No one asked her to leave. No one asked her to join. They simply sang toward her, the melody bending slightly in her direction the way plants bend toward light, and she backed out of the doorway and walked fast and did not run, because running felt like something the dark below would enjoy watching.

The Hendersons' pasture was on her route home. She stopped at the fence because stopping was inevitable, the way looking at a car wreck is inevitable. The cattle had been removed, but the grass still held the radiating pattern of their deaths, and at the center of it, facing the barn, the ground itself had begun to sag—a shallow depression the size of a grave, then another beside it, then another, a field of dimples like the skin of something breathing just under the surface of the world.

Her phone camera was in her hand before she decided to raise it. She took one photograph. On the screen, the depressions arranged themselves into a pattern she refused to interpret, and she lowered the phone and walked on.

* * *

Dusk found her on the porch with her laptop open and her data spread across the screen like evidence at a trial she was losing.

The airflow graph from the sinkhole: temperature climbing steadily for six days, humidity climbing faster, the breathing rhythm shortening—ninety seconds exhale becoming eighty, becoming seventy, becoming a pant. The well depth log: six feet, six feet, five-eleven, five-eight, and then a vertical cliff of numbers plunging toward zero. Overlaid on both, marked in red because she'd marked it herself with hands that wanted to shake: the date she'd driven into Harrow Glen.

Every curve bent upward at that line. Every single one.

"You keep looking at it like it'll change," Hollis said.

He was sitting on the porch rail, which he did sometimes, though she'd never seen him climb onto it. He watched the last light drain out of the valley with the expression of a child watching parents argue through a wall—hearing everything, understanding most of it, powerless over any of it.

"Pruitt says I did this." She didn't look up. "That my equipment woke it. That I'm the reason the water's rising."

Hollis was quiet long enough that she finally raised her eyes.

"He's not wrong," Hollis said.

Something in her chest went very cold and very still. In all the weeks of his company, Hollis had deflected, hedged, spoken in riddles shaped like comfort. He had never once simply agreed with a terrible thing.

"Hollis."

"It heard you coming," he said. "Not the machines. *You.* The machines are just how you touch the world, Wendell, but underneath the touching, underneath the measuring, there's you—you, walking around with a hole in you shaped like this town, like this house, like me. Things that sleep a hundred years dream lightly. They feel footsteps. And you didn't just walk in, you came *home*, and homecoming rings like a bell, and it heard the bell, and it started climbing toward the sound." He swung his legs, a child's gesture, horribly casual. "Pruitt thinks it's the lasers. It's not the lasers. It's that you came back, and some part of you wanted to, and wanting is loud."

"I didn't want to come back. I came to sell a house."

"You came to sell a house," Hollis agreed gently, "the way people go to graves to say goodbye. And it heard the goodbye, and it heard the grief, and grief sounds like singing, Wendell. Grief sounds exactly like singing."

The laptop screen dimmed, then slept. In the black mirror of it she caught the reflection of the porch, the rail, the yard—and Hollis, sitting beside her, his mouth moving slightly, shaping notes she couldn't hear.

She turned to look at him directly. His mouth went still.

"What happens now?" she asked. "If the water keeps rising. If it reaches the brim."

Hollis looked out at the valley, where lights were coming on one by one in the windows of Harrow Glen, and behind each light, she understood now, someone was singing, or preparing to sing, or lying awake listening to neighbors sing, holding the dark down with their throats the way you hold down a lid with your whole body weight.

"Now," Hollis said quietly, "it starts asking for the fourth verse."

"There is no fourth verse. Pruitt said it was never written down."

"It was never written down," Hollis agreed, "because it can't be learned from paper. Or from mouths." He turned and looked at her, and for the first time since the porch, since she was nine years old, since the beginning of whatever he was, he looked afraid. "It can only be learned in one place. And the water's going to be at the brim by Friday."

Below them, beneath the floorboards, beneath the clay and the limestone and the hundred years of borrowed quiet, the ground breathed in—warm, wet, and shorter than yesterday—and did not breathe out for a very long time.
