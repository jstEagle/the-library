## 13. Pruitt Goes Down Singing

The knock came at four in the morning, and it was not a knock so much as a rhythm—three soft raps, a pause, three more—like someone testing whether the door of her grandmother's house still resonated at the right pitch.

Wendell had been awake anyway. She'd been awake most nights since the water in the old town well had come up over the stones, since the sinkhole behind the barn had started breathing warm and wet against the back of her hand like something panting in its sleep. She opened the door with the chain still on.

Deacon Pruitt stood on the porch in his good coat, the black one with the frayed cuffs, and he was holding a book against his chest the way you hold a hymnal in church—with both hands, spine out, as if it might be taken from him.

"Miss Okonkwo," he said. "I'd like to give you something before I go down."

The chain stayed on. "Go down where."

He didn't answer that. He held the book up instead, through the gap in the door, and she saw that it was bound in oxblood leather gone soft and bald at the corners, swollen with damp, older than anything else she'd touched in this town except the tunnels themselves.

"You came here to sell a house," Pruitt said. His voice was gentle about it, which made it worse. "I remember when your grandmother came here to do the same thing. Nineteen sixty-one. She never managed to leave either. Not because anyone stopped her." He smiled, and his clouded eyes caught the porch light and gave nothing back. "Because by then she knew too much to be anywhere else."

Wendell unhooked the chain. Not because she believed him—she had stopped believing in things in this town the way you stop believing in the honesty of a river that keeps taking pieces of the bank—but because refusing the book felt like refusing something heavier than a book.

* * *

They sat in the kitchen, the one with the yellow wallpaper her grandmother had never gotten around to replacing, and Pruitt set the hymnal on the table between them like a patient setting down a diagnosis.

"Open it."

She did. The pages were blank. Every one of them, front to back, blank—not empty the way an unused notebook is empty, but empty the way a room is empty after the furniture's been carried out, holding the shape of what had been there. When she tilted a page toward the light she could see faint depressions, ghost-grooves where words had once been pressed and then removed, and the grooves didn't run left to right. They ran in curves. In staff lines. In the long looping contours of a melody written out by hand across decades.

"The verses were taken out," Wendell said. "By whom?"

"By everyone who ever learned them. That's how it works. The song doesn't live on paper. Paper is just where we pretend to keep it." He turned the pages for her, past all the absences, to the last few leaves, and there—at the very end—the only ink in the book. Instructions. Diagrams. A drawing of a ribcage with arrows showing the direction of breath, drawn with the loving precision of an anatomist. A second drawing of a throat, cross-sectioned, with a note in faded brown script: *the note lives here, below speech, where you scream from.*

"It's a manual," Wendell said slowly. "For singing."

"For holding." Pruitt laid his hand flat on the page. "Anyone can sing, Miss Okonkwo. Children sing. Birds sing. What the well requires is different. It requires a voice that does not stop. You learn to breathe so the note never breaks—you take air in through the nose while pushing sound out through the mouth, a circle, a wheel turning—and you hold that note until your body forgets it was ever meant to do anything else." He looked at his own hands, spotted and knuckled like tree roots. "My wife held the low note for eleven years before she went down to finish the job. Forty years ago now. Some mornings I can still feel her in my chest, keeping time."

Wendell closed the hymnal. The sound of it shutting was very loud in the kitchen, and beneath it—faint, patient, coming up through the floorboards the way cold comes up through a cellar—she heard the well breathing.

"Why are you giving me this?"

Pruitt was quiet for a while. Outside, a bird started up and then thought better of it.

"Because I'm going down tonight," he said, "and after me there's no one left who knows the breath work. There's no one left who can teach it." He met her eyes, and for the first time since she'd arrived in Harrow Glen, the old man looked afraid—not of dying, she realized, but of the thing coming after the dying. "The choir is nine voices now. Nine, without me. The low note dies with me tonight unless you carry it. And if the low note dies, the hymn has a hole in it, and things pour through holes."

"I haven't agreed to any of this," Wendell said. "I don't believe in any of this."

"No," Pruitt agreed pleasantly. "Neither did my wife, right up until the day she caught herself humming verse two while hanging laundry, and realized she hadn't learned it from anyone. It learns *you*, child. Belief has nothing to do with it. Belief is just what we tell ourselves so the surrender feels like a choice."

* * *

He left the hymnal on the table and walked home through the blue dark, and Wendell stood in the doorway watching him go, small and straight-backed down the middle of the empty road, and she noticed that every house he passed had a light on. Every one. And behind every window, a silhouette, standing, watching him pass—the way you watch a coffin go by.

Hollis was sitting on the porch rail.

She'd stopped flinching at that. That was its own kind of horror, how quickly the flinching stopped.

"He's really going to do it," she said.

Hollis nodded. His face in the porch light was exactly nine years old and exactly wrong, the way a photograph of a living person is wrong once you know they're dead. "He's been getting ready for forty years. Practicing the circle-breath in his sleep. His wife taught him before she went under." Hollis swung his muddy boots, and the boots made no sound against the wood. "He wants to hear her again. That's what he told the choir on Wednesday. He said the low note and her note are a fifth apart, and when they're both sounding, it's like—" Hollis paused, searching, and Wendell realized with a lurch that he was searching for a feeling, reaching into himself for something human to describe it with. "It's like being held," he finished. "From underneath."

"That's not romantic," Wendell said. "That's drowning with extra steps."

"Yeah," Hollis said softly. "But it's what they've got."

* * *

They gathered at the sinkhole at dusk, all nine of them plus Wendell, standing in the trampled grass behind the barn where the ground fell away into the dark. The hole had widened again—she measured it out of habit, out of the stubborn reflex of a woman who trusted instruments more than sermons—and it breathed up at them, warm and wet and slow, carrying a smell like pennies and pond-bottom.

Pruitt stood at the edge in his good coat with his shoes off, set neatly side by side on the grass, toes pointing toward the pit. Someone had folded a towel over them. That detail undid Wendell more than anything else—the towel, the domestic tenderness of it, nine people who had done this enough times to have a protocol.

"We'll begin on my mark," Pruitt said, in the voice he probably used for announcing potlucks. "I'll take the note down as far as I can. When my voice gives out, the ground will carry it the rest of the way. Your job is the upper parts. Keep the harmony whole above me. Don't follow me down." He looked around at their faces, each in turn, a shepherd counting sheep. "Don't follow me down," he said again, gentler. "Not yet. Not until you're called."

Then he turned to Wendell.

"Miss Okonkwo. Would you do me the honor?"

Every instinct she owned said *no*. She stepped forward anyway. Her body had been making decisions without consulting her for days now.

"Hold the first pitch in your mind," he said. "Just in your mind. Don't let it out. While I'm descending, you keep it up there, in the attic of your skull, and you think of me whenever it tries to slip. Can you do that?"

"Why does it matter what I think?"

"Because the thing below listens through whatever's nearest," Pruitt said simply. "And lately, miss, that's you."

He faced the hole. He filled his lungs the way the diagrams showed—a slow spiral intake, ribs spreading like a bellows—and he began to sing.

It was not a note like other notes. It came out of the old man's chest at a pitch she felt in her fillings, in the fluid of her eyes, in the wet architecture of her inner ear, and it did not waver. Ten seconds. Thirty. A minute. His face went purple and calm, purple and calm, the circle-breath turning, air in through the nose and sound out through the mouth, a wheel, a wheel, and the sinkhole drank the note down and gave back harmonics she had no names for, and around her the eight remaining voices of the Choir of Harrow Glen rose to meet him, threading their parts above his descent like hands lowering a body into water.

And Wendell held the pitch in her mind. Just in her mind. She felt it sitting there behind her eyes, humming in the bone, and she hated how natural it felt, how *at home*, like a word in a language she'd been spoken as a baby and never forgotten.

Pruitt stepped off the edge mid-note.

He did not fall so much as descend, the way a bubble descends in reverse, and his voice kept coming up out of the ground long after his coat disappeared—longer than lungs should allow, longer than physics should allow, the note growing richer and lower as it went down, as if the earth itself were learning it from him, taking it into its throat. The choir sang. The ground sang. Somewhere in the middle, seamlessly, Pruitt's voice stopped being Pruitt's voice and became the well's voice, and nobody flinched, because everybody had heard this transition before.

Except Wendell. Wendell bit the inside of her cheek until she tasted copper and kept the pitch locked behind her teeth, and she understood, with terrible clarity, that this was the lesson. This was what the hymnal's blank pages were for. The last verse would come to her the same way—offered freely, rising up through the dark, asking only that she open her mouth—and everything depended on what she did with her mouth when the moment came.

The singing lasted two hours. Then the choir, eight voices ragged and shining with sweat, let the harmony resolve, and the sinkhole exhaled one long satisfied breath, and the water at the bottom—she could hear it now, she hadn't been able to before—was closer.

* * *

She measured it at midnight with a plumb line and a flashlight, lying on her stomach at the lip of the hole with her surveyor's discipline wrapped around her like armor.

Another foot. Twelve inches gained in a single day, where the refill had been taking a week per foot before. She did the arithmetic twice, hoping to be wrong both times, and the numbers came back the same: at this rate, the well reached the brim in six days. Maybe five.

She sat back on the dead grass and pulled Pruitt's hymnal into her lap and turned to the breath diagrams, and she practiced the circle-breath in the dark, alone, telling herself it was research, telling herself it was anthropology, telling herself all the lies a skeptic tells while her body learns the shape of its own surrender.

Hollis appeared beside her, cross-legged in the dirt, watching the plumb line sway over the void.

"Six days," she said.

"Five," Hollis said. "It speeds up when it's happy. Tonight made it happy. A new low note, all the way from the bottom, sung by somebody who loved what he was singing to." He picked at the mud on his overalls. "You should know what happens next, Wendell. With the choir down to eight and no deacon, the hymn gets thinner every night. Thinner hymn, louder dreams. The thing below is going to start reaching for whoever's closest to the surface of sleep." He looked up at her, and his nine-year-old eyes were full of something ancient and sorry. "It already knows your name. It's known it since you were little. Since it gave me to you."

"You were a gift," Wendell said flatly. "From the well."

"You were lonely," Hollis said, "and it was generous. Those aren't different things down there."

She looked at the hole. The plumb line hung dead still now, the breathing paused, as if the well were listening to them talk about it.

"Pruitt left me his book," she said. "His wife's been down there forty years. He went to join her. They call that love." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it. "What do you call it when the person who teaches you the song is the same thing the song is keeping asleep?"

Hollis was quiet a long moment.

"I call it the reason I'm begging you," he finally said, "to learn the fourth verse faster than it learns you."

Above them, in the house, every window lit up at once—warm yellow light spilling into the yard, though she had left not a single lamp burning—and from inside, faint and sweet and unmistakable, her grandmother's kitchen radio began to play a tune with no station behind it.

Verse one. Waiting for her to come in and practice.
