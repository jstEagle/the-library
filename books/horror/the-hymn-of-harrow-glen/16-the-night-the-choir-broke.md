## 16. The Night the Choir Broke

The chapel bell rang at seven, and I counted the cars that answered it.

Nine. I counted them twice, standing at the edge of the gravel lot with my hands in my jacket pockets, because the number refused to stay still in my head the first time. Nine cars for a chapel that used to hold the whole town, back when the photographs in the historical society's case showed folding chairs crammed into the aisles and children spilling down the front steps like spilled marbles. Nine cars. And two of those belonged to people who, I'd learned, no longer drove so much as steered.

The chapel itself hadn't changed since Wednesday. Whitewashed walls gone gray at the corners where the damp came up through the foundation. Pews worn smooth in the middle of each seat, hollowed like spoons. The hymnals in the racks were the same ones Pruitt had shown me—thin, cheap-bound, printed in 1931—and every copy fell open to the same page without anyone touching it, the way a dog turns to its master.

I sat in the back row. Nobody asked me to leave. That was the thing about Harrow Glen I kept failing to learn: they didn't need to ask, because the town had already decided what I was. After the second verse had taken my reflection, after they'd started setting a place for me at the diner counter and singing, on their porches in the evening, *toward* my house like a lullaby aimed through the walls—the town had quietly reclassified me. Not a visitor. Not a skeptic. Something in between, something being *prepared*.

I told myself I was there for data. I had the recorder in my pocket, the laser mapper in my bag, the notebook with Pruitt's breath instructions copied out in my own handwriting because I couldn't bear to let his hymnal out of the house. I told myself a lot of things that week.

The nine of them took the front pews. I recognized them all now, which was its own kind of horror—I'd been in town eleven days and I knew these people the way you know characters in a book you can't put down. Mrs. Aldous with her clouded eyes and her hands folded like a closed book. The Terhune brothers, who farmed the north fields and hadn't spoken a word to each other in years but harmonized like halves of one throat. A woman named Cora Finch who had waved at me every morning from her garden with the same smile, the same angle of the wrist, like a recording set to loop.

And at the center of them, where the low note should have come from, an absence shaped like a man. Deacon Pruitt had gone down the well three days ago, singing, and the hymn had been bleeding ever since.

There was a pause before they began. The kind of pause an orchestra takes when the conductor doesn't show and the first violin has to decide. Mrs. Aldous looked at the empty space beside her, looked for a long time, and then lifted her chin.

They sang.

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened, because precision is the last thing I own.

The first verse went the way it always went. That lullaby my grandmother sang to me over the smell of coffee and bacon, the one I'd learned before I could talk and only now understood I'd been *taught*—the melody rising soft and round, the words about water and sleep and the green dark under the hill. Nine voices. Thin. The chapel ate most of it, swallowed the sound into the rafters the way it had been swallowing it for a hundred and fifty years, and the walls did what walls do.

The second verse was where it started to go wrong.

The second verse needs a harmony line. I'd heard it on my recordings a dozen times, that low counter-melody that used to be Pruitt's—his wife's, before him, and before her, someone else, all the way down. The nine of them reached for it together and it wasn't there. It was like watching nine people reach for a handrail that's been removed. Their voices wobbled, searched, slid off each other. Mrs. Aldous's chin dropped an inch. The Terhune brothers glanced at each other for the first time in years, and what was in their faces wasn't blame. It was the look of people watching a fire start in a house they'd been told was fireproof.

And then the ground answered.

I felt it before I heard it—through the pew, through the soles of my boots, up the bones of my legs. A vibration with a direction to it, coming up from below, and then the sound itself: the missing harmony line, sung in a voice that was almost Pruitt's. Almost. It had his warmth, his grain, the little catch he took before the third phrase. It had everything except the breath behind it. It was Pruitt's voice the way a photograph of a man is the man—every detail correct, and nothing inside.

The choir kept singing. That was the part I couldn't get over, then or now. They didn't stop. Mrs. Aldous's eyes filled and spilled over, and she kept singing, because that was the covenant, that was the whole bargain: you sing, and the thing below holds up its end, and if your part falters, the well will lend you the note. The well was *helping*. The well was being generous. And every note it lent was a hook set a little deeper.

The third verse came, and the ground sang most of it.

By the last phrase, the nine human voices had thinned to a thread, and the sound rising through the floorboards had thickened to fill the chapel—Pruitt's low note, and beneath it other notes, older ones, voices I didn't recognize and then, horribly, one I did: a woman's alto, bright and sure, that I had heard on the surviving choir photograph's era of recordings, that I had heard in my own memory of a kitchen in 1994. My grandmother's voice, coming up out of the ground of the town where she'd been born, singing a hymn she'd been struck from the roster for refusing to finish.

I didn't run. I want that on the record of my own conscience, such as it still was. I sat in the back pew with my recorder running and I listened to my dead grandmother's voice rise through the floor, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like a physical blow, exactly what I had done.

It heard you coming. That's what Hollis had said. The refill was early because it had heard me coming—my mapper, my instruments, my skeptical, mapping, *measuring* attention, all of it a kind of knocking. And then I'd learned the first verse, and the tunnels had answered, and I'd learned the second, and the ground had learned me right back. And now Pruitt was down there holding a note in the dark because I hadn't been fast enough, because I'd spent three days telling myself I had time, and the well was filling his absence the way water fills a hull, and it was filling it with *songs it had stolen from the people it had taken*.

The hymn ended. The ground held the final chord a moment longer than the singers did, savoring it, and then let it go.

Nobody spoke. Mrs. Aldous wiped her face with a handkerchief that had been washed soft as cloth can get, and the nine of them rose and filed out into the dark, and I sat alone in the chapel for a long time, listening to the floorboards settle, listening for the thing beneath them to settle too.

It didn't. Beneath the silence there was a hum, patient and content, like a throat after a good meal.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I went home and played the recording back and heard what I'd known I'd hear: the ground's voice on the tape was not one voice. It was dozens, layered, a choir of everyone the well had ever taken, and the tape picked up something else too—between the phrases, in the gaps, a sound like a great slow inhalation. The sinkhole behind the barn, breathing in. The refill coming up the throat of the town.

At some point past two in the morning I found Hollis sitting on the kitchen counter, which he'd never done before. He always sat on things that belonged to children—porch steps, fence rails, the edge of a bathtub. The kitchen counter at night, in the dark, was new, and it frightened me more than anything he'd said all week.

"You have to finish," he said. "Tonight, tomorrow. You can't wait for the weekend to be convenient."

"I'm learning as fast as I can."

"You're learning as fast as you can *while telling yourself you might still walk away*." His voice was patient, slightly wrong, the voice I'd built for him at nine out of patience and wrongness, and it had never once lied to me. "The third verse is already in your mouth. You bit your lip awake two nights ago. It's not waiting for you to be ready, Wendell. It's waiting for you to be *ripe*."

"I'm not doing this for the town." It came out harsher than I meant. "I'm doing it because Pruitt went down holding a note I was supposed to be learning, and because Cora Finch waves at me every morning and I don't even know if she knows she's doing it, and because—" I stopped.

"Because you hummed at the window last night," Hollis said gently. "I know. I was there."

"I don't remember humming."

"I know."

He slid off the counter and stood in the dark kitchen, small and muddy and twenty years unaged, and for a moment he looked like what he was—not my friend, not my invention, but a fragment of something vast that had gotten loose and learned to love being small. "The choir broke tonight," he said. "You understand what that means. It's not a metaphor. The hymn is a machine and it has a broken part, and the thing below is going to keep lending notes until the lending *is* the hymn, and then it won't need singers at all. It'll have everyone's voices already. It'll sing itself awake."

"How long?"

"Days," Hollis said. "Maybe less. The water's a foot from the brim of the old well. You've seen it."

I had. I'd stood at the stone rim that afternoon with my depth gauge and watched the black water sit a foot below the lip, perfectly still, perfectly level, like an eye that had finished opening.

"Then I go down tomorrow night," I said. "I need one more day to get the third verse solid. I need to be able to hold all three in my head at once, silent, while it—" I couldn't finish. *While it offers me the last verse in my grandmother's voice.* "One day."

Hollis looked at me for a long time. There was something in his face I'd never seen on it, and it took me a moment to name it, because I'd only ever seen it on adults: it was the look of someone deciding to let a child believe a kind thing a little longer.

"One day," he said.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of well water, gray-green and dim, and I was at my bedroom window with a cup of coffee I'd made and not touched when I saw them.

Three figures on the road, walking toward the barn. Walking in the loose, unhurried way of people out for a morning stroll, except it was six in the morning and the road went nowhere but the sinkhole.

Mrs. Aldous first. She had put on her good coat, the wool one with the brass buttons, the one she'd worn to the chapel every Wednesday of her life, and she walked with her hands folded in front of her like she was processing down an aisle. Behind her came one of the Terhune brothers—I never learned which, and I will carry that to my grave, that I watched a man walk into the earth and never learned his name. And behind him, waving at me as she passed my gate, the same smile, the same angle of the wrist, Cora Finch.

I hammered on the glass. I don't know what I thought the glass would do. I shouted their names—Aldous, Terhune, Cora—and Cora Finch turned her head and smiled up at me, and her mouth moved around words I couldn't hear through the window, and I have spent every night since trying to read her lips, and every night I read something different. *Come with us. It's warm. You're late. You're next.* All of them true.

They went through the gate behind the barn, the three of them, in single file, and the sinkhole took them without ceremony. No scream, no sound of falling. The breathing air rose up around them like a curtain drawn aside and let them in, and then it closed, and the ground was smooth and quiet, and three more voices joined the choir below.

I stood at the window with the coffee going cold in my hand and my own throat working around a melody I had not chosen to hum. I felt it in my chest before I heard it in my ears—the first verse, rising on its own, my lips shaping it, my lungs feeding it, my body singing toward the three people who had just walked into the dark because I had needed one more day.

I clamped my jaw shut. The melody kept going behind my teeth, a hum with no exit, buzzing in the bones of my skull like a trapped wasp. I hummed the whole first verse that way, silent, jaw locked, tears I didn't remember starting running down into the coffee I still hadn't touched, and when the verse ended my mouth tried to begin the second and I had to press both hands over my face and breathe through my fingers until it passed.

It took four minutes. I know because I timed it, because timing things is the only prayer I know.

When I took my hands away, Hollis was standing in the doorway of my bedroom, and he wasn't looking at me. He was looking at the window, at the sinkhole, at the place where the ground had closed over three of the last nine voices in Harrow Glen, and his small muddy face was doing something I had never seen it do.

He was crying. Silently, without the mechanics of it quite right—tears coming, but no sob, no shudder, like a machine running a program it had only read about—and when he finally spoke, his voice was the most wrong it had ever been, because it was the most human.

"That was my fault," he said. "The first one. Mrs. Aldous. I told you one day because I thought one day would be enough, and I was wrong, and she heard me say it." He wiped his face with the back of his hand, a child's gesture, borrowed. "I'm a piece of it, Wendell. Everything I say, some part of it hears. I've been trying to be on your side with a traitor's mouth."

The hum started again in my chest, low, the second verse this time, and this time I let it come—because Pruitt's hymnal was open on my desk to his instructions on breath control, and because the third verse was already in my mouth, and because there were six singers left in Harrow Glen and the water was a foot from the brim and falling no more.

"Then tell it this," I said, and I stood up, and I let the melody rise all the way to my lips without letting a single note out. "Tell it I'm coming down tonight. And tell it to save its voice."

Hollis looked at me, and behind his eyes something vast turned over in its sleep and, for the first time, sounded afraid.
