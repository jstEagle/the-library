## 20. The Last Verse

The water at the bottom of the well was black in a way that black water should not be. Not murky, not shadowed—*finished*. As if every color had been asked for its opinion and the water had declined them all. Wendell stood at its edge with the lantern in one hand and Pruitt's hymnal pressed flat against her ribs, and she understood, with the terrible clarity of a woman standing at the lip of her own grave, that the surface was not reflecting the lantern.

It was reflecting her heartbeat.

Each pulse moved across the stillness in a slow ring, widening, fading, and the thing beneath the water was keeping time with her. She could feel it listening the way you feel a hand resting on the back of your neck—not painful, not even unwelcome, just *there*, patient as geology.

Beside her, Hollis stood with his toes at the waterline, his muddy overalls hanging loose on a body that had not aged a day in twenty years. He was not looking at the water. He was looking at her.

"Don't answer it," he said. "Whatever it does. Whatever it sounds like. Don't answer."

"I know the rules," Wendell said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. The tunnels drank it anyway; the grooves carved into the walls drank everything, and tonight they were faintly luminous, each channel glowing the color of old ivory, waiting to be filled.

"You think you know the rules." Hollis crouched, hugging his knees the way he had when she was nine and the storms came, the way he had always crouched when something frightened him, because she had invented him that way—brave enough to stay, small enough to be honest about fear. "But you've never heard the last verse. Nobody who's heard it has ever been able to tell anyone what it sounds like. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means they sang it."

"It means there was nothing left of them *to* tell anyone." He looked up at her, and his eyes—she had never been able to decide what color they were, all these weeks, all these chapters of her disbelief—were wet. "It's going to offer it to you in voices you love. That's how it works. That's how it's always worked. The verse isn't written down because it can't survive being written. It has to be given. Mouth to ear. And it always, always comes dressed as someone you'd walk into fire for."

The lantern flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

Wendell set the light down on the stone shelf at the water's edge and took the hymnal out. Pruitt's handwriting filled the first pages—breath counts, phrasing marks, warnings in a shaking hand: *Breathe from the floor of you, not the chest. The chest is where it gets in.* The rest of the book was blank, page after page after page, and she understood now that the blanks were not emptiness. They were restraint. Forty years of a man writing nothing down because writing was the first step toward singing.

"Grandma knew the last verse," Wendell said. "Didn't she. That's what the journal meant. *It is remembered. Do not remember it.* She learned it. She held it her whole life and never sang it, and that's why the well stayed asleep, and that's why she never—" Her voice cracked. "Why she never told me. Why she made me promise I'd never come back."

"She was holding the note," Hollis said softly. "For sixty years. Alone, at the end. When she died, the note started to slip. That's why the water's rising. That's why it's early. It isn't hungry, Wendell. It's *forgetting how to sleep*."

The water moved.

Not rippled—moved, the way a sleeping animal moves, a long slow swell from the center outward, and the first note rose out of it without breaking the surface at all. It came up through the stone, through the soles of her boots, through the marrow of her, and it was—

It was her grandmother's voice.

Not the thin, papery voice of the last years. The voice from before, the voice that had sung her to sleep in this very town when Wendell was small enough to be carried, warm and low and a little smoky at the edges, the voice that had hummed the first verse over dishwater and funeral casseroles and long dark drives home. The voice sang the first verse—the one Wendell knew, the lullaby, the one that had come to her too easily, the one the tunnels had harmonized with—and then it kept going, past the end of what Wendell knew, and the melody turned a corner into territory she had never heard, and every hair on her body stood up and *reached*.

Her throat opened.

She caught it with her teeth. Bit down on the note before it was born, jaw clenching so hard her molars sang with pain, and the verse went on without her, sweet and patient, her grandmother's voice singing the fourth verse as though it had been written for a kitchen, for a porch, for a child's ear, as though it were the most natural thing in the world, and it was, that was the horror of it, it was the most natural melody Wendell had ever heard, the way a river is natural, the way gravity is natural, and her lungs were filling with the shape of the next line and she had to *not*—

"Don't," Hollis whispered. "Listen. Only listen. Hold it. Don't give it back."

*Hold it.* She understood then what Pruitt's breath exercises had really been for. Not singing technique. *Container* technique. The verse wanted to be sung the way water wanted to run downhill; learning it meant catching it, and catching it meant becoming the vessel, and a vessel that sang poured itself out.

She opened her hymnal to a blank page. Her hands were shaking. She could not write the verse—she knew that now, knew it in her bones, the way her grandmother had known it—but she could mark it, the way Pruitt had marked his breathing, the way the tunnel walls themselves were marked, grooves without notes, a staff waiting for a melody that must never be written on it.

The verse climbed. Her grandmother's voice climbed with it, and then—

Then it changed.

* * *

The second voice was Hollis's.

Not the Hollis beside her—the Hollis of 1994, the Hollis she had invented on a summer afternoon in the barn loft, bright and slightly wrong, the voice she had built out of nothing and loved with the whole uncomplicated heart of a nine-year-old. That voice took up the verse where her grandmother's had left it, and it was worse, so much worse, because that voice had never sung anything but games and secrets, and hearing it carry the fourth verse of the Harrow Glen hymn was like watching a child's drawing move on its own.

Beside her, the real Hollis—the fragment, the escaped piece of the dreaming thing below—flinched as if struck.

"That's not me," he said. "That's not—Wendell, that's what I sounded like to *you*. It's using what you made me. It's using your love for what you made me."

"Stop it," Wendell said, to the water, to the dark, to the thing beneath. Her voice came out ragged. "Stop using him."

"It can't hear you," Hollis said. "It can only *sing* to you. That's all it's ever been able to do. That's why the town sings back—someone decided, a hundred and fifty years ago, that the only way to answer a song was with a song, and they've been answering it ever since, and every answer feeds it, and every singer goes down to hold the note, and it grows fatter and more awake and more *certain*—" His voice broke. "It's certain of you now. It heard you coming. It's been waiting its whole long sleep for someone stubborn enough to listen all the way to the end."

The verse turned again, and Wendell felt the turn in her chest like a hand turning a key.

Her grandmother's voice and Hollis's voice braided together now, and beneath them a third voice rose, thin and clear and nine years old.

Her own.

The voice she had had before the world taught her to be embarrassed of it. The voice that had sung to the dark in the barn loft, that had named a friend out of mud and loneliness, that had believed—absolutely, without evidence, without apology—that the world was full of things that only needed singing to. That voice sang the last lines of the last verse, and the lines were simple, the lines were *kind*, the lines were a promise that nothing is ever really lost, that everything remembered is kept, that the dark below is not a grave but a *library*, and oh, God, it was beautiful, it was the most beautiful lie ever constructed, and Wendell's whole body was screaming to sing along.

Her jaw ached from clenching. Tears ran hot down her face and she did not dare wipe them because her hands were needed, both hands, pressing the hymnal flat, her pencil scratching the only marks that were safe—breath counts, rests, the architecture of the verse without its soul. *Four counts in. Hold two. The high line comes on the exhale, not the inhale. Do not breathe where it breathes. Do not breathe where it breathes. Do not—*

The three voices—grandmother, friend, child—sang the final line together.

And the final line was her name.

Not *Wendell*. The name the thing below had for her, the true one, the one Hollis had confessed to giving it, the name it had been holding in its sleep for twenty years like a coin under a pillow. It sang her true name in three voices at once, and the water did not move, and the lantern did not flicker, and the whole world narrowed to the width of her locked jaw.

*Answer it*, the song said, without words. *You already know the words. You've known them since you were nine. Sing it back and be kept. Be remembered perfectly, forever. Nothing will ever hurt you again. Nothing will ever be lost again. Your grandmother is here. Your friend is here. You are here, you were always here, you never left—*

Her lungs burned. She realized she had been holding her breath—not strategically, not as Pruitt had taught, but the way you hold your breath at a funeral, the way you hold it when the thing you love most is balanced on the edge of something.

The verse ended.

The silence afterward was the loudest thing she had ever heard.

* * *

For a long moment, nothing. The water lay still. The grooves in the walls glowed and dimmed, glowed and dimmed, like a held breath.

Then, beneath the silence, she heard it: the melody continuing *down there*, past the last line she had learned, going on and on into depths the hymn had never mapped, and she understood that the fourth verse was not the end of the song. It was the end of the *invitation*. Everything after it was the thing itself, singing to itself in the dark, dreaming the world, and the hymn was only the handle on the door.

"You have it," Hollis said. His voice was very small. "All of it. Every note. It's in you now and it will never leave, and that's the bargain nobody tells you: the verse can't be unlearned. You'll hear it when you're old. You'll hear it when you're dying. You'll have to hold it your whole life, the way she did." He swallowed. "The way I was going to."

Wendell opened her eyes. She hadn't known they were closed. Her face was soaked; her jaw unclenched with a crack like river ice; blood tinged her spit where she had bitten the inside of her cheek. She looked down at the hymnal, at the breath marks and rests marching across the blank page like a fence around an empty field, and she began, very quietly, to laugh—the broken, gasping laugh of someone who has just set down a weight they'd forgotten they were carrying and can feel the shape of it bruised into their shoulders.

"It offered me everything," she said. "It offered me *her*. And all I had to do was open my mouth."

"That's all it's ever asked of anyone."

"And if I sing it—here, now, at the water—"

"Then it wakes up *full*." Hollis stood. He was looking at the water the way you look at a door you know you'll have to walk through. "But that's not the plan, is it. Your plan is worse. Your plan is to seal it. To hold the verse inside yourself and walk out of here and let it starve on silence for a hundred years." His chin trembled, and for the first time since she'd known him—since she'd *invented* him—he looked like what he was: a small thing made of someone else's dreaming, about to be put away. "And when you seal it, Wendell. When you close the door with the song it gave you—"

"I know," Wendell said.

"It forgets everything the song touched."

"I *know*."

The water shivered. Not from within this time—from the walls, from the town above, from the chapel where nine voices were singing their failing three verses into the small hours, from the sinkhole breathing wet and warm behind the barn. The refill was close. She could feel it in her teeth, in the water climbing toward them, in the metronome of her own heart that the thing below was still counting, patiently, lovingly, the way you count the breaths of someone you are waiting to wake.

Wendell closed the hymnal. She pressed it once, hard, against the sternum where her grandmother's voice seemed still lodged, and she looked at the black water, at her own heartbeat ringing across it, and she spoke the words she had carried down through the dark like a coal cupped in both hands.

"Then let's finish it," she said. "While it's still listening. While I still remember how."

The water began to rise.
