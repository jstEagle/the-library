## 14. The Class of '74

Theo Pell had spent his whole life believing his grandfather was a boring man.

That was the trick of Ambrose Pell—ninety-one years old, cardigan the color of oatmeal, a man who fell asleep during sermons and woke to complain about the coffee. Theo had grown up in Tulsa thinking of Marrow Creek as a place his grandfather had escaped *from*, not something he'd escaped *to*. The town was a footnote. A water-stain on the family history. Grandpa Ambrose came out every Thanksgiving and told the same three stories about fishing the creek with a cane pole, and Theo had memorized them so thoroughly he could recite them in his sleep.

Which was, he would come to understand, exactly the problem.

Because for eleven nights now, he had dreamed the hymn.

It didn't have words at first. It was more like weather—a sound that moved through the dream the way wind moves through wheat, bending everything toward it. He'd wake with his jaw aching from humming along, though he'd never heard the tune awake. On the fourth night, words arrived. They were old-fashioned, hymnal, the kind of language that smelled like dust and varnish:

*Deep calls down and marrow answers,*
*what we gave we give forever—*

He'd looked up the verses online. Nothing. Not in any hymnal, not in any archive. A song that existed only in his sleep, sung by a voice that sounded unsettlingly like his own but pitched lower, older, like his voice played back through forty years of sediment.

On the eleventh night, the dream changed. In it, he stood in a dry field under a white sky, and a woman with dark hair and a coat too heavy for the season said: *"You're Ambrose's grandson. I need you to hear this while you're still afraid."*

He woke at 3:14 in the morning—he'd checked the clock, because some part of him already knew that number mattered—and booked a flight to Arkansas before the sun came up.

* * *

Marrow Creek in late September smelled like pond water and cut hay, and underneath both, faint as a watermark, something mineral and cold. Theo rented the last room at the Creekview Motel—the clerk, a woman with a burn scar crawling up her throat like ivy, took his cash without meeting his eyes and said, "Don't hum after dark," as if she were telling him not to feed the raccoons.

Juniper Eastwick found him there the next morning.

She knocked once, softly, and when he opened the door she looked at him the way you look at a photograph you've been carrying too long—like she was checking him against something. She was thinner than her pictures in the newspaper clippings his grandfather kept in a shoebox, which Theo had found only after the dreams started, because apparently his grandfather's shoeboxes were also part of the inheritance.

"You dreamed it," she said. Not a question.

"Eleven nights."

"Words yet?"

"*Deep calls down and marrow answers.*" His mouth went dry saying it aloud in daylight. It sounded worse awake. Smaller. Like a thing that could fit inside your chest if you let it.

Juniper closed her eyes briefly, the way people do when a diagnosis is confirmed. "That's the second verse. There are seven. You'll get them all eventually unless we finish this first." She stepped past him into the motel room like she owned it, set a leather folder on the bedspread, and unzipped it. "Sit down, Theo. I'm going to tell you what your grandfather did, and then I'm going to ask you to help me undo it. And you're going to want to leave. Everyone wants to leave. But the hymn doesn't stop at the county line anymore—it stopped respecting borders the night they poured concrete over that pit. So you can run, but you'll be running with the music."

* * *

She told him everything.

The drought of 1974, the wells gone to chalk, the corn standing brown in the fields like congregations waiting for a God who'd stopped taking calls. Seven founding families. A ledger. An iron door beneath the old cistern, sealed from the inside since before anyone living could remember, and a bargain struck at 3:14 in the morning: seven children given below, water granted above. Prosperity bought at retail. Interest accruing for fifty years.

"The ledger lists them by family name," Juniper said, laying out photocopied pages on the motel bed. "But Mabel Voss—the librarian, she was supposed to be the eighth—kept the real record. First names. Ages." Her finger moved down the column. "Ruth Ann Coleridge, nine. Daniel Voss—" she paused; that one cost her something, "—no relation to Mabel, or all relation, depending how you count. Eleven. Sarah Marsh, ten. Ezekiel Odum, twelve. Grace Hargrove, eight. Thomas Wren, thirteen. And Cora Pell."

The name landed in Theo's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He waited for the splash.

"Cora," he repeated.

"Ambrose's sister. Your great-aunt. She was ten." Juniper watched him take it. She had the particular kindness of someone who has recently been gutted themselves and knows the shape of the wound. "Your grandfather signed her name himself. Mabel showed me the entry. His handwriting shakes, but it's his."

Theo sat very still. Outside, a sprinkler ticked across the motel lawn, patient and idiotic, watering grass nobody looked at. He thought about Grandpa Ambrose asleep in the recliner, the cane-pole stories, the way the old man never once—not in fifty years of Thanksgivings—asked about the creek. You don't fish a river you helped fill with your sister.

"He knew," Theo said finally. "When I called him about the dreams, he said—God, he said, *'Is it the second verse yet?'* Like he was asking about the weather. Like he'd been waiting for my turn."

"It comes down the bloodline. That's how it collects. The debt doesn't die with the signers—it inherits." Juniper gathered the pages. "Six of the seven families still have descendants in or near the county. I've been to four houses in two days. Come with me today. I need someone they can't dismiss as the crazy Eastwick girl."

"Why me?"

"Because you're fresh." She almost smiled; it didn't survive the trip. "Everyone else in this town has had fifty years to build a story they can live inside. You've had eleven nights. You still remember what the truth feels like."

* * *

The first house belonged to Dale Odum, grandson of Ezekiel, a heavyset man in his fifties who ran a feed store and answered the door holding a shotgun he made no effort to hide.

"I know why you're here," Dale said, before either of them spoke. "Ida Marsh called everybody. Said the Eastwick girl was going door to door stirring up grave dirt." He shifted the gun, not aiming it, just keeping it in the conversation. "My granddaddy drowned in that quarry in '74. Everybody knows that. It was an accident. It was always accidents."

"Mr. Odum," Juniper said evenly, "your grandfather's body was never found. There's no grave. There's a headstone in the churchyard with nothing under it—I checked the sexton's records Tuesday night."

Dale's face did something complicated. Grief and fury fighting for the same muscles. "Get off my property."

"Ray Odum dug at that pit with his bare hands until his heart quit," Juniper said. "Frostbitten in July. He was your cousin. Whatever your family agreed to in 1974, it's coming due, and it doesn't care whether you believe in it."

"GET OFF MY PROPERTY."

They got off his property. In the truck, Theo's hands shook on the wheel. "He knew," Theo said. "Under it. The way he said *always accidents.*"

"They all know somewhere. That's the architecture of it. The town is built like the door—sealed from the inside." Juniper looked out the window at the passing fields, green and fat and wrong. "Look how well everything grows here. Fifty years of rain. My grandfather traded two infants for irrigation, and every lawn in this county is a receipt."

* * *

Sarah Marsh's granddaughter, a schoolteacher named Bethany, listened to all of it sitting rigid at her kitchen table with her hands folded like a woman in church, and when Juniper finished, Bethany said, "My grandmother used to sing to us. A song nobody else knew. She said her mother taught it to her, and her mother learned it *away*, and she never said away where." Her hands began, very slightly, to tremble. "I sang it to my own daughter last month. At bedtime. I haven't been able to stop humming it since. I hum it in the grocery store. I hum it during faculty meetings." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and terrified. "Am I sick?"

"No," Juniper said gently. "You're being invoiced."

Bethany wouldn't commit to anything—wouldn't touch the ledger copy, wouldn't say the names aloud—but she wrote her phone number on the back of a church bulletin and pressed it into Juniper's hand like contraband. "If it gets bad," she whispered, "call me. I won't help. But call me anyway."

"That's how it starts," Juniper told Theo in the truck. "That's how every alliance in this town will start. With people who won't help but want to be asked."

* * *

The Wren house was empty. Had been for years, according to a neighbor—the family moved to Little Rock after the last Wren died, and the neighbor, an ancient man mowing his lawn in the heat of the afternoon, shut off his mower to deliver one sentence with terrible clarity: "That family left in a hurry in June. Left food in the fridge. Left the dog." He restarted the mower and would say nothing else.

Grace Hargrove's line ended at Dell Hargrove, the funeral director—who turned out to be a grand-nephew twice removed, and who met them in the preparation room of his funeral home because, he said, "it's the only place in this county where people tell the truth, and mostly because they're dead when they do it." He was a long, gray, courteous man with the unhurried hands of someone who handled the fragile for a living.

He listened to everything. He looked at the ledger page for a long time—at the name Grace Hargrove, age eight—and then he said, "I dressed Ray Odum's body myself. Frostbite in July. I've done this work thirty-two years, Miss Eastwick, and I have prepared six members of those seven families in the last five months alone. Six. I told myself it was coincidence, because the alternative was unemployment of the soul." He folded his glasses. "I can't march with you. I have a business, and half my clients are on that ledger. But when you go down to that door—and you will go down, everyone in this town can feel it coming like a storm in their teeth—I'll make sure the ground above it stays open. Officially. Paperwork-wise. Nobody pours concrete on my watch without a death certificate I haven't signed."

It wasn't courage. It was bookkeeping. Theo understood, watching him, that in Marrow Creek even defiance came disguised as procedure.

* * *

The Ruth Ann Coleridge descendants had scattered to Memphis generations ago; Juniper had a phone number, an answering machine, and a plan to drive there herself if she had to. Which left one name on her list that she saved for last, and she saved it visibly, turning the truck toward the north road with the reluctance of someone approaching a mirror.

"Pell," Theo said.

"Pell."

"My grandfather."

"Your grandfather is the only founder still lucid, which means he's the only one who can confirm the terms, and the only one who can renounce his share of the water rights in a legally meaningful way. Mabel says the covenant dissolves if the bloodlines surrender it willingly. All seven lines. Before the equinox." She glanced at him. "I can't make him do it. But you can stand in the room. Sometimes shame needs a witness with the family chin."

Ambrose Pell's house sat at the end of a gravel drive, white clapboard gone gray, a garden gone gloriously to seed. The old man was on the porch in his oatmeal cardigan, shelling peas into a bowl, and when he saw Theo climb out of the truck he set the bowl down with the care of a man setting down something breakable.

"Well," Ambrose said. "The dreams got you."

"Grandpa—"

"How many nights?" His voice was steady, but his hands, Theo noticed, had begun to shake—the same shaking hand, Theo realized with a lurch, that had written *Cora Pell* in a ledger fifty years ago.

"Eleven."

"Faster than mine. Mine took a month. Then again, I was already guilty." Ambrose looked at Juniper. "Miss Eastwick. You have your brother's stubbornness. Or hers. I never could keep straight which twin was which, and I suspect by now neither can you." He gestured at the porch chairs. "Sit. I expect you've come for my signature, and I expect you've earned it, and I expect it won't matter a damn unless all seven lines sign, and I expect at least three of them won't, because fear makes men love their chains. But sit anyway. Peas don't shell themselves, and confessions are easier with something in your hands."

So they shelled peas while the old man talked—about the drought, the dust that came through window cracks and tasted like pennies, the meeting at the cistern in the dark with lanterns, the voice below the iron that spoke in each listener's own voice, offering terms. About carrying ten-year-old Cora down the hill himself because she trusted him, because he told her they were going to see the water. About the sound she made. About the sound the door made after.

"And the renewal clause," Juniper said quietly. "*When the last debt walks home.*"

"When the last debt walks home," Ambrose agreed. "We thought it meant the children. We thought the debt was paid the night we paid it." He looked at his ruined, pea-stained hands. "It meant the grandchildren. It means you, Miss Eastwick. It means my Theo. The contract reads bloodline, and blood is a thing that keeps arriving."

"Then renounce it," Theo said. His voice cracked on it. "Sign the paper. Give back the water."

Ambrose looked at his grandson for a long moment, and something in his face was younger and more broken than ninety-one years should allow. "Boy, I've been waiting fifty years for somebody to ask me that." He reached into his cardigan pocket and produced a fountain pen, uncapped, as if he'd been sitting on this porch shelling peas and waiting, every day, for the question. "Fetch me the paper."

* * *

They mapped it that night in Juniper's kitchen—Jasper's kitchen, though she still couldn't say *Jasper's* without a pause like a skipped heartbeat. A poster board, markers, the ledger photocopies pinned in a column. Seven names. Beside each, a status.

**Coleridge** — unreachable, Memphis trip planned.
**Voss (Daniel)** — Mabel; already given, already committed.
**Marsh** — Bethany; willing-adjacent, terrified.
**Odum** — refused; door slammed, gun in hand.
**Hargrove** — procedural ally; will hold the ground.
**Wren** — fled; address unknown.
**Pell** — signed, surrendered, witnessed.

"Six recoverable," Juniper said, capping the marker. "One already spent." Her pen hovered over the seventh line, and Theo watched her not write what they both knew—that the seventh debt, the Eastwick debt, the twin-shaped hole in this kitchen, had already been paid down once, in a crib, fifty years ago, and might be asked to pay again.

"One spent," she said instead, flat, final, and moved the marker on.

Theo stared at the board. Six checkmarks and a question mark, a handful of the frightened and the compromised and the brave, an equinox nineteen days out, and beneath his feet—beneath all their feet—the faintest vibration, felt more than heard, like a hymn sung through sixty feet of limestone.

Then the lights flickered, and from the floorboards rose, soft and clear and in perfect unison, the second verse.

Juniper went white. Theo grabbed the edge of the table. Because the hymn had never, in eleven nights, come to him awake.

And it was singing words none of them had taught it yet—words that hadn't been written yet, a third verse, new, addressed by name:

*Theo, Theo, marrow-deep,*
*your grandfather sowed, now you must reap—*

It stopped. The refrigerator hummed back on. Juniper looked at Theo across the kitchen, and whatever she saw in his face made her nod slowly, like a general confirming a recruit.

"Nineteen days," she said. "And it just told us it knows your name."

Outside, far to the south, toward the quarry, something knocked three times—patient as a metronome—and the dogs of Marrow Creek, every one of them, began to howl.
