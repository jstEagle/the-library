## 17. Which Twin

The rain had stopped an hour before the burial, but the ground hadn't learned about it yet. It gave under Juniper's shoes like something soft that didn't want to be stepped on—like flesh, she thought, and hated herself for thinking it, and thought it again anyway. The cemetery sat on the hill above the quarry, close enough that from the fence line you could see the excavation pit below, the iron door down in its wound of earth, sweating rust even now, even at a distance, like a thing trying to bleed politely.

They had buried Jasper Eastwick on a Wednesday because Dell Hargrove said Wednesdays were traditional, and because the town had run out of reasons to delay.

Juniper stood at the head of the grave with her hands folded the way mourners fold them, knuckles white, fingers laced so tight her rings bit. The casket was closed. It had been closed since the funeral home, since Dell's careful hands and his careful face, since the moment she'd asked to see him one last time and Dell had said, *I'd advise against it, Miss Eastwick,* in a voice that wasn't unkind but was very practiced, the voice of a man who had spent fifty years learning how to say no to grieving people without telling them why.

She hadn't pushed. That was the cowardice of it. She had stood in the green-carpeted viewing room with its humming fluorescent light—the same hum, some low part of her had noted, the same pitch as the floorboards—and she had let him say no, because some animal part of her already knew what was under the lid, and knew it wasn't Jasper, and couldn't bear to be right.

The minister read the Twenty-Third Psalm. His voice went through the words the way water goes through gravel, leaving nothing behind. Around Juniper the town of Marrow Creek stood in its black coats, forty or fifty of them, faces she half remembered from childhood summers: the Pell jawline, the Marsh nose, the Voss stoop. Mabel Voss stood near the back under an umbrella she didn't need anymore, holding it anyway, as if sheltering from something the sky hadn't gotten around to yet. Theo Pell stood beside her, pale, sleepless, his eyes finding Juniper's every few seconds and then flinching away, like a man checking a stove he knows is hot.

Ambrose Pell was three days dead. They had buried him Tuesday, up the hill, under a stone that said *PILLAR OF THE VALLEY.* At sunset he had whispered *it kept its half; we never kept ours*, and then he had been done, finished, a ledger line drawn clean. Juniper had not cried for Ambrose. She was beginning to suspect she would never cry again for anyone the ordinary way, that grief had changed chemistry in her sometime during the last month and become this other thing—this cold, clarifying, terrible attention.

*Pay attention,* the grief said. *You've spent fifteen years looking away. Look now.*

So she looked.

The casket was walnut. Dell Hargrove had offered oak, cheaper, and she had said walnut without thinking, and only later understood why: Jasper's bedroom door at the house on Cutter Street was walnut. He had slept behind walnut for thirty-nine years, listening to the floorboards, keeping the vigil she had never once thanked him for. He should go into the ground behind the same wood. Some debts you pay in materials.

The minister said, "We therefore commit his body to the ground, earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust," and the first shovelful rang off the lid like a bell, and Juniper did not hear the rest of the service at all.

Because the sound—that dull bronze knock, wood struck by earth—went through her like a key through a lock, and behind the lock was the crib.

---

She had been carrying the memory for fifteen years the way you carry a stone in your boot: not forgotten, exactly, but accommodated, walked around, explained away so many times the explanation had worn smooth as river glass. *You dreamed it. Children dream strange things. Grief makes the mind rearrange furniture.*

But the shovel had knocked, and the lock had turned, and there it was whole, undamaged by all her years of not-looking:

The night of the humming. She was four years old—no. She was not four years old. She was an infant, and infants don't remember, that was the whole problem, that was the wall she had built her life against: *infants don't remember.* But she remembered it the way you remember a burn, not as image but as fact, as heat, as the body's own testimony older than language.

The nursery had two cribs. Hers by the window, Jasper's by the door. And in the dark before dawn—it was always the hour before dawn, she knew that now, 3:14, though she hadn't known the number then, only that the dark at that hour had a texture, a thickness, like standing water—something had come up through the floor.

Not footsteps. Humming. A hymn with no words she could hold onto, sung in a voice that was warm the way rot is warm, and it came up between the boards beneath her crib and it rose around her like water rising in a well, and she had been *chosen*. She had known it even then, in the wordless grammar of infancy: *this song is for me. This door opens onto me.*

And she had screamed. Or tried to. And what came out instead—

What came out instead was silence, because her throat had filled with the humming, because the hymn was pouring into her the way water pours into a vessel, filling her up from the feet, and she could feel herself being *counted*, weighed, measured against some standard written into her marrow before she was born, and she was found sufficient, she was found *ready*, and the floorboards beneath her crib had begun—

—to open. Not break. Open. Politely. The way a hand opens.

And from the other crib, from the crib by the door, Jasper had made a sound.

Not crying. Infants cry; this was older than crying. He had made a sound like a hinge deciding, like a small deliberate clearing of the throat, and the humming had paused—paused, the way a creditor pauses when a second debtor steps forward—and in that pause, in the gap the size of a held breath, their grandfather's voice had come through the nursery door, hoarse and fast and praying, saying words Juniper would not read in a ledger for another fifty years, words about *contribution* and *terms* and *the girl child is the stronger vessel*—

—and Jasper had sung.

One note. One small cracked infant note, thrown across the dark room like a rope across a chasm, and the humming had turned. She had felt it turn, felt the attention in the floor swing away from her crib like a searchlight swinging, and land on him, and the boards beneath his crib had opened instead, and the hymn had poured into *him*, and she had lain there in the sudden terrible quiet of being passed over, feeling the hymn drain out of her throat, feeling herself become *unclaimed*, and the last thing she remembered of that night—the true last thing, the thing under fifteen years of explanations—was the sound of her brother's breathing changing as the floor took the weight of what was coming for him.

He had breathed differently after that night. Their mother used to say it. *Jasper breathes like he's listening for something.* Juniper had always thought it was a figure of speech.

It was a diagnosis.

---

"—Miss Eastwick? Miss Eastwick."

The minister's hand hovered near her elbow, not touching. The mourners were dispersing, black coats moving down the hill in twos and threes, and the grave was a mound of raw earth now, and the flowers were already wilting in the way cut things wilt, honestly, without pretense. Rain was starting again, fine as sifted flour.

"I'm staying," she said.

"Of course. Take whatever time—" He retreated. They all retreated from her lately. Word had gone around town, the way word goes around towns like Marrow Creek, that Juniper Eastwick was either cursed or crazy or both, and both options bought her solitude, which was fine, which was what she needed, because what she had to say next was not for witnesses.

She waited until the last car ground down the gravel road. Then she stood alone over her brother's grave in the thin rain, and she said it out loud, because Mabel Voss had told her the rite required truth spoken aloud, blood-witnessed, willing—and maybe this wasn't the rite, maybe this was just a woman talking to a hole in the ground—but she had learned in the last month that Marrow Creek did not distinguish cleanly between those two activities.

"You wore my coat," she said.

Her voice came out steady. That surprised her. Fifteen years of dodging this sentence and it arrived steady, the way a bullet arrives steady.

"The coat they pulled out of the quarry with you. My name stitched in the collar. Mama had it made special my senior year—*J. EASTWICK* in red thread, right where anyone could read it, right where anything could read it." Her breath fogged. "Everyone in town looked at me sideways about it. Wearing your sister's coat, Jasper? And you laughed. You laughed and said you liked the color."

The rain ticked on the raw earth.

"But that's not why you wore it." Her throat tightened around the next part, and she forced it through anyway, because the rite required willingness and willingness meant not stopping. "You wore it because you knew. You'd known for years—you wrote it in the journals I found, page after page, the scratching, the hymn, *it stopped when you left, it never stopped for me*—and you knew the debt had a shape, and the shape was me. The coat was a decoy. You put my name on your body and walked down to the quarry and let the water think it was taking what it was owed."

Her knees wanted to bend. She locked them.

"And here's the part I've been running from since Dell Hargrove said *the hands are wrong*." She looked at the mound, at the flowers, at the wet. "Because the hands weren't wrong, Dell. Not exactly. They were *fifteen years old*. They were the hands of a boy who drowned in 1974, preserved in the cold and the dark and the whatever-it-is down there, waiting. Because the swap has happened twice. Because it started in the crib."

She said the rest fast, before the animal in her could bolt:

"It was supposed to be me. In '74, in the nursery, at 3:14 in the morning, the hymn came up through the floor for *me*. Grandfather sold me before I could walk—I was the stronger vessel, he said so, it's in the ledger, my name and yours with mine listed first. And you sang one note, Jasper. One note from the other crib, and it took you instead. It has been taking you instead for fifty years. Every time. Every renewal, every debt, every knock under the floorboards—it reached for me and you stepped in front of it, over and over, your whole life, and I built a career three states away and called it ambition."

The rain thickened. Down the hill, through the gray, the quarry pit held its dark like a cupped palm.

"Fifteen years ago I left," she said, "and the humming stopped in that house—for me it stopped—and I told myself I'd escaped something. I told myself I was free." A laugh came out of her with no humor in it, just air wearing a laugh's clothes. "I wasn't free. I was *unclaimed*. There's a difference, and the difference is named Jasper Eastwick, and I just buried him behind walnut because he spent thirty-nine years sleeping behind walnut, listening, guarding, paying."

She knelt. The mud took her knees without complaint. She pressed her palm flat to the raw earth, and it was cold, colder than October had any right to make it, cold as quarry water, cold as the inside of a door.

"So here is my confession, brother, since you can't make it yourself: the coat fit you because it was always going to have to fit someone, and you made sure it was you. The body in the casket isn't the twin who died in the quarry. The twin who died in the quarry died in 1974, a little, every year, by installments. What they fished out was the receipt. Proof of purchase. Fifty years of you buying my life in installments, and the final payment due—" 

She stopped.

Because the earth under her palm had answered.

Not moved. Not shaken. *Answered*—a single pulse of warmth rising through the cold soil into her hand, deliberate, unmistakable, the thermal signature of a hand pressed to the other side of a membrane. The way a patient squeezes back.

Juniper snatched her hand away and knelt there in the rain, heart slamming, and the pulse came again, gentler, patient as a metronome, and with it—faint, rising not from the grave but from everywhere, from the ground itself, from the aquifer, from the capped well of the world—a thread of the hymn.

One voice.

Hers.

Except it wasn't singing the verse. It was singing the *answer*—the answering phrase, the call-and-response structure Mabel had mapped in the contract's margins, the part that waited, the part that required a second voice to complete. The hymn had been unfinished for fifty years. It was unfinished because it was a duet. It had always been a duet.

And the voice in the ground was holding the last note open. Waiting for her. Patiently. The way Jasper had waited his whole life.

"Oh," Juniper whispered, and the word broke in half. "Oh, you're still *in there*. You're not—Dell's hands, the age of them, the body in the casket—that's the *receipt*, that's what they kept, but you—" She pressed both palms to the mud now, heedless, weeping at last, the fifteen-years-late tears scalding. "You're below. You've been below since the crib. Every sacrifice was yours and none of them ever finished it because the terms were never one twin—the terms were *both*, one above and one below, forever, and you've been down there bargaining for fifteen years to keep me out of the verse—"

The pulse came once more, and this time it carried something with it, something shaped like meaning, arriving in her chest the way the hymn had arrived in her infant throat: not words, but weight.

*Come finish it.*

Not a threat. An invitation. His voice, worn smooth by decades of dark, asking his sister to come down and finish the song he'd started with one cracked note in a nursery at 3:14 in the morning.

Juniper rose. Mud slid from her knees. Below the hill, at the edge of the excavation, the iron door sweated rust into the rain, and far off across the flooded fields the water was moving the wrong way, uphill, thin silver threads crawling toward the pit like the world's veins reversing.

The equinox was tomorrow.

She wiped her face with a muddy hand, and she picked up the shovel the gravediggers had left leaning against the oak, and she began—not to fill the grave, but to open it.

"If you're down there," she said to the earth, to the water, to the door, to her brother, "hold the note. I'm coming to answer it."
