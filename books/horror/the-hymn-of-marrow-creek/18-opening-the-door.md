## 18. Opening the Door

The equinox came in on a wind that smelled of iron and old rain, and Juniper woke before dawn with the hymn already finished inside her.

That was how she thought of it now—not heard, not remembered, but *finished*, as though some hand had been writing it into her chest for weeks and had finally set down its pen. She lay in Jasper's bed with her eyes open to the ceiling's water-stained continents and let the melody turn over once more, all four verses, including the one no one living was supposed to know. It fit her throat like a key fits a lock it was cut for. She could feel where it would sit when she sang it: behind her sternum, in the hollow the doctors had always called a harmless murmur.

She got up. She dressed in the coat—her coat, the one that had come home without her brother—and she took the ledger from beneath the floorboard where Mabel had told her to keep it, and she drove through a town gone quiet as held breath to the excavation site.

Theo Pell was already there. He stood at the lip of the pit with his hands jammed in his jacket pockets and his breath steaming, though it was September and the morning was warm. Everything around him was cold. The grass at the rim wore frost in July's stubborn afterlife; the crane above the pit hung motionless, its cable furred white, creaking like a ship's rigging in a sea that wasn't there.

"You heard it too," he said. Not a question. His face had aged ten years since the rehearsal, and there was something almost grateful in his exhaustion—the relief of a man who has finally been allowed to stop pretending he isn't drowning.

"All of it," Juniper said. "Every verse."

Mabel Voss arrived last, walking slowly up the access road with a canvas satchel over her shoulder and her spine straight as a hymnal's binding. She was seventy-one years old and she had been running for fifty-three of them, and this morning she walked like a woman who had set the running down somewhere behind her and did not intend to pick it back up. She stopped beside them and looked down into the pit for a long moment before she spoke.

"It's open from below," she said. "I can tell by the smell. Fifty years I've remembered that smell." She swallowed. "Wet stone and candle smoke. Like a church that never got built."

Juniper smelled it too. Rising out of the earth, faint but unmistakable: stone dust, tallow, and underneath it something sweeter and older, like apples left too long in a cellar, like the inside of a violin.

They went down.

* * *

The ladder Ray Odum's crew had bolted into the pit wall rang under their feet with each rung, a low bell-note that traveled up through Juniper's boots and settled in her teeth. Forty feet is not far. She had climbed higher ladders a hundred times without thinking. But forty feet down, the light changed. The dawn above shrank to a coin of gray-gold, and the walls closed around them in strata—clay, then gravel, then a band of black soil that predated the town, predated the county, maybe predated the names of things—and then the door.

It filled the bottom of the shaft like a lid fills a jar. Iron, green-black with fifty years of damp, wide enough that three people could have lain across it head to foot. The bolts were what Juniper had come to think of as impossible and had now simply accepted: six of them, thick as a man's forearm, thrown from *inside*. The metal around them was scarred where they had turned, ground bright in crescents, as if the door had been testing its own locks the way a tongue tests a loose tooth.

And it had opened. Three inches—the width of Ray Odum's last work, done with his bare hands and his frozen smiling mouth—but three inches was enough. From the gap seeped the cold, pouring upward like water finding its level, carrying the smell of that unbuilt church.

Mabel knelt at the threshold without being asked. Her knees cracked. She opened the satchel and laid out the things with the deliberation of a woman setting a communion table: seven candles of rendered tallow, one for each name. A bowl of quarry water. The contract itself, unfolded, its wax seals dark as dried blood, its ink still legible after half a century because whatever had witnessed it had wanted it legible.

"Names backward," she said. "Debts on the threshold. Blood to witness." Her voice was steady, and Juniper understood suddenly that the steadiness cost her everything she had left. "I've said these words maybe ten thousand times in my head since 1974. Practicing. Like a coward rehearses an apology she never delivers." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and fierce. "Say them with me. All three of us. It takes bloodline, and Theo's got Pell in him, and you—" she touched Juniper's wrist, gently, over the stitched collar of the coat "—you're the debt itself, child. You're the last word of the sentence."

Juniper knelt. The cold came up through her jeans and found the marrow of her knees. Theo knelt on her other side, and his hand found hers, and his grip was cold too but alive, alive, trembling with a pulse.

They lit the candles. Seven small flames stood up straight in the windless dark, and their light made the door look less like iron and more like the hull of something vast that had chosen to lie down here and wait.

"Marrow Creek," Mabel began, and her voice dropped into a register Juniper had never heard from her, formal and old, the voice of someone reading aloud in a room full of the dead. "*We return what was taken.*"

"*We return what was taken,*" Juniper and Theo answered.

"Alder Voss. Ssowl-A-red—" the reversal was ugly in the mouth, a word being unmade, and that was the point, Juniper realized. You didn't speak the names to summon them. You spoke them wrong on purpose, to give them back their true shape, which was not the shape the contract had used. "Voss, Alder. Taken at eleven years. Returned."

She tipped the first candle sideways and let a bead of tallow fall onto the iron, sealing the name to the door.

One by one they went around the circle of flames. Alder Voss. Ruthie Calloway. Samuel and Edith Marsh, twins, taken together at nine. Daniel Okafor—Juniper flinched at the name, some distant cousin line she'd never known about, roots going deeper than she'd guessed. Peter Hollis. Grace Underwood. And last, seventh, the one already spent, the one whose body had worn Jasper's jacket in the flooded dark for fifty years while the town called her a runaway:

"Annalee Pruitt," Mabel said, and here her voice finally broke, and she let it. "Taken at twelve. Spent. Forgiven if it can be. Returned if it must be."

Seven beads of tallow. Seven names lying on the threshold like coins paid to a ferryman.

Then Mabel drew the lancet from the satchel—a plain thing, a bookbinder's tool, appropriate for a librarian—and cut a shallow line across her own palm, and held it out to Juniper, and then to Theo, and then pressed her bleeding hand flat against the iron between the candles.

"Blood to witness," she whispered. "Not to bind. To *witness*. There's a difference. We learned that too late."

Juniper cut her palm. The pain was small and clean, and the blood welled up shockingly red in the candlelight, and she pressed her hand to the iron beside Mabel's. Theo did the same. Three prints, three generations of guilt, marked on a door that had been holding its breath since Nixon was president.

For a long moment, nothing.

The candles burned. The cold breathed. Somewhere far above, the coin of sky went from gray to gold, and none of its light reached them.

Then the bolts began to turn.

* * *

There was no violence in it. That was the worst part, Juniper would think later. No groaning of metal, no shudder through the ground. The six bolts simply rotated, smooth and unhurried, the way a hand turns a key in its own front door, and the scars around them gleamed fresh-bright, and one by one they drew back into the iron with soft clicks that sounded horribly like courtesy.

*You came,* said a pressure against the inside of her skull, not words exactly, more like the memory of words, the way you remember warmth after the fire's out. *You came, you came, we kept your places.*

The door swung inward and down, becoming a stair.

Light rose out of the earth—not candlelight, not sunlight, but a pale diffusion like the glow off snow, sourceless—and with it came the singing.

Juniper had heard the hymn in two voices all her life, scratching up through floorboards, humming out of drains, leaking from the walls of her brother's house. This was not two voices. This was seven. Seven voices that had started as children's voices in 1974 and had gone on singing in the dark for fifty years, and so they were children's voices still, and they were not—they had aged the way trees age, adding rings without changing shape, and the sound they made was sweet and cracked and patient beyond all human patience, and every note landed in Juniper's chest like a hand pressing on a bruise.

"They're alive," Theo breathed. His face was gray. "Oh God, they're *alive*."

"They were never dead," Mabel said. Her voice was ash. "That was the bargain's cruelty. Nothing dies down there. Nothing gets to."

They descended.

* * *

The stair went further than forty feet should allow. Juniper counted steps and lost the count past a hundred, and the walls around them changed from clay to stone to something that might have been wood or might have been root, ribbed and pale, arching overhead in vaults that grew more deliberate the deeper they went—until she understood, with a vertigo that had nothing to do with depth, that the architecture was growing *toward* them, that the space had spent fifty years learning what a cathedral was from the prayers of the children it kept, building itself a nave out of its own body.

The floor leveled. They stepped into the vastness.

Roots everywhere—great pale columns of them rising from darkness into darkness, braided into pillars, branching into ribs, and between them pools of standing water lay perfectly still, mirroring the vaults so exactly that the space seemed to double, cathedral above and cathedral below, heaven and its reflection. The air was dry. That was the detail that undid her: fifty feet beneath the water table, in a town drowning toward a reservoir, the air down here was dry as paper, dry as a held secret, and the water stayed where it was put, obedient, contained, *kept*—the way everything down here was kept.

And they were waiting.

Seven figures stood among the root-pillars, spaced apart like candles on an altar. They had been children once—Juniper could see it in the bones of them, in the way the smallest one shifted her weight the way little girls do—but the dark had raised them. Their skin was the white of things grown away from sun. Their hair had gone long and fine as root-hair. Their eyes, when they turned toward the newcomers, were calm and bottomless and terribly, terribly kind, and Juniper felt her mind skid away from them the way a tire skids on ice, because kindness was the one thing she had not prepared for.

The smallest one—twelve years old forever and also fifty, both at once, impossibly—stepped forward. Annalee Pruitt. She wore a boy's jacket, decades too big, the cuffs rolled and re-rolled as she'd grown, and Juniper knew it instantly, had seen it in photographs, had zipped its twin up over her own chin a thousand times.

Jasper's jacket.

"You brought it back," Annalee said. Her voice was the hymn's voice, all seven voices folded into one small mouth. She looked down at herself, at the jacket, with something like fondness. "He gave it to me the first night. Said I'd catch cold. He was only a baby, and he was worried I'd catch cold." She smiled, and the smile was gentle and it went through Juniper like a spear. "He's been coming down ever since. In pieces. In dreams. Learning our song so he could teach it to you."

"We came to settle it," Mabel said, and her voice shook, and she made herself go on. "All of it. Every name. The contract's voided—we return what was taken, freely, by bloodline, before the equinox, and the covenant dissolves—"

"Oh, we know the terms," said a voice from the far end of the nave. "We wrote them."

* * *

It came walking out from between the root-pillars the way dawn comes, gradually and then all at once, and Juniper's heart stopped and started and stopped again.

Two figures, walking side by side, close as a single shadow. On the left: a young man with Jasper's crooked grin and Jasper's scar through one eyebrow, wearing the coat with her name in the collar. On the right: the same young man, identical, except that his eyes held the calm of the seven, the deep-water patience, and where Jasper's grin always arrived a half-second late, shy, apologizing for itself, this one's grin was already there, had always been there, whole and unbroken and *complete*.

Both twins. Whole. The thing below had spent fifty years collecting halves—it had Annalee's life and Mabel's guilt and the founders' signatures and a drowned boy in a borrowed coat—and now it stood before her wearing the only halves it had ever wanted: the twins, joined, finished, the verse completed.

"Juniper," said the one with Jasper's eyes, and the voice was Jasper's voice, warm and rough and fifteen years of missing compressed into a single word.

"That's not him," Theo whispered. "Juniper. That's not—"

"It's both of us," said the other twin, and this one's voice was hers, was Juniper's own voice speaking from outside her body, and it smiled with Jasper's mouth. "It was always both of us. That's what none of you understood. The contract didn't ask for a child. It asked for a *pair*. One above, one below. Two voices for the hymn. Your grandfather signed for twins because twins are the only thing down here that sounds like the beginning of a song."

The Jasper-shaped one stepped closer, and up close Juniper could see it: the seams, faint as watermark, where two truths had been pressed together. The thing was wearing her brother like she was wearing her coat. And underneath—

Underneath, something looked back at her. Something that had been bargaining for fifteen years in the dark. Something whose final journal entry had read *it never stopped for me*, written in a steady hand, because he had wanted her to find it, because it was a message, because it was a *map*.

"You came to finish the verse," the entity said, both mouths moving, both voices braided. It spread its hands—four hands, two pairs, an argument of hands—and gestured at the seven waiting children, at the still water, at the vaulted dark that had learned to be beautiful. "Look what we kept. Look how well we kept it. The town drank its water and forgot our names, but *we* never forgot anything. We are very good at keeping." The grin widened, gentle as Annalee's, terrible as a locked door opening. "So finish it, Juniper Eastwick. One twin above, one below. Forever. Those were the terms. Those were always the terms."

"And if I refuse?"

The entity tilted its head, and for just a moment the two faces fell out of sync—one sorrowing, one smiling—and the dissonance was worse than any roar.

"Then the verse stays unfinished," it said, "and unfinished songs don't end. They just keep asking."

Among the pillars, the seven children drew breath together, and the hymn began again—and this time Juniper could hear the gap in it, the hole shaped exactly like a second voice, waiting, waiting, patient as a metronome, for her to sing.
