## 19. The Verse Unfinished

The door stood open, and what waited inside was not darkness.

Juniper had expected dark. She had braced for it the way you brace for cold water, the way you hold your breath before a needle. But the cathedral of roots beyond the iron threshold was lit from below, a green-gold glow seeping up through standing water that did not ripple, as if the light came from somewhere the physics of Marrow Creek had never reached. Roots thicker than church pillars rose from the flooded floor and vanished into a ceiling she couldn't see. The air smelled of wet stone and, impossibly, of Jasper's room—pencil shavings, the cedar of his closet, the faint mineral tang of the chalk he used for his sigils.

The seven children stood in a half-circle at the far end, ankle-deep in water that should have been quarry-cold and was not. They were children still, in the way a photograph is still—young faces carried on bodies that had aged fifty years in the dark. The girl in Jasper's jacket, the one Juniper had found below the quarry, stood at the center. Her eyes found Juniper's with terrible gentleness, the way you look at someone you have been waiting a long time to forgive.

And between them and the door stood the twins.

Both of them. Jasper as Juniper had buried him—no. Not as she'd buried him. As he'd been at nineteen, before the vigilance had carved lines into his face, before the journals, before the chalk dust in his lungs. And beside him, herself. Juniper at nineteen, in the coat with her name stitched in the collar, smiling with a smile that was almost right, played at the wrong speed.

"You brought the names," the two voices said, and they spoke as one, the way a chord is one note that is also several. "Six debts and a witness. Good. Good. But the verse is unfinished, Juniper Eastwick. It has been unfinished for fifty years."

Behind her, she heard Mabel Voss begin to weep, quietly, the way people weep in hospitals. Theo Pell's breathing had gone shallow and fast beside her. Neither of them spoke. The rite had been Mabel's to teach, but the door had its own liturgy, and it was conducting now.

"The original terms," the entity said. The Jasper-thing stepped forward, and the Juniper-thing matched him step for step, mirror-perfect. "One twin above. One below. Forever. That was the shape of the bargain your grandfather made—not a life, not a coin, not a name on a ledger. A *pairing*. A song needs two voices, Juniper. We have been singing half a hymn since 1974, and half a hymn is a wound that never closes."

Juniper's mouth was dry as the drought-year earth. "The children," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "The seven. You took seven."

"We *kept* seven." The Juniper-thing tilted its head, and the gesture was so precisely her own—the little tilt she did when she was listening, the one Jasper used to tease her about—that her knees nearly went. "Kept is not took. Kept is a promise. They sing for us. They have always sung for us. But a hymn of seven voices and no eighth is a hymn with a hole in it, and the hole is shaped like a twin."

The girl in Jasper's jacket lifted her chin. When she spoke, her voice was a dry rustle, autumn leaves in a gutter. "He bargained," she said. "The one you buried. He bargained the whole time."

Juniper's heart stuttered. "What?"

"He was never supposed to be down here," the girl said. "He was supposed to be up there, sleeping, forgetting, like the rest of you were meant to. But he *talked*. From the first night. From the crib." She almost smiled. "Fifteen years of talking. He's very stubborn, your brother. He's been renegotiating since before he had words."

* * *

Jasper had learned the hymn before he learned to walk.

That was the thing nobody understood, the thing he had never been able to put in the journals because the journals were for Juniper and some truths would have broken her. The humming hadn't come from under the floorboards to him. It had come *through* him, out of him, the way water comes through limestone—patient, finding every crack. He had been the instrument before he was the boy. The bargain his grandfather struck had needed a vessel, and the vessel had been chosen before the umbilical was cut: the twin born second, the one who came into the world already listening.

But a vessel can have opinions.

That was the first thing he'd understood, down here in the green-gold dark, the first night after the swap—after he'd walked into the quarry in his sister's coat because the thing below had called him by her name and he had answered with his own, and the ledger, hungry for a twin, had taken the substitution. He'd expected to drown. Instead he'd woken standing in water that held him like a hand, in a cathedral of roots, surrounded by the class of '74, and the hymn had risen around him like weather.

And he had thought, with the clarity of the very young and the very doomed: *I can sing this wrong.*

Not badly. Badly would be noticed, corrected. Wrong in one note. A third where a fourth belonged. A held breath where the verse wanted release. He was six years old in the world and ageless down here, and he had begun the work of a lifetime: learning the hymn so completely that he could sabotage it so subtly that the thing conducting it would take years to hear the flaw.

It took the entity nine years to notice.

He remembered the night it did. The water had gone still in a new way, attentive, and the two-faced thing that was both twins and neither had turned all of its attention on him like a lighthouse beam, and it had said: *You have been flat, little vessel. For nine years, you have been flat.*

And Jasper, nineteen years old in the counting, fifteen years drowned and not drowned, had smiled his sister's smile and said: "Then you need a voice that isn't. You need the other twin. And you can't have her."

That was the night the bargaining began in earnest.

* * *

"Here is what your brother understood," the entity said, and the two of them—the false Jasper, the false Juniper—began to walk the half-circle of the kept children, their footsteps making no sound on the flooded floor. "A hymn requires a willing voice. That is the oldest term, older than your grandfather, older than the drought, older than the town. We cannot compel the song. We can only *invite* it. Every child in 1974 was invited. Every child said yes—because their parents asked them to, because children say yes to the hands that feed them, because yes is the only word children are given." The false Juniper's eyes were wet, and the wetness was Juniper's own grief, harvested, worn. "Your brother said yes at six months old, in his way. But a vessel's yes is not a singer's yes. And so the verse has waited. And so the debt has walked home. And so you are here."

Juniper felt Theo shift behind her, felt Mabel's hand hover near her shoulder without landing, and she understood with a terrible, settling calm what she had come down here to do.

She had known since the graveside. She had known since the coat, since the quarry, since *It stopped when you left. It never stopped for me.* Every sacrifice in her brother's life had been made in her place, and there was no version of her that would let the last one stand. The hymn needed a willing voice. Fine. She was willing. She had been willing since the journals, since the ledger, since the girl in Jasper's jacket looked up at her through fifty feet of green water.

"I'll sing it," Juniper said.

The cathedral went quiet. Even the standing water seemed to stop reflecting.

"One twin above, one below, forever," she said, and her voice cracked on *forever* and she let it, because a willing voice should crack, should be human, should be *hers*. "That's the original terms. I'm the twin. I'm the one the coat was for, the one the crib was for, the one every bargain since has been trying to reach. Take me. Let the others go—the six debts, the descendants, the town. Let them surrender the water and be done. I'll sing your hymn. I'll sing it *true*. I'll hold the note for a hundred years if that's what forever costs."

"Juniper—" Theo started.

"No." She didn't turn around. "Theo, if you say one word I'll lose it, and I need to not lose it. This is the whole reason I came back. This is the whole reason I was *born*."

The entity regarded her, and in its doubled face she saw something she had not expected: hesitation. The false Jasper and the false Juniper looked at each other, and for a moment the mimicry slipped, and what looked out of both faces was old and tired and *hungry* in the way that hunger is a kind of patience.

"You would sing true," it said. "Yes. We believe you. Grief makes honest singers." It stepped closer, and the green-gold light bent around it. "But hear the whole of the terms, Juniper Eastwick, because your grandfather did not, and the whole of the terms is why the debt has festered. One twin above, one below. The one below sings the hymn into the water, and the water carries it up, and the town drinks, and the town prospers, and the debt renews. The one above—" the false Juniper smiled, and it was the worst thing yet, because it was tender— "the one above *forgets*. That is the mercy we extend. The one above wakes in a town with full reservoirs and no memory of a twin. That is the shape of the bargain. That is what your brother has spent fifteen years refusing to let us give you."

The words went into Juniper like ice water into a lung.

*Forgets.*

She thought of the fifteen years. The fog around her childhood. The way she had left Marrow Creek at nineteen and never once, not once, been able to say why the town made her skin crawl—only that it did, only that she'd dreamed of floorboards and humming and woken weeping without knowing for whom. She had thought it was trauma. She had thought it was grief for something she couldn't name.

It had been the bargain, already working. The forgetting, already poured into her like concrete into a failed pit.

"Jasper," she whispered. "Oh God. You've been fighting this. All of it. The journals, the sigils, the vigil—you weren't just keeping it out. You were keeping it from taking *me*."

* * *

Down in the green-gold dark, in the body he wore the way a song wears a singer, Jasper Eastwick heard his sister say his name, and it came through the water to him the way everything came through the water, and he let himself feel it for exactly three seconds before he folded the feeling away, because he had work to do and three seconds was all the feeling he could afford.

Fifteen years of work. That was the arithmetic of it. Fifteen years of singing flat, of wedging his own small wrongness into the hymn like a stone in a gear, of learning the entity's terms so thoroughly that he knew their loopholes the way Juniper knew the stitching in a coat collar. He had learned that the hymn required willingness. He had learned that the entity could not compel, could only invite, could only *wait*, because it had waited since before the drought and patience was its native language. He had learned that the swap could go both directions—that a body given below could be exchanged for a body given above, if the exchange was voluntary on both ends, if the ledger's ink would accept it.

And he had learned, in the last year, the most important thing of all. The thing the girl in his jacket had finally confirmed for him, the thing that had made him stop fighting and start *planning*:

The hymn required a willing voice. It did not specify *which* twin.

The entity wanted the pairing restored. One above, one below. It had never cared which was which. It had assumed, all these years, that it would end with Jasper below and Juniper above and forgetting, because that was the original casting, because vessels stay in their molds. But Jasper had spent fifteen years becoming something the mold had never accounted for. He had become a negotiator. He had become, in the only way that mattered down here, a *founder*—the eighth signer of a contract written in a drowned boy's stubbornness.

And he had one term left to extract.

* * *

"Before you answer her," the false Jasper said, and the entity's voice shifted, and Juniper felt the change in her teeth, in the water at her ankles, in the roots overhead, "you should hear what we have been offered."

The kept children stirred. The girl in Jasper's jacket closed her eyes.

"Your brother has been talking to us for fifteen years," the entity said. "At first we thought it was fear. Then we thought it was defiance. It took us a decade to understand it was *drafting*. He has proposed amendment after amendment to a contract older than your town. Most we refused. Some we kept. The forgetting—that was his doing, did you know? He bargained the mercy into the terms. He could not stop us from taking a twin, so he made us promise the one above would not suffer. He has spent his entire death making your captivity comfortable, Juniper Eastwick. That is what his love looks like from below."

Juniper's vision blurred. "Stop."

"And last night," the entity went on, relentless as water, "he made his final proposal. The amendment that ends the unfinished verse. Shall we hear it? He has been waiting years for you to arrive so it could be spoken in your presence. It requires a witness of the bloodline. You are the witness. You are the *terms*."

The false Jasper stepped forward, alone now, the false Juniper receding into the root-shadow, and for a moment—just a moment—the mimicry fell away entirely and the face looking at Juniper was her brother's true face, nineteen years old and drowned and *smiling*, smiling the way he'd smiled at her across the crib, across the kitchen table, across fifteen years of floorboards.

"Jasper," she breathed. "Are you in there? Is that you?"

"Every word," he said, in his own voice, the voice from the journals, the voice from before, and she made a sound she would never be able to describe to anyone. "Junie. Listen fast. It's going to try to stop me."

The entity moved, and the water moved with it, and the roots overhead groaned like a ship in a storm.

"The terms," Jasper said, fast, and his voice was fraying at the edges as the hymn rose around them, the entity counter-singing, trying to drown him in his own cathedral. "The verse needs a willing voice. I've been flat for fifteen years so the verse *couldn't finish* without a replacement. It needs one twin above, one below. It has you now. But the ledger accepts substitution—*I proved that*, Junie, in the quarry, in the coat. So here is the amendment, and you have to witness it, you have to *say you witness it*, out loud, now—"

"You're not going down there," Juniper said. "I am. That's the whole—"

"I've been down here fifteen years," he said, and his smile cracked open into something that was laughing and grieving in the same breath. "You think I'm going to let them trade me *up*? Junie. Junie, listen. The forgetting. You know about the forgetting now. If you stay below and I go above, I forget you. I forget all of it. The vigil, the journals, the hymn—gone. And the entity gets what it always wanted: the vessel above, hollowed, and a true voice below, and the debt renews forever with nobody left who remembers the terms were ever crooked." He was shouting now, over the hymn, over the groaning roots, and the green-gold light was flickering like a dying bulb. "But if I stay and you go—if the substitution runs the *other* way—then you go up remembering. All of it. Every journal, every name, every term. You go up as the one person in Marrow Creek who knows the whole contract, and Mabel publishes the ledger, and the descendants surrender the water, and the covenant *dissolves*, Junie. The entity doesn't get a forgetting twin above. It gets a witness. And a witness above is the one thing it cannot survive, because its whole power is that the town forgets what it signed."

"You'd be down here *alone*," Juniper said, and her voice broke apart entirely. "Forever. Singing. You'd be the hymn."

"I've been the hymn since I was six months old," Jasper said gently. "This is just the first time I get to choose the key." He turned his head, and his voice rang out across the flooded cathedral, and it was not a drowned boy's voice anymore; it was a founder's voice, a signer's voice, a voice with ink on it. "I am Jasper Eastwick, born second, vessel by theft, singer by fifteen years of practice. I witness the amendment. The substitution runs below-ward. I remain. She ascends. The debt of the Eastwick line is settled by the twin who was always its instrument—and the one above keeps her memory, whole, as surety and as witness. *I say yes.*"

The hymn stopped.

The silence was total, absolute, the silence of a cathedral between movements, and in it Juniper heard the entity's stillness like a held breath, like a creditor reading fine print it had not expected to be read back to it.

"The hymn requires a willing voice," Jasper said into the quiet, softly now, only for her. "And Junie—that's the thing it never understood. Willing isn't the same as wanting. I don't *want* this. I have never wanted this. But I *will* it. There's a difference, and the difference is you."

"Say you witness it," the entity said at last, and its voice had gone very careful, very smooth, the voice of something that has just realized it has been out-negotiated by a child and is deciding whether to honor the letter or break the spirit. "Say the words, Juniper Eastwick. The ledger is open. The ink is wet. The amendment stands or falls on your witness."

Juniper looked at her brother—her true brother, drowned and stubborn and smiling, fifteen years dead and more alive than anyone she had ever known—and every part of her that was still a twin, still a sister, still the girl who had shared a crib and a coat and a whole unremembered childhood with him, screamed *no*.

And every part of her that had read the journals, that had cleaned out his house, that had found the hidden room and the list titled *Ways In* and the photographs of herself asleep—every part of her that knew what fifteen years of his vigilance had bought—knew that refusing him now would be the one betrayal he could never forgive.

"Theo," she said, without turning. "Mabel. When we get up there—whatever it takes, whatever it costs—finish it. Publish it. Surrender it. *Dissolve it.* Promise me."

"We promise," Theo said, and his voice was wrecked. "Juniper, we promise."

She turned back to her brother. The green-gold light steadied around him, waiting.

"I witness it," Juniper said. "The substitution runs below-ward. Jasper Eastwick remains. Juniper Eastwick ascends, remembering, as surety and as witness. I witness the amendment. *I say yes.*"

The ledger—she felt it more than saw it, a vast turning of pages somewhere beneath the water, beneath the roots, beneath the town—closed with a sound like a door like a grave like a book.

Jasper smiled at her, and it was the smile from the crib, the smile from the kitchen, the smile that had been keeping her safe since before she had words to know she needed keeping.

"Go up, Junie," he said. "Sing the answering verse when it comes to you. I'll teach it to you in a dream. I've had fifteen years to write it, and it's a good one."

The water began to rise.

* * *

It rose the way the aquifer had reversed—upward, gently, insistently—lifting Juniper, Theo, and Mabel toward the iron door and the gray daylight beyond it. The kept children watched them go, and the girl in Jasper's jacket pressed her aged hand briefly to Juniper's ankle as she passed, a touch like a signature, like a blessing, like *tell them what we paid*.

Juniper looked back until the roots took him from view. Her brother stood in the green-gold dark with the hymn rising around him at last in his own true key, and he was not flat anymore, and the sound that came up out of the cathedral behind them was the most beautiful and terrible thing she had ever heard, and she was still hearing it, still climbing, still weeping, when the iron door began—bolt by bolt, patient as a metronome—to close above their heads.

And from somewhere above them, thin and wrong and getting closer, a voice began to hum.

It was not Jasper's.
