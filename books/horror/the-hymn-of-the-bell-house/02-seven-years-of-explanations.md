## 2. Seven Years of Explanations

The bell house had a sound it made at night, and I had spent thirty-one years learning not to hear it.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only inheritance my family ever left me. Not the house — though God knows they left me that too, three stories of salt-rotted clapboard on the last lot before the marsh, with its crooked tower and its bell that hadn't rung since before I was born. Not the debt of grief I'd carried down the coast road from the funeral home with Elias's ashes in a box on the passenger seat, buckled in like a child. Precision. The habit of naming things so exactly that they lost their teeth.

*That's not singing,* I used to tell myself at nine years old, lying rigid under quilts while something below moved through the rooms in slow circuits. *That's the wind finding the chimney flue.* The wind did find the chimney flue. That was true. It was also true that the wind had never once, in any other house I'd lived in, sounded like a woman holding one long note just slightly wrong, the way you'd sing if someone had taught you the melody but never the words.

I catalogued them all, over the years. It was almost a comfort, the way some people count sheep. Every noise got a name, and every name was boring, and boring things can't hurt you.

The pipes. Old houses have pipes that knock and groan when temperatures shift; everyone knows this; there are entire internet forums for it. Our pipes knocked in patterns. Three knocks, pause, three knocks, pause, then a long dragging scrape like furniture being moved by someone who didn't want to wake the neighbors. But pipes expand. Pipes contract. Pipes, I told myself for three decades, are simply dramatic.

The raccoons. There were raccoons in the walls — Mama swore to it, and once, memorably, a pest control man confirmed it, charging Grandma four hundred dollars to remove two raccoons and their nest from the attic. What he could not explain, and what I chose not to ask about, was why the sounds continued in the crawlspace beneath the floorboards after the attic was sealed. Raccoons travel, I reasoned. Raccoons are resourceful. Somewhere under the kitchen, a raccoon was pacing a circuit it had walked for years, pausing at intervals, as if checking whether anyone was listening.

The settling wood. Houses settle; this is practically physics. A hundred-year-old house on marsh soil would creak and pop and sigh through every change of season, and ours did, beautifully, musically, in what I recognized even as a child as something close to harmony. Four-part harmony, if I'm honest, which I am trying to be now, here, alone in this house with the boxes. Two parts low and steady, one part wavering above them, and a fourth voice underneath everything, so quiet you could convince yourself you'd invented it. Settling wood doesn't harmonize. I knew that even at nine. But knowing a thing and admitting it are different organs, and mine had atrophied early, trained out of me by a mother who said *grief makes people see things* and a grandmother who said nothing at all, only looked at me over her sewing with an expression I couldn't read until much later, and understood too well: the expression of a woman listening to see whether the child could hear it too.

So: precision. Here is my inventory, taken honestly, on my first night back in the bell house after seven years away.

Seven years. That number again. Seven years since Elias and I had stood in this kitchen at nineteen, and Grandma had said something I've spent seven years failing to remember, and Elias had gone white as paper and said we should go, now, tonight, and we went, and we didn't come back except for holidays we kept mercifully short. Seven years since whatever happened in this house happened, and I still couldn't retrieve it, the way you can't remember the exact shape of a dream by noon. Only the residue. Only the fact that Elias stopped humming after that visit. He'd hummed his whole life before it — tunelessly, constantly, driving teachers mad — and after that week in the bell house he never hummed again. I asked him once, years later, driving home from Christmas. He looked out the window at the dark and said, "You don't want to know what I almost learned," and turned up the radio, and I let him turn up the radio, because that was our whole religion, my family's: don't ask, don't answer, keep the noise explained away.

Now he was dead — drowned, they said, off a pier two states away, alone, at 3 a.m., which made no sense because Elias couldn't swim and was afraid of deep water and always had been — and the house was mine to settle, and I had nowhere else to put my grief except inside these walls, which seemed, as I stood in the doorway with my bags, to be waiting for exactly that.

* * *

The first day was work, and work was mercy. I opened windows and fought the smell — camphor and dust and underneath it something sweeter, older, like flowers left too long in standing water. I swept the kitchen. I found Grandma's reading glasses folded on top of her Bible, waiting, as if she'd stepped out to check the mail and simply never finished coming back. She'd died in her sleep eight months ago, in the back bedroom, and the house had been closed up since, held in probate, held in silence, held.

The boxes were in the parlor. Eleven of them, taped shut in Grandma's careful way, each labeled in her small upright hand: *PHOTOGRAPHS — MARCHETTI*, and beneath that, on several, a second line I didn't examine closely at first: *DO NOT DEVELOP THE NEW ONES.*

I assumed she meant film. Grandma had been the family's amateur photographer for sixty years; there was a darkroom in the basement she'd built herself, red bulb and trays and a line strung with clothespins, and no doubt rolls of undeveloped stock had accumulated toward the end. *Do not develop the new ones* was probably practical advice, or sentimentality, or the ordinary confusion of a dying woman sorting her affairs. Everything was explicable if you worked at it. I had a lifetime's practice.

I started with the oldest box, because grief likes chronology; it gives you somewhere to stand.

My mother at six, gap-toothed, on the front steps. My grandmother young, impossibly, laughing at something outside the frame, her hand resting on the shoulder of a man I took a full minute to recognize as my grandfather, dead before I was born. The bell tower behind them all in every shot, leaning like a question. And in the photographs from the fifties and sixties, children I didn't know — neighbor kids, maybe, or cousins whose names had dissolved — scattered across the lawn in stiff Sunday clothes.

One girl appeared more than the others. Small, dark-haired, with a serious face and a dress that was always slightly too big for her, sleeves past her wrists. She stood at the edge of group photos the way shy children do, half-turned, as if she'd been photographed mid-departure. In one picture she sat on the porch steps with a hymnal open on her knees, far too young to be reading it, looking directly into the camera with an expression no child should own: patience. Absolute, terrible patience, like something waiting for the rest of the world to catch up.

"Cousin," I said aloud, to test the word in the empty room. It didn't fit. Nothing about her fit — not the dress, not the face, not the way that in photo after photo, decade after decade, she never aged. I turned the picture over. On the back, in pencil, in handwriting I didn't recognize — a child's handwriting, round and careful — was written: *me, age 6.*

I set it face-down on the table and told myself the date on the front was smudged. Told myself families reused names. Told myself old prints warp and lie.

The architecture of rationalization is a strange building. You never see yourself adding the beams. You only notice, years later, how load-bearing the lies have become — how the whole structure of your calm depends on them, and how one cracked window lets in a draft that whistles through every room.

By evening I'd unpacked six boxes. The light went amber, then gray, then blue, and I worked by lamplight with the radio on loud, because loud was another beam in the building. And around nine o'clock, as I taped the seventh box shut and reached for the eighth, the house made its first sound since I'd arrived.

Not a pipe-knock. Not raccoon-scrabble. Not settling wood.

A chair, moving across the floor above me. Slowly. Deliberately. The wooden legs stuttering against boards in short drags, the way you move a chair when you're tired, when you're the last one up, when you're pulling it to a table where someone has saved your place.

I stood with my hand flat on the box lid and listened to it cross the ceiling overhead — dining room to parlor wall, maybe ten feet of travel — and stop.

"Pipes," I said to the empty parlor, and heard how thin my voice had gotten, and hated it.

There are no pipes in a ceiling. I knew that. I added it to the catalogue anyway, filed it under *settling wood*, and felt the beam take weight, and felt the draft come through.

* * *

I slept badly, in Grandma's bed, under quilts that smelled of her — lavender and wool and, faintly, smoke, though the woman never smoked a day in her life. Around 2 a.m. I woke to the fourth voice.

I need to explain the fourth voice, because it will matter later, and because saying it plainly is the only penance available to me. All my life, the house's night-noise had been that near-harmony I described: two low steady tones, one wavering above, and beneath them a fourth so quiet it was less a sound than a pressure, a note felt in the sternum rather than heard. As a child I'd named it *the furnace*. Furnaces hum. This one hummed in key.

At 2 a.m., lying in my grandmother's bed, I realized the harmony had changed. The wavering voice was gone. The two low tones remained, patient as tide. And the fourth voice — the furnace, the pressure under the floor of the world — had moved up. Into the space the third voice left. Filling it. Sliding into the vacancy the way water fills a footprint in sand, completely, without effort, without hurry.

Elias died singing, I thought, before I could stop the thought. Then: *No. He drowned. Drowned men don't sing.* Then: *Then who took his part?*

I lay awake until dawn doing what my family does best, which is nothing, which is explaining, which is building. By morning I had it handled: dreams. Grief makes people hear things. Mama's law, invoked like scripture, and it held, mostly, the way wet paper holds.

* * *

The ninth box was heavier than the others, and when I cut the tape I understood why: glass. Dozens of framed photographs packed in Grandma's dish towels, and beneath them, wrapped separately in a pillowcase, a shoebox of negatives and a note card in her hand:

*For whoever settles this. Some pictures were reprinted. Don't ask me why. Burn what you can't explain.*

Burn what you can't explain. My grandmother, the great architect of silence, leaving me a demolition order in her final months. I laughed — one dry note, too loud in the parlor — and unwrapped the frames.

They were our photographs. Mine and Elias's. Birthday parties, school pictures, summers on this lawn, Christmases under a tree I remembered decorating. Ordinary documents of an ordinary childhood, except that every single frame was a reprint — newer paper, newer processing, the colors shifted the way old photos shift when reprinted from the same negative decades later. Grandma had reprinted our entire childhood. Why? Sentiment, surely. An old woman's hands needing work. A gift she never gave.

I arranged them along the parlor floor in chronological order, because order was the only prayer I knew. Me at four on a tricycle. Elias and me at six, gap-toothed twins in matching sweaters, arms slung around each other's necks. Me at nine beside the creek. Elias at twelve, taller, squinting into the sun. Me at fifteen, scowling, all elbows. Elias at seventeen, laughing at something outside the frame.

It was the laughing one that stopped me. Because I remembered that photograph. Elias at seventeen, taken by Mama on the dock at Lake Verret, and in the original — I was sure, absolutely sure, sure the way you're sure of your own heartbeat — Elias had been sitting on the dock steps. Alone. I remembered because I'd teased him for posing alone like a loser, and he'd shoved me, and we'd both laughed.

In the reprint, Elias sat on the dock steps. And beside him, cropped at the edge of the frame, close enough that its armrest nearly touched his elbow, was a wooden chair.

Old-fashioned, straight-backed, the kind of chair that belongs in a church basement or a very old kitchen. Empty. Angled slightly toward him, the way a friend sits. The way family sits.

I knelt on the parlor floor with the frame in both hands and went backward through the row, faster now, my breath going shallow. Me at fifteen, scowling: the chair stood against the fence behind me, ten feet away, empty, patient. Elias at twelve: the chair at the edge of the yard, smaller with distance but unmistakable. Me at nine beside the creek: the chair on the bank above me, and I remembered that bank, and there had been no chair on that bank, there had never been a chair anywhere, we owned no such chair, I would swear it on anything —

Me at six, matching sweaters with Elias, arms around each other: the chair stood behind us both, centered, close, its back just touching our shoulders.

And me at four, on the tricycle, the earliest photograph of me that exists:

The chair was beside the tricycle. Close enough to touch. Empty, straight-backed, angled toward a four-year-old boy with the same terrible patience I'd seen on the face of the dark-haired girl in the hymnal, fifty years ago, age six, forever.

The house settled around me. Or harmonized. At that moment I genuinely could not have told you the difference, and that — that failure of my one true gift, that collapse of precision — frightened me more than the chair did.

Outside, dusk was coming down over the marsh, and the bell tower's silhouette leaned against the last gray light, and somewhere below the floorboards, soft as a held breath, the fourth voice began to hum a melody I almost knew.

In the bottom of the ninth box, under the frames, lay the shoebox of negatives and a single unexposed roll of film, and Grandma's note card, and beneath the note card, folded twice, a page torn from a book — heavy cream paper, ruled for music, covered edge to edge in notation in no system I recognized, and one line of English penciled at the top in my grandmother's hand:

*If you're reading this, Noor, then you've seen the chair. Go down to the darkroom. Print the new roll. And whatever you do, do not sing along.*

I had not yet told her my name was on the estate papers. I had not yet told anyone I was coming at all.

The house hummed, patient, in four parts, and waited for me to decide which kind of heir I was going to be.
