## 8. The Third Sibling

The county records office smelled of toner and old paper, the particular staleness of a building where the air itself had been filed and forgotten. Noor signed in at a desk manned by a woman with reading glasses on a beaded chain, who looked up at him with the flat patience of someone who had watched a thousand people come looking for their own histories and leave disappointed.

"Genealogy?" she asked.

"Estate settlement," he said. It was close enough to true. "Family property. I need birth records for the Marchetti family — all of them."

"All of them," she repeated, and something flickered behind her eyes, the small professional curiosity of a woman who had worked this desk long enough to know that *all* was never a casual word.

He gave her his grandmother's name — Odette Marchetti — and the address of the bell house, and she tapped it into a terminal older than he was. The screen glowed green-white against her face.

"Twins," she said. "Elias and Noor Marchetti, born June third, 1991." She glanced up. "You're one of them."

"Noor."

"Congratulations on being alive." She said it without humor, without cruelty, just the dry arithmetic of her job, and turned back to the screen. Her fingers paused. "There's a third record here. Restricted flag."

Noor's mouth went dry. "Restricted how?"

"Sealed by family request." She tilted the monitor slightly toward him, an act of small rebellion or small kindness. "Colette Marchetti. Born September fourteenth, 1990. Nine months before you two." She frowned at the dates, doing the math any stranger would do. "That's a tight gap."

"We were triplets," Noor said, and heard his own voice as if from another room. "We were — I don't remember. I don't remember her."

The woman's frown deepened. "Record's sealed until 2065. But the death certificate would be public record, if there is one." More tapping. "Here. Colette M. Marchetti, deceased." She pulled up the image and printed it without asking him if he wanted to see it, sliding the warm page across the counter like a prescription.

He read it standing there, under the fluorescent hum.

*Cause of death: respiratory failure following prolonged illness.* Date of death: August 2, 1999. He would have been eight. Elias would have been eight. They would have been three children, and then they were two, and no one had told them. No one had ever told them.

But that wasn't the thing that made his hands go cold against the counter.

The signature line at the bottom read *Odette R. Marchetti, informant* — and the date beside it was not 1999. The ink was different, bluer, fresher, the handwriting looped and certain in a way he knew down to his bones, because he'd seen it on every birthday card, every recipe, every note tucked into his lunchbox: *Be good, my songbirds.*

His grandmother had signed her granddaughter's death certificate forty-one years after the girl had supposedly died.

Noor stood very still while the printer hummed out a second copy he hadn't asked for. The clerk watched him fold the pages into his jacket pocket with the careful movements of a man handling something that might still be alive.

"You want the birth record too?" she asked quietly. "I can't give you the sealed file. But births are births."

"Yes," he said. "Please."

She printed it. Colette Marguerite Marchetti, born at home, attended by a midwife, father listed as Henri Marchetti, mother Odette. And in the margin, in faded typewriter font, a notation he had to squint to read: *See also: institutional commitment, St. Dymphna's Home for Children, 1996.*

Six years old. Committed at six years old, nine months after her baby brothers were born.

"Thank you," Noor said, and his voice came out wrong, scraped thin.

"Son," the clerk said, before he could turn away. She took off her glasses and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "I've been at this desk thirty years. Families seal records for two reasons. Shame, or worse than shame." She slid a business card across the counter. "St. Dymphna's closed in 2004. Records went to the diocesan archive. If you're settling an estate, settle all of it."

---

He didn't go straight back to the house. He drove instead to the edge of town, to the diner where he and Elias had eaten pancakes every Sunday after church, back when church was still a thing their family did, back when their family was still a thing that pretended to be whole. He sat in the same booth, under the same water stain on the ceiling shaped like a hand, and ordered coffee he didn't drink, and laid the documents out on the laminate like tarot cards.

Birth. Commitment. Death.

A life in three documents, each one thinner than the last.

He kept trying to remember her. That was the worst part — not the absence of memory but the sense that memory had been *removed*, that there were shapes in his childhood where a person should have been. A third high chair that Mama had called "an extra we never got rid of." A lullaby Mama sang with a hitch in the middle, a skipped beat, like a stair with one step missing. The way Grandma Odette had flinched, once, when little Noor had drawn a picture of three stick figures holding hands and asked, *who's the other one?*

*Just a mistake, sweetheart,* she'd said, and taken the crayon from his hand, and that was that. Seven years old, and already learning the family language: what is not spoken does not exist.

Except the house spoke. The house had always spoken. Pipes, raccoons, settling wood — he'd spent his whole childhood translating its voice into things that couldn't hurt him. And now, sitting in a diner booth with a dead girl's paperwork, he understood that he hadn't been translating at all. He'd been *censoring*. The family trade. Loyalty meant silence; silence meant belonging. He had been fluent since before he could read.

His phone buzzed on the table. Mama. He let it ring out. There were six voicemails from her already, slurred 3 a.m. sermons about burning the hymnal, about the house, about *the girl we gave them* — and he wasn't ready. Not yet. First he wanted the whole shape of it, the whole architecture of the lie, so that when he finally called her back, nothing she said could rearrange the facts.

Facts first. Facts were the only solid ground left.

St. Dymphna's Home for Children. The diocesan archive. He photographed the documents with shaking hands and got back in the car.

---

The shoebox was in the attic of the bell house, which was the last place he thought to look and therefore, he was learning, exactly where the house kept things.

He'd gone up for Christmas decorations — an absurd errand, muscle memory from childhood, as if he were going to celebrate anything in this place — and found the box wedged between a rotted wicker bassinet and a trunk of moth-eaten coats. It was a plain shoebox, the kind sneakers came in decades ago, the cardboard soft with age, and across the lid in pencil, in a child's careful letters:

*MINE. DO NOT TAKE. THIS MEANS YOU ELIAS.*

Noor sat down on a trunk and held the box on his knees for a long time before he opened it.

Inside: hospital bracelets. Three of them, infant-sized, the plastic yellowed and brittle. *NOOR MARCHETTI. MALE. 06-03-91.* *ELIAS MARCHETTI. MALE. 06-03-91.* And a third, smaller, worn almost smooth, as if it had been handled thousands of times: *COLETTE MARCHETTI. F. 09-14-90.*

Under the bracelets: photographs. Dozens of them. A dark-haired girl with a serious face and a gap between her front teeth, holding one twin or the other, both babies nearly as big as she was. A girl pushing a swing. A girl asleep on a couch with a book open on her chest, *A Child's Garden of Verses*, her finger keeping her place. A girl standing in front of the bell house, squinting into the sun, mid-laugh, caught by whatever joke or wonder had found her in that instant.

In every photograph, she wore the bracelet.

And in every photograph, Noor felt the floor of his understanding tilt. Because these weren't hidden photos, weren't tucked-away shame. Someone had kept them together, curated them, this box of a girl's whole short life — and then written *MINE. DO NOT TAKE* on the lid in a child's hand and buried it in the attic like contraband.

At the bottom of the box was a notebook. Composition book, marbled cover, half filled with a child's uneven cursive. Noor read it by the light of his phone flashlight, kneeling in the dust, and the attic went very cold around him, though the afternoon sun was still hammering the roof.

*Dear Diary. Mama says I have to go stay at the school for a while because I am special. She cried but she said special children get extra help. I do not want extra help. I want to stay home and help with the babys.*

*The house sang to me last night. It knows my name. Mama says houses dont sing. But this one does. It sings the pretty song and when I sing along the walls get warm like somebody hugging you. Mama got mad when she heard me. She said NEVER sing the fourth part. I dont know what the fourth part is. I only know the song.*

*I made friends with the house. It is better than the school. At the school they hold your hands down when you sing. Here nobody holds your hands down.*

Noor stopped breathing.

*Last page. Tomorrow I go away for real. Grandma says if I am very brave and very quiet the house will remember me instead of the school remembering me. I dont understand but I said ok. I hope the babys forget me fast so it doesnt hurt them. Grandma says forgetting is a kind of love. I think that is a lie but I am only six.*

The last entry was dated three days before the commitment papers. And beneath it, in an adult hand — his grandmother's hand, unmistakable, the loops and certainties of the death certificate — a single line added years later, ink browned with age:

*Forgiveness is not owed to us. But the debt is still ours to pay.*

Noor closed the notebook. In the silence of the attic, faint and unmistakable, the house began — very softly — to hum.

Not the hymn. Something gentler. A child's tune, skipping in the middle, one beat missing.

He gathered the box against his chest and carried it downstairs, past the photographs on the wall where the extra chair waited in every frame, patient as furniture, patient as grief. He set the box on the kitchen table and laid out the documents beside it: birth, commitment, death. Three papers. One girl.

They hadn't lost her. They hadn't mourned her. They had *administered* her — committed her, erased her, and then, decades later, quietly signed her out of existence with a pen stroke, closing the account, balancing the books. And through all of it, the house had kept her room warm. The house had sung her song. The house remembered what the family had agreed to forget.

Loyalty, Noor thought, staring at his grandmother's forged signature. All those years he'd been proud of it — the Marchetti closeness, the way they circled each other, the way no outsider was ever quite let in. It had never been love. It had been a cover-up with Sunday dinner attached.

His phone buzzed again. Mama. Then buzzed again — a text this time, which she almost never sent, which meant she was sober enough to type or frightened enough to try:

*Dont dig. Whatever you found, put it back. Some songs are sung once so they dont have to be sung twice. Please baby. Come home and we will talk. NOT in that house.*

Noor looked at the text for a long moment. Then he typed his reply, slowly, each word chosen like a stone placed on a cairn:

*I found her name. Her name is Colette. Why doesn't anyone say it?*

The reply came fast, as if Mama had been holding it for fifty years, waiting:

*Because the last person who said it out loud in that house was your grandmother. And the house hasnt stopped singing since.*

Down in the basement, faintly, through the floorboards, Noor heard the darkroom door swing open on its own.

He hadn't left it open.
