## 20. The Last Verse

The house sealed itself at dusk.

Noor heard it happen — not a sound so much as an absence, the last of the evening birdsong cut off mid-note as though someone had drawn a velvet bag over the world. He stood at the kitchen window and watched the front door swing shut on its own, slow and courteous, the way his grandmother used to close doors when she didn't want to wake the children. The lock turned with a click that sounded almost like a kiss.

*Mama.* She was somewhere in the east wing, where the hallway had been breathing all afternoon, walls expanding and contracting like the ribs of something asleep. He had tried to reach her an hour ago and found the corridor grown over with chair railings and wainscoting that hadn't been there before, wood grain swirling into patterns that hurt to look at directly — faces, maybe, or hands.

*Odile.* At the threshold. He'd seen her through the parlor window, standing on the porch with her key held against her chest like a medal, too old to force the door and too stubborn to leave. Her lips were moving. Praying, or rehearsing. Maybe both.

And Elias — Elias was everywhere now. In the tremor of the lightbulbs. In the way the floorboards hummed a half-step below middle C. His brother's presence pressed against Noor's back like a hand between the shoulder blades, guiding him up the stairs, and Noor let himself be guided because there was nowhere else left to go. The choir loft was waiting. It had been waiting for fifty years.

* * *

The loft had assembled itself above the sanctuary of what had once, long ago, been the house's chapel — a room Noor had never known existed, hidden behind the linen closet, its door disguised as wallpaper until tonight. Now it stood open, exhaling cold air that smelled of hymnals and pond water and something sweeter underneath, like lilies rotting in a warm room.

The bell rope hung down through the ceiling, frayed into braids of dark hair that stirred without wind. Above it, invisible but audible, the great bell waited in its tower, humming its single patient note.

The chairs were arranged in two rows of four. Three of them were occupied.

Not by bodies. Not exactly. By shapes the way photographs are occupied by people — flat presences that nonetheless had weight, had attention, had *faces*. Noor recognized his grandmother immediately. Alma Marchetti sat in the second row with her hands folded, her cardigan buttoned wrong the way she always did in her last years, and her eyes were kind and terrible and patient as stone. Beside her sat a man Noor had never met but knew from the wedding photograph on the stairs: his grandfather, mouth open mid-verse, forever. And in the third chair, small and still, a girl of about nine with dark hair cut short and hospital bracelets stacked on one thin wrist like bangles.

Colette.

She looked up at him. Her eyes were photograph-paper eyes, silvered and flat, but behind them something moved — something that had been waiting so long it had forgotten how to hope, and was trying to remember.

"Noor." Grandma's voice came from everywhere, from the walls, from the rope, from under the floor. Warm. Reasonable. The voice of every family dinner where the hard thing went unsaid. "You came. I knew you would. You were always the honest one."

"I came for Elias."

"You came because you finally stopped pretending." She smiled, and the smile was love, and the smile was a trap that had been baited since before he was born. "That's all this house has ever asked, sweetheart. Stop pretending. Take your place. We've kept your brother safe, we've kept Colette company, we've kept *everyone* — no one is ever lost here, no one is ever alone. Isn't that what you've wanted your whole life? An end to explaining?"

And God help him, some starving animal part of him said *yes*. Fifty years of silence in this family. Seven years of pretending Elias had never had a sister. A lifetime of noises explained away, of questions swallowed, of grief eaten whole so no one else had to taste it. The choir loft was warm. The chairs looked soft. Somewhere in the harmony he could hear rest — real rest, the kind that doesn't require lying.

He thought of Mama at nineteen, singing along, and spending forty years half-in, drinking herself toward the other half.

He thought of Odile on the porch, keeping her vigil for a sister the house still held.

He took out the torn page.

* * *

Colette's handwriting was a child's handwriting, round and careful, the letters leaning forward like they were eager. Noor had read it forty times since finding it inside the chair's hollow seat, and each time the same line stopped him:

*A willing voice may sing the singer free.*

A release clause. Written by a nine-year-old who had understood, even trapped in the house's memory, that the song was a contract — and contracts have exits, if you can read them. But the final verse as the house taught it, as the bell engraved it, ended differently. The bell's version demanded substitution. A name given up so another could be taken. That was how the trade worked. That was how *he* was supposed to work: walk in, sing, take Colette's place, free Elias. One for one. The arithmetic of loyalty.

His grandmother's arithmetic.

Noor stood at the front of the loft, the torn page trembling in his hand, and understood that the trap wasn't in the singing. The trap was in the *terms*. The house needed him to accept its math — that people are interchangeable, that silence is protection, that a debt paid in secret stays paid. Seven years ago they had traded a daughter in secret to save a son, and the house had collected anyway, because secrets don't settle debts. They only defer them.

So he wouldn't sing it their way.

"Grandma," he said, and his voice cracked in the middle like a boy's. "I'm going to sing the last verse."

Her face lit with triumph, and beneath the triumph, relief — fifty years of guilt finally set down. "Good boy. Good boy. Take the chair beside—"

"But I'm changing the ending."

The warmth in the room dropped ten degrees. The three seated figures flickered like film catching in a projector. "Noor," his grandmother said, and the kindness was gone, and what was underneath was very old and very hungry. "The verse is the verse. It does not change. It has never changed."

"Everything changes," Noor said. "That's the whole point of being alive instead of being a house."

He lifted the page. He closed his eyes. He thought of Elias laughing in the summer kitchen, flour on his nose. Of Colette's bracelets. Of Odile's hand on his arm saying *the song needs four voices and always has* — and how she'd been wrong, or lied to, or lied to herself, because the song didn't need four voices. It needed four *silences*. Four people agreeing not to say a name.

He opened his eyes, looked straight at Colette, and began.

* * *

The melody rose out of him like water finding a crack. He had learned it verse by verse over the past weeks, working from the bell's engraving with Odile's flashlight and his own shaking hands, and now the first three verses came without effort, in a language no dictionary held but every Marchetti's blood remembered. Around him the house began to sway. The walls breathed in time. His grandmother's mouth moved along with his, silent, conducting.

The fourth verse. The trade verse. *One voice for another, one name for a name, the singer descends where the singer became—*

Here. The ending line. The hinge of everything.

The house leaned in. He felt Elias press close against his shoulder blades, felt Colette's paper eyes fixed on him, felt his grandmother's will bearing down like a hand on the back of his neck, bending him toward the chair, toward the words the bell had engraved, toward *I take her place, I take her place, I take her place—*

Noor sang:

"*I do not take her place. I speak her name.*"

The note he hit on *name* was not a note. It was a key turning. It was a door opening onto daylight after fifty years.

"*Colette Marchetti,*" he sang, and the syllables rang through the loft like struck metal. "*Born third. Born wanted. Born before the lie. I saw you. I see you. You were never a debt. You were never a payment. You were a girl, and your name is Colette, and I am the witness, and the witness owes nothing—*"

The house screamed.

It was not a sound; it was the removal of every sound at once, a vacuum of noise that popped his ears and sucked the breath from his lungs. The walls convulsed. The wainscoting rippled like the skin of a struck drum. Downstairs, furniture slammed and skidded — he heard the dining table overturn, heard china burst, heard decades of rearrangement tearing loose all at once as the house tried to hold its shape around the one thing it could not survive:

Being *named*. Being witnessed. Truth spoken aloud, in front of everyone, breaking the spell of the family silence that had fed it for three generations.

Loyalty had built this place. Loyalty — the twisted, loving, suffocating kind that keeps secrets to protect people and calls the keeping love. And truth was the solvent, and Noor was pouring it into the foundation.

"*Your name is Colette,*" he sang again, louder, tears streaming freely now, voice raw. "*And I refuse the trade. I refuse it. I refuse it. I REFUSE IT—*"

Above him, the great bell rang.

Once. Enormous. A sound that was also a shattering — and then the second toll never came, because the bell *cracked*, a jagged lightning-strike of failure running through fifty years of cast bronze, and the note it released as it split was the truest sound Noor had ever heard: not the hymn, not the harmony, just pure broken ringing, a bell finally allowed to be damaged, to be finished, to stop.

The rope of braided hair unraveled into nothing.

The choir loft dissolved around him like sugar in rain. His grandmother's figure looked at him as she came apart — and her expression, in the last instant, was not rage. It was gratitude. Her mouth shaped two words — *thank you* — and then she was dust and draft and gone, fifty years of complicity forgiven by the only person left to forgive it. His grandfather followed. The chairs folded inward like hands unclenching.

And the singers came out.

They came out of the walls, out of the floors, out of the photographs — Noor felt them pass through him, dozens of them, generations of them, cold streams of presence flowing toward the cracking bell and beyond it, toward whatever waits outside a song when the song ends. He glimpsed faces. A woman in 1960s cat-eye glasses holding a smaller woman's hand. Farmhands. Children. Odile's sister, pausing for one heartbeat to touch Noor's cheek with fingers like the memory of wind, before streaming upward and away.

Mama's voice, sobbing somewhere below, suddenly *whole* — the half of her that had been missing since nineteen coming home to the half that remained, and the sound she made was the sound of a person becoming one piece again, and it was terrible and holy.

And Colette.

She stood in the ruins of the loft, solid for the first time, a real girl with real eyes — brown, not silver — looking down at her own hands, turning them over, crying without knowing why. Hospital bracelets slid off her wrist and crumbled to rust on the floor.

Noor knelt. "Hi," he said, uselessly, wonderfully. "Hi, Colette. I'm your brother."

"I know," she said. Her voice was nine years old and fifty years deep. "You sang my name. Nobody ever sang my name."

The bell groaned overhead, shedding flakes of bronze like autumn leaves. The house around them was convulsing through its death throes — or its birth throes, Noor couldn't tell — walls settling into ordinary angles, doors swinging open onto ordinary rooms, the air warming degree by degree toward something a living person could breathe.

Colette was already fading at the edges, going translucent, and she was smiling. "I don't want to stay," she said. "I want to go. I've wanted to go for fifty years. Will you remember me? Not the secret me. *Me.*"

"Every day," Noor said. "Out loud. Where everyone can hear."

She touched his face, the way her friend's sister had, the way family does, and then she let go — willingly, easily, a girl walking out of a house at last — and the place where she'd stood filled with evening light from a window that hadn't existed an hour ago.

Noor was alone in the settling dust, throat shredded, heart hammering, alive.

Then, from downstairs — from the kitchen floor, from inside the ordinary, silent, merely-old house — came the sound of someone gasping awake.

A sound he knew better than his own name.

"Elias," Noor whispered, and ran.
