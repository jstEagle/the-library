## 1. The Wrong Grave

The train did not so much arrive in Hollowmere as give up on it.

Ada Okafor watched the platform slide into view through a window streaked with October rain—two lamps, one bench, a sign whose letters had been repainted so many times the word HOLLOWMERE sat atop older words like a scar over scars. The carriage exhaled them onto wet gravel, and then it was gone, taking with it the last of the light and the last of Ada's certainty that coming back was a good idea.

Twelve years. Twelve years since they had left at nineteen with two suitcases and a scholarship and a promise to Emeka: *I'll send for you when I'm settled.* Twelve years of postcards that got shorter, calls that got rarer, until the calls stopped being returned and Ada told themselves it was simply what happened to siblings—distance calcifying into silence, silence hardening into habit. They had told themselves many things about Emeka over the years. It was a kind of craft, denial. You got better at it the way you got better at anything.

Now there was nothing left to tell themselves except *come home and bury him.*

The walk from the station took twenty minutes along a lane lined with hawthorn gone black with rot. Hollowmere revealed itself the way drowned things reveal themselves in murky water—a roofline first, then a chimney, then the whole grey village assembled out of the drizzle: slate and stone and shuttered windows, a single street climbing the hillside in nine uneven terraces toward the church. Ninth Street, the locals called it, though Ada remembered their mother calling it something else, a name she'd only ever said once, quietly, as if apologizing to the house itself.

Ada's boots found the old rhythm of the hill without asking permission. That was the cruelty of home: your body remembered it even when you wished it wouldn't. The smell came next—wet peat, coal smoke, and beneath both something sweeter and wronger, like flowers kept too long in a closed room. Chrysanthemums. Funeral flowers. The whole village smelled faintly of a wake already in progress.

* * *

St. Brannoc's stood where it had always stood, squatting at the top of the hill like a toad that had decided, long ago, that it would not be moved. Its flint walls drank the rain. Its tower rose black against a sky the color of dishwater, and Ada noticed—only noticed, only filed away—the oddness of it: no bell hung in the belfry arch. Just an empty stone mouth, open to the weather, silent as a pulled tooth.

They had never questioned that as a child. Churches without bells were simply churches without bells. Now, standing in the churchyard gate with rain needling the back of their neck, Ada felt the absence the way you feel a missing stair in the dark—one step where your foot expects weight and finds nothing, and for half a second the whole world tilts.

Mourners clustered under umbrellas by the open grave. Ada counted them without meaning to—an old habit, counting exits—and got fourteen. Fourteen people to bury Emeka Okafor, who had lived his whole life here. Some faces Ada knew: Mrs. Aldous from the shop, grown small and papery; the Pennell brothers, red-faced and uncomfortable in suits; old Maren Voss at the edge of the group, standing apart the way she always had, her coat buttoned to the throat despite the crowd's warmth pressing close. She was looking not at the grave but at the empty tower, and her lips moved around something that was not a prayer—or if it was a prayer, it was one said backward.

No one looked at Ada. That too was familiar. The village had never quite known what to do with the Okafor twins, the doctor's children, brown-skinned and strange-named in a parish where everyone else could trace their family to a gravestone older than the church. Ada had spent childhood learning to be unremarkable, and apparently twelve years away hadn't dulled the skill. They drifted to the edge of the gathering like smoke finding a draft.

Reverend Ilse Marsh stood at the head of the grave. Ada remembered her vaguely—a tall woman, severe, who had taught Sunday school with the gentleness of someone handling explosives. She had aged into her severity beautifully; she looked carved now, cheekbones like flint knapping. When she began the committal her voice carried clean across the churchyard, pitched to reach every ear and touch none of them.

*"We have come together to commit to the ground the body of our brother—"*

A rope creaked. Under the green baize, the mechanism groaned, and the coffin began its descent.

And beside the grave, waiting to be set in place after the earth went in, stood the headstone.

Ada saw it sideways first, the way you see things you are not meant to see—peripherally, in the corner of the eye where truth lives before politeness edits it. Grey granite. A simple cross. And cut deep into the stone, filled with lead lettering gone silver in the rain:

**EMEKA OKAFOR**
*1990 – 2012*
*Beloved son. Gone before his time.*

That was right. That was correct. Ada's eyes moved down the stone expecting nothing, expecting blank granite below the dates, and found instead a second line of text, smaller, in the same careful lead:

**AND HIS TWIN, ADA OKAFOR**
*1990 – 2002*

The world did not stop. That was the terrible thing. The world went on exactly as before—the rain fell, the rope creaked, Reverend Marsh's voice flowed on like water over stones—and Ada stood in the middle of it with their own death date ringing in their skull like a struck bell.

*2002.* They had done the arithmetic before conscious thought caught up, the way you flinch before you know why. 2002. The year they turned twelve. The year they couldn't remember—not the summer, not the autumn, not the weeks between Emeka's birthday and their own. There was a hole there, a smooth-walled absence they had never examined because examining it felt like pressing a bruise. Their mother had said *you were ill that year, darling, a fever, you slept through most of it.* Their father had said nothing at all, which had been his way.

The coffin reached the bottom of the grave. Earth began to fall.

"Sorry—" Ada heard themselves say, and their voice sounded foreign, thin, a child's voice. "Sorry, I think—I think there's been a mistake."

Heads turned. Fourteen faces, and now they all looked at Ada at once, and Ada understood in one cold flood why they hadn't looked before. It wasn't indifference. It was the specific, practiced aversion of people who have agreed not to see something. Mrs. Aldous's mouth opened and closed. One of the Pennell brothers took a step back and put his umbrella between himself and Ada as though rain might come off them.

"You must be the cousin," Reverend Marsh said smoothly. Her eyes were pale grey and utterly still, like water over a well. "From London. We weren't certain you'd come."

"I'm not the cousin." Ada's heart was slamming. "I'm Ada. I'm Ada Okafor. Emeka is my—that was my brother. My twin."

Something passed through the little congregation then, quick and silent as a rat under a hedge. Not surprise. Recognition. As if a thing long predicted had finally arrived and everyone was checking their watches.

Maren Voss turned her head. She was the only one who looked at Ada directly, and her face—deep-lined, weathered, one eye clouded white with cataract—held an expression Ada would spend months trying to name and never quite manage. It was pity. It was apology. It was, underneath everything, fear.

"The records," Reverend Marsh said, and her voice remained perfectly level, perfectly kind, which made it worse, "are quite clear. Ada Okafor died in the autumn of 2002. Fever." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "It happens, in parishes like ours, that families—grieve in ways that confuse them. You have my sympathy. Truly."

"I'm not confused." But even as Ada said it, the word *fever* landed somewhere old and soft inside them, and the smooth-walled hole in their memory of 2002 yawned wider, and for one vertiginous moment they were twelve years old again and burning and sinking and—

Rain on their face brought them back. The mourners had begun to disperse, folding umbrellas, murmuring condolences at nobody, leaving the grave as quickly as decency allowed. Within two minutes Ada stood alone at the graveside with the sexton, who was waiting with visible impatience to finish his work, and the headstone, and the rain, and the second line of lead lettering shining up at them like an accusation.

*AND HIS TWIN, ADA OKAFOR.*

Their own name. Cut in granite. Dated twelve years dead.

The sexton cleared his throat. "Begging pardon," he said, not unkindly, "but that stone's been ordered near a year now. Paid for by the parish council, special. Stone doesn't lie, missus—it says what it says." He touched his cap, awkward. "My gran always held the parish books don't lie either. Folk do. Books don't."

Ada didn't answer. They were looking past him, up at the tower, at the empty belfry arch where no bell hung, and they were remembering—suddenly, completely, with the awful clarity of a wound reopening—something they had forgotten for thirty years.

Emeka, age nine or ten, sitting cross-legged on the bedroom floor, counting on his fingers. *Nine tolls, Adda. I counted nine but Mum says the bell was melted before we were born. Somebody's ringing a melted bell.*

They had laughed at him. They had told him he'd dreamed it.

The sexton picked up his shovel. Somewhere down the hill, a door closed. The rain thickened, softening the edges of the churchyard until the world looked unfinished, a sketch abandoned by its artist.

Ada turned and walked down through the lych-gate, out of St. Brannoc's, down the nine terraces of Ninth Street toward the house they had inherited and did not want, and behind them the empty tower stood open-mouthed against the sky, and though no wind stirred and no bird called and no bell existed anywhere in Hollowmere to make a sound—

Ada counted, without meaning to, without wanting to, nine seconds of perfect silence before the rain resumed.

* * *

The house at Number Nine, Ninth Street, had not changed, which was somehow worse than if it had. Same green door swollen in its frame. Same brass knocker shaped like a fish, worn featureless by a century of hands. Ada let themselves in with the key from the solicitor's envelope and stood in the hallway breathing the smell of it—dust and cold ash and, beneath that, the ghost of their mother's cooking, some spice memory clinging to the plaster that no amount of years could evict.

Two mugs on the draining board. Ada stood looking at them for a long time.

One was theirs, from the last visit—the chipped blue one, the one they'd always claimed. The other was Emeka's, the heavy white one with the hairline crack. Both still on the board, unwashed, as if the kitchen expected them back. As if whoever had last stood here had stepped out for a moment and meant to return.

Ada washed the mugs. It seemed important, suddenly, unbearably important, that the mugs be washed. Their hands shook doing it, and the water ran cold because the boiler had died months ago, and when they finished they dried each mug and set them side by side on the shelf, and then they pressed both palms flat against the counter and breathed until the shaking slowed.

Outside, the dark came down early, the way it does in villages built in valleys—the hills swallowing the sun an hour before it truly sets. Ada lit the lamp in the front room, the room where the piano used to be, and unpacked nothing, and sat in their father's old chair with their coat still on, listening to the house settle.

Houses settle. Pipes knock. Birds get into chimneys. Beams contract in the cold. Ada had a lifetime's catalogue of rational explanations filed and cross-referenced, and they began reciting it now like a litany, because from upstairs—from Emeka's room, the locked room the solicitor had no key for—came a sound.

Not knocking. Not footsteps. Something softer. A hum. Low, tuneless, patient, rising and falling like breath.

Pipes, Ada told themselves. Birds. Settling beams.

The hum paused. Then, distinctly, in the pause: a sound like a boy laughing.

Ada did not sleep that night. They sat in the lamplight until the bulb dimmed and warmed and dimmed, and at some hour they could not name—they would learn the hour later, would learn it in their bones—they heard, far off, high up, impossibly, from a tower that had held no bell since 1911:

A single toll.

Then another. Then seven more.

Nine.

Ada sat very still in the dying light, their own name fresh-cut in granite behind them on the hill, and understood—with the calm that arrives when dread has nowhere further to fall—that whatever had taken their brother had never finished with this family at all.

On the windowpane, though the fire was out and the night was mild, frost was beginning to form.

Inside the glass.
