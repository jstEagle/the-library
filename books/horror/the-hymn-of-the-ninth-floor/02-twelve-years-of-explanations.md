## 2. Twelve Years of Explanations

The house on Ninth Street had been waiting for Ada the way a spider waits: patiently, without appetite, in the exact center of its own geometry.

They stood in the hall for a long time after the funeral car pulled away, keys still in hand, coat still buttoned, as if movement might commit them to something. The air inside smelled of dust and old lavender and, beneath both, something faintly mineral—the smell of a cellar, or of stone that has been rained on. Emeka's house now. Their house. The solicitor's phrase kept replaying: *your late brother's estate passes to you as sole surviving kin.* Sole surviving kin. As if there had been others once, and the word *surviving* was doing quiet, terrible work.

Ada hung their coat on a hook that had never held a coat before—it was too high, mounted for someone taller—and climbed the stairs to the landing.

Twelve years since they'd last slept under this roof. Twelve years since the morning they'd left for university with two suitcases and a promise to come back at Christmas, and Hollowmere had let them go with a relief they hadn't understood until now. Everyone else stayed. Everyone else always stayed. And the ones who left—

No. They weren't going to do this. They were going to make tea, open windows, sleep twelve hours, and drive back to London on Thursday with a box of Emeka's things and a grief they could carry like everyone else carried theirs.

The kettle ticked as it heated. Somewhere above them, a pipe knocked twice.

*Pipes,* Ada thought, and the word arrived pre-worn, smooth from handling, like a coin carried in a pocket for years. They knew that word. They had built whole childhoods out of that word.

* * *

Here is what Ada remembered, lying awake that first night in Emeka's bed because their own room felt like a held breath:

When they were six, a sound came up through the floorboards of the bedroom they'd shared—three slow knocks, spaced too evenly to be anything alive, too soft to be anything mechanical. Ada had run to Mum, who'd said, without looking up from her sewing, *pipes.* And Ada had believed her, because children believe the architecture adults build over the dark, and because the alternative was unthinkable, and because belief is easier than courage when you're six.

When they were eight, something had moved along the gutter outside the window on a windless night—a dragging, deliberate weight, like a body being pulled by the ankles. Dad had gone out with a torch and come back saying it was a bird, a big rook probably, caught in the mesh. There was no bird. There was no mesh. But Dad's face had been pale in a way Ada hadn't known how to read then, and so: *a bird.*

At nine, the humming. That was the one Ada had never fully rationalized, only buried. A tune drifting down the stairwell at night, thin and patient, eight notes rising and then stopping—always stopping, always just short of a ninth note, as if the singer were waiting for permission. Mum had said it was the woman next door and her radio. Mrs. Calloway next door had been deaf for a decade.

And at ten—

Ada sat up in the dark.

At ten, there had been the journal. Two journals, actually, bought in a matching set from the stationer's in Ashcombe, blue covers, elastic bands. One for each twin. Ada's had filled with drawings of horses and complaints about school dinners. Emeka's had been found years later during a clear-out, and Ada had skimmed it, laughed at his spelling, put it back in the box.

But there had been an entry—one entry—that Ada had never been able to explain, and so had never tried. An entry written in a hand that was not Emeka's, though it was exactly Emeka's handwriting, letter for letter, slant for slant. Twins' hands are similar; Ada knew that; they'd been told that all their lives, told it like a comfort. But this wasn't similarity. This was identity. Ada had once signed Emeka's name on a detention slip as a joke and fooled even Mum. But no one could fool themselves. Ada had looked at that journal page at eleven years old and known, with the cold certainty of the body rather than the mind, that they were looking at their own writing that they had never written.

*It knows your name already,* the entry said. *Don't learn the ninth verse. Don't sing along even if he asks nicely. Especially if he asks nicely.*

Ada had shown it to no one. Had decided—had *chosen*, with the full machinery of a child's denial, which is the most powerful denial there is—that they must have written it themselves and forgotten. Sleepwalking. A game. Some half-dreamed experiment. Children forget things; it was simpler than every alternative; simplicity is the shape denial takes.

They had not thought of that journal in twenty-two years.

Now, in Emeka's dark bedroom, in Emeka's silence, it rose in them whole, every word intact, and Ada lay staring at the ceiling and understood that they had spent their entire adult life not thinking about it, the way you spend your life not thinking about a door you've nailed shut.

The pipes knocked again. Twice. Paused. Twice.

Ada counted the interval between the pairs. Four seconds. Then again. Four seconds. It was not water. Water does not keep time.

*Settling beams,* they told themselves, and got up, and turned on every light in the house, and did not sleep again.

* * *

Morning made everything smaller and safer, as mornings do, and Ada spent it doing the practical work of grief: contacting the utility companies, finding Emeka's bank statements, standing in his kitchen making inventory of a life that had apparently been small and orderly and full of music paper. Sheet music everywhere. On the piano, in drawers, folded into books. Hymnals, mostly—old ones, their pages soft as cloth. Ada set these aside without opening them. Grief enough for one week.

It was on the second day, looking for spare keys in the sideboard drawer, that they found the box.

Cardboard, water-stained at one corner, labeled in Mum's handwriting: *EMEKA — SCHOOL.* Inside: certificates, a brass recorder, a photograph of two gap-toothed children squinting on a wall. And at the bottom, wrapped in a plastic bag against damp, the blue journal.

Ada's hands knew it before their mind did. The elastic band, perished and cracked. The cover's corner softened white from thumbing. They carried it to the kitchen table, set it down, and sat looking at it for a long while while the radiator ticked and the light moved slowly across the floorboards.

Then they opened it.

Emeka's handwriting, age ten, marched across the early pages—*my tetchr is unfair*, *we won 4-2 i got 1 goal*, entries that broke Ada's heart in the ordinary way, the way all dead children's handwriting breaks hearts. Ada turned the pages gently, smiling wet-eyed at a drawing of a dog the family had never owned, at a list titled *things im scared of* that included spiders, deep water, and, in smaller letters pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages, *the singing.*

They stopped turning.

The entry was where they remembered it being. Third from the end. Dated the fourteenth of October, the year they were both ten.

Ada knew what it would say. They had known for twenty-two years; they had simply declined to know it. Now they read it anyway, because Emeka was dead and the dead deserve witnesses, and because some part of them had already begun, quietly, horribly, to suspect that this entry was not a child's strangeness but a message—one half of a correspondence, sent across a lifetime, waiting for the other twin to finally come home and read it.

*It knows your name already,* the entry said. *I heard it say yours first and then mine after, like it was checking them off. Don't learn the ninth verse. Don't sing along even if he asks nicely. Especially if he asks nicely. He sounds like Daad but underneath he doesnt sound like anyone.*

Beneath that, in the same impossible hand, smaller:

*I think it lives on the ninth floor but our house only has 2 floors. I counted the stairs when nobody was home. There were 9 landings. I counted 3 times. When I told Miss Prowse she wrote to Mum and Mum cried in the kitchen and said I mustnt ever count the stairs again.*

Ada set the journal down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be sleeping.

Their own hands were shaking. Not with fear—not yet—but with recognition, the vertigo of seeing a childhood reorganize itself around new load-bearing walls. Because they remembered Miss Prowse now. They remembered Mum crying in the kitchen, and Dad's voice low through the door, saying *he's a sensitive boy, Ilse says they're often sensitive at that age,* and Ada had filed all of it away under *grown-up things* and never once, not in thirty years, taken it out and looked at it.

*Ilse.* Even then. Reverend Marsh had been the vicar even then, youngish, watchful, kind in the way of people who are managing you.

Ada got up and walked to the foot of the stairs.

The staircase rose ahead of them in the afternoon gloom: ground floor, landing, first floor. Two floors. Thirteen steps to the turn, nine more after. They had climbed these stairs thousands of times as a child and never once counted, because counting had never occurred to them, because why would it?

*I counted 3 times.*

Ada put their foot on the first step.

The house went quiet. Not silent—quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone in it stops breathing to listen. The radiator's tick ceased. The wind at the window ceased. Even the blood in Ada's ears seemed to pause, courteous, attentive.

They climbed. Thirteen steps to the turn—they counted aloud, voice absurdly loud in the hush, and their voice sounded wrong on the stairs, flattened, as if the house were absorbing the edges of it. Landing. Nine more steps. First floor.

One flight. One landing. Two floors, as there had always been.

Ada stood on the first-floor landing with their heart slamming and laughed—a single cracked note of relief—and turned to go back down.

The stairs behind them were longer.

Not much longer. Not impossibly longer. Just—more steps than they'd climbed up, five or six extra, shadowed where the light from the kitchen didn't reach, descending at precisely the same angle into precisely the same hall. Ada blinked. Stood perfectly still. Counted what they could see: fourteen steps visible below the turn, and the turn itself sitting lower than it should, closer to the ground floor, as though the whole staircase had stretched while their back was turned.

*There were 9 landings.*

"No," Ada said aloud, and their voice came back to them immediately, warm and normal, and the stairs were thirteen steps again, and the hall beyond was the hall, and the kettle they'd left on the hob was ticking as it cooled, and everything was exactly, blessedly ordinary.

Except that the ticking was coming from upstairs.

Ada stood at the bottom of the stairs for a long time, holding the blue journal against their chest like a shield, listening to the small domestic sound of a cooling kettle issue from the empty floor above them. Rational explanations presented themselves in order of desperation: echo, plumbing, memory, madness. They examined each and found, beneath all of them, the same foundation they'd been building on since they were six years old—the foundation their parents had poured for them, pour by loving pour: *pipes, birds, settling beams, sensitive boy, don't count the stairs.*

The architecture of denial. Ada saw it suddenly, whole, the way you see a building's skeleton in a fire: every beam named, every joint mortared with a parent's frightened kindness, every load-bearing wall inscribed with the same instruction—*do not look up, do not count, do not sing.* It had held for thirty years. It had held right up until a headstone with their own name on it, and a bell that rang with no bell, and a brother given like a tithe.

And now Emeka was dead, and the house was counting, and somewhere above them—on a floor that did not exist, up a stairwell that grew when unobserved—something patient had finished checking off names and was waiting, politely, for the last Okafor to come up.

Ada did not go up. They took the journal, and their coat, and Emeka's spare keys, and walked out into the grey afternoon, and did not stop walking until they reached the churchyard, where the fresh earth of the grave lay dark under a sky the color of dishwater.

They stood over the headstone a long moment. *ADA OKAFOR. 1987–2012.* Twelve years dead, according to the stone. Twelve years of explanations, according to the living.

"Frost," said a voice behind them.

Ada turned. Tovi Okonkwo-Bell stood at the lychgate with a camera bag and the fierce, sleepless look of someone who had been waiting days for this conversation—though that meeting, properly, came later, and Ada did not know them yet. What Ada saw was a teenager watching them with an expression no sixteen-year-old should have: pity braided with dread.

"Sorry?" Ada said.

"On your windows, last night." The kid's breath fogged. "You had frost on the *inside*. I know because I've been photographing your house since Tuesday." A swallow. "It spells something."

The churchyard was utterly still. No birds. No wind. Just the two of them and the dark earth and the name on the stone, and somewhere far off—or not far off at all, or nowhere, which was worse—the memory of a tune that always stopped at eight.

"NINTH," Tovi said. "It spelled NINTH."

* * *

That night Ada locked every door, stacked the blue journal on the kitchen table beside Emeka's hymnals, and sat facing the stairs with the lights on until dawn, telling themselves the story they had told all their life: pipes, birds, beams, grief, imagination.

At 3:09, the sound began—not ringing, not yet, not tonight; tonight only a hum, rising up through the bones of the house, eight notes climbing and then pausing, pausing, waiting, with the terrible courtesy of a thing that has all the time in the world and knows, now, that its guest has finally read the letter.

On the ninth beat, the silence where the note should have been, Ada heard—clearly, unmistakably, in a voice like their own reflected in dark water—their name.
