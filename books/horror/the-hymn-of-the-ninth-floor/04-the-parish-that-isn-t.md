## 4. The Parish That Isn't

The church stood at the end of the high street the way a held breath stands at the end of a sentence.

Ada had walked past it twice already since arriving in Hollowmere—once on the way from the bus stop with their suitcase rattling over cobbles, and once at dusk, when its windows glowed amber and someone inside was moving between them, slow as a wick burning down. Both times Ada had looked away. There had been too much else to look at: the wrong grave, the wrong name, the house on Ninth Street with its patient dark windows. A person could only carry so much strangeness in one day before their arms gave out.

But grief, Ada was learning, did not permit deferral. It wanted an accounting. And Emeka's funeral had been held here, twelve years ago—or so the vicar's letter claimed, the one that had summoned Ada back with its careful, apologetic phrasing: *a discrepancy has come to light regarding the parish records.* The letter had not said what discrepancy. The letter had not needed to. Ada already knew which name sat where it should not.

So on the third morning, under a sky the color of dishwater, Ada Okafor crossed the lychgate of St. Brannoc's and let the door swing shut behind them with a sound like a book closing.

* * *

Inside, the cold was different from the cold outside. Outside it had been the ordinary damp chill of an English autumn, indifferent and general. Inside, the cold had texture. It lay against Ada's skin like a hand laid flat, checking for fever.

The nave stretched ahead, low-beamed and candlelit, smelling of beeswax and old stone and something faintly sweeter underneath—cut flowers, perhaps, or the memory of cut flowers kept alive past their season. Morning light came through the east window in slanted panels, laying red and blue across the flagstones like cards dealt face down. The pews were dark oak, worn smooth along their edges by two centuries of shoulders and hands. Everything about the church said *old, loved, continuous.*

And yet.

Ada could not have said, at first, what was wrong. The feeling arrived before its reason, the way a change in pressure arrives before weather. They stood in the aisle and let their eyes travel the room the way a tongue travels a mouth after dental work, probing for the gap.

There. Along the north wall, where the pews ran in two long ranks toward the chancel steps—the rows were numbered. Small brass plates, green with age, screwed to the end of each pew. Ada counted without meaning to, the way you count stairs in the dark.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight.

Ten.

Ada counted again, walking now, their footsteps loud in the hush. Eight. Ten. Eleven. Twelve. The brass plate for nine was simply not there. But the wood remembered it. On the end panel of the eighth pew, where the ninth plate should have been screwed, there was a paler rectangle in the varnish, sharp-edged, like a photograph left too long on a windowsill. Someone had removed the number and then sanded the wood and re-varnished it, carefully, recently enough that the patch still caught the light differently. Decades ago, maybe. Or last year. Time in Hollowmere seemed to move like water in a locked cellar—present everywhere, going nowhere.

A pew had been removed. Not just a number—a whole pew. Ada looked at the spacing. Between the eighth row and the tenth there was a gap wide enough for another bench, and the flagstones in the gap were cleaner than their neighbors, less scuffed, as if nothing had ever been permitted to stand there. As if the floor itself had been instructed to forget.

"Can I help you?"

Ada turned. A woman stood near the font—a tall, spare woman in a grey cardigan, her white hair pinned back so tightly it drew small crow's-feet at her temples. She held a brass polishing cloth in one hand and a candlestick in the other, and she held both very still, the way people hold things when they are using them as an excuse not to do something else.

"You must be the Okafor sibling," she said, before Ada could answer. "I'm Ilse Marsh. Vicar here." Her voice was pleasant, measured, the voice of a woman who read lessons aloud and had learned to make every sentence sound like it had been rehearsed once and then set free. "I wrote to you."

"You wrote that there'd been a discrepancy." Ada heard their own voice come out flat, and did not soften it. "In the records."

"Yes." Reverend Marsh set the candlestick down on the font ledge with a small deliberate click. "I wonder if we might sit."

"I'd rather stand." Ada turned back to the pews. "Why is there no pew nine?"

Something moved behind the vicar's face—not a flinch exactly, but the shadow a flinch would cast. "Old reordering," she said. "Nineteen-seventies, I believe. Many churches rearranged their seating then."

"They didn't remove the numbers from the other pews when they reordered."

"No," Marsh agreed mildly. "They didn't."

Ada walked on. At the front of the nave, set into the wall between the chancel arch and the north transept, hung the stations of the cross—small carved reliefs in dark wood, each with a Roman numeral beneath. I. II. III. IV. V. VI. VII. VIII. Then a gap in the sequence of carvings, a stretch of blank plaster slightly less faded than the plaster around it, and then X. XI. XII. XIII. XIV.

Nine stations of the cross. Fourteen were traditional. St. Brannoc's had thirteen, and the thirteenth had been renumbered by hand—Ada could see the older numeral ghosting beneath the newer paint, a ten that had become a nine, an eleven that had become a ten, all the way down the wall like a queue of people shuffling forward to fill a vacancy no one would name.

Someone had razored Christ out of the garden. Someone had decided that the meeting between the risen Lord and Mary Magdalene was one scene too many, and had sawn it off the wall, and had repainted every subsequent number, and had expected—had *required*—that no one ever count.

Ada counted. Of course they counted. Emeka had been the counter in the family; he'd counted stair treads and ceiling tiles and the seconds between lightning and thunder, and some of that habit had lived in Ada too, twin-deep, shared blood carrying shared compulsions. Emeka would have counted. Emeka probably *had* counted, once, standing in this same nave—and look where counting had gotten him.

"The hymnals next," Ada said quietly. It wasn't a question, but Marsh answered it anyway.

"In the cupboard at the back," she said. "If you must."

* * *

The hymnals were stacked in a wooden cupboard by the west door, maroon covers gone velvet-soft with handling. Ada took one at random and opened it to the index. The pages were thin, onionskin-fine, and the numbering ran 1 through 8, then a single glued stub where page 9 had been, then 10 onward, clean and unbroken to the end.

Every hymnal. Ada checked six of them, then stopped checking, because the result was not in doubt. Page nine had been excised with a blade—razored out close to the spine, leaving a stub smooth as a scar. Whoever had done it had been thorough and unhurried. Whoever had done it had done it a very long time ago; the glue of the stubs had yellowed to the color of weak tea.

"What was on page nine?" Ada asked. Their voice sounded strange to them, small in the stone quiet.

Marsh had followed them to the cupboard. She stood with her hands folded, the polishing cloth draped over her wrist like a falconer's glove. "I don't know," she said. "It was before my time."

"But you know it was removed."

"I know that the parish made decisions in 1911 that have been maintained ever since." Marsh's eyes came up and settled on Ada's face, and Ada felt the weight of that gaze like a physical thing—too steady, too attentive, the gaze of someone watching a gauge. "Decisions made in fear are often maintained longest. Fear is very good at dressing itself up as tradition."

"And the bell?" Ada asked. "The one that rang last night?"

The cloth tightened in the vicar's grip. For three full seconds, Ilse Marsh said nothing, and in those three seconds the church seemed to lean in around them—all that old wood and older stone gathering close to hear what would be said next.

"There is no bell at St. Brannoc's," she said at last. "There hasn't been since 1911. It was melted down. The frame is still in the tower, empty. You're welcome to climb up and see for yourself, Mr.—Ms.—Okafor." A pause, delicate as a hairline crack. "Forgive me. Our records are unclear on certain particulars."

"My brother knew," Ada said. "Emeka. He knew what was on page nine. He knew why there's no pew nine. He grew up here, Reverend. Children notice gaps. Children count."

"Children sing," Marsh said.

The words dropped into the silence between them and did not echo, and somehow that was worse than an echo. Ada stared at her. The vicar's face had gone smooth again, composed, pastoral—but her hands, folded at her waist, had gone white at the knuckles, and her thumb was pressing hard against her own ring finger, pressing and pressing, as if testing whether she still had a pulse.

"I beg your pardon?"

"It's nothing. A turn of phrase." She unfolded her hands with visible effort. "Mr. Okafor. May I speak plainly? Just for a moment, and then never again, if you prefer."

"Please."

"Hollowmere is a village that has survived by subtraction." Marsh's voice stayed level, but something underneath it trembled, a note held too long. "We subtracted the bell. We subtracted the verse. We subtracted the pew, the station, the page. Every generation subtracts a little more, and tells itself the building is lighter for it. Easier to carry." Her eyes flicked, briefly, toward the tower door in the corner of the transept—a plain oak door with an iron ring handle, painted over so many times it sat proud of its frame like a healed wound. "You've come home to bury your brother. Let him be buried. Grieve him, and go. Some absences are load-bearing, Mr. Okafor. Pull one out and you find out what the whole roof was resting on."

"That's not how absence works," Ada said. "Absence isn't structural. Absence is a hole shaped like whatever used to be there. And I've seen the shape of this one." They touched the hymnal stub, the smooth razored scar of it. "It's shaped like my family."

For the first time, Reverend Ilse Marsh looked afraid—not of Ada, Ada realized, but *for* them, which was a different thing entirely, and a more terrible one. She reached out as if to touch Ada's sleeve, thought better of it, and withdrew her hand.

"The service for Emeka will be Thursday," she said. "Eleven o'clock. His plot is beside—" she hesitated over the word the way a foot hesitates over a rotten stair— "beside yours."

* * *

Ada did not leave immediately. They told themselves it was to look at the memorial plaques on the south wall, and partly it was—names and dates going back three centuries, and among them, here and there, small anomalies that snagged like burrs: a boy of eleven *lost upon the fell*, 1934; a girl of nine *taken by the river*, 1958; a whole family line that simply stopped mid-century, its last entry a child's name with no date of death at all, only a dash, as if even the stone had declined to finish the sentence.

But really Ada stayed because of the tower door.

It stood in the corner of the north transept, oak and iron ring, and it was locked—of course it was locked—but that wasn't what held Ada there. What held them was the sound. Standing close to the door, Ada could hear the tower breathing. Not wind; the day was still. Something slower than wind, deeper, a vast soft oscillation like the sound of the sea heard through a wall, or like the resonance of a struck bell long after the striking, sustained past all physics, fading and renewing, fading and renewing.

Ada pressed their palm flat against the oak. The wood was warm. In a church that cold, in a corner that never saw sun, the door was warm as a sleeping animal.

And beneath the breathing, so faint it might have been their own pulse conducted through their hand into the timber, Ada heard—almost heard—voices. High voices. Children's voices. Not singing, not yet. Holding a note. Holding it the way the children in the photograph Tovi Okonkwo-Bell would later show them held their breaths, mid-hymn, mid-century, mid-air.

Ada snatched their hand back.

Across the nave, Reverend Marsh had resumed her polishing, but she was not looking at the candlestick. She was looking at Ada, and she was not pretending otherwise anymore, and her expression was the expression of a woman watching someone she had warned walk to the edge of ice and put their weight on it.

"Thursday," she called softly. "Eleven o'clock. Come to say goodbye, Mr. Okafor. Only that."

Ada walked back up the aisle, past the gap where pew nine should have been, past the blank plaster where the ninth station had been sawn away, past the cupboard full of books each missing its ninth page. At the west door they paused, hand on the iron latch, and looked back once.

The morning light had shifted. It fell now directly across the clean flagstones in the space of the missing pew, and in that light Ada could see what the cleaner stone had hidden: marks, faint and old, scored into the surface by small knees and small shoes. Nine sets of them, arranged in a crescent, facing the tower.

As if something had once gathered there. As if something still did, every night at 3:09, in a church that had subtracted everything it could and called the subtraction safety.

Ada pushed open the door and stepped out into the grey morning, and behind them, faint through the closing door, from the top of a tower that held no bell—

—one toll.
