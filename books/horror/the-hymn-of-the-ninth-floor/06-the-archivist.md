## 6. The Archivist

The house on Ninth Street had a smell Ada had forgotten they knew: cold plaster and old smoke and something underneath both, faintly sweet, like apples rotting in an orchard no one harvested anymore. They stood in the doorway of what had been Emeka's room for three days now, not entering, before giving up and walking back down the stairs to the kitchen, where the phone lay face-down on the table like something dead.

Tovi Okonkwo-Bell had messaged them at 2:14 in the morning. Not a friend request—Ada didn't have friends here, hadn't since they were eleven—but a plain text from a number with a Hollowmere prefix:

*you're asking about the bell. don't ask ilse marsh. come to me. the old bakery on weaver's lane, tomorrow, after four. bring nothing.*

Ada had stared at it for a long time. The frost word NINTH still ghosted through their mind every time they blinked. *Bring nothing* was either the paranoia of a teenager or the hard-won wisdom of someone who understood that in Hollowmere, everything you carried could be read.

At ten past four, Ada pushed open the door of the old bakery. The ovens had been cold for thirty years; the shopfront was boarded; but the side door stood ajar, and warm light spilled out onto the cobbles like something spilled.

Inside, the bakery's back room had been transformed. Cables ran along the skirting boards in neat black rivers. Three monitors glowed on a trestle table. Shelves of biscuit tins lined one wall, each labeled in careful handwriting: *1911–1930*, *1931–1968*, *LEDGER SCANS (DO NOT SHOW MARSH)*. And in the middle of it all sat a sixteen-year-old in an enormous cardigan, hair cropped close, fingers already moving across a keyboard before Ada had fully closed the door.

"You came." Tovi didn't look up. "I gave it sixty-forty odds."

"Against me coming?"

"Against you being sane enough to come. Most people who see the frost stop asking questions. They buy a train ticket instead." Tovi finally turned, and their eyes were quick and dark and utterly without fear, which was somehow more unsettling than fear would have been. "You're not leaving. I checked the station camera feed. You haven't bought a ticket. So you're either trapped or committed, and either way—" they gestured at the empty chair beside them, "—sit down. We should talk about 1911."

* * *

Ada sat.

The chair was wooden and cold and it seemed important, suddenly, to ground themselves in its coldness, in the ache of it, because everything else in this village had begun to feel provisional. "How did you know I was asking about the bell?"

"Because everyone asks about the bell eventually, and nobody asks out loud." Tovi pulled up a file. "Because your brother died wrong—I've seen the coroner's paperwork, or rather the absence of it, which is worse—and because Reverend Marsh has been in the parish office until midnight every night this week, which she only does when a tithe is due or disputed." They said *tithe* the way other people said *weather*. Flat. Practiced. "And because," Tovi added, quieter, "your name is in the ledger too. Dated next month. I found it Tuesday."

The room went very still. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe ticked—pipes ticked, Ada told themselves, pipes had always ticked—and outside, a jackdaw argued with the silence of Weaver's Lane.

"Show me," Ada said.

Tovi hesitated. It was the first hesitation Ada had seen in them, and it lasted perhaps two seconds, but in those two seconds the teenager looked every one of their sixteen years, and younger. Then they clicked the file open.

It was a scan of a parish council minute book, the handwriting copperplate and confident. The page was headed *BELL FUND — SUBSCRIPTIONS AND OBLIGATIONS*, and beneath it ran a column of names. Some were faded almost to nothing. Others were sharp as if written yesterday. Ada read down the list—Harrowgate, Voss, Bell, Marsh, Ashdown, Crane—generations braided together, and then, near the bottom, in ink that looked wet:

*Emeka Okafor — received.*

And below that, with a date Ada recognized as three weeks from now:

*Ada Okafor — expected.*

* * *

Tovi watched the stranger read their own name in the ledger and thought: *there it is.* The look on Ada Okafor's face was one Tovi had catalogued before, in photographs, in the faces of descendants who'd come sniffing around the archive and then left town fast—the moment when a person understands that Hollowmere is not a place where things happen but a place where things are *arranged*.

Most people closed the laptop at that point. Metaphorically. This one leaned closer.

"The ink isn't wet," Ada said. Their voice was steady, which impressed Tovi more than screaming would have. "That's a trick of the scan resolution."

"It's wet," Tovi said. "Or it behaves wet. Every time someone new opens the file, the last entry looks freshly written. I've tested it. Different monitors, printed copies, microfilm I made myself from the original in the council safe. Same effect." They shrugged. "The ledger wants to be legible. That's the part people don't get about this village. It's not hiding anything. It's *presenting*."

"Presenting to whom?"

"To whoever's next." Tovi let that sit for a moment, then softened—not much, but some; they were learning, slowly, that other people's terror needed handling. "Look. I'm not going to pretend I'm calm about this. I've been building this archive since I was eleven because my gran kept saying our family owed something and never said what, and I thought if I could just *see the whole shape of it* it would stop being scary. It didn't stop being scary. But it stopped being *vague*, and vague was the worst part." They spun the monitor toward a different folder. "So. You want to know why your name is there. You want to know why Emeka's name got *received*. And I think—I think you already know it starts with the choir. Everyone in this village knows it starts with the choir, and everyone pretends they don't. But you're not from-here-anymore, so I can show you what nobody shows anybody."

Ada's throat worked. "Show me."

* * *

The photograph loaded in sections, top to bottom, like a body assembling itself out of grey water.

Nine children on the steps of St. Brannoc's, arranged in two rows—four seated on the stone, five standing behind, arms linked in the stiff way children hold each other when an adult has told them to. All nine wore white surplices gone yellow with age. All nine were squinting into a sun that had set a century ago. Behind them, blurred by long exposure, the church door stood open onto darkness.

The caption, typed by Tovi in small grey letters, read: *St. Brannoc's choir, May 1911. Nine days before the ninth of June.*

"Names?" Ada asked.

"I'll get there." Tovi's voice had changed—lower, slower, the voice of someone approaching a thing they'd circled for years. "First look at the faces. Really look. Take your time."

Ada took their time.

The boy seated at the far left had a cowlick and a chipped tooth and an expression of barely suppressed laughter. The girl next to him held her hands folded like a small disapproving duchess. The tallest boy in the back row had his chin lifted, defiant, as though he already knew what was coming and had decided to meet it standing up. Ada moved down the row, face to face, letting each one arrive—

—and stopped.

The girl seated second from the right, hands folded, chin tilted, was Ada. Not resembled. *Was.* The same wide-set eyes. The same scar—no, not a scar yet, but the same small asymmetry at the left eyebrow that Ada had carried since a fall from a wall at age six. The same mouth that went crooked when it refused to smile for a camera. A century dead, and the face was Ada's own, looking back at them across the grey water of the photograph with an expression of patient, terrible recognition.

"That's—" Ada began, and couldn't finish.

"Tovi," Tovi said quietly. "Her name was Tovah Bell. My great-great-aunt. I found her when I was twelve. I cried for a week." They tapped the screen, moving one face along. "And him. Standing, far right."

Ada looked.

Emeka. The same long jaw, the same slight downward turn of the mouth that meant he was concentrating, the same way of holding his head as though listening to music no one else could hear. In the photograph he was maybe ten years old. He had been twenty-four when he died—or when he was *given*, said the voice in Ada's head that was getting harder to silence—and yet the face was unmistakable, twin to twin, generation to generation, as if the photograph had been waiting a hundred years for someone to finally see it clearly.

"What is this," Ada whispered. It wasn't a question. Questions have question marks. This was a wound.

"I don't know," Tovi said. "I've spent four years not knowing, and I've got theories, and none of them survive contact with the evidence. Reincarnation doesn't work—too many overlaps, wrong dates. Bloodline doesn't work—Okafor wasn't even *in* Hollowmere until your parents moved here in the nineties. Genetic memory doesn't work. Coincidence—" they laughed, short and bitter, "—doesn't work. What works, the only thing that works, is that the hymn doesn't take bodies. It takes *shapes*. And it keeps reusing the shapes it likes."

* * *

Outside, the light was going amber and long, and Tovi made tea in an electric kettle balanced on a shelf where bread had once proofed, and the two of them sat in the smell of dust and bergamot and talked until the windows went dark.

Tovi showed Ada everything. Scans of the 1911 inquest—nine verdicts of *misadventure, cause unknown*, signed by a coroner whose signature deteriorated across the page until the final one was barely a scratch. The bill of sale for the bell, melted down in November 1911, sold to a foundry in Leeds that had burned to the ground the following spring. A school register with nine names struck through in red. A letter from the vicar of the time, Reverend Aldous Crane, to the bishop, which Tovi had found sewn into the lining of a donated hymnal:

*We did not teach them the verse. I want that recorded. Whatever they climbed the tower to sing, they brought it with them, or it was brought to them, and I have prayed every night since that it stays up there with them. Melt the bell. Melt the stairs. Salt the stone. I am an old man and I will sign whatever you require of me, only make it stop being audible.*

"They knew," Ada said. "From the beginning. They knew what happened and they buried it under *misadventure*."

"They knew, and then they made a deal with it." Tovi pulled up another document—a page from the same minute book, earlier, the first entry, dated June 1911, nine days after the children vanished. The handwriting here was frantic, pressed so hard the pen had torn the paper. *It rings though there is no bell. It rang at 3:09 this morning and Mrs. Harrowgate heard her son's voice in it, clear as church. We cannot unmake what is above us. We can only feed it so it does not come down. Proposal: a tithe. One name, given freely by the council, at intervals not to exceed—*

The rest was water-damaged into illegibility.

"Given freely," Ada repeated. "But Ilse Marsh told me—" They stopped. Careful. They weren't ready to reveal that conversation, not entirely, not yet. "People say disappearances. Accidents."

"People say accidents because accidents can't be refused." Tovi wrapped both hands around their tea mug. "My gran used the word *tithed* exactly once, when she was dying, and when I asked her what she meant she looked at me like I'd asked her to open a vein. The families don't talk about it. They *maintain* it. There's a rota. I've never seen the rota itself but I've reconstructed the pattern—every eleven years, give or take, someone goes. Always from the founding lines, or married into them. Until your brother." They looked up. "Your family isn't a founding line, Ada. Your parents rented the house on Ninth Street. Which means either the rules changed, or somebody broke them, or—" Tovi paused, choosing the words with visible care, "—or the bell chose. And if the bell can choose, then the deal isn't a deal. It's just hunger with paperwork."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Down the lane, a dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

"Why show me all this?" Ada asked. "You don't know me. You could send me away. You could pretend you never wrote that message."

Tovi was quiet for a while. When they answered, they didn't quite meet Ada's eyes, and for the first time they looked young—not frightened-young, but lonely-young, the particular loneliness of a child who has known a secret too heavy to share with anyone their own age.

"Because I've been the only person in this village who knows for five years," they said. "Five years of finding things I can't tell anyone. Five years of watching the rota families at the shop and knowing what their great-grandparents signed. Five years of my own family name in that ledger, going back to 1911, Tovah Bell with her folded hands." They swallowed. "And then you walked into the funeral on Tuesday and I watched the whole congregation look at you like you were a debt arriving, and I thought: *they know. They're the only other person in Hollowmere who knows, and they don't even know it yet.*" Finally they looked up, and their eyes were fierce and wet and absolutely certain. "I'm not showing you this out of charity. I'm showing you because I need an ally, and you need one more, and neither of us survives this alone. That's the whole pitch. Alliance. Shared obsession. Terrible company at 3:09 in the morning."

Ada laughed—it escaped before they could stop it, cracked and rusty, the first laugh since the phone call about Emeka's body. It startled them both. And in the startling, something shifted, some load-bearing wall inside them settling into a truer position. Grief was still there. Terror was still there. But threaded through them both now was something warmer and more dangerous: *purpose, with a witness.*

"Alliance," Ada said, and put out their hand, formal and absurd over the tea mugs, and Tovi shook it with grave ceremony, and that was that.

* * *

They worked until nearly midnight. Tovi taught Ada the archive's architecture—the naming conventions, the dead-man switches, the printed backups hidden in three locations Tovi declined to specify ("in case you're ever followed; plausible deniability cuts both ways"). Ada told Tovi about the frost on the glass, the nine tolls, the journal in Emeka's handwriting that wasn't Emeka's. Tovi took notes with the speed of a court stenographer and twice said *say that again, slower*.

It was near the end, packing up, that Tovi froze over the 1911 photograph one last time.

"Wait," they said. "The back row. Look at the tallest one. Chin up."

Ada looked. The defiant boy. "What about him?"

"His surplice collar. There's writing on it. I always assumed it was laundry marking—initials, a number." Tovi zoomed, enhanced, sharpened, the image dissolving into grain and then reassembling, pixel by pixel, into something legible. Their breath went out of them slowly. "It's not initials."

Ada leaned close. The letters were tiny, stitched in dark thread against white linen, in a hand that trembled:

*the ninth verse is not the end of the song*

Below it, smaller still, half-lost in the weave:

*ask the woman who would not sing*

The bakery was silent. The monitors hummed. Somewhere far off—miles off, or inches, impossible to say—a single note hung in the air like frost forming, and faded before either of them could swear they'd heard it.

"Maren Voss," Tovi whispered. "Ninth cottage on Fen Road. She's ninety-one. Half her voice is gone." They looked up at Ada, and the warmth between them had sharpened into something with an edge. "Everyone says she's mad. Everyone says leave her alone."

"Everyone also said the bell was melted down," Ada said, reaching for their coat. "And yet."
