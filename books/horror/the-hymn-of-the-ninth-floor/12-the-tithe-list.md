## 12. The Tithe List

Tovi Okonkwo-Bell had three rules for the archive, and the first one was *never work on it in the house*, because the house was where her mother's voice lived, and her mother's voice said things like *you're wasting your life on dead people's paperwork* and *why can't you be normal* and, once, in the worst week of last winter, *some things are buried for a reason, Tovi, and you'll dig up something you can't put back.*

So she worked in the back room of the library, which was technically a storage closet, technically not hers, and technically off-limits after six p.m., which is why she had a key she'd copied from a key that had been left in a book about canal engineering since 1997. Nobody missed it. Nobody ever came down here. The room smelled of damp cardboard and old glue and the particular sweetness of paper that was quietly becoming soil, and Tovi loved it the way other people loved churches—which, in Hollowmere, was probably the safer devotion.

The parish council minutes were the problem.

Everything else—the census returns, the mine pay-rolls, the school registers, the photographs with their silver-fish faces—everything else had been digitized months ago, sitting on two encrypted drives and a server in Reykjavik that cost her eleven pounds a month and was worth more than the whole village. But the council minutes from 1911 onward were sealed. Not archived-sealed, not dusty-and-forgotten sealed. *Sealed.* A wax-paper wrapper, a red string, a stamp: BY ORDER OF THE PARISH COUNCIL, NOT TO BE OPENED BEFORE [blank]. The blank bothered her more than anything. Not even a date. Just an open mouth where a year should have been.

They lived in a locked cabinet in the vicarage, which meant Ilse Marsh's house, which meant Tovi couldn't get near them without being seen, and Ilse Marsh saw everything. She watched people the way the bell tower watched the rooftops: patiently, from above, without blinking.

But here was the thing about Hollowmere, the thing Tovi had understood at thirteen and never told anyone: the village kept its secrets in paper, and paper got *copied*. Somewhere in a hundred years of clerks and secretaries and bored treasurers, someone had always made a duplicate. A fair copy for the diocese. A summary for the bank. A typescript made by a vicar's daughter learning to type in 1954, practicing on the only interesting document in the house.

Tovi had spent four nights searching the digitized diocesan deposits at the county record office—scanned, indexed, gloriously unguarded—and on the fourth night, at 1:47 in the morning, wrapped in a duvet with a mug of tea gone cold beside her, she found it.

File: STB/PC/1911–1988 (typescript copies). Deposited 1989 by Rev. E. Marsh. Access: open.

*Marsh.* Ilse's father, or grandfather—she'd have to check which. Even the sealing had been done by a Marsh. Even the lock on the secret had the family name on it.

Her hands were shaking so badly she had to retype her password twice.

* * *

The file was enormous. Four hundred scanned pages, the early ones typed on a machine with a bad "e" that printed every letter slightly drunk, the later ones clean laser printouts. Tovi did what she always did: she built the index first. Names, dates, cross-references. It was the part everyone found boring and the part she loved—the moment when chaos agreed to become a list.

It took her twenty minutes to notice the pattern in the headings.

The council met monthly. Most meetings were sewage rates and roof repairs and a decades-long feud about the allotment hedges. But once a year—sometimes twice, in hard years—there was a heading that appeared in no index, in no table of contents, because whoever compiled the official summaries had cut it out like a tumor:

**ITEM 9.**

Always item nine. Never discussed under any other number. And always, without exception, the page following ITEM 9 was shorter than the rest—typed lighter, or photocopied fainter, as if the paper itself had tried to look away.

The first one, June 1912:

*Item 9. The matter of the tithe. Proposed by Councilor Voss, seconded by Councilor Bell, that the arrangement entered into following the regrettable events of October last be continued according to custom. Carried, nine in favor, none against, none abstaining.*

*Tithe.* Tovi sat very still. Outside the storage-closet door, the library's heating pipes ticked and groaned, and she catalogued the sound automatically—pipes, thermal expansion, iron contracting—because that was rule two: *always name the ordinary sounds before they can name themselves.*

She scrolled on. 1913: Item 9, carried nine in favor. 1914: carried nine in favor. Through the war years, through the flu, through the depression when the council could barely afford lamp oil but somehow always, always carried Item 9 unanimously. Nine votes. Every time. Nine families, nine hands raised, nine yeses, decade after decade, like a heartbeat.

And then, tucked between the motions and the minutes, the ledger began.

Not called a ledger. Called, in the clerk's careful hand, *the schedule of customary payments*. But it was a ledger. Names. Dates. And against each name, in a column headed simply **RECEIVED**, a small notation.

Tovi read them the way you read a list of the dead, because that is what it was, whatever the clerk had called it.

*October 1921 — received: Thomas Grieve, aged 34, miner. Cause recorded: shaft collapse, No. 3 pit.*
*March 1929 — received: Edith Grieve, aged 11. Cause recorded: drowned, the mill race.*
*November 1936 — received: Samuel Okonkwo-Bell, aged 40. Cause recorded: heart failure.*

She stopped. Read the name again. *Okonkwo-Bell.* Her surname. Her great-grandfather's name, probably—she'd have to check, she *would* check, but the shape of it was already there, already hers, already threaded into her own wrist and voice. She thought of her grandmother, who never came to church, who crossed herself backwards when she passed St. Brannoc's, who had once told seven-year-old Tovi: *We don't sing in this family. We hum, if we must, but we don't sing.*

*We don't sing in this family.*

Because singing was how it started. Because harmonizing was answering. Because her family had been paying into this thing for a hundred years and had survived by teaching their children silence.

Tovi's tea had gone completely cold. She didn't remember making it. She didn't remember the last hour. The clock on the wall said 3:12 and she flinched at the number before she knew why—three minutes past, safely past, the hour already fed and gone.

She kept reading. She made herself keep reading, because Ada Okafor deserved the whole list, and because fury was better than fear, and the list was turning her cold fear into something with heat in it.

*April 1947 — received: Mary-Anne Voss, aged 16. Cause recorded: pneumonia.*
*September 1953 — received: Peter Halloran, aged 29. Cause recorded: fell from the belfry stair.*

Halloran. She wrote the name down. There were still Hallorans in the next valley; someone might want to know.

Page after page. Decade after decade. Sometimes two years between names, sometimes five, sometimes—in the hungry years, 1968 and 1969, 1988 and 1989—only months. And always the same structure, always the same vote, always nine in favor, none against, none abstaining, until 2003, when the handwriting changed and the vote became eight in favor, one abstaining, and Tovi knew whose hand that abstention belonged to before she checked the attendance roll.

*M. Voss.*

Maren Voss, the woman who refused. She hadn't just refused the tithe. She'd sat in that room, year after year, the only raised-nothing among the raised hands, refusing over and over, a single dissenting note held against a century of unanimous hymn. Tovi pressed her palm flat against the desk and breathed. That was what refusal looked like. Not one dramatic no. A hundred quiet ones, in a room full of neighbors, knowing it changed nothing, doing it anyway.

* * *

She reached the modern pages near dawn. Laser-printed now, the council having finally entered the century, but Item 9 remained, faithful as a scar.

And the ledger continued.

*2011 — received: Daniel Pryce, aged 22. Cause recorded: road traffic accident, the B-road.*
*2016 — received: Susan Anselm-Grieve, aged 45. Cause recorded: house fire.*

Anselm. Father Anselm's wife? His sister? The defrocked priest who drank in the Fox and Hounds and talked in riddles about bells—had he known? Had he sat in that room? No—he'd been clergy, not council. But the name was there, in the column marked RECEIVED, and Tovi felt the whole village rearrange itself around her, every shopkeeper and dog-walker and schoolmother suddenly suspect, every friendly face a possible raised hand.

Then the last pages. The recent ones. The ones dated within her own lifetime.

*2021 — received: Jonah Bell, aged 71. Cause recorded: stroke.*

Jonah Bell. Her grandfather. The one who taught her to code, actually—who'd bought her the first laptop, who'd said *learn everything, little one, knowledge is the only door nobody can lock.* He'd died in his chair on a Tuesday evening, and the doctor had said stroke, natural, his age, and the family had nodded, and the council had minuted it under Item 9, and nine hands had gone up, and somewhere a bell that didn't exist had swallowed him.

Tovi did not cry. She noted, distantly, that she should probably be crying, and that instead she felt something colder and more useful assembling itself in her chest, brick by brick, entry by entry. Her grandfather's death had been *minuted*. It had been *carried*. Someone had seconded it.

She scrolled to the final entries with her jaw set so tight it ached.

*2025 — received: Emeka Okafor, aged 27. Cause recorded: aneurysm.*

There it was. The proof Ada had come home looking for without knowing it. Emeka hadn't simply died; Emeka had been *received*. Entered into the schedule. Carried by nine votes, or eight, or however many hands Ilse Marsh had gathered in the weeks before the funeral. The word *aneurysm* sat there in its tidy column like a receipt, like something stamped PAID.

Ada's twin. Given the way her grandfather was given. And Ada had stood at the graveside watching their own name lowered into the earth, and the earth had been hungry, and the hunger had been *scheduled*.

Tovi's eyes went to the bottom of the page, expecting blankness, expecting the clerk's closing line—*meeting closed, 9:40 p.m.*—

But the page wasn't finished.

There was one more entry. Dated forward. Dated in the future, which should have been impossible, which broke every rule of how minutes worked, except that this wasn't really minutes, was it—it was a menu, an order form, a reservation.

**Next meeting — Item 9. Proposed: that the customary payment be accepted from:**

**Ada Okafor.**

**Date of receipt: [the twelfth of next month].**

Next month. Three weeks. Nineteen days, Tovi counted, nineteen days from tonight, and the date wasn't a guess or a proposal pending—it was written in the confident hand of people who had never once, in a hundred and fourteen years, failed to carry Item 9. They had already voted. Or they would vote, and the voting was a formality, the way a signature is a formality when the contract was signed generations ago. Ada Okafor wasn't a resident of Hollowmere anymore. Ada Okafor was an *entry*. A line in a column. A payment awaiting collection.

The cursor blinked. The heating pipes ticked. Somewhere above her, out in the dark over the rooftops, nothing rang—but Tovi felt the silence of the melted bell pressing down on the village like a held breath, like a choir sucking air before the downbeat.

Cold fear would have frozen her. She could feel it trying—fingers of it around her ribs, the animal urge to close the laptop, copy the key back, never come down to the storage closet again, go home and hum, never sing, stay silent, survive. Her grandmother's whole strategy, laid out for her like an inheritance: *be quiet, be small, be unentered.*

But her grandfather had also left her something. *Learn everything. Knowledge is the only door nobody can lock.*

Well. They had locked this door. Sealed it with wax paper and red string and a hundred years of unanimous votes. And she had just walked through it anyway, and she was not going back through alone, and she was not going back through empty-handed.

Tovi exported the file. All four hundred pages. Then she exported it again, differently, and again, and sent copies to three servers in three countries, hashed and timestamped, so that no fire, no council vote, no *regrettable accident* could unmake what she now held. If they came for the archive—if they came for her—the list would already be loose in the world, multiplying, unkillable. You want to feed it names? Fine. She could play that game. She had a ledger of her own now, and hers ended in *witnesses*.

Then she opened a new message, and stared at the recipient field for a long moment, because this was the part that couldn't be undone either. Once she sent this, Ada Okafor would know—not suspect, not dread, but *know*—that the village had booked them. That the funeral they'd attended had been a delivery confirmation. That somewhere in a minute book, in a neat column, their name was already filed under RECEIVED, pending.

Tovi thought about the photograph in her archive—the 1911 choir, nine children mid-hymn, two faces that belonged to the twins. She thought about Maren Voss raising nothing, year after year, in a room of raised hands.

She typed: *Found the sealed minutes. Found the ledger. Your name is in it. So is my grandfather's. Come to the library tomorrow—I'll leave the side door open. Bring Maren if you can. We need to talk about Item 9 before the twelfth of next month.*

She hesitated over the send button exactly long enough to hear, clearly, in memory, her mother's voice: *you'll dig up something you can't put back.*

"Good," Tovi said aloud, to the empty closet, to the damp cardboard, to the whole buried village. "I don't want to put it back."

She hit send.

Outside, far off across the sleeping roofs, in the exact middle of the silence where a bell used to hang, something that was not a bell gave—one soft, single toll, like a throat clearing, like a clerk dipping a pen.

Nineteen days. And it already knew she'd been reading.
