## 13. Anselm's Last Mass

The bottle had been empty for three days when Anselm decided to stop being a coward.

He noticed it the way he noticed most things now—slowly, and with a kind of distant embarrassment, as though observing the failures of a stranger who happened to share his name. The bottle sat on the windowsill of his rooms above the pub, catching the grey light of a Hollowmere morning that never quite committed to being morning at all. He turned it over in his hands. The glass was warm from the sun, or from his hands, or from something else entirely; in this village he no longer trusted warmth to have an honest origin.

Three days sober. Not by virtue. By accident. The pub's cellar had run dry of anything worth drinking, and the walk to the off-licence in Marrowbridge felt, lately, like crossing open ground under fire. Something in Hollowmere did not want him leaving. Something in Hollowmere did not want him doing much of anything except sitting very still and listening.

And God forgive him—he had been listening.

* * *

Forty years ago, on the day of his ordination, a bishop had laid his hands on Anselm Crowe's head and told him he would be a shepherd. The words had felt enormous then, weighty as stones, and Anselm had wept into his stole like a boy. He had believed, with the whole unbroken surface of his heart, that the Church was a wall built against the dark, and that he would stand on it.

Then came Hollowmere. Then came the parish council meetings where old men spoke in a dialect of silences. Then came the year he found the razored hymnals and asked Reverend Marsh—no, not Marsh, the one before her, old Cotterill—why page nine of every book in St. Brannoc's had been cut out with such care, so carefully that the blades had barely whispered. Cotterill had looked at him with pity, the way you look at a man who has asked about the tide while standing in it.

"You're new," Cotterill had said. "That's all. You're new."

Anselm had not been new for long after that. They had taken the collar from him gently, the way you take a bone from a dog—not because the dog did wrong, but because the bone was never really his. He had stayed anyway. Cowardice is its own kind of vocation. It has hours, and duties, and a uniform: in his case, corduroy and gin.

But the girl Okafor had come back from the dead—the wrong dead, the ledger's dead—and she had stood in the pub three nights ago with her brother's grief still wet on her face, and she had asked him questions no one had asked since 1911, and something in Anselm's chest had shifted like a joist under snow.

She was going to climb those stairs. He could see it in her the way you can see weather coming over a ridge. And half-measures would kill her. Half-measures had already killed Father Anselm Crowe, forty years ago, in everything but name.

So: no more half-measures. There was one full measure left in him, and it was an old one, and it was forbidden, and it was his.

A requiem. Unsanctioned, unannounced, sung alone in a church that had been quietly amputated for a century. A Mass for the dead who were not dead. A Mass for nine children and one man named Emeka Okafor, whose body they had buried but whose voice, Anselm now understood, was still somewhere above the village, held between two notes like a moth between the pages of a book.

If the bell wanted a tithe, let it come and take it from a priest who owed it nothing—and who had, at last, something worth losing.

* * *

He dressed properly for the first time in years. The cassock still fit through the shoulders; grief, at least, had kept him lean. In the mirror behind the pub's door he looked like a photograph of himself, slightly foxed at the edges, and he said aloud—to the room, to the village, to whatever listened through the walls—"Forgive me, Father, for I intend to sin magnificently," and laughed until the laugh turned into something else and he had to sit down on the stairs.

At eleven o'clock at night he let himself into St. Brannoc's with the old key, the one they had forgotten to take from him because taking it would have meant admitting the belfry stairs existed.

The church at night was a held breath. Moonlight came through the east window in slabs of blue and garnet, laying colour across the flagstones like offerings no one had asked for. Anselm walked the aisle slowly, his footsteps ringing on stone, and counted the pews out of habit. Eight rows. Of course eight. He had sat in the ninth row at his first Mass here—he was almost sure of it, though the memory slid away from him like a fish in dark water, and when he tried to hold it, what surfaced instead was the smell of cut grass and a child's voice singing something about a river, and he stopped trying.

The altar was bare. He had brought what he needed in a canvas bag: bread, wine, a stub of beeswax candle, and the one thing he had sworn never to touch again—the old Roman missal, printed before the war, its pages soft as cloth. Page nine was intact. He had checked a hundred times over the years, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Whatever the parish had excised from the hymnals, they had missed the missal, or dared not touch it, or perhaps—this thought came to him coldly, kneeling at the altar rail—they had left it as bait.

He lit the candle. The flame stood up straight and made a small gold country in the darkness, and Anselm knelt inside it and began.

"In nomine Patris, et Filii, et Spiritus Sancti."

His Latin came back the way a language comes back to a drowning man—all of it at once, and better than he'd ever spoken it. The words filled the church and the church took them and gave them back changed, deeper, with harmonics underneath that had no business existing in a building this empty. He noticed it by the second collect: his own voice returning to him a half-second late, doubled, trebled, a congregation of one man made into nine.

He should have stopped then.

He wants it recorded, whoever reads this, whoever remembers—he wants it known that he knew. That was the horror of it, the plain human horror: not deception but consent. He heard the harmony rising under his voice like water rising in a lock, and he kept singing, because the requiem was working, because the names were being said aloud—*Emeka Okafor, Edith Prunty, Wilfred Voss, the nine, the nine, the nine*—and for the first time in a century someone in Hollowmere was saying them over consecrated ground with intent, and the intent itself was a kind of music.

"Requiem aeternam dona eis, Domine—"

The candle flame bent sideways. There was no draught. The flame bent toward the tower, toward the blocked stair, the way a compass needle bends toward iron, and beneath his feet Anselm felt the flagstones begin—very faintly, very politely—to hum.

* * *

Time went strange after midnight. He knew because the moon moved wrong, sliding across the east window like a coin pushed along a table, and the shadows of the pews lengthened and shortened without any agreement among themselves. He sang the Kyrie. He sang the Dies Irae, which was either the bravest or the stupidest thing he had ever done, singing the day of wrath into a church that had been feeding on wrath's interest for a hundred years. His voice held. His voice held better than it had held in decades, and that frightened him more than cracking would have, because he understood, dimly, that it wasn't only his voice anymore. Something was lending him breath. Something was investing in him.

The bell did not ring. That was the worst part. All night, as he sang, the bell did not ring—and yet he could feel it overhead the way you feel a storm front, a pressure, a leaning-in. The melted bronze that hung nowhere and everywhere above Hollowmere was listening. He could feel its attention like frost forming on the inside of his skull.

Near what might have been three o'clock—he had stopped trusting the church clock, whose hands had begun to move like insects—he reached the Libera Me.

"Libera me, Domine, de morte aeterna—" Deliver me, O Lord, from death eternal.

And the church answered.

Not in words. In harmony. A line rose beneath his own, low and patient, a bass note that seemed to come up through the floor, through the foundations, through whatever the church was founded on, and it was singing with him, and it was singing well, and it was singing the way Maren Voss had once warned him things sing here: *never harmonize, never answer.* But he was past answering. He had been answering all night. Every word of the Mass had been an answer, and now the thing below the floor and above the tower had learned the tune, and it was closing around his voice like a hand around a bird—not crushing it, never crushing it, just holding, just keeping, just keeping.

Anselm sang the absolution. He sang it for the nine children whose faces he had seen once in Tovi Okonkwo-Bell's archive, small and solemn in their choir robes, standing on a belfry ladder in 1911 with their mouths open mid-hymn. He sang it for Emeka Okafor, whom he had watched across the pub for years without ever once asking why the young man flinched at bells. He sang it for himself, last and least, the coward in the borrowed cassock, and if there was mercy anywhere in the mathematics of that night, he hoped some fraction of it would find him too.

The candle went out.

The dark that followed was not the absence of light. It was a presence, textured, attentive, and in it Anselm's voice went on singing without him—rose up out of his throat and continued the Libera Me in his own timbre while he stood in the blackness with his mouth closed and his heart hammering, listening to himself sing.

That is how it takes you, he thought, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful. Not with claws. With your own voice, returned to you, improved.

* * *

They found him at dawn—or Ada found him; he would learn that later, piecing together the girl's white face and Tovi's shaking phone-camera and the smell of cold wax. But dawn, for Anselm, arrived differently. Dawn arrived as a greying of the dark, and the dark loosened its grip on his voice the way ice loosens from a branch—not releasing it, but letting it hang, heavy, ready to fall.

He was standing in the center aisle. He did not remember walking there. His legs ached as though he had been standing for a week. His throat—

His throat was the problem. When he tried to speak, what came out was sound, technically. A fraying. Like a rope dragged over stone. He could feel the edges of his voice, could feel where it had been worn through, and beyond those edges was not silence exactly but a waiting, a held place, a pew reserved for him in a church with no ninth row.

He had bought time. He clung to that. Somewhere in the night's terrible arithmetic, the requiem had cost the bell something—a night of collection postponed, a hunger deferred. He didn't know how he knew. He knew the way you know the weight of a thing you've carried. Three days, maybe four. Long enough for a girl with her twin's eyes to find the verse, if the verse could be found. Long enough for a sixteen-year-old archivist to finish reading a century of erasures. Long enough.

It would not be long enough for him. He understood that too, standing in the blue morning light with birds beginning their ordinary, merciful noise outside. The silence was already in him, settling in like a lodger, polite and permanent. By tonight he would hum. By tomorrow he would find himself mouthing harmony to nothing—to the kettle, to the wind, to the space between the pub's floorboards. And one day soon he would open his mouth to say good morning and produce, instead, a perfect third above a melody no one else could hear, and after that he would not be Anselm Crowe anymore. He would be a verse. He would be a fragment. He would be one more voice in the choir the village pretended it had never had.

Half-measures. This was the price of half-measures, paid in the only currency he had left.

* * *

Ada Okafor came to the pub at noon, and Anselm was waiting in the back room with the canvas bag on the table between them, and his voice lasted just long enough.

He had written some of it down during the morning, in a hand that shook: *The verse is split. Nine pieces. You know this. What you don't know is that fragments need vessels, and vessels wear out. Don't waste time grieving me. Grief is how it leads you. It always leads with love.*

But the important things he said aloud, spending his fraying voice like a miser's last coins, each word costing him a visible effort, each word arriving thinner than the last.

"The key." He put it in her palm and folded her fingers over it. Iron, old, warm from his pocket. "Belfry stair. East side, behind the vestry panel. They bricked the lower arch in 'eleven but the stairs go down further than they go up—you'll see. You'll see what I mean." His voice cracked and he pressed on before it could close. "I sang the Mass. Bought you time. Three days. Maybe less, now, after—" he gestured vaguely at his own throat, at the ruin in it, and managed something like a smile, "—after the interest."

"Father Anselm—" Ada started, and he raised a hand.

"Don't." The word came out almost gone, a whisper wearing the ghost of a voice. He looked at her then with everything he had left, forty years of cowardice and one night of courage gathered into a single gaze. "When you climb. When you sing. Sing it *given*. Do you hear me? Everything it has ever taken, it took. It doesn't know how to hold a gift. It will choke on it." His hand found hers and gripped, hard, a priest's grip, a penitent's grip. "Give it your name like it costs you nothing. Even if it costs you everything. Especially then."

He tried to say more. He tried to say *forgive me*, or *thank you*, or simply the old blessing, the one from his ordination, the one the bishop's hands had pressed into his scalp like a seal. But when he opened his mouth, what emerged was not words.

It was harmony. A single, clear, sustained note, pitched a perfect fifth above nothing, beautiful and terrible, filling the back room of the pub while Ada stared at him and the afternoon light went thin. His own voice, returned to him, improved, singing with something that was not in the room.

It lasted four seconds. Then it guttered out like the candle had, and Anselm Crowe sat down heavily on the bench, and touched his throat, and looked at Ada with eyes that were already learning to be elsewhere.

From the direction of the church—half a mile away, through stone walls, through the ordinary noise of the village waking—one clear bell toll rolled across Hollowmere.

The first of nine.

Ada looked at the key in her hand. Anselm looked at the window, where the light was already failing, though it was barely past noon.

Three days, he had promised her. Watching her face, watching her understand, he spent the last of his voice on a single word, and even that came out frayed, half-gone, a rope's end:

"Hurry."
