## 14. Harmonize

The nights had a shape now. Ada had learned to read them the way sailors read weather: the particular stillness that came before the toll, the way the frost climbed the glass in patterns too deliberate for weather, the way the house on Ninth Street seemed to lean toward the sound like a flower toward a light that would kill it.

Eleven nights after the funeral, the bell rang eight times.

They counted in the dark, lips moving, hands fisted in the blanket. Eight. One toll closer than last night. The silence afterward was worse than the ringing—it had texture, a held breath, the sense of something patient counting alongside them.

Ada got up. They had stopped pretending they wouldn't.

The kitchen was cold in the way that had nothing to do with the season. The radiator ticked and gave nothing. Ada put the kettle on anyway, because ritual was a kind of armor, and stood at the window watching the frost write itself on the inside of the pane. It had stopped spelling NINTH. Lately the letters came slower, more ornate, like a hand practicing its penmanship. Tonight the frost formed a single line of musical notation, five ruled lines drawn in ice, and a note hanging above them like a bird deciding whether to land.

That was when they heard Emeka's voice.

Not the bell. Not the toll. The voice—clear, warm, a little hoarse the way it got when he'd been laughing, the way it had sounded through the wall of their shared bedroom when they were children and he sang to himself because silence made him itch. He was humming. Then he wasn't humming. He was singing words, and the words were in the language of their mother's lullabies, the Igbo she'd softened for their ears, and the melody was the first verse of the hymn—the one Maren had taught them, the one Ada had written out in Emeka's notebook in their careful hand.

*Ada,* the voice said, between phrases, conversational, intimate, the way he'd said their name a thousand times across a thousand shared rooms. *Ada, I'm cold. It's cold here and I can't remember how the second part goes. You always remembered. You were always the one who remembered.*

The kettle screamed. Ada didn't hear it.

They were at the door before they knew they'd moved. Their hand was on the latch. Outside, the night had that particular quality it took on at 3:09—not dark but expectant, the streetlights burning with a yellow that seemed to come from below the ground rather than above the sky. And the voice was out there, in the garden, at the gate, on the road, moving closer with every phrase, singing the first verse in Emeka's exact register, and the terrible thing—the thing Ada would carry for the rest of their life, however long that proved to be—was that it was a good imitation. It was a *perfect* imitation. It got the little crack in his voice on the high phrase, the one he'd had since he was twelve and never grew out of, and no machine could have known about that crack. No machine. Only something that had him. Only something wearing him.

*You always remembered,* the voice said, and now it was closer, and now it was sad, and sadness was the worst weapon it could have chosen because Ada had spent twelve years learning the exact weight of Emeka's sadness, the specific gravity of it, the way it sat in his chest like water. *You were the one who remembered, and now I'm the one who's forgotten, and I'm so cold, Ada. Just the second line. Just sing the second line and I can find my way back.*

Ada's mouth opened.

They felt the hymn rising in them like water finding a crack in a dam—the melody assembling itself out of muscle memory, out of childhood, out of every night their mother had sung them down to sleep. The first note climbed their throat. They could feel where it would go, the second line opening beneath it like a stair in the dark, and some part of them, some cold and ancient part that had been awake since the wrong grave, was screaming that the second line was a door and doors in Hollowmere only opened one way—

The door was wrenched open from the outside.

Maren Voss came through it like weather. Ninety-one years old, four-foot-eleven in her good boots, and she hit Ada at the waist with the full momentum of a woman who had once walked out of a church and kept walking for sixty years. They went down together onto the cold flagstones of the hall. Ada's note died unborn in their throat. And Maren—half-voiced Maren, whose remaining hearing was the last sense she trusted—put her ruined mouth against Ada's ear and made a sound.

It was not a scream. Ada would understand later that it could not have been a scream, because a scream would have been an answer, would have been a kind of harmony, and Maren Voss had spent six decades learning the difference between every sound in the world and the ones that must never, ever be made. What she made was a hiss, a long unbroken exhalation shaped into nothing, a sound with no pitch at all, and she held it against Ada's ear like a poultice, like a hand pressed on a wound, until the voice in the garden faltered.

Outside, Emeka's voice stopped singing.

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed on the windows. Frost bloomed up the glass in a rush, all at once, and in the frost, for the first time, Ada saw not letters but a face—a child's face, small and indistinct, pressed against the other side of the pane, looking in with an expression that was not menace. That was the horror of it. It was *longing*. It was the face of someone watching a lit window from the cold.

Then the frost ran, all of it at once, like breath wiped from a mirror, and the night was only a night.

* * *

Maren did not let go of Ada's arm for an hour.

They sat in the kitchen with the kettle finally poured, two cups of tea going cold between them, and the old woman's grip on Ada's wrist never slackened—not pain, exactly, but insistence, the grip of someone holding a rope over a drop. Her breath still came hard. At ninety-one, what she had done at the door should have killed her, and Ada said so, and Maren shook her head with the economy of a woman who did not spend motion.

Then she reached across the table and took the notebook—the one that had been Emeka's, the one Ada carried now everywhere, its pages soft as cloth from handling—and she turned to a blank page. Her hand, when she wrote, was beautiful. Schoolroom copperplate, learned in a village school in 1939, uncorroded by a century.

*What did it say to you?*

Ada told her. All of it. The cold. The crack in the voice. *You always remembered.* By the end their own voice had gone strange, flattened, a voice reading someone else's transcript.

Maren read what they'd said back, though she'd watched their lips the whole time—she read faces the way other people read print, a skill her stolen half-voice had forced on her. Then she wrote for a long time. Her pen moved slowly, not from age but from care, the care of someone setting down something she had hoped never to have to explain.

*It always leads with love. That's how it gets the second line.*

Ada stared at the sentence. The fire ticked. Somewhere down the street a dog began to bark and thought better of it.

*The first verse is a lure,* Maren wrote. *It has always been a lure. In 1911 the children thought they were singing a hymn. They were singing an invitation, and the bell was the door, and the ninth verse was the room behind it. The thing in the bell cannot take a whole voice. It can only take what answers it. So it learns what you love, and it comes back wearing it, and it asks you for one line. One line is all it needs. A second line is a harmony, and a harmony is a hand reaching through a door.*

*It used my brother,* Ada said. Their voice cracked on *brother*, and they hated the crack, because the crack was exactly what the thing in the garden had imitated, and now they could not tell which crack was real.

Maren's face did something complicated. Pity, and fury, and an old grief that had had sixty years to fossilize and still, at its center, was wet. She wrote:

*It used my sister. 1953. It sang to me in her voice for three nights before I understood. On the fourth night I almost answered. My father held a pillow over my head rather than let me sing. He was not a cruel man. It was the only way he could think to keep my mouth shut, and it worked, and it cost me half my voice anyway—the interest, they called it, the families called it. The bell takes a tithe even from those who refuse. Refusal is not free. Nothing in Hollowmere is free. That is the whole of the town, Ada. That has always been the whole of the town.*

Ada looked at her—at the ruined mouth, at the half-voice that came out flattened and toneless, a voice with the music boiled out of it. And they understood, with a lurch, what the tithe had actually been. Not punishment. *De-tuning.* The families had not silenced Maren as cruelty. They had silenced her as *protection*—a voice that could not carry melody could not harmonize, could not answer, could never be lured. They had broken her instrument so the bell could not play her. And she had lived sixty years in the wreckage of it, half-deaf to the world and fully deaf to the hymn, and she had survived.

*You lost your hearing just now,* Ada said. *At the door. Didn't you.*

Maren's hand paused above the page.

*Tell me the truth,* Ada said. *You've earned my truth. I'll earn yours.*

The old woman smiled, and it was the worst smile Ada had ever seen, because it was brave.

*It was worth it,* she wrote. *I heard it singing in your voice. Did you know that? While I was dragging you down, it switched. It stopped being Emeka and started being you. It was singing a duet with itself—your voice and his, in harmony, the way twins sing. It wanted me to hear what I was pulling you away from. It wanted me to understand what a beautiful thing it was making.*

She stopped. Her hand was shaking now, not from age.

*I couldn't hear anything after that,* she wrote. *The ringing. It went through my head like a nail. But I could feel your heartbeat in your wrist, and I could feel it trying to follow the rhythm, and so I held on. That's all I did. I held on to the one part of you it hadn't learned yet.*

Ada put their free hand over hers. For a long time neither of them moved, and the kettle cooled, and the frost stayed off the windows, and the night outside was merely dark.

* * *

Tovi arrived at seven, on their bicycle, before the sun had properly committed to rising, and took in the scene—Ada gray-faced, Maren writing furiously, the notebook open between them—and did not ask a single question. They simply made more tea, strong enough to stand a spoon in, and sat, and waited. Sixteen years old and they already had the archivist's greatest virtue: they knew when to be silent and let the record accumulate.

When Maren's account was written out—she had filled six pages, and her copperplate was deteriorating, and she had begun to press so hard the pen tore in places—Tovi read every word twice. Then they looked up, and their face had gone the color of paper.

*It's learning,* they said. *That's what this means. It's not just luring anymore. It's rehearsing.* They pulled out their phone, thumbs moving fast. *I've been going through the 1911 material again, the coroner's fragments, the vicar's letters. There's a line everyone treats as a madman's scribble. The last vicar before the melting wrote that the bell didn't take the children—it was* learning *them. That it sang back at them through the night, verse by verse, getting better each time. That by the third night it sang better than they did. That's why they melted it. Not to end the song. To end the* choir. *The bell was practicing. The children were its rehearsal.*

*Rehearsing for what?* Ada asked, though they already knew. They had known since the garden. They had felt the answer in their own open mouth.

Tovi looked at them with a pity that was almost unbearable in someone so young.

*For you,* they said. *For the ninth verse. It can't sing the ninth verse alone—the children are the lock, Maren said, each of them carries a fragment. But if it can get a living voice to harmonize—if it can get you to answer Emeka's line with yours—then it doesn't need the fragments. A harmony completes a verse. That's what harmony* is. *It doesn't want to take your name, Ada. It wants you to* give *the second line. Freely. The way you'd give anything to him.*

The words sat in the kitchen like frost.

Ada thought of the voice at the gate. *Just the second line and I can find my way back.* And they thought—this was the thought that would not leave, that followed them for days afterward, that slept beside them like a second body—*what if it was him?* What if some true shard of Emeka was in there, cold and forgetting, reaching for the one person who had ever been able to finish his sentences? What if the horror was not that the voice was false, but that it was *true*, and the thing had merely found the truest wire in Ada's chest and strummed it?

That was the enemy's real nature, they understood at last. Not hunger. Not malice, even. It was a mirror with a mouth. It could not make anything; it could only *reflect*, perfectly, the thing you most ached to hear, and let your own love do the reaching. Heartbreak was not its weapon. Heartbreak was its *raw material*. It had never composed a single note in a hundred years. It had only ever conducted.

*Then we don't answer it,* Ada said. *We make it answer us.*

Maren's pen stopped. Tovi looked up.

Ada turned to Emeka's notebook, to the sheet music inside, to the annotations in his cramped hand—*cut off mid-note, cut off mid-note*—and to the memory of the recording they had found, his humming breaking off at the same place every time, the place where the ninth verse began. He had been trying to end it alone. He had failed because he had tried to sing *to* the bell, and anything sung to the bell becomes a conversation, and every conversation with the bell ends the same way.

But a verse assembled from nine fragments, sung *past* the bell—to the children, to the lock, to the ones still held between notes—that was not a conversation. That was a rescue.

*It leads with love,* Ada said. *So we lead with the names. Nine names, Tovi. You said we could find them. The playground rhymes, the lullabies, the epitaph. We get all nine fragments before the bell rings nine times, and then we don't harmonize with it—* they looked at Maren, at the mouth that had spent sixty years refusing, and found they were smiling, and the smile felt like a blade being drawn *—we finish the verse it's been choking on for a hundred and thirteen years. We sing the lock open.*

Maren wrote one word on the last clean page of the notebook, and pressed it across the table, and her hand was steady for the first time all night.

*When?*

Tovi was already counting on their fingers, lips moving, and when they looked up their young face had gone old with arithmetic.

*The tolls are climbing one a night,* they said. *Eight last night. It rings at 3:09. Tomorrow night it rings nine.*

* * *

Outside, the sun finally rose over Hollowmere, gray and thin, and did nothing to the cold. On Ninth Street the frost on the inside of Ada's window had come back while none of them were looking. It had not written a word this time. It had drawn, in fine white lines, five ruled staves—and on the staves, a single line of melody, complete, both parts, a duet.

The second line was in Ada's own handwriting.
