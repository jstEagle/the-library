## 21. Last Verse, Sung Alone

The belfry had no ceiling.

Ada understood this the way one understands things in dreams—not with the eyes but with some older organ of knowing, buried beneath language. Above them, where rafters and slate should have held back the sky, there was only a slow turning dark, like water seen from underneath, like the inside of a bell that had swallowed the world and was still ringing with it. The air tasted of cold iron and old paper and, faintly, unbearably, of Emeka's room: pencil shavings, cedar, the ghost of his soap.

Nine children stood in a half-circle around the bell.

They were not ghosts. Ada had expected ghosts—translucence, sorrow, the pale theater of it—but these were solid as memory is solid, more real than anything in Hollowmere had been since Ada arrived. A girl in a 1911 pinafore stood with her mouth open on a note she had been holding for a hundred and thirteen years. A boy beside her gripped a hymnal so tightly the cover had molded to his fingers. Their eyes were open. Their eyes had never closed. They were mid-hymn, caught between two notes like insects in amber made of sound, and the silence that poured off them was louder than any bell.

And at the end of the row, newest, faintest, already going gray at the edges like a photograph left in the sun—Emeka.

*Don't,* he had said on the eighth landing. *Turn around. Go home.* But home was here now; home had always been here, in this place that was also nowhere, on this floor that did not exist until the bell needed somewhere to put what it was given. Ada had climbed past the workhouse landing where small cots stood made up for children who would never sleep again, past the minehead where a cage lift hung on a rope of shadow, past the schoolroom with its slates still chalked with the first eight verses in nine different hands. They had not turned around. Love had tried to stop them twice tonight, wearing Emeka's voice both times, and love had failed, because Ada Okafor had finally learned the difference between being called and coming freely.

That difference was the whole of everything. Maren had said it without saying it, her ruined voice scratching out the warning: *never sing along*. Anselm had learned it standing in his empty church at dawn, harmonizing with nothing, giving the bell exactly what it wanted—a voice answering a call. The bell could take. The bell could claim, collect, tithe, digest. But it had never once, in a hundred years of hunger, been *given* to.

Ada stepped forward. The floorboards rang softly under their feet, each one a different pitch, the whole belfry an instrument waiting to be played.

"I know why you're all still here," Ada said. Their voice sounded strange in the soundless space—too loud, too alive. "You were singing something no adult taught you. Something you found. And it needed nine voices, and when you finished—or didn't finish—the bell kept you between the notes." They looked down the row of faces. The girl in the pinafore blinked, slow as a church door closing. "You're not prisoners. You're the lock. And locks can be opened."

Emeka's mouth moved. No sound came out—he was too far gone into the note for words now—but Ada had spent twenty-two years reading that mouth across breakfast tables and school corridors and hospital waiting rooms, and it said, clearly as speech: *you found it.*

"We found it," Ada corrected gently. "Tovi found the ledger. Maren remembered eight verses. You—" their throat tightened, and they let it tighten; there was no more use for denial in this place, "—you wrote the ninth one down in fragments, trying to do this alone. You shouldn't have tried alone. That's my fault. I wasn't listening."

The fragments were in Ada's hands. Tovi had assembled them from a skipping rhyme chanted outside the post office, from a lullaby Ilse Marsh's grandmother had sung with tears she couldn't explain, from an epitaph on a grave so weathered only a sixteen-year-old archivist kneeling in the frost with a phone flashlight could have raised it. Eight lines. One for each child who carried a piece, and then the ninth line, the one that had lived nowhere, written by no one, because it could only be sung by someone who chose to stand here.

Maren had told them what the ninth line cost. *It wants a name,* she'd written on the back of a receipt, her handwriting enormous now that her hearing was gone, letters like a child's, like a confession. *Not taken. Given. It can't digest a gift. Nothing that eats can swallow something handed over freely—it chokes on consent. That's the whole secret, girl. That's the verse under the verse.*

The bell hung above them, vast and melted and wrong, its bronze surface running like candlewax frozen mid-drip, and though it made no sound, Ada felt it listening. Felt it lean. A hundred years of tithes had taught it patience, but patience frays, and the debt was due, and the ledger downstairs said *Okafor* in fresh ink.

"You want my name," Ada said to it. "Everyone up here gave theirs whether they meant to or not. Taken. Claimed. Collected." They lifted the paper with the assembled verse, and let it fall. It did not land. There was no floor for it to land on; it simply stopped existing below knee-height, absorbed into the dark. "So I'm going to give it. Not because you called. Not because the families paid. Because I choose to. Try swallowing that."

The silence changed texture. If a bell could flinch, this one flinched.

Ada turned to the children. To Emeka last, longest.

"Sing with me," they said. "Finish it. All of you. I'll carry the part that opens the lock."

And Ada began.

The first note tore out of them rough and untrained, and for one terrible heartbeat nothing answered—then the girl in the pinafore moved her mouth, and a century of held breath came out of her in a single silver thread of sound, and the boy with the hymnal followed, and the next, and the next, nine voices rising out of the amber one by one like people waking, and the hymn that had been interrupted in 1911 resumed exactly where it had broken, in the same key, in the same breath, as if the intervening years had been a single held pause.

It was the ugliest, most beautiful thing Ada had ever heard.

The verses Maren had remembered wove through the belfry—the workhouse verse, the mine verse, the schoolroom verse—and with each one, a landing below lit up and emptied, the cots folding flat, the lift cage dissolving, the slates wiping clean. The song was not a summoning. It had never been a summoning. It was a *counting*, a naming of every place the bell had made out of people, and naming unmade them. The founding families had heard only the hunger in it and fed the hunger, generation after generation, because feeding a monster is easier than understanding a song.

They reached the eighth verse. The children's voices were strengthening, color returning to their faces, the gray falling away from Emeka like ash from a live coal. Below them, far below, through floors that were and weren't, Ada heard the village wake—doors opening, dogs beginning to howl, and somewhere on Ninth Street, Tovi screaming at Maren that the frost was climbing the inside of every window in Hollowmere, spelling not NINTH now but a word none of them had seen before, a word Tovi would later transcribe into the archive with shaking hands: *ENOUGH.*

The ninth verse.

There was no music for it. There had never been music for it. Ada opened their mouth and let the line come up out of the place where names are kept, and it came out in their own voice and Emeka's voice at once, twin-stranded, braided, the way it should always have been sung:

*I was not taken. I came of my own will.*
*What is given cannot be kept.*
*What is loved cannot be owed.*
*Ring no more. Hold no more. Hunger no more.*
*My name is a door, and I am opening it.*
*Go home, little ones. The hymn is finished.*
*Go home, brother. I will remember you.*
*Remember me.*
*Amen — and amen — and let it be silent.*

On the final line, Ada gave the bell their name.

Not spoke it. *Gave* it—reached inside themselves to the place where *Ada Okafor* lived, the whole architecture of it, the childhood noises rationalized, the journal written in a hand identical to their brother's, the wrong grave, the frost, the fear, the fury, the found family waiting at the bottom of the stairs—and lifted it out and held it up like bread, like a key, like a hand extended in peace, and offered it freely, asking nothing back.

The bell tried to take it.

Ada felt the attempt the way you feel a wave break over you—a vast cold attention closing around the offered name, drawing it in, expecting the usual meal, the usual surrender, the usual soul filed under TITHE PAID IN FULL.

And then the bell choked.

It was not dramatic at first. It was almost comic—a shudder, a stutter, the great bronze mass hiccuping on something it could not process, because a name given freely is not food, it is a *ransom*, and ransom reverses the direction of every transaction. The hunger that had run one way for a hundred years ran suddenly the other way, and everything the bell had swallowed began to come back up.

The crack started at the crown and ran down the middle with a sound like winter ice letting go of a lake—a long, groaning, splitting report that Ada felt in their teeth and their sternum and the roots of their hair. Light came out of the crack, ordinary yellow lamplight-colored light, warm as a kitchen. The bell split like a mouth opening, like a fist unclenching after a century, and fell silent—not faded, not faded *away*, but finished, the way a song is finished, the way grief is never finished but finally, finally set down.

The children stepped out of the note.

There was no other way to describe it. One moment they were figures in amber; the next they were simply *here*, whole, blinking, looking at their own hands with the astonishment of people waking in a room they thought they'd died in. The girl in the pinafore touched her own open mouth and closed it. The boy's hymnal crumbled gently into dust that smelled, impossibly, of summer. None of them spoke—they had been holding one note for a hundred and thirteen years, and perhaps words would come later, or perhaps they would go straight to whatever waits beyond the ninth floor, which was not a floor at all but a door, now wide open, and full of the smell of cut grass and someone's mother calling someone in to tea.

They went through it one by one. As the smallest girl reached the threshold she turned and looked at Ada—really looked, the first time anyone in Hollowmere had looked at them properly since the funeral—and inclined her head, solemn as a curtsy. *Thank you*, said the nod. *We heard every word.*

Then Emeka.

He was solid now, or nearly—still translucent at the edges, still newly arrived and barely settled, but himself, entirely himself, and walking toward Ada across a floor that rang like a piano under his feet. Ada's legs gave out. They went to their knees on the boards and Emeka knelt too, and took their face in his hands the way he had when they were six and Ada had lost a tooth and been certain they'd lost a piece of themselves forever.

*You absolute idiot,* his face said, and then, immediately, *thank you, thank you, thank you,* and then, because he was Emeka and had always been Emeka: *Tell Tovi the archive needs a better backup system. Tell Maren I'm sorry about her hearing. Tell Anselm—* and here his expression did something complicated and tender and amused—*tell Anselm his requiem was actually lovely. Wrong, but lovely.*

"I can't stay," Ada whispered. It wasn't a question. They had known since the eighth landing. A name given is a name spent; the ransom worked because it cost everything. Somewhere below, in the world that still had hours and weather and bread, three people were keeping a space for them, and the magic that erased would erase, and that was the price, and Ada paid it gladly, was paying it already, feeling themselves grow lighter and less specific with every breath.

Emeka smiled. It was the smile from the photograph, from the coffin, from every summer of their shared childhood, and it was aimed at Ada with a gratitude so complete it hurt worse than the losing.

His lips shaped the words one last time—no voice, but Ada read them as easily as their own name, which was, by then, already gone:

*You were always the one who listened.*

And he kissed their forehead, twin to twin, the oldest seal there is, and walked backward through the door into the grass-smelling light, and the light closed behind him like water, gentle, without a ripple.

* * *

Ada woke on the belfry stairs, on the third landing, in the ordinary ruined dark of St. Brannoc's, with frost melting on the stone around them and dawn coming up gray through the broken louvres.

The bell was in two pieces on the belfry floor, split clean down the middle, its bronze already dulling toward green, already just metal, just history, just the relic of a disaster that had finally, after a hundred years, been allowed to be over. No hum came off it. No hunger. Ada laid a palm against the cracked face and felt nothing but cold, honest, dead weight, and laughed—a cracked, sobbing, human laugh that echoed up the stairwell and sounded, for the first time, like it belonged to someone.

Below, feet on the nave flags. Voices. Tovi's voice, high and cracking, calling their name—calling *Ada*, and the name still fit, still answered, which was the first miracle. Then Maren's silence moving fast, and Anselm's heavier tread, and then they were in the doorway of the stairwell, the three of them, filthy and smoke-smelling and alive, staring up at Ada as though at a resurrection, which, in every way that mattered, they were.

"You did it," Tovi said. "The bell's dead. The frost is gone. Every window in the village is just—glass. Just windows." They were crying without noticing. "But Ada—there's something wrong. The parish register. The gravestone. Mum's photo albums. Your name's—" Tovi's voice broke and steadied. "Your name's fading everywhere. Like it's being erased. Everyone in the village looked right through me when I asked about you. Except—" they held up a battered notebook, its pages dense with Tovi's careful hand, "except what I wrote down. What we write down. It's not fading. It's *holding*."

Ada came down the stairs on legs that shook, into the gray morning light of the church, and Maren Voss took their face in her two old hands and studied it with her clouded eyes, and nodded once, the same solemn nod the smallest child had given, and wrote on her receipt-pad in her huge child-letters:

*Still here. Good. We'll remember you loudly enough for the whole village.*

Outside, Hollowmere lay silent under the first clean dawn in a hundred years—no bell, no frost, no ledger, no debt. Only the sound of Tovi already talking, feverishly, about rebuilding the archive from the notebook, about names that survive because someone chooses to carry them, about a grave on Ninth Street that would need a new inscription, the right one this time, carved deep enough to outlast forgetting:

*Here lie nine children, free. And here stands the tenth, who stayed.*
