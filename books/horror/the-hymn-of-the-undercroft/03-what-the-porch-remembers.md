## 3. What the Porch Remembers

Ada did not sleep so much as she was set down in the dark and left there, like a package no one wanted to sign for.

The dream—if it was a dream—was of water rising over pews. She stood waist-deep in a church basement she had never seen, and the water was warm as bathwater and black as ink, and somewhere above her, muffled through floorboards, a choir was singing a hymn she almost knew. Every time she reached for the melody it slipped sideways, the way a name slips when you're falling asleep, and the slipping hurt worse than the cold would have.

She woke at 4:17 a.m. with her teeth aching and her window fogged from the inside.

That was the first wrong thing. Her bedroom window had never fogged in October. Hollow Creek autumns were dry, crisp, all rustle and woodsmoke; the furnace didn't kick on until November if you were stubborn about it. But the glass was misted, and when she dragged her palm across it, the condensation came away in feathers—fine white filigree branching outward from the corners, fern-patterns, the kind of frost that takes hours of hard cold to build and builds instead in seconds under a child's fingernail when the breath is right.

Ada sat on the edge of the bed for a long time, watching the patterns creep another inch toward the center of the pane, and told herself: old house, bad seals, cold snap coming early. The radio station's ancient thermostat read sixty-one degrees when she finally went downstairs. Sixty-one. Warm enough for shirtsleeves. Warm enough that the grass outside was still green and the maples along Delmore Street were only just thinking about turning.

The frost on her bedroom window was not an anomaly, she decided. It was a coincidence. It was ductwork. It was anything at all except what it looked like, which was winter arriving through a keyhole meant for something else.

She made coffee she didn't drink. She sat at the kitchen table with Ruth Ellery's obituary open on her laptop—the *Hollow Creek Courier* ran obituaries online now, three paragraphs each, mostly boilerplate—and read it again for the eleventh time since two o'clock.

*Ruth Anne Ellery, 61, passed away Tuesday evening at Hollow Creek General following complications from pneumonia. A retired schoolteacher, Ruth taught fourth grade at Delmore Elementary for thirty-four years and was known for her garden, her pies, and her devotion to the late service at First Methodist. She is survived by a brother in Ohio. Services were held Saturday.*

Tuesday evening. Which meant that when Ruth had called the show at 11:52 p.m.—when she'd said *you were there too, Ada, don't you remember the singing*—Ruth Ellery had been four days dead.

Ada closed the laptop. Opened it. Closed it again. The coffee went cold in its mug with a thin skin forming on top, and she watched that skin wrinkle and thought, absurdly, of skin on water, of a surface holding something down.

At dawn she slept for ninety minutes sitting up in the armchair, and dreamed of nothing, which was somehow worse.

* * *

The day passed the way days pass when you are waiting for night to prove you crazy or correct: slowly, and then all at once.

She called in sick to the station—which Marcus accepted without argument, which frightened her more than an argument would have, because Marcus never accepted anything without argument—and drove out past the county line to the big-box grocery in Marlow because she could not face the IGA on Main Street, where everyone knew everyone's business and someone would ask how the show was going and she would have to say *fine* with her mouth while her eyes said *a dead woman asked me about a fire.*

She bought salt. She did not know why she bought salt. Three canisters of it, iodized, the cheap blue boxes, and she carried them out to the car and set them on the passenger seat and stared at them the whole drive home, trying to locate the memory that had sent her hand to that aisle. There wasn't one. There was just the impulse, cool and certain, like a hand on the small of her back steering her. Salt for thresholds. Salt for—

For what? Who put that in me?

By late afternoon the light had gone long and amber across the yard, and the air off the fields still held the day's warmth, sixty-eight degrees by the thermometer screwed to the porch post, and Ada stood in her doorway with her keys in her hand and understood, before she saw it, that something was waiting for her to come home.

The frost started at the north corner of the porch and spread toward the steps in a fan, white lace laid over gray boards, thickening as it went until the planks nearest the door wore a rime half an inch deep, glittering in the slant light like sugar. It climbed the railing. It furred the post where the thermometer hung. And it stopped—stopped precisely, cleanly—at the edge of the bottom step, so that the yard beyond lay green and untouched, autumn all the way to the fence line, and the porch itself belonged to January.

Ada stood very still. The keys bit into her palm.

Frost needs cold. Frost needs moisture and cold and hours. This was none of those things. This was a drawing. Someone had drawn winter on her porch with a very fine pen, and the pen had been patient, and the pen had known exactly where to stop.

And there, in the middle of it, in the deepest white, were footprints. Small ones. Bare feet, child-sized, pressed into the rime and melted clean through to bare wood, walking up the steps and stopping at her door and then—this was the part that made her stomach drop through the floorboards—turning around. Walking back down. Sitting.

She followed them with her eyes. Down the steps. Across the frozen boards. To the far corner of the porch, where the light pooled amber and the shadows of the maple leaves fell long and trembling.

Someone was sitting there.

* * *

He was small. That was the first thing—not his face, not his clothes, but his size, the way he took up so little room against the porch post, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, chin resting on his wrists. He couldn't have been more than seven or eight years old. He wore corduroy pants gone soft at the knees and a striped shirt with the collar stretched out and canvas sneakers with the laces double-knotted, the way children knot them because no one has taught them yet to be embarrassed about it. His hair was brown and cowlicked and needed cutting. His feet were bare. The sneakers sat empty beside him, toes pointed toward the yard, and his bare feet rested in the frost without melting it, without any effect on it at all, as though the cold had simply agreed to hold him.

Ada knew him.

Not recognized—knew, the way you know your own handwriting, the way you know the specific creak of the third stair. Knowledge rose in her from somewhere below memory, below childhood even, from some cellar of herself she hadn't visited in thirty years, and it came with a feeling so violent and so tender that her knees nearly went. It was homesickness. That was the only word for it, and it made no sense—you cannot be homesick for a person—but there it was, that ache, that terrible sweet ache of standing in a doorway of a house you were taken from too young, and the house is this boy, this small boy on her porch, and every year of her life since had been a year spent away.

"Wren," she said. Her voice came out cracked, half air.

He looked up. His face was round and freckled across the nose, with a gap where a front tooth should be, and his eyes were the gray-green of creek water over stones, and they were the same eyes she had drawn a thousand times in crayon on the backs of homework pages, always the same, always getting them slightly wrong and knowing they were slightly wrong and not being able to fix them.

They weren't wrong now. They were exactly right, and that was the most frightening thing she had ever seen.

"Hi, Ada," Wren said.

His voice was unchanged. That was impossible—thirty years, voices change, children become strangers—but it was unchanged, high and clear and slightly hoarse at the edges, a boy who talked a lot and shouted sometimes, a boy whose voice she had heard every day of her sixth, seventh, eighth years saying *race you to the culvert*, saying *don't look, I'll tell you when it's safe*, saying, once, in a tone she had never forgotten and could never place, *I'll take care of it. You won't have to.*

"You're not real," Ada said. She said it the way you say a rope holds, testing weight on it. "You were—I made you up. When I was little. Kids do that. Imaginary friends. It's—it's developmental, it's normal, there are studies—"

"I know," Wren said, agreeably, the way he'd agreed with everything, the way he'd let her win every race to the culvert and she'd known he let her and loved him for it. "You made me up."

"Then how—" She gestured at him, at the whole impossible amber-lit fact of him, and her hand was shaking badly enough that she hid it against her thigh. "How are you here?"

Wren considered the question with the seriousness he'd given all questions, head tipped, gap-toothed, unhurried. Around him the frost glittered. In the yard behind him a jay screamed once and went silent, as if it had seen enough.

"You kept your piece," he said.

The words landed in her chest like stones dropped down a well. She waited for the splash of understanding and got only depth.

"My piece," she repeated. "My piece of what? Wren, what piece?"

But he had turned his face toward the yard, toward the west, where the sun was going down behind the water tower in a smear of copper, and his expression had changed—had gone, for a moment, older. Not adult. Just tired in a way children aren't, a tiredness that has been waiting a long time without being allowed to complain.

"It's almost October," he said, which made no sense, because it was October, had been October for nineteen days, and she opened her mouth to say so and found she couldn't, found the correction dissolving on her tongue like frost on a warm hand. "It's almost first frost. You have to remember before then, Ada. They're bringing them back to you. All five. One at a time."

"Who's bringing what?" She came down one step, then another, standing on the border where the frost ended and the warm boards began, the exact line where winter met October, and the cold coming off the white lace raised the hair on her arms. "Who's bringing what back? Ruth? Was it Ruth—is Ruth doing this?"

Something moved through Wren's face at the name—grief, quick and swallowed, the grief of a child who has learned grief goes where it's pointed and stays there. He looked at his bare feet in the frost.

"She liked my humming," he said quietly. "In the fourth grade. She caught me humming it under my breath during the spelling test and she didn't yell. She just said, *that's pretty, where's it from?* And I couldn't tell her." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and eight years old. "I've never been able to tell anybody where it's from. Neither have you. Have you."

It wasn't a question, and the answer lived in her body anyway: her teeth ached, faintly, at the mention of humming, the same ache from the second caller, the ache that had woken her at 4:17 with the window feathered white. Somewhere in her was a hymn she had never heard, and it was pressing on the inside of her like a bird on an eggshell.

"The fire," Ada said. "Ruth asked about a fire. On the show. She said I was there."

Wren was quiet for so long that the shadows moved, lengthened, crawled up the porch posts like dark water filling a glass. When he spoke, his voice had gone careful, the carefulness adults use around children and children use around broken things.

"You'll find it," he said. "It's all in the library. It's all in the paper. It's been waiting for you down there in the dark for fifty years, printed in little letters nobody reads." He unfolded himself, stood—small, still small, the top of his head level with her sternum—and looked up at her with an expression she had spent three decades failing to remember and now recognized instantly, completely, the way you recognize your own reflection: he was afraid *for* her. "Go look, Ada. Go look tomorrow. And when you find the names—and you'll find the names—"

He stopped. The frost around his feet had begun, very slowly, to brighten, to thicken, to climb his ankles in fine white feathers, and where it touched him he did not flinch, only grew fainter, the way a photograph grows fainter, the way breath fades from glass.

"—remember that I never lied to you," he finished. "Not once. Not even when you asked."

"Asked what?" Her voice broke on it. "Wren, asked *what?*"

But the porch corner held only shadow now, and the frost was already retreating from the boards in slow tides, pulling back toward the north corner like a tide going out, taking its glitter with it, leaving the wood dark and damp and ordinary. The sneakers were gone. The footprints were gone. Only the cold remained, pooling at her ankles, and the smell—that smell she hadn't smelled since she was six, pencil shavings and creek water and cut grass, the smell of a boy made out of summer—and then that was gone too, and the evening was just an evening, sixty degrees and crickets, and Ada Marchetti stood alone on her own porch with her keys still biting into her palm and tears running freely down her face for a friend she had, apparently, invented.

*Imaginary,* she told herself, over and over, climbing the stairs, locking the door, checking it twice. *Imaginary friends are normal. Imaginary friends are healthy. Imaginary friends do not leave footprints.*

In the bathroom mirror her face looked gray and her eyes looked haunted and her breath, when it hit the cold glass, bloomed and faded, bloomed and faded, and in the fading she could almost see the shape of it linger—a small bird, wings folded, watching her.

She did not sleep. At 6 a.m., when the county library opened, she was sitting in her car in its empty lot with the engine ticking, watching the doors, and the microfilm room was in the basement, and she thought: *of course it is.*
