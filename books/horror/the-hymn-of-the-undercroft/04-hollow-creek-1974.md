## 4. Hollow Creek, 1974

The county library closed at five, but the archivist, a stooped man named Pell who smelled of pipe smoke and dust, had taken pity on her. He'd unlocked the microfilm room in the basement, shown her how to thread the reel, and left her alone with the machine and its green-glowing screen.

"First Methodist," he'd said, pausing at the door. "You'll want October of '74." Then, after a silence long enough that she looked up: "My mother wouldn't go near that church after. Wouldn't say why."

Now the reels turned, and Hollow Creek scrolled past her in jerking gray columns—the *Hollow Creek Courier*, week by week, forty-nine years deep. Ada had come straight from the station after sign-off, still in yesterday's clothes, coffee gone cold beside the machine. She hadn't slept. Ruth's voice was still in her ears, patient as water finding a crack: *You were there, Ada. You just don't remember.*

*October 17, 1974.* The headline was small—buried below a story about the harvest festival and an ad for carpeting at Delgado's. That was the first wrong thing. A church fire should have been front page. Instead:

**BLAZE AT FIRST METHODIST CLAIMS BASEMENT; SIX CHILDREN RESCUED**

Six children rescued. No deaths. And yet the article read like an obituary for something no one could name.

*Fire broke out shortly before nine o'clock Thursday evening in the undercroft of First Methodist Church during a meeting of the children's choir. Cause remains under investigation. Six children, ages eight through eleven, were trapped when floodwater from a ruptured main combined with smoke in the lower level. All six were recovered alive by volunteer firefighters after approximately forty minutes.*

Forty minutes. Ada did the arithmetic without wanting to. Forty minutes underwater, or near it, in smoke and blackness, and all six walked out.

She fed the reel forward. The next week's paper carried a follow-up, shorter, stranger:

**INVESTIGATION INTO CHURCH FIRE CONTINUES; FAMILIES DECLINE COMMENT**

*Pastor Edwin Marsh has declined requests for interviews. Sources close to the families report the children have "been kept home from school" and that the church's undercroft has been sealed "pending structural review." The review has not commenced. When asked about reports of singing heard from the sealed level, Pastor Marsh stated only: "Some doors are better left shut."*

Singing. Ada's hand drifted to her throat without her permission.

* * *

The smoke came first, rolling down the stairs like something poured.

Ruthie Voss, nine years old, stood at the bottom step and watched the light above her turn orange, then red, then disappear entirely behind a wall of gray. Above them, the choir loft was burning. She could hear the piano going—one string popping like a wire under strain, then another—and she could hear Mr. Aldous shouting names into the dark.

*"Ruthie! Danny! Where are you—"*

The water was already at their ankles. Cold as January, rising fast, smelling of rust and old stone. It came from everywhere and nowhere, welling up between the flagstones as if the floor itself were bleeding.

"There's a door," said Thomas Greer. He was ten and bossy and his voice cracked now, high with fear. "Behind the boiler. Papa showed me once. It goes out to the culvert."

"The culvert's flooded," Ruthie said.

"Then we swim."

Nobody moved. The six of them stood in a half circle around the one electric bulb that still burned, a bare bulb swaying on its cord, throwing shadows that swung too wide, as if there were more of them down here than there were.

Ada Marchetti was the smallest. She was eight, and she wasn't crying, which frightened Ruthie more than the smoke did. Ada was staring at the water with her head tilted, listening to it, the way you'd listen to music.

"Ada," Ruthie whispered. "Come on."

"I know," Ada said. "He says to wait."

"Who says?"

But then the bulb went out, and in the black there was a sound none of them would ever describe the same way again—a sound like a hymn played backward, like breath drawn through stone—and the water rose past their knees, and they ran.

* * *

The obituaries started three pages later, and Ada found them the way you find a bruise—by pressing where it hurt.

She'd written down the six names from the rescue article before she let herself look: **Ruth Voss. Daniel Okafor. Thomas Greer. Miriam Aldous. Peter Sandoval. Ada Marchetti.**

Her own name sat in that list like a splinter. She read it four times. *Ada Marchetti,* age eight, daughter of Joseph and Elena Marchetti. Her father's name. She had never—not once in her life—heard her mother speak of him, and here he was in the gray grain of 1974, listed among the parents of the rescued children.

Then the obituaries.

**RUTH VOSS,** age 58, of Hollow Creek, passed away Tuesday, October 8th, at her home on Corliss Street. Beloved schoolteacher, retired. Preceded in death by her husband, Raymond. Services private.

Last Tuesday. The Tuesday before Ruth called the show.

Ada's mouth went dry. She threaded the next reel with hands that wanted to shake and made them be still, made them move like someone else's hands, someone calm, someone who hadn't heard a dead woman say *you were there.*

**DANIEL OKAFOR,** age 57, died Friday, October 11th, at St. Vincent's. A carpenter by trade, Daniel was known throughout the valley for his birdhouses, which he gave away freely to any child who asked. Survived by two sisters.

Friday. Four days ago.

**THOMAS GREER,** 59, passed suddenly Sunday evening. Longtime owner of Greer's Hardware—

Sunday. Two days ago.

**MIRIAM ALDOUS,** 56, died Monday morning at home, surrounded by family—

Yesterday.

Five names. Five dates, marching toward her across forty-nine years like footsteps coming up a stairwell. Ruth. Danny. Thomas. Miriam. One more. Her finger hovered over the fifth obituary, over the name **PETER SANDOVAL**, age 60, died Wednesday, October 16th—

Today. He had died today. Somewhere in Hollow Creek, while she sat in this basement reading about him, Peter Sandoval had stopped breathing, and she had the sudden, unshakable certainty that somewhere in his last hour, he had picked up a telephone.

Five of the six children from the undercroft. Dead within seven days of one another, fifty years almost to the season after the fire. And the sixth—

The sixth was sitting in a basement reading about it.

* * *

They found the door behind the boiler, and the door was locked, and the water was at their waists, and Danny Okafor was the one who saw the other way.

Not a door. A hatch in the floor, iron-ringed, half-lifted already by the pressure of the water beneath. The undercroft went deeper than anyone knew—a lower level, older than the church, older maybe than the town, and the floodwater was pouring *up* through it, which meant the space below was not yet full.

"We can breathe down there," Danny said. "Till they find us."

"It's dark," said Miriam Aldous, who was eleven and the oldest and trying so hard to sound like it.

"So's up there," said Thomas, and coughed, and the cough had smoke in it.

They went down, one by one, into the cold. Ruthie last, because she was holding Ada's hand and Ada would not let go of the ladder rung. Ada kept looking back up—at the orange glow, at the shapes moving in it—and she said, clear as a bell in all that roaring:

"He doesn't want us to go up."

"Who, Ada?" Ruthie shouted. "*Who?*"

And Ada smiled at her—smiled, in the middle of everything, with such sweetness that Ruthie would carry the memory of it her whole life and never be able to explain it—and said:

"My friend."

* * *

There was no photograph in the paper. Ada searched every follow-up, every column inch, and found no faces, no names beyond the list, nothing but the strange, hollow language of a town agreeing not to look at something. Even the editorial page, a week later, offered only this, unsigned:

*The people of Hollow Creek are resilient. We rebuild. We remember what serves us, and we are gracious about the rest.*

*We are gracious about the rest.* Ada read the sentence until it stopped looking like words.

Pell appeared at the top of the stairs near midnight, keys jingling, and paused when he saw her face. "Find what you needed?"

"I don't know," she said honestly. "Do you remember the fire? You'd have been—what, a teenager?"

"Born in '71." He shook his head slowly. "But my mother talked about it once, when she was sick at the end. Said the whole town got quiet that fall. Not sad—quiet. Like everyone had agreed to hum instead of talk." He rubbed his jaw. "She said the strangest part wasn't the fire. It was that nobody could tell you what the church basement looked like afterward. Flooded, they said. Sealed. But ask anybody what was down there, and they'd just—" he made a vague gesture, "—go smooth. Like a pond settling."

"Did your mother ever say why?"

"She said the children paid for it." He said it simply, the way you'd report weather, and then seemed startled by himself. "That's—no, that's not right, I'm putting words together wrong. Forget an old man's midnight." But he didn't meet her eyes as he said it, and he left the lights on for her anyway.

* * *

Below, in the breathing dark, the water held them.

It was warmer down there—or it felt warmer, after the smoke—and it was quiet, and the six of them clung to pipes and each other in a space so old the stones were round as river rocks. Time went strange. Forty minutes, the paper said. It felt like a night and a year.

It was there, in that drowned room beneath the flooded floor, that the voice spoke to them.

Not out loud. Not in any ear. It arrived the way cold arrives, everywhere at once, and it was patient, and it was old, and it was *gracious*.

*The way out is through me,* it said, or something like it; memory would soften the words later, sand them smooth. *The stair is gone. The door is gone. But I am a door, little ones, and doors open—for those who pay the toll.*

"What toll?" Thomas demanded, because Thomas bargained with everything, even drowning.

*A memory. One apiece. Something precious, freely given, and you will walk out of this water and live long lives and never feel the weight of what you left with me.*

"And if we don't?" Miriam asked.

Silence, then. A long, listening silence, and the water rose an inch, gentle as a hand.

It was Ruthie who understood first, Ruthie who felt the shape of the thing in the dark the way you feel a draft. "It's not lying," she whispered. "It's not even threatening. It's just—it's *waiting*. Like winter waits."

One by one, shivering, sobbing or silent, they began to give. Thomas gave the sound of his mother calling him home at dusk. Miriam gave her first kiss, stolen behind the feed store that June. Danny gave his grandmother's lullaby, every note of it. Peter gave his own name—gave it whole, so that afterward he had to learn it again like a stranger's. Ruthie gave her mother's face, and spent fifty years unable to picture it, and told herself it was grief that took it.

And Ada Marchetti, eight years old, smallest of the six, stood in the black water and refused.

"No," she said—to the dark, to the cold, to the patient waiting thing. "No. Take me instead, take all of me, but I'm not giving you anything, and neither are they, you leave them *alone*—"

And the cold gathered itself around her like a coat being put on, and the others screamed her name, and then—

Then something stepped between Ada and the dark. Something small and quick and bright, something that had been with her since before she could talk, something only she had ever seen. The cold recoiled from it. Hesitated. Considered.

*This is not hers to give,* the dark said at last, almost gently. *But it will do. It will do for now. Fifty years, little bird. Keep the piece safe. Bring it home when the frost comes, or I will come collect the town.*

And the water began, impossibly, to drain.

* * *

Ada sat in the green glow of the microfilm reader until nearly two in the morning, and what she felt was not fear, exactly. Fear was simple. This was worse: it was recognition. Every fragment fit a groove already worn inside her—the flooded floor, the sealed door, the hymn she'd never heard but knew, Wren on her porch saying *you kept your piece.*

Five children had paid. Five children had died last week, on schedule, like a debt called in.

Which meant the sixth child owed something still. Which meant the frost on her porch wasn't a warning aimed at her.

It was a bill, and it had her address.

The reader clicked as the reel ran out. In the silence after, in the basement of the county library, Ada became aware of a sound she couldn't place—faint, rhythmic, coming up through the floor. Humming. Someone in the stacks above, humming four notes over and over, soft and sweet and terribly familiar.

She climbed the stairs. The library was empty. Every window was frosted white, though the October night outside was mild as milk.

On the glass of the front door, drawn in the frost in a child's careful hand, someone had written a single word:

**SOON.**
