## 17. The Descent

The radio station smelled like burnt coffee and old carpet, the way it always did, and Ada stood in the doorway of Studio B for a long time before she could make herself go in.

Midnight was four hours away. The frost had reached the edge of her porch at dusk—she'd watched it come up the driveway like a tide going backward, white lace spreading over the blacktop one crystal finger at a time, and she had understood with a calm that frightened her more than panic would have that this was the last night the town would look like itself. Tomorrow, if she failed, Hollow Creek would forget its own name. It would forget its children's names. It would stand in its streets in the cold and not know why it was crying.

She packed the portable radio first. Marcus's recorder was already in her coat pocket, along with a flashlight whose batteries she'd checked three times, and a thermos of coffee she knew she wouldn't drink. On the desk lay the hymn—the whole hymn now, five fragments stitched to the melody that had lived in her teeth since childhood, written out in Marcus's careful hand on station letterhead because neither of them owned manuscript paper. She folded it into a plastic bag and sealed it, then sealed it again, as if water respected diligence.

"You don't have to do it this way," Marcus said from the doorway behind her. He'd been saying versions of it all day. *Let me go back down. Let me take the recorder. Let me find another way.* There wasn't another way. They both knew it, and knowing it had made him quieter with every hour, until now his voice came out sanded smooth, almost gentle.

"Yes, I do." She zipped the bag into her inner pocket, against her chest, where it sat like a second heartbeat. "It has to be me. I'm the one who never paid."

"That was fifty years ago. You were six years old."

"I know." She turned to face him. He looked terrible—three days without sleep, his grandmother's absence carved fresh into him, grief sitting on him like wet wool. He had gone down alone and come up missing a face he'd loved, and he still stood here offering to go again. She loved him for it in a way she didn't have words for yet. Maybe she would later, if there was a later. "That's exactly why it has to be me, Marcus. Everything else they took was taken. Mine was given. Wren stepped in front of it before I even understood there was a bill." Her throat closed. She made herself keep talking. "The bargain doesn't know what to do with a gift. That's the crack in it. That's where I'm going."

He crossed the room and hugged her, hard, his chin on top of her head, and for a moment she let herself be held like something worth saving. Then she pulled back, picked up the portable radio, and went to do the last show of her life—or the first show of whatever came after it.

* * *

At 11:04, Ada Marchetti walked down the aisle of the First Methodist Church carrying a radio tuned to her own voice.

It was a strange sentence to live inside. The sanctuary was dark except for the flashlight, and dark in a way that felt architectural, deliberate, as though the building had opinions about light. The pews rose around her in rows like sleeping congregations. Frost climbed the walls in feathered patterns, thick near the floor, thinning toward the ceiling, and her breath came out in small ghosts even though the air itself wasn't cold—not yet, not up here. The cold lived below. The cold had an address.

Behind the organ, the undercroft door stood open. She had left it open two nights ago when she fled, and no one had closed it, and somehow that seemed right. You didn't close a door you intended to walk back through.

On the radio, her recorded voice was reading the names.

"Ruth Calloway. Harold Voss. Miriam Okafor. Thomas Greer. Danny Pruitt." A pause, engineered by Marcus, who was upstairs in Studio B keeping the board alive, keeping the signal strong enough to reach the water. "Five voices. Five pieces of a hymn. If you're listening—if you've ever listened—I need you now. Sing with me. Whatever note you have. However off-key. The thing under this church has been counting silence for fifty years. Let's give it something else to count."

And then, faintly, through the static, the town began to sing.

Ada stopped at the top of the stairs and listened. It wasn't beautiful, not exactly. It was ragged and human—a hundred voices across Hollow Creek humming a hymn none of them remembered learning, some of them flat, some of them wavering, an old woman's alto braided with a child's uncertain soprano, a man who was probably crying, someone whistling because they couldn't carry a tune. It sounded like a congregation assembled out of everyone who had ever been lost in the dark. It sounded like the opposite of dead air.

Her eyes filled, and she let them. There wasn't time to be embarrassed.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the stairwell, to the black water waiting at the bottom. "I'm coming."

* * *

The water was worse than she remembered, and she remembered it perfectly, which was the problem.

It came up over her shoes in the first three steps, and the cold was not temperature so much as intention—it climbed her legs like something with hands, patient hands, hands that had all night. She gasped and kept walking, one step, another, the flashlight beam shivering across flooded pews, their wooden backs just breaking the surface like the spines of drowned animals. The hymn played tinny and brave from the radio in her left hand. Somewhere above, muffled by stone and earth, the town sang with it.

"Keep talking," Marcus's voice said in her earpiece, thin and crackling. "Narrate. It helps. I don't know why it helps, but Ruth says it helps."

"Ruth says?" Ada waded deeper. Water at her thighs now. Her jeans had become lead.

"She's here. They're all here. Danny's drawing pictures on the console with his finger. He says—" Static swallowed him, then gave him back. "He says the water remembers being rain. He says tell you the water used to be on your side."

"The water used to be on my side," Ada repeated, and found that saying it changed something in her legs, in the way the cold gripped them. Slightly. Not enough. But slightly.

She reached the far wall, where the oldest part of the undercroft opened into darkness the flashlight couldn't price. Here the frost grew thickest, white ferns unfurling across the stone in patterns she recognized from her porch, from her windows, from the photograph in her mother's locked drawer—and in the center of the pattern, where the wall met the water, there was a shape. A door-shaped absence of frost. An invitation drawn in negative space.

She stood before it, chest-deep now, the radio held high, and did the hardest thing she had done in fifty years of hard nights.

She put her feet down and waited.

* * *

It came up slowly, the way dawn comes up, the way dread comes up—the way anything comes when it knows you cannot leave.

First the water around her went still. Perfectly still, in a way water never is, each ripple smoothing itself out as if the surface were being ironed. Then the cold changed key. It had been gripping her; now it began to *listen*. She felt it listening through her skin, through the bones of her pelvis and spine, through the fillings in her teeth. The hymn on the radio faltered—static chewing at the signal—and Ada pressed it closer to her mouth and hummed into the interference, adding her voice to the town's, and the signal steadied.

Then the frost-thing rose.

It did not rise out of the water so much as assemble itself from the water, from the frost, from the dark, a tallness of white and shadow that had no fixed outline—every time her eye tried to trace its edge, the edge moved politely elsewhere. It was vast. It was patient. It was, she understood with a lurch, *hungry* the way winter is hungry, without malice, without appetite even, only the enormous indifferent necessity of taking.

And it wore faces.

They surfaced across it like reflections surfacing on a pond. Her father's face—she knew it instantly, from the photograph, from the way her mother's mouth went soft saying his name—floating on the thing's shoulder, eyes closed, singing. Her third-grade teacher, whose name she'd lost decades ago but whose kindness arrived whole in her chest the moment she saw the face. A boy from the bus route. A dog, some dog, some beloved dog. Her grandmother's hands. A woman who might have been an aunt, humming. Face after face after face, everyone she had ever forgotten, kept here like pressed flowers, worn like jewelry, and she understood suddenly what she was looking at: not a monster wearing trophies. A ledger. A collection. Every memory ever paid into the undercroft, kept alive down here in the cold, tended, the way you tend things you intend to keep forever.

"Oh," Ada said. The word came out broken. "Oh, you *kept* them."

The faces turned toward her. All of them. Her father's eyes opened.

*You kept yours too,* said a voice that was not a voice, that arrived as frost blooming across the inside of her skull, delicate and total. *The little bird. Fifty years we have wondered what to do with the little bird.*

"He isn't payment," Ada said. Her voice shook. She let it shake. Bravery, she thought, was just fear that had somewhere to be. "He never was. And I'm not here to pay. I'm here to give."

*Give. Pay.* The faces rippled, amused or sorrowful, impossible to tell. *The words are the same shape in the water.*

"Not tonight." She lifted the radio higher, water streaming off her arm, and the hymn poured out of it—ragged, human, a hundred voices strong—and beneath it, faintly, five voices that had died in 1974 were singing too, and the frost-thing flinched, actually flinched, its edges stuttering like a picture losing signal. "Tonight the words are different shapes. Tonight you're going to learn what a gift does to a bargain."

She walked forward.

The water took her to her ribs, her shoulders, her chin. The cold closed over her ears and the world became the radio's small brave sound and the pressure of the deep and the faces of the forgotten drifting around her like lanterns. Her father's face passed close enough to touch. She didn't touch it. She would give it back the right way or not at all.

Below her feet, in the deepest dark, something opened.

*Come down, then,* said the winter. *Come down, sixth child. Show us the shape of your word.*

Ada Marchetti took a breath that hurt all the way down, and let the water take her.

* * *

Down was not like down. Down was like being remembered.

The light changed first—black water giving way to a pale glow that came from everywhere, frost-light, moonlight that had never touched a moon. She sank through it slowly, radio clutched to her chest, its glow a warm amber island against all that white, and around her the flooded undercroft revealed itself the way a dream reveals itself: pews rising in rows, an altar draped in weed, and everywhere the frost, growing upward toward the world above like white grass, like white wheat, like a field that had been planted with everything the town had ever lost and left fallow for fifty years.

The faces drifted with her. Her father. The teacher. Strangers she grieved without naming. They moved alongside her descent not as captors, she realized, but as escorts—as though even the taken things wanted to see how this ended.

In her earpiece, impossibly, faintly, Marcus's voice: *"Signal's holding. We're here. We're all here, Ada. Don't stop singing."*

She didn't stop singing.

The hymn came out of her in bubbles and broken phrases and then, as she sank past the point where lungs should work, in some other medium entirely—sound moving through water, through frost, through the old stones, the completed melody unwinding from her like thread from a spool, and the five dead voices joined it from above, filtering down through fathoms, and the town's hundred cracked voices layered over them, and the hymn filled the undercroft the way water filled the undercroft, completely, without gaps.

And the frost-thing came down to meet her.

It descended through its own cathedral of ice, vast and white and wearing its congregation of faces, and it stopped before her in the glowing deep, close enough that she could see that the faces were singing too—that they had always been singing, that this was what the undercroft was, a choir of the paid-for, held in frozen amber, and the thing itself was only the frame around the music. Only the debt, given a body. Only winter, doing what winter does.

*Sing, then,* it said, and the words frosted her eyelashes from the inside. *Finish the hymn. We are curious what the last verse sounds like, after fifty years of silence.*

Ada looked at it—at all of it, the hunger and the hoard and the hurt—and felt, beneath the terror, beneath the cold crushing her like a fist, something steady and warm and absolutely certain.

"It sounds like this," she said.

And she opened her arms, and let go of the radio, and began the final verse—the verse about giving, the verse about the bird, the verse that had been waiting inside her since 1974 for exactly this water, exactly this dark, exactly this hour—

—and the frost-thing leaned in to listen, and every forgotten face in the deep turned toward her at once, and somewhere far above, through stone and soil and forty feet of black October water, the town of Hollow Creek sang the last line with her:

*What was borrowed, freely given, / what was kept, set free—*

The water began, very gently, to thaw.
