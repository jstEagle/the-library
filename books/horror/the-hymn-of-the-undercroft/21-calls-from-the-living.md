## 21. Calls From the Living

The board lit up at 11:47, three minutes before sign-off, and Ada almost didn't take it.

She had learned, in the weeks since the water, to be careful with the board. There were still nights when the lines glowed and her stomach dropped the old way—when the red light looked like a wound, when she picked up the handset and braced herself for a voice that had no business having lungs. But the calls had changed. That was the thing she kept having to relearn, like a woman relearning a town after a flood: the water had gone down, and what it left behind was not ruin. It was people.

"Undercroft Hour, you're live."

"Hi, Ada." A woman's voice, warm and slightly hoarse, the way a voice gets when it has been talking all day to children or to God. "It's Ruth. Ruth Calloway. I know it's late, but I made the pie tonight and I wanted to tell somebody before I lost my nerve."

Ada closed her eyes and let the name settle. Ruth, who had called her that first night out of the dark and asked about a fire Ada didn't remember. Ruth, who had been dead for a week when she did it, drowned in her own bathtub with the radio playing, and who had come back at 12:01 on a cold November morning coughing water into a towel while her neighbor screamed and then wept and then put the kettle on, because that was the sort of town Hollow Creek was when it remembered how to be a town at all.

"What kind of pie, Ruth?"

"Apple. But it's not the apples that matter." A pause; Ada could hear her turning something on the counter, a ceramic dish against laminate. "It's the crust. My mother's recipe. You have to use lard, and you have to be mean to it. People are too gentle with pie dough. You have to press it like you mean it or it never holds together."

"Is that a pie crust or a philosophy?"

"Both, honey. Everything worth holding is like that."

Ada laughed, and heard the laugh go out over the airwaves of Hollow Creek, over the houses with their lights on and their windows clear, and thought: *this is what the show was always supposed to be.* Not a séance. Not a lighthouse for the drowned. Just a kitchen with the door open.

She took Ruth through the recipe step by step on the air, because that was the ritual now, the new liturgy. Ruth recited; Ada repeated; somewhere out in the dark a hundred people wrote it down on the backs of receipts and church bulletins and the margins of crossword puzzles. When Ruth got to the part about crimping the edge with her thumb, her voice caught, just slightly.

"My mother's been gone forty years," she said. "I got her face back in September, you know. Out of nowhere, sitting at the diner, and there she was, whole as anything. I cried into my eggs." Another pause. "I don't know what I lost to get her back. I've tried to find the hole where it used to be, and there isn't one. That's the strange part. It's like the thing never existed. But Ada—I remember *missing* it. I remember the shape of the missing, even though the missing is gone. Does that make any sense?"

Ada looked at the empty chair by the window. She kept it there now, had moved it herself one afternoon without knowing why, and had never moved it back. It faced the porch. Sometimes she caught herself looking at it the way you look at a word you've written so many times it stops looking like a word.

"It makes perfect sense," she said. "Go mean with that crust, Ruth."

* * *

They called every night now. That was the arrangement nobody had ever spoken aloud, the family of record assembling itself one call at a time.

There was Ruth, Monday nights, with her recipes and her forty years of stored-up gossip, delivered in the tone of a woman making up for lost time, which, Ada supposed, she was.

There was Eleanor Voss, who had lost a language in 1974—not English, but the Italian her grandmother had sung over the stove, every word of it, gone like a snuffed candle—and who had gotten it back in the water's retreat and now called Tuesdays to sing. Just sing. Neapolitan folk songs, lullabies, once a whole aria she'd learned from a record in 1963, her voice thin and old and absolutely certain of itself. Listeners called in afterward just to say they'd sat in their cars in driveways to hear the end of it.

There was Harold Finch, who had lost his first love—a girl named Delia Marsh, a whole summer of her, 1961, gone from him for sixty years—and who called Wednesdays to tell Ada about Delia in exhaustive, tender detail, as though he were repaying a debt to the memory itself. The way she'd chewed her gum. The way she'd cheated at miniature golf and lied about it with total conviction. "I'm not in love with her," Harold had clarified, the third week, sounding almost offended. "I'm eighty-one. But she was the first person who ever thought I was funny, and a man ought to get to say that out loud somewhere."

There was Sister Agnes, who had lost her name—her given name, the one before the veil—and who had come back from the undercroft's bargain with it restored, and who now signed off every Thursday call with it, softly, like setting down something fragile: *Margaret. My name is Margaret.* The first time she'd said it on air, the station's phone lines had jammed for an hour with people calling in to say thank you, though none of them could say what for.

And there was Danny.

Danny called on Sundays, and Danny was the one Ada had to be careful with, because Danny was still nine years old, and would be nine forever, and had died in a church basement in 1974 with his shoes filled with water, and had come back anyway, because the thing in the deep had unraveled into ordinary cold and its debts had come undone with it. He called from a phone that did not exist. His voice had the particular brightness of a kid who has been given permission to talk as long as he wants.

"Ada! Ada, guess what. I found my baseball cards. Well, not *found* them. But I remembered where I hid them, under the loose board in the garage on Kestrel Street, and I made Marcus drive me—well, he drove past it, I mean, because obviously—and the garage is still there! The board is still loose! Somebody else lives there now and I couldn't—Marcus says I can't dig up a stranger's garage, which is so unfair—"

"Danny. Breathe."

"I'm breathing. I'm always breathing now. It's my favorite thing." A giggle, and Ada pressed her hand flat against the desk and held on. "Did you know breathing has a taste? Nobody tells you. It tastes like cold pennies in the morning."

The town had mostly decided not to ask too many questions about Danny. Hollow Creek had spent a lifetime practicing denial and had gotten very good at it, and now it aimed that skill in a kinder direction. The Kestrel Street family had, in fact, found a rusted cigar box under a loose board that autumn, and had brought it to the station, and Ada had put the cards in a plastic sleeve and kept them in the desk drawer, and every Sunday Danny talked about them like they were the crown jewels, which, Ada thought, they were.

* * *

It was Marcus who noticed the pattern, because Marcus noticed everything now. He had come back up from the undercroft in October missing his grandmother's face, and the town's memories had flooded back around him in November, and hers had returned with them—but he never forgot what it had cost, and he never let Ada forget that she was the one who'd paid the larger bill.

"You're doing it again," he said, one Thursday night after sign-off, standing in the studio doorway with two coffees from the gas station, both terrible, both offered anyway.

"Doing what?"

"Hosting." He handed her a cup. "You're hosting like it's a job. It's not a job, Ada. It's a séance in reverse. Those people aren't calling to be entertained. They're calling because you went down into the water and gave away the one thing you loved most, and now they're trying to fill the hole, one recipe at a time, and you're letting them think it's working."

Ada looked into the coffee. "Isn't it?"

Marcus sat down on the couch—the couch, not the empty chair; he never sat in the empty chair, though he never said why—and considered her for a long moment. He was a heavy, careful man with his grandmother's hands, and when he finally spoke it was gently, which from Marcus was rarer than kindness.

"It's working the way a cast works. It's holding the bone straight while it knits. But you keep the cast on too long, you forget the arm underneath. You've got to feel the ache sometimes, Ada. The ache means it's healing."

"I don't ache," Ada said. "That's the thing. Everyone got their missing piece back and I gave mine away on purpose, and I keep waiting to feel like something's torn out of me, and instead I just feel—" She searched for it. "Light. Unbearably light. Like a room after the furniture's been moved out. Nothing's wrong. There's just all this echo."

Marcus nodded slowly, the way he did when a caller said something true on air and he didn't want to break it.

"My grandmother used to say grief isn't always heavy," he said. "Sometimes grief is just space. You keep expecting the thing to be there, and it isn't, and the not-being has a shape. You learn to live in the shape." He raised his terrible coffee. "So live in the shape. And let the Calloways and the Finches and the Vosses of this world keep bringing you casseroles. That's not a cast. That's a family. You don't take the cast off a family."

* * *

The nights got longer. December came down over Hollow Creek like a held breath, and the town waited—for frost, for the anniversary, for the fifty-year seal that would never come now—and the frost did not come. The windows stayed clear. The church on Alder Street, condemned and empty, grew a wreath on its door anyway, hung by persons unknown, and nobody asked who, and everybody knew.

Ada kept the show going. She had stopped calling it *The Undercroft Hour*, mostly; on the logs it still said that, but on the air she just said, "It's late in Hollow Creek, and I'm Ada, and the lines are open." The callers were living now. All of them. She checked sometimes, quietly, the way you check a scar. All living.

On the third Sunday of December, Danny called early, at nine o'clock, which was unusual.

"Ada, I can't stay long tonight. It's the pageant at the Methodist church—the new one, the one they built after—and Sister Margaret got me a part." His voice was bursting. "I'm the innkeeper. One line. 'There's no room.' Isn't that the funniest thing? Me, of all people, saying there's no room."

Ada laughed until she had to mute her mic. "You're going to be wonderful."

"I'm going to say it *mean*," Danny said solemnly. "Like Ruth's pie crust. You have to mean it or it doesn't hold."

He hung up, and the studio was quiet, and Ada sat in the glow of the ON AIR sign and felt the shape of the space around her, the room with the furniture moved out, and thought that Marcus was right, that this was a family, that families are not the people who don't hurt you but the people who show up after, with casseroles and terrible coffee and one line in a pageant, meaning it.

* * *

It was the Sunday before Christmas when the four notes came.

The show had run long—Eleanor had sung, and then Harold had called in with an addendum about Delia Marsh's gum, and then a woman from the far end of Kestrel Street had wanted to know if anyone else remembered the name of the street they used to call Bellows Road, and Ada had said yes, Bellows Road, it was Bellows Road until 1988, and the woman had wept with gratitude over a street name, and Ada had wept a little too, off-mic, because that was the job now: holding the town's memories up to the light, one at a time, like a woman showing photographs.

At 12:14, past sign-off, one line blinked.

She almost didn't take it. Past sign-off was her time, the dead air she'd earned, the silence she'd learned to live in. But the light blinked, patient, and something in her chest—the shape, the echo, the room with the furniture moved out—turned over like a sleeper.

"Undercroft Hour," she said. "We're past the hour, but I'm here."

Silence. The particular silence of someone breathing close to a mouthpiece.

"Hello?"

And then—softly, uncertainly, the way a child hums when they think no one is listening—four notes.

Ada's whole body knew them before her mind did. Her teeth ached, the old ache, the one from the second caller in October, except it didn't hurt this time. It *ached*, the way Marcus had said, the way a bone aches when the cast comes off and the blood comes back. Four notes, rising, turning, unfinished—the fragment that had started everything, the hymn she'd carried down into the water and sung whole and given away, and here it was again, four notes, hanging in the dark of the studio like frost on glass.

"Who is this?" she whispered.

The humming stopped. A breath. And the caller said, in a voice Ada did not know and knew completely, in a voice like her own but younger, like a voice she'd heard across a porch thirty years ago and every day since without ever once hearing it:

"You kept your piece, Ada."

The line clicked dead.

Ada sat very still. The ON AIR sign hummed. Outside the studio window, the December dark lay over Hollow Creek, clear and frostless and quiet, and the empty chair by the window waited the way it always waited, facing the porch, facing the door, patient as a held breath.

Her chest ached. Sweetly. The way it ached at the first four notes of every song her mother used to sing. The way it ached, she understood now, would always ache, in the room where the furniture had been moved out and the shape of the missing had learned to live.

She reached for the board, and dialed out, and her hand was not shaking at all.

"Marcus," she said when he answered. "Marcus, I need you to come in. I think—" She looked at the window. At the clear, clear glass. "I think something's listening again. And I think, this time, it might be saying thank you."

Outside, on the porch she could not see from here, on the top step, the air had begun—very faintly, very gently, like breath on a cold window—to bloom white.
