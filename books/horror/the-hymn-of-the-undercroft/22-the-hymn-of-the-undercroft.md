## 22. The Hymn of the Undercroft

The last night before winter comes in quiet, the way forgiveness does.

Ada Marchetti stands at the studio window with a cup of tea going cold in her hands, watching Hollow Creek settle into itself like an old dog circling its blanket. The streetlights along Corvair Street—she knows the name now, knows all the names again—throw long amber cones through the early dark, and somewhere down the block the Kowalczyks are arguing cheerfully about the placement of their inflatable snowman, their voices carrying the way voices do in a town that has decided, collectively, to stop being afraid of its own breath.

Six weeks since the water. Six weeks since Marcus hauled her out of the undercroft at 12:01, her lips blue as a hymnal's ribbon, her eyes open and empty and looking at him like he was a stranger he was glad to see.

She remembers none of it. Not the descent, not the singing, not the cold that rose to meet her wearing faces she'd loved. She remembers a chair by the window that she cannot explain. She remembers the shape of a melody at the back of her throat like a stone worn smooth by a river she's never visited. She remembers that she used to have something, the way you remember a room you lived in as a child—not its contents, only its weight, the way it held you, the way leaving it felt like a small death you were too young to name.

The tea has gone cold. She drinks it anyway. It tastes like December.

* * *

The show has changed, and the town has noticed, and the town has decided to love her for it.

'The Undercroft Hour' used to be insomniacs and truckers and the lonely arithmetic of the night—callers who wanted the weather, a song, the sound of another human voice confirming that 2 a.m. was happening to more than one person. It still is all of that. But now there is more, and the more has a texture Ada can only call *warm*, the way a stone stays warm after the fire is out.

Ruth calls on Tuesdays. She talks about her garden, the one she never got to plant, the tomatoes she'd planned for the summer of 1975 that never came. She describes them so vividly—Beefsteaks, she says, the size of your fist, the color of a struck match going out—that Ada has started keeping a notebook of other people's memories, writing them down in a careful hand because she has learned that memory is a thing that can be carried for someone else when they cannot carry it themselves.

"Did I ever tell you about my mother's hands?" Ruth asks tonight, and Ada says no, and Ruth tells her: flour in the creases, a wedding band too loose because she lost weight the year Ruth's father shipped out, a way of pressing dough with the heel of the palm like she was holding the whole kitchen still.

"You'd have liked her," Ruth says.

"I think I did like her," Ada says, and neither of them says what they both know: that Ada never met Ruth's mother, that Ruth herself has been dead for fifty years, that some of the voices on her show belong to people the county has no record of. It doesn't matter. It stopped mattering somewhere around the time the frost retreated and the town woke up remembering its children's middle names, and the five of them—Ruth, and Eleanor, and Sam, and the twins, Delia and June—began calling in not as hauntings but as family.

Eleanor calls on Wednesdays with recipes. Sam calls on Thursdays and reads aloud from the book he never finished writing, a chapter a week, and the listeners have started calling in to argue about the ending. The twins call together on Fridays, finishing each other's sentences the way twins do, and last week they sang a round over the air that made three separate listeners phone in just to say they'd had to pull over, they'd had to sit on the side of Route 9 and let something pass through them, something old and sweet and aching.

And Marcus is there, in the chair beside hers, running the board, his grandmother's face returned to every memory where it belonged. He still doesn't talk much about the night he went down alone, about what the frost took from him before the hymn gave it back. But sometimes, between songs, he hums. Four notes. The same four notes he's hummed his whole life without knowing why, and now he knows why, and he hums them anyway, gently, like setting a flower on a grave.

Ada has never asked him to stop.

* * *

At 11:40 she signs off the hour and leaves the board running, the way she does now on the last night before winter. The station's license covers her until midnight, and past midnight there is only the hum of the transmitter and the dark and whatever the dark wants to send her.

She drives home through streets she knows by heart again. The Frost Diner is open, its windows fogged with the breath of the late shift. Someone has chalked a wreath on the door. A dog trots the median of Main Street with the confidence of a small official. The church—First Methodist, condemned, its undercroft flooded and sealed and, if the town is lucky, sleeping—stands dark at the end of Bellwether Lane, its windows like closed eyes. The town council voted last month to demolish it in the spring. Ada voted yes. She has never told anyone that she also drove out here alone, twice, and sat in her car in the cold and could not say why.

Her porch, when she pulls into the drive, is bare.

And then it is not.

She sees it the way you see a face in the wallpaper—assembling itself out of nothing, out of the ordinary rime of a cold night, out of the breath of the season. Frost, blooming in a slow silver spill across the boards, spreading from the top step outward in feathering branches, in filigree, in—she kneels, her knees finding the cold wood, her breath hanging in front of her like something offered—in a shape.

A small bird.

A wren.

Its tail is a flick of white. Its wing is folded, patient. Its eye, where a knot in the wood darkens the frost, is a single point of deep clear blue, and Ada looks into that point and feels something move through her that has no name in any language she speaks. It is grief. It is gratitude. It is the ache she has carried for six weeks, the ache of the empty chair, the ache of a love with no address—and for the first time it does not hurt. For the first time it feels less like a wound and more like a window, open in winter, letting in the cold and the stars together.

"Oh," she says. Just that. Just the smallest word, the first word, the one you say when you find something you didn't know was lost.

The frost-bird does not move. Frost never moves. But the porch holds the shape the way the town holds its memories now—loosely, gratefully, with both hands—and Ada sits down on the top step in her coat and her cold-bitten boots and she stays, because some visits you do not cut short.

* * *

She hums before she knows she is humming.

It starts low, in the place behind her sternum where the melody has lived since the night she came up out of the water with her mind scoured clean. Four notes first—Marcus's four notes, the seed of it, the fragment that made her teeth ache that first October—and then, without her permission, without her memory, the rest unspools. The phrase Ruth used to hum, the one that climbed and turned like smoke finding a draft. The twins' round, the interlocking parts. Sam's descending line, the one he said was the sound of the water itself. And then the final verse, the one she sang down there in the dark, the one she does not remember singing, the one her body remembers the way a riverbed remembers rain.

She does not know where she learned it. She will never know where she learned it. That is the shape of her life now: a hymn with no first page, a love with no photograph, an empty chair by a window that she dusts every Sunday and sits in sometimes with her tea, facing the yard, facing the street, facing whatever comes up the walk.

The melody comes out of her into the cold and the cold takes it, and she would swear—not as a radio host, not as a woman who deals in voices and knows all their tricks, but as the plain fact of her own ears—that the night takes it up. That the wind moving through the bare oak carries it a half-second behind her, the way a round is sung, the way the twins sang it, the way a call is answered.

The frost-bird's blue eye holds the porch light.

"Whoever you were," she says to it, quietly, the way you speak to a sleeping child, "I hope it was worth it. I hope wherever you are, it's warm."

She does not know who she is talking to. That is the mercy and the wound of it, braided together so tightly she can no longer tell which strand is which. She knows only that there was someone. That the someone was small and bright and hers, that she gave him away with both hands, that she would do it again—that she *did* do it again, every night, in the only way left to her, by living in a town that remembers, by hosting a show where the dead call in with recipes, by keeping a chair by the window for a reason she will never write down because she does not have the reason, only the chair.

The frost begins to fade. Not melting—*fading*, the way breath fades from glass, withdrawing into the wood grain, the bird dissolving tail-first, wing-first, until only the eye remains, and then only the faintest blue ghost of it, and then only cold wood under a cold sky.

Ada stays a while longer anyway. Old habit. Or new one. She can't tell the difference anymore, and has stopped trying.

* * *

Inside, the house smells like cedar and the last of the coffee. She passes the chair by the window—she always passes it slowly, the way you pass a door that used to open onto a room that isn't there anymore—and she does not stop tonight. Tonight she has somewhere to be.

The drive back to the station takes eleven minutes. The town is mostly asleep, but not all of it. The Frost Diner's windows still glow. A light burns in the Kowalczyks' upstairs window. And on the corner of Main and Corvair, where the old payphone used to stand before the county removed it—a payphone that a nine-year-old boy once called her from, a boy dead fifty years with a voice like a small bell—someone has installed a bench. The town council approved it in November, no discussion, no one able to say afterward whose idea it was. It faces the church.

Ada parks behind the station and lets herself in. Marcus is already there, of course. He is always there on this night. They have never discussed why. They have never needed to.

"Ruth called," he says. "Wanted me to tell you she's making the tomato thing. The one her mother made. She's bringing it Sunday."

"Tell her I'll bring the bread."

"Delia and June want to do the round again. The full version. They say the listeners are ready."

"They're right. The listeners are ready."

He looks at her then, across the dark of the studio, across the board with its little lights like a town seen from the air, and his face does the thing it does on this night every year now—the thing where he almost says something and then decides, with visible kindness, to let the silence hold it instead. But this year, at the last moment, he doesn't let it go.

"Ada," he says. "The porch. Tonight. Did it—"

"Bird," she says.

He closes his eyes. When he opens them they are wet, and he is smiling, and he does not wipe them. "Yeah," he says. "Yeah, it was a bird."

They don't say the name. There is no name. That is the whole of it, and the whole of it is enough.

* * *

At 11:58 she settles into her chair, pulls the microphone close, and watches the clock's second hand climb. Outside the studio window the first true snow of the year is beginning—not frost now, not the other thing, just snow, honest and slow, the kind that means the season has arrived the ordinary way, through the sky, like a letter.

The red light comes on. ON AIR.

"Good evening, Hollow Creek. It's the last night before winter, and you're listening to The Undercroft Hour. I'm Ada Marchetti, and I'll be here as long as you need me."

She lets the silence breathe. She has learned that from the dead: the value of a held silence, a room made safe enough for whatever wants to come next.

"The phones are open. Ruth, I know you're out there. Eleanor, save me the recipe. Sam—don't give away the ending, we've talked about this. Delia, June—warm up. And Marcus is here, and he's humming, and I'm not going to stop him."

Beside her, softly, Marcus laughs.

"Something happened on my porch tonight," she says, and her voice does not break, though it comes close, and the coming-close is its own kind of music. "I can't tell you what. I don't have the words for it, and maybe that's all right. Maybe some things aren't meant to be remembered. Maybe they're just meant to be *kept*—the way you keep a song without knowing where you learned it. The way you keep a chair for someone who's already home."

The snow thickens against the window. Somewhere in the town, five voices are leaning toward their radios, and a hundred living ones besides, and the transmitter is pushing her voice out into the dark over the rooftops and the frozen fields and the sealed and sleeping water beneath the old church, where the cold is only cold now, ordinary and honest, dreaming whatever water dreams.

"So here's what we're going to do," Ada says. "We're going to do what we always do. We're going to sit up together and keep the light on. And before the hour's out, I'm going to sing you something. I don't know where I learned it. I don't know what it's called. But I know every note, the way you know the shape of a hand you held once, a long time ago, in a place you can't find on any map."

She looks at the window. The snow is coming down like a blessing, like static, like applause.

"And this one's for whoever's listening," she says, "wherever you are."

She hums the first four notes.

And from somewhere—up the line, down the dial, out of the snow and the dark and the warm lit windows of the town that remembers—someone begins to hum them back.
