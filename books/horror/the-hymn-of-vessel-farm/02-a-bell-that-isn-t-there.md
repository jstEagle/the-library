## 2. A Bell That Isn't There

The bell woke her at three o'clock precisely.

Marguerite Fairweather had kept a nurse's relationship with the small hours for eleven years—she knew them the way a farmer knows weather, by texture and weight. The hour between three and four was the honest one. Pain stopped performing. The dying stopped being polite. Whatever a body truly meant to say, it said then, in the dark, when no one was awake to be reassured. She had learned to sleep with one ear open like a door on its latch, and so she was already half-risen in her narrow bed when the sound came through the frost-feathered glass of her cottage window.

It was not a sound she could name at first. It arrived as vibration before it arrived as tone—a pressure against the sternum, a shiver in the water glass on her nightstand that drew thin rings across its surface, ring after ring after ring, like something enormous turning over beneath the floor of the world. Then the tone resolved, and it was bronze, and it was deep, and it was unmistakably a church bell.

She sat up. The cold of the room climbed under the blankets and found her spine.

The bell tolled again. A long, unhurried note, swelling from nothing into a fullness that seemed to press the walls gently outward, then decaying into a hum that lingered far longer than any bell she'd ever heard—as though the air itself had been asked to remember the note and was reluctant to let it go. Then silence, long enough that she thought it finished. Then another toll.

Three o'clock. Three tolls? No—the count didn't match. She lay still and counted, because counting was what she did; counting was the whole architecture of her life at Vessel Farm. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen. Seventeen. Eighteen. Nineteen. Twenty. Twenty-one. Twenty-two. Twenty-three. Twenty-four. Twenty-five. Twenty-six. Twenty-seven. Twenty-eight. Twenty-nine. Thirty. Thirty-one. Thirty-two. Thirty-three. Thirty-four. Thirty-five. Thirty-six. Thirty-seven. Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine. Forty.

Forty tolls.

The last one faded so slowly that Marguerite, sitting upright in the dark with her arms wrapped around her knees, felt it pass through her body rather than past it, the way you feel a train through the ground before you hear it. Then the ordinary sounds of the farm resumed—the tick of the stove cooling, the wind worrying the ivy against the east wall—and the night was only a night again.

Except that Vessel Farm stood four miles from the village, and the village's church, St. Brannoc-under-the-Hill, had no bell.

She knew this the way she knew the location of every linen closet and the dosage chart for every resident: thoroughly, without thinking about it. There was no bell tower on St. Brannoc's squat stone stump of a steeple. There was a flat cap where a belfry should have been, and pigeons, and a weathervane bent into an attitude of permanent apology. She had walked past it a hundred times on her rare trips down for supplies. She had never once questioned the absence. Churches without bells were simply churches that had fallen on quieter times.

But a bell had rung. Forty times. And the water glass on her nightstand still held the ghost of it, a faint trembling at its rim that would not settle, as if the glass were listening too.

Marguerite got up. She did this without deciding to, which frightened her more than the sound itself. Her body moved toward the window the way her hands moved toward a patient in distress—by older instructions than thought. She pressed her palm to the cold pane and looked out over the grounds.

The lawn lay silvered under a moon thinned by high cloud. The manor rose black against the sky, its many windows dark except one on the third floor, where a candle burned behind glass that always burned there—Odile Vessel's window, the old woman's nightlight, burning as it had burned every night since Marguerite had come to the farm, and, according to Pell, for decades before. Beyond the manor, the fields went down toward the lane and the wall and the gate that the residents' families had long since stopped visiting. Beyond the wall, the village slept. Beyond the village, the hills.

Nothing moved. Nothing rang. The night was a held breath.

And yet the frost on the inside of the window, where her breath touched it, had melted in a perfect circle around her hand, and within that circle the glass was warm.

* * *

In the morning, Marguerite did what she always did with things that did not fit: she wrote them down.

Her ledger lived in the bottom drawer of the nurses' desk in the east corridor, beneath the medication log and the visitors' book that hadn't been signed in two years. It was a plain ledger, cloth-bound, its corners soft with handling. Eleven years of deaths, recorded in her own careful hand: name, date, hour, the number each soul had counted down from, the number they'd reached when they let go. Always zero. They always reached zero. She had never once seen a resident stop counting partway, never seen one refuse, never seen one die mid-number. They finished. Whatever the counting was—prayer, arithmetic, surrender—it was completed work, and she honored it by writing it down.

*Edwin Hallow, admitted the 4th of October. Sleeps poorly. Speaks of his mother's kitchen as though he is standing in it.*

That was the entry she'd made yesterday evening, and beneath it, in the margin where she sometimes noted oddities:

*3:00 a.m. Bell heard. Forty tolls. No bell exists.*

She read the line back and felt the strange vertigo of seeing her own handwriting assert something impossible. Ink was supposed to make things true. That was why she kept the ledger—that was the private faith of it, the devotion no one had ever asked her for. The residents came to Vessel Farm to die, and the world outside had forgotten them before they'd even finished arriving, and so Marguerite wrote them down so that someone, somewhere, in some future drawer or attic box, would know they had been here, had counted, had ended cleanly at zero. Documentation as prayer. She had never used those words even in her own head, but the shape of it was that. Each entry a small candle lit in a church with no congregation.

Now the ink said *no bell exists*, and the ink sounded, for the first time in eleven years, like a lie.

She closed the drawer and went to find Pell.

* * *

She found him in the potting shed behind the walled garden, doing something patient and violent to a rosebush with a pair of shears. Pell was seventy if he was a day, built like something grown rather than assembled—all knuckle and gristle and weathered brown skin—and he had tended the grounds of Vessel Farm for longer than anyone could remember, including, Marguerite suspected, Pell himself. He spoke perhaps forty words a day and spent most of them on plants.

"Pell," she said. "Did you hear anything in the night?"

The shears paused. Not stopped—paused, the way a clock pauses between ticks, and Marguerite noticed the pause because she had spent eleven years learning to notice the pauses in dying men's breathing, and this pause had the same quality. Something listening underneath.

"Wind," Pell said.

"It wasn't wind." She stepped into the shed. It smelled of loam and copper and the sweet rot of fallen apples. "It was a bell. Church bell. It rang forty times at three o'clock."

The shears hung motionless in the air. Pell's back was to her, broad and stooped, and she watched the tendons of his neck move once, as if he were swallowing something large.

"There's no bell," he said.

"I know there's no bell. I'm telling you I heard one."

"Then you heard wind."

"Pell." She kept her voice level, the voice she used at bedsides. "St. Brannoc's has no bell. I know that. But I also know that a bell rang last night, forty times, and I would like very much for you to tell me why the village church doesn't have one, because in eleven years I have never once wondered, and now I can't stop wondering, and that frightens me."

The shears came down slowly. Pell turned. His face was the face of all his weather at once—sun and frost and long rain—and his eyes, pale as pond water, settled on her with an expression she had never seen him wear. It took her a moment to identify it, because it belonged to a different category of person entirely. It was the look of a man deciding how much truth a woman can carry.

He decided, apparently, on a spoonful.

"Melted down," he said. "Nineteen-eleven. Before either of us was born, near enough. The parish sold the bell to pay for the farm. This place." He nodded vaguely upward, toward the house, the grounds, the whole gray organism of Vessel Farm. "Wasn't a hospice then. Was a convalescent home. Tuberculosis, mostly. Rich folk's charity. They needed money for beds and linen and doctors, and the bell was the only thing the church owned worth anything, so down it came, and off it went to the foundry at Marleigh, and that was that." He turned back to the rosebush. "No bell. Never been a bell. You dreamed it."

"I don't dream at three o'clock, Pell. I'm usually awake. I'm usually *working*."

"Then you heard wind in the chimney."

"The chimney doesn't ring forty times. The chimney doesn't hold a note for ten seconds. The chimney doesn't leave my water glass ringing until dawn." She heard her own voice climb and brought it down. "Forty times, Pell. I counted."

Something happened to his shoulders then. It was very small—a settling, a lowering, like a load shifting on a cart—but she saw it, because she saw everything; noticing was the entire craft of her life. He began cutting the rosebush again, harder than the bush deserved, and he said, to the plant rather than to her:

"You counted."

"Yes."

"Don't count it again."

"What?"

"If it comes back—" The shears stopped. He stood very still among the green smell and the copper smell, and when he spoke again his voice had gone somewhere old and tired, a room in his voice she had never been shown. "If it comes back, Miss Fairweather, you put your head under the blanket and you go to sleep, and in the morning you write in your little book whatever makes the day easiest. Wind. Owls. The milk cart on the lane. Write anything but the number."

"Why?"

He didn't answer. He cut the rosebush down to a fist of thorns and swept the fallen stems into a barrow, and Marguerite understood that the audience was over. She left him to it. At the door of the shed she turned, and found him watching her—not his face, just his eyes over his shoulder, quick as a bird's—and there was something in the look that she would turn over for days afterward without resolving it. It wasn't warning, exactly. It was closer to pity, and pity, from Pell, from a man who had never once pitied anyone aloud, was the most frightening thing the morning produced.

* * *

At breakfast, she asked the others.

Breakfast at Vessel Farm was a ritual of gentle deceptions: real china, real butter, conversation steered away from death and toward the garden, the weather, the scandalous price of coal. The residents who could come down to the morning room did—five of them, of nine, wheelchairs and walking frames arranged around the long table like a fleet at anchor. Mrs. Tansy, who was ninety-three and sharp as a hatpin. Mr. Lovelace, who had been a schoolmaster and still corrected people's grammar mid-sentence, including Odile's, which no one else dared. Sister Ruth, retired, who knitted through meals. Edwin Hallow, gray and quiet, eating almost nothing. And at the head, in her high-backed chair that might have been a throne in a poorer house, Odile Vessel herself, ninety-some years folded into silk and lace, hands like paper lanterns around a cup of tea she never drank.

It was Odile's table, Odile's house, Odile's morning. Everything at Vessel Farm orbited the old woman the way the rooms orbited the stairwell, and Marguerite had long ago accepted that she worked in a solar system with a fixed center.

"A curious thing," Marguerite said, pouring tea, keeping it light, keeping it nothing. "I thought I heard the church bell in the night. From the village. Did anyone else hear it?"

The silence lasted two seconds. She counted them; she couldn't help it.

Mrs. Tansy frowned prettily. "Bell? There's no bell, dear. Haven't been for years."

"That's what I thought. But I was sure I heard—"

"Wind in the chimneys," said Mr. Lovelace. "This time of year. The flues moan terribly. I've complained to Pell about it a dozen times."

"You've never complained about anything in your life, Mr. Lovelace."

"I complain constantly. You simply haven't been listening." He smiled his schoolmaster's smile and ate his toast, and the smile was warm and entirely closed, like a door painted on a wall.

Sister Ruth's needles clicked. Clicked. Stopped. "There's no bell at St. Brannoc's," she said, to her knitting. "There hasn't been since before the war. The first war." Click. Click. "They melted it."

"For the farm," Marguerite said.

Sister Ruth's needles stopped again. For a moment the old nun looked up, and her eyes—mild, watery, lifelong-forgiving eyes—did something Marguerite had never seen eyes do: they looked at Odile's chair before they answered, a flicker, a glance, fast as a tongue checking a broken tooth, and then came back and were mild again.

"For charity," Sister Ruth said. "For good works. It was a long time ago, dear. Nobody remembers."

"Someone must remember. Someone poured the metal. Someone carried it down from the tower. That's a hundred men's work at least."

"Nineteen-eleven," said Odile Vessel.

Everyone stopped. Even the fire in the grate seemed to lean in. The matriarch had not spoken since Marguerite entered the room; she rarely spoke before her second cup of tea, and she never drank the second cup of tea, and no one had ever remarked on this arrangement aloud. Now she set down the untouched first cup with a click of porcelain that sounded, in the hush, exactly like a single small toll.

"They melted the bell in nineteen-eleven," Odile said. Her voice was dry silk drawn over something with edges. "I watched them take it down. I was a girl. It made a sound coming out of the tower—not ringing. More like..." She considered, and her ancient face did something terrible and brief, something that on a younger face would have been called grief. "...like a throat clearing. After that, quiet. We've been a quiet parish ever since." She lifted her milky gaze to Marguerite, and smiled, and the smile was kind, and kindness from Odile Vessel had always made the hairs on Marguerite's forearms rise, though she had never let herself wonder why. "You heard wind, Nurse Fairweather. This house is full of old throats. They clear themselves at night."

She drank her tea. First sip of the morning. The room exhaled, and breakfast resumed, and the conversation flowed on to coal prices as though a bell had never been mentioned, as though a bell had never existed, as though the absence of a bell were the most natural thing in the world to defend so unanimously.

Marguerite looked down the table at her patients—at Mrs. Tansy smoothing her napkin, at Lovelace buttering toast, at Edwin Hallow pushing an egg around a plate with the gray patience of a man waiting for something—and understood, with a coldness that started in her teeth, that none of them were lying.

They believed it. Every one of them believed there had never been a bell, or had agreed to believe it so long ago that belief and agreement had become indistinguishable. The way you can agree, over years, to call a scar a birthmark.

* * *

That night she did not sleep. She told herself it was the tea, and then she told herself it was the season changing, and then she stopped telling herself things, because lying to yourself in your own head at two in the morning is a poor craft and she was a fine craftsman of other kinds.

She sat at her kitchen table with the ledger open under the lamp, and she read eleven years of entries, and she waited.

The house creaked. The stove ticked. Upstairs in the manor, nine souls breathed in nine rooms, and one of them, in the blue room at the end of the east wing, breathed shallowly and slept badly and spoke of his mother's kitchen. Outside, October stripped the chestnuts along the drive leaf by leaf, patiently, like a clerk removing papers from a file.

At three o'clock, the bell rang.

Not forty times. She counted through the window with the lamp off and her lips moving, and the tolls came slow and vast and patient, each one swelling out of the ground itself, it seemed, out of the roots of the hill, out of some iron memory buried deeper than the village, deeper than the church, deeper than the parish's tidy amnesia—

Thirty-nine. Forty. Forty-one.

Forty-one.

The last note hung in the frozen air for a long time, longer than the others, and Marguerite stood at her window with her breath smoking on the glass and her heart going hard and stupid in her chest, and she understood two things with perfect clarity, the way you understand things at three in the morning, when the mind's usual furniture has been carried out of the room.

The first was that the bell was counting. It was counting the way her patients counted—one number per night, backwards, downward, toward something. Last night forty. Tonight thirty-nine. Tonight forty-one. No—she pressed her hands to her temples—last night forty, tonight forty-*one*. Not descending. Ascending. Growing. As if something were being added to. As if a debt were accruing interest, or a ledger were gaining entries, or a grave were getting deeper by exactly one measure per night.

The second thing she understood was that tomorrow she would write it down. *Bell heard. Forty-one tolls.* Because writing things down was what she did; because the ink was her candle and the page was her altar and eleven years of faithful entries had never once been answered, but she had never stopped believing that somewhere, somehow, the record mattered—that somebody or something kept accounts too, and that her small honest book balanced against it, however faintly, the way a single lit window balances against a whole dark countryside.

She opened the ledger. Her pen hovered over the fresh line.

And in the silence after the forty-first toll, from the direction of the manor—from the third floor, from behind the one window that always burned—came a sound so faint she would spend the rest of her life unsure whether she had truly heard it or whether the night had finally gotten inside her.

Humming. An old woman's humming, cracked and tuneless and patient.

Holding the bell's note. Perfectly. Like a throat that had learned, a century ago, how to keep a sound alive.

Marguerite wrote: *3:00 a.m. Bell heard. Forty-one tolls. No bell exists.*

Then, after a long moment, her hand moving as if it belonged to someone braver, she added a second line, and the pen scratched in the quiet like a small animal digging:

*But something remembers the note.*
