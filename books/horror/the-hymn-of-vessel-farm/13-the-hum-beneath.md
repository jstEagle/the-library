## 13. The Hum Beneath

The pantry at Vessel Farm had always been colder than the rest of the house, and I had always assumed this was a virtue of its architecture—the thick north wall, the flagstones laid over clay, the shelves of preserves that liked their dark and chill the way the dying liked their quiet rooms. For four years I had gone into that pantry every morning for the honey Odile took in her tea, every evening for the salted plums old Mrs. Aldery ate to keep her appetite from leaving entirely, and never once—not once—had I noticed that the room was deeper than it should have been.

It was Iris who found it, though she didn't know she had.

She had been drawing again. The bell, still. She filled page after page with it now, the great bronze shoulder of it, the crack running down through its crown like a vein of lightning caught mid-strike and made permanent. She drew it from angles no child could know: from above, as if hovering; from inside, looking out through the mouth of it at a sky full of watching faces. I had taken to collecting the drawings quietly when she wasn't looking, folding them into the back of my ledger, because I could not bring myself to throw them away and could not bear to look at them either.

That morning she came to me in the kitchen corridor with her fists sticky with jam and said, "Mummy, the humming's louder by the jam."

"The what?"

"The humming." She said it the way children say things they believe everyone already knows, with a small impatience at having to say them aloud at all. "Under the floor. It's loudest where the jam is."

I knelt down to her level. Her eyes were her father's eyes, grey-green, unbothered, and that was somehow the worst part—that whatever she was hearing, she heard it without fear, the way you might hear a lorry on a distant road or rain against glass. A fact of the world. Furniture of the universe.

"Where did you first hear it?" I asked.

She considered. "Before," she said, which was her word for everything that happened before last week, before the bell began ringing in the nights, before Edwin Hallow started counting down from forty like a man reading his own eulogy one number at a time. Before was a country she remembered better than I did. Before was the year I could not find.

"Show me," I said.

* * *

The pantry smelled of beeswax and cold stone and, beneath those, something older—a smell I could only describe as earth that has been turned recently, though nothing here had been disturbed in living memory. Iris led me past the shelving to the far corner, where the wall met the floor in a skirt of shadow, and pointed at the baseboard with the solemn authority of a small physician indicating an organ she has only ever seen in diagrams.

"There," she said. "Listen with your knees."

I did not understand what she meant until I obeyed her. I lowered myself to the flagstones, pressed my palms flat against them, and felt it before I heard it: a vibration, faint and continuous, rising up through the stone and into my wrists and along the bones of my forearms like water finding its way through a wall. Not machinery. Not pipes. Pipes pulse; pumps stutter. This was steady, sustained, patient—a single held note that had been holding itself so long it had worn smooth all the places it touched.

I put my ear to the floor.

And there it was. Humming. Low, round, resonant, pitched somewhere below the range of comfortable listening, the kind of sound you feel in your sternum more than your ears. I knew the note. God help me, I knew it instantly, the way you know your own name called from another room. It was the bell. The bell that did not exist, melted down in 1911, rung each night at three by no hand and hung in no tower—it was humming under the floor of my pantry, sustained, unbroken, as if it had never stopped ringing at all but had only learned to ring inwardly, the way a struck tuning fork keeps singing in the hand long after the ear gives up on it.

"Mummy," Iris said, crouching beside me, her voice dropping to match the register of the thing below us, "the lady hums it so it doesn't forget how."

I looked at her. "What lady?"

But she had already lost interest, or appeared to; she wandered back toward the light of the doorway, licking jam from her thumb, and left me kneeling on the cold stones with my heart going hard and fast and the note rising through my kneecaps like a second pulse that did not care whether mine continued or not.

I should have stood up then. I should have walked out of that pantry and taken my daughter by the hand and walked out of the farm altogether, papers be damned, ledger be damned, Odile's soft voice and Pell's sixty years of silence be damned. Any mother in any sane story would have.

Instead I stayed on my knees and began, methodically, horribly, to look for the door.

* * *

Because there had to be a door. The humming had a direction—I understood that now, once I let myself understand anything at all. It rose strongest from the corner behind the preserve shelves, the oldest shelves, the ones built into the wall itself rather than set against it, oak so dark with age it looked cut from night. I emptied them. Jar by jar I carried the preserves to the kitchen counter—quince jelly, damson cheese, pickled walnuts, jars labeled in hands that were not mine and dates that went back further than any hospice kitchen needed—and when the shelves stood bare I ran my fingers along the back board and found, near the bottom, where the wood met the stone, a seam.

Not a crack. A seam. Cut deliberately, dressed with mortar that was newer than the mortar around it, mortared over with a care that suggested someone had wanted the wall to look whole to anyone who glanced and blank to anyone who looked twice. I got the kitchen knife and worked it into the seam, and the mortar crumbled away in gray ribbons, and behind it the stones were not stones at all but a wooden hatch, iron-banded, its handle a ring of black metal polished bright—as if by use, though surely not recent use, surely not—

The ring was warm.

Everything else in that pantry was cold enough to ache. The ring fit my hand like something that had been waiting for exactly the shape of my grip, and when I pulled, the hatch swung open on hinges that made no sound whatsoever, which frightened me more than a scream would have. Soundless hinges are maintained hinges. Maintained hinges mean visits. Visits mean someone has been going down.

Cold air breathed up out of the dark, and with it the smell—turned earth again, and bronze, and something sweetish underneath that I refused, for the moment, to name. And the humming, no longer muffled by floor and joist and shelf, rose up around me entire. It filled the stairwell the way water fills a well shaft. Standing at the top of those steps with the hatch open, I felt less like a woman discovering a cellar and more like a head leaning over the rim of a throat, listening to the voice in the chest below.

I am a nurse. I want that recorded plainly, in whatever part of me is still keeping records: I knew what I was hearing. I had spent years at bedsides learning the difference between the sounds a body makes when it is at peace and the sounds it makes when something is wrong inside it—when breath must be pulled through fluid, when the heart works against its own failing valves, when the throat must shape around an obstruction. The hum beneath Vessel Farm was not the sound of a thing at rest. It was the sound of a thing being held. Sustained. Kept. The way you keep a note. The way you keep a patient.

The way you keep a debt accruing.

I went back for the lamp.

* * *

I told myself I was being professional. That is the sentence I reached for, and I want to be honest about reaching for it, because honesty is becoming the only currency I trust. I told myself: you are the keeper of this place's dying, and a nurse investigates what threatens her patients. Edwin Hallow was at thirty-one. Mrs. Aldery at nineteen. Whatever was under the pantry was connected to the counting—I felt that connection in my wrists, in my teeth, in the way the numbers had begun to arrive in my head unbidden, in order, like stations on a line. A nurse investigates.

But the truth is smaller and worse. The truth is that the humming had gotten into me. That since Iris said *the lady hums it*, some part of me had been leaning toward that sound the way the dying lean toward the window at dusk. The farm had chosen its moment, or I had, and the distinction no longer mattered.

The stairs went down farther than a cellar's stairs should. Twelve steps would have been right for the depth of the house; I counted twenty-two, then lost count, then counted again and got thirty, and stopped counting because the number was beginning to feel like a joke at my expense. The walls sweated. The lamp threw my shadow huge and wavering ahead of me, and the shadow descended faster than I did, always a step or two below, as if it knew the way and was impatient.

At the bottom, the passage opened into a low vaulted space, and I raised the lamp, and I understood why the pantry was cold.

The room was a root cellar the way a cathedral is a barn. Earth floor, packed and dark and damp, and out of that floor—half-buried, half-emerged, like something surfacing from sleep or sinking into it—lay the bell.

Not a model of it. Not a fragment. The bell itself, the great bell of the parish, the one the records said had been broken up and sold by the pound in 1911 to fund the building of the farm—here, whole, or whole enough, lying on its side in a cradle of soil as if the earth had grown around it the way a tree grows around a fence wire. Bronze greened to the color of deep water. The crown cracked, just as Iris drew it, just as she drew it, the fissure running down through the metal in a line too clean to be accident, a line like a signature. And from the crack—from within the bell, from the hollow dark of its mouth—came the humming.

I stood at the edge of the lamplight with my hand over my mouth and watched the soil around the bell move. Not much. A tremor, rhythmic, in time with the note. The earth itself breathing with it, or being breathed.

And ringing the bell, spaced evenly in a circle, were the graves.

Small ones. Forty-one of them—I counted twice, needing the number to be wrong, needing it to be anything other than the number it was. Forty-one low mounds, each no longer than a coffin for a child, each marked not with stone or cross but with a wooden peg driven into the soil, and on each peg, a strip of lead. Blank strips. Every one of them blank, as if names had been written there once and scraped away, or as if the names had never been written anywhere at all, which was worse, which was so much worse, because I had begun by then to understand that at Vessel Farm the unwritten was not merely forgotten.

The unwritten was kept.

The humming swelled—or seemed to swell; perhaps it was only my blood, my terror, my lamp-hand shaking—and in the swell I heard what I had refused to hear on the stairs: voices in it. Not words. Threads. Like a hymn heard through a wall, each voice a strand, and the strands braided into the single note, and the note held, and held, and held, the way a held breath holds, the way a held door holds, the way a held debt holds against the day it comes due.

Forty-one graves. Forty-one entries in a ledger in my handwriting that I never wrote. Forty-one souls the farm had harvested, one per year since the year the bell was poured and broken and buried, and every one of them down here, under the jam and the quince jelly and the ordinary cold, sustaining the note that kept the residents of Vessel Farm alive past all decency, past all arithmetic, past the honest end of every life that should have closed like a book.

The lady under the floor. My daughter had known. Children always know; it is the mothers who are kept from knowing, and I suddenly understood with a clarity that felt like a blade laid flat against the skin that the smoothness in my memory, the missing year, the featureless gap where a life should have been—that smoothness was not absence. It was maintenance. Someone had sanded me down the way the hatch had been mortared over, carefully, lovingly, so that I would look whole to anyone who glanced and blank to anyone who looked twice.

The lamp guttered. In the stutter of its flame the bell seemed to roll a fraction closer, though I knew—I told myself I knew—it had not moved.

And then, beneath the hum, distinct for the first time, threaded through the braided voices like a silver wire through rope: a new voice. Thin. Familiar. Counting.

*"Thirty."*

Edwin Hallow's voice, rising out of the earth of the cellar floor, out of the mouths of the dead, out of the bell's held note—one number, clear as a coin dropped in a well, and the hum gathered it in and carried it upward through thirty feet of stone and soil and oak shelving and jars of damson cheese, up into the house above, where I knew—I knew with the certainty of the documented—that every resident of Vessel Farm had heard it in their sleep, and smiled.

The lamp steadied. The hum resumed its patience. It could afford patience. It had been paying attention for a hundred years.

I climbed the stairs with my legs shaking and my mind doing the one thing it knew how to do, the thing Father Anselm would later tell me was the only thing standing between this house and honesty: I was writing it down. Even as I climbed, even as I pushed the hatch shut and crumbled the loose mortar back into the seam with fingers that would not stop trembling, some clerk in the back office of my soul was entering the record. *Cellar discovered behind pantry. Bell extant, interred, humming. Forty-one graves, unnamed. Voice audible: E.H., thirty.*

Thirty.

Three nights ago he had been thirty-two. The count was accelerating. And somewhere above me, in a room that smelled of lavender and old paper, an ancient woman was sharpening a pen for a signing, and the date she had chosen was coming toward us all like the last number in a countdown nobody had agreed to.

Behind the sealed hatch, muffled now, gentle as a mother at a cradle, the lady under the floor went on humming the bell's note—holding it, holding it, holding it—for the house, for the harvest, for me.

And I realized, standing in the dark of my pantry with the knife still in my pocket, that I had left the lamp burning down there.

I had left it burning, and from below, faint and unhurried, came the sound of footsteps crossing the earth.
