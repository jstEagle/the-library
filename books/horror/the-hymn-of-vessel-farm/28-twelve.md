## 28. Twelve

Twelve.

Edwin Hallow said it the way other men said *good morning*—settled into it, comfortable as an old coat. He was propped against his pillows with the window cracked to let in the grey of the October morning, and his hands were folded on the coverlet like a man waiting for a train he had long since made peace with catching.

Marguerite stood in the doorway with her ledger under her arm and did not go in at once. There had been a time, only weeks ago, when entering a patient's room was the simplest act of her day. Now every threshold felt like a page she might smudge.

"You're up early," she said.

"I'm always up early now." He smiled. His face had thinned since the counting began, but not in the way dying usually thinned a man. Edwin was not wasting. He was *condensing*, the way water condenses on cold glass—gathering himself toward some final, deliberate shape. "The nights are loud."

"The bell," Marguerite said. It was not a question. For eleven nights the bell had tolled his number at three o'clock exactly, and for eleven nights the whole household had heard it—not just her, not anymore. Last night, when it rang seventeen times, old Mrs. Aldous had stood at her window in her nightdress with her hands pressed flat to the glass, and when Marguerite asked her what she was doing, Mrs. Aldous had answered without turning around: *Listening to my turn come.*

"Sit down, Nurse Fairweather." Edwin patted the edge of his bed. "I want to talk to you properly, while I still have the words for it. They get slippery near the bottom. Like coins dropped in deep water—you can see them shining down there, but your fingers won't close on them."

She sat. The ledger rested on her knees, and she became aware of its weight in a new way—as though it were not paper and board but something with a pulse.

"Ask me what my number is," Edwin said.

"Twelve," she said. "You've told me every morning for eleven mornings."

"No." His eyes were bright and terribly clear. "Ask me what my number *is*. Not how many are left. What it means."

Marguerite looked at him. Outside the cracked window, the grounds were silvered with frost, and the elms stood bare as ribs. Somewhere below them, beneath the pantry floor, beneath the sealed stair she had found and then pretended she hadn't, something hummed its single sustained note—the bell's note, the note that never stopped, that she had learned to hear the way you learn to hear your own blood.

"It's a death sentence," she said slowly. "A countdown. You count backwards, one number a night, and when you reach zero—"

"—I die. Yes. That part's true." He turned his head against the pillow to look at her fully. "But you've got the shape of it wrong, Marguerite. A sentence is something done *to* you. This isn't done to me. I chose it. We all chose it. Every soul in this house who ever counted from forty—they weren't being executed." He smiled again, and there was such gentleness in it that her chest ached. "We were paying."

"Paying," she repeated. The word landed in her stomach like a stone dropped down a well.

"A payment schedule." He said it the way a bank clerk might say it. Matter-of-fact. Almost kind. "Forty installments. One per night. And when the last payment clears—" he lifted one hand from the coverlet and let it fall, a small soft slap of skin on wool, "—the farm calls in the balance."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a boat tilts when someone shifts their weight. Marguerite gripped the ledger.

"Calls in the balance," she said. "What balance? Edwin, what do you owe?"

"That's the beautiful part." He laughed—a dry, papery sound, but genuine. "That's the part I couldn't understand either, back when I signed. You don't know what you owe until they collect it. Because what you owe isn't money, is it. It isn't years, exactly, either. It's—" He searched, his brow furrowing, and for a moment he looked like any confused old man in any hospice bed in England. Then his face cleared. "It's the difference. Between the life you were given and the life you kept. Nobody gets to stay past their number for free. The farm keeps us breathing, and breathing past your time costs, and the cost comes due all at once, at zero, and the bell rings it out so everyone knows the account's been settled."

"Settled with *what*?" Her voice came out sharper than she intended. She softened it. "Edwin. Please."

"With everything the extra time was made of." He said it simply, without drama, and somehow that was worse than any shriek would have been. "Every hour I've lived since my diagnosis should have killed me—that's the arithmetic of it, plain as sums. The farm lent me those hours. Forty nights' worth, compounded. And at zero, the loan comes home." He looked out the window at the frost-bright elms. "I've had eleven good nights since the counting started. Slept well. No pain. My heart does what it's told. Do you know why?"

"Why," she whispered.

"Because it's not mine anymore. None of it is. It's borrowed goods sitting in my chest, and I'm just the caretaker of myself until the owner comes round." He turned back to her, and his eyes were wet, but he was smiling. "And here's the thing you have to understand, because you're going to be angry, and you have every right—I *don't mind*. That's what nobody tells you. When you sign, when you take the loan, the fear goes first. Fear is the deposit. You pay it upfront and then the rest of the term is smooth sailing. That's why we smile, Marguerite. That's why the dying here die smiling. It's not possession. It's *credit*."

*It's not possession. It's credit.*

The phrase went through her like a needle finding a nerve, because she had heard something like it before, hadn't she? In a voice not Edwin's. In a room with green wallpaper and a pen laid out on a table like an offering. *Sign, and the children forget the farm entirely.* Odile's logic, which was genuinely merciful, which was the trap. Everything in this house was generous right up until the moment it collected.

"Who holds the debt?" Marguerite asked. "Not the farm. The farm is a building. Who's the creditor, Edwin?"

He was quiet for a moment. The hum beneath the floor seemed to rise slightly, or perhaps she only leaned toward it, the way you lean toward a fire.

"I saw her once," he said. "Down there. First night of my counting, before I understood what I'd agreed to. I got up to use the water closet and my feet took the wrong stairs—old houses do that, they rearrange themselves for people who haven't learned their manners yet—and I was in the cellar before I knew it, standing in the dark, and there was soil heaped up in the middle of the floor like a grave that hadn't decided on its occupant, and it was humming. The soil itself. And under the hum, if you listened the way you listen for a word in another language, there was a woman's voice keeping the note alive." His hand trembled slightly, the first tremor she had seen in him since the counting began, and he looked at it with mild interest, as if it belonged to a neighbor. "She wasn't cruel. That's what I keep trying to tell people and no one believes me. She sounded *tired*. Like a mother singing the same lullaby for a hundred years because if she stops, the children wake up screaming."

"The lady under the floor," Marguerite said. Iris's words. *The lady under the floor hums it.*

"If that's what the children call her." Edwin shrugged. "She's the ledger under the ledger, maybe. Or she's the first debtor. I don't think even Odile knows anymore which came first—the debt or the woman carrying it." He reached over and tapped the ledger on Marguerite's knee with one finger. "But I know this. Your book. The priest told you true, didn't he? It's the only honest thing in this house. Every name you write down, every memory you restore—it's a payment made on someone else's account. You've been settling debts for weeks, Marguerite. Can't you feel it? The hum's gone quieter. The nights are shorter. You're paying us down."

She could feel it. God help her, she could feel it—fifteen nights of obsessive writing, restoring struck-through names onto the page, and each restored name had quieted the note beneath the floor by some fraction, the way bailing a boat quiets the water sloshing at your ankles. She had thought of it as rescue. Edwin was telling her it was accounting.

"And when you reach zero," she said, "when the farm calls in your balance—what happens to you, Edwin? Where do you go?"

His smile widened, and for just a moment—half a heartbeat—something moved behind his face that was not Edwin at all. Something patient and vast and very old, looking out through the windows of his eyes at her, at the ledger, at the frost-bright morning. Then it was gone, and he was only a dying man again, kind and clear and nearly finished.

"I go where the payments go," he said. "Into the ground. Into the note. Maybe someday somebody writes my name back onto a page and I get to be a memory instead of a sound." He sighed, contented. "Honestly, Marguerite? After forty nights of no pain and no fear and sleeping like a boy? It's a fair price. Don't grieve for me. Grieve for whoever they find to lend to next."

The words hung in the room.

*Whoever they find to lend to next.*

Because the count didn't reset. She knew that now, knew it in the marrow-deep way you know things you refuse to say aloud. She had watched the numbers, done the arithmetic in the dark hours: twelve nights left in Edwin's schedule, and the signing date circled on Odile's calendar fell on the same morning. Zero and signature, arriving together like two hands closing.

And Tam had begun sleep-counting. Small numbers, low numbers, in a voice pitched just below his own. Nine. Eight. Seven. As though somewhere inside her son, an account had already been opened, a deposit already placed, fear quietly withdrawn from him while he dreamed.

"Edwin," she said, and her voice was steady, which surprised her. "If I tore up the papers. If I burned the contracts, broke the seal on the cellar, dragged that bell out of the dirt and melted it proper this time—would your debt vanish?"

He considered it honestly, she gave him that. He looked at the ceiling and thought, really thought, the way he had once thought about engineering problems, back when he had been a bridge inspector and had trusted the world to hold things up.

"No," he said at last. "Debts don't vanish. They transfer. That's the whole architecture of this place, Marguerite—it's not a trap, it's a *relay*. Somebody has to hold the balance. Somebody has to make the payments. If you break the mechanism before the accounts are settled, the balances don't disappear. They just go looking for the nearest solvent party." He looked at her then, gently, so gently. "And you're the most solvent person in this house. You and those beautiful children of yours, with all those unspent years."

The frost on the window had begun to melt in the strengthening light, running down the glass in thin bright threads, like the window itself was weeping and trying to hide it.

"So the kindest thing," Marguerite said, "the merciful thing, is to let you finish. Let the count run. Let the farm call in your balance."

"Yes."

"And then it starts again. With someone else."

"Yes." He reached out and covered her hand with his, and his skin was warm—too warm, furnace-warm, the warmth of borrowed goods burning brightly before repossession. "Unless the debts get paid by other means. Unless somebody settles the whole book. The old entries. The forty-one. The foundryman. All of it, named and numbered and read aloud over the graves." His grip tightened, brief and fierce. "Pell couldn't do it. Agnes couldn't do it. Odile won't—she's spent too long being the lender to remember she was the first borrower. But you—" and here his voice dropped, went soft and urgent, the clarity in it almost unbearable, "—you write things down, Marguerite. You've always written things down. Even the year you lost. Especially the year you lost. There's a reason the ledger chose you. There's a reason your handwriting was already in it before you ever picked up the pen."

Outside, far off across the frosted fields, a rook called once and fell silent, as if the morning itself were holding its breath.

"Eleven more nights," Edwin said. He lay back against his pillows, arranged his hands, settled himself like a man drawing a blanket up. "Then the bell rings for me one last time, and the account closes, and I sleep. And then—" he closed his eyes, smiling, "—then the farm calls in the next balance. And we both know whose name is on that invoice, don't we."

Marguerite rose. Her legs felt distant, operated by remote control. At the door she stopped, because there was one more question, the one that had been sitting in her chest since the first night the bell rang, and she asked it now or she never would.

"Edwin. When you signed. When you took the loan. Was there a moment—just one moment—when you could have walked away?"

He opened his eyes. And for the first time in eleven days, the comfort fell away from his face like a mask slipping, and underneath it she saw something young and terrified and human, a flash of the man he had been before the fear was taken as deposit—and then it was gone, smoothed over, paid down, and he was serene again, and that serenity was the most horrifying thing she had seen in all her weeks at Vessel Farm.

"There's always a moment," he said. "That's what makes it work."

She walked back down the corridor with the ledger against her chest, and beneath her feet, faint and patient and unhurried, the hum went on—counting nothing, fearing nothing, waiting, the way a bank waits, the way winter waits—for the twelfth night, and the thirteenth, and the zero after that.

In her room, Tam's door stood half open. He was still asleep, curled small under his covers, and even from the hallway Marguerite could hear it, barely, a thread of sound:

*Five. Four. Three.*

Two nights ahead of schedule.

The invoice was coming early.
