## 29. The Missing Year, Restored

The truth did not come to her all at once. It came the way water comes through a ceiling—first a stain, then a darkening, then the whole rotten weight of it giving way.

Marguerite was in the linen room when it began, folding sheets that smelled of lavender and something older beneath it, something like iron and river mud. She had taken to working with her hands now, always, because idle hands let the mind wander toward the gap, and the gap was worse than any wound she had ever dressed. A wound at least had edges. The year between last October and this one had no edges at all. It was smooth as the inside of an eggshell, and every time she pressed her thumb against it—*what did I do that autumn, where did I sleep, who sat across from me at supper*—her memory slid off it like rain off glass.

She had asked Pell about it twice. Both times he had found urgent business elsewhere: a drain to clear, a gate to oil, anything rather than meet her eyes.

She had asked Iris once, gently, over toast. *Darling, do you remember last winter? Do you remember where we were?* And Iris had gone very still, the way children go still when they have been told a thing is not to be spoken of—not by anyone, not ever—and had said only, *You said we don't talk about Papa.*

*I said what?*

Iris had looked down at her plate. *You said the less we say his name, the safer he is.*

Marguerite had laughed then, a bright hollow sound, and said something about how children misremember, how they stitch dreams into fact. But she had not slept that night, and she had not slept well any night since, because there was no husband-shaped absence in her heart. There was no absence at all. That was the horror of it. When she searched for him she found nothing to grieve—not a face, not a voice, not even the ache of having lost one. Her love had been amputated so cleanly that no scar remained, and a body does not miss a limb it cannot remember having.

Until the night the ledger showed her.

* * *

It was past two in the morning. Edwin had counted twelve the evening before—twelve, in that voice grown strange and resonant, like a man speaking through the wall of a well—and the bell had tolled twelve times overhead, though there was no bell, there had been no bell since 1911, and the whole household had stood at their windows listening with faces like candle flames. Marguerite could not sleep after that. None of them could. So she took the ledger to the kitchen table and lit the lamp and began, methodically, nurse-fashion, to do the only thing that had ever steadied her: she read.

She had been reading it front to back for days now, hunting the pattern. Forty-one entries in her own handwriting that she had never written. The oldest dated 1911, in ink gone brown as dried blood, describing Agnes Vessel trading her memory of her son to keep him breathing past death. Entry after entry after entry, each one a trade, each one a struck-through line of red, each one signed *M. Fairweather* in a hand identical to her own.

She was halfway through the book, in the pages belonging to the previous spring, when she saw it.

The date meant nothing to her. That was the first wrongness—the page was dated the fourteenth of November, and November should have meant frost and Tam's birthday and the smell of woodsmoke, but the date sat on the page like a coin from a country she'd never visited. Then her eye caught the signature, and her breath stopped in her throat.

*M. Fairweather.* Not written. Not pre-inked, waiting, like the contract in the room of signatures. This one was *finished*. Written in full, pressed deep into the paper, the way a person signs when their hand is shaking and they are trying to hold it steady.

And below the signature, in the same desperate hand, the entry itself:

*Traded this night: my husband Daniel's memory of me. All of it. Every year, every morning, the wedding, the children's births, my face, my name. In exchange: his name is removed from the count. The farm will not take him. He will leave here whole, and he will not look back, because looking back requires remembering, and he will have nothing to look back with.*

*I ask that this be recorded honestly. I ask that someone, someday, tell me what I did. I know I will sign to forget it. I can feel myself already deciding to forget it. If you are reading this, Marguerite—you. You are the someone. Tell yourself.*

The lamp flame leaned. Outside, far off, something hummed under the earth like a wineglass rubbed at its rim.

Marguerite read it three times. Her hands were perfectly steady, which frightened her more than shaking would have; some part of her had learned, a year ago, how to hold still while the world ended.

Daniel.

The name landed on her like a stone dropped into deep water, and the ripples went out and out, and where they passed, things surfaced. Not memories—she would not get memories, those were gone, spent, spent on him, spent gladly—but *facts*, rising up through the eggshell-smooth gap like drowned furniture coming loose from a wreck. She knew suddenly that his hair had gone grey early, at the temples first. She knew he had hummed when he shaved, tunelessly, and that it had driven her mad and that she would give anything now to hear it badly done again. She knew he had carried Tam on his shoulders through the orchard and pretended the low branches were attacking. She knew—he knew it, she knew it, the knowledge arriving without any picture attached, like a letter whose envelope has burned away—that he had gotten sick in the autumn. Sick in the way the residents got sick. Sick in the way that started the counting.

He had begun at forty. Of course he had. They all began at forty.

And she—she had gone to Odile. Or Odile had come to her. The order of events was lost with everything else, but the shape of it remained: a lamp-lit study, papers on the desk, the old woman's voice gentle as falling snow. *There is a price, Mrs. Fairweather. There is always a price. But you may choose what is paid, and by whom. Most keepers pay in other people. You would be the first to pay in yourself.*

She had thought about it for one night. She was almost sure she had thought about it for one night. And then she had signed, because the alternative was sitting beside his bed for forty nights listening to him count down toward the bell while she wrote his number in her ledger in her careful devoted hand, and she had known—even knowing nothing else, she had known—that she could not do it. That she would burn the farm down first. That Odile knew she would burn the farm down first, and had priced the trade accordingly.

So she had paid. Not with her life. With her being *known*. She had purchased her husband's survival with the one currency the farm valued above all others: witness. Memory was the ledger's true ink. Strike a name from remembrance and the debt dissolved; the unwritten could not be collected. She had made herself unwritten to him—every breakfast, every argument, every time he'd said her name against her hair—and the count had closed his account like a book snapping shut, and he had packed his coat and kissed the children goodbye—

Had he? Had he kissed the children? He must have. Surely he—

No. She couldn't have it. The pictures wouldn't come. Only the facts, cold and clean and unbearable: *he left in December. He walked out the gate. He did not look back, because he couldn't. Somewhere in the world there is a man walking around with a wife-shaped hole in his life and no idea what shape fits it.*

* * *

She did not remember standing up, but she was standing. She did not remember crossing the hall, but the pantry door was open before her, and behind the sacks of flour the seam in the wall waited—the sealed stair, the humming stair, the throat of the house. The hum rose up through the floorboards to meet her, that single sustained note, patient as tide.

"You knew," she said aloud, to the house, to the bell in its grave of soil, to whatever wore the farm like a dress. "You knew I'd find it. You left the page where I'd find it."

The hum did not change. But she understood, suddenly, why the entry survived when the memory didn't. The ledger was the one thing the farm could not edit, because the ledger *was* the farm's honesty—or had been, once, before it became the farm's appetite. Father Anselm had told her that: your book keeps us honest. Now she saw the trap inside the mercy. The farm needed her record the way a fire needs air. It could take her memories, but it could not take her testimony, because testimony was the mechanism of collection. Every trade required documentation. Every documentation required a documenter.

Which meant that a year ago, when she signed, some part of her had known exactly what she was doing. She had written herself a letter and hidden it in the one place guaranteed to survive.

*Tell yourself,* the entry said. *You are the someone.*

"I'm telling myself," Marguerite whispered. Her voice cracked on the second word. "I'm telling myself, Daniel. I'm sorry. I'm sorry I made you forget me. I'd do it again. God forgive me, I'd do it again tomorrow."

She put her palm flat against the pantry wall, against the cool plaster, and let herself weep—not the tidy grief of a nurse, but the raw animal grief of a woman mourning a marriage she could not picture, a love she could only *know* the way she knew the boiling point of water. Facts without warmth. A eulogy with no body.

Above her, faint and terrible, the bell tolled once. Twelve. Edwin's number, holding, holding, like a breath drawn and not released.

* * *

She was still at the kitchen table near dawn when Iris came down, barefoot, dragging her blanket, the way children do when they've dreamed of something they can't name. Marguerite hadn't heard her on the stairs. The house had gone quiet in the wrong way, the way a forest goes quiet.

"Mama." Iris climbed onto the chair opposite and studied her mother's face with those grave dark eyes that had lately seemed far too old for six years old. "You found it, didn't you."

Marguerite's heart stopped and restarted. "Found what, love?"

"The page." Iris said it simply, without drama, the way she'd said *the lady under the floor hums it*. "In the book. About Papa."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled, or didn't.

"How do you know about the page, Iris?"

"Because I remember him." Iris folded her small hands on the table, an unconscious imitation of her mother's posture at bedside, and Marguerite felt the ground tilt under the ordinary kitchen, under the whole world. "You gave his remembering-you away, but you couldn't give ours. You tried. You went to ask. And the lady said—" Iris's brow furrowed, reaching for words that had been given to her in a dream or a hum or a funeral song, "—the lady said the children's memories weren't hers to take yet. Not until the papers were signed. So we kept them. I kept him. I've been keeping him for you."

Marguerite could not speak. Her daughter, six years old, had been carrying a man in her head for a year—a secret cargo, guarded, unmentioned, because her mother had once said *we don't talk about Papa*, and Iris obeyed the way soldiers obey, without understanding, without question.

"What do you remember?" Marguerite managed. "Tell me. Everything. Start anywhere."

Iris shrugged, matter-of-fact, devastating. "He sang the wrong words to songs on purpose to make you laugh. He burnt the toast every single morning and ate it anyway. When Tam was born he cried in the car park and pretended it was the cold." She paused. "And at the end, when he was sick and counting, you held his hand all forty nights—no. No, that's not right." The frown deepened. "You held his hand for one night. And then you went downstairs, and came back up, and he wasn't counting anymore. He was sleeping like a normal person sleeps. And in the morning he made toast and burnt it, and he looked at you—" Iris stopped, and her small voice wavered for the first time. "He looked at you like you were a stranger, Mama. And you smiled at him anyway. You smiled the whole time he packed. You smiled at the gate. You didn't stop smiling until the car was gone, and then you knelt down in the lane and I thought you were going to be sick, but you were just—emptying. Like a jug."

The kitchen was very cold. The hum beneath the floor had risen by a half-tone, almost imperceptible, the way a note bends when the glass begins to give.

"He asked," Iris whispered. "At the gate. He asked your name. He said, *I'm sorry—I know I ought to know you—what's your name?* And you told him, and he said it was pretty, and then he drove away."

Marguerite put her hand over her mouth. Behind her eyes, nothing came—no image of the gate, the car, the smile—only the knowledge, the awful clean knowledge, arranging itself around the wound that had no scar. She had stood in the lane and answered a stranger's polite question with her own name. She had watched him drive off into a life that no longer contained her, and she had smiled, because the trade required it, because grief itself had been itemized in the contract, because Odile Vessel understood, better than God, that the cruelest price is always paid in installments.

"Why didn't you tell me?" Marguerite asked. "All this year. Why didn't you ever say?"

Iris looked at her with pity so adult it was obscene. "Because you asked me not to. You said—" and here the child's voice changed, deepened, took on a cadence that was Marguerite's own cadence, worn smooth by repetition, *you said it so many times that Iris had learned it like a psalm*: "You said, *If I remember him too soon, I'll go after him, and then who will keep you safe?*"

Outside the window, the sky was turning the grey of old pewter. Down the corridor, in the east room, Edwin slept with his lips moving around a number. Twelve. Eleven soon. Ten.

And Marguerite Fairweather, keeper's keeper, widow of a living man, sat in the ruins of her own chosen sentence and understood at last what Odile had known from the beginning: that she had never been recruited a year ago. She had been *harvested* a year ago—gently, patiently, the way the farm harvested everything—and the signing Odile planned for this week was not the beginning of her servitude.

It was the renewal.

The pantry door, sealed these hundred years, swung slowly open on its own, and the hum poured out into the kitchen like light under a door, and beneath it, unmistakable now, came a voice—old, dry, almost tender—speaking from the foot of the cellar stairs:

"Now you understand, dear. Shall we discuss terms?"
