## 33. Zero

The night Edwin Hallow reached zero, the frost came early and stayed like a guest who would not be asked to leave.

Marguerite had known it was coming for weeks—had counted alongside him in her head each evening as she tucked his blankets, adjusted his morphine drip, recorded his pulse in her ledger with hands that no longer shook because shaking required surprise and she had none left. Forty nights ago he had begun at forty, one number per night, spoken into the darkening room with the calm of a man reading a train timetable. Thirty-nine. Thirty-eight. The bell had tolled each number over the grounds, that impossible bell melted down in 1911, its note rising from beneath the floorboards as if the house itself had learned to hum.

Tonight the number was zero.

She sat with him from eleven o'clock onward, in the chair by the window that she had come to think of as hers, though nothing at Vessel Farm belonged to anyone so much as everything belonged to it. The moon was a thin paring of light. Frost ferned across the glass in patterns too regular to be natural—hexagonal, deliberate, like something drawn by a patient hand.

Edwin's breathing had gone shallow and sweet-smelling, the particular sweetness of the very near end, which Marguerite knew better than her own signature. But his eyes were open, fixed on the ceiling, and they were bright. Brighter than they had been in months. Brighter than eyes had any right to be in a body this far spent.

"Nurse Fairweather," he said. His voice was thin but clear, a wire pulled taut. "Will you write it down properly? When it happens. Not just the time. All of it."

"I always do," she said. "Name, date, hour. The number you started from. The number you ended on."

"No." He turned his head with agonizing slowness, and she saw that he was smiling. He had been smiling more these last weeks, and every smile had frightened her more than any scream could have. "All of it. What I traded. Who I was before. Write me back onto the page, Marguerite. You've been doing it for the others—the ones you restored. I can feel the house getting quieter where you've written. Like rooms being swept."

Her throat closed. "Edwin—"

"Please." The word cost him. She watched it cost him, watched his chest labor around it. "I gave them my daughter's face. That was my trade, forty years ago when I first came here—not to die, you understand, not then. To keep my wife alive one more winter, and then another, and the price kept being things I didn't know I'd agreed to give. I can't remember what my daughter looks like anymore. I remember that I loved her. I remember the shape of loving her. But her face—" His smile wavered, and for one moment the old man underneath showed through, terrified and ninety-one years deep. "It's like a photograph left in the rain. Write her name in your ledger. Eileen Hallow, born March the third. Write that she had a gap between her front teeth and laughed through her nose. Write that I'm sorry."

Marguerite took out the ledger. Her hands were steady now; she noticed that the way you notice a stranger's face in a familiar mirror. She wrote: *Eileen Hallow, b. 3 March. Gap between the teeth. Laughter through the nose.* And beneath it, *He is sorry.*

The humming under the floor changed pitch. Softer. Almost pleased.

"There," Edwin whispered. "You see. It works both ways. That's what nobody understands about this place—it isn't only a mouth. It can be given to, as well as taken from." He closed his eyes. "One hour to zero, nurse. Will you stay?"

"Every minute," she said.

* * *

The household did not sleep.

She heard them in the corridors as midnight passed—one o'clock, two o'clock—soft shuffling footsteps, the creak of floorboards under feet that should have been resting. When she stepped into the hall for water she found them standing at their doors or gathered at the windows, residents she had nursed for years, all facing the same direction. Toward the east wing. Toward Edwin's room. Mrs. Aldery, who had not stood unassisted since autumn, stood straight as a fence post with her hands folded. Young Mr. Creel, thirty-four years old and dying of something the doctors in the city had never managed to name, stood with tears running silently down his face, and he was smiling too.

They were all smiling.

"Go back to bed," Marguerite said, and her voice sounded small in the corridor, a match struck in a cathedral.

No one moved. No one spoke. They simply breathed together, in and out, a slow tidal rhythm, and Marguerite understood with a lurch that went through her like cold water that they were counting. Not aloud. In unison, silently, keeping time with something beneath the floors. Keeping time with Edwin.

She fled back to his room.

* * *

At ten minutes to three, Edwin began to speak of the foundry again.

He had been doing this for days now—describing, in loving detail, a place he had never seen. The Vessel bell had been cast in 1911 by a foundryman whose name matched an entry in her ledger, and Pell had confessed at last that he had been the man's apprentice, that he had poured the metal himself, that the bell had been rung for the first time over a grave and had never stopped ringing since, not really, not in whatever place bells go when they are melted down but not finished.

"The sand was black," Edwin murmured. "Do you know why the sand was black, nurse? Because it had been used before. Every casting uses the memory of the last casting. The mold remembers." His fingers plucked at the blanket. "There was a crack in the crown from the first day. Old Pell—he was young then, young as you are now—he wanted to recast it. But Agnes Vessel said no. She said the crack was where the voice got out."

"Edwin," Marguerite said gently, "rest."

"I am resting." He smiled at the ceiling. "This is what rest looks like here. Everything is backwards. The dying count down instead of up. The living pay instead of receiving. The bell rings without existing." His breath caught, held, released. "Five minutes, I think. Stay close. I don't want to arrive alone."

"Arrive where?"

But he only smiled, and outside, far off toward the village that would not say the word *Vessel*, something that was not wind moved through the frozen fields.

* * *

Zero came at three o'clock precisely, because of course it did. Everything at Vessel Farm kept the old hours, the church hours, the hours of a bell that had been scrap metal for over a century.

Edwin Hallow opened his eyes all the way for the first time in a week, and they were not clouded, not dimmed, not the eyes of a dying man at all. They were clear as a child's. He looked directly at Marguerite—at her, into her, with an attention that made her feel newly sketched, provisional—and he said:

"It's paid."

And he died smiling.

Not the rictus of the end. A true smile, warm and finished, the smile of a man setting down a weight he had carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body. His chest rose once, twice, and stilled. The frost on the window bloomed outward all at once, ferns becoming feathers becoming wings, and then the whole pane went clear as if the glass had been breathed upon from the other side.

Marguerite sat without moving. Her pen was in her hand. She looked down and found she had already written the hour: *3:00.* She had not written it. Her hand had.

Then the bell tolled.

Once. A single stroke, deep and vast, rising not from the east fields where the church had stood but from directly beneath the floor, beneath the foundations, beneath the cellar stair she had sealed with consecrated earth nine nights ago. The note swelled and filled the room the way water fills a lung, and every window on the ward sang faintly in sympathy, and somewhere below, in the dark, forty-one small graves shifted in soil that suddenly seemed less like soil and more like a throat clearing itself.

One toll. For Edwin. For the debt discharged, the account balanced, the payment schedule complete.

Marguerite waited for the count to reset. That was how it worked—that was what she had told herself, what the pattern promised. One resident's count ends, the ledger closes his line, and the farm waits, patient as arable land, for the next dying soul to begin their own descent. Forty numbers, forty nights, a rhythm older than the melt-down, older than the parish records. The count belonged to the dying. It was theirs. It was how they paid.

The silence stretched. Five seconds. Ten. The frost crept back across the window, slower now, almost hesitant.

Then, from down the corridor—from the family cottage, from the small bedroom with the rocket-ship wallpaper that she had hung herself in another life, in the year that was missing—

Tam began to count.

* * *

She ran. She did not remember deciding to run; her body simply understood before she did, the way it had crossed the room toward Odile's pen, the way it had signed things she did not recall signing. The corridor tilted. Doors stood open on either side, residents watching her pass with their terrible gentle smiles, and Mrs. Aldery whispered something as she went by—*"Oh, the poor dear"*—and Marguerite could not tell which of them the pity was for.

The night air hit her like a wall. Frost had sealed the grass; her boots cracked through it. The cottage windows glowed amber, and inside, Iris stood in the hallway in her nightdress, perfectly still, perfectly awake, looking at Tam's door with an expression Marguerite had never seen on a seven-year-old's face. It was the expression of someone standing watch. Of someone who has been standing watch for some time.

"Mama," Iris said, without turning around. "He started at forty-one."

Marguerite pushed open the door.

Tam lay on his back in his narrow bed, arms at his sides, blanket smooth as if he had been arranged rather than tucked. His eyes were closed. His lips moved. And the voice that came out of him was not his voice—it was not any child's voice—it was low and resonant and patient, a voice with gravel and years in it, a voice like a bell's clapper striking felt, and it said:

"Forty."

A pause, long as held breath.

"Thirty-nine."

"TAM." She was across the room, she had him by the shoulders, she was shaking him though every instinct she possessed as a nurse screamed that you do not shake them, you do not break the rhythm, you do not— "Tam, wake up, look at me, look at Mama—"

His eyes opened.

They were brown, her son's eyes, the same brown as his father's, the father who had walked out of her erased life carrying an erased memory of her—and they were awake, they were her son's, and he looked at her with such naked confusion that her heart broke cleanly down the middle.

"Mama?" he said, in his own voice, small and sleep-thick. "Why are you crying?"

And then his face slackened, his lids drooped, and the other voice returned, unhurried, almost kind:

"Thirty-eight."

* * *

Iris stood in the doorway. She had not followed; she had simply arrived, the way children and cats and ghosts arrive places, and she held her sketchbook against her chest like a shield, the one with the drawings of the bell, accurate to the crack in its crown.

"He'll do it every night," Iris said. Her voice was flat with a knowledge no child should carry flatly. "One number less each time. That's how it goes. That's how it's always gone."

"That's how it goes with the *dying*, Iris. Your brother isn't dying. He's healthy, he's—"

"He's not paying for himself." Iris looked at her mother then, and her eyes were wet and furious and ancient. "Don't you get it yet? Nobody here pays for themselves. Edwin wasn't paying his own debt. He was paying *the farm's*. And now the farm needs a new payer, and the farm picked—" Her voice cracked, finally, into seven years old. "The farm picked my brother, Mama. Because we live here. Because you *kept* us here."

Marguerite knelt on the frozen ground of the moment, in the wreckage of every assumption she had built her devotion on. The ledger. God, the ledger—she had thought it a comfort, a prayer, a kindness. Then a binding. Now she understood it was worse than either. It was an accounting, and accounts must balance, and the farm had just transferred forty-one units of outstanding debt from a dead man's column into a living child's.

"Thirty-seven," said the voice from the bed.

Forty-one nights. She did the arithmetic with a numbness that felt like drowning in still water. Forty-one nights until zero. And Odile Vessel's signing was scheduled for the thirty-ninth day.

The papers. The guardianship. *Safe within the grounds forever.* The word she'd glimpsed in the margin: *succeed.*

It had never been about succession. It was about *transfer*. Odile didn't want Marguerite to inherit the farm. Odile wanted Marguerite's family to inherit the *debt*—to become the next keepers in the way Pell was a keeper, in the way the bell was a keeper, bound to the count, unable to leave, unable perhaps even to die, until the balance was paid in full. And a child counting down from forty-one was not a patient. It was collateral.

"Mama," Iris whispered. "What do we do?"

Below the floor, beneath the consecrated earth, beneath forty-one nameless graves, the hum rose—satisfied, sustained, patient as interest accruing. And in the east wing, in a room already going cold, the nurses' call system registered a death that Marguerite had not yet documented, because for the first time in her career at Vessel Farm, Marguerite Fairweather did not know what to write.

She knew only this: she had thirty-six nights, a ledger with blank pages, a daughter who remembered what she could not, and a son whose mouth was learning a language she had to make sure he never finished speaking.

"Thirty-six," said Tam, in the bell's voice, and smiled in his sleep.
