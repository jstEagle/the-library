## 4. Wren's Version

Wren had rehearsed this a hundred times in the dark of the house on Fenwick Lane — the words arranged and rearranged like bones on a table, trying every order to see which one would make Bram believe. In the daylight version, they were calm. They spoke slowly. They did not cry.

The actual version began with Bram throwing a coffee cup into the sink hard enough to crack it, three days after the funeral-that-wasn't, and saying, "You're going to tell me what you think happened. All of it. And I'm going to sit here and not interrupt, because one of us is insane and I'd like to know which."

So Wren sat at the kitchen table with their hands flat on the wood, the way Grandmother used to before she said anything that mattered, and told it.

* * *

"It was the eleventh of January," Wren said. "You remember the cold that week. The pond behind the church froze so hard the kids were skating on it, and Father Aldous came out and chased them off, and you laughed at him from the bank. You said a bell-shaped hole in the ice would be funny. You said — God, Bram, you said *the pond wants its tithe.*"

"I was joking."

"You were always joking about things that turned out to be true. That was your whole personality." Wren's voice cracked on it, and they let it. There was no point performing steadiness for someone who could read them like a palm. "We went out anyway. You wanted to test the ice near the middle where the spring fed in, because you'd read somewhere that moving water freezes unevenly, and you wanted to *know*, because knowing things was your religion before you lost it. I told you it was stupid. I want that on the record. I said it was stupid and you went out anyway, walking backwards, facing me, arms out like you were balancing on a beam instead of a frozen pond over black water nine feet deep."

Bram said nothing. Their jaw was set in the way it set when they were filing something away rather than arguing with it. Wren took it as permission to continue.

"The ice didn't crack all at once. That's the part nobody believes. It made a sound first — a long sound, like a note, like something *sang* under us — and then it went, all in one piece, the way a floor goes when the joists have been cut. No warning. One second you were standing there grinning at me, and the next there was just a hole with steam coming off it, and your hat floating."

Wren stopped. Their hands had gone white against the table.

"I went in after you. Of course I went in after you. Everyone keeps asking me why I didn't wait for help, as if waiting is something our family does. I broke through at the edge of the hole and the cold hit me like a wall falling over, and I couldn't see anything. The water under that ice is the color of tea left standing, and it was full of your coat — no, that's wrong, it was full of *you*, but all I could find was the coat. I grabbed it and pulled and you came up heavy, so heavy, like the pond was arguing with me about it, like it only let go because it chose to."

"Wren—"

"You said no interruptions." But Wren's voice had gone soft, and they reached across the table and put their hand over Bram's, and Bram let them. "I got you to the bank. I did compressions until my arms gave out. Old Man Petrie heard me screaming and came with his tractor and we wrapped you in the tarp off his trailer and drove you to the house because the ambulance would have taken forty minutes and you were already blue. Aunt Sable met us in the yard. She looked at you and she didn't scream, Bram. That's the thing I can't get past. She looked at your face and she said, *so it's this year then,* and she went inside and started running the bath hot."

Bram pulled their hand back slowly, like drawing a limb out of a trap.

"That's not — Sable doesn't talk like that."

"Sable talks exactly like that when she thinks no one who matters is listening. You've been gone eleven days. You don't know what she sounds like anymore." Wren stood up, paced to the window, paced back. Sitting still was impossible; the memory had a current in it. "Dr. Voss came. He worked on you for an hour on the kitchen floor. He never said the word dead. He said *gone* and he said *I'm sorry* and he said *the cold* — and then Sable sent everyone home and closed the parlor doors, and I sat with you all night in the front room because I wouldn't let them take you upstairs. I held your hand. It was your hand, Bram. I've known your hands since before I knew my own name. There's a scar on the left thumb from the tin can lid when we were six. I checked. I checked like a madwoman, in the dark, by candlelight, because the power was out and the mirrors were doing — never mind what the mirrors were doing. It was your scar. It was your face. It was you, and you were dead, and I have spent eleven days being the twin who lived, and now you're sitting in front of me eating toast and telling me you fell asleep driving and woke up on the road outside Hollowmere with no memory of the last two weeks, and you want me to explain how both of those things are true, and I *can't.*"

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, the pipes ticked as they cooled, a small patient counting.

"So say it," Bram said quietly. "Say the impossible part. You've been circling it for ten minutes."

Wren laughed — a short, broken sound with nothing in it of humor. "Fine. Here's the impossible part. Here's the part I haven't said out loud because saying it makes it load-bearing." They came back to the table and leaned down, close, the way they used to lean over shared homework, conspiratorial, urgent. "When I pulled you out of the water, you were wearing my coat. Not yours. Mine. The green parka with the torn cuff that I'd lent you because yours was at the tailor's getting the zipper fixed. I know because I zipped it myself that morning, and I teased you about drowning in it if you fell in, and you said *worrying is your job, not mine.*" Their voice dropped further. "And when Dr. Voss cut it open to do compressions, underneath it you weren't wearing your sweater. You were wearing mine. The gray one. With the burn hole at the hem from the bonfire in October. We'd swapped coats at the car, Bram. Do you remember swapping? Because I remember it. I remember handing you my coat and taking yours, and I have lain awake every night since trying to understand why I remember it, when the body in the water was dressed the other way around."

Bram's face had gone carefully blank — the rationalist's mask, the one they'd worn since childhood whenever the world presented a bill they couldn't pay. "Memory reconstructs. Under stress, people misremember trivial details constantly. Coat colors, sweaters—"

"Your zipper," Wren said. "Yours was broken. It jammed halfway and you always had to work it with two fingers, and you complained about it so much that Sable offered to fix it four separate times and you refused because you said a flaw you understood was better than a repair you didn't. The body I pulled out of the pond had a working zipper, Bram. I remember because I grabbed it to drag you and it moved smooth as silk, and some stupid animal part of my brain thought *that's not right* even while the rest of me was screaming your name." They straightened up. Their eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady. "I know what I pulled out of that water. I have turned it over ten thousand times. And I am telling you, as the person who has loved you longer than anyone alive, that whatever came out of that pond wearing your face was not entirely you — or else you are not entirely what came out, and one of us is standing on the wrong side of a door."

* * *

They ended up in the hallway without deciding to go there. It was where the mirror hung — the tall oval one in its frame of carved oak leaves, older than any Oyelaran could account for, the one Sable kept polished and never looked into directly. Wren realized where Bram was heading a half-second too late and caught their sleeve.

"Don't."

"It's a mirror, Wren."

"It's a mirror that's been wrong since the night you died." The word *died* came out of Wren's mouth and hung between them, enormous, undeniable, and neither of them flinched from it, which was somehow worse than flinching. "That night, when I carried you in — I passed this hall and I saw us in it. Both of us. Me soaked through, carrying you, and in the glass it was — it was *right*, it was exactly right except that the reflection was a half-step ahead of me. Like it already knew where I was going. And when I laid you down and came back, the glass was fogged. From the inside. On a night cold enough to freeze a pond solid, the mirror was breathing."

Bram stared at them. Wren watched the exact moment the skepticism curdled into something more dangerous — curiosity. It had always been Bram's fatal organ. Even as children, it was Bram who opened the sealed boxes and Bram who asked the questions at funerals and Bram who, at nine years old, had asked Grandmother why the family portraits in the parlor had their faces scratched out, and been told *because we keep what we're given, dear,* and had accepted it with the terrible serenity of a child who does not yet know what they've agreed to.

"One test," Bram said. "One. Then we drop it."

"Bram—"

"One test, or I walk out of this house tonight and you can explain to Sable why her miracle ran away." They stepped up to the glass.

Wren hovered at their shoulder, hating it, hating the whole arrangement of the world. In the mirror, the two of them stood together in the dim hall: Bram pale and sleepless, Wren wrung out and red-eyed, twins to the last detail, the family resemblance so complete it had unsettled strangers their whole lives. *Which one are you?* people asked, delighted with themselves. Neither twin had ever found an answer that felt true.

"Wave," Bram said.

Wren waved. In the glass, Wren waved — a beat late. A full, unmistakable beat, like a film dropping frames, like a dancer hearing the music a moment after everyone else. Then the reflection caught up, and it was only a mirror again, silvered and honest and dumb.

Bram went very still. Then they raised their own hand, slow, deliberate, and snapped their fingers beside their ear.

In the glass, the reflection raised its hand — and paused, fingers cocked, for one long second, before snapping. The sound came from the glass a fraction late, thin, like a snap heard through water.

"Do it again," Wren whispered.

"No." Bram's voice had changed. Wren knew every register of that voice — the lecture voice, the joke voice, the gentle voice they used on frightened animals and frightened people — and this was none of them. This was the voice from the night on the ice, the one that said *watch this* right before the world sang under their feet. "No. If it does it again, it means it's learning how far it can lag before I notice. And I don't want to teach it anything."

They stood there together, shoulder to shoulder, staring at their own faces. The house ticked and settled around them. Outside, the January dusk was coming down blue and total, and somewhere across town — faint, or perhaps imagined, or perhaps simply patient — something that might have been a bell rang once, so softly it could have been a spoon against a cup, and both twins heard it and neither spoke.

Then, in the glass, Bram's reflection smiled.

Bram wasn't smiling.

It lasted less than a heartbeat — a small, courteous curl of the mouth, almost apologetic, the smile of someone greeting an old debtor across a crowded room — and then the reflection matched Bram's real face again, grave and pale, as though it had never done anything at all.

Wren's hand found Bram's hand in the dark and gripped it, hard, the way they'd gripped it through the ice, the way they'd gripped it over every scraped knee and broken promise and buried secret of thirty years. Tender and furious in the same fist.

"The registry," Wren said. "If you want to know what we are, it's in the parish archive. Grandmother kept the ledger. I've never had the nerve to open it alone."

Bram's eyes stayed on the mirror a moment longer. Then they turned away, and their voice, when it came, was the old voice — the knowing-things voice, the religion-before-it-was-lost voice.

"Then tomorrow we open it together."
