## 13. Through the Glass

Bram had not slept. Sleep required a certain faith in the continuity of things — that the person who lay down would be the person who woke — and faith was the one currency they had never carried.

Instead they sat on the floor of their childhood bedroom with a notebook open across their knees, recording the night in the flat, clinical hand that had gotten them through graduate school and through Wren's funeral and through everything since. *2:14 a.m. Hallway mirror lag measured at six seconds using metronome app. Reflection raised left hand at count of four; I raised mine at ten.* Below that: *Ivo has not spoken since Tuesday. The family sets a place for him at dinner anyway.*

Below that, because honesty demanded it even in a private notebook: *I am starting to believe, and I hate it.*

The house creaked its old creaks. Rain worked at the windows with small patient fingers. Somewhere below, in the walls or under the floors, there was a sound like a held breath.

Bram got up because sitting still had become unbearable, and because the notebook had run out of useful things to say, and because — though they would not have admitted it under oath — they wanted to see it again. The lag. The stolen second. The place where reality went soft and something else showed through.

They told themselves it was research.

* * *

The hallway mirror was the tall one, Victorian, its silvering gone cloudy at the corners like breath on glass. It had hung in the same spot for as long as Bram could remember, and as children they and Wren had used it for sword fights and shadow puppets and the game where you tried to move so fast your reflection couldn't keep up. They had always won that game. They'd assumed they were quick.

Now they stood before it in the dark hallway, lit only by the green exit-glow of the old clock at the stair's turn, and watched their reflection watch them back with a delay that had stopped being funny somewhere around five seconds.

"Hello," Bram said aloud, because saying nothing felt worse.

The reflection's mouth moved a beat later. No sound came through — it never did — but Bram had begun to read lips again, an old habit from a deaf great-uncle, and the shape the mirror made was not *hello*.

It was *you're late*.

Bram's skin went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the drafty house. "That's not—" They stopped. Swallowed. Started over, quieter, the way you speak to something wild. "What am I late for?"

The reflection tilted its head. So did Bram, a half-second after, and the wrongness of it — being the one who lagged now, in their own hallway — made their stomach drop like a missed stair.

*You're late,* the mouth said again. And then, slower, each word deliberate as a hand placing stones: *We kept your seat.*

Bram stepped back. Their heel found the edge of the runner rug and they caught themselves on the wall, heart slamming, and the reflection caught itself on the reflected wall a full seven seconds afterward, and in those seven seconds Bram stood perfectly still and watched their own body go through the motions without them — flinch, catch, settle — like watching a film of someone wearing their face.

"I'm not doing this," they whispered. "I'm not."

But they were already reaching for the light switch, already telling themselves they were going to bed, already knowing they weren't. Because here was the thing Bram Oyelaran had spent thirty years refusing to learn about themselves: terror and curiosity lived in the same room in their chest, and curiosity had the louder voice. Every rationalist bone in their body screamed that the correct response to a haunted mirror was distance, documentation, maybe a priest if the family had one lying around. But underneath the screaming was a smaller, older voice, the one that had made them a researcher, the one that had made them unbearable at funerals: *but what is it?*

What was on the other side of the glass?

They went down to the parlor instead of to bed.

* * *

The sealed parlor's door stood open now — Bram had broken it open two nights ago, and no one in the family had remarked on it, which was somehow worse than anger would have been. Sable had only looked at the splintered frame, then at Bram, and said, "Well. Now you know," in the tone of a woman closing a ledger.

Inside, the parlor waited the way it had always waited: dust-sheeted furniture like hunched animals, the wall of portraits with their faces scratched out, the bell rope hanging in its corner of shadow, thick as a ship's cable, frayed at the end where generations of hands had gripped it. Grandmother's contract lay where Bram had left it, unfolded on the writing desk, its ink brown and patient.

*One twin per generation, freely given.*

And beneath the parlor door, spilling into the hallway, lay the long cheval mirror — the narrow standing glass that had once lived in grandmother's room. Bram didn't remember carrying it down. They didn't remember anyone carrying it down. But there it was, angled against the parlor's threshold like a servant waiting to be noticed, and when Bram approached it, the lag in its surface was different. Not delayed.

*Deep.*

Their reflection stood not quite on the surface of the glass but behind it, the way a figure stands behind frosted windowpane, and the room around that figure was not this room. It was the parlor — unmistakably the parlor, same proportions, same scratched portraits — but the dust sheets were gone, the furniture bare and dark, and the air behind the glass had a quality of underwater stillness. River water. Bram knew that smell suddenly, impossibly, seeping through the seam between worlds: silt and cold stone and drowned leaves.

The accident. The ice. The lake that had taken one of them — whichever one of them it had taken.

"No," Bram said, to themselves, to the house, to whatever listened. "No, I'm not—"

They reached out.

Later, they would try to reconstruct the moment honestly, and what they would remember — what they would write in the notebook, in that flat clinical hand, pressing hard enough to tear the page — was that the decision was not theirs. That their arm rose the way an arm rises toward a flame, toward a cliff edge, toward anything with gravity enough to pull, and that belief arrived not as conviction but as surrender. All their life they had demanded proof before faith. Now the proof reached out and took their hand first.

Their fingertips touched the glass.

And went through.

* * *

Cold.

Not the cold of glass, which is brief and clean. This was the cold of deep water without the water — cold that entered through the skin and kept going, cold that had opinions. Bram's hand passed through the surface the way a hand passes through a soap bubble's skin, except the skin resisted, parted slowly, clung, and then gave all at once, and their arm was inside the mirror up past the wrist, and the air on that side was thick and mineral and smelled overwhelmingly of the river.

The reflected parlor surrounded their hand. Solid. The writing desk was there, close enough to touch, and when Bram stretched their fingers they brushed its surface and it was *wood*, real wood, grain and chill and a fine grit of silt along its edges. On the desk sat the contract — the same contract, or its twin — and beside it a bell rope coiled like a sleeping snake, and beyond that, in the corner where the real parlor's rope hung, there was a bell. Not the parish bell. A small handbell, tarnished green, resting on a cushion of rotted velvet.

Bram's whole body shook. They could feel the vibration of it in their teeth. This was impossible. This was the most impossible thing that had ever happened to them, and they had buried a twin who attended the funeral, and still this outranked it, because this was happening *now*, to their *hand*, and their hand was cold, genuinely cold, objectively cold, and objectivity had never felt so much like drowning.

They should pull back. Every instinct said pull back.

Curiosity said: *the desk drawer.*

With their arm buried to the elbow in another world, Bram hooked two fingers into the reflected drawer's handle and drew it open. Inside: a child's shoe, small, filled with dark river sand. A photograph gone white with water damage — figures, faces erased by the flood of it, though one silhouette remained, a girl perhaps twelve years old, standing very straight. And a strip of paper, folded many times, dry despite everything, covered in handwriting Bram recognized with a lurch.

Grandmother's hand. The same hand that had marked both twins' names in the registry.

Bram couldn't read it at this angle. Couldn't reach it properly. Their shoulder was pressed to the mirror's frame now, cheek almost touching the glass, and the glass felt warm against their face — warm as skin — while their arm shivered in the cold beyond it, and the wrongness of that, warmth here and cold there, made them sob once, a short involuntary sound.

The drawer held nothing else. They let it slide shut.

And that was when something touched them back.

* * *

It was a hand. There was no mistaking it for anything else — five fingers, a palm, the specific pressure of a grip — closing around Bram's wrist from the far side, from the dark behind the reflected desk, gentle as a nurse and firm as a debt collector.

Bram screamed. They would deny it later; the notebook would say *vocalized involuntarily*. But they screamed, a raw tearing sound in the empty parlor, and pulled, and the hand did not resist — it simply held, the way you hold a bird so it won't hurt itself, and in the green-dark of the reflected room Bram saw the rest of the figure resolve: a woman, slight, gray-haired, dressed in the fashion of forty years ago, her cardigan buttoned wrong, her face —

Her face was familiar. Family-familiar. The set of the jaw was Sable's jaw. The eyes were the eyes from every photograph in the front hall, the ones no one ever explained.

"You're the aunt," Bram gasped. "You're the aunt from 1984. You're — Sable's sister. You're the one who volunteered."

The woman smiled, and it was a kind smile, and that was the worst thing yet, because kindness in this place was clearly a language learned secondhand.

"You're reading me backwards," she said, and her voice came through the glass thin and bubbling, like speech heard through a wall of water. "Or I'm reading you. It's hard to tell which way the words travel." Her grip shifted, became less a hold and more a clasp, almost a greeting. "You're the new one. Or the old one. Which are you, dear? Dead or owed?"

"I'm not dead," Bram said, and hated how young their voice sounded. "Wren died. Wren went through the ice. I'm the living one."

The aunt's expression did something complicated — pity folding into sorrow folding into something older than either. "That's what the last one said too," she said softly. "That's what they all say. The bell doesn't take sides, love. It takes *one*. And it keeps the books." She turned Bram's wrist gently, examining it, and where her thumb rested, Bram saw — in the dim green light of the other parlor — a faint mark rising on the skin. A ring. A thin dark line circling the wrist like a bracelet, like a tally, like a cuff.

Like a price tag.

"Stop," Bram whispered. "Let go. Please."

"Of course." She released them at once, instantly, and the courtesy of it was more frightening than force would have been. "I only wanted to look at you. We don't get visitors. Well." Her gaze slid past Bram's shoulder, to something in the real parlor behind them, and her face tightened. "We get the rope sometimes. When they ring it. Did you come to ring it?"

"No. I came to—" Bram's arm slid free of the glass, and the transition was hideous, cold peeling off their skin like wet cloth, and they stumbled back into the real parlor clutching their wrist, where the dark ring-mark faded even as they watched, sinking under the skin like a stone into pond water. "I came to understand."

"Ah." The aunt — visible now only as a pale shape behind the cheval glass, dimming like a lamp turned low — nodded slowly. "Understanding. Yes. I understood for the first twenty years." She began to fade, or to step back, or both. "Ask Sable what happens at the count, dear. Ask her properly. She'll lie with her silence, but watch her hands. Hands tell the truth even when the family trains them not to."

"Wait—" Bram pressed forward, palm flat against the glass, which was only glass now, cool and ordinary and dumb. "Wait, what happens at the count? What does the bell do with us? Are you alive? Are you — are you *kept*?"

The shape in the glass stilled. And then, faintly, in a voice like the last of the water going down a drain:

"The bell keeps what it's owed. Unless something better is offered." A pause, long enough for Bram's heart to beat three times. "No one has ever offered anything better. Think about that, before the twelfth chime. Think about what you have that a bell would want more than a person."

The reflected parlor went dark, room by room, like windows going out down a street.

* * *

Bram sat on the parlor floor until the sky outside the boarded windows went from black to bruise-gray, their back against the writing desk, their notebook open, their pen moving.

*Contact achieved. Entity responsive, coherent, identifies as M— (aunt, payment generation 1984). Physical interface possible through cheval glass. Sensation: cold, pressure, smell of river water consistent with accident site. Mark received on left wrist — circular, temporary, purpose unknown. Message received: "The bell keeps what it's owed unless something better is offered."*

They stared at that last line for a long time.

Then, beneath it, in smaller letters, the honest line, the one they would show no one:

*I believe now. God help me, I believe, and it didn't take faith — it took a handshake.*

From upstairs, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of the hallway clock striking six. But beneath it, woven into it, half a beat behind — lagging, always lagging — Bram heard a second strike. Six chimes, and then six more, muffled and bronze-deep, coming from nowhere, from everywhere, from inside the walls.

Counting. Practicing. Getting the mouth ready.

Bram closed the notebook, and their eyes went to the bell rope swaying gently in its corner, though there was no draft, though every window was sealed, though the house had been still as held breath all night.

The rope was swinging. Very slightly. Like something on the other side had just let go of it.

And on Bram's wrist, faint as a watermark, the ring-mark rose again — and this time, it did not fade.
