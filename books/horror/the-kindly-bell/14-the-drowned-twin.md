## 14. The Drowned Twin

The mirror did not break when Bram went through it.

That was the first thing they understood, hanging in the cold between one world and the other: there had been no shattering, no falling, no dramatic rending of the self. There had only been the moment in the hallway when Bram had pressed their palm flat against the glass — the glass that had been lagging eleven seconds behind them now, the glass that showed the parlor door opening when no door in the real house was open — and the glass had *agreed*.

It had agreed the way water agrees when you step into it.

Now Bram stood in a parlor that was the parlor and was not. The same wallpaper, the same faded roses climbing toward a ceiling that wasn't there — the ceiling simply darkened into a green-black murk, like the surface of a pond seen from below. The same furniture, upholstered in the same wine-dark velvet, but everything was *wrong* in the way dreams are wrong: the armchairs sat a half-inch too far apart, the fireplace held no ash and no fire, and the light — there was light, gray and sourceless, the light of an overcast noon that never ended — came from everywhere and belonged to nothing.

The air smelled of river.

Not the memory of river. Not the suggestion. It smelled of the Ouse in February, of silt and stone and the particular mineral cold of water that has been holding something. It filled Bram's mouth when they breathed, and their clothes — they looked down at themselves — were dry, but their skin remembered being wet. Their hair hung in damp ropes. When they touched their own cheek, their fingers came away cold as coins.

"Hello," said a voice behind them. "You're taller than I expected."

Bram turned.

A woman stood by the false fireplace. She was perhaps thirty — no, Bram corrected themselves, she was *dressed* as perhaps thirty, in a blouse and wool skirt of a cut that had gone out of fashion before Bram was born, her hair pinned in a style their grandmother might have worn. Her face was smooth, unlined, and terribly, perfectly preserved, the way things preserved in cold water are preserved. Her eyes were the family eyes. Bram had seen them in photographs in the hallway upstairs, in the faces of Sable and of Wren and, if they were honest, in their own reflection — before the reflection had started keeping its own schedule.

"You're her," Bram said. Their voice came out strange in here, flattened, as if the room were eating the edges of it. "Aunt Maren."

"Maren Oyelaran." She said it the way one recites a name at a registry. "And you're the other one. The one they didn't strike through." She smiled, and the smile was kind, and the kindness was the worst thing in the room. "I wondered which of you it would be. Your grandmother marked you both, you know. I watched her do it. She had a steady hand, that woman. She only ever hesitated once, in forty years, and it was over your names."

Bram's legs wanted to carry them backward, out through the mirror, back into the hallway where the air was merely cold instead of *held*. But the wall behind them — the wall that had been glass — was glass again now, and through it they could see the hallway of the real house, dim and distant, like a room viewed through the wrong end of a telescope. Their own reflection stood on the far side, looking in at them. It raised one hand. Slowly, Bram raised theirs.

The reflection did not.

It only stood there, watching them with an expression Bram could not read, and after a moment it turned and walked away down the hallway, and Bram was alone in the water-colored parlor with their drowned aunt.

"You can't go back that way," Maren said, not unkindly. "Not yet. The glass only opens from the near side, or when the bell wants it to. And the bell is counting, isn't it? I can hear it. Even in here. Even after forty years." She touched her own throat, just below the jaw, as if she could feel the chimes resonating in the water of her blood. "Nine," she said. "It said nine. It's never gotten past nine before. Your grandmother's bargain always reset it. But your grandmother is dead, and the debt is standing, and the bell is a patient creditor." She looked at Bram with those family eyes. "You came through on purpose. Why?"

"Because I wanted to see what it keeps."

Maren was quiet for a moment. In the silence, Bram became aware of a sound beneath the silence — a vast, slow pressure, rhythmic, like a heartbeat heard through a wall, like the pulse of water against a hull. The house around them breathed with it.

"Sit down," Maren said. "It's easier if you sit down. The first hour is the worst. After that, you stop noticing you're underwater."

"I'm not underwater."

"No," she agreed, and gestured to the armchair. "That's what makes it interesting."

* * *

Bram did not sit. They stood by the false window — the window that showed, instead of the garden, a green-gray depth with shapes moving in it, slow and enormous and never quite resolving into anything — and they made themselves look at her. Really look. The rationalist in them, the part that had spent thirty years cataloguing and dismissing, was screaming its old litany: *hallucination, hypoxia, grief psychosis, the mirror lag is a perceptual artifact, the woman is a projection of your own guilt* — but the rationalist's voice had grown thin and distant in here, like a radio playing in another room. In the parlor behind the glass, disbelief did not survive long. Belief arrived the way drowning does: against your will, all at once, and then it was simply the condition you lived in.

"Tell me," Bram said. "Tell me what happened. In 1984."

Maren folded her hands. She had the stillness of someone who had had a very long time to practice it.

"You know the shape of it, I think. Sable told you some, or you read it in the registry. Our generation had two daughters — Sable and me. Sable was the elder. The bell's ledger came due, as it does every generation, and my mother — your grandmother — went to the parish and opened the book, and the rule is the rule: one twin, freely given. Except we weren't twins, Sable and I. Only sisters. The bell is particular about twins. It likes the symmetry. Two made from one, so one can be returned to the whole." She shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "It took me anyway. Because Sable volunteered first, and I — I couldn't let her. I said *take me instead*, and I said it *first*, in the end. By half a breath. We argued about it, actually, at the rope. Two grown women, pulling the bell rope back and forth like children fighting over a toy. And the bell listened to the argument the way a scale listens to weights."

"And it chose you."

"It chose the *offer*," Maren corrected. "That's what no one understands, what your family has never understood in six generations. The bell doesn't take. It *accepts*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. A thing taken can be stolen back, can be ransomed, can be mourned as a crime. A thing accepted — a thing *given*, freely, with a whole heart — is a payment, and a payment closes the account." Her eyes went distant, toward the window and the shapes in the deep. "I gave myself. And the bell accepted me, and it rang eleven times that night — I counted, I have had forty years to count — and the twelfth never came. Because the payment was good. Because my sister loved me enough to try to die for me, and I loved her enough to let her live, and the bell found that acceptable."

"Then why are you still here?"

The question came out harsher than Bram intended, and Maren flinched — actually flinched, a small motion of the shoulders, the first unpracticed thing she had done — and Bram understood, with a compassion that arrived like a physical blow, what forty years in a water-colored parlor does to a person. What it means to be *kept*. Not tortured. Not punished. Kept, the way one keeps a receipt.

"Because," Maren said, "the bell keeps what it's owed."

The pressure-sound beneath the silence swelled, just slightly, and settled.

"That's the part your family gets wrong, every generation. They think the bargain is *a life for peace*. Give the bell a twin and the town stays quiet and the family stays whole. But that's not the bargain. The bargain is *a life held in trust*. The bell accepts the payment and it keeps the payment, and the payment waits — here, in this room, in this light that isn't light — until something better is offered." She looked at Bram. "Something better. Those are the words. My mother's words, when she signed. I read the contract before she folded it into the wall. *The Kindly Bell shall hold in trust that which is freely given, until such time as a better offering is made.* And in forty years, no better offering has been made, because no one in my family has ever understood that a better offering was even *possible*. They only ever offered people. Sisters. Twins. Children." Her voice did not break, but it thinned, the way a wire thins. "Do you know what I did for the first ten years? I sat in that chair and I waited for Sable to come through after me. I wanted her to. I hated her for living. And then one year — I don't know which year, the years don't move in here — I saw her at the glass, on the other side, old now, gray, and she had her hand pressed against it, and she was crying, and I understood that she had spent her whole life trying to find a way to trade herself back in, and the bell wouldn't take her, because the account was closed. And I stopped wanting her to come. I started wanting her to *live*. That's the cruelty of it, Bram. The bell doesn't just keep you. It keeps you *loving* the people it took you from."

Bram's eyes were wet, and in the parlor's air the tears felt like river water running down their face.

"You said something better," they said. "Better than a person. What could the bell possibly want more than a person?"

Maren smiled again, and this time the kindness in it had teeth, and the teeth were hope.

"That," she said, "is the question your family has never had the imagination to ask. You're the first one who ever came through the glass *asking* instead of *paying*. Do you understand what that makes you?" She stepped closer, and up close Bram could see the preservation in her — the faint translucence of her ears when the gray light passed through them, the way her pulse, when she pushed back her sleeve, beat slow and greenish at the wrist. "You're not a payment, Bram Oyelaran. You're an *auditor*."

"I don't understand."

"The bell is a ledger," Maren said. "It was a ledger before it was a bell. It rang for the crimes the courts wouldn't touch — the father who beat his children behind locked doors, the magistrate who took bribes, the men who drowned the Oyelaran miller's son in 1841 and called it an accident. It settled what the law excused. And it took its fee. But a ledger, Bram — think about what a ledger *is*. A ledger is a record. A record exists for one purpose: to be *read*. Six generations, and your family has only ever read the debt column." Her hand closed around Bram's wrist — cold, cold as the river, cold as truth — and her family eyes burned into Bram's family eyes. "The bell has been keeping accounts for two hundred years. Every crime it settled. Every name it struck through. Every payment it accepted. It's all *in there*. And it has been ringing in the dark for a century and a half, waiting — the way I have waited, the way everything it keeps has waited — for someone to ask it the only question that matters."

Bram's mouth was dry, which was absurd, in a place made of water.

"What question?"

"*Who paid?*" Maren said. "And *for what?* And *was it just?*" She released Bram's wrist. "The bell is not evil. I have had forty years to study it from inside its own throat, and I will tell you what I know: the bell is *fair*. Terribly, perfectly, inhumanly fair. It does not want your sister. It does not want you. It wants what every creditor wants. It wants the debt *acknowledged*. It wants the account read aloud, line by line, in front of everyone who benefited from the silence. Hollowmere has been peaceful for two hundred years because Hollowmere's sins were paid for in Oyelarans — and no one in Hollowmere has ever had to *hear* that. Not once. The bell has rung in the dark, and the town has slept, and your family has buried its dead in secret and called it duty." She stepped back, and the gray light moved across her preserved face like water over stone. "A person is a payment. But the *truth* — the whole ledger, read aloud, witnessed — that is something better. That would empty this room. That would ring the twelfth chime and let the bell finally, finally rest."

The shapes in the window had stopped moving. Bram realized, with a lurch, that they were all facing inward now — all the slow enormous somethings in the deep — as if they, too, were listening.

"The truth," Bram repeated. Their voice shook. "You want me to testify. Against my own family. Against the whole town."

"I want you to do what no Oyelaran has ever done," Maren said. "I want you to stop *paying* and start *counting*."

* * *

They talked for what might have been an hour and might have been a year. Time in the parlor moved the way light moves in deep water — present, directional, meaningless. Maren told Bram about 1984 in detail: the argument at the rope, the eleven chimes, the way the twelfth had hung un-rung in the air like a held breath, and how she had felt the bell *consider* her, the way a mouth considers a word. She told Bram about the others — not all the payments had been Oyelarans, not at first; in the early decades the town had offered its own, the guilty and the inconvenient, and some of them were still here, in the rooms behind the other mirrors, and some of them had gone quiet so long ago that even the house had forgotten their names.

"They're not suffering," she said, when Bram asked. "That's the thing you have to understand. The bell is kind. That's not a lie in the name — it is *kindly*. It keeps us the way you keep a letter you can't bear to throw away. But a kept thing is not a living thing, Bram. I have been thirty years old for forty years. My sister is an old woman now. When you see her, tell her—" Maren stopped. Started again. "Tell her I stopped hating her in 1996. Tell her I'm proud of how she kept the house. Tell her the chair by the window is comfortable, and I'm not being brave, it genuinely is."

Bram nodded, and could not speak, and nodded again.

And then, through the glass wall — through the mirror that stood at the parlor's edge like a door standing ajar — Bram heard a sound from the real house. Footsteps. Quick ones. And a voice, Wren's voice, calling their name down the hallway, once, twice, and then a third time in a tone that turned Bram's blood to ice water, because Bram knew that tone. It was the tone of someone reading something they were not supposed to read. It was the tone of someone standing in front of the sealed parlor, or the registry, or the contract — of someone who had just learned the family arithmetic and was already, already, doing the division.

"Wren," Bram said, and pressed their hands against the glass, and the glass was cold and gave nothing. "Wren, don't—"

Maren appeared beside them. Her reflection, Bram noticed, did not appear in the glass at all.

"The count is at ten," she said quietly. "Can you hear it? It's in the walls of your house now. It's in the water pipes. It's in the spaces between your family's words at dinner." She touched the glass, and where her fingers rested, words surfaced in the mirror's silvering like bodies rising in a pond — backwards, reversed, etched from the other side: **X**. "One more, Bram. One more chime and the bell comes to collect what's standing. And your sister is in that house, and your sister loves you, and your sister has just spent the evening learning that love, in this family, has always been a currency." Her voice dropped to almost nothing, and it was the kindest and most terrible thing anyone had ever said to Bram: "Go and stop her. Because if Wren gets to that rope before you find something better to offer — the bell will *accept*. And I will have company in this chair for another forty years. And you will spend the rest of your life being the twin who lived, and I can tell you from experience, Bram, from forty years of experience—"

The gray light flickered. The pressure-sound beneath the world swelled, and somewhere impossibly far away, felt in the teeth rather than heard by the ear, the eleventh chime began.

"—it is not a life," Maren finished. "It's a receipt."

The glass softened under Bram's hands like the surface of the river, and the parlor tilted, and the last thing Bram saw was Maren Oyelaran standing in her water-colored room, thirty years old forever, raising one hand in a wave — not goodbye, Bram understood. *Hurry.*
