## 13. First Attempt

The kitchen smelled of cinnamon and old wood, and August hated how much she loved it.

That was the trick of it — she understood that now. The house had been warming itself around them for days, the way a body warms a bed. Every surface had softened. The draft under the back door had stopped. The light through the lace curtains fell in long honeyed bars that made even the chipped linoleum look like something from a photograph of a happier decade, one that maybe never happened but should have. Delia moved through it humming. Theo sat at the table with his hands wrapped around a mug he wasn't drinking, and his eyes kept drifting toward the pantry door, which did not open onto canned goods anymore but onto a corridor of warm lamps and closed doors, and which he looked at the way a man looks at a woman across a room who has already smiled at him once.

August laid the ledger on the table between them.

It was Ida's ledger, the family census, the book with its patient columns and its dates in faded brown ink. She had spent two nights with Wren going over the rules until her voice went hoarse reciting them. *It cannot take what refuses to be kept. Refusal must be witnessed and named aloud.* Wren had said it simply, sitting on the porch rail with her unchanging seven-year-old hands folded in her lap, and then she had added the fine print, because there is always fine print: *But it will answer you. It always answers. That's how it keeps things.*

August had thought she understood. She had faced down condemned buildings, floors that breathed, stairwells that went soft as gristle underfoot. She had stood in the cellar corridor itself, three weeks ago now, or three years, time ran strangely down there, and heard that kind voice ask her so politely to sign in, and she had run. Running had been easy. Fear was easy. Fear was fuel; she'd been running on it for years without knowing it was the same tank the cellar drew from.

Refusal was not fear. Refusal required standing still.

"I'm going to say it out loud," she told them. "Both of you have to hear it. You're my witnesses. It has to be witnessed and named."

Delia frowned over her tea. "Say what, sweetheart?"

"The refusal." August turned the ledger so her mother could see it. Delia's eyes slid across the columns of names the way they slid across everything now — gently, without purchase, like feet on ice. "Mom. Look at the names. Montrose. All of us. There's a date next to yours."

"There's always been a date next to mine," Delia said pleasantly. "March fourteenth, 1958. That's my birthday, dear."

The date next to Delia's name was not March 14th, 1958. It was eleven days from now, written in ink so fresh it still shone. August didn't correct her. Correcting her mother's memory had become like arguing with weather.

"Theo," she said instead. "You're listening?"

Theo's jaw worked. He was thirty-four and had arrived for Thanksgiving looking like a man who'd driven through a storm to get somewhere he couldn't name, and since then he had slept better than August had seen him sleep in fifteen years. That frightened her more than anything else in the house. Her brother, who had left at nineteen and called twice a year, whose voicemail greeting was just a sigh — her brother was sleeping eight hours a night in this house, in this town, above this cellar, and waking up rested.

"I'm listening," Theo said. His voice was careful. "I want you to know I'm taking this seriously. I know you think I'm not, but I am. I dreamed about it again last night. The corridor." He turned the mug a quarter turn on the table. "It asked me if I wanted to see the library."

"The library."

"There's a library down there," Theo said, with the mild certainty of a man reporting the weather. "All the books nobody finished writing. Somebody's got to read them, Aug. Somebody's got to keep them company."

*Somebody's got to keep them company.* August felt the words go through her like a splinter finding its depth. That was the whole architecture of the thing, laid bare in her brother's sleepy voice. Not hunger. Loneliness. The cellar didn't want to eat them. It wanted to *keep* them, the way a miser keeps coins — touched often, counted nightly, never spent, never gone.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Then listen to all of it."

She stood up straight, both palms flat on the table, the way she'd rehearsed on the porch with Wren watching from the yard with those too-old eyes. She made her voice level. She made it formal, because formality was a door and doors were the only language the cellar respected.

"I am August Wren Montrose," she said, and heard Wren flinch faintly behind the window glass at the middle name — she'd chosen it deliberately, a small cruelty of her own, a hook to keep her emissary close. "Daughter of Delia, granddaughter of Ida, sister of Theo. On behalf of every Montrose living and dead, I refuse the keeping. I refuse the count. I refuse the hospitality of the cellar beneath this house and beneath the waterworks and beneath every street in this town. We are not residents. We are not yours to document. No Montrose signs your ledger, now or ever. Witnessed by my mother and my brother, in this house, on this day. Refused."

The word rang in the kitchen and died.

For a moment nothing happened at all, and August allowed herself one full breath of stupid, human hope. The refrigerator hummed. A car passed outside. The world remained the ordinary world, and she thought, *it worked, it actually worked, we say it again louder and—*

Then the light changed.

Not dimmed. *Warmed.* The honeyed bars through the curtains thickened to amber, to the exact color of late afternoon in a photograph of a childhood you're not sure you remember. And the smell of cinnamon deepened into something underneath it — bread baking, woodsmoke, rain on a hot sidewalk, a dozen smells that had no business sharing a room and yet belonged together the way notes belong in a chord.

"Oh," Delia said softly.

And August watched the cellar begin its counter-offer.

* * *

It started with her mother. Delia set down her teacup with a click and looked at the middle distance, and her face — her tired, memory-frayed, fifty-nine-year-old face — became twenty-six years old in the space of a heartbeat. August saw it happen like watching frost crawl backward off a window.

"Tommy," Delia whispered.

August had never heard the name. She would learn later that Tommy Ashford had sat behind Delia in eleventh-grade biology and had died in a car accident on Route 9 the summer after graduation, and that Delia had never once spoken of him in August's entire life. But the cellar knew. The cellar had been under this town for a century, under the gymnasium where the spring dance was held, under the road where the car left the pavement, and it had kept the moment the way it kept everything — perfectly, patiently, at temperature.

"He's asking me to dance," Delia said. She was smiling. Her hand came up and settled on an invisible shoulder, and she swayed, barely, in her kitchen chair, to music August couldn't hear. "He was too shy all year and then at the dance he just — walked right up. Nobody believed it. I never told anyone he asked me first, before the song, before everyone was watching. He said, *I figured I shouldn't wait for a reason.*"

Her mother laughed, wet-eyed, swaying with a boy forty years dead, and August stood with her refusal still ringing in her ears and understood, with a nausea that reached all the way down past her ribs into the tunnel-dark, what Wren had meant by *it will answer you.*

It answered loneliness with presence. That was all. That was the whole terrible machine. Every person in this town carried a sealed room inside them — a door marked with the name of someone absent, something unfinished, some warmth that had ended too soon or never started — and the cellar had spent a hundred years learning the location of every single door.

"Mom." August touched her mother's arm. "Mom, there's no one there."

Delia looked at her with absolute, radiant pity. "Of course there isn't, sweetheart," she said kindly, the way you speak to someone who can't see the beautiful thing right in front of them. "But he was. He *was*, and that counts. Someone finally understands that it counts."

* * *

Then it came for Theo, and August saw it coming and could do nothing, because the rule cut both ways: refusal must be witnessed and named, but the answering — the answering needed no witness at all.

Theo's mug slipped from his fingers. August caught it an inch off the table. Her brother was staring at the pantry door, at the corridor beyond it, and tears were standing in his eyes without spilling, the way tears stand in the eyes of people who have stopped being embarrassed about crying.

"She's there," he said. "Aug. She's *there.*"

August knew before he said the name. There was only one name it could be. There was only one person Theo had ever loved without armor, and her name was Caitlin Reyes, and she had been nineteen, and it had been August's own senior year, and the river in April, and the search that went on for nine days, and her brother coming home from the banks each evening gray-faced and silent while their parents' marriage quietly came apart in the rooms upstairs. Theo had left for good that June. August had always known the town itself was what he was fleeing. Now she knew the town had never let him finish fleeing.

"She's sitting on the steps," Theo said. His voice had gone young. Nineteen. Younger. "She's got her shoes off. God, she always took her shoes off, drove me crazy, barefoot everywhere—" He laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle. "She says she's been waiting. She says it's not my fault, Aug. She says she knows I've been carrying it and I can put it down."

"Theo." August gripped his wrist. His pulse was slow. Slow and calm, the pulse of a man falling asleep in a warm bed. "Theo, look at me. It's showing you what you want. That's all it does. That's all it *is.* It's a room that shows you what you want."

"It's not showing me." He turned and looked at her, and his eyes were clear, clearer than she'd seen them in fifteen years, and that clarity was the most frightening thing in the house. "It's not showing me anything, Aug. It's letting me *remember.* There's a difference. Don't you feel it? Don't you feel how quiet it is in here? When's the last time anything was quiet?"

The kitchen was very quiet. The cinnamon-warm air pressed gently, like a hand on the shoulder of someone grieving at a funeral, saying *there, there.*

"You hate this town," August said. "You told me. You said this town ate Caitlin and then ate Mom and Dad's marriage and then chewed you up and spat you out."

"It did." Theo nodded slowly, reasonably. "And then it spent fifteen years learning how to say sorry." He looked back at the corridor, at the lamplight, at the steps where a barefoot girl waited with all the patience of the preserved. "Everyone else just left me alone with it, Aug. Everyone else said *time heals, move on, she wouldn't want you to grieve like this.* This is the first thing that ever said, *you don't have to put it down. Give it to me. I'll hold it for you. I'll keep it safe. I keep everything safe.*"

His hand moved toward the pantry door.

August grabbed it. Held it. Her brother's fingers were warm — that was the worst part, they were warm, everything in this house was warm now — and under her grip she felt him trembling, felt the war in him, the part that knew and the part that ached, and she understood with terrible clarity that the ache was bigger. The ache had always been bigger. Fear was a season; loneliness was a climate.

"Theo, please. Say it with me. Say *I refuse.* Just the words. You don't have to mean them yet. Just say them."

He looked at her. And for one second — one raw, naked second — her brother was nine years old again, standing in the backyard at dusk, asking her whether Wren was real, and she had said *of course she's real, she's my friend,* and he had believed her, because that was the kind of brother he was. He believed her now. She watched him try.

"I—" Theo began.

The corridor lights brightened. Somewhere down there, politely, a door opened.

And Theo's face went slack with longing, and his hand turned in hers — not pulling away, just turning, palm up, the gesture of a man accepting a gift — and he said, in a voice like a man confessing:

"Aug, I'm so tired of being the one who lost her."

* * *

August got him to the living room. She didn't remember crossing the kitchen. She got him onto the couch and put her body between him and the hallway, between him and the pantry, between him and every door in the house, and she talked to him — about the river, about Caitlin, about the funeral he'd refused to attend, about the voicemail Caitlin had left him two days before the end that he still had saved on an old phone in a box in Seattle, August knew about the box, she'd found it once and never told him — she poured out every true thing she had, and Theo cried, great silent adult sobs, and held her hand, and did not go to the door.

But when she finally eased back to check on Delia, she found her mother standing at the picture window, looking out at the yard, where the light was failing into that particular amber the cellar liked best.

"He stayed for the whole song," Delia murmured contentedly. "Nobody ever stays for the whole song anymore."

Behind August, in the kitchen, the pantry door stood open onto the corridor, and the warm light lay across the linoleum like a welcome mat, and from far down the hall of closed doors came a sound she hadn't heard before. Not the voice. Not the counting.

Humming. A girl's voice, barefoot on basement steps, humming a song from 2004, patient as stone, waiting to be visited.

And August realized, with a cold that finally cut through the cinnamon warmth, that the cellar hadn't failed tonight. It had *opened accounts.* It had shown each of them their deepest loneliness, answered — and now the answer existed, now it hummed and swayed and waited ten steps away, and refusing a thing you've never seen is easy, but refusing a thing you've met, a thing that knows your dead, a thing that holds what you cannot—

Wren appeared in the doorway, small and unchanged, and for the first time in twenty years she looked afraid.

"August," she said. "You need to come downstairs. Your grandmother's tape — there's a second side. And August—" the little girl's voice wavered, "—I think Ida tried to trick it. I think that's why it wants *you* so badly. You're the one who documents everything. You're the one it can finally *keep honestly.*"
