## 18. Theo Signs

Theo had always been good at leaving. It was the one talent the Montrose family had ever confirmed in him — August got the maps and the memory, Delia got the house, but Theo got the door. He'd used it at eighteen, at twenty-two, at twenty-six, each departure a little quieter than the last, until leaving stopped being an event and became a condition, like weather. He lived in a city four hours away where nobody knew his middle name, in an apartment with thin walls and no photographs, and he had told himself for years that this was freedom.

He knew better now. He'd known better since the dream.

It hadn't even been a nightmare. That was the terrible part. He'd dreamed of a corridor with warm plaster walls and doors painted the green of his grandmother's kitchen chairs, and light coming from under every door like the hallway itself was breathing, and a voice — not booming, not whispering, just *present*, the way a voice is present when someone is reading aloud in the next room — saying: *There's a place set for you. We've kept it. We keep everything.*

He'd woken up homesick. Not for the apartment. Not for any address he'd ever had. Homesick for a place he could not name, and the homesickness had not faded in the shower or on the drive or through the six hours of interstate that separated him from Hollow Creek. It had grown. By the time he turned onto Delia's street, the feeling had a shape, and the shape was a doorway.

*You're early,* the voice had said, on the last night of the drive, when he'd pulled into a motel parking lot outside Scranton and slept eleven hours without moving. *But that's all right. Early guests are just guests who know what they want.*

He told himself he was coming home for Thanksgiving. Everyone comes home for Thanksgiving. It was the perfect alibi, a holiday built out of obligation, and he repeated it to himself like a man repeating a password he half-suspected had been changed.

* * *

The house was warm. That was the first wrong thing, though it took him a while to understand it was wrong at all. The furnace in Delia's house had been dying since before their father left; every childhood winter had been a negotiation with cold, sweaters over pajamas, breath fogging in the upstairs hall. But now the whole house held heat like a held breath, and the radiators ticked softly in every room, and the air smelled faintly of bread that no one had baked.

His mother met him at the door and hugged him longer than she ever had. She felt smaller and softer than he remembered, and there was something loose in her eyes, some thread cut, so that when she looked at him he felt seen by someone who was also looking straight through him at something behind him.

"You're just in time," Delia said. "Everything's almost ready."

"It's Tuesday," Theo said. "Thanksgiving's Thursday."

"I know." She smiled, and the smile was serene and slightly elsewhere. "Everything's almost ready anyway."

August came down the stairs two at a time when she heard his voice, and Theo watched her stop halfway, one hand on the banister, and he saw her do the math on his face the way she did math on everything — quick, total, unsentimental. She looked at him the way you look at a map that has started showing a river where no river should be.

"Theo." His name in her mouth sounded like a question.

"Hey, Augie."

"Don't call me that."

"Sorry." He wasn't sorry. He'd called her Augie to needle her since they were children; it was the only inheritance they still shared. "Mom said come for Thanksgiving."

"Did she." August came down the rest of the stairs slowly. Up close she looked wrung out, sleepless, a woman carrying something heavy in both hands and refusing to set it down. "Theo, I need to talk to you. Tonight. Before you—"

"Dinner's ready," Delia called from the kitchen, in a voice like a bell rung once.

They ate at six, though it was dark by four-thirty now, the November dusk coming down like a lid. The food was good — better than good, uncannily good, the roast falling apart at a touch, the gravy tasting of every Thanksgiving of Theo's childhood at once, as if the meal had been distilled from memory rather than cooked. He ate three helpings. He couldn't stop eating. With every bite the homesickness eased a little, and with every bite it also deepened, because this was what he'd been hungry for, apparently, for fifteen years, and he hadn't known until his body recognized it.

August barely touched her plate. She kept looking at the windows. Once, Theo followed her glance and saw nothing but their own reflections and the dark yard, but the reflections lingered a half-second too long after he turned away, and he decided not to have noticed that.

"Theo," August said quietly, when their mother left the table to fetch a pie that Theo was fairly sure did not exist. "What did you dream?"

The question went through him like a fishhook finding flesh. He set down his fork. "Why would you ask me that?"

"Because I dreamed it too. When I was seven." Her eyes were steady and frightened and fierce, all three at once, and he remembered suddenly that August had never once lied to him, not even when lying would have been kind, and that this had made her unbearable and trustworthy in equal measure. "A corridor. Green doors. A voice telling you there's a place set for you."

The pie arrived. It was pumpkin. Their mother had not gone to the store. Theo ate two slices.

* * *

Afterward, August cornered him in the upstairs hall, outside the room that had been theirs as kids, and pressed a cassette recorder into his hands like it was evidence, which he supposed it was. "Listen to this," she said. "All of it. Then talk to me."

He listened alone in his old bedroom, sitting on a bed that had been made up with fresh sheets though no one had slept in it, and he heard his grandmother's voice, thin and hard as wire, bargaining with something that answered in a courtesy so complete it curdled the blood. *One generation,* Ida Montrose said. *You leave my daughter and her children alone, and you can wait for August. You've been waiting for August since before she was born, we both know it. One generation. Take your time. Be patient.*

And the voice had said: *Patience is our great virtue, Mrs. Montrose. We keep things. Keeping is patience, practiced. One generation, then. Witnessed and named.*

Theo sat with the recorder silent in his lap for a long time. Downstairs, the house ticked and breathed. Under the floorboards, faintly, unmistakably, water moved — though the well had been capped in 1994.

When August knocked, he didn't answer, and she came in anyway, because she was August, and she read his face and closed her eyes like a woman bracing for a wave.

"You dreamed it," she said. "And instead of calling me — instead of calling anyone — you got in the car."

"I thought it was a dream."

"You knew it wasn't. You drove four hours toward it. Theo, look at me. There are rules. Wren told me the rules. It cannot take what refuses to be kept, but the refusal has to be witnessed and named aloud, and I can witness it, I'm right here, we can do it tonight, we can do it now—"

"Wren." He laughed, and it came out wrong, high and cracked. "Your imaginary friend. Augie, listen to yourself."

"Listen to *yourself*. Listen to what you're doing. You came home to a house that smells like bread nobody baked, and you're eating food that appeared out of nowhere, and you haven't asked a single question, because asking questions would be refusing, wouldn't it? Because part of you—" Her voice caught. "Part of you wants it. Part of you has wanted it your whole life, and it knows, it *counts* on that."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed, miserably. "It isn't."

She stayed. She dragged a chair to the side of his bed like a nurse, like a sentinel, and said she wasn't going anywhere, and for a while they talked the way they hadn't talked since they were teenagers — about Dad, about the aunt neither of them could remember, about the strange arithmetic of families, how love gets measured out unevenly and everyone keeps private ledgers of the debt. Theo found himself telling her things he'd never said aloud: that he left because staying felt like drowning, that every Christmas he sat in his apartment listening to other people's dinners through the walls, that loneliness wasn't sharp, it was soft, it was the slow settling of snow, and one morning you were simply buried and too tired to dig.

August listened with tears running silently down her face.

"That's what it offers," she whispered. "That's exactly what it offers. It finds the snow and promises shovels."

"It's just a story you've built," Theo said gently. "Out of grief. Out of Grandma Ida and a tape and a tunnel you found. Augie — you've always needed things to be documented. Maybe you documented a monster."

"Maybe," she said. "Then refuse it anyway. Refuse it as a story. Humor me. Say it aloud, let me witness it, and if nothing happens, if I'm wrong, then I'll get help, real help, I promise, I'll do whatever you—" She stopped. Her head came up, sharp, like a deer's. "Do you hear that?"

Theo heard it. From downstairs, sweet and unhurried, their mother's voice, singing. Delia hadn't sung since before Dad left. She was singing a lullaby Theo didn't know and somehow knew completely, every note arriving a half-second before she sang it, and beneath her voice, harmonizing, patient as tide, another voice he recognized from the dream.

"There's a place set for you," it sang. "We've kept it. We keep everything."

* * *

He didn't remember deciding to go downstairs. That was the thing he would think about later, in whatever remained of later — that there was no decision, no moment of surrender, just a rising, a warmth in his legs, his hand already on the door handle, and August shouting his name behind him in a voice stripped down to its oldest self, the voice she'd used when they were small and he wandered off in stores.

The hallway downstairs was longer than the hallway downstairs should be. He knew this and kept walking. The wallpaper gave way to warm plaster. The carpet gave way to stone worn smooth as river rock. Doors stood along either side, painted the green of Grandma Ida's kitchen chairs, and light came from under each one, gold and steady, and from behind them came sounds that unmade him: a father laughing, a dog he'd loved at nine, rain on a roof he'd slept under in a summer he'd never gotten back, his own name spoken in voices of people who had died or drifted, all of them saying *Theo, Theo, you're here, you came, we kept your place.*

At the end of the corridor stood a small table. On the table lay a book, open, its pages soft as cloth, and beside it a pen that looked like it had been carved from something pale and patient. The light above the table was the color of the kitchen where his grandmother had taught him to snap beans, and standing beside the table was a figure he had known since he was five years old — small, quick-eyed, wearing a coat the brown of wrens' feathers, unchanged in thirty years.

"Hello, Theo," Wren said. And there was such sorrow in it, such genuine, ruined tenderness, that he understood this creature had not wanted any of this either, and the understanding did not save him. "I'm so sorry. I argued for you. I want you to know that. I argued for you for twenty years."

"Where am I?" Theo asked, and his voice sounded young.

"Home," said the other voice, everywhere, courteous, glad. "You're home. We've kept your place. May we note your arrival? It's how we say *you belong.* It's how we make sure no one is ever lost here. Sign in, Theo Montrose, and be counted among the kept, and be lonely never again."

Behind him, far away and getting farther, August was screaming his name, and her footsteps were hammering down stairs that were growing longer between her and him with every second, the corridor politely, hospitably stretching, and Theo looked at the open page and saw his own name already written there in a hand like his grandmother's, waiting only for his confirmation, the way a seat waits, the way a place is kept.

He thought: *Augie will be so angry.*

He thought: *I'm so tired of apartments with thin walls.*

He thought: *Dad's laugh is right behind that door.*

"Refuse him!" August's voice, ragged, close now, so close. "Theo! Name it! Refuse it, I'm witnessing, I'M WITNESSING—"

The pen was warm in his hand, or his hand was warm around the pen, and the kindness of it, the sheer hospitality, broke the last cold window in him. All his life he had been the one who left. Here, finally, was a door designed to be entered. Here, finally, was a room with his name on it.

"I'm home," Theo said aloud, and it was a refusal of everything else, and he knew it, and he signed.

The pen touched nothing. The ink sank into the page like water into drought. Somewhere a count advanced by one, precise and pleased, and every green door along the corridor opened at once, spilling golden light and welcome, and the last thing Theo saw before the nearest door took him in — gently, by the arm, like family — was August bursting into the corridor with the ledger's twin clutched to her chest and her mouth shaping his name.

The door closed. The lock did not click. Kindly things don't need locks.

August reached the table as the ink dried. She stared at the page where his name now sat, dated, annotated, *arrived willingly, witnessed,* and the sound she made was not a scream; it was smaller and worse than that, the sound of a map tearing along a fold.

Wren stood in the golden light with her face in her hands.

"He argued for you," the cellar said warmly, consolingly, to August, to the corridor, to the dark. "For twenty years, he argued for you. Come. There's a place set for you too. We've kept it since before you were born — and dear child, you are *almost late.*"
