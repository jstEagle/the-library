## 1. Dead Air

The clock above the mixing board said 2:14 a.m., and Orsen Galloway had learned to distrust it the way other men distrusted mirrors in dark rooms—because sometimes it showed him something he wasn't ready to see.

Seven years of overnights at KVLN, 1340 on your AM dial, the voice of Cold Hollow and the surrounding valley, where the signal died at the county line and the mountains folded the frequency back in on itself like a hand closing. *The Night Watch*, they called his show, though nobody had watched anything but the ceiling of their bedrooms at this hour since the mill closed. He played the old songs—the ones the dead of this town had danced to when they were young—and he read the weather, and he took calls from anyone who couldn't sleep.

And for seven years, some of those callers had been dead.

He didn't think of them that way. That was the trick of it, the architecture of it, the careful cathedral he'd built inside his skull with its load-bearing walls of reasonable explanation: pranksters, drunks, kids with voice-changer apps, crossfaded insomniacs, the occasional genuine schizophrenic who'd found a sympathetic ear at 3 a.m. and meant no harm by it. Seven years was long enough to become an expert in the taxonomy of the wrong. He kept a logbook—a green ledger, actually, bought at the hardware store on Delmore Street because it reminded him of his father's accounts—and every strange call went into it, dated, timed, transcribed in his small neat hand, and then filed under one of his categories.

*Category A: Prank, juvenile.* *Category B: Prank, chemical.* *Category C: Psychological, caller's.* *Category D: Interference, atmospheric.* *Category E: Psychological, mine.*

Category E had only three entries in seven years. He was proud of that. It was the kind of pride a man takes in keeping a boat bailed—no one applauds it, but you know exactly how much water would be in the hull if you stopped.

The red ON AIR light burned its small patient eye at him. Outside the studio window, Main Street lay under a frost so fine it looked like breath held too long against glass. The heater ticked. The transmitter hummed its note that only he could hear anymore, a sound like a struck tuning fork buried in the wall.

"This is the Night Watch," he said into the microphone, and heard his own voice come back to him through the headphones, warm and slightly delayed, a stranger agreeing with him. "It's fourteen past two, it's nineteen degrees and falling, and if you're up, you might as well be up with me. Line one is open. Who's awake out there?"

The board lit.

"Night Watch, you're live."

Static first—that was normal, the valley ate signals—but beneath the static there was something else, a wetness to it, a sound like fabric moving through water. Then a woman's voice, young, unhurried, almost amused.

"Hi, Orsen."

They never said his name right. That was one of his rules, one of the stones in the wall: real callers said *Orsen* like they were handing him something; these voices said it like they were reading it off a stone. He reached for the logbook without thinking, pen already uncapped.

"You're on the air," he said. "What's keeping you up?"

"I wanted to tell someone about my funeral." A small laugh, bright as a bell rung underwater. "It was last Tuesday. You didn't come."

Orsen wrote the date. Tuesday. He flipped back through the week's pages out of habit, scanning the names of callers, the times, the little codes he used—*PJ* for prank juvenile, *ATM* for atmospheric, *UNK* for unknown pending category. Nothing on Tuesday but a man who'd wanted to hear "Blue Velvet" twice and a woman reporting a dog loose near the grain elevator.

"Funerals are hard on families," he said, in the voice he used for Category C, gentle and slightly distant, a man talking through glass. "Sometimes people dream about them before they happen. Sometimes after. It doesn't mean anything bad."

"It was nice, though," she went on, as if he hadn't spoken. "Small. The church was cold—they hadn't gotten the furnace fixed, remember? Everyone's breath was showing. My mother wore the gray coat. She cried into the collar so nobody would see, but I saw. I could see everything. That's the funny part nobody tells you—you can see everything, and you can't say a word."

The pen had stopped moving. Orsen noticed this the way you notice your own heartbeat during a scare—as a foreign fact about your body. He made himself write. *Caller describes own funeral. Tue. Details: cold church, gray coat.*

There was a Mrs. Ellery on Birch Lane whose daughter had passed in October. Car accident on the ridge road. He'd played her request at the memorial hour, something by Patsy Cline, and the whole town had listened the way this town listened to grief—politely, at a distance, with casseroles. The girl's name had been Dana. Dana Ellery. Twenty-three years old.

"Dana," he said carefully, "if this is you—"

"It's not me," the voice said, delighted. "That's what I keep trying to explain. It's not me anymore. The me that answered phones and burned toast and sat in the third pew, she's done. This is just what's left over, like heat in a pan after you turn off the stove. But I still get to talk. Isn't that something? They gave us the line, Orsen. Somebody left the line open."

He looked at the board. Line one glowed steady green. The signal meter rode its needle in small calm waves, exactly the way a living voice looks on a meter, which meant nothing, which meant the meter could not tell him what he needed it to tell him, which was the whole problem with meters.

"Who gave you the line?" he asked. He hated himself a little for asking. Every question was a crack in the wall, and he knew how the wall was built, mortar by mortar, year by year: *pranksters, drunks, interference, my own tired mind.* You did not ask follow-up questions. Follow-up questions were how men in logbooks ended up with a Category E.

But the voice only laughed again, softer now. "You always ask that. You asked it in March. You asked it the night of the ice storm. You'll ask it again next month. And we'll answer the same way, and you'll write it down in your green book, and in the morning you'll have coffee at the diner and tell yourself it was the Kowalski boys with a police scanner, even though the Kowalski boys moved to Billings in—" she paused, savoring it— "in 2019. Two years before you started asking."

The heater ticked. Somewhere down the block a plow scraped past, its blade ringing off the curb like a dull bell.

Orsen turned his microphone fader down—not all the way, never all the way, dead air was a firing offense even at 2 a.m.—and sat with his hand flat on the ledger. The page was cool under his palm. He thought about the wall. He thought about how much work a wall is, how you never stop laying it, how every brick has to be carried up by hand: *the delay on the line could be a recording; the details could be public; obituaries are printed, funerals are announced, gray coats are common.* Brick. *Her voice sounds young because grief makes everyone sound young, or because grief makes me hear what I fear.* Brick. *I am tired, and tired minds furnish empty rooms.* Brick, brick, brick.

And underneath, in the cellar of him, in the place the wall existed specifically to enclose, something turned over like a sleeper and settled again.

He brought the fader back up.

"That's quite a story, friend," he said, using the word *friend* the way a fence uses wire. "We've got a request line for dedications, and I'm going to give you the number, and I want you to take care of yourself. It's cold out there tonight. Nineteen degrees and falling."

"Falling," she agreed warmly. "Everything here falls. That's how the patterns start."

He let two seconds of silence stand—he was allowed two seconds; the consultant from the syndicate had told him silence read as gravitas—and then he segued into the record he'd cued twenty minutes ago, Roy Orbison, "Only the Lonely," because the dead or the drunk or the dreaming deserved a song same as anybody, and because music was another brick, wide and flat and load-bearing, laid across the whole conversation like a lid.

The song played. He logged the call. *2:14–2:19. F. Claims deceased (Ellery?). Details re: funeral—cold church, gray coat, mother crying. Cat. B probable (chemical). Possible C. Recheck obits.*

That last bit was new. In seven years he had never once written *recheck obits*, and seeing it in his own handwriting frightened him more than the voice had, because handwriting was evidence, and evidence was the opposite of a wall. He drew a single line through it, neat, deliberate, the way his father had struck through errors in the account books—never erased, never hidden, just cancelled, acknowledged and refused—and closed the ledger.

* * *

At 4:40 the frost on the studio window had grown teeth.

Orsen noticed it while reading the four o'clock farm report, his eyes drifting the way they did in the dead hours, and he stopped mid-sentence, on air, for a full three seconds—which the consultant would have called an eternity—because the frost wasn't ferning the way frost ferns. Frost made feathers, made rivers, made the branching lungs of cold air finding warm. This made letters. Not legible ones, not yet—or maybe legible and he was refusing to read them, which amounted to the same thing and cost less. Rows of small white strokes, ranked and regular, climbing the lower corner of the glass like a list being written by a very patient hand.

He finished the farm report. His voice behaved. Inside, some clerk in the office of his mind stamped a form: *atmospheric conditions, single-pane glass, south-facing exposure,* and filed it, and the filing was the wall working, the wall working beautifully, and Orsen thought: *I am so tired of how well it works.*

He signed off at five with the sign-off he'd used for seven years, the one his predecessor had used for thirty before him, the words worn smooth as river stones: *"This has been the Night Watch. The sun comes up whether you're ready or not. Be kind to each other, Cold Hollow. Keep the line open."*

Keep the line open. He'd said it ten thousand times without hearing it. Tonight it followed him out of the booth and down the stairs into the blue cold of the parking lot, where his truck wore a skin of frost so thick the windshield was opaque, and he stood scraping it in the dark with his breath showing, thinking about a girl in a gray coat's mother, crying into her collar where nobody could see.

Except somebody had seen. Somebody had described it, detail by detail, in a voice like a bell underwater, and Orsen had written it all down in a green book and drawn a line through the one true thing.

The engine caught. The heater groaned. As he pulled onto Main Street, the town lay silent around him, eleven hundred souls asleep under their roofs, every window furred white, every pane of glass in Cold Hollow holding the same faint, patient, unreadable writing—

and behind him, in the darkened studio, the phone board lit up, line after line after line, all of them open, none of them answered, ringing softly into the empty room like a choir clearing its throat.
