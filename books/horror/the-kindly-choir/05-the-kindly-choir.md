## 5. The Kindly Choir

The change came on a Tuesday, the way most quiet catastrophes do, wearing the clothes of an ordinary night.

Orsen had been on the air for two hours when the first voice arrived—not speaking this time, but humming. A single held tone, low and round, like a cello bow drawn through cold water. It came in on the board as a call from a number he had long since stopped logging, one of the dead lines that lit up on nights when the frost was thick on the transmitter tower, and instead of the usual recitation—*give it back, Orsen, give back what was taken*—there was only that note, sustained, patient as a held breath.

He let it run. He told himself he was waiting for it to resolve into words, into the familiar accusation, into something he could file under interference or prank or the slow erosion of his own mind. But the truth was simpler and worse: the note was beautiful, and he did not want to cut it.

It lasted forty seconds. Then it faded the way a tide withdraws, not ending so much as going elsewhere, and the dead air afterward felt like the silence after a church bell.

"KGLW, one-forty on your dial," Orsen said, and heard how his voice had gone soft. "That was... a request, I suppose. From an old friend."

He did not say *from the dead*. He had spent seven years building a vocabulary of evasion—*pranksters, line noise, atmospheric bounce, a tired man's imagination*—and the vocabulary held, the way a levee holds, right up until the night it doesn't.

* * *

The second voice came at 2:14. The third at 2:31. They did not speak either. They hummed, and their tones were different—lower, higher, a minor third apart—and when they sounded together, Orsen felt the harmony settle into his chest like a hand laid flat over his sternum, not pressing, just resting. Just confirming he was there.

He knew the intervals. That was the terrible part. He had sung in the Methodist choir as a boy, back before his voice broke and his family scattered, and he knew what it meant when voices found each other in the dark and locked into consonance without rehearsal. It meant they had sung together before. It meant they had *practiced*.

By three o'clock there were six of them, and they were not humming anymore.

They were singing a melody.

It was simple—four notes, descending, then rising again like something surfacing and submerging, surfacing and submerging. No words. No accusation. No recitation of his private sins, no naming of the things only he could know. Just the melody, passed voice to voice like a bucket down a line, each singer taking it up exactly where the last had set it down, so that it never broke, never breathed, never stopped.

Orsen sat with his hand hovering over the fader and did not pull it down.

He thought of Maren Voss's face in the studio doorway two weeks ago, frost melting off her coat, her eyes red-rimmed and furious. *It's on my windows, Orsen. It's writing. My crew hears the radio in trucks with no batteries. Tell me what it wants.* He had told her the truth, which was that he didn't know, and had watched her decide he was either a liar or a coward, and had not blamed her, because he had spent seven years deciding the same thing about himself.

But this—this was not wanting. He knew what wanting sounded like. Seven years of calls had been want, had been hunger, had been *give it back, give back what was taken, Orsen, Orsen, Orsen*, a debt called in nightly with the patience of something that had already waited fifty years and could wait fifty more.

This was different. This was the sound of something *preparing*.

"You're rehearsing," he said into the live microphone, very quietly, knowing the board was open, knowing they could hear him the way he could hear them. "Aren't you. You're rehearsing something."

The melody paused. All six voices, at once, the way a congregation pauses when the minister lifts his hand.

Then, from the deepest register, a voice he recognized—Edith Harrow's, or the thing wearing the memory of Edith Harrow, the woman whose tapes he had listened to until 4 a.m. with the lights on—said, gently, almost kindly:

*Not yet, Orsen. Not yet. But soon.*

And the singing resumed.

* * *

The calls started coming in at 3:40. Living calls. He knew they were living before he answered because the board lit up with numbers he recognized—the Kesslers' farm line, the diner's back office, the number for the Voss site trailer that Maren had given him in a moment of grim alliance.

"Orsen." Mrs. Aldous Kessler, seventy-one, her voice hushed the way voices get in hospitals. "Orsen, what *is* that? I've had the radio on low since midnight, the way I always do, and—there's music. There's singing. I thought at first it was the choir from St. Ambrose practicing for Christmas, but it's October, and it's—Orsen, it's coming up out of the floor. I can feel it in my feet."

"I hear it," Orsen said.

"It's *lovely*," she said, and then, horrified at herself: "Orsen, why did I say that? Why does it sound lovely? It shouldn't sound lovely. It should sound like—"

She didn't finish. He heard her breathing, quick and shallow, and then the line went soft with the particular click of an old woman setting down a receiver very carefully, as if the phone might bite.

The next caller was the night manager at the diner. "Whatever you're playing over there, play it again. My whole shift's been standing around listening. Nobody's complained. Nobody's *talked*, even. We just—" He laughed, and the laugh had a strange, loosened quality, like a man unclenching a fist he'd forgotten he was making. "We just listened. Is it a tape? Is it yours?"

"It's not mine," Orsen said.

"Whose is it?"

And Orsen, staring at the six lights glowing on his board like votive candles, heard himself say: "It's theirs. It's been theirs a long time. They're just letting us hear it now."

* * *

By four, the melody had grown a second part. A descant, high and thin, threading above the main line like frost ferning across glass. Orsen could not have said where the pattern was going—music theory had abandoned him along with everything else he'd once trusted—but his body knew what his mind refused to say. The melody was *building*. It had the architecture of a thing being assembled note by note, verse by verse, the way you'd build a wall, or a bridge, or a door.

*The door unbolts itself*, Edith's tapes had said, in the flat voice of a woman dictating her own damnation. *When the singing is finished, the door unbolts itself.*

He had thought, listening alone in the archive room, that she meant something small. A cellar door. The one the excavation crew had found at the Harrow farm, bolted from the inside, bolted from within by hands that had then gone on being dead for fifty years.

Now, listening to the choir climb toward a cadence it refused to reach, he understood that the door might be larger than that. Doors usually are.

The seventh voice joined at 4:22. The eighth at 4:40. Each arrival was marked on the board by a dead line flickering to life, and each new voice found its place in the harmony instantly, seamlessly, as though it had been standing just offstage all along, waiting for its cue. Orsen found himself counting them the way you count the days until something you dread—or something you long for; the arithmetic was identical.

Nine.

Ten.

He should have pulled the plug. He knew that. Somewhere in the station's guts was a master breaker, and one hard decision would have put him off the air, would have silenced the choir for every listening household, would have bought the town another night of not knowing. He had spent seven years making exactly that kind of decision, night after night, cutting the calls, logging them as noise, keeping the levee standing with his two bare hands.

His hand lay on the desk. It did not move toward the breaker.

*You're rehearsing something,* he had said. *Not yet, Orsen. Not yet. But soon.*

The melody turned. It was the closest thing to speech the music had offered—a turn in the phrase, a leaning, the way a sentence leans toward its final word. And in the turn he heard, or believed he heard, or wanted so badly to hear that hearing and wanting became the same act: the eleven names. Not spoken. Sung. Folded into the descant like seeds into winter soil, one by one by one, the names Edith Harrow had recorded on her cassette tapes in a shaking voice, the names the bound volumes of the *Gazette* did not contain, the names the town had paid for fifty years to keep out of its mouth.

The choir was learning the names. The choir was going to sing the names to everyone.

And Orsen Galloway, who had built his whole careful life on not knowing, sat in his studio at the top of the water tower hill with frost creeping down the inside of the glass in long, deliberate strokes—letters, he realized, they were forming letters, the frost was *writing*—and found that he did not want it to stop.

That was the horror, he understood then. Not the dead. Not the debt. The horror was that the singing was beautiful, and he was tired of silence, and some traitorous chamber of his heart had already chosen a side.

* * *

At 5:47, eleven minutes before the end of his shift, the melody stopped.

Not faded. Stopped—mid-phrase, mid-breath, all eleven voices (when had it become eleven? when had it become *exactly* eleven?) cutting off together like a door closing. The dead air that followed was so total he could hear the transmitter humming two rooms away, could hear his own pulse in his ears, could hear the frost ticking against the window as it wrote.

Then Edith Harrow's voice, alone, close, as though her mouth were pressed to the microphone on the other side of whatever separates the living from the paid:

*We've saved you a part, Orsen.*

*The melody's finished. All it needs now is the words.*

*Read us the names.*

The board went dark. Every light, every line, all at once, and the studio was so quiet that Orsen could hear, faint and far below the floor, beneath the concrete, beneath the hill itself, a sound like a great bolt—slowly, patiently, with fifty years of practice behind it—

beginning to slide.
