## 6. Nineteen Seventy-Four

*The tapes were found in a bread tin beneath the floorboards of the Harrow farmhouse, wrapped in oilcloth and wax paper, forty-one cassettes in all, labeled in a woman's careful hand: dates only, running from November 3rd, 1974 to February 28th, 1975. The crew turned them over to the county historical society. The historical society turned them over to no one. They sat in a banker's box for eleven months before Maren Voss carried them out under her coat and brought them to the station, because she had stopped trusting institutions and had started trusting Orsen Galloway, which frightened her more than the frost did.*

*What follows is transcribed from the recordings of Edith Harrow, née Voss, aged sixty-one at the time of taping.*

---

**TAPE ONE — NOVEMBER 3RD, 1974**

The hiss first. Then a chair creaking, the clink of glass on wood, and a voice like dry leaves being raked.

"This is Edith Harrow. If anyone ever hears this, I expect they'll be sorting through what's left of this house, so let me save you the trouble of wondering. I am recording what happened here because I don't trust my memory anymore, and I trust the people of this town less.

"Today the Alderman came to my door with his hat in his hands, which is how he comes when he wants something he's ashamed of wanting. He sat where you're sitting—well, where nobody's sitting—and he looked at my husband's photograph for a long time before he said a word. Then he said: *Edith, we have a problem, and the problem has a name, and the name is hunger.*

"I'll write down what he told me as best as I can hold it, though holding it is like holding water.

"There is something under this valley. Under the frost line. He didn't say it the way I'm saying it—he said it the way men say things when they've said them to themselves too many times and the words have gone smooth as river stones. But that was the shape of it. Something old, something patient, something that was here before the town and will be here after, and every so often—in hard winters, in hungry winters—it wakes up and asks.

"And the town answers."

---

**TAPE FOUR — NOVEMBER 17TH, 1974**

"They met at the grange hall on Thursday. I wasn't invited. Nobody was invited; that was the point. But Dale Kettering's wife talks in her sleep and her sleep talks to me, and so I know.

"Eleven names. That's what I keep coming back to. Eleven. There were eleven families at the founding potluck in 1911 and someone decided that was arithmetic worth honoring, or maybe it's just how many the thing below asked for—I don't know, and I hate that I don't know, and I hate more that part of me doesn't want to know.

"The names weren't drawn from a hat. Let no one ever say they drew lots, because lots would be honest. They made a list. They sat around a folding table with coffee and store-bought cookies and they made a *list*, and the criteria were discussed aloud, I'm told, in the tone of men discussing fence lines.

"The Hollis brothers first—they'd been poaching off county land and one of them put a shotgun through a window at the deputy's house. Then the Marchetti family, three of them, because they were new and spoke loud and the bank had already been circling their farm. The woman who lived up past the quarry—the one who read cards, who some of the churchgoing ladies secretly visited and then lied about visiting. Her name was Serafine. She went on the list because she knew things, and knowing things is dangerous when the thing you know is the thing everyone agreed not to say.

"A drifter called Tomlinson. A boy of nineteen named Amos Pell who got a girl pregnant and wouldn't say whose girl. The two Vances, uncle and nephew, who drank and fought each other and everyone else. And the last name—the one that made the room go quiet, Dale's wife says—was a child. Not a child of the town. A foundling, left on the church steps that October, that nobody had claimed, that the minister had been feeding out of his own pocket.

"They put the baby on the list because, and I am quoting what was said in that room, *it would be crueler to leave it.*

"You understand what I'm telling you. They dressed it up as mercy. They always do. Cruelty wears kindness's coat in this town the way frost wears grass."

---

**TAPE NINE — DECEMBER 2ND, 1974**

"It happened last night. I heard it. God forgive me, I heard it, and I did not go out, and I did not call anyone, and I lay in my bed with the quilt over my head like a child and listened to my neighbors walk eleven people into the white dark.

"Not dragged. Walked. That's the detail I can't get out from under. There was singing. Low, unhurried, the kind of singing men do when they want to seem calm to themselves. And underneath it, the ones being walked were quiet—not silent, quiet, the way you're quiet when you've been told there's no use making noise, or the way you're quiet when something has gotten into your head and smoothed it over like a hand smoothing bedclothes.

"My window frosted over from the inside. In December, in a warm house, with the stove going. It frosted over and the frost made shapes, and I pressed my thumb against the glass to break them and my thumbprint stayed black for two days.

"At dawn the men came back without coats stiff with cold but with faces loose and wrong, and by Sunday they were at church singing louder than anyone, and Pastor Emmons preached on Abraham and Isaac and never once said the word *substitute*, and everyone nodded along like it was a fine sermon, a kindly sermon, and the coffee after was hot and the pie was good and nobody's hands shook except mine.

"That's when I understood what the Alderman meant about warmth. It isn't the furnace. It isn't the woodpile. The town stays warm because the town pays, and the payment is people, and the people are always the ones nobody will miss—or the ones everybody will agree not to miss, which is easier and cheaper and requires no digging at all."

---

**TAPE TWENTY-TWO — JANUARY 14TH, 1975**

"I saw one today. Not one of the eleven—one of the *others*. The things that come up when the debt is paid on time. Or maybe it was one of the eleven. Maybe that's worse. Maybe the ones they give it aren't ended but changed, sent back out as its hands and mouth.

"It stood at the tree line behind the barn at dusk, in the exact shape of a person but with the frost moving inside it instead of blood—you could see it, the white branching through it like veins seen through skin, like ferns growing in fast time. It didn't approach. It just stood, and I understood it was counting the windows of my house. Learning the layout. Filing me away.

"Then it raised one hand—not a wave, nothing so human—and pressed its palm flat against the air, and everywhere its fingers touched, frost bloomed out of nothing, hanging in the cold like writing on invisible glass.

"I think it was signing something. I think everything it does is signing something, and the snow is its paper, and this whole valley is a ledger it keeps, and we are all entries in it whether we signed or not.

"I did not sign. I want that written down somewhere besides my own guilty heart: *I did not sign.* But my husband's land touches theirs, and my water comes from their ground, and my winter wheat grows fat on soil that drinks what it drinks, and so I eat from the same table. That's the trick of it. That's the filthy genius. You don't have to agree. You only have to stay."

---

**TAPE THIRTY-SEVEN — FEBRUARY 19TH, 1975**

"The Alderman came again. He knows about the tapes. Of course he knows; this town has ears where its conscience should be. He didn't ask me to stop. He asked me to keep them somewhere safe, and then he sat with his hat in his hands again and said the thing I have been dreading since November, the thing I now set down so that whoever finds these will understand what they're living on top of.

"He said: *It's not a debt, Edith. Debts end. It's an inheritance.*

"Every family that stays inherits it. Every child born under this frost is born owing, whether they know it or not, whether they consented or not—which none of us did, which none of our children ever will. The eleven in December bought us one calm winter. One. And the interest is already accruing, and the next payment will come due the way payments do, quietly and then all at once, and the list will be made again at a folding table with coffee and store-bought cookies, and the names on it will be different and the arithmetic will be the same.

"He said his grandfather signed the first agreement in 1911. He showed me the page. Paper gone brown as autumn, ink gone the color of dried blood, and at the bottom, thirty-one signatures, and beneath the signatures, in a hand that shivered even then, a single line:

***We give willingly so that we may remain.***

"Willingly. I looked at that word a long time. Willing as a man is willing with a knife at his back. Willing as a town is willing when the alternative is written in frost on every window.

"And then the Alderman said the other thing. The thing that made me start these tapes tonight instead of burning them.

"He said: *Someday someone will refuse. Someone will say the truth out loud, on the airwaves maybe, or in the square, and the seal will break, and everything the silence has been holding shut will need somewhere to go.* He said it like a man describing weather coming from far off. He said: *When that day comes, the door will open from the inside, and someone will have to walk through carrying what we did, or the frost will finish its pattern across every soul who stayed quiet.*

"I asked him how he could know such a thing.

"He said: Because it told us. At the beginning. It told us exactly what it wanted, and we were so relieved it only wanted that, we signed without reading the rest."

---

**TAPE FORTY-ONE — FEBRUARY 28TH, 1975**

"Last tape. My hands aren't good tonight. The frost is on the windows again and this time it isn't shapes. This time it's letters. This time it's my own handwriting, spelled out in ice across the north pane, and I did not write it, and it says:

***EDITH. THE CHOIR IS PRACTICING. COME AND HEAR.***

"I won't go. I want that recorded. I am sixty-one years old and I did not sign and I will not walk anywhere I'm summoned.

"But I hear it anyway, through the walls, through the ground, low and patient and beautiful, God help me, beautiful the way a trap is beautiful when you finally see all its working parts. They're singing the eleven names. All night they've been singing the eleven names, over and over, softer each time, the way you'd sing a child down to sleep.

"And now—now they've added a twelfth name to the song. A name that hasn't been given yet. A name that sounds, if I lean close to the glass, if I press my ear to the frozen pane until my skin burns—

"It sounds young. It sounds like it belongs to someone not yet born.

"It sounds like it's waiting for a radio man.

"If you're hearing this—if you're the one it's waiting for—I'm sorry. We were all so busy staying warm that we built the cold a door, and we bolted it from the inside, and we told ourselves that was safety.

"Open it. Open it and read the names. That's all it ever asked. That's all it's been asking, all these years, patient as frost:

*Say what we did. Say it out loud, to everyone, and then come down and give it back.*"

*The tape ends mid-breath. On the reverse side, unmarked, there is ninety seconds of singing—eleven voices, low and harmonized, repeating a twelfth name over and over.*

*The name is Galloway.*
