## 4. Yago's Room, Unchanged

The hallway to Yago's room smelled the way it always had—cedar and dust and the faint green ghost of the alfalfa fields drifting in under the window sash. Yara stood outside his door for a long moment with her hand flat against the wood, as if she might feel him through it, some residual warmth, the way a stove holds heat after the fire dies. The wood was cool. Of course it was cool. The dead do not warm rooms, and whatever was lying in the casket in the front parlor was not, in any way that mattered, her brother.

She went in anyway.

The room was unchanged. That was the first cruelty of it. Twenty years she'd been gone—college, the city, a life measured in leases and sublets and boxes marked *miscellaneous*—and this room had been waiting for her like a held breath. The same model airplanes dangled from the ceiling on fishing line, their propellers stilled mid-turn. The same pennant from the county fair, sun-bleached to the color of weak tea. The same desk under the window with its carved initial—*Y*—that Yago had made with a pocketknife at eleven, and beside it, in a smaller, guiltier hand, a second letter she had always pretended not to see: *y*, lowercase, tucked into the curve of his like a chick under a hen's wing. He'd carved her in with him. He'd been carving her in with him since before either of them understood that people could leave.

She touched the desk. Dust came away on her fingertips in a soft gray bloom, and beneath it the wood was smooth as river stone, worn by a boy's forearms through a thousand homework assignments and a thousand idle afternoons. Someone had dusted this room. Someone had kept it. Her mother, presumably, moving through it once a week with a cloth and a silence, tending the shrine of the twin who stayed.

*The twin who stayed.* Yara pressed her knuckles to her mouth. The corpse downstairs had her face. She kept circling back to that, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. She had looked at the dead man—her dead brother, the death certificate said, Yago Emilio Sandoval, thirty-one years, agricultural accident, the county coroner's tidy script—and she had seen her own cheekbones, her own jaw, her own crooked left incisor, and the small white scar above her eyebrow where she'd split her forehead open falling off the tractor tire at seven. The scar. The mortician had even gotten the scar right, a thin crescent in the wrong eyebrow, mirrored, as though someone had held her face up to a glass and copied what they saw there.

Her mother had stood at the edge of the room with her hands folded and her eyes on the carpet, and when Yara had said, *Mama, that's my face*, Elena Sandoval had answered, in a voice like a woman reading a grocery list, "Grief does strange things to the eyes, mija."

Grief does strange things to the eyes. As if the whole town hadn't filed past the casket and shaken Yara's hand with condolences for *her own* death. As if old Mrs. Pruitt hadn't looked into the box and then looked at Yara and gone gray as dishwater, crossing herself with a hand that shook.

Yara turned in a slow circle in the middle of her brother's room, breathing the dust, and made herself look at everything.

The bed was made with hospital corners, though no one had slept in it. His boots stood under the chair, laces tucked in the way their father had taught them both, so the leather wouldn't crease. On the nightstand, a glass of water gone to dust and a rosary coiled like something sleeping. And on the desk, half under the lamp with its green glass shade, a notebook. Black cover, cloth-bound, the kind sold at the drugstore in town for three dollars. The cover was bowed with use, swollen with everything stuffed inside it, and a rubber band held it shut, gone brittle, snapped in two when she touched it.

She didn't sit down. She stood at the desk the way you stand at a grave, and she opened the book.

The first thing she saw was tally marks.

They covered the page in neat rows of four with a fifth struck through, the way prisoners count days, the way children count anything—fence posts, crows on a wire, the seconds between lightning and thunder. Row after row, page after page. She turned the pages and the tallies went on, and on, and somewhere in the middle of the notebook they began to change character. They got smaller. Denser. They began to cluster toward the bottom right corner of each page as though the numbers were afraid of something at the top. And in the margins, in Yago's cramped left-slanting hand, dates. She found the most recent one and her stomach dropped through the floorboards of herself.

*October 14.* Three days ago. The tallies on that page stopped at 341.

She counted backward. It took her a moment to understand what she was counting, and when she understood it, she had to put her hand flat on the desk and breathe, because the room tilted. Three hundred and forty-one days. A year, minus the twenty-four days left until the harvest moon. He'd been counting down to it. Not counting up from some beginning—counting *down*, the way you count down to a thing you know is coming, the way you count down when you already know the number the count ends at.

The harvest moon. The whole town said those two words the way other towns said *homecoming* or *county fair*, with a fondness that had a floor of something else beneath it, the way ice has water beneath it. *See you at the harvest moon, Yara.* *Big moon coming, always a good moon for the Sandovals.* She'd thought it small talk. She'd thought it was the way farm people talked about weather and luck.

She turned back to the beginning of the notebook, because that was what she did, what she had always done: when the world went wrong, she went looking for the first page of it.

The earliest pages were not writing at all. They were drawings.

Crayon, mostly, the wax laid on thick and waxy-bright, the kind of drawings a child makes with their whole arm and their whole heart. A sun with too many rays. A farmhouse with a door and two windows and smoke curling from a crooked chimney. Two stick figures holding hands in the yard, one taller, one shorter, and she knew without knowing how she knew that the taller one was Yago and the shorter one was her, because the shorter one had a scribbled scar over her stick-figure eye.

And beside them, a third figure.

Yara went very still.

The third figure was drawn in a different color—brown, a brown so dark it was nearly black, pressed so hard into the paper that the crayon had left a waxy ridge she could feel with her fingertip. It was the same height as the two children. It held no one's hand. It stood a little apart, at the edge of the yard, at the very border of the drawing where the grass stopped and the white of the paper began, and it had no face. Not a blank face—a *no* face. Where the eyes and mouth should have been, the paper itself seemed to have been rubbed thin, almost through, as if a small hand had gone over and over that spot with an eraser, or a thumb, trying to make a face appear and failing, or trying to make one disappear and succeeding.

She knew him. Of course she knew him. Her hands knew before her mind did; they were already turning the page.

*Tam.*

The name arrived in her head with the smell of cut grass and the taste of penny candy and the sound of a porch swing's chain creaking, and Yara sat down hard in her brother's desk chair, because her knees had made the decision for her. Tam. Their Tam. The friend they'd invented the summer they were seven, the one they'd blamed for the broken window and the missing pies and the muddy footprints on the white kitchen ceiling that their father had stared at for a long, long time before deciding not to ask. Tam, who had a face they could never quite describe to their mother, who lived, they'd told her, *in the spaces between the house and the outside*, who could only come as close as the porch. Tam, who they'd stopped playing with when they were nine or ten, the way you stop believing in things, gradually and then all at once, and who they'd never once mentioned again, not in twenty years, not to anyone, as though they'd agreed without agreeing to bury him in the same unmarked grave where children bury their gods.

Yago had never stopped drawing him.

Page after page. Tam in the yard. Tam on the porch swing. Tam standing at the fence line in the rain, drawn in blue-gray, the rain falling *around* him but not on him. Tam at the edge of the alfalfa field at night, and here the crayon had been pressed so hard the paper tore. And in every drawing, the same no-face, the same rubbed-thin absence, except—Yara leaned close, her nose nearly touching the paper, her breath fogging the old wax—in the later drawings, faintly, in pencil over crayon, in a hand older and shakier than a seven-year-old's, Yago had begun to give him features. Two dots for eyes. A line for a mouth. And the mouth was smiling. It was smiling in every single one, and it was always a little too wide, and it never reached the eyes, and the eyes never changed at all.

*He's not imaginary,* Yago had written once, in pencil, at the bottom of a drawing dated—she checked the date, checked it twice—dated the year they were twelve. *He's just ours.*

* * *

The light moved across the room while she read. It went from gold to copper to the bruised violet of early evening, and she did not get up, did not turn on the lamp, sat in the gathering dark of her brother's room reading his life the way you'd read a letter from someone who died before they finished it.

The drawings gave way, slowly, to words. Sentences in the laborious print of a child learning that letters go on lines. Then cursive, taught by their mother at the kitchen table, the loops of it loosening over the years into something fast and slanted and private. The notebook wasn't a diary, exactly. It was a log. A record. Yago had kept it the way a lighthouse keeper keeps a log of ships, or the way a man who lives under a bad roof keeps a record of every crack in the ceiling, watching for the ones that spread.

*June 3. Tam sat on the porch swing for 4 hours. He doesn't swing. He just sits and the swing doesn't move even when the wind moves the trees.*

*August 19. The tally is at 200. Mama put salt on the windowsills and told me it was for the ants. We don't get ants.*

*September 2. Asked Delgado about the field where nothing grows. He looked at me like I'd asked him about a funeral. Maybe I had.*

*October 30. Harvest moon tomorrow. Mama made me stay inside. She made Yara stay inside too but Yara doesn't know why. I heard her and Mama arguing through the floor. Mama said "not this generation, I won't." Grandpa's voice was in it too but Grandpa's been dead six years. I didn't tell Yara. She'd want to look. Yara always wants to look.*

Yara read that last line three times. *Yara always wants to look.* She could hear his voice saying it, that dry half-smiling tone he'd had, the older-by-four-minutes tone, and something in her chest cracked along a seam she hadn't known was there. She had left. That was the whole of it, the plain ugly whole of it. At eighteen she had left this farm, this town, this brother, and she had told herself it was ambition, it was scholarships, it was a girl getting out while the getting was good, and some nights, in the honest dark, she had admitted it was simpler: she had left because Yago was staying, and one of them had to, and she had made sure it wasn't her. She'd let him be the one who stayed the way you let someone else take the weight end of the load. And he had stayed, and he had watched, and he had counted, and he had written it all down in a three-dollar notebook because there had been no one else to tell.

*She'd want to look. Yara always wants to look.* He had protected her from the looking. And she had repaid him by not being here when the looking finally came for him.

The pages darkened as the years went on. The handwriting tightened. The entries got shorter, the way men talk less when the thing they're carrying gets heavier.

*The tally is at 100.*

*Tally 50. Saw him at the fence line. He waved. He has Mama's wave now.*

*Tally 12. Mama chalked the porch again. I watched her do it. She saw me watching. Neither of us said anything. We're very polite in this house. We're so polite I could scream.*

*Tally 3.*

*Tally 2.*

And then the last page. The last entry. No tally at all—just a date, yesterday's date, the day before he died, and beneath it, in handwriting pressed so deep into the paper it had torn through to the cardboard backing, four words:

*It's not me they want.*

Yara sat in the dark with the notebook open in her lap and read the four words until they stopped being words and became a sound, a ringing, like a struck bell that would not finish decaying. It's not me they want. Not me. Not the twin who stayed, who kept the count, who learned the rules, who watched his mother chalk the porch and said nothing because that was the family way, the town way, the polite way. Not the brother whose face should have been in the casket and wasn't.

Her face was in the casket.

The scar was in the wrong place. She had noticed it at the viewing and filed it away as the mortician's error, grief's trick, some small mundane wrongness in a week already too full of wrongness. But it wasn't an error. It was a mirror. Whatever lay in that box had been made from her—not from memory, not from photographs, but from *her*, the way you'd copy a key from the original—and mirrors reverse. It had studied her face so closely, so lovingly, that it had reproduced even the scar, and reproduced it backwards.

It's not me they want. It's not *me* they want.

The house settled around her, the old bones of it ticking as the day's heat left the walls, and every sound was suddenly a footstep, and every silence was suddenly a held breath. Yara became aware, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming, of the window at her back—the dark square of it, the fields beyond it, and somewhere out there, at the border where the yard became the world, the fence line where a no-faced thing had stood in every drawing her brother had ever made, waiting, patient as a debt.

She looked at the window's reflection in the black glass of the dead television across the room. Her own face floated there, pale in the dark, and for one vertiginous second she could not swear which of them it was—the living sister or the thing in the box—and she understood, then, with total and terrible clarity, what her brother had understood at the end, alone in this room, with two days left on his count.

He had died so that it wouldn't be her. He had stood in the door of this room and let the accident take him, or had walked into it, or had made some bargain of his own—because the tally was at two, because the moon was coming, because *it's not me they want*, and Yago Sandoval had spent his whole life carving his sister's initial into the curve of his own.

She pressed the notebook to her chest and the sobs came up out of her like water from a broken main, ugly and endless, grief and guilt in one indistinguishable flood. She cried for the boy who had counted to three hundred and forty-one. She cried for the man in the box with her face. She cried for the seven-year-old she'd been who had invented a friend and then abandoned him, abandoned everything, driven away with the radio loud so she wouldn't hear her brother's hand slapping the roof of the car.

The crying ran out, the way crying does, leaving her hollow and ringing.

And in the ringing silence, from downstairs, from the front door of the farmhouse—clear, warm, unmistakable, a sound she had known since before she had words—

Someone knocked.

Three knocks. A pause. Two more.

Yago's knock. The knock they'd invented together at seven, the secret knock, the *it's us* knock. And then, muffled through the floorboards, through the walls, through twenty years of silence, a voice she knew better than her own called up the stairs, gentle and patient and horribly kind:

"Yara? It's me. Let me in, *yarita*. It's cold out here."
