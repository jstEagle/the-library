## 6. Delgado's Debt

Yara found him at the edge of the church parking lot, the way you find a man who wants to be found but doesn't want to ask. Old Delgado stood beside a pickup truck the color of dried blood, hat in his hands, watching the funeral home windows like they might watch back.

He'd been at the wake. They all had—the whole town had come through in a slow, murmuring procession, casseroles and condolences and eyes that slid off her face the way water slides off waxed tin. But Delgado was the only one who hadn't looked away fast enough. He was the only one whose look had held something other than pity.

"Miss Sandoval." He said it like an apology. "You got a minute?"

"I've got nothing but minutes," she said. "That's sort of the problem."

He didn't smile. He opened the passenger door of the truck for her, and something about the gesture—old-fashioned, courtly, final—made her understand that this was not a conversation he intended to have standing up in daylight where anyone could see them.

She got in. The cab smelled of diesel and tobacco and, underneath it, faintly, of chalk.

* * *

Delgado drove them out past the last of the town lights, past the co-op with its darkened grain elevator, past the water tower with HARMONY painted on it in letters that had faded to the color of old bone. He drove without speaking, and Yara let him, because she had learned in the last three days that silence in this town was not empty. It was full. It was a jar packed to the brim with things nobody would say, sealed tight, passed hand to hand down the generations.

The truck turned onto a gravel road she didn't know, though she'd grown up ten miles from here. That was the thing about Harmony—there were roads you knew and roads that seemed to keep themselves private, unmarked on any map, known only to the families who needed them.

He stopped where the road ended. Beyond the fence line, a field stretched out under the early dusk, flat and pale and utterly bare.

Not fallow. Not plowed-under. *Bare.* No stubble, no weeds, no volunteer corn, none of the stubborn green trash that invades any field left alone for a single season. The soil lay smooth as poured concrete, gray-brown, untouched even by birds. Yara had seen desert lots in Phoenix with more life on them.

"What is this place?" she asked.

"My field," Delgado said. "Forty acres. Best soil in the county, my grandfather used to say. Best soil God ever turned over." He killed the engine. In the sudden quiet, Yara could hear how complete the silence was out there—not one insect, not one bird settling into the fencerow. October should have been loud with crickets. "Nothing grows here now. Nothing has since 1987."

* * *

Hector Delgado had been twenty-six years old when the land stopped taking what he planted, and he had spent thirty-seven years learning why.

He'd been raised on the bargain the way farm kids are raised on weather—absorbed without instruction, understood without explanation. His father had told him once, drunk on Christmas Eve, that the Sandovals and the Delgados and the Okonkwos and the Vances were founding families, and that founding meant something different out here than it did in the history books. Founding meant signing. There had been a paper, or there hadn't; there had been words spoken over turned earth in 1849 by men whose names were now on the courthouse plaque, and the earth had listened, and the earth had answered.

*Give us one,* the land had asked—or so the story went, though Hector suspected the asking had come from the other direction, from whatever had been here before the plows, patient under the prairie grass, hungry in a way that had nothing to do with soil. *One of your own, each generation. Invited in freely. And the ground will never fail you. The rains will come when you need them and stop when you don't. Your cattle will fatten. Your children will live.*

And the ground had kept its word. Through the Dust Bowl, while counties around them blew away in black walls, Harmony's fields had stayed green. Through the eighties, when the farm crisis took half the towns in the state like a scythe takes wheat, Harmony's banks stayed open, its tractors ran, its families ate. Prosperity sat on this town like a blessing, and everyone drove past the plaque on the courthouse lawn and said grace over it at supper and never, ever said the rest aloud.

Because the rest was simple. Every generation, one of their own went into the ground—or rather, something came out of the ground wearing one of their own, and the one who had been invited did not come back. Not dead, exactly. Harvested. The way you harvest is not the way you kill.

Hector's father had explained it to him the winter Hector turned nineteen, sitting in this same truck, pointing at the field through the windshield. *It won't be me,* his father had said. *I paid already—I gave my brother. It'll be you or your sister, when your time comes. You'll get a feeling about it first. A knowing. And when you feel it, boy, you don't run. Running makes it worse. Running means they take somebody extra to cover the interest.*

Hector had laughed. He'd been nineteen and immortal and the fields were green.

In 1987 his son Mateo was nine years old, gap-toothed, left-handed, afraid of thunderstorms and in love with the horses two farms over. In September of that year, Hector had begun to feel the knowing. It came on him slowly, like weather: a pressure behind the breastbone, a sense of being watched by something that lived in his own reflection. He told himself it was the drought stress. He drank more. He prayed differently than he'd been taught, which was to say not at all.

On the night of the harvest moon, Mateo had gone out to the barn to check on a sick calf, because the boy was responsible that way, because Hector had raised him responsible. And Hector had stood in the kitchen doorway and watched his son walk across the yard toward the barn, small against the enormous red moon, and he had felt the knowing settle over him like a hand on the back of his neck, and he had done nothing.

He had done nothing.

That was the part he could never get past, all these years later—that the bargain required nothing of him but stillness. No knife, no rope, no signature in blood. Just a man standing in his own doorway, letting his child walk into the dark, because the alternative was a town that starved, and because some deep animal part of him had already understood that the thing walking toward the barn was not entirely his son anymore, and that the thing waiting in the barn had already been invited. By whom, he'd never learned. That was the cruelty of it—the invitation was always written by someone who loved you, and they never signed where you could see.

Mateo had screamed once. Briefly. The way a calf screams.

What walked back out of the barn wore Mateo's face and Mateo's overalls and smiled at his father with Mateo's gap-toothed grin, and it lived in the house for eleven days, eating breakfast, feeding the horses, saying *goodnight, Papá* in a voice that was almost right, until Hector couldn't bear it and the neighbors came with lanterns, as neighbors do, and stood at the fence line witnessing while the thing walked itself out to this field and lay down in the bare dirt and the ground closed over it like water closing over a stone.

After that, nothing grew here. The land had taken what it wanted and marked the account settled, and settled accounts, in Hector's experience, were barren places.

* * *

"He was nine," Delgado said. He'd been talking for a long time, and the whiskey bottle between them on the seat was a third lighter than when they'd started. Yara had drunk twice. She was holding her glass and not drinking it, the way you hold something when letting go of it feels dangerous. "Nine years old. I want you to understand that before anything else. Before the history and the bargain and all the fine old-family language we dress it up in. They took a nine-year-old boy, and I stood in my kitchen and let him walk."

"You didn't have a choice."

"There's always a choice." He looked at her then, and his eyes were wet and furious and terribly old. "That's the lie they tell you, that there's no choice. There's a choice every generation. Somebody could say no. Somebody could burn the whole town down instead, salt the fields, take the loss. Nobody does. My father didn't. I didn't. Because the land is generous, Miss Sandoval. That's the devil of it. It's *kind*. The rains come. The kids don't get leukemia. The bank forgives. You can live a whole soft, blessed life here, and all it costs is one person, every twenty-five or thirty years, and everybody agrees not to count them."

"Count them," Yara said. Her voice came out strange, flat. "You mean remember them."

"I mean both." He took a long pull of whiskey. Out the windshield, the bare field had gone blue-gray with true dark, and it seemed to Yara that the darkness over it was thicker than the darkness over the road, denser, like a pond at night versus a puddle. "There's a page in the county ledger, back before the state archives took over, where the founding families recorded their harvests. Dates and names. My grandfather showed me once. Eleven names before mine. Then Mateo." His jaw worked. "They keep the ledger in the courthouse basement, under lock, and the lock's been broken since before I was born, and nobody's ever stolen the page. You understand? Everybody knows where it is. Nobody touches it. That's what complicity looks like out here. It looks like a broken lock nobody fixes."

Yara stared at the field. The grief she'd carried for three days—the ordinary, human grief for her brother, the grief that had brought her home—was shifting underneath her, tectonic, becoming something else. Something with edges.

"The casket," she said. "The body in the casket. It wasn't Yago."

"No," Delgado said. Not gently, not cruelly. Just true.

"It had my face."

"Yes."

"And my mother—" She stopped. Her hands had gone cold around the glass. "My mother wouldn't look at me. At the wake. Everyone kept looking at me like—" Like a lamb counts its remaining legs, she thought, and didn't say.

Delgado was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped, gone rough, the voice of a man stepping carefully onto ice he already knows will hold him.

"Your family's turn has been coming a long while," he said. "People talk. Not openly—never openly—but there's talk. Your grandmother gave hers in '61, a cousin on your grandfather's side, and folks figured the Sandoval account was good for another generation at least. But accounts run out. And your mama..." He shook his head slowly. "Your mama's been writing something under that porch of yours for three years now. Chalk. My wife sees Elena at the market, and Elena's hands—"

He stopped. Not mid-sentence this time, but deliberately, the way a man stops when he realizes he's about to say the thing that cannot be unsaid.

Yara set the glass down on the dashboard with a click that sounded very loud.

"Under the porch," she repeated.

"Miss Sandoval." Delgado turned to face her fully, and in the dark cab his face was mostly shadow and the shine of his eyes. "I'm going to tell you the last thing I know, and then I'm going to drive you home, and after tonight I can't help you anymore. Not because I don't want to. Because helping you isn't in me to give. Understand that about this town—we're not evil. We're worse. We're *polite*."

He looked out at the field where his son lay under forty acres of nothing.

"The invitation has to be written by kin and given freely, and it has to name somebody specific, and once it's written, it can't be unwritten—it can only be redirected. That's the old law. Redirected, but only at a host. Only at someone who owes. And the harvest moon comes tomorrow night, a day earlier than the almanac says, which hasn't happened since 1961, and when the moon comes early it means the invitation's been moved up, which means somebody got impatient." His breath shuddered out of him. "Go home, Yara. Go home and dig under your porch, and whatever you find down there in the dark, don't read it out loud. Whatever you do, whatever knocks, whatever calls you by name in your brother's voice—"

The field made a sound.

It was small, as sounds go—a soft exhalation, like wind moving through a crop that wasn't there—and both of them heard it, and both of them turned to the windshield, and the bare gray field lay exactly as bare and exactly as silent as before, except that now, far out in the middle of it, maybe fifty yards, maybe a hundred, there was a shape standing where no shape had been. Small. Boy-sized. Standing very still in the moonless dark, facing the truck.

Delgado's hand found the ignition. "Don't look at it," he whispered. "Don't look at it long."

But Yara looked. She couldn't help it. The shape was too small and too still and it stood the way children stand when they're waiting to be acknowledged, weight on one hip, head slightly tilted, and even at that distance, even in that dark, she felt it looking back at her with an attention that had nothing childish in it at all—an attention like a hook, like a hand reaching up out of cold water.

"Drive," she said.

He drove. In the mirror, the field fell away, and just before the road bent and took it from view, Yara saw the small shape raise one arm—not waving, she told herself, not waving—and point, unhurried, back the way they had come.

Back toward the Sandoval farm. Back toward the porch. Back toward whatever her mother had written in chalk under the floorboards, in the dark, with those stained hands, three years ago.

Delgado pulled up in front of the farmhouse at half past nine. The porch light was on. Behind the lit window, her mother's silhouette moved through the kitchen, steady and domestic, setting out a glass, washing a dish, a woman keeping a house ready for company.

"Tomorrow night," Delgado said quietly, as she opened the door. "Sunrise after. If you're still yourself at sunrise, you're safe till next time." He gripped the wheel with both hands, knuckles pale. "And Yara—if a man named Tam speaks to you, you listen to him. He's the only one of us who ever said no."

She stood in the gravel driveway until the taillights vanished, and then she turned and looked at the porch, at the swing hanging motionless in the windless dark, at the boards beneath it where the shadows pooled thickest.

Then she went to find a shovel.
