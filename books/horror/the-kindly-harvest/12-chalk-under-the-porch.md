## 12. Chalk Under the Porch

The flashlight found the crawlspace door at two in the morning, and Yara stood over it for a long time before she touched it.

It was a hatch of gray boards set into the lattice beneath the porch, low and square, hinged with rust. As a child she had believed it was a grave. She and Yago had dared each other to open it every summer of their childhood, and neither ever had, because Tam had told them—*Tam had told them*—that some doors are only doors from one side.

She crouched now in the wet grass, the cold soaking through her jeans, and thought about how many times in the last three days she had obeyed a rule that child had invented. Don't answer the door. Don't say your full name. Hold out until sunrise. She had stopped asking whether the rules were real. The rules were real the way gravity was real: you didn't have to believe in them to fall.

The chalk marks under the porch had been Elena's first slip. Yara had seen them yesterday, kneeling to retrieve a dropped earring near the porch steps—a smear of white at the edge of the boards, a curve of it, deliberate. Elena's hands had been dusted with the same white when Yara confronted her, and Elena had looked down at her own fingers as though they belonged to someone else, and said nothing.

So now, with the house breathing behind her and the harvest moon still a night away—or so she'd believed until the sky began to lie—Yara hooked her fingers under the hatch and pulled.

The hinge screamed like something waking up.

* * *

The smell came first: earth and lime and something older, sweetish, like flowers left too long in a closed room. Her flashlight beam swept across packed dirt, across the fieldstone piers holding up the porch floor above her head, across—

Circles.

Dozens of them. Chalk circles drawn on the dirt floor of the crawlspace, overlapping, layered, some so old they were only ghosts of white in the soil, others sharp enough to be from this week. Each circle held a name written in its center, and each name held a date.

Yara got down on her knees in the dirt and crawled inside.

The space was shallow—she could sit upright but not stand—and the porch floor pressed close overhead, close enough that when she moved, the boards above whispered against their nails. She swept the light slowly across the ground, and the ground answered her, circle after circle after circle, going back into the dark beneath the oldest part of the house like rings inside a tree.

The nearest ones were her mother's handwriting. She knew it instantly, the way you know your own name spoken by a stranger—the slightly formal capitals, the way Elena crossed her sevens. *MARIANA SANDOVAL VEGA,* one read, dated 1994. Another: *RAÚL DELGADO HERRERA,* 1987. The Delgado name made her stomach drop through the dirt. She thought of the old man's face at the wake, the sentence he couldn't finish, the field where nothing grew.

She crawled deeper.

The older circles were harder to read, worn to smudges, but here and there the chalk had been protected somehow—covered by later drawings, or sunk into soil that had baked hard around it. A name from 1962, half legible: *T—* something. The rest was gone, rubbed away or erased, and the erasure itself felt deliberate, felt like a wound in the record. She stared at it longer than she could explain. Something about the shape of that missing word itched behind her eyes, the way a forgotten dream does.

Deeper still, and the handwriting changed. Rounder. Older ink mixed with the chalk, brown-black, written with a nib pen directly onto the stone of the farthest pier. Yara pressed the light against it and read:

*ISABEL SANDOVAL ORTEGA — HARVEST TAKEN, LAND KEPT.*

And below it, smaller, in the same hand: *We do not choose who is owed. We only choose who pays.*

Her grandmother's handwriting. Yara had seen it on recipe cards, on the flyleaf of a Bible nobody opened. Isabel Sandoval Ortega, dead twenty-three years, buried up the hill behind the church, had knelt in this same dirt—had maybe died in this same dirt—and written that line with a steady hand while the chalk dust settled on her wrists.

*We only choose who pays.*

Yara sat back on her heels. Above her, through the gaps in the porch boards, she could see the kitchen window glowing faintly. Elena was awake. Elena was always awake now, moving through the house at night like a woman pacing a cell, and Yara understood suddenly that she wasn't pacing because of grief. She was pacing because the date was coming. Because she had written it herself.

Yara kept crawling.

* * *

The oldest circles were beneath the hearth end of the porch, where the chimney's foundation sank into the earth, and there the air changed—colder, stiller, as if the ground itself were holding its breath. Her light found a ring of small stones arranged around a circle so old the chalk had become part of the soil, a pale stain shaped like a moon.

But beside it, fresh. Bright. New.

One circle, drawn within days—she could see the drag marks of the chalk's edge, the place where Elena's knee had pressed into the dirt beside it, the fine print of a shoe heel in the soil. And in its center, in her mother's careful, formal capitals:

*YARA LUZ SANDOVAL MÉNDEZ*

Dated tomorrow.

Yara did not scream. That surprised her most, afterward—that what she felt was not terror but a terrible, settling clarity, like water going still. Her full name. Every name she owned, the middle name from her grandmother, the surname from her father who had left when she was nine and never once called on her birthday. All of it, given freely, written in chalk by the hand that had braided her hair before her first communion. Dated tomorrow, as though tomorrow were an appointment. As though she were a delivery.

The invitation hadn't been negotiated. It had been drafted, signed, and filed. She was not being hunted. She was being *collected*, and the paperwork had been in order since before she arrived—maybe since before she left, all those years ago, when she'd promised Yago she'd come back for the harvest festival and then let eleven years go by like water under a bridge she never crossed again.

*Absence made her easier to give up.* The thought arrived whole, uninvited, and she hated it, and she could not make it leave.

Her hands were shaking now. She noticed distantly, the way you notice weather. She pressed her palm flat against the fresh circle—not touching the letters themselves, some instinct keeping her fingers on the empty chalk beyond the final Z—and the dirt beneath was warm.

Warm, in October, at two in the morning, in a crawlspace that smelled of graves.

* * *

She heard the porch boards shift above her before she heard anything else—a slow distribution of weight, someone walking the length of the porch above her head, unhurried, barefoot by the sound of it. Then stillness, directly overhead. Then a voice, soft, filtered down through the gaps between the boards like water through a sieve.

"You found the ledger."

Tam's voice. Unchanged. Patient as furniture.

Yara's throat worked. "Is that what this is? A ledger?"

"Every debt gets written down." A pause. Above her, the weight shifted closer to the hatch, and pale light spilled through the opening—moonlight, though the moon was wrong tonight, swollen and early, and she realized with a lurch that the clouds had broken ahead of schedule, that the safe hours were already bleeding out of the night. "The land keeps count even when the family forgets."

"Whose handwriting is the oldest?" she asked. "Under the stones."

Silence. Long enough that she thought he might not answer. When he spoke again his voice had changed—smaller, she thought, or perhaps just more careful, the way people get careful around their own wounds.

"Mine," Tam said. "I wrote mine."

The words went through her like cold water. She looked back at the half-erased circle from 1962, at the missing name, and the itch behind her eyes became a scream she refused to let out. *Don't say your full name.* He had written his own name in this dirt, sixty years ago, and something had come to collect it, and whatever was left had been standing on thresholds ever since, teaching children rules instead of games.

"Tam—" she started, and stopped, because she almost said the rest of it, almost asked *what was your real name*, and some instinct older than the house clamped her jaw shut. Names were currency here. She would not spend his, and she would not ask him to.

"I know," he said, as if she had asked anyway. Of course he knew. He always knew; that had been the whole of him, when he was imaginary—knowing things no seven-year-old knew, finishing their sentences, warning them away from the creek ice. They had thought it was imagination. It had been apprenticeship.

"Why show me this?" she asked. "Why now?"

"Because you needed to see it written." The voice was very quiet. "People can survive almost anything except proof. Your mother survived it by writing it down. Writing makes it a transaction. Transactions feel clean." A pause, and then, with something that might have been sorrow: "You don't get to feel clean, Yara. Not if you stay. But you get to feel *true*. That's worth more."

Above her, the weight withdrew, footsteps receding along the porch toward the swing, whose chains gave their familiar small complaint—the sound of her entire childhood, greeting her at the worst hour of her life.

Yara stayed in the dirt a moment longer. She took out her phone—no signal, there had been no signal since the county road—and photographed the fresh circle anyway, the flash stark and obscene in the dark, her own full name burning white in the little screen. Evidence. For whom? For what court? She thought of Delgado's face at the fence line of his memory, of the whole town's polite bowed heads, and knew the photograph was evidence for no one but herself. A witness needs a record. Even when the only trial will be held at a threshold, in the dark, with no judge but the thing that comes to collect.

Then she saw it—one last thing, at the very edge of the light, half tucked beneath the foundation stone where the newest circle met the oldest stain.

A second piece of chalk. Lying in the dirt. Fresh, unbroken, white as a tooth.

Waiting.

Not for her mother. Her mother's work was done; the circle was signed, dated, delivered. The chalk was waiting for whoever came next. For whoever knelt here next year, or in ten years, or in thirty, when some other child of this valley came home to bury somebody and found the ledger under the house.

The bargain didn't want her specifically. The bargain wanted *a* Sandoval, *a* founding-family name, any name, forever. Her grandmother had known it. *We do not choose who is owed. We only choose who pays.* And the chalk would keep waiting, patient as Tam, patient as the thing wearing her brother's smile, until someone paid or until someone ended it.

Yara picked up the chalk.

It was cold, colder than the dirt, cold in a way that felt less like temperature than like attention—as if the thing it was meant to call had turned, somewhere far off in the fields, and begun to walk. She closed her fist around it and climbed out of the crawlspace into the red-ringed light of the early moon, and stood in the yard with the hatch gaping behind her like a mouth.

Across the yard, at the edge of the field where nothing grew, something was standing very still among the corn stubble. It wore her brother's shape. It did not approach. It simply watched her hold the chalk, and tilted Yago's head the way Yago used to tilt his head when he was deciding whether to tell her a secret.

Behind her, inside the house, her mother's footsteps stopped.

Yara looked down at the chalk in her fist, and understood, with the calm of someone stepping off a ledge, exactly what it was for—and exactly who else in this house knew how to use it.
