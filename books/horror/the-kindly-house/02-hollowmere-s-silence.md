## 2. Hollowmere's Silence

Lior woke on his second morning in Hollowmere to a silence so complete it had texture, like felt pressed against the ears. No traffic — there was no traffic. No gulls, though the moors were only a day's walk west and he'd have sworn he could smell salt on the air. Just the creak of the inn's old beams and, far off, a rooster that seemed to be crowing out of politeness rather than conviction.

He lay in bed with his notebook on his chest and reviewed yesterday's recording for the fourth time.

The old woman — Mrs. Tansy Hale, eighty-one, born in the house she still lived in — had sung him a harvest lullaby called *The Reaping Kind*. Her voice had been extraordinary: thin as paper but true, each note placed with the precision of someone setting stones in a wall. He'd recorded forty seconds of it before it stopped. Not faded. Stopped. Mid-word, almost — her mouth open on the *and* of a line that never arrived, her eyes going somewhere else entirely, and then the small apologetic cough and the offer of more tea.

He played it again. Listened to the space where the verse should have continued. On good headphones, in the quiet of his room, that space had a quality he couldn't name. Not empty. *Held.* Like a breath drawn in and kept.

He got up, made tea he didn't drink, and wrote at the top of a fresh page: **Day 2. The silence is not an absence. It is a practice.**

---

The village had perhaps sixty souls, and by noon Lior had spoken with nine of them, which in Hollowmere constituted a social event of some magnitude. Word had gone round about the folklorist with the strange black machine; they came to him willingly enough, even eagerly, drawn up chairs in kitchens and parlours and the back room of the inn, and when he asked them to sing, they sang.

That was the thing he hadn't expected. They sang *beautifully*.

Edric Moss, the wheelwright's grandfather, gave him two ploughing songs in a baritone like a cello being played by a patient man. The Cooper sisters, unmarried twins in their sixties who finished each other's sentences but never their songs, harmonised a May carol so lovely that Lior forgot, for nearly a minute, to watch the levels on his recorder. A boy of maybe ten, sent in shyly by his mother, sang a skipping rhyme with the ruthless accuracy children reserve for things learned by heart rather than by page.

And every single one of them stopped at the same place.

It wasn't the same song. That was what unsettled him most, once he'd laid the recordings side by side that evening. The ploughing songs were different from the carol, different from the rhyme, different from Mrs. Hale's lullaby. Different melodies, different centuries by the feel of them, different words entirely. But somewhere in each one — not always the third verse, though often; sometimes the second, once, eerily, the fifth — there came a line that ended, and the voice ended with it.

Not trailed off. Not forgotten. Edric Moss didn't fumble for lyrics; he simply closed his mouth on the last syllable like a man shutting a gate against weather, and sat looking at his hands. The Cooper sisters stopped in unison, which should have been impossible unless they'd rehearsed the stopping, and perhaps they had, and perhaps everyone had, and perhaps that was the whole answer and he could go home. But their faces — their faces were not the faces of people performing a rule. They were the faces of people standing very still near an edge.

The boy was worst. When the rhyme broke off, he looked up at his mother with something close to panic, as if he'd wet himself, and she smoothed his hair and said, "There's a good lad," and gave Lior a smile that asked him, without words, to please not make it happen twice.

"Is it the same verse?" Lior asked her gently. "Across the songs? Do they share a line, or—"

"They're just songs," she said. "You know how songs are. They end."

They don't, he wanted to say. Songs end where singers decide to end them, or they trail into laughter, or they're interrupted, or they dissolve — but they do not stop mid-phrase like clocks running down, all across a village, all in the same breathless way, for—

For how long?

He began asking that question in the afternoon, obliquely at first, then less so. How long had they sung them this way? Had their parents sung them whole? Old Edric considered the question with genuine scholarly interest, which Lior appreciated, and answered: "My father sang them short. His mother, I think, sang them long. There was a difference in the villages then." He would not say what difference. When Lior pressed — kindly, he thought, professionally — the old man's face did something complicated, a folding-in, and he said only: "You'll want the vicar for questions like that. Reverend Fenn knows the shape of things."

*The shape of things.* Lior wrote it down because it was a good phrase, and because he was beginning to collect these phrases the way other collectors collected songs: *finished elsewhere*, said by Mrs. Hale's neighbour when he'd mentioned the lullaby; *it isn't ours to carry further*, said by the innkeeper; and now this. The villagers spoke about their silences the way sailors spoke about shoals. Not superstition, exactly. Navigation.

---

Reverend Fenn received him in the vicarage study at four o'clock, among books that smelled of dust and candle smoke, though Lior had seen no candles. The vicar was perhaps fifty-five, grey-bearded, with the particular gentleness of men who have spent decades delivering difficult news. He poured tea without asking and gestured Lior into the chair opposite, the good chair, which struck Lior as either courtesy or strategy.

"Mrs. Aldous says you've been collecting," Fenn said. "Songs."

"A dozen or so already. It's remarkable material. The melodies are — I don't want to overstate it, but some of them look very old indeed. Pre-Norman, possibly, if the modal structures are anything to go by. And the whole village sings. That's rare. Mostly you get one or two bearers of a tradition and the rest have let it go. Here it's—" He caught himself gushing and smiled apologetically. "I'm sorry. I do go on. It's just that I've been to forty villages in six years and none of them sing like this."

"No," Fenn agreed. "I don't expect they do." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer, a full rotation, watching it as though it might say something. "And you'll have noticed they don't finish."

"I noticed."

"May I ask what you concluded?"

"That it's a convention. A local performance practice. Perhaps a superstition attached to certain verses — there are precedents, songs considered unlucky, harvest songs especially, where a line is spoken rather than sung, or a word substituted. In some Cornish traditions the final verse of a wassail is hummed precisely so the words aren't 'spent.' I assumed something of that order."

"And does that satisfy you?"

"It's a working hypothesis," Lior said, "which is a scholar's way of saying it satisfies me until something better comes along."

Fenn smiled. It reached his eyes, but tiredly, the way light reaches the bottom of a well. "You seem a careful man, Mr. Winterbourne. So I'll be careful with you. I won't insult you with nonsense, and I won't tell you things I'm not free to tell. What I will say is this." He set the cup down. "Some songs here are finished elsewhere. That is not a figure of speech, though I understand it must sound like one. The verses we do not sing are not lost. They are not forgotten. They are *elsewhere*, and they are finished there, and it is better for everyone in this parish — yourself included, and I mean that with real warmth — that the elsewhere and the here remain separate."

Lior laughed before he could stop himself. It was a kind laugh, or meant to be. "Reverend, with respect — that's a lovely formulation. Honestly, it's better than most of the folklore I collect. 'Finished elsewhere.' Is it yours, or is it traditional?"

"It's traditional."

"Then somewhere in the tradition there's an elsewhere. A named place, presumably. A hill, a barrow, a churchyard—" He watched Fenn's face as he said *churchyard* and saw nothing, absolutely nothing, which was itself informative; men who had nothing to hide about their churchyards blinked. "Somewhere the unfinished verses went. If I could find the elsewhere, I could find the completed songs, and that would be — forgive me, but it would be the discovery of my career. A living tradition preserved in negative. The silence as archive."

Fenn looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the light was going amber and long, and somewhere a dog began to bark, three notes, then stopped as if corrected.

"You record everything?" the vicar asked.

"Everything worth keeping."

"Then keep this." Fenn leaned forward, and his gentleness took on an edge, fine as a scalpel. "In six years and forty villages, you have learned that folklore is a living thing, and living things defend themselves. You have written that down, I imagine, in some form. Apply it here. Hollowmere has been generous with you. It has given you its beautiful half-songs and its hospitality and its patience with your machine. Take those, and take my warning with them, and consider whether the kindest thing you can do — for the village, and truly, Mr. Winterbourne, for yourself — is to let the missing halves stay missing."

"The silence as mercy," Lior said. "That's a new one. Usually it's the song that's sacred."

"Yes," Fenn said quietly. "Usually it is."

---

He walked back through the village as the light failed, recorder bag heavy on his shoulder, mind churning. The lane passed along the edge of the churchyard, and he slowed without quite deciding to — habit, professional habit, churchyards were where the oldest inscriptions lived — and found himself reading headstones in the last grey light. Weathered names. Dates reaching back in orderly succession. And then a stretch of ground, maybe a quarter of the yard, where the grass grew longer and the stones stood closer together than seemed decent, crowded like people huddling, and the dates on those stones stopped.

Not eroded. Stopped. The latest legible year was 1743.

Beyond them the yard continued — newer stones, fresh flowers, a child's grave with a windmill that turned in no wind he could feel — but that quarter lay fallow, untended, its grass knee-high and silvered with seed. Nobody mowed it. Nobody, apparently, had mowed it for a very long time.

He raised his camera, purely reflexively, and through the viewfinder noticed what he hadn't seen with the naked eye: the long grass in the old quarter bent. All of it, uniformly, away from the church, toward the far wall — combed flat in a wide slow spiral, as if the ground beneath had been breathing, or turning over in its sleep.

A gust, he told himself, lowering the camera. Slope drainage. Some perfectly reasonable hydrology of a churchyard built on whatever Hollowmere was built on.

Behind him, footsteps on the path. Quick ones.

"You shouldn't photograph there."

She was young — early twenties, dark-haired, wearing a sexton's canvas apron with earth on the knees and a spade over her shoulder like a soldier's rifle. Her eyes were the colour of rain over slate and they were fixed on his camera with an expression that was equal parts hostility and something else, something almost like pity.

"I'm sorry," Lior said. "I didn't realise it was restricted."

"It isn't restricted." She shifted the spade. "It's just not photographed. There's a difference." She looked past him at the leaning grass, and her jaw tightened. "Come away from the wall, please. It's getting dark."

"It's a churchyard," he said, mildly amused despite himself. "Surely it's at its most atmospheric after dark. That's rather the point of them."

"Most people here think so too," she said, and there was no humour in it at all. "That's part of the problem. Come away from the wall."

Something in her voice — not fear exactly, but the tone of someone talking a jumper back inside a railing — made him step back without argument. She fell into step beside him as he walked toward the lychgate, close enough that he could smell woodsmoke and turned earth, and at the gate she stopped.

"You're the one collecting songs," she said.

"Lior Winterbourne."

"Mara." She didn't offer a surname, though he'd already heard it — the sexton's daughter, the innkeeper had said, good girl, keeps the yard herself since her mother passed. "How many have you got?"

"A dozen. Why?"

"Have any of them ended?"

He hesitated. "They've all ended where they ended."

Her eyes searched his face, and whatever she found there made her look away, out at the darkening churchyard, at the combed-flat grass. "Don't finish them," she said. "Whatever you're planning — and I can see you're planning something, you've got the face for it — don't finish any of the songs. Not the ones they gave you, and not the ones you think you've worked out. Promise me that much."

"I don't make promises I don't understand."

"No," she said softly. "I suppose clean men don't." And she lifted the spade, turned, and walked back into the churchyard — not toward the gate of the vicarage, not toward any house, but down the path between the stones, into the deepening dark, toward the old quarter where the grass lay flat and breathing, and Lior stood at the lychgate watching until the dark took her, telling himself he was only waiting to be sure she got home safely, and knowing, with the first cold thread of real dread unwinding in his chest, that home was not the direction she was walking.
