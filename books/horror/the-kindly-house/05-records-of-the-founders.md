## 5. Records of the Founders

The parish archive sat behind the vestry in a room that smelled of dust, candle wax, and the particular sweetness of old paper slowly giving itself back to the air. Lior had asked the vicar for access that morning, expecting the usual dance — the polite deferral, the promise to "have a look and see what might be suitable." Instead the Reverend Aldous Crane had simply studied him for a long moment, his pale eyes narrowing as though Lior were a puzzle whose edges didn't quite fit the frame, and then handed him a key on a loop of greying twine.

"Mind the damp," was all he said. "And mind what you carry out. Some things are heavier than they look."

Lior had laughed — a scholar's laugh, easy and dismissive — and the vicar had not smiled back. That was the thing about Hollowmere that was beginning to wear on him: nobody smiled back. He had spent three days in the village now, three days of beautiful voices cutting off like severed wires, of conversations that bent away from the churchyard the way water bends around a stone, and his rationalist's patience was fraying into something that felt uncomfortably like obsession.

He let himself into the archive at nine in the morning, set up his lamp on the scarred oak table, and began.

The room was small and low-ceilinged, one narrow window set so high in the wall that the light fell at an angle useless for reading. Shelves of parish registers lined the walls — baptisms, marriages, burials, the whole bureaucratic skeleton of a community laid out in fading iron-gall ink. Lior worked through them methodically, as he always did, letting his fingers learn the texture of each volume. The registers were ordinary enough. Births and deaths in the usual proportions, the usual gaps during the wars, the usual cluster of infant mortality in the hard years. He noted dates, copied names, filled his notebook with the patient scaffolding of scholarship.

It was when he reached the bottom shelf — the one set slightly apart, as if the books there had been put away rather than stored — that he found the ledger.

It was bound in vellum gone the color of weak tea, its corners worn round as river stones. There was no title on the spine, no date, nothing but a small stamped device on the front cover that Lior had to hold to the light to make out: seven small houses arranged in a ring, their doors facing inward, toward a common center.

His pulse picked up. He told himself it was the scholar's reflex, the cheap thrill of the archive, the same flutter he'd felt over a thousand modest discoveries. He carried the ledger to the table, set it down with the care such things deserved, and opened it.

The first page was a list of names. Seven of them, written in a hand so fine and controlled it looked almost printed:

*Edric Mowbray. Sibbe Halloway. Tomas Ferren. Agnes Low. Roderick Vale. Maud Crane. Josiah Wren.*

Lior sat back. Crane. The vicar's surname — though that proved nothing; Cranes would have been in Hollowmere for centuries, as the Mowbrays and the Vales clearly had been. He recognized the other names too, with a small cold jolt: they were on the graves outside. The oldest stones, the ones nearest the churchyard wall, the ones Mara had steered him away from with that half-smile of hers. *Nobody visits those,* she'd said. *We keep them the way you keep a wound — covered.*

He turned the page.

The next leaf was written in verse.

Not a poem, exactly — or not a poem in the way Lior understood poems. It was a covenant, laid out in seven stanzas of seven lines each, the ink varying in shade as though different hands had taken up the pen stanza by stanza. The language was archaic, formal, with the deliberate gravity of a legal document that had chosen to dress itself as a hymn. Lior read it once quickly, then again slowly, and by the third reading his mouth had gone dry.

*We who break ground do break it twice,*
*Once for the house and once for the door,*
*And what we seal we seal at price,*
*And what we feed we feed evermore.*
*Seven names to hold the ring,*
*Seven voices taught to sing,*
*And the last verse given to the thing beneath the floor.*

He read on. The second stanza established the tithe — though it never used the word, preferring the gentler phrase *the portion owed*. The third spoke of songs, of verses *held back from the air* so that *the listener below should not wake hungry before its season*. The fourth and fifth stanzas grew darker, more specific: harvest moons, a door kept shut, hands that would come *when the singing stopped*. The sixth stanza named the office of *keeper*, though its duties were described only in a phrase that made Lior's skin prickle: *to stay, and staying, to be stayed*.

The seventh stanza he read four times.

*And when the moon is full and low,*
*And the portion owed comes due,*
*One voice alone must go below*
*To sing the verses through —*
*Not taken, not compelled, not driven,*
*But given, given, freely given,*
*For the house wants nothing it does not ask for, and it asks for you.*

Lior pushed back from the table. The lamp flame steadied; his hands did not. He was aware of his own heartbeat, absurdly loud in the little room, and of the silence outside the high window — that particular Hollowmere silence, which he was beginning to understand was not an absence of sound but a discipline of it.

*Superstition,* he told himself. *Folk religion. A covenant of the usual kind — fertility rite, foundation sacrifice, the whole comparative toolkit. Villages all over Europe sealed things into foundations: coins, shoes, cats, corpses. This is just a more literate version.*

The explanation was sound. It was well-supported. It was also, he noticed, doing nothing at all to slow his pulse.

He turned the pages on. After the covenant came what appeared to be a record of observances — brief entries, one per year, in varying hands. *Portion rendered. All quiet. The door holds.* Year after year, decade after decade, the same formulae in the same dutiful rotation, the seven family names appearing and reappearing like figures in a round dance. Lior copied out a dozen of them, his scholar's discipline reasserting itself, finding comfort in the transcription, in the act of turning mystery into data.

Then he reached 1743.

The entry for 1743 was half-written. The ink stopped mid-word — *the portion shall be rend* — and the rest of the page was blank. The next leaf had been torn out. So had the one after that. In fact, from 1743 onward, the ledger became a lacework of absence: pages torn out in blocks, sometimes whole gatherings of leaves, the stubs left behind like the stumps of pulled teeth. Whatever record had been kept after 1743, someone had removed it with care — the tears were clean, deliberate, done with a blade and a steady hand.

Lior sat very still.

He was not a man who frightened easily, and he was not frightened now, he told himself. He was *intrigued*. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole of his profession. A torn archive was a story in itself: censorship, scandal, some eighteenth-century vicar ashamed of what his ancestors had written. He began turning the stubbed pages faster, hunting for anything that survived, and near the back of the ledger he found it — a single leaf missed by the blade, tucked against the binding where the tearer's knife couldn't reach.

It was written in a different hand — smaller, hurried, the letters slanting as though written by someone looking over their own shoulder. And it was verse. Fragments of verse, seven lines, with the first words of each line underlined as if to mark them as anchors:

*We who — ... — the door,*
*... — ... — price,*
*... — ... evermore,*
*... — taught to sing,*
*... — ... the thing beneath the floor.*

Lior's breath left him slowly.

He knew this. Not the words — but the *shape* of it. The meter. The rhyme scheme. The way the lines folded back on themselves. He had heard this song three days ago, sung by an old woman in the common room of the Lamb and Lantern, a harvest lullaby that had cut off mid-line as if the singer's throat had been closed by an unseen hand. He had heard it again and again since, from a dozen mouths, and every voice in Hollowmere stopped at the same place, the same verse, the same syllable — as if the song were a road that ended at a cliff edge and everyone in the village knew, without being told, not to look down.

He pulled his notebook toward him with hands that were no longer entirely steady, and found the transcription he'd made the first night — the lullaby, noted in careful staff notation, the melody trailing off into nothing where the old woman had fallen silent. He set it beside the recovered leaf. Line against line. The rhythm matched. The rhyme-words matched. The underlined anchors — *door, price, evermore, sing, floor* — fell exactly where the villagers' voices broke off, as if each of them had been carrying a fragment of the covenant in their mouths all their lives, a stone in the shoe of every song, worn smooth by generations of careful not-singing.

The missing verses of the song were the missing pages of the ledger.

His folklore was not a memory. It was a manual.

Lior became aware, gradually, of how long he had been sitting there. The angle of light through the high window had shifted; somewhere in the church a clock struck noon, the sound muffled and strange, as if the building itself were keeping the sound the way the village kept its verses. He looked down at the ledger, at the seven houses with their doors facing inward, and felt the first true cold of his stay in Hollowmere move through him — not fear exactly, but the vertigo of a man who has been walking on a floor and suddenly wonders what is beneath it.

Because here was the thing his rationalism could not file away, the thing that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: the covenant was not written in the past tense. He had read it three times. It was written in the present. *What we seal, we seal. What we feed, we feed.* And the entries, the dutiful annual entries — *the door holds* — they did not stop in 1743 with the torn pages. He had assumed they did. But he had been reading a book with its heart cut out, and a book is not the same as a practice. The songs stopped at the same verse in every throat in the village. The graves were never visited. The old woman had gone white as bone when he'd asked her why.

The practice had not ended. It had simply stopped being written down.

He thought of the churchyard at dusk, of the way Mara had stood between him and the far wall with her arms folded and her eyes sharp and amused and — he saw it now, replaying it — *afraid*. Not of him. Of where he was standing. Of what he might have been listening to.

He thought, too, of the phrase from the sixth stanza, and it came back to him now with the clarity of a bell struck in an empty room: *to stay, and staying, to be stayed.* Keeper. The office was still open. Offices like that did not stay open for three hundred years unless someone was filling them.

Lior closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child, and sat with his hands flat on the vellum cover, feeling its faint residual warmth as though the book had been held recently, as though it were not paper and skin at all but something with a circulation of its own.

*You are a folklorist,* he told himself. *You have seen a hundred covenants, a thousand apotropaic rites, ten thousand customs built on fear of the dark. This is a text. It is evidence. It is not a summons.*

But even as he thought it, he understood — with the particular, humiliating clarity that comes to rational men at the worst moments — that he had already begun to obey it. He had come to Hollowmere to collect songs, and he had collected them, and he had noticed where they broke, and he had written the breaks down, and now he sat in a locked room with the only complete version of the song in existence open under his hands, and some part of him — the collector's part, the completist's part, the part that could not bear a fragmentary text — wanted nothing in the world so much as to finish it.

To sing it through. To see what came.

He heard, then, footsteps in the vestry beyond the door — unhurried, deliberate, the tread of someone who had known exactly where to find him. Lior slid the ledger's leaf back into its hiding place against the binding, closed the cover, and did not move from his chair.

The footsteps stopped outside the archive door. And through the wood, in a voice he recognized — low, level, and stripped of all its usual wry warmth — Mara said:

"I know you've found it. And I need you to listen to me very carefully, Lior Winterbourne, because I am only ever going to ask you this once."
