## 7. The First Verse Returns

The rain came in off the moor that evening the way it always did in Hollowmere — sideways, patient, as though it had all the time in the world and intended to use it. Lior watched it silver the window of the inn's common room while he ate a supper of mutton and boiled roots he could not name, and told himself, for perhaps the fortieth time since his arrival, that there was nothing here but habit. Old habits. Deep ones. The kind of habit that could look, from certain angles, exactly like fear.

He had been in Hollowmere nine days. He had filled two notebooks and half of a third. He had recorded eleven singers, and every one of them had stopped at the same place in every song — mid-verse, mid-breath, sometimes mid-word, their mouths closing like doors.

*Finished elsewhere*, the vicar had said, with that gentle, terrible smile of his, as if songs were letters forwarded to another address.

Lior did not believe in elsewhere. He believed in transcription errors, in taboo, in the folk memory of some long-forgotten scandal or plague year that had made a particular verse unlucky. There was always a reason. Reasons were his trade. He collected them the way other men collected coins, and he had never yet met one he couldn't turn over in his fingers until it gave up its face.

The old woman sat by the fire as she did every evening, in the chair closest to the hearth, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate. Her name was Edith Marlow, and she was — by the innkeeper's careful accounting — ninety-one years old, the last of the Marlow family, and the finest singer in the parish, though her voice had gone thin as paper left out in weather. Every night she sang the harvest lullaby. Every night she stopped at the same line.

*"Hush now, hush, the house is warm,"* she would sing, and then her mouth would close, and her hands would fold, and the fire would crackle into the silence like a guest filling an awkward pause.

Tonight, when she began, Lior reached into his bag without thinking and drew out the recorder. It was reflex more than intention; he had learned not to ask permission anymore, because permission was always refused, and refusal taught him nothing. He set the small machine on the table beside his plate, angled its microphone toward the fire, and pressed record. The red light glowed like a drop of blood on snow.

The inn was nearly empty. A farmer dozed over his cider by the door. Two women whispered over cards near the window. The innkeeper himself stood behind the bar polishing a glass that had been clean for ten minutes, his eyes fixed on Edith Marlow with an expression Lior had come to know well — not boredom, not affection, but *attention*. The attention of a man watching a fuse burn down toward something buried.

Edith's voice rose, cracked, steadied. She sang of the reaping and the binding, of the last sheaf carried home on shoulders bent with gratitude, of bread shared at a long table where no seat stood empty. Her hands moved on her lap like birds settling. And Lior, who had heard this song eleven times now, felt the familiar tightening in his chest as she approached the verse — the fourth, the one that ended everything, the one that stopped them all.

*"Hush now, hush, the house is warm,"*

Here it came. Here the silence would fall, gentle as a dropped cloth.

*"the hearth is lit, the door is barred—"*

She kept singing.

Lior sat up so quickly his chair scraped, and the two women at the window looked up, and even the sleeping farmer stirred. Because Edith Marlow was still singing. Her voice had changed — deepened somehow, or rather *widened*, as though a second voice had been laid beneath hers like a second floor beneath a floor, and the words came out of her in a cadence that was older than the room, older than the language around its edges:

*"and none who knock need stand outside,
for what we keep, keeps us in turn;
what we have fed will feed us whole,
and what we love will not be lost."*

The fire seemed to lean toward her. Lior would remember that afterward — the absurd, undeniable impression that the flames had inclined in their grate like listeners turning their heads. The rain against the glass went quiet, all at once, as if the moor itself were holding its breath.

*"Sleep, my dear, and dream no fear,"*

Her eyes were closed. Her face had gone smooth, younger, transfigured — the face of a woman remembering something wonderful.

*"the house below is kind tonight."*

And then she opened her eyes, and looked directly at Lior across the room, and smiled — a smile of such perfect, finished peace that his skin crawled from scalp to heel — and she sang the last line:

*"it only wants to hear you sing."*

The note held. It should have ended; the melody demanded its resolution, the phrase leaned toward its close the way a body leans toward sleep. But the note held, sustained impossibly, swelling slightly, and Lior realized with a lurch that Edith Marlow's mouth had stopped moving. Her lips were parted and still. Her chest did not rise. The note continued anyway, thinning now, rising in pitch, becoming something less like a voice and more like a wire pulled taut between worlds —

— and then it broke, and she was dead.

Not slumped, not fallen. Simply *finished*. She sat in her chair by the fire with her hands folded and her eyes open and her face wearing that same serene smile, and she was as empty as a house whose family has gone away. The innkeeper crossed the room in four strides and put two fingers to her throat, and then he did something Lior had never seen a man do at a death: he bowed his head, and he said, quietly, clearly, as though reporting to someone who needed to hear it:

"She finished it. Sixty years, and she finished it."

The two women by the window had risen. They did not weep. They did not wail or cry out or cross themselves. They stood with their hands clasped before them like mourners at a graveside who have known the date of the funeral for years, and the elder of them said only: "Send for the vicar. And send for the sexton."

The sexton. Mara's father. Lior found he was standing, though he had no memory of rising. His legs felt borrowed.

No one looked at him. That was the thing he noticed most, in the first strange minutes — the deliberate, practiced way the villagers' eyes slid past him, past the table, past the small red light still burning on his recorder. As though he were not there. As though he were the ghost, and the dead woman the living one.

*It only wants to hear you sing.*

He snatched up the machine and killed the recording with a thumb that shook more than he liked.

* * *

They laid her out in the back parlor, because Hollowmere had no mortuary and apparently no need of one. Lior hovered at the edge of things, offering help that was refused with unfailing courtesy, until the innkeeper — a broad, sad-eyed man named Tam Rooker — took him gently by the elbow and steered him toward the door.

"You'll want your rest, sir."

"I want to know what happened."

Rooker's hands paused on the doorframe. For a moment something moved behind his face — pity, maybe, or its exhausted cousin — and then it was shuttered again. "An old woman died in her chair by the fire," he said. "That happens everywhere, sir. Even in Hollowmere."

"Not like this."

"No," Rooker agreed softly. "Not like this." And he closed the door with the care of a man shutting a book he does not intend to reread.

Outside, the rain had stopped. The village lay under a sky scoured clean and black, stars scattered across it like salt, and the air smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke and — faintly, unmistakably, though Lior told himself he imagined it — of earth turned fresh for a grave. Lights were coming on in windows down the lane. Doors opened and shut. Without any bell being rung, without any word being sent that he could detect, the village was gathering.

He followed the little streams of lantern-light toward the church, and stood in the shadow of the lychgate watching them arrive: the seven families, he supposed, though he could not have said how he knew which were which. They came in their good coats and their Sunday faces, old men walking arm in arm with grandsons, mothers carrying children too young to be awake, and they filed through the churchyard gate in silence and gathered in the nave, and above them the bell hung motionless in its tower, unrung, unneeded.

Through the open door Lior could see the vicar standing beside the bier where Edith Marlow lay in state among candles. The vicar's face was gray. Not grieving — *gray*, the color of a man reading a bill that has come due. His lips moved constantly, and Lior caught fragments on the wind: not prayer, or not only prayer. Counting. He appeared to be counting days.

Lior counted backward himself, automatically, the scholar's reflex firing even now. Nine days since his arrival. The harvest moon — he'd noted it in his journal the first night, the way he noted everything, moonrise tables being useful for scheduling field recordings — the harvest moon was nine days away.

The coincidence sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

"Mr. Winterbourne."

Mara's voice, low, at his shoulder. He turned and found her standing just outside the lychgate, lantern in hand, dressed in black, her face pale and set. She had been crying — recently, briefly, efficiently — and had decided, apparently, to be done with it.

"I'm sorry about your—"

"Don't." The word came out flat and hard. Then she breathed, and softened by a degree. "Don't offer condolences you don't mean, and don't ask questions I can't answer. Not tonight."

"What happened to her?"

"You saw what happened."

"She finished the song. After sixty years of stopping, she finished it, and then she died with the note still— " He stopped, because saying it aloud made it worse, made it real in a way his notebooks refused to make anything real. "Songs don't kill people, Mara."

"No," she said. "They don't." She looked past him at the church, at the candlelight moving on the walls inside like something swimming. "But finishing them can. Some verses are debts, Mr. Winterbourne. You don't pay a debt early unless you're ready to go home."

"That's not an explanation. That's a proverb."

"It's all you get tonight." She turned to go, then stopped, and he saw her wrestle with something — he watched it happen, watched duty and something else pull her spine straight and bend it soft by turns. When she spoke again her voice had dropped almost to a whisper. "Your recorder. The one you had running tonight. Did it catch her?"

"Some of it. Why?"

"Listen to it," she said. "Alone. With the door shut. And when you've listened — don't play it for anyone else. Don't play it twice. And Mr. Winterbourne—" she hesitated on his name, and something in her jaw tightened, "—don't stop the tape next time. Whatever you hear. Stopping it doesn't stop it. It only means it has to start over."

She walked away across the churchyard, her lantern swinging, and Lior stood in the dark with the cold coming up out of the ground through his boot-soles, and understood that he had just been given instructions. Instructions implied a manual. Manuals implied authors.

He walked back to the inn through streets that hummed with held breath.

* * *

His room was small and low-ceilinged, under the eaves, with a bed built for a smaller century and a window that looked out over the inn yard toward the distant black shoulder of the church tower. He lit the lamp, sat on the bed with the recorder in his lap, and for a long moment simply looked at it.

Play it once, she'd said. Alone. Door shut.

He pressed the button.

First came the ordinary sounds of the common room: cutlery, the fire, the creak of a chair. Then Edith Marlow's voice, thin and papery, beginning the lullaby. He knew every creak of it by heart now. He let it wash over him, waiting for the fourth verse, telling himself that the tape would show him what his ears had refused to believe — that the woman had simply sung on, that grief and suggestion had dressed an ordinary death in extraordinary clothes, that the note breaking at the end was a mechanical artifact, a stretched tape, a failing battery, *anything*.

The fourth verse arrived. She sang through it. The second voice beneath hers was there on the recording, unmistakable, a low harmonic that no ninety-one-year-old throat could produce, and it was not coming from her direction alone — it seemed to come from everywhere the microphone could hear, from the fire and the walls and the space between the tables, as though the room itself had joined in.

Then the final line. *It only wants to hear you sing.* The note held. On the tape it held longer than it had seemed to hold in the room — thirty seconds, forty — thinning, rising, and underneath it, growing slowly audible as the voice faded, was another sound.

Lior's thumb hovered over the stop button. *Stopping it doesn't stop it.*

He forced his hand flat against his knee.

Underneath the fading note, the recorder had captured a rhythm. Soft, regular, unhurried. At first he took it for the fire, or a clock, or the pulse of blood in his own ears transmitted through his gripping hand. But it was none of those. It was slower than a heart. Deeper than breathing. It rose and fell with the patience of tide, and it filled the whole track, floor to ceiling of sound, and as the note died away entirely the rhythm remained, steady, content, *attentive*, and Lior sat in his narrow room under the eaves and understood with absolute, sickening clarity what he was listening to.

Breathing. Something, somewhere, breathing. The microphone had not been able to tell him where, because the microphone had been in the common room, and the breathing had not been in the common room. It had been in the machine. In the signal itself. As though the sound had traveled not through air but through the act of recording, through the little red eye of the thing, drawn to it the way a moth is drawn to a lit window — and had settled there, comfortable, and stayed.

The tape ran on. Ninety seconds of breathing. Two minutes. The counter ticked. Lior did not move. The lamp flame stood perfectly still, and the room was very warm, warmer than it should have been, warm the way a hand is warm, and he became aware that his own breath had unconsciously matched the rhythm on the tape — in, out, slow, deep — and that it felt, God help him, *pleasant*, like sinking into a bath drawn by someone who loved him.

He tore the headphones off. He fumbled the machine, caught it, stared at it lying in his palms like a small black heart.

There was no tape after this one. He checked. The spool had run out minutes ago, in the common room, before the death. Everything he was hearing — the second voice, the held note, the breathing — had been recorded onto a length of tape that did not exist, onto a side of the cassette already blank, onto nothing at all.

The lamp flickered. Outside, far off, a dog began to howl, and then another, and then all of them, the whole scattered chorus of Hollowmere's dogs, rising together in the dark.

Lior set the recorder on the table, faced it toward the wall, and covered it with his shirt. Then he sat on the edge of the bed with his head in his hands and did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do since the fire leaned toward a dying woman's face.

Nine days. Seven families. One song, finally finished, by the oldest singer in the parish, on the night the excavation crew struck stone.

Unbelief, he discovered, did not shatter. It cracked — along fault lines he had never mapped, hairline fractures running through everything he was — and through the cracks, cold and patient and horribly gentle, something breathed.

A knock at his door. Soft. Twice.

He knew, before he opened it, who it would be. And he knew, in the same breathless instant, why she had come — because everyone downstairs had seen the red light on his table, and everyone in Hollowmere understood something he was only beginning to learn:

The house below had heard him recording.

And it had liked the sound.
