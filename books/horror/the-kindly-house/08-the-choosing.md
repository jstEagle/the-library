## 8. The Choosing

Mara knew the meeting was happening before anyone told her. She knew it the way she knew rain was coming — not by seeing it but by the pressure behind her ears, by the way the dogs went quiet and the jackdaws left the tower all at once, as if the churchyard itself had exhaled.

Her father dressed for it in his good coat, the black one with the worn cuffs he kept for funerals and vestry business, and he would not meet her eyes while he buttoned it. That was how she knew what kind of meeting it was.

"Where's it to be?" she asked, though she already knew.

"The vestry." He tugged the coat straight over his belly, a man trying to make himself presentable for something neither of them wanted named. "You're not wanted there."

"I'm never wanted anywhere it matters."

"Mara." He said her name the way he said *Amen* — flatly, finally, a word that closed things. He took his hat from the peg. At the door he stopped, half-turned, and for one moment she thought he might say something else, something that would undo whatever was about to be done in that low stone room. Instead he said: "Supper'll keep."

Then he was gone into the blue evening, and Mara stood alone in the sexton's cottage with the fire ticking and her mother's empty chair looking at her the way it always did, patient and accusing at once.

She gave him ten minutes' head start. Then she put on her boots and followed.

* * *

The vestry sat against the north flank of St. Aldhelm's like a growth on an old tree, its windows fogged with leaded glass gone milky, its door thick enough to swallow sound. But sound never stayed swallowed in Hollowmere; the village had been built around listening, and the vestry's one window — high, narrow, its glass long since replaced with a board — opened onto the strip of grass between the churchyard wall and the yew, where Mara had hidden as a girl whenever she wanted to hear what the grown-ups decided about her life without her.

She crouched now in the shadow of the yew, knees in wet grass, and listened.

Seven families. Seven chairs brought up from the hall and arranged in a crescent before the vicar's little desk. She could name every seat and who filled it without looking: the Hollises, oldest of the old, whose farm had been a farm when Hollowmere was only a clearing; the Tregennas; the Prides; old Mrs. Voss, ninety-one and sharp as a scythe, who had buried two husbands and one daughter and remembered everything; the Cranes; the Fenns; and the Winters — no relation to Lior Winterbourne, though God alone knew what the house below made of the coincidence — whose family had kept the ledger until the pages ran out.

And her father, not one of the seven but always summoned, because the sexton dug the graves and the graves were part of the bargain. He stood by the door inside, hat in his hands, a servant at the feast rather than a guest.

The vicar's voice came through the board-window thin and papery. "We are agreed, then, on what was broken."

Not a question. A sentence being signed.

"It weren't us broke it," said Tom Pride, and his voice had the whine of a man building a defense before any accusation came. "It were that crew with their diggers and their maps. We told them to leave the ground be. We told them."

"You told them it was bad drainage," Mrs. Voss said dryly, and someone huffed a laugh that died quickly.

"And they'd have laughed us out of the county if we'd said otherwise," the vicar replied. "What's done is done. The seal is cracked. I felt it the night the dogs cried — felt it in my teeth, like a tooth breaking. What matters now is not who broke it but whether we pay before the moon fills or after."

Silence then, the particular silence of people waiting for someone braver to speak first. Mara pressed her cheek against the cold bark of the yew and watched moths bump the board over the window, their shadows flickering across the candlelight inside like small frantic souls.

"The harvest moon," said Edith Crane, and her voice was very steady, which meant she had rehearsed this. "Nineteen days. The tithe has been paid on the harvest moon since the founders set it so. If we pay early—"

"If we pay early, we insult it," Mrs. Voss finished. "If we pay late—" She didn't finish. Nobody finishes that verse. Mara's hands curled in the wet grass.

"We have paid late once," said the vicar quietly. "1743. You've all heard what 1743 cost. Half the village gone by spring, and no sickness the doctor could name. The house does not punish. It only takes what it was promised, plus interest. It is very fair. That is the horror of it — it is scrupulously fair."

Mara's breath fogged in the cooling air. *The house.* Her mother had used that word too, in the last year, in the low voice women in her family used for the thing under the churchyard — not *it*, never *it*, because *it* was rude and rudeness invited attention, but *the house*, as if speaking of a neighbor, a landlord, something owed courtesy.

"So we pay," said Hollis, the eldest, and his voice was like a door closing. "On the moon. As always. The question is the offering."

Here it was. Mara had known it was coming since the dogs howled, since the foreman's joke fell dead in the inn, since the stranger with the recording machine started asking his bright, ignorant questions. She had known it the way you know a storm is coming — and still, hearing it spoken aloud in the vestry where her mother's name had been spoken aloud twenty years ago, her body reacted as if struck. Her stomach dropped. Her mouth went dry.

"The usual won't serve," the vicar said. "The seal is broken. A broken seal wants more than a lamb and a song. It wants mending. And mending wants—"

"An outsider," Mrs. Voss said.

The word hung in the vestry like smoke.

"Not just any outsider," she went on, and Mara could picture her exactly: upright in her chair, hands folded on her stick, eyes bright and terrible. "I've been thinking on it since the dogs cried. Why outsiders? Why has it always wanted the ones from away? And I think it's this: we're all of us marked. Baptized in fear before ever we were baptized in water. Our mothers sang us the songs with the holes in them before we could walk. By the time we can talk we're already half-claimed — the house knows our taste, our shape, our names. There's nothing clean in us to give. But a stranger—" the stick tapped the floor, once, like a gavel, "—a stranger comes unmarked. Unbaptized by our fear. Clean of belief. He doesn't know the rules, so he hasn't broken any. Giving such a one is giving the house something new. Something it can taste properly."

"He's a folklorist," said young Will Fenn, and there was something awful in the way he said it — not horror, but interest, the interest of a man discussing livestock. "Been here three weeks. Been asking after the songs. Been down to the churchyard twice that I know of, and once to the excavation, near enough."

"He's been nothing of the sort," Mara's father said suddenly, and Mara's heart lurched. "He's a scholar. He'll write his book and go."

"Will he?" Mrs. Voss asked mildly. "Has the bus come lately, sexton? Has his car started?"

Mara closed her eyes. They knew. Of course they knew. The roads looped; the buses passed Hollowmere by as if the village had slipped between the pages of the map; and every soul in that room understood why, and none of them had told the man walking in circles at the crossroads.

"The choosing isn't ours alone," the vicar said. "It has never been ours alone. We propose. The house confirms. Seven families speak the name, and if the name is right, the house will let us know it's right."

"How?" Will Fenn asked. "How does it let us know?"

"You'll see," said Mrs. Voss. "Or hear. It was bells, the year they chose my sister. Rang at midnight with no hand on the rope. The year they chose Alice Fenwick it was the bees — swarmed on the churchyard wall in November and hummed the third verse, all of it, the verse nobody sings. The house is not subtle when it's pleased."

Seven families. Mara counted the seats through the fogged glass, seven shapes in candlelight, and understood what came next, and understood that she was going to stand in the wet grass under the yew and listen to them say his name, and say nothing.

Say nothing.

She had known that too, hadn't she? Some part of her, honest beneath everything, had known since the night she'd caught him among the unvisited graves with his recorder held out like a lantern — caught him and walked him out and said almost nothing, warned him in the smallest coin a warning comes in, *some stones you don't read twice* — and had watched him smile his rationalist's smile and thought: *he doesn't believe, and that will save him, and that will kill him.* Because she had known even then that his unbelief wasn't armor. It was the qualification.

"Hollowmere has kept its bargain two hundred years," the vicar said, "and the keeping has been hard, and the price has been paid in tears as often as in blood, and I will not pretend otherwise. But the alternative—" He stopped. Started again. "The alternative is 1743. The alternative is the door coming off its hinges and everything under the churchyard coming up into the light, and I have seen the seam of that door, friends, I have put my hand on it, and it is warm, and it is patient, and it is *kind*, and I tell you the kindness is the worst thing about it."

"Aye," said Hollis, simply, the way men say *aye* at gravesides.

"Then let us choose," said the vicar. "Speak the name, if your family stands for it. Hollis?"

And old Hollis, whose voice sounded like earth falling on a coffin lid, said: "Winterbourne. Lior Winterbourne."

"Tregenna?"

"Winterbourne."

"Pride?"

A pause. Tom Pride, who had whined about the diggers, who had wanted it to be anyone's fault but his own — Mara heard him swallow. "Winterbourne."

"Voss?"

"Winterbourne." No pause at all. Mrs. Voss had probably chosen him the night the dogs howled.

"Crane?"

"Winterbourne."

"Fenn?"

"Winterbourne," said Will Fenn, quick and eager, and Mara hated him for the eagerness.

"Winters?"

And then her father's voice, from beside the door, cracked open like ice on a pond: "He's a good man. He's done nothing. He came here with respect — more respect than any of us have shown that ground in a hundred years. He listens. He *listens*, and you want to feed him to the thing he's been listening to—"

"Six families have spoken, sexton," the vicar said gently. "The seventh is yours by custom, for the digging falls to you."

A silence so long that Mara heard her own heart, heard the moths against the board, heard somewhere far off the first owl of the evening calling across the fields.

Then her father said, in a voice scraped hollow: "Winterbourne."

And out of the dark under the world — out of the ground beneath the vestry floor, beneath the crypt, beneath the sealed door with its bolts thrown from the inside — came an answer.

It was not loud. It did not need to be. It rose through the stone and the soil and the bones of the churchyard the way warmth rises through a floor, and it was singing. One line only. The line that ends the second verse, the line that had not been sung whole in Hollowmere since before Mara's grandmother was born — sung now in a voice like many voices folded together, like sediment, like strata, sweet and deep and unmistakably *pleased*.

The candles in the vestry guttered. Someone gasped. Mrs. Voss began, softly, to weep — not with grief, Mara thought. With relief. With gratitude. That was the worst of it, worse than the voice, worse than the name spoken seven times: that these people, her people, her father among them, were *thankful*. Thankful that it was him. Thankful that the house was pleased. Thankful that the terrible arithmetic had found its number and they could go back to their suppers and their unfinished songs with the account balanced for another generation.

Mara got up out of the wet grass and walked away from the yew with her fists clenched so hard her nails bit blood out of her palms, and she did not look back at the lit window, and she did not scream, though screaming was in her like water in a locked cellar.

Because she had said nothing.

She had stood in the dark and heard her death sentence pronounced on the man she — and she flinched from the word even in her own head, even now — the man she loved, and she had said nothing, and her silence had been the eighth vote, the one that mattered most, the one the house had been counting on.

* * *

She found him at the inn, of all places, in the taproom with his recorder and his notebooks spread out like a surgeon's instruments, buying old Mrs. Tredinnick a sherry and asking her, gently, endlessly, about the lullaby. The lamplight made him look younger than he was. His sleeves were rolled. There was ink on his fingers. He looked up when she came in and smiled — actually smiled, warm and unguarded, glad to see her — and Mara felt that smile go through her like a nail.

"Lior," she said. Her voice came out steady. She was grateful for that, at least; some Joubert in her bones knew how to keep a level tone over an open grave. "Walk with me. I want to show you something."

"Now? It's nearly dark."

"That's rather the point."

He laughed, capped his flask, gathered his notebook — gathering his notebook the way other men gathered their courage — and followed her out into the blue dark, past the inn sign creaking on its bracket, toward the churchyard gate.

Behind them, in the taproom, unnoticed, his recorder lay where he'd left it, still running, its little red eye blinking patiently in the lamplight.

And from somewhere far beneath the floorboards — beneath the cellar, beneath the clay, beneath the door — came a sound too low for any living ear in the room: a hum of contentment, patient and warm, like a house settling, like a keeper turning in sleep, like something that had waited two hundred years and could wait nineteen days more.

But it did not have to wait nineteen days.

That night, for the first time, the humming rose all the way up through the churchyard soil, and every bell in the tower stirred on its wheel without a hand touching them, and Mara, standing with Lior among the unvisited graves with words dying in her throat, felt the ground under her feet go gently, terribly warm.
