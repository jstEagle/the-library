## 10. Run, Then

The morning bus was due at seven-forty. Lior was at the crossroads by seven-fifteen, duffel bag over one shoulder, the recorder zipped into its case against his chest like a second heart. He had not told anyone he was leaving. That had been deliberate — not out of any superstition, he told himself, but because he had learned in twenty years of fieldwork that departures announced in advance became departures argued against, and he had no patience for arguments he could not win with evidence.

The evidence was this: an old woman dead with a finished song on her lips. A recorder full of static that breathed. A ledger with its pages torn out like teeth. A village that sang beautifully and stopped, all of it, every voice, at the same line, as if the silence were a note they had all agreed to hold forever.

He was a folklorist. He collected the things people could not say and put them in archives where they could be studied at a safe distance. But Hollowmere had begun collecting *him* — he could feel it, the way one feels a gaze between the shoulder blades — and the only rational response was to remove himself from the collection.

The crossroads stood a mile east of the village green, where the lane from Hollowmere met the old turnpike. A leaning signpost, its paint flaked to grey wood, pointed toward Ashford and the coast. The morning was cold and colourless, the hedges beaded with mist, and the road ran straight and empty in both directions as far as he could see.

At seven-forty the bus did not come.

At eight-ten it did not come.

He checked the timetable he had photographed on his first day, as he photographed everything. *Hollowmere — Ashford, daily, 7:40.* The photograph was still there, crisp and undeniable. He stood at the crossroads with the proof in his hand and the bus did not come, and by nine the mist had thickened rather than lifted, folding the road into itself fifty yards out, so that walking toward Ashford felt like walking into a room where the lights had not yet been turned on.

Fine. He had a car. It was parked at the inn, a rented saloon he had driven up from the coast eleven days ago. Eleven days. It felt like a season.

He walked back through the village, and the village watched him go with the particular stillness of things that are pretending not to watch. Mrs. Aldery at the bakery window. Two men by the pump, their conversation dying as he passed. A child on a doorstep who did not wave, only looked, with an oldness in her face that children should not have, and hummed — three notes of a tune he now knew too well — before her mother pulled her inside.

*They know,* he thought. *They all know, and they are watching to see what I do.*

The car sat where he had left it, under the elm by the inn's side door. Its windscreen was filmed with condensation, though the morning was dry. He opened the driver's door, and the smell reached him before anything else: dark water, stagnant and sweet, the smell of the churchyard in high summer when the ground gave up its secrets to the nose before it would give them to the spade.

He looked down. The footwells were full of water — black, still, perfectly level, as though the car had been standing in a flooded lane rather than on dry gravel. It lapped at the base of the seats without a single ripple. When he reached in and pulled the bonnet release and walked around to lift the hood, the engine bay was brimming, every crevice filled with the same black water, and the dipstick, when he drew it, came out dripping and smelled of turned earth and old flowers.

There had been no rain. There had been no rain in eleven days.

He stood there with the dipstick in his hand and made himself think like a scholar. Condensation, perhaps. A leak. Groundwater. He said the words aloud, one after another, and heard how thin they sounded in the empty lane, and understood that he was no longer reasoning but reciting — that rationality, for him, had become a liturgy, and he was saying the words without the belief that had once given them meaning.

He fetched a bucket from the inn's scullery without asking, and no one stopped him, and no one helped him, and no one seemed surprised. It took him until midday to bail the water out, and the water came black and cold and endless, as though the car were a well, and when he finally got the engine dry enough to try, the starter turned and turned and turned with a sound like something being drowned, and did not catch.

* * *

He tried the roads on foot that afternoon.

The turnpike east, toward Ashford: he walked it for two hours, measuring by the mile-marker he passed at the start, and at the end of two hours he passed the same mile-marker, on the same side, with the same lichen map spreading across its face like a continent he had memorised without meaning to. The mist had not lifted. The hedges were the same hedges. A crow sat on the same fence post, and when it saw him the second time it did not fly, only tilted its head, and he could have sworn — he would not swear it, he was a rationalist, he would *note* it — that the crow looked bored.

He tried the coast road south. Same result, though it took him longer, because he kept telling himself the loop was a trick of exhaustion, of identical English lanes, of a mind primed for pattern. He tried the north road, which climbed toward the moors, and came down into Hollowmere from an angle that did not exist, past a field of standing stones he had never seen and could not later find, and arrived, sweating and furious, at the village green as the light went out of the sky.

The green was empty except for one figure, sitting on the low wall by the pump with her coat buttoned to the throat, as though she had been waiting a long time and had made her peace with waiting.

"You walked the north road," Mara said.

"You were following me."

"Everyone follows everyone here. It's not spying, it's just — there's nowhere else to look." She stood. Her face in the last light was tired in a way that had nothing to do with the day. "Your car's still full of water, I expect."

"You know that."

"I know what happens to people who try to leave." She said it flatly, without drama, the way one reports weather. "It doesn't work, Lior. It's never worked. Not for anyone the house has looked at."

"The *house*."

"You know what I mean. You've been down to the door. Don't tell me you haven't. You have the look."

"What look?"

She was quiet a moment. "My mother had it. Near the end."

The words dropped between them like stones into the black water in his engine. He heard what was underneath them — the shape of a story she had not yet agreed to tell — and every instinct he had as a collector leaned toward it, and something newer and less professional leaned away, because he found he did not want to know what had happened to her mother, not if knowing would make Mara's face look like it looked now.

"I'm walking out," he said. "Tonight. The mist will lift, or the loop is psychological, or—"

"Or it isn't." She pushed off the wall and stood close enough that he could see the chapped skin of her hands, the grave-dirt worked into the creases that no washing ever fully cleared. Sexton's hands. "Come on, then. I'll walk with you. I'd rather you heard it from me than from the road."

They walked out the east lane together, past the crossroads, past the mile-marker with its lichen continent, into the mist. She carried a lantern, unlit. Neither of them spoke for the first mile, and the silence was not comfortable but it was honest, and he was grateful for it in a way he could not have explained.

* * *

It was on the second circuit — when the mile-marker rose out of the mist again and Mara stopped beside it and touched it with two fingers, almost tenderly, like touching a headstone — that she told him.

"I was nineteen," she said. "It was eleven years ago, this autumn. My mother was the sexton before me. Before that she was — other things. Keeper-things. You don't have the words yet; I didn't either, then." She turned the lantern over in her hands, not lighting it, just holding it. "One night in October she walked out of our house and down into the churchyard and didn't come back up. My father knew. He sat at the table and didn't stop me when I ran out after her. I think — I think some part of him wanted one of us to try. To prove it could be done."

"And?"

"And I walked." She said it simply. "All night. The coast road, the moor road, the river path. I walked until my boots filled with blood, and every road came back to the churchyard gate, and the last time it came back, my mother was standing at the gate in her nightdress, barefoot in the frost, and she wasn't cold. She took my hands and they were warm, warmer than living hands, and she said—" Mara's voice did not break, but it narrowed, the way a road narrows toward a bridge. "She said, *It's kind here, love. It's kind, and it's hungry, and those are the same thing, and you'll understand when it's your turn.* And she kissed my forehead and walked back through the gate and the ground took her feet like the tide takes a footprint, gentle, and my father was suddenly beside me holding me up, and neither of us ever said her name out loud again."

Lior stood in the mist with his heart going like a trapped bird. "Your mother is down there. Under the churchyard. In the house."

"She's in the house." Mara looked at him, and her eyes in the lantern-light she still hadn't lit were wet and furious and terribly steady. "Singing. She always sang best of anyone in the village. Now she never has to stop."

"And you tried to leave, and you couldn't, and you stayed. And now they've chosen me, and you're the one meant to—" He stopped. "Prepare me. That's what you said. What did you mean, Mara? What does preparing involve?"

"Don't." She turned away. "Don't ask me that tonight."

"I have a right—"

"You have a right to the truth, yes, and you'll get it, every word of it, I promise you. But not on a road that's walking us in circles, not while the house is listening through the mist. It listens better when we're afraid. Fear seasons things." She said it with the weary fluency of a catechism learned too young. "Come back to the village. Eat something. Sleep, if you can. And tomorrow I'll tell you what I was sworn to do, and you can hate me, and you should, a little, because I let them say your name and I said nothing, and I will carry that however this ends."

The confession landed in him and did not wound the way he expected. It settled instead, cold and specific, like the black water finding its level. She had said nothing. She had watched the seven families speak his name and kept her silence, and she was telling him so on a looping road at night because she would not let him learn it from anyone kinder.

"You could have run with me," he said. "Tonight. Both of us. If the roads loop for me, maybe—"

"They'd loop for both of us. They looped for my mother for forty years, Lior. She knew every verse of the covenant and she never found the way out, because there isn't one. There's only through." She finally lit the lantern, and its small flame stood up straight in the dead air, casting their two shadows long and joined across the mist. "Through the door, or through the years. Those are the choices Hollowmere gives. My father chose the years. My mother chose the door. And I—" She looked at him then, fully, and the war in her face was naked at last, duty and something warmer and more terrible fighting for the same ground. "I don't know what I'm choosing yet. That's the truth. That's the first free thing I've said in eleven years."

The mist pressed close. Somewhere behind them, though neither had heard footsteps, the mile-marker stood with its lichen continent, patient as a grave.

"Then choose now," Lior said quietly. "Walk with me. Not back to the village. Just — walk. All night, like you did. If the roads bring us back, they bring us back, but we'll come back together, and whatever you have to tell me, you'll tell me on the way, and I'll hear it standing next to you instead of across from you."

She stared at him. The lantern flame trembled — or the air around it did, the whole night leaning in to listen.

"You'd want to hear it," she said, "knowing what it means? That I'm the one who was meant to deliver you?"

"I've recorded dying women's last songs and grave-diggers' jokes and a vicar lying through his teeth about a ledger. I can hear one more hard thing. Especially from you."

For a long moment she didn't move. Then she reached out and took his hand — her fingers cold, his colder, both of them grave-cold now, the village's cold, the house's cold — and turned them both back down the lane, away from the marker, into the mist that was already, gently, without malice, folding the road behind them and laying it down again ahead.

They walked. And three miles on, when the crossroads rose to meet them out of the dark, Mara did not seem surprised — but she stopped, and looked at the signpost pointing toward Ashford and the sea, and said, so softly that he almost missed it:

"Last time I walked this road, the mist sang back."

And far off, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the world the mist allowed, something below the churchyard — warm, patient, glad — began, very quietly, to hum the first verse of a song Lior had not yet taught it.
