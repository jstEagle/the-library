## 7. Doors That Count Back

The manor at night was a different animal than the manor by day.

By day it had been merely wrong — the claw-grooves beside every doorframe, worn smooth as river stones, the way the light fell strangely in rooms that should have been symmetrical. By day Elodie could hold it at arm's length with a clipboard and a torch and the small professional comforts of procedure: photograph, note, measure, move on.

But night two had no clipboard. Night two had only him, a thermos of coffee gone bitter, and the house.

He had set up camp in the great hall, deliberately — the one room with four exits he could see from any position, the one room where nothing could come around a corner without him knowing. He'd arranged his equipment in a semicircle: EMF meter, thermal camera, the good flashlight with the spare batteries taped to its body, and, because he was who he was, a roll of masking tape and a marker. He had numbered the doors. One through eleven, in his own handwriting, at eye height on each frame.

Eleven doors. He had counted them three times that evening and gotten eleven all three times, which should have been reassuring and somehow wasn't. It felt less like confirmation and more like permission. Like the house was letting him settle into the number before it did something to it.

At 11:40 p.m., door six opened.

Not slammed. Not creaked. It simply swung inward, slow and smooth, on hinges that had screamed rust at him all afternoon when he'd tested them, and stood open like a mouth.

Elodie did not move. He watched it for a full minute, then two, marking the time in his notebook because writing things down kept his hands busy and his hands busy kept his mind off the fact that there was nothing in this house that could open a door — no wind, no draught, no plumbing pressure — and yet the door was open, and beyond it the corridor lay black as a throat.

Then door seven closed.

The sound was soft. Almost polite. A click of the latch, far down the hall, precise as a knuckle cracking.

Then door eight opened.

He understood what was happening before he understood that he understood it — the way you understand a tune before you can name it. The doors were moving in sequence. His sequence. The order he'd numbered them in, the order he'd walked them that morning with his tape measure and his little green stickers. The house had watched him count, and now it was counting back.

*It knows my system,* he thought, and the thought went through him like cold water through cloth. *It's not just opening doors. It's speaking my language.*

Door nine closed. Door ten opened. Each movement came slower than the last, deliberate, savoring itself, and Elodie realized with a lurch that the rhythm wasn't random either. It matched his footsteps. That morning, walking the circuit, he had paused roughly four seconds between doors — long enough to check the frame, run his thumb near the grooves without touching them, make his notes. The doors were keeping his pace. Somewhere in the dark of the corridors, something was walking his walk, pausing where he paused, and finding each of his eleven doors in turn the way a reader runs a finger down a familiar page.

Door eleven opened last, and stayed open.

And then the whole house exhaled. Every door at once — all eleven, in every wing, on every floor, he felt it more than heard it, a shudder through the floorboards like the building drawing breath — and the silence afterward was worse. It was the silence of an audience settling.

"Right," Elodie said aloud, because his voice worked better than his nerve. "Right."

He got up. He knew he shouldn't follow the sequence; every instinct honed on a hundred haunted jobs said *never go deeper, never let the pattern lead you.* But the alternative was sitting in the hall until dawn while the house rearranged itself around him, and Elodie Iversen had never once in his life been able to sit still inside a question. His grandmother used to say he'd have opened Pandora's box just to count what was inside.

His grandmother. He pushed the thought away. It kept surfacing lately, unbidden, like a body in a pond.

He took the corridor toward door six.

* * *

The hallway was longer than it should have been.

He knew this objectively, because he had measured it — forty-two feet, wall to wall, and he had the figure in his notebook in his own square handwriting. But he had been walking for what felt like forty-two feet and the door at the end hadn't grown any closer, and behind him, when he glanced back — and he made himself glance back, made himself do it twice — the entrance to the great hall was still visible, still lit by his camp lantern, but smaller. Dimmer. As if it were receding at exactly the speed he advanced.

"Not tonight," he told the house. "I've done labyrinths. This is amateur hour."

The house did not answer, which was somehow more insulting than if it had.

He passed door six. It stood open onto a drawing room he'd catalogued that morning: dust sheets like shrouded furniture, a fireplace big enough to roast an ox, water damage blooming across the ceiling in brown continents. Nothing moved in it. But as he went by, he caught the smell — faint, domestic, horribly out of place — of flour. Baking flour, raw and powdery, the smell of a kitchen at work. The same smell from the cottage, from the painted-shut windows with the small handprints dusted across their insides.

His skin crawled up under his collar. He kept walking.

Door seven was shut. He put his hand on the handle — wood, cold, ordinary, and he hated how much comfort he took from that ordinariness — and stopped. Beside the frame, at knee height, the claw-grooves ran their smooth vertical courses. He'd photographed them that morning. Four grooves per doorframe, evenly spaced, too wide for a cat, too narrow for a grown man's spread hand. Worn smooth by decades of touching. By decades of *something* touching them, over and over, patiently, the way a dog wears a path at a fence line.

Except these weren't at knee height anymore.

He saw it slowly, the way you see a change in a loved one's face. The grooves climbed the frame. They rose past his hip, past his waist, and ended — fresh-cut, pale-wooded, sharp-edged where the old ones were polished — at his shoulder. At *his* shoulder. At the exact height his own hand would fall if he stood at this door and reached out the way he'd reached out that morning, checking the frame for movement.

The new grooves were hours old at most. Maybe minutes.

"It's been measuring me," he said quietly. The words fogged in air that shouldn't have gone cold. "Since the cottage. Since the first bloody cottage."

Because that was the thing he'd been refusing to assemble all day, the picture that only completed itself now, here, in the dark with his hand on a cold handle: the flour-dusted handprints on the inside of those sealed windows hadn't been random. They'd been small — child-sized, or nearly — but they'd been placed at intervals, spaced along the glass, and he'd noted at the time, professionally, idly, that they seemed *deliberate*. Arranged. And he had walked that cottage room by room, and the prints had appeared in the rooms after he'd passed through them — he'd told himself he'd missed them on the first pass, tired, rushing, but he'd known, some animal ledger in him had known, that he hadn't missed anything.

It had followed him through the cottage the way it was following him through this corridor. Learning his height. His stride. Which hand he favored. How long he paused at doors.

It wasn't haunting the house.

It was studying *him.*

Elodie let go of the handle and stood very still in the dark, listening to his own heart, and made himself think it through to the end, because thinking it through to the end was the only weapon he'd ever had. If it studied him, it wanted something from him specifically. Not a warm body — the village had plenty of those, generations of them, humming their unnamed hymn. Him. His particular shape. His particular habits. The way he counted doors and kept lights on, the rituals he'd carried since childhood without ever asking himself why, the way you don't ask why you breathe.

Somewhere behind him, deep in the house, a door closed. Then another. Then another — moving *toward* him this time, unhurried, each latch-click a little louder, a little closer, keeping perfect time with his pulse.

Elodie ran.

* * *

He didn't remember choosing a direction. That was the trap of it — the corridors turned him without turning him, gentle as a current, and when he finally stopped, chest heaving, back against a wall he could feel breathing cold through his jacket, he was somewhere he had never surveyed. A narrow service passage, low-ceilinged, lined with doors. Small doors. Servants' doors, maybe, once — but there had been no servants' quarters on any plan of this house, and there were too many of them, eight along one wall, ten, twelve, receding into the dark faster than the geometry of the building allowed.

He raised his flashlight.

Every door had grooves beside it. Fresh ones. All at his shoulder height. All still showing pale wood, still sharp, as though the whole corridor had been clawed within the hour — within the minute — a signature written over and over and over, patient and adoring, the way a child covers a page with the same word.

And on the doors themselves, at his exact eye level — five foot ten, he'd measured it a thousand times for fitting smoke alarms — were handprints.

Wet ones.

They glistened down the length of the passage, dozens of them, each print perfect, five-fingered, pressed flat as if someone had stood at each door and held their palm there a long moment — feeling, or listening, or *waiting.* The prints were fresh. As he watched, the nearest one moved. Not the hand — there was no hand — but the print itself slid a half-inch down the door, dragging slightly, the way a palm slides when someone leans close to peer through a keyhole.

Elodie's flashlight beam shook. He steadied it with both hands, the way he'd taught a dozen nervous apprentices to steady theirs, and the steadiness of his hands was the only part of him left that was professional.

Behind him, at the far end of the passage, a door opened. Ahead, at the other end, another. To his left — there had been no door to his left — one swung silently inward onto darkness.

They were herding him. Politely. Gently. With all the terrible courtesy of something that had learned his manners along with his measurements. It was walking him the way he would walk a rat — not chasing, never chasing, just closing the routes one by one, leaving always exactly one path open, the path it wanted, because it had watched him work and knew he would take the open path, knew he trusted openings, knew that about him better perhaps than he did.

"No," Elodie said. His voice cracked on it and he said it again, harder. "No. You don't get to steer."

He did the thing no rat does and no sensible person does: he sat down. Right there, in the middle of the passage, cross-legged, flashlight upright between his knees like a candle, and he took out his notebook, and he began to count.

Aloud. Slowly. In his grandmother's cadence, though he hadn't let himself think that phrase until it was already out of his mouth — she had counted the same way, he realized, a small rising lilt on the last number, a little exhale after, and he had inherited the rhythm without ever inheriting the reason.

"One," he said. "Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven."

Eleven doors in the passage. He counted them twice. Eleven both times.

The wet handprints, all down the walls, all at his eye level, seemed to lean in and listen.

"Eleven," he said again, to the house, to whatever wore the house. "You hear that? Eleven. That's the number. That's *my* number. And I'm going to sit here, and keep my light on, and count it as many times as it takes until sunrise, and you are not going to make me move one more step in the direction you want."

The house was quiet. The passages were quiet. Even the wet prints stilled, gleaming in the torchlight like a congregation holding its breath.

And then, softly, from the smallest of the twelve doors — the one at the very end, half-hidden in shadow, the one he had not counted because he had not wanted to see it — came a sound.

A voice. Faint, warped, coming through the wood as if through water.

It counted back.

"...nine," it said, in a voice almost his own, "ten... eleven..."

A pause. The little rising lilt on the last number. His lilt. Learned from him, or given to him — he could no longer tell which, and that was the worst of it.

Then, gently, almost kindly, almost tender:

"*Twelve.*"

The twelfth door began, very slowly, to open.
