## 22. Hands Like Mine

The call came on a Tuesday, three weeks after Hollowmere, and Elodie let it ring out twice before he answered.

He was in the kitchen of his flat above the shop, eating toast he had no appetite for, watching the light come up grey over the rooftops of a town that had never once asked him to belong to it. The phone buzzed against the table like something trapped under glass. He knew the number's shape before he looked — rural exchange, landline, the kind of phone that sat in a hallway on a doily and was answered by someone who already knew why you were calling.

He let it go to voicemail. He made tea. He stood at the window with the mug warming his palms and told himself, reasonably, that there were other pest controllers. There were men with better vans and worse imaginations, men who would drive out, set some traps, invoice for rodents, and drive home again without ever counting a single door. The world was full of them. It had managed for centuries.

The voicemail light blinked anyway.

* * *

He played it at noon, standing in the doorway of the shop with the radio off and the sign still turned to CLOSED, as if the building itself wanted privacy for what he was about to hear.

"Mr. Vasquez? This is Marjorie Tullis, from Undermere — it's up past the reservoir, you may not know it." A pause, the careful breath of someone rehearsing. "We've got a problem with the church, see. Or rather — we don't have a church bell anymore. Melted down in the war, they said. Nineteen-forty-one. But three nights running now, at midnight, we've heard ringing. And there are handprints, Mr. Vasquez. On the inside of the vestry windows. Small ones. Painted shut those windows have been since my grandmother's day, and there are small handprints on the inside of the glass, and nobody will say whose hands they are."

Another pause. Longer. When she spoke again her voice had dropped, gone flat in the way voices go when they stop performing and start confessing.

"There's an old ledger in the vestry safe. I shouldn't be telling you this over the phone. But the last name in it is Tullis, and my sister's girl is expecting in the spring, and I would rather — " She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I'd rather it were me than her. If it has to be one of us. You'll come, won't you?"

Elodie stood very still. Outside, a delivery van went past, ordinary as bread. Somewhere a dog barked twice and gave up.

"I'll come," he said, though the line had already gone dead, though she couldn't hear him. He said it the way you say a vow — not because anyone requires it, but because saying it makes it true.

* * *

That evening he took the Hollowmere ledger out of the lockbox where he kept his passport and his mother's rings and the few things that mattered more than money, and he laid it open on the kitchen table under the good lamp.

Three weeks, and he had not opened it once. He had told himself that was recovery. Now, looking at the cracked leather and the water-stained pages, he understood it had been cowardice wearing recovery's coat.

The names ran back and back, page after page, in hands that changed with the centuries — copperplate giving way to spidery Victorian loops giving way to a tired modern scrawl — but the ink never changed. It was always the same brown-black, the colour of dried blood thinned with rainwater, and it never faded. He had checked. He had left the book shut for a week and opened it and the newest entry was as dark as the oldest.

*Vasquez, Edith. Keeper by blood. Fed willingly, 1911.*

His grandmother. Eighteen years old, the year the bell came down.

He turned pages now with fingers that did not quite feel like his own, except that they did — long-boned, narrow, knuckles that swelled early the way hers had in the photographs, nails bitten to the quick the way hers had been in every story his mother told. *You have your grandmother's hands,* his mother used to say, holding them up to the kitchen light like something precious. *She could quiet a bird with those hands.* He had liked that, as a boy. He had liked being told he carried something of hers.

He wondered what Edith Vasquez would think of him now, using those hands to turn the pages of the book that recorded her surrender.

Because that was what it was. He had read the letter eleven times before burning it — no, he hadn't burned it; he had meant to, and then folded it instead into his jacket pocket, where it rode against his heart like a splinter working toward the bone. *If you are reading this, you have my hands.* She had fled rather than serve, and the debt had waited seventy years in the blood, patient as rot, until it found him standing in an empty cottage counting doors.

And now Undermere. And now a Tullis woman offering herself for her sister's granddaughter, which was exactly the arithmetic the thing loved — one life weighed against another, kindness bent into a lever.

He closed the ledger. Opened it again. Made himself look at the full sweep of it: not one village, not one arrangement. The entries clustered — Hollowmere, yes, but references threaded through the older pages, place-names he half-knew from maps of the county's forgotten folds. *Undermere. Carrick Fell. Nether Wold.* Wherever there was a melted bell, there was a buried one beneath it. Wherever there was a buried one, there was a ledger, and a family, and a debt passed hand to hand like a dish too hot to set down.

How many? He counted. Of course he counted; it was the only prayer he knew. Eleven arrangements still active. Eleven bells silenced. Eleven families keeping their terrible appointments through the generations, feeding something patient and tender and hungry so that it would take only what was offered instead of everything.

Eleven. And one man with a van, a sledgehammer, and his grandmother's hands.

* * *

He drove out to Undermere on Thursday, taking the long way around the reservoir because the short way crossed a bridge he didn't trust himself to stop on. The village sat low in its fold of hills, stone and slate, smoke rising straight from chimneys in the windless cold. It looked like nothing. That was the trick of them all, he was learning. Hollowmere had looked like nothing too, right up until midnight.

Marjorie Tullis met him at the churchyard gate. She was seventy-odd, small and square as a gatepost, with her sister's girl's future written all over her face in the language of dread.

"You came," she said, and gripped his hand in both of hers, and Elodie felt the contact all the way up his arm — not warmth exactly, but recognition. Her eyes went to his hands where hers held them, and something moved behind her face.

"Tullis hands," she said softly. "My mother had those hands. So did hers."

"I know," Elodie said. "I'm going to need to see the vestry."

* * *

The vestry windows confirmed everything the phone call promised and worse. Small handprints, five-fingered, pressed into the dust on the inside of glass painted shut since before either of them was born — and at his eye level now, not a child's height. Fresh ones, still tacky at the edges, arranged not randomly but in sequence, left to right across the panes.

Counting, he realized. It was counting windows. Learning the arithmetic of the house before it learned the house.

"It knows I sent for you," Marjorie said behind him. Her voice had gone thin. "It's been waiting for a keeper who'd come instead of run."

"Then it's been waiting a long time," Elodie said, "because I'm not here to keep anything."

He set his bag down on the vestry floor and began, methodically, to unpack: crowbar, chisel, the heavy mallet, coils of rope, a lantern with fresh batteries. Marjorie watched him lay the tools out like a surgeon laying out steel, and he saw the hope move through her and frighten her, because hope was dangerous in villages like this — hope made people say yes to things.

"The bell," he said. "Your records say melted in 1941. Show me where."

She led him down into the crypt without asking how he knew to ask. Old women in old villages always knew; that was the whole disease of the place. The crypt was cold and dry and smelled of stone and candle grease, and in the far corner, beneath a slab marked only with a mason's square, the ground had been disturbed within living memory and tamped back down.

Elodie knelt and laid his palm flat against the slab.

Warm. Faintly, impossibly warm, like a hand resting just beneath the stone.

"They buried it face-down," Marjorie whispered. "So it couldn't speak. Grandfather helped carry it down. He never slept right again."

"No," Elodie said. "I don't suppose he did."

He thought of Hollowmere's bell, of the hour he'd spent swinging the sledgehammer while the sky went pale and the true peal rolled out over the fields and the thing unraveled door by door by counted door. He thought of the silence afterward, the first real silence, and of Ashe standing in the churchyard unable to meet his eyes, remembering everything, forgiven nothing.

He thought of his own hands, wrapped around the hammer's haft, blistered and bleeding and finally, finally his own.

"We dig tonight," he said. "Before moonrise. And Mrs. Tullis — when it rings, you get everyone into the church and you keep them there till dawn. Whatever they hear outside, whatever calls them by name — they stay inside. Can you do that?"

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way people nod at funerals.

"And after?" she asked. "After it's done — will you go on? To the others? Carrick Fell has a melted bell. So does Nether Wold. We've all heard each other's midnights, over the years. We never say it aloud, but we know."

Elodie looked down at his hands, spread in the lantern light against the warm stone. Long-boned, narrow, his grandmother's hands, the inheritance no ledger needed to record. He had spent three weeks trying to hate them. Standing there, he found he couldn't. They were just hands. What they carried was a choice, and choices, unlike curses, could be made new every morning.

"Eleven arrangements," he said. "I counted."

Something that was almost a smile touched Marjorie Tullis's mouth. "Then you'd best get digging, Mr. Vasquez."

He picked up the shovel. Above them, faint through sixty feet of earth and stone, the sun went down, and somewhere in the walls of the vestry, patiently, tenderly, something began to count the windows — and stopped, as if listening, at twelve.
