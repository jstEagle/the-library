## 4. Verse One: The Water Rises

Beatrix woke in the Grey Heron's best room to the sound of a village pretending to be innocent.

Birdsong. A cart wheel squeaking somewhere down the lane. Mrs. Coram — or perhaps it was Coram himself; they seemed to share a single set of lungs between them — sweeping the step below his window with the metronomic patience of someone who had swept that same step for forty years and intended to sweep it for forty more.

He lay still and took inventory of himself. Name: Beatrix Marchetti. Profession: none anyone would envy. Belief system: firmly secular, aggressively so, the kind of man who read horoscopes purely to disagree with them. Sister: dead. Or possibly not dead, depending on which undertaker you believed, and there was only one undertaker in Hollowmere, which was one too many.

The bed was soft. That annoyed him. He distrusted comfort in places like this; comfort was how villages got you. You arrived for a funeral intending to stay two days, and by day three you'd been adopted, fed, and quietly absorbed, and twenty years later your grandchildren were burying strangers in your name.

He got up, dressed, and went downstairs with the specific intention of being unpleasant to someone until he felt better.

* * *

Mrs. Aldery was already in the inn's front room when Beatrix came down, installed in the good chair by the window as though she owned it, which — given the way Coram hovered around her with tea and deference — she very nearly did. She was a small woman folded into a large shawl, with white hair pinned up in a style that had gone out of fashion before either of them was born, and eyes that were quick and brown and, Beatrix noted with some relief, entirely sane.

"There he is," she said, before he'd even crossed the threshold. "The Marchetti boy."

"I'm fifty-one years old, madam."

"And I'm eighty-four, so you'll be a boy until I say otherwise." She patted the sofa beside her. "Sit. You've got the look of a man who slept badly in a good bed, which is the worst kind of sleeping."

He sat, mostly because refusing an old woman before nine in the morning felt like a losing opening move. Tea appeared at his elbow without anyone visibly carrying it. Coram, he noticed, retreated from the room with the smoothness of a man who knew exactly when he wasn't wanted.

"You're Beatrice's brother," Mrs. Aldery said.

"Twin."

"Mm. She said you'd say that."

Something cold walked down Beatrix's spine on small feet. "When did she say that?"

"Oh, years ago. Before she went away." Mrs. Aldery waved a hand, vague as weather. "She used to come sit with me, you know. After her mother died. Bright girl. Too bright for this place, we all said so. This place doesn't care for bright things. It likes them dim and steady, like lamplight through a curtain." She sipped her tea. "You're not bright, are you? No offense meant. You've got the other quality."

"Which is?"

"Slipperiness." She said it approvingly. "You've spent your whole life making sure nobody could get a proper grip on you. Good instinct. Keep it up."

Beatrix opened his mouth to ask what she meant — and then closed it again, because Mrs. Aldery had begun to hum.

It was nothing, at first. An old woman humming over her tea, the way old women do, half a melody with no particular shape, the musical equivalent of doodling. But the tune had a structure underneath the vagueness, a rising figure and then a falling one, like water climbing a wall and sliding back down, climbing and sliding, and Beatrix realized with a small, unwelcome jolt that he recognized it.

He couldn't have. He'd never heard it before. And yet the shape of it sat in his ear like a key sitting in a lock it had no business fitting.

Then Mrs. Aldery began, absently, to talk over her own humming, the way people do when their mouth runs on two tracks at once.

"My Thomas will be along this afternoon, he always comes Thursdays, brings me my tablets and complains about the bus. Thomas. My son. He's got a face like—" The humming paused. Her teacup paused. Everything about her paused, a full-body hesitation, like a clock deciding whether to keep going. "Like a—"

"A face like what?" Beatrix asked gently.

She looked at him. Her eyes were still sane. That was the terrible part. Behind them, something was being erased, and she could feel the eraser moving, and she could do nothing whatsoever about it.

"He has..." She set the cup down with great care, the way you set down something when your hands have become untrustworthy. "I have a son. I know I have a son. He comes Thursdays. He brings the tablets. His name is—" Her brow creased, deepened, worked. "It begins with a T. I'm nearly sure it begins with a T."

"Thomas," Beatrix said. "You just said Thomas."

"Did I?" Genuine surprise. Then, worse, genuine gratitude. "Did I? Oh, thank you, dear. Thank you." She picked her cup back up, steadied, smiled at him — and resumed humming exactly where she'd left off, the rising-and-falling figure, water climbing and sliding back.

Beatrix sat very still.

People forgot things. He knew that. His own father had forgotten entire decades toward the end, had called Beatrix by the name of a man he'd served with in the war, had wept over a dog that died before Beatrix was born. Forgetting was human. Forgetting was sad and ordinary and happened in warm rooms with tea.

But forgetting didn't happen *mid-word*. Forgetting didn't happen while a tune was playing, neat as a signature, tidy as a transaction. And forgetting certainly didn't leave the forgetter *humming*, contented, as though a piece of her had been collected like rent and she'd signed the receipt without looking.

"Mrs. Aldery," he said carefully. "That song you're humming. Where's it from?"

"Hm?" She surfaced from the tune like a swimmer. "Oh. It's just the bell-song, dear. Everyone here knows the bell-song. We're sung it before we're taught to walk." She frowned faintly. "Well. Some of us are. Not the Marchettis, of course."

"Why not the Marchettis?"

Her frown deepened into something that wasn't quite confusion and wasn't quite fear. It was the face of a woman reaching for a drawer and finding it nailed shut. "I don't... isn't that funny. I know there's a reason. There's a very good reason. Your family and the bell, there was..." She shook her head, and the tune slipped out of her again, filling the gap, papering over the hole. Rising and falling. Climbing and sliding.

"Where can I find it written down?" Beatrix asked.

"The hymnals, I suppose. Chapel keeps them. Though—" and here she laughed, a small dry sound with no humor in it anywhere, "—though I don't suppose you'll want the last verse. Nobody sings the last verse."

"Why not?"

And Mrs. Aldery looked at him then, straight on, with those quick sane brown eyes, and said, with the mildness of someone remarking on the weather:

"Because we tore it out, dear. All of them. Every copy. In 1911." She patted his knee with a hand like a dried leaf. "More tea?"

* * *

The chapel of Hollowmere stood at the end of Church Lane behind a yew tree so old and so vast that the chapel seemed less built beside it than permitted by it. Its tower — Beatrix had checked last night, standing in the cold with his collar up — was roofless. Open to the sky like a broken tooth. Whatever bell had once hung up there had hung for no one for a long time.

Inside, the chapel smelled of dust and candle grease and, beneath both, faintly and unpleasantly, of pond water. Morning light came through windows whose colored saints had faded to ghosts of themselves, so that the light fell onto the pews in washes of pale grey and paler grey, the whole spectrum of a place that had given up on color.

The hymnals lived in a wooden cupboard by the door, and Beatrix went through them with the methodical patience of a man who had spent a career fact-checking life itself. Forty-one books. He pulled each one, opened it to the index, found the entry — *The Bell-Song, Traditional* — and turned to the page.

Every single copy ended the same way.

Verse one. Verse two. Verse three. And then a ragged stub of paper where verse four should be, torn so close to the spine that whoever had done it had needed fingers or a knife and no hesitation at all. Forty-one books. Forty-one identical wounds.

He sat down in the front pew with the best-preserved copy and read what remained.

*Verse one* was about water. Of course it was. *The water rises, the water calls, it knows the names of all who fall.* Cheerful stuff. The kind of thing you'd teach a child if you wanted the child to grow up checking the bath for intent.

*Verse two* was about names, or the losing of them — *and what the water takes away, no tongue remembers come the day* — which explained Mrs. Aldery's Thursday problem with a clarity Beatrix did not appreciate.

*Verse three* was short. Four lines, and strange ones, addressed directly to the singer: *hold fast your voice, hold fast your breath, the third verse buys a little death, and what returns across the ford* — and then the page simply stopped, mid-sentence, mid-thought, mid-world.

Beatrix stared at the torn edge for a long time.

Someone had decided, in 1911, that whatever came after *the third verse buys a little death* was too dangerous to exist on paper. Not dangerous to read — dangerous to *exist*. They hadn't burned the books; burning was public, and public things invite questions. They'd torn every page by hand, quietly, thoroughly, and then presumably carried the verses out into the churchyard and buried them or burned them privately or — his eye drifted up, unwillingly, to the roofless tower, to the open sky where a melted bell should not have been able to ring —

No. He stopped himself. He was not going to do that. He was not going to stand in a dusty chapel and let a hole in a hymnal make him afraid of a bell tower with no bell in it. Things had explanations. Records existed. In 1911 somebody had melted a bell — wartime scrap drives, metal shortages, some perfectly dull municipal reason — and somebody else had gotten enthusiastic with a knife, and the two facts had nothing to do with each other, and Mrs. Aldery's missing Thursday was age, plain and simple, and the tune in his own head that had started up again without his permission —

He caught himself humming.

His own voice, low, rising and falling, water climbing and sliding back. He clamped his mouth shut so hard his teeth clicked in the empty chapel. The sound of it echoed anyway, or seemed to, off stone that had no business carrying anything.

"Right," he said aloud, to the saints, to the yew shadow, to nobody. "That's quite enough of that."

* * *

He found Mrs. Aldery that afternoon on a bench outside the chapel gate, wrapped in her shawl against a wind that wasn't blowing particularly hard, watching the road the way people watch roads when they're waiting for someone who always comes.

"Your Thomas," Beatrix said, sitting beside her. "Does he come today?"

"Thursdays," she agreed comfortably. "He brings my tablets. He's got a face like—" The pause came again, right on schedule, a train arriving at its station. Her hands tightened on the shawl. "Begins with a T."

"Thomas," Beatrix supplied.

"Thomas. Yes." She exhaled. "You're a useful boy. Have you thought about staying?"

"No."

"Shame." She gazed down the empty road. "My Thomas. He's a good son. He looks like his father, which is a mercy, because his father I can still—" She stopped. Not the humming pause this time. A different pause, a colder one, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped as if the wind might carry it somewhere. "It's the verses, isn't it. I hum the first one and I lose the little things. Edna Corby hums the second one now, since winter, and she's lost her wedding day, her whole wedding day, forty-nine years of it, gone like milk spilt. And old Tam Weller—" She pressed her lips together. "Tam sang the third verse once, drunk, on a dare, in 'sixty-one. He came back from it. Mostly. But he never said another word in his life, and he'd sit by the well of an evening, just sitting, listening down into it. Smiling."

Beatrix kept his voice level. "What happens if someone sings the fourth?"

The look she gave him was long and level and, for one moment, stripped of every year of comfortable old-lady armor, and what was underneath it was terrified.

"Don't," she said. "Whatever you're thinking, and I can see you're thinking, because you've got your mother's thinking-face on — don't. The fourth verse isn't for singing. It's for *being sung*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and I'd tell you what it is except—" she tapped her temple, "—they've nailed that drawer shut too. Isn't that funny. I know there's something I'm not supposed to help you with. I can feel the shape of it. Like a tooth that's been pulled. You keep putting your tongue in the gap."

Down the empty road, a bus appeared, far off, wobbling with distance.

"That'll be Thomas," Mrs. Aldery said, and stood, and gathered her shawl, and then paused, and looked at Beatrix with sudden fierce clarity, the sanest he'd seen her yet.

"She left you something, you know. Your sister. Before she went away. She said, *if my brother comes, and he will come, because he's stubborn as a nail — give him the book.*"

"What book?"

"I don't remember," Mrs. Aldery said, and her face smoothed over into pleasant blankness, and she turned to wave at the approaching bus, humming her rising-and-falling tune, water climbing and sliding back, while Beatrix sat frozen on the bench with the word *book* ringing in him like a struck nail.

He had gone through her effects. He was certain he had gone through her effects. Two suitcases, sent ahead to the inn, inventoried the first night with grim thoroughness: clothes, papers, a photograph of the two of them aged nine and furious about something.

He had inventoried everything.

Hadn't he?

Behind him, faintly, from the roofless tower of the bell-less church, came a single note at dusk. Soft. Patient. Almost fond.

Beatrix did not turn around. He walked back to the inn instead, fast, with his collar up, telling himself all the way that there was an explanation, there was always an explanation — and wondering, with the first honest fear he'd let himself feel since arriving, why the parish register he'd need to check tomorrow suddenly felt less like research and more like an appointment someone else had made for him.
