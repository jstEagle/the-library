## 8. The Congregation of the Drowned

The invitation came under his door at half past six, folded into a triangle like a schoolboy's note, and Beatrix stood holding it for a long moment before he opened it, because he had already guessed what it would say and wanted, briefly, to live in the world where he hadn't.

*You are welcome at evening service,* it read, in a copperplate so careful it looked printed. *The congregation hopes you will sit with us. Mrs. Aldery has asked especially.*

He should have said no. He knew that even then, standing in the narrow room above the inn with the paper trembling slightly in his hand — not from any wind, the window was shut, but from the particular tremor a man develops when his body knows something his mind is still arguing about. He should have said no, locked the door, pushed the wardrobe against it, and spent the night cataloguing the innkeeper's lies until dawn made them manageable.

Instead he found himself, twenty minutes later, walking up the lane toward the church with his collar turned up against the dusk, telling himself that a skeptic attends things. That was the whole point of being a skeptic. You went and looked. You did not cower in rented rooms while a village sang hymns at the dark.

Besides — and here was the thought he would not examine too closely — if they were going to do something to him, better to be there when they tried.

* * *

The church of St. Maren-at-the-Water had no roof.

Beatrix had noticed this on his first morning, from the ridge road: a stone rectangle open to the sky like a swimming pool drained of its water, the tower rising from one corner with its top sheared off clean, as though some enormous hand had simply taken the bell away and couldn't be bothered to put anything back. The guidebook — the one dog-eared copy in the inn's lobby — called it "picturesquely ruined," which was the sort of phrase guidebooks use about places where something went wrong that nobody wants to name.

Tonight the ruin was full of people.

That was the first wrongness, and it arrived before he'd even reached the lych-gate. He had been in Hollowmere four days now, and in four days he had counted perhaps thirty residents, most of them elderly, several of them the kind of rural sparse that makes you wonder who grows the food and fixes the fences. But the pews tonight held what looked like two hundred souls, packed shoulder to shoulder, and every one of them turned to look at him as he came through the gate, and every face was smooth and pleasant and *familiar*, in the way that faces in dreams are familiar — not anyone you know, but everyone you've ever failed to place.

"Mr. Marchetti," said a woman near the door, warmly, moving aside to make room. "We saved you a seat."

"I'm not— I don't attend services," Beatrix said. It was true. He attended nothing. Churches, weddings, funerals, office parties — he had built an entire life on the principle that belonging was the first step toward being owed, and he had never once been owed.

"We know," the woman said, and smiled, and the smile had such genuine kindness in it that he sat down.

The pew was cold stone beneath him. Above, where a roof should have been, the sky had gone the color of a bruise healing, deep violet shot through with the last orange of sunset, and a few early stars pricked through already. Moths moved in the open air. Somewhere behind the broken tower, the hills made their long dark shapes against the sky.

Mrs. Aldery was three rows ahead. She turned and caught his eye and patted the pew beside her, and Beatrix, feeling like a man wading into water whose depth he can't see, got up and moved to her.

"You came," she whispered. Her hands were folded in her lap, knuckles like walnuts under thin skin. "I hoped you would. It's better, dear, if you see it while you still can choose to."

"See what?"

She didn't answer. The service was beginning.

* * *

There was no priest. That was the second wrongness, and Beatrix noted it with the small part of his mind that still took notes, the part that had kept him sane in boarding schools and army barracks and his sister's chaotic, brilliant orbit: *no clergy, no book, no candles.* Just the congregation, settling into silence the way water settles, and then, without signal or cue, the singing began.

It was the song. Of course it was the song. Four days in Hollowmere and he had heard it everywhere — hummed over garden fences, murmured by children skipping rope with a slowness that made the rope look heavy, whistled by the postman in a major key that somehow made it worse. He had assumed it was a folk tune, some local round about harvest or drowning or whatever pastoral misery these songs usually carried.

Sung by two hundred voices in a roofless church at nightfall, it was none of those things.

It was beautiful. That was the revulsion of it — that was the thing that caught in his chest like a fishhook — because he had braced himself for wrongness, for discord, for the obvious horror-movie dissonance, and instead the harmony rose off the stones and up through the missing roof and out into the violet sky, and it was *lovely*. It sounded like his mother's kitchen. It sounded like rain on a tent roof when you're warm inside. It sounded like every safe place he had ever invented for himself during the bad years, set to music.

And the mouths were wrong.

He saw it within the first verse, once he stopped fighting the beauty and started watching. The sound came in waves, whole and unified, but the mouths moved separately — some opening on syllables the others had finished, some closed entirely while their owners' voices carried on regardless, one old man near the front singing with his lips pressed tight as a sealed jar, his jaw working underneath like something trying to get out. A girl of perhaps ten sang a half-beat behind her own face, so that Beatrix could watch her mouth form the word and then hear it arrive late, like thunder after lightning, like a letter posted years ago finally delivered.

*They're not singing it,* he thought. *It's singing them.*

His skin crawled. He looked down at his own hands, flat on his knees, and made sure — carefully, deliberately — that his own mouth stayed shut.

Beside him, Mrs. Aldery sang too, but softly, and her mouth matched her voice, and her eyes were open and wet and fixed on the broken tower as though she could see something there that the rest of the sky was hiding.

"Don't listen with your heart," she murmured between verses, not turning her head. "Listen with your ears only. It gets in through the other way."

"What gets in?"

"The tune, dear." She said it the way you'd say *the flu*. "It's looking for somewhere to live."

* * *

Between the third and fourth verses — there seemed to be only four; the congregation sang three and then circled back, again and again, a tide going out and returning — Beatrix risked speaking.

"Mrs. Aldery. Why are they all here? This village has thirty people in it."

"It has more than that." Her voice was very quiet. "It has everyone it's ever had. Look properly, Mr. Marchetti. You're a clever man. Look at the coats."

He looked. And once she said it, he couldn't stop seeing it: the clothing was wrong. Not theatrical, not costume-party wrong — subtler than that. A woman two rows down wore a dress cut in a style fifty years out of date, and wore it the way you wear your own clothes, carelessly, comfortably. A young man near the aisle had on a soldier's greatcoat with brass buttons gone green, and his boots were mud-caked as though he'd walked far to be here. Nobody looked preserved. They looked *current*, each in their own decade, as though the church held not a congregation but a stack of photographs someone had shuffled together and brought accidentally to life.

"The drowned," Mrs. Aldery said. "Or near enough. Everyone the well took, and everyone the bell called, and everyone who heard the whole song and stayed anyway. They come back for service. It's polite of them." She paused. "They were always polite, this village. That was always the trouble with it."

"And Beatrice?" The name came out of him before he could stop it, rough-edged. "Is she — do they—"

Mrs. Aldery's hand found his wrist. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be.

"Not here," she breathed. "Not in front of them. They'll tell you what you want to hear, dear, and they'll believe it while they say it, and that's the cruelest kind of lie there is. Wait for me. After service. I've something to give you, and I'd rather give it while my own name is still mine."

"Your name?"

But the congregation surged into the song again, louder, and whatever she said next was swallowed.

* * *

He tried, in the fourth repetition, to be scientific about it. That was his refuge and always had been: when the world went strange, you measured it. He counted the singers — lost count past a hundred and forty. He checked the acoustics — the sound should have scattered skyward through the missing roof, and instead it pooled, sitting low over the pews like fog, thick enough that he felt it press gently against his eardrums. He watched the tower, that truncated stump of masonry, and confirmed for the hundredth time that there was no bell in it, no beam, no rope, nothing but open sky and stars.

And yet under the voices, so faint he could deny it, he heard the overtone. The bell-note. Ringing in time with the song, ringing *as* the song, as though the melted bell of 1911 had soaked into the village the way rust soaks into groundwater, and everything here — stone, throat, evening air — rang with it now.

Only he heard it. He was almost sure only he heard it. Vale — no. Not Vale. She wasn't here yet. He corrected himself with a small internal wince; he'd been doing that lately, reaching for people who hadn't arrived, as if the village were pre-populating his future the way it had pre-populated his past.

The song ended — or rather, stopped, mid-phrase, all two hundred voices ceasing at once like a door closing — and the silence afterward had texture. Weight. Beatrix realized he had been gripping Mrs. Aldery's arm hard enough to leave marks, and let go, muttering an apology.

"No need," she said. "Hold on to something. That's what it's for."

The congregation rose. Not all at once — in a slow ripple, pew by pew, front to back, and as each row stood, its members turned toward Beatrix. Not staring. *Regarding.* The way a jury regards. The way family regards across a table where a difficult thing must be said kindly.

An old man in the green-buttoned greatcoat inclined his head to Beatrix as he passed. "You have her look," he said, in a voice like gravel under water. "Your sister. Around the eyes. We remember."

"I never met you," Beatrix said.

"No," the man agreed pleasantly, and walked on, out through the lych-gate, and where the moonlight touched his greatcoat it went through him just slightly, the way light goes through smoke, and then didn't, and Beatrix decided firmly that he had imagined it.

The pews emptied. The violet sky deepened toward black. In ten minutes the roofless church held only Beatrix, Mrs. Aldery, and the moths.

* * *

"Well," Beatrix said, and found his voice had gone hoarse. "That was certainly a service."

Mrs. Aldery laughed — a real laugh, sudden and cracked and human, the first fully human sound he'd heard all evening, and he could have wept with gratitude for it. "Oh, you're your mother's son, aren't you. She used to say things exactly like that. Deflecting with the joke before the fear could get its boots on."

"You knew my mother?"

"I knew everyone, dear. That was my job, more or less. Keeping track." She gathered herself up from the pew slowly, joint by joint, a process like watching a ship come about. "Walk me home. My legs are done with stairs and my door has a step, and I'd rather say what I have to say on the way, in the open, where the walls can't practice their listening."

They came out through the lych-gate into a night gone suddenly, enormously clear. Stars in reckless quantity. The lane pale under the moon. From the village below came the ordinary sounds of ordinary evening — a dog, a door, a kettle — and Beatrix clung to those sounds like a man clinging to driftwood, because they were proof that somewhere the world still worked normally.

"They sing it three times," Mrs. Aldery said, as they walked. "Every service. Three verses, over and over, and never the fourth. Do you know why?"

"Because the fourth is torn out of the hymnals. I found one. There's a stub where the page was removed."

"Mm. And do you know why it was torn out?"

"I assumed vandalism. Or censorship. Someone decided the verse was inappropriate."

Mrs. Aldery stopped walking. She turned and looked up at him, and in the moonlight her face was ancient and grave and full of a pity so vast it frightened him more than the congregation had.

"Someone decided it was *dangerous*, Mr. Marchetti. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole history of this valley." She resumed walking, slower now. "A song with three verses is a song. A song with four is an invitation. The fourth verse isn't sung to the congregation. The fourth verse is sung *to one person*. One name, one voice, one debt. And when the bell finds the throat it wants—" She stopped herself, pressed her lips together. "I've said too much and not enough. Both at once. Old women do that."

"Mrs. Aldery—"

"You asked about your sister." Her voice dropped. "Beatrice came to me in the spring. Sat in my kitchen just where you're walking now, asking sharp questions in that bright way of hers, and I liked her instantly and feared for her instantly, and both proved correct, as they generally do." Her breath caught. "She heard the song whole, dear. I don't know how — she wasn't meant to, she wasn't the one it wanted, but she was clever and curious and she pulled the thread. And after that she changed. She started writing letters she didn't send. She started saying her own name like she was checking it still fit. And then she went down to the well one evening, and I was too slow, and I have been too slow for ninety years in this village and I will die too slow."

The lane tilted, very slightly, the way a deck tilts. Beatrix kept walking because stopping would mean falling.

"She's dead," he said. "There's a body. I've seen it. It's—"

"It's a body," Mrs. Aldery agreed. "Whether it's hers, whether it's yours, whether it's anyone's — that's a different question, and I'm too old to answer it, and there's someone coming up the lane behind us who shouldn't hear the rest."

He turned. The lane was empty. Moonlight, hedgerows, the distant smudge of the chapel.

"There's no one there."

"No," Mrs. Aldery said. "Not yet. But there will be, and soon, and when they come they'll be carrying lanterns and singing beautifully, and you'll want to go with them, because that's the trick of it — they'll be so *kind*." She reached into her coat and pressed something into his hand, small and stiff-cornered. Paper. "Take this. Don't read it tonight. Read it when you've seen the well do something you can't explain, and not one moment sooner, because the song reads along with you, dear, and I won't have it learning the rest before you do."

"What is it?"

"A page," she said. "From a hymnal. The one they think they all destroyed." Her grip tightened on his hand, walnut knuckles and surprising iron. "Guard it, Mr. Marchetti. And guard yourself. And whatever you do, however much you grieve, however much you miss her —"

The old woman swayed. Caught herself on the gatepost of her cottage. Laughed shakily.

"— never learn the last verse whole," she finished. "Now goodnight. And Beatrix —"

She had never used his first name before. It landed strangely, warmly, like a hand on the shoulder.

"When you catch yourself humming," she said, "and you will, everyone does — stop at three."

Then her door closed, and Beatrix stood alone in the moonlit lane with a folded page in his fist and the song, unbidden, already rising in his own chest — one verse, two, three —

He stopped.

Below in the village, faint and patient, something that was not a bell rang anyway.
