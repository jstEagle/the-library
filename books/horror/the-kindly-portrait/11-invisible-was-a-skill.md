## 11. Invisible Was a Skill

The trouble with being a twin, Beatrix had learned by the age of six, was that nobody ever looked for you twice.

They found Beatrice and assumed they'd found both of you. Teachers did it. Aunts did it. The man at the fairground who ran the coconut shy did it, handing Beatrice two prizes because "the little one's already gone off to the swings," when in fact the little one was standing directly beside her, holding her hand, being comprehensively overlooked.

Beatrix hadn't minded. That was the strange part, the part he'd spent his whole adult life not examining too closely. He *hadn't* minded. Being unseen was a comfort, like a coat you grew into before you knew you'd need it.

He sat now in Coram's inn, in the hour before dusk when the light through the window went the color of weak tea, and let himself remember — properly remember, for the first time since arriving in Hollowmere — how he had learned it. How the skill had been built, brick by patient brick, by a boy who understood something about houses made of attention: that they could be slipped out of quietly, through the back door of yourself.

* * *

Their mother, Rosa Marchetti, used to say that God dealt twins the same hand of cards but shuffled them differently, and in the Marchetti household it was Beatrice who got all the face cards.

Not that she was prettier — they were identical, mirror-twins, so much so that their own grandmother once kissed Beatrix on the forehead and told him his new dress suited him, and he'd said thank you, because correcting her would have required being noticed, and that was against policy. No, it wasn't beauty. It was *voltage*. Beatrice walked into a room the way weather walks into a valley. She asked questions adults weren't ready for. She once, aged seven, stood up in church and asked Father Anselm — loudly, in the pause after the sermon — who exactly had counted all the souls in purgatory, and whether they'd shown their work.

Father Anselm had turned a color usually reserved for beets and said something about holy mystery, and Beatrice had said, "That's what people say when they haven't done the reading," and the entire congregation had made the sound a crowd makes when it isn't sure whether it's witnessing a scandal or a miracle.

Beatrix, in the pew beside her, had slid down six inches until only the top of his head showed above the wood, and felt perfectly, gloriously safe.

Because here was the arithmetic he'd worked out early, working it out the way other children worked out football scores or the combinations of padlocks: attention was a finite substance. Every room contained only so much of it. And every ounce that landed on Beatrice was an ounce that didn't land on him.

She was the lightning rod. He was the house.

"I don't know why you hide," she told him once, aged nine, sprawled on the floor of their shared bedroom doing a jigsaw upside down, because right-side-up jigsaws were for people who needed help. "You're interesting. You notice things."

"Noticing things is how you get caught noticing things," Beatrix said.

"That's not a sentence."

"It's a very good sentence if nobody hears it."

Beatrice had laughed so hard she knocked over the jigsaw, and then blamed him for it, and then made him help rebuild it, which took three weeks, during which they invented an entire country — its rivers, its postal system, its one regrettable war — and this was the thing about Beatrice that the village never saw and Beatrix never forgot: her brightness wasn't performance. It was heat. It kept you warm even when you were standing behind her, out of the light.

* * *

School taught him the rest of the curriculum.

There was a teacher, Mr. Pell, who believed passionately in "drawing children out." He had read a pamphlet. He had a system. Shy children, in Mr. Pell's cosmology, were simply flowers awaiting the sun of his personal encouragement, and Beatrix was the shyest flower in the bed.

"Marchetti!" Pell would boom. "Your sister always has an answer. What say you?"

And Beatrix would say nothing, beautifully. Nothing, delivered with correct posture and a mild, pleasant blankness, was a masterpiece. Nothing could be sustained for a full minute while thirty classmates watched, and Pell's gaze would slide off him like rain off glass, seeking purchase, finding none, moving on to some child insufficiently trained in the art of vacancy.

Beatrice, meanwhile, would have her hand up so fast it audibly cut the air.

"He knows the answer," she'd announce, betraying him instantly, loyally, uselessly. "He told me at breakfast. Ask him again."

"*Don't*," Beatrix whispered, mortified.

"You *do* know it."

"That's not the point."

"What is the point, then?"

"The point," Beatrix hissed, "is that knowing things is private."

Beatrice stared at him as though he'd said breathing was private. But she stopped volunteering him, mostly, and later — much later, years later, the night before she left for Hollowmere the first time — she'd bring it up again, turning it over like the jigsaw piece that never fit anywhere.

"You know what I think?" she'd said. "I think you decided invisible was safer. And maybe it was. Maybe it is. But Bee—" She alone called him Bee; it was the one asymmetry between them, hers to give. "Bee, invisible isn't nothing. Invisible is a *skill*, and skills are for something. You've been practicing your whole life for something, and neither of us knows what."

He'd laughed and thrown a pillow at her and not thought about it again for four months, which was exactly as long as she had left.

* * *

But memory, in Hollowmere, does not stay where you put it. Sitting in the inn with the tea-colored light dying, Beatrix found the recollections coming faster now, and darker at the edges, like photographs developing in the wrong order.

He remembered the summer they were eleven, the summer of the well game.

There had been a dry well behind the chapel even then — every village has one dead thing it keeps without admitting it — and the local children dared each other to lean over it and shout their names down, so the echo would say them back. *A game*, Beatrix thought now, sitting very still. *We played it as a game.*

Beatrice went first, naturally. She leaned over the lip, hair swinging into the dark, and shouted "BEATRICE MARCHETTI" with such force that a rook fell off the chapel roof, and the well gave her name back to her in a voice slightly deeper than her own, and all the children cheered.

Then someone — Danny Cobb, probably, Danny Cobb whose entire personality was other people's dares — said, "Now you, Twin."

And Beatrix, who had been practicing invisibility for five years by then, who had a whole interior architecture of not-being-perceived, felt something cold move through him that he had no vocabulary for. Not fear of the well. Fear of the *echo*. Because an echo was attention, distilled. An echo was the world saying *I see you, I hear you, I have your name,* and everything in him, every careful brick of the hidden house, said *no.*

"No thanks," he'd said lightly. "I'll hold your coat."

"You're not wearing a coat," said Beatrice.

"I'll hold Danny's coat."

Danny Cobb did not own a coat either. It was August. Nobody pursued the point, because pursuing points required noticing Beatrix, and he had already folded himself back into the scenery. Beatrice had looked at him for a long moment — she was the only person alive who could find him when he hid, the only one who knew the trick was that he didn't go anywhere, he just stopped being looked *at* — and then she'd shrugged and shouted her name down the well a second time, louder, for both of them.

"For me *and* him!" she'd called. "Two for the price of one!"

The well had said her name back twice.

Beatrix set down his cup now, forty years later, and noticed that his hands were not entirely steady.

*Two for the price of one.* Children's words. A joke, tossed over her shoulder, sunshine in her voice. And yet the ledger-keeper in him — the archivist of small wrongs that Hollowmere seemed determined to staff — wrote it down anyway, in the column marked *things my sister said that the dark may have taken literally.*

* * *

There was one more memory, and it came last, the way the worst guests come last, apologetic and inevitable.

He was fourteen. Beatrice was fourteen. Their mother had sent them to the shop together, which was itself unusual — normally Beatrix did the errands precisely because errands involved shopkeepers, and shopkeepers talked, and talking required being a person with a face. But it was raining, and Beatrice wanted company, and Beatrice wanting company was a force of nature against which there was no insurance.

In the shop, Mr. Harrow the grocer was talking to another customer about Hollowmere.

They'd moved away from Hollowmere when the twins were four — Rosa Marchetti packing them onto a bus with a suitcase each and a look on her face that Beatrix had filed, at the time, under *tired*, and only much later re-filed under *fleeing*. So Hollowmere was just a word to them, a place in the family's closed drawers. But the grocer's customer was from near there, or had been, and was saying, in the easy way people say terrible things about places they've left:

"—and of course the bell's been gone since before the war. Melted down, 1911, the lot of it. There's nothing up in that tower but sky and pigeons. But you ask anyone from Hollowmere and they'll tell you it still rings at dusk. Swear on their mothers, they will. My aunt lived there sixty years. Never heard it herself, mind. Said the ones who hear it are the ones the bell wants."

"And what does it want them *for*?" Beatrice asked, from the biscuit aisle, bright-eyed, delighted, leaning into the conversation like a plant toward a window.

The customer laughed. "Nothing good, love. Nothing good."

Beatrix had stood by the door with the shopping list going soft in his damp grip, and felt the cold thing move through him again, the same cold thing from the well, older and more certain now. He hadn't known why. He'd told himself it was the rain.

But he remembered — with the terrible fidelity that skeptics are cursed with, remembering everything because they refuse the mercy of forgetting — that Beatrice had hummed, all the way home. Some tune neither of them knew. Something with a rise and fall to it, like water finding stairs. She hummed it for three days. On the fourth day she'd stopped, mid-hum, in the middle of the kitchen, and frowned, and said, "Huh. Lost it," and never hummed it again.

And Beatrix, standing beside her with the teacups, had felt — for one unaccountable second — that he knew how the tune ended. Knew it the way you know a stair that isn't there in the dark: as a falling, as a missing step, as a place where the song should have gone and didn't.

He had said nothing. Of course he had said nothing. Saying nothing was the whole of his art.

* * *

The lamp in the inn's corner guttered, and Beatrix realized the light had died entirely, and that somewhere outside, the towerless tower was gathering its breath.

He pressed his palms against his eyes until the darkness went orange, and let himself say the thing he'd been circling for days, the way you circle a wound before touching it:

She burned bright. She burned bright her whole life, and I stood behind her in the shade of it, warm and safe and unremarkable, and I told myself it was temperament. Told myself we'd each chosen our natures, hers the candle, mine the room. But rooms don't choose. Rooms are *built*. Somebody builds them, brick by brick, out of every dare declined and every answer swallowed and every name refused at the lip of a well.

And if the bell wants a Marchetti —

If the bell has always wanted a Marchetti —

— then for forty years I was the twin who couldn't be found, and she was the one standing in the open, lit up like a signal fire, saying *two for the price of one* to anything with ears beneath the earth.

His hands came down. His eyes adjusted. In the black window, faintly, the inn's lamplight showed him his own reflection — his own face, the face that had greeted him from inside his sister's coffin, aged twenty years and waiting.

Behind him, on the stairs, a floorboard creaked.

Coram stood there with a candle he didn't seem to need, watching Beatrix with an expression that was not quite apology and not quite hunger, and said, quietly, "You're thinking about her. Good. Then maybe you're ready to talk business."
