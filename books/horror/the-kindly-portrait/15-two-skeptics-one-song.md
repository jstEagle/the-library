## 15. Two Skeptics, One Song

Beatrix found her at the parish records office on a Tuesday morning, because of course she was there. He had learned by now that the only people in Hollowmere who moved with purpose were the ones who didn't belong to it.

She was a tall woman in a coat too good for the county, standing at the window ledge with the 1911 vestry minutes spread out before her like a patient she suspected of lying. Her hair was pinned up in a way that suggested it had lost an argument that morning and she intended to win the war.

"You're reading about the bell," Beatrix said, by way of introduction.

"I'm Dr. Imogen Vale." She didn't look up. "Folklorist. And I'm reading about a bell that was melted down for scrap metal one hundred and thirteen years ago, according to this very official document with a very official wax seal, and yet—" she tapped the page twice, "—every account of Hollowmere since mentions its ringing. Dusk, mostly. Sometimes dawn if there's been a death. There's been rather a lot of deaths."

"Have you heard it?"

That made her look up. She had gray eyes, the flat honest gray of weather coming in off the sea, and they went over his face with the thoroughness of someone cataloguing specimens. "No," she said. "I've stood in the square at dusk three evenings running like an absolute lunatic, notebook in hand, and heard nothing but rooks and Mrs. Tallow's dog. The villagers look at me as though I'm deaf."

"They look at me as though I'm mad," Beatrix said. "So we're each half of something."

"And you are?"

"Beatrix Marchetti. I came to bury my sister." He let that sit. "The coffin held a stranger wearing my face."

Most people flinched at that. Most people did the small social arithmetic of how much he'd had to drink, or grieve, or both. Vale did neither. She closed the vestry book, tucked it under her arm like a clutch purse, and said:

"Well. You'll want tea for that. And I'll want a witness who isn't from here. Come along."

* * *

**Vale**

He talked for two hours, and Imogen Vale took eleven pages of notes, which for her was practically a love letter.

She had spent twenty years collecting stories that didn't hold their shape under lamplight—the singing stones of Cornwall, the vanishing fair at Wexley Cross—and she had developed a professional immune system against the shiver a good tale gave her. A story was data. A story had variants, sources, transmission vectors. A story could be weighed.

Marchetti's story weighed far too much.

It wasn't the corpse-in-the-coffin part; grief invented stranger things, and twins especially were prone to a doubled imagination. It was the paperwork. He produced it the way other men produced alibis: the guestbook signature, the undertaker's ledger, the letters in his sister's hand describing a village visit four months before her death. He produced the fact—flatly, without drama—that the church tower had been open to the sky since 1968, when the spire came down in a storm, and that no bell hung in it, and that nevertheless at dusk the air over the village carried a sound he could only describe as *a note trying to remember itself*.

"You hear it," she said. "They hear it. I don't."

"No."

"Which means either we're both suffering a shared folie à deux with an entire village—which would be a paper of its own—or the sound is selective." She wrote *selective?* and underlined it until the pen nearly went through. "Sound doesn't discriminate, Mr. Marchetti. Sound is physics. Physics is indifferent."

"So is this village," he said. "Indifferent isn't the word I'd use, though. It's more like—" he searched, and she watched him search, and found herself leaning forward to catch whatever he brought up, "—attentive. Like something taking notes on us."

Vale put down her pen. That was the trouble with fieldwork; sooner or later the folklore started taking notes back.

"The melting," she said briskly, because briskness was how she kept the shiver outside the tent. "1911. The vestry sold the bell to a foundry in Ashcombe to pay for roof repairs. There are receipts. There is a foundry stamp. I intend to verify all of it tomorrow, and then I intend to stand in your haunted tower at dusk with a decibel meter, and then we will both feel much better."

"Do you believe me?" he asked. It was not a needy question. It was asked the way a man asks whether a bridge will hold—wanting the answer, braced for it.

"I believe you're not lying," she said. "Lying takes energy, and you look like a man running on fumes and spite. As for belief—" she capped her pen, "—I don't believe things, Mr. Marchetti. I verify them. It's colder work but the tea's the same."

Something in his shoulders came down half an inch. She pretended not to notice, and noted it anyway.

* * *

**Beatrix**

The tower smelled of pigeon and old rain. Beatrix climbed the spiral stair behind Dr. Vale, who took steps like a woman billing someone for every one of them, and emerged onto a platform of rotted beams under open sky.

She hadn't exaggerated. There was no roof. There was no bell. There was a square hole where the bell had once hung from its headstock, and below it, wedged into the joists and pooled across the floor like something spilled, a mass of dark metal.

"That's odd," Vale said, in the tone of a doctor saying *hmm* over an X-ray.

"What is it?"

"Slag." She crouched, gloved fingers hovering without touching. "Foundry waste. When they melt a bell down, you get pourings, dross, the bits that never went into the ingots. But this—" She worked a loose piece free and turned it in the light. It was dull iron-gray, misshapen, utterly formless, and yet.

And yet.

Beatrix looked at it and felt the floor tilt gently, the way it had the first time the undertaker had smiled at him and called him by name. Because the slag was formless, and the slag was nothing, and the slag was also unmistakably the shadow of a bell. Not shaped like one—shaped like the *space* one left behind. As though the metal had melted and run, and what remained was the memory of the mold, cast in absence.

"It's like a footprint," he said.

"It's like a negative," Vale corrected, and her voice had gone carefully light, the voice of a woman holding a door shut against a draft. "A casting of the space where the bell used to be. Which is nonsense, because slag doesn't do that. Slag has no manners whatsoever. Slag does not *pose*."

She set the piece down, wiped her hands on her handkerchief, and consulted her watch. "Twenty minutes to dusk. We'll wait. We'll be scientific. And when nothing happens, Mr. Marchetti, you're going to help me find a room that serves food past nine, because I have had enough of ham."

So they waited, two skeptics on a rotten beam under a cold sky, and Beatrix found—to his mild astonishment—that waiting beside her was the first hour in Hollowmere that hadn't felt like drowning. She narrated her own skepticism the way other people hummed: the decibel meter came out, the notebook came out, she read him a passage from a 1902 folklorist named Cray who had visited Hollowmere and written, in full, *"The bell of Hollowmere is unremarkable and the villagers tiresome."*

"He must have been fun at parties," Beatrix said.

"He was my great-great-uncle, actually, which is why I'm here, so kindly restrain yourself." But she was almost smiling when she said it, and Beatrix realized he was almost smiling too, and that his face had forgotten how, and that it remembered now, painfully, like a stiff joint.

Then the dusk came down, and the note began.

He heard it first as always—a thin thread drawn through the air, a sound trying to recall its own purpose. He watched Vale instead of listening. He watched her go still. He watched her raise the decibel meter with steady hands and stare at the number, which was zero, and stare at the sky, which was empty, and stare at the slag, which—

The slag was ringing.

Not moving. Not glowing. Nothing so generous. But the pool of dark metal had begun, very faintly, to sing along—the same thread of sound, resonating in dead foundry waste the way a wineglass answers a voice it was poured for.

"Tell me you hear that," Beatrix said.

Vale didn't answer. She was looking at the slag with an expression he recognized from mirrors: the exact face of a rational person watching their rationality get quietly, politely strangled. Then she opened her mouth, presumably to say something scientific, and what came out instead was a hum.

Three notes. Low, even, unhurried. The first phrase of the bell-song—he knew it now the way you know a word you've read too many times—and they were coming out of Dr. Imogen Vale, folklorist, great-great-niece of the man who found the villagers tiresome, in a voice soft and contented as a child's.

She stopped. Blinked. Looked at him with genuine puzzlement.

"Why are you looking at me like that?"

"You were humming."

"I don't hum." She said it with real offense. "I am many irritating things, Mr. Marchetti, but I do not *hum*."

"You just did. Three notes. The opening of the song."

The color left her face in stages, like a tide going out. She looked at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger who'd borrowed them. Then, being who she was, she picked up her pen and wrote, aloud as she wrote it: *"Subject—myself—unconscious vocalization of fragment, duration approximately two seconds, trigger unknown, distress level—"*

Her pen stopped.

"Distress level," Beatrix prompted gently.

"Moderate," she said, and wrote it, and her hand was perfectly steady, and Beatrix thought: *there it is.* The thing he'd been alone with since the coffin. The crack in the skeptic, widening by degrees, and the terrible discipline of pretending it wasn't.

"We should get down," he said.

"In a moment." She was staring at the slag again. "Mr. Marchetti. When you hear the bell at dusk—does it ever sound like it's *learning*?"

He thought of the last week. Of the note growing fuller, warmer, more confident. Of the phone call yesterday when the innkeeper had answered and said, puzzled, *"Bell? Who is this?"*

"Yes," he said.

Vale nodded slowly, closed her notebook, and stood. On the ladder down, in the dark, she hummed again—three notes, caught herself, swore with scholarly precision—and Beatrix followed her down into the village, thinking that he had come here to bury a sister and had somehow acquired instead the one thing more dangerous than an enemy:

An ally who was starting to sing.
