## 24. The Village Votes

The notice was pinned to the inn's door with a brass tack, and it was written in a beautiful hand.

That was the first thing Beatrix noticed—the hand. Not Coram's crabbed scrawl, not the constable's schoolroom capitals, but a flowing copperplate that seemed almost too elegant for the word it spelled out. *MEETING*, it said. *All souls of Hollowmere, Thursday dusk, churchyard. Matter of common welfare. Tea provided.*

Tea provided. As if this were a fete. As if someone were going to stand up and propose new guttering for the tithe barn, and everyone would tut thoughtfully and vote yes, and Mrs. Aldery—no, not Mrs. Aldery, she was gone now, dissolved into the fog like sugar in hot water—as if someone else would bring the lemon slices.

He stood in the cold morning light with his breath steaming and read the notice four times. On the fifth reading he noticed the smaller line beneath, added in different ink, in a hand that trembled slightly:

*Mr. Marchetti is warmly invited.*

*Warmly invited.* He almost laughed. It had the flavor of an invitation to a wedding where you already knew which side of the church you'd be seated on. Or perhaps a funeral. His own, as it happened, though apparently the village felt one funeral per family per century was quite enough, and so they were going to handle the arrangements differently this time.

Vale would have told him not to go. Vale was three days gone into the well, and her notebook was under his arm, and her last instruction rang in him like a struck glass: *names can be given, not just sung.* But Vale wasn't here to tell him not to walk into a room full of people who no longer remembered their own children's names, and so Beatrix did what skeptics do when the evidence points one way and dread points another: he went anyway, because not going meant letting them decide everything in his absence, and he had spent fifty years learning that absence is where the worst things are decided about you.

* * *

The churchyard at dusk was full.

That was wrong, for a start. Hollowmere had perhaps sixty souls left, and half of those were more soul-adjacent than anything, nameless folk who smiled too readily and forgot mid-sentence. But the churchyard held over a hundred people, standing among the leaning stones in their good coats, and Beatrix counted twice before accepting it. Some of them he recognized from photographs in the parish records. Some of them he recognized from the ledger of burials. One elderly gentleman near the front had been interred in 1934 according to the register, and stood now with his hands folded, patient as furniture.

A trestle table had been set up by the lychgate. On it: a samovar, mismatched cups, a plate of shortbread cut into bell shapes. Someone had thought about the shortbread. Someone had stood in a kitchen that morning and pressed a cutter into dough and thought, *he'll want something sweet after,* and that was somehow worse than torches. Torches you could run from. Shortbread you had to sit with.

Coram stood at the table, pouring. His face had the gray, wrung look of a man who hadn't slept since the night the water took him halfway down and then, inexplicably, let him climb back out. When he saw Beatrix he set down the pot and came around the table, wiping his hands on his apron, and there was something in his gait—a hesitancy, a shame—that gave Beatrix a thin thread of hope.

"Mr. Marchetti." Coram's voice was low. "You didn't have to come."

"I know."

"There's still—" The innkeeper glanced back at the crowd, at whatever stood behind the crowd, though the tower was empty sky and had been for decades. "There's still time for you to be elsewhere. I'd drive you myself. I'd carry you if your legs had opinions."

"My legs have excellent opinions," Beatrix said. "They say stay. Somebody has to watch the vote."

Coram flinched as if the word had teeth. "It isn't a vote. Not really. It's—" He stopped, tried again. "You'll see. I'm sorry. That's all I've got left to give you, and it isn't worth much, but it's true: I am sorry."

"You voted for my sister's coffin to hold a stranger," Beatrix said quietly. "Save the apology. Spend it on yourself. You're going to need it."

Something moved through the crowd then—not wind, because the yew branches didn't stir—and every head turned toward the roofless tower in perfect unison, a hundred faces rotating like flowers toward a sun that wasn't there. The sound came a moment later. Not ringing, exactly. The melted bell had no metal left to ring with; what came down from the open sky was more like the memory of ringing, the idea of a note, felt in the breastbone rather than heard by the ear. And threaded through it, unmistakable now, was a timbre Beatrix had been hearing in his own voicemail messages for two weeks.

His own voice. Calling the meeting to order.

* * *

They sat him in the front row.

Of course they did. There were no rows in a churchyard, strictly speaking, but folding chairs had appeared—folding chairs, in a village whose shops had closed before the war—and a space had been left in the very center of the first line, with a cushion. A cushion. Beatrix sat in it because refusing the cushion would have made a scene, and some deep-bred instinct told him that scenes were exactly what the thing in the tower wanted. Grief made noise. Noise was a kind of singing. He kept his hands flat on his knees and his mouth shut and he watched.

A woman he didn't recognize rose to speak. She was perhaps seventy, dressed in brown wool, with the kind of face that had once organized jumble sales and remembered everyone's allergies. She held a sheet of paper, and her voice, when she began, was gentle as bedtime.

"We're gathered to consider the matter of Mr. Beatrix Marchetti," she said, "who came to us in sorrow to bury his dear sister, and whom we received, I'm afraid, with less honesty than hospitality."

Murmurs of agreement. Genuine-sounding. Somewhere in the third row a man said, "We did him a discourtesy," with the sincere regret of a man apologizing for arriving late to dinner.

"The parish has discussed the matter"—the woman glanced up at the tower, deferentially—"with guidance, and we find ourselves in debt to the Marchetti family. A debt of long standing. A hundred years and more. And debts, friends, are not ours to forgive. They are only ours to pay."

Beatrix's hands curled against his knees. *Say something,* urged the part of him that had argued with headmasters and landlords and one memorable customs officer in Trieste. *Stand up. Tell them they're mad.* But he looked at their faces—all those kind, lined, forgetful faces, turned toward him with such tender concern—and understood that argument required a shared premise, and the premise here was a hole in the sky that sang in his voice, and you cannot out-reason a hole.

The woman continued. "It has therefore been proposed—and seconded, and discussed over tea, and prayed upon, insofar as any of us remember how—that the debt be settled at the equinox. Four days hence. By the giving of Mr. Marchetti to the well."

There it was. No euphemism. No velvet phrase like *passed beyond* or *joined the water*. Just the plain arithmetic of it, delivered in the tone of a treasurer reporting the roof fund.

And then—this was the part that would keep him awake for the rest of however long the rest was—they turned to him, all of them, and apologized.

A man near the aisle spoke first. "I'm sorry it has to be you, sir. Truly. If it could be me, I'd go tomorrow." And Beatrix believed him, which was the horror of it. The man meant it the way you mean it when you tell a widow you'd take her grief if you could.

An old woman patted his forearm as she passed to the tea table. "You'll feel no fear, love. They say the water is warm in autumn. My grandmother went in singing and came out of the dream smiling, or so we used to say, before we stopped saying things." She smiled at him, apologetic, absolute, and offered him a cup. "Milk?"

"No milk," Beatrix said. His voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "No thank you. And no equinox."

The murmuring stopped. A hundred faces regarded him with something that was not anger—it was worse. It was patience. The look of adults waiting for a child to finish being upset about bedtime.

"It isn't a choice we're offering," the chairwoman said gently. "It's a choice we're informing you of. There's a difference, and we're sorry for it, but the difference isn't ours to mend. The debt is a hundred years old. The interest alone—" she consulted her paper, though Beatrix would have bet his life the figures were written nowhere but in the wet dark below the chapel—"the interest comes to one believer per generation, and we are overdue, and the village has been so tired, Mr. Marchetti. You've seen how tired. We can't remember our sons' names. The documents go blank. Last week the postman delivered a letter to himself and neither of them knew what to do."

A ripple of rueful laughter. Warm. Human. He might have been at a parish meeting about parking.

"So we ask you kindly," the woman finished, "to come to the well at dusk on the equinox. Wear something comfortable. There will be singing—we'll sing most of it for you, so you needn't trouble yourself with the words. And afterward the village will sleep properly for the first time in a century, and your family's account will be closed, and we will remember you fondly, for as long as remembering lasts."

She sat down. The silence that followed had texture, like felt.

Then a hand went up in the fourth row. An actual hand, raised politely, attached to a youngish man with ink-stained fingers—the schoolteacher, Beatrix realized, the one who still had his name, or most of it. "Point of procedure," the teacher said. "Shouldn't we vote? It was moved and seconded, but we never formally—"

"Oh, of course." The chairwoman looked genuinely chagrined. "How remiss. All in favor?"

Every hand in the churchyard rose. Including, Beatrix noticed with a distant, floating clarity, the hands of the dead. The gentleman buried in 1934 raised both. The motion carried without a single dissenting voice, without a single abstention, with a unanimity so complete it stopped resembling democracy and became something older—weather, tide, the agreement of rain to fall downward.

"Carried," said the chairwoman, with satisfaction. "Thank you all for coming. Shortbread on the way out, and mind the step by the Aldery plot, it's sunk again."

* * *

They filed past him afterward, each one pausing, each one kind.

"Sorry about all this, Mr. Marchetti."

"Wear the brown jumper, it suits you."

"My wife knitted you something for after. Well. For during. You'll see."

Coram came last, carrying two cups of tea he had clearly poured as a pretext. He sat down heavily in the chair beside Beatrix, in the emptying churchyard, under the empty tower, while the shortbread plate emptied and the folding chairs folded themselves away—or were folded, by hands that had done it a thousand times at a thousand meetings, and that was the thing, Beatrix thought, that was the unbearable thing: none of this was strange to them. This was simply how Hollowmere settled its accounts. It had always been how. The kindness wasn't a mask over the cruelty. The kindness was load-bearing.

"They mean it," Coram said. "That's what I couldn't make you understand before. There's no villain in this, Marchetti. There's just a bill, and a hundred years of people paying it politely, and now it's yours." He stared into his tea. "I voted yes. I want you to know that I voted yes, and that I hate myself for it, and that I'll vote yes again in four days, because the alternative is my neighbors forgetting their own children until there's nothing left of anyone at all. One of you, or all of us. That's the menu. It's always been the menu."

"And if I don't come to the well?" Beatrix asked. "If I simply leave? Walk out tonight and never look back?"

Coram was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, his voice had dropped to nearly nothing, and he did not look up from the cup.

"Then the bell sings the rest of the song itself," he said. "Unfinished. Unconsented. And what it calls up then won't be warm water and a pleasant dream, Marchetti. What it calls up then will come out of the well instead of going in, and it will not stop at the village boundary, and it will not be polite." He finally met Beatrix's eyes, and there was something in them Beatrix hadn't seen before—not fear for himself. Fear on Beatrix's behalf. "Your sister learned that. It's why she wrote what she wrote. It's why she sent *you*, specifically, the one Marchetti alive who'd never believe—because belief opens the door, but refusal, proper refusal, from the right bloodline, from the name itself—" He stopped. Pressed his lips together. "I've said too much. They hear everything said near the tower."

"Coram." Beatrix leaned close. "My sister's letters. The ones she never sent. You've read them—you people read everything here. Was there anything else? Anything hidden in them?"

The innkeeper's teacup rattled once against its saucer, and Beatrix saw it: the flicker. The guilty glance toward the lychgate, toward the road, toward the small tin box Coram had carried for three days like a man with a stone in his boot.

"Equinox eve," Coram said, standing abruptly, spilling his tea. "Come to the inn at midnight, before they come for you with lanterns. Come alone, and come *believing nothing*, and I'll give you what I should have given you the day you arrived." He was already walking away, fast, a traitor in both directions and aware of it. Over his shoulder, barely audible:

"She hid it where only a twin would think to look. In the part of her you never signed your name to."

Beatrix sat alone in the churchyard as full dark came down, the folding chairs stacked, the samovar cold, the shortbread crumbs already scattered for the birds. Above him the open sky where a bell should be hummed one low, contented note—in his voice, patient as a creditor—and four days away, at the bottom of a dry field behind the chapel, something black and warm waited to be paid.

He opened Vale's notebook to the last page she'd written, and read her final line again, and began, for the first time since childhood, to pray to nobody in particular that his sister had been cleverer than the whole of Hollowmere put together.

He had until midnight to find out what Beatrice had hidden in the unsent letters—and until the equinox to decide whether Coram's midnight gift was mercy, or bait.
