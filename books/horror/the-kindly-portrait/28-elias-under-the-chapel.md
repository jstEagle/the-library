## 28. Elias Under the Chapel

The chapel floor was not, as it turned out, a floor.

Beatrix had spent two days with Elias Marchetti's diary open on his knees at the inn, reading by lamplight because Coram's generator had died and nobody in Hollowmere seemed to remember how to fix it—or perhaps nobody remembered that light could be fixed, which amounted to the same thing in this village. The diary's last legible entry, dated the third of October, 1911, said only:

*They have put me where the singing cannot reach me. I have asked them to brick me in and they have agreed, because asking kindly is all they know how to do now. If you are reading this, you already know where. Everyone always knows where. We simply agree not to.*

Everyone always knows where.

Beatrix sat in the chapel at noon, when the bell was quiet and the roofless sky poured down honest gray daylight, and looked at the floor the way you look at an old dog that has been lying to you for years. The flagstones were uneven—of course they were, everything in Hollowmere was uneven—but one section near the eastern wall, behind the rotted remains of the choir stalls, was *too* even. Too regular. A rectangle of stones laid with a care that had nothing to do with worship and everything to do with keeping something shut.

He counted the stones. Nine. He pressed his palm against the center one and felt, or imagined he felt, a coolness coming through that had no business being there in October.

"Right," he said aloud, to the empty chapel, because talking to himself was the only conversation left in Hollowmere that didn't frighten him. "You've been sitting on a man for a hundred years. The least I can do is knock."

The pry bar came from Coram's cellar, along with a lantern, a coil of rope, and a bottle of brandy that Beatrix took without permission on the grounds that Coram owed him considerably more than a bottle of brandy. The mortar between the nine stones had been mixed with something dark—ash, maybe, or worse—and it crumbled away from the pry bar like old bread. Whoever had bricked the room in had done it quickly and had done it sorry. You could feel the apology in the masonry.

The first stone came free with a sound like a held breath released.

Behind it: darkness, and air that had waited since 1911 to be breathed again. It smelled of dust and stone and, faintly, impossibly, of lamp oil—as if someone had burned a light down there within living memory, though Beatrix knew better than to trust his own nose anymore. His nose had told him the well water smelled of nothing at all.

He widened the gap stone by stone, working from the edges inward, and by late afternoon he had an opening wide enough to lower himself through. He tied the rope to the choir stall, tested it twice, and hung the lantern over the edge first, watching its light fall down into a small square room perhaps eight feet to a side.

The light found the walls first. They were covered in scratches—not frantic, not desperate, but patient. Lines of text cut into the plaster with something sharp, repeated over and over, floor to ceiling, in a hand that started strong and grew weaker and then, strangely, grew strong again, as if the writer had rested and returned and rested and returned across years that should not have been possible.

And in the corner, arranged with terrible neatness, were the bones.

* * *

He climbed down slowly, telling himself the whole way that he was a grown man, a skeptic, a person who had once watched a dentist extract a wisdom tooth without flinching, and that none of these facts were helping even slightly.

The room was exactly what the diary promised: a cell, bricked from outside, with a niche carved into the wall that might once have held a candle and a shallow channel cut in the floor—a drain, or a means of passing bread, or both. Someone had fed him. Someone had watered him. The village had kept Elias Marchetti alive down here the way you keep a dangerous animal alive: kindly, dutifully, and with the door forever shut.

The skeleton sat against the far wall, knees drawn up, in the posture of a man resting after a long walk. The hands—the small, fine bones of the hands—were folded in the lap. And between them, held where a book would rest, was a page.

Not the hymnal leaf. A copy. Written out in ink gone brown as tea, on paper so old it had begun to turn the color of the skin that had once touched it. Four verses. Beatrix knew the first three by heart now, God help him; Vale's notebook had made sure of that before she—

Before.

He crouched beside the bones, close enough to read, and made himself look at the fourth verse.

It was there. All of it, except the line that mattered. Where the crucial words should have been, the paper was worn through—not torn, not stained, but *worn*, rubbed to translucence and then to nothing by the passage of a thumb. Back and forth. Over and over. Ten thousand times. Twenty thousand. The ghost of the letters remained, a shadow of ink pressed into the fibers below the surface, but the surface itself had been loved away, the way a coin's face goes smooth in a pocket, the way a stair goes hollow in an old house.

Elias Marchetti had known the verse. Had held it every day of whatever unimaginable span they'd kept him. And he had read it, and read it, and read it—and he had never, not once, spoken it aloud.

"How long did they keep you?" Beatrix asked the bones quietly.

The bones declined to answer, which he appreciated. Everything else in Hollowmere answered too readily.

But the walls answered instead. He raised the lantern and read the scratched lines properly for the first time, and his stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. They weren't random. They were tallies. Dates, scratched in rows, month by month, year by year. 1911. 1912. 1913. The hand deteriorated around 1915—shorter entries, cruder strokes—and then, impossibly, resumed. 1921. 1924. 1930. The dates climbed the wall like ivy, thinning, faltering, but continuing, continuing, until the last scratchable year:

1959.

Fifty years. They had kept him fifty years, in the dark, with bread through a slot and a page in his hands, and the only mercy anyone had shown him was the mercy of never asking him to sing.

Beatrix sat down on the cold floor across from the skeleton, because his legs had decided the matter without consulting him.

"I'm sorry," he said. It seemed insufficient. It was insufficient. But the whole of Hollowmere ran on insufficient things done with great sincerity, and he refused to do even that. "I'm sorry they did this to you. And I'm sorry—" he laughed, a short ugly sound, "—I'm sorry I'm the reason you're not the worst thing that ever happened to a Marchetti in this village."

The lantern flame leaned, though there was no draft.

* * *

He worked by lamplight into the evening, because leaving felt impossible and staying felt like trespass and he split the difference by making himself useful. There was a tin cup near the niche, corroded green. A wooden comb. A child's marble, blue glass, worn cloudy—which broke something open in Beatrix's chest that he hadn't budgeted for, because what does a man take with him into a voluntary tomb except one small beautiful thing?

And there was the diary's missing companion: a second book, smaller, bound in oilcloth, tucked behind the bones where the folded hands had guarded it. Elias had kept it behind himself, literally, even in death. Even dead, he'd positioned his remains so that his body shielded it.

Beatrix unwrapped the oilcloth with the care of a man defusing something.

Inside was a notebook. Not a diary—the diary was upstairs at the inn—but a working document, dense with Elias's careful hand: observations, experiments, arguments with himself. The handwriting of a man who had decided that if he was going to be buried alive, he was at least going to *understand* it.

*I have tested it,* one entry read. *I spoke the first verse aloud on the ninth day. The bell above answered. Not rang—answered, the way a dog answers a whistle. I did not speak the second. I will not speak the second. The song is a ladder and each verse is a rung and I am beginning to suspect the ladder does not lead up.*

Another entry, later, the hand tighter:

*They bring bread and they smile and their smiles are wrong, stretched, like men smiling underwater. Mrs. Petch says my sacrifice keeps the orchards heavy. I have told her I am not sacrificing anything, I am refusing, and she pats my hand through the slot as if I were a child who has said something sweet and foolish. Refusal is not a word they have anymore. I think the village gave its refusal to the well a long time ago and has been borrowing back pieces of it ever since.*

And then, on a page set apart, boxed in double lines as if to contain it:

*THE RULE, which I have learned at cost and write here so that cost buys something:*

*The final verse is not a song. It is a signature. To sing it is to sign. The bell does not want your death—it has plenty of deaths, it wades in them. It wants a VOICE. It wants someone willing to say the name forever, and the name is the lock and the voice is the key and the well is the door and I will die in this room before I am any part of that mechanism.*

*If you find this: do not learn the verse whole. A half-known verse cannot be sung by accident. Keep yourself ignorant. Ignorance is armor here. It is the only armor the village has ever issued, and they issue it to everyone but themselves.*

Beatrix read that last line four times.

*A half-known verse cannot be sung by accident.*

He thought of Vale, waking with the third verse already in her mouth. He thought of Mrs. Aldery dying mid-hum. He thought of the congregation singing verses one through three with a silence shaped exactly like a fourth, a silence with his name waiting inside it like a bullet in a chamber.

And he thought of himself, kneeling here, holding the complete verse in his hands—the worn place and all—for the first time in a century, the only living soul who had ever seen it whole besides Elias and whoever had torn the hymnal.

He was holding the ladder. All four rungs.

"Ten thousand times," he murmured, looking at the thumb-worn hollow in the page. "You read it ten thousand times and never slipped." He shook his head slowly. "I've known you for twenty minutes, Elias, and you're already the best man in this family."

The lantern flame stood very still, listening.

* * *

He copied the verse.

That was the argument he had with himself for an hour, pacing the little cell like a man pacing a dock deciding whether to board a ship, and copying it won—not because he wanted to know it, but because ignorance had failed everyone else. Aldery had been ignorant. Vale had been ignorant. Ignorance in Hollowmere just meant the song arrived unannounced, in your sleep, in your mouth, wearing your voice. Elias's armor hadn't been ignorance in the end; it had been *understanding*. He had known the verse perfectly and understood it perfectly, and the understanding was what held.

Beatrix sat cross-legged opposite the bones, set Vale's notebook on his knee, and transcribed the fourth verse in pencil, line by line, including the worn-away line—or rather, including the ghost of it. Because here was the thing he discovered as he wrote: the smudged line wasn't entirely gone. Beneath the wear, pressed deep into the paper's grain by that same ten-thousand-fold thumb, the letters survived as indentations. Run your pencil sideways across the page, the way he'd once revealed a childhood note passed under a door, and the shadows of the words rose up.

He did it. He couldn't not do it. That was the truth of him and he'd stopped pretending otherwise somewhere around the drowned constable.

The graphite whispered across the hollowed line, and the hidden letters surfaced one by one, faint as fish under ice.

The first word was *the*. The second was *name*. The third was *is*.

And then the fourth word—the word the whole verse existed to carry, the word the bell had been ringing toward since 1911, the word the village had buried a stranger to avoid, the word his sister had died rather than become—

The indentation there was different. Not worn away. *Smudged deliberately*, smeared sideways by a thumb pressed hard and dragged, once, with intent. Elias hadn't worn that word out by reading it. He had destroyed it on purpose, at the end, so that no one who came after—even a finder as foolish and thorough as Beatrix—could read it whole.

Even in death, the man was still standing guard.

Beatrix laughed. It came out wet. "You absolute stubborn old devil."

The bones sat in their patient fold, guarding nothing now, guarding everything still.

He looked at the partial line in his notebook. *The name is—* and then blankness. He knew, with a certainty that lived in his teeth rather than his mind, what the word was. He had known since the coffin, since the face that wore his own aged twenty years, since the guestbook signature fading letter by letter. The bell had been trying to tell him for weeks, patiently, dusk after dusk, in his own stolen timbre.

Knowing it in his teeth was not the same as seeing it on the page. That distinction, he suspected, was about to matter more than anything else in the world.

Above him, through nine stones and a century of silence, the chapel floor creaked.

Not settling. Not wind. A footstep. Then another, slow and deliberate, crossing the nave toward the eastern wall—toward the gap in the floor where the lantern light must be rising up like a wound in the boards.

Beatrix closed the notebook and reached for the pry bar, and in the quiet of the little room, clear as a struck glass, the bell began to ring.

It was dusk. Of course it was dusk.

And the bell was humming the fourth verse—hmming it, wordless, patient, the way you hum to a child you're waiting to name.
