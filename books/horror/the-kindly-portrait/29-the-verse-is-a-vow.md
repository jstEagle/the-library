## 29. The Verse Is a Vow

Beatrix Marchetti had always believed that the worst thing a person could do with their last night of freedom was spend it reading. Now he knew better. The worst thing was spending it reading *Elias Marchetti's diary*, by candlelight, in a dead man's cottage, while somewhere across the village a bell rang in a voice that was becoming uncomfortably close to his own.

The diary itself was unremarkable — a schoolboy's exercise book, its cover gone soft and grey as old bread, the spine held together with a strip of leather belt. Elias had been, by the evidence of his handwriting, either a poet or a doctor or a man writing on a train during an earthquake. Beatrix had spent two hours simply learning to read him.

*Tuesday.* Elias wrote everything under headings like *Tuesday*, regardless of whether it was Tuesday, and several entries were headed *Tuesday* twice in a row.

*Tuesday. Mother says I must not go up the tower. Mother says the tower is not there anymore. I have seen the tower. It is there.*

Beatrix turned the page carefully. The paper had gone the colour of weak tea, and in places Elias's pencil had pressed through three sheets at once, so that sentences from different weeks lay ghosted over one another like voices heard through a wall.

He'd found the cottage at noon, after a night of not sleeping. It stood at the end of the lane past the chapel, half-swallowed by ivy, and the door had opened at his touch with the particular willingness of doors that have been waiting. Inside: dust, a chair, a table, a cold hearth, and on the table, squared neatly to its edge as though its owner had only stepped out to fetch water and would be back directly, the diary.

Fifty years, Coram had said. Kept kindly underground. And then the village had stopped speaking of Elias Marchetti altogether, the way you stop speaking of a thing you are ashamed of, and eventually the way you stop speaking of a thing you have forgotten, which is the same motion performed more slowly.

*Tuesday. I learned the whole song today. Mr. Petch taught me the last verse and made me swear on my mother's grave never to sing it aloud. He wept while he swore me. That frightened me more than the verse did.*

Beatrix sat back. The candle guttered, righted itself.

There it was. Not the verse — he wasn't sure he wanted the verse, not yet, not whole — but the shape around it. A man who taught a boy a terrible thing and wept doing it. A boy who kept the secret for — how long? The entries went on for pages, years compressed into Tuesdays, and the song ran underneath all of them like groundwater.

*Tuesday. Heard it again at dusk. Only the first verses now. The tower is open sky but something rings. I have decided to write down what I know in case I am not permitted to remember it later. If you are reading this, you are probably a Marchetti, because who else would come?*

"Charming," Beatrix said aloud, to the empty room, because the empty room was better company than his own thoughts just then. "You're a regular welcoming committee."

But his hands weren't steady, and the joke came out thinner than he'd intended.

He read on. Outside, the light was going the colour of pewter, and he knew without checking his watch that dusk was coming, and that the bell would ring, and that it would ring in his voice, and that he would hear it through these walls and hate every note. He lit a second candle from the first. Two flames threw two sets of shadows, and he tried not to notice that one of them moved a half-second behind him.

*Tuesday. I have worked it out. I think I have worked it out. God forgive me if I am wrong, and forgive me harder if I am right.*

Then a long passage, written smaller, faster, the pencil digging so deep it tore the paper in one place. Beatrix bent close and read it twice, three times, his lips moving though he hadn't given them permission.

---

The understanding arrived the way floodwater arrives — not all at once but everywhere at once, through every gap at the same time, so that by the time you noticed your feet were wet the room was already drowning.

Elias had understood the mechanics of Hollowmere the way a locksmith understands locks: not by studying the door but by studying the keyhole. And the keyhole, in this case, was the song.

Everyone in the village assumed the well took people. That was the story, wasn't it? The bell sang, the song hollowed you out verse by verse, and when you were empty enough you walked into the water. A machine for making the drowned. Cruel, but legible. A wolf is cruel, but a wolf is at least honest about being a wolf.

Except the well had never taken anyone who didn't walk. And no one walked until they had heard — or sung — the final verse. Mrs. Aldery had died mid-hum, nameless but still in her bed. Coram's congregation sang verses one through three every dusk and remained, if diminished, upright. The villagers who lost their names lost their names; they did not lose their feet.

Only the ones who had gone into the water — the ones across a century, the ones in the ledger, the ones whose faces sometimes surfaced in the black reflection — only those had known the fourth verse.

And here was the line that made Beatrix set the candle down because his hand had begun, very slightly, to shake:

*The last verse is not a summons. It is a signature. Whoever sings it aloud consents. That is the whole trick of the place. The well cannot take what has not offered itself. It has waited a hundred years for someone to say yes in the only language it can hear.*

He read it again.

*It wants a voice, not a body. The bell was melted in 1911 but a bell is only metal shaped around a silence, and the silence is still down there, and a silence cannot sing. It needs a throat. It needs someone to stand at the wellhead and give the verse air, and the moment they do, they stop being theirs and start being its. Forever. The drowned don't die, Elias wrote. They are employed.*

Beatrix laughed. It came out wrong — a single dry syllable with nothing behind it, the laugh of a man reading his own obituary and objecting mainly to the font.

"Employed," he said. "Well. At least it's honest work."

But the humor was a reflex, an old skill, one of the many small tools he'd forged in childhood for surviving rooms he shouldn't have been in, and it did what it always did: bought him four seconds of steadiness. He used them.

Because the trap's true shape was assembling itself in his mind now, and it was worse than death, and it was elegant, and he hated elegance.

The well didn't want to kill him. Killing him would be wasteful. A corpse sings nothing. What the well wanted — what it had wanted since 1911, since the bell came down and the slag in the tower cooled into the shadow of a bell that no longer existed — was consent. Freely given, aloud, in song, in his own voice. Because a forced yes is worth nothing. The thing below the water could make people forget, could drown a constable in two feet of blackness, could wear his sister's face like a borrowed coat. But it could not *make* anyone sing. Every mechanism it had — the forgetting, the longing, the grief corrupted into invitation — every single one existed for one purpose: to bring a person, willingly, to the edge of a yes.

That was why they'd chosen him. Not despite his skepticism. *Because* of it.

Coram had said it plainly enough, drunk and weeping: they needed a Marchetti who doesn't believe. Beatrix had taken it to mean that unbelief kept the door shut from inside — that a skeptic was safe, a vault. But that was only half of it, the flattering half. The other half was this: a believer would sing too soon. A believer, hearing the bell, feeling the pull, might give the verse air in a moment of rapture, and a rapturous yes was still a yes, but perhaps — perhaps — the thing below wanted something better than that. Wanted a hard yes. A yes fought for. A man dragged kicking toward the water who, at the last, at the very last, opened his mouth and sang — that was a consent worth having. That was a consent that would hold for another century.

They hadn't picked him because he was immune. They'd picked him because he was *difficult*. Because the prize was sweeter if the winner struggled.

"Oh," Beatrix said quietly. "Oh, you absolute bastards."

The candle flames bowed, though there was no draught.

And Beatrice — Beatrice, whose brightness had drawn every eye in every room she ever entered, who had come to Hollowmere months ago full of belief and grief and curiosity, everything he was not — Beatrice had been the wrong instrument. She would have sung too easily. She'd have heard the final verse and felt it as beauty, and beauty was the fastest road to yes there was. So the village had buried a stranger in her coffin and sent for him instead, and let her wait below, or let her be nothing below, and prepared for him a slow courtship of forgetting and longing, designed to end with his mouth opening at the wellhead.

Unless.

His eyes went back to the diary. There was more. There was always more; Elias had been thorough the way only frightened men are thorough.

*Tuesday. I will not sing it. Let it keep me. I have read the verse ten thousand times and I will read it ten thousand more and I will die with my mouth shut, and the place will have to start over. Mother says the tower is not there anymore. I say let it ring at a locked door forever.*

And beneath that, in pencil worn nearly to nothing, pressed so faintly it might have been breath rather than writing:

*If you are a Marchetti and you are reading this: it does not want your death. It wants your agreement. Do not agree. Do not agree to anything. Not kindly, not politely, not to make the singing stop. It is patient. Be more patient.*

Beatrix closed the diary.

For a long time he sat in the dead man's cottage with two candles burning and his own heartbeat loud in his ears, and he thought about patience. Elias had been patient for fifty years underground, reading a smudged verse by no light at all, keeping his mouth shut in the dark while a village above him prospered on the interest of his refusal. And in the end the village had simply — stopped. Forgotten him. Started over. Waited for the next generation, the next Marchetti, the next difficult, unbelieving, stubborn son of a stubborn line.

Patience, it turned out, was not a strategy. Patience was only a longer way of losing.

Because the thing in the well had all the time there was, and Beatrix had three days.

Three days. Vale's gift. Vale, who had hummed herself into the bell's keeping and walked into the black water with her chin up and her notebook left behind on his bed, weighted with a stone, open to a page that said: *the final verse is a name, and names can be given, not just sung.*

He had read that line forty times since. He had thought it meant what it seemed to mean — a loophole, a technicality, some clerical error in the contract that he could exploit the way he'd exploited every bureaucracy he'd ever hidden inside. Give the well a name, any name, a false name, and slip away while it counted its winnings.

But Elias's diary rearranged the furniture. Names could be given, Vale had written. And the verse was a name. And the verse was also a vow — a signature, Elias called it, a consent. Which meant the giving and the singing were the same act. You couldn't hand the well a lie. A false name was no name at all; it would pass through the water like a coin through a ghost. Whatever he gave it had to be true, had to be *his*, had to cost.

So the question was not how to escape the choice. The question was what, exactly, he could afford to lose.

He got up and paced the little room, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, the floorboards complaining under him in a rhythm that was almost, but not quite, a tune. He refused to identify the tune.

The village claimed him. That was the whole architecture of the thing. *Marchetti* — the name in the ledger since 1890, generations before his birth, pre-written, pre-buried, pre-owed. The debt the well had been collecting, patiently, century upon century, was not Beatrix. It had no use for Beatrix, the skeptic, the drifter, the man who had survived thirty-odd years by belonging nowhere. The debt was the *name*. The family. The line. The village had traded on that name for prosperity, had buried strangers under it, had fed it to the well in installments, and the well had accepted the installments because installments meant the principal still existed. As long as a Marchetti lived and answered to the name, the account stayed open.

Close the account, and —

And what? He stopped pacing. The candles stood very straight, as if listening.

Close the account and the well had nothing to collect. No debtor, no debt. The congregation of the drowned — Coram, Vale, Aldery, a century of the nameless — released, maybe, the way a lien dissolves when the property burns. Or maybe not. Maybe closing the account was itself a kind of consent, a transaction, a yes in the only language the thing could hear. Elias hadn't known. Elias had chosen the locked door instead of finding out.

But Elias had also died in the dark believing the verse could only be sung. Vale had died in the water believing it could be given. Two truths, a century apart, and somewhere between them — in the gap between *sung* and *given* — there might be room for a third thing. Something Elias couldn't have imagined and Vale had only glimpsed: that a name surrendered freely might not bind the giver at all. That renunciation was not consent. That telling the well *you may have the name, but you may not have me* was not a yes but a settlement — the account closed, the creditor paid in full, in the only coin that was actually owed.

Or it might be the worst mistake available. The well might take the name and keep the man, since a man without a name is already halfway to the water.

Beatrix looked at the diary. He looked at the candles. Somewhere far off, the first note of dusk began — his own voice, pitched low and bronze, rolling across the fields toward the cottage, patient as interest accruing.

"Not tonight," he told it.

He packed the diary inside his coat, next to Vale's notebook and the letters with their acrostic he still hadn't finished decoding — *Don't save me. Save the name,* Beatrice had written, and he had wept over it and obeyed it halfway and understood it not at all. Save the name. Don't save me. He'd thought she meant: rescue the family legacy, the ledger, the line. Now, standing in Elias's cottage with the trap's full shape finally visible, he wondered if she'd meant something simpler and stranger. Save the name — *from* me. Take it out of circulation. Spend it where it'll do damage.

The second verse of the bell-song drifted through the wall, or through his skull, indistinguishable. *The names dissolve, the names dissolve.* He clamped his jaw against the third, which rose after it unbidden, sweet and awful, and stopped himself at the edge of it the way he now stopped at every edge.

Outside, full dark. Inside, two candles burning down like hourglasses.

Two days left, now. Tomorrow he would need the hymnal page from the foundation stone, the water-stained line, whatever the diary could tell him about what happened to a man who gave the well less than it asked for and more than it expected. Tomorrow he would go back under the chapel, to the bricked room where Elias had kept his mouth shut for fifty years, and he would ask a dead man's permission to try the thing the dead man hadn't dared.

Tonight, he allowed himself one small human thing: he blew out the candles, lay down on Elias's narrow bed in his coat, and slept — badly, but asleep — while across the village the bell rang his name in fragments, getting the pronunciation almost right, and could not, quite, finish the sentence.

In the morning there would be a plan. In the morning there would be a shovel, a crowbar, and a chapel floor.

In the morning, he intended to find out what a name was actually worth — and he intended to sell high.
