## 31. The Refusal at the Wellhead

They came for him at dusk, as he'd known they would, and they came carrying lanterns, which was somehow the worst part.

Not torches. Torches would have meant mob, meant fear, meant something Beatrix could have understood and therefore fought. Lanterns meant care. The brass fixtures were polished; the flames were trimmed; Mrs. Aldery's granddaughter—what was her name, what *was* her name, the girl had no name anymore, none of them did—had tucked a wool blanket over her arm in case he got cold on the walk down.

"Mr. Marchetti," said Coram's nephew, who had inherited the inn along with his uncle's guilt and none of his uncle's capacity for it. "It's a lovely evening for it."

"It's an evening," Beatrix said. "That's all I'll grant you."

The procession moved through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and pond water, past houses whose windows glowed amber, and from every doorway came people who fell into step behind him without being asked. They didn't grab his arms. They didn't bind his hands. They simply surrounded him the way water surrounds a stone, gently, on all sides, with the absolute patience of something that has all the time in the world and knows the stone doesn't.

He counted forty-one of them. He counted because counting was a thing *he* did, a thing that belonged to him, and if he kept doing things that belonged to him then presumably he still existed to do them. Forty-one lanterns. Forty-one faces turned toward him with expressions of such tender apology that he wanted to scream.

"We're sorry about this," said the woman who used to be the schoolteacher. "Truly we are. If there were another way."

"There is another way," Beatrix said. "It's called *not*."

She smiled at him the way you smile at a child who insists the stove isn't hot. "You'll understand soon," she said kindly. "Everyone understands eventually."

"That's the most terrifying sentence anyone has ever said to me, and I once shared a bunkhouse with a man who narrated his dreams."

A ripple went through the congregation—not laughter, exactly, but the place where laughter would have gone if any of them still had the machinery for it. They walked on. The bell tower rose against the bruised sky, roofless, open to the stars like a ribcage, and inside it the melted bell hung silent, waiting. It had been ringing all day, angry, arrhythmic, cracking the air like ice on a pond. Now it held its breath.

The well stood behind the chapel where it had stood for three hundred years, brimming with its black water, and around it the whole village had gathered—or rather, the whole village *was* gathered, because Beatrix realized with a lurch that some of these people had been standing here since morning, motionless, patient as fence posts. Old Mr. Tull, who had died in 1987 according to the parish ledger Beatrix had read with his own eyes, stood at the wellhead holding a lantern, and his face was pleasant, and his face was wrong, and nobody else seemed to find anything remarkable about it at all.

*They don't see it,* Beatrix thought. *Or they see it and it doesn't matter. Or it matters and they've forgotten why.*

The crowd arranged itself in a ring around the well, and the ring opened a gap facing him, an aisle, an invitation. At the far end of the aisle the black water lay perfectly still, and in it he could see the sky, and the lanterns, and—faintly, deep down where reflections shouldn't reach—a shape like a woman standing upright in the dark, waiting for him with her arms open.

His chest did something complicated and traitorous.

"No," Beatrix said aloud, to himself, in his own voice, before anything else could use it. "No. That's not her. You know that's not her. She never stood like that. She stood like she was about to argue with someone."

He walked down the aisle anyway, because refusing to walk would only mean they carried him, and he would arrive at the same place either way, and he intended to leave it under his own power too.

At the wellhead he stopped. The stones were cold through his shoes. Up close the water smelled of iron and old coins and, beneath that, faintly, of the particular green rot of a cellar that has been shut for a very long time.

"Beatrix Marchetti," said Coram's nephew, formally, like a man opening a parish meeting. "The village thanks you. The village apologizes to you. The song wants finishing, and the finishing wants your voice, and we have all agreed—" he gestured at the ring of lanterns, the ring of kind, hollow faces— "that it should be yours. Will you sing?"

"No," said Beatrix.

The word came out steady. He was almost proud of it. Somewhere behind his ribs, the part of him that had spent fifty years perfecting the art of not belonging anywhere stood up and dusted itself off and said: *finally, a job I'm qualified for.*

The nephew blinked. "Will you sing?" he asked again, more slowly, as though Beatrix hadn't heard, or as though the word had come out in the wrong language.

"I heard you the first time. No. N-O. It's a complete sentence, it's one of my favorites, I've been practicing it since I was six years old." He looked around the ring. "I know what the last verse is. I know what it does. I know the bell doesn't want my death—it wants my *consent*, and consent is the one thing I have never given anybody, not once, not ever, and I'm certainly not starting tonight for a hole full of bad water and worse intentions."

The villagers looked at each other. There was a soft shuffling, a consultation without words, and Beatrix felt the temperature of the gathering change—the way a room changes when a plan moves to its second page.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, you've got a backup plan. Of course you've got a backup plan. You're a *congregation*, congregations always have backup plans, it's in the bylaws."

Mrs. Aldery's granddaughter stepped forward, blanket still folded over her arm, and her face was so gentle it made his teeth ache.

"We'll sing it for you," she said.

And they did.

* * *

It began softly, the way all their singing began, a hum first, finding the pitch, and then the words—and the words were verses one, two, three, the ones Beatrix knew now the way he knew his own heartbeat, the water rises, the names dissolve, the bell learns your voice—and then they came to the place where the song had always stopped, the torn place, the silence shaped like a name.

And they filled it.

Forty-one voices, unison, tender, unhurried, sang:

*"Bea-trix Mar-chet-ti."*

Just his name. Sung plainly, sweetly, to the old tune, slotted into the verse like a key into a lock that had been cut for it a century before he was born.

The water in the well shivered.

"No," Beatrix said. "No no no—you can't—that's not how vows work, a vow is *mine*, you can't sing somebody else's vow, that's not—that's forgery, that's identity theft, that's—" he groped for the word— "*fraud*. I want to report a fraud."

They sang it again. *"Bea-trix Mar-chet-ti."* And again, weaving it into the melody, and each time the water climbed a little, a dark tongue rising up the inner stones, and each time the bell above them gave a low groan like a ship changing its mind about sinking.

He understood then, with horrible clarity, what they were doing. The vow needed a voice—but perhaps it didn't need *his* voice, only his *name*, sung whole, sung willing-adjacent, sung into the well while he stood at the wellhead and let it happen. Consent by acclamation. Democracy as a weapon. He thought of chapter twenty-four of his own life, the vote, the raised hands, the kindness of it, and wanted to laugh and vomit in equal measure.

"You need me to *listen*," he said. "That's the trick. The song needs an audience. A vow spoken to nobody binds nobody. So I just—"

He clapped his hands over his ears.

It was not dignified. It was, he suspected, the single least dignified thing he had ever done, and he had once fled a wasp nest through a wedding reception. But dignity was for people who planned to be remembered, and he had spent his whole life learning how to be forgotten, and right now forgettability was armor.

Muffled, the song continued. He could still hear it through his palms—of course he could, flesh was a poor door against forty voices and a bell—but thinner, blunted, the words losing their edges. He squeezed his eyes shut and did the thing he had done in every terrible room of his childhood: he went somewhere else. He built the somewhere else out of load-bearing details. Beatrice at nine, teaching the dog to shake hands with both paws. The smell of their mother's coffee going bitter on the stove. The exact weight of a library book, the specific silence of a train at 3 a.m., the taste of oranges peeled in one long spiral the way she'd taught him—

*"Bea-trix Mar-chet-ti."*

The ground trembled. Not much. The way ground trembles when something very large below turns over in its sleep.

Through his hands, muffled, the chorus swelled, and now other voices were joining it—voices that were not coming from the ring of villagers, voices coming up, wet and resonant, out of the well itself, the drowned congregation adding its harmony, a century of the nameless singing his name back to him in voices he half-recognized: Coram's rumble, Vale's crisp alto, Aldery's papery whisper, and underneath them all a voice like his own but older, worn smooth, Elias Marchetti keeping time across a hundred years.

*They're harmonizing,* Beatrix thought, absurdly, hysterically. *I'm being murdered by a choir and they're harmonizing.*

He pressed harder. His skull ached. His shoulders burned with the effort of holding his own head shut like a man bailing a boat with his bare hands.

And the water rose.

Not up the stones this time. *Out.* It lifted from the wellhead in a single unbroken column, black and glass-smooth, rising past the lantern-light, past the chapel eaves, hanging in the air like a pillar of night that had gotten lost on its way to midnight. The villagers stopped singing. Even the drowned stopped. Every lantern flame bent toward it, and in the surface of the standing water Beatrix could see reflected faces—hundreds of them, pressing close, mouths open, watching him with an attention so vast and so hungry and so *patient* that his knees nearly went.

The column leaned toward him. Slowly. Courteously. Like a waiter presenting a dish.

*All it needs,* he thought, *is for me to look. All it has ever needed is for someone to look, and listen, and say yes.*

So he looked—at the stones instead. At the wellhead's rim, where generations of hands had worn two shallow grooves, the places where the desperate had gripped. At the mortar between the stones, and the initials scratched there by some bored mason in 1890—*E.M.*, small and stubborn, a man insisting he existed. At the folded paper in his own breast pocket, Vale's notebook, heavy as a heart, with her last instruction written in a hand already halfway to someone else's:

*The final verse is a name. And names can be given, not just sung.*

"Not tonight," Beatrix told the column of water, conversationally, because his voice was the only thing in the world that was entirely his and he intended to keep using it until further notice. "I have plans. Poor ones. But mine."

The water hung there a moment longer, considering him, and he had the distinct impression of a vast intelligence consulting its notes and finding, to its irritation, that the paperwork was incomplete.

Then, all together, the villagers drew breath—

And Beatrix, hands still clamped to his ears, saw it: the youngest of them, a boy of maybe twelve who had been standing at the back of the ring, and the boy's mouth was moving out of sync with the sound, ahead of it, and what the boy's mouth was shaping wasn't the chorus at all.

It was four words. Over and over. Silent. Urgent. Aimed straight at Beatrix like a thrown rope.

*Listen to the letters.*

The column of water collapsed back into the well with a sound like a held breath released, and the congregation surged forward toward the wellhead, singing, kind, apologetic, absolute—and in his pocket, against his hammering heart, the letters from Beatrice seemed suddenly, impossibly, to weigh more than paper should.
