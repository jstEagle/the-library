## 32. Beatrice's Real Message

The water did not behave like water.

That was the first thing Beatrix understood, crouched behind the lych-gate with his palms pressed so hard against his ears that he could hear his own pulse complaining about it. Water, in his experience, went where gravity told it. This went where the song told it. It climbed out of the well in ropes and ribbons, hung in the air like curtains in an open window, and from every hanging strand came voices.

Not a chorus. That was the terrible part. A chorus would have been one thing, one sound, something a man could shout over. This was a congregation — hundreds of them, thousands maybe, each voice distinct, each one *kind*. They weren't screaming. They were helping.

*It's all right, Beatrix.*

*You've carried it long enough, dear.*

*We only want to finish it for you.*

His mother's voice was in there somewhere. He was almost sure of it. That was the trap inside the trap: he couldn't verify anything anymore. The well had been wearing Beatrice's voice for days now, reciting memories she'd owned outright — the summer they'd buried the hamster under the lilac and told their father it had run away to the seaside; the way she'd once eaten an entire jar of maraschino cherries on a dare and cried for two hours — and then, without changing tone by so much as a semitone, it had begun reciting things she couldn't have known. The color of the wallpaper in a room Beatrix had never described to anyone. The number of steps down to the cellar of a house they'd left when he was four.

Grief, he was learning, made a wonderfully sloppy lock. Anything shaped roughly like your dead could turn it.

So he did what he had trained himself to do since childhood, the one skill Hollowmere had never managed to breed out of him: he made himself small, and dull, and *elsewhere*. He folded himself behind the lych-gate among the wet leaves, pressed his hands to his ears until the world reduced to blood-noise, and waited for the flood to lose interest in him.

Unremarkable. Invisible. A man-shaped absence.

The water sang itself hoarse over the village for the better part of an hour. Then, finding no mouth open to receive it, no throat willing to complete the fourth verse, it sighed back down into the well the way a crowd files out of a cathedral — reluctantly, in ones and twos, still murmuring courtesies. By full dark the lanes were merely wet, and the bell in the roofless tower tolled once, twice, with the sulky irregularity of a clock that knows nobody is listening.

Beatrix unpeeled his hands from his ears. His head rang. His heart was going like a telegraph key.

And underneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, a thought arrived with the clarity of cold water up the nose:

*She wouldn't be in there.*

He didn't know how he knew. He only knew it the way you know a wrong note — not by analysis but by flinch. Everything the well had shown him of Beatrice had been *almost* right, and he had spent thirty-one chapters of this nightmare mistaking almost-right for right because wanting it to be true had felt the same as it being true. But the real Beatrice had been allergic to almost. She'd returned library books early. She'd corrected strangers' pronunciation of her own name with cheerful ruthlessness. If Beatrice Marchetti were trapped in a well, she would not have spent her time mouthing verses at him through black water like some drowned siren in a penny dreadful.

She'd have left instructions.

* * *

The inn was empty. Of course it was. Half the village had lost its name and the other half was standing in the market square in the drizzle, politely waiting for equinox, humming. Coram's taproom sat abandoned with its chairs upturned, its fire burned down to a red memory, and its guestbook lying open on the bar where Beatrix's signature had faded to a ghost of a ghost — three pale loops and a tail, like a bird's footprint in snow.

He lit the lamp, built the fire back up with hands that shook only a little, and sat down at the corner table where he and Vale had argued folklore over bad sherry what felt like a geological age ago.

Then he took out the letters.

There were nine of them. Unsent, undated, or dated in ways that made no sense — Beatrice had written them across the months of her own visit to Hollowmere, the visit the village swore she'd never made, and had tucked them into the lining of her writing case like a woman hiding money from burglars she expected to be polite about it. He had read them a dozen times since finding them. He knew them the way you know a hymn: by shape, by habit, without ever quite hearing the words again.

*Dear Bee,* (she called him Bee; everyone else had settled for Trix, and she'd declared it undignified), *the village is exactly as charming as advertised and exactly as wrong as I suspected...*

*They asked after you today, which is odd, because I never mentioned I had a brother...*

*I don't think the bell melted in 1911. I think it was *dismissed*, and there's a difference...*

*They need a Marchetti who doesn't believe. Don't come here. If you're reading this, you came anyway, which means you never listened to me, which means you're my brother after all...*

He read them again now, aloud, in a whisper, partly to keep his own voice alive in his own ears — the bell had been learning his timbre, and he'd developed the superstitious habit of talking to himself just to prove the voice still answered to him — and partly because reading her handwriting silently had started to feel like eavesdropping on someone mid-sentence.

Somewhere around the fifth letter, his whisper snagged.

*—and honestly, Bee, if you must come, then **don't** do anything brave, **save** your skepticism like matches, keep it dry, and **me** — well. **Me** you may have to let go...*

He stopped.

Read it again.

*Don't save me. Save the name.*

No. No, that wasn't — that was coincidence, that was his grief doing what grief does, finding faces in wallpaper, finding messages in weather. He read the sentence straight through as prose and it said nothing of the kind. It was Beatrice being wry about her own danger, telling him to guard his disbelief like dry matches and accept that she might be beyond rescue.

But his eyes kept sliding off the prose and onto the letters themselves. Onto the *first letters*.

Because Beatrix Marchetti had grown up with a twin who played games with everything, including language. Acrostics. She'd hidden their ages in birthday poems. She'd signed one school essay to their terrifying form mistress with an acrostic that spelled out *MRS. PRITCHARD SMELLS OF CABBAGE*, and when the woman had finally decoded it, two years later, Beatrice had received the caning of her life grinning like a saint. She could not write six sentences without hiding something in them. It wasn't cleverness. It was breathing.

He laid the nine letters out on the table in the order he believed she'd written them — the order of her deepening dread — and read down the first letter of each opening line.

D.
S.
T.
N.
M.
E.
S.
A.
V.

Nothing. He rearranged them by postmark guesswork. D, S, M, T, N, E, V, S, A. Nothing. He tried last lines. He tried closing signatures. His heart was hammering now, and he hated himself for the hope, because hope in Hollowmere was bait.

Then he noticed that the letters weren't all the same size.

Handwriting has tides. You don't notice them unless you've spent a lifetime watching one particular hand, and Beatrix had spent forty years watching this one across breakfast tables and hospital beds and Christmas cards signed *Bee & Bea, double trouble.* Beatrice's capitals swelled when she was emphasizing something. Not much. A hair's width. But she'd been writing these letters knowing they might be read by eyes that were not friendly — knowing the pages might be searched — and so whatever she'd hidden, she'd hidden the way she'd hidden the cabbage insult: small enough to survive scrutiny, big enough to be found by the one person who knew her hand like his own.

He got Coram's good magnifying glass from behind the bar — the one the innkeeper used for reading labels, and, Beatrix now suspected, for other kinds of reading entirely — and went through the letters line by line, marking every capital that stood even slightly proud of its neighbors.

In the first letter, the *D* of *Don't*.

Second letter, the *S* of *Save*.

Third, an *M* — *My dear stubborn brother*.

Fourth, the *E* of *Even if I'm gone*.

Fifth, *Me*.

Sixth, *Save*.

Seventh, *The*.

Eighth, *Name*.

Ninth, a single word, swollen and deliberate, the largest capital in the whole set, underlined — no, not underlined, *flourished*, with the little downward flick she used when she wanted him to pay attention and also wanted to make him laugh while doing it:

*Yourself.*

He sat back. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the bell tolled its sulky half-hour, and the sound slid off him like rain off oilcloth.

*Don't save me. Save the name. Yourself.*

"That's cheating," he said aloud, to the empty taproom, and his voice cracked clean through the middle of it. "That's — you absolute menace. You couldn't have just written it on the envelope?"

But of course she couldn't. Whatever came to Hollowmere wearing loved ones' voices read what was plainly written. The well had quoted her letters back at him — he realized it now with a chill — but it had quoted the *prose*, the surface, the skin of the words. It had taken the letters at face value, because that was the nature of the thing below: it dealt in what was said, never in what was meant. It was a creature of the sung verse and the spoken vow. Subtext was holy ground it couldn't walk on.

Beatrice had known that too. Somehow, months before any of this, she had worked out the rules of the game well enough to beat it — and she'd beaten it the only way she'd ever beaten anything, which was by being cleverer than everyone in the room and insufferable about it afterward.

He laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and he let it, because there was no one left in the room to see him and the one person who'd have teased him mercilessly for crying was the person he was crying over.

*Don't save me.*

All week he had been trying to save her. Digging toward her voice, leaning over the well's black mirror, nearly going in after her when Vale hauled him back. Every instinct he had — every decent, ordinary, human instinct — pointed down into that water, where something wore her face and mouthed her verse and waited for him to lean one inch too far.

And she had seen it coming. From wherever she was — and he was done pretending he knew where that was; the fog this morning had held a hundred dissolving faces and hers had not been among them, and he would carry that particular mercy like a stone in his shoe forever — she had looked ahead at her twin brother and predicted, with twin-perfect accuracy, exactly how stupidly he would behave.

*Save the name.*

Marchetti. The name the ledger had claimed for generations before his birth. The name the villagers sang in verses one through three. The name the torn page held, the name the bell wanted completed, the name the whole rotten century of Hollowmere had been trying to collect. He had assumed the name was the debt. He'd had it backwards. The name was the *collateral* — and collateral, unlike a debtor, could be handed over.

*Yourself.*

Not the body. Not the belief. Not the life. The village needed a Marchetti who didn't believe, Coram had confessed, because unbelief kept the door shut from inside — but a door needs a frame, and the frame was the name. Strip the name away, renounce it, give it to the well freely and aloud, and there would be nothing left for the song to hold onto. No Marchetti at the wellhead. Just a man with no claim on him at all, standing in the rain, belonging nowhere — the one condition he'd spent his whole miserable, careful life perfecting.

She'd known that too. God help him, she'd known that about him better than anyone alive. *You never belonged anywhere, Bee, and look how well you've survived it,* she'd written once, years ago, in a Christmas card, teasing him, meaning it kindly, and he'd sulked about it for a month.

Now it was the load-bearing wall of his soul.

He gathered the letters up with both hands, carefully, the way you handle something that has just become the most important object in the world, and held them a moment against his chest before folding them away.

"Right," he said to the fire, to the empty chairs, to the faded signature in the guestbook. "Instructions received."

Outside, the drizzle had thickened toward proper rain. Down the lane, the market square glowed with lantern-light — the congregation gathering early, kind and apologetic, rehearsing their harmonies for tomorrow. And above the roofless tower, against the bruised underside of the clouds, the bell tolled once — and this time, unmistakably, in the pause after the note died, he heard it try a word. Half a word. The first syllable of a name.

*Mar—*

It stopped. It would try again at dawn. And when it tried, it would find no one named Marchetti left in Hollowmere to answer to.

Beatrix pulled his coat on, turned up the collar, and went out into the rain to give the well exactly what it had asked for — and nothing else besides.
