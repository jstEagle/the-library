## 4. The Kindly Room

I want to say I slept badly, but that would be a lie, and the house has made me sensitive to lies.

The truth is I slept better than I had in years — eight unbroken hours in the front bedroom on an air mattress, no dreams, no 3 a.m. ceiling-stare, nothing. I woke at seven with sunlight through the boards and the specific, guilty contentment of someone who has just been very gently lied to. My body felt rested. My body had no right to feel rested. Derelict houses are supposed to give you a stiff neck and a low hum of dread, not the sleep of a well-fed infant.

"Thank you," I said to the room, sarcastically, and then heard myself and stopped.

That's the thing about talking to empty houses: you do it once as a joke and then you're doing it all day, and by the second day it isn't a joke anymore, it's a relationship.

* * *

I spent the morning doing what I'm actually paid for, which is measuring things and pretending the measurements mean something. Hollowmere is a Georgian shell wearing a Victorian overcoat over a medieval skeleton, and every generation added a corridor like a scar. My job was to produce a report that would let the county decide whether to sell it, condemn it, or quietly forget about it, which is the option counties prefer, being the one that requires no signatures.

By noon I'd logged eleven rooms on the ground floor, nine above, cellars I wasn't eager to revisit. The 1974 file said twelve rooms upstairs. I had nine. I assumed the old surveyor had counted closets, or counted twice, or been the kind of man who rounded up out of optimism. Surveyors are allowed their small dishonesties; it's how we survive our own profession.

It was while checking the east wing — the part of the house where the wallpaper hangs in long dead tongues and the floor gives underfoot like a tired lung — that I noticed the wall.

There was a door in it.

Not a hidden door. Not a cleverly disguised panel. Just a door, ordinary, painted the same exhausted green as every other door in Hollowmere, standing in a stretch of wall where yesterday there had been plaster and peeling roses. I know there hadn't been a door because I have photographs. I photograph everything; it's the only way I trust my own memory, which has always been more accommodating than I'd like. Yesterday afternoon, 4:17 p.m., frame forty-one: blank wall, water stain shaped like Portugal. This morning: door, hinges, handle worn bright in the middle from hands.

Hands. Plural. Over years.

I stood in front of it for a long time doing the professional thing, which is cataloguing reasons not to be afraid. Old houses shift. Doors get boarded and unboarded. People hang wallpaper over openings all the time. All true. None of it explained the handle, polished by use, in a house that had been sealed since before I was born.

"Right," I said, in my firmest voice, the one I use for dogs and planning officers. "Let's see what you've got."

I want it noted that I did consider leaving. I considered it thoroughly, the way you consider exercise. But the road loops — I didn't know that yet, that came later, that came after — and besides, I had a contract, and more than a contract. I had the small cold thrill of a woman who has spent thirty-four years making herself unremarkable finally being remarked upon by something. Even if the something was a house. Especially then.

I opened the door.

* * *

Warmth came out first.

Not the fuggy warmth of a boiler cupboard or the trapped heat of a sun-facing room. Warmth like a kitchen on a Sunday morning. Warmth with intent. After three days in a house where my breath fogged indoors, stepping into that little room was like walking into an embrace from someone you can't see, which is either lovely or the worst thing that can happen to you, depending on how starved you are. I was, as it turns out, quite starved.

The room was small. Maybe ten feet by twelve, with a sloped ceiling that meant it lived in the roof space somehow, though the roof space I'd measured yesterday had been a single cold attic full of pigeon filth and broken chairs. A window looked east over the drowned garden, clean glass, no grime, as if someone had wiped it recently for the view.

And the smell. Bread and lavender. That's such a gentle combination, isn't it? It's the smell of grandmothers, of holiday cottages, of the idea of home rather than the practice of it. Lavender for the linen, bread for the heart. Somebody had curated this smell. Somebody had *wanted* it.

The bed was turned down.

One corner of the quilt folded back at a precise, hospitable angle, the pillow plumped, a hot water bottle — an actual hot water bottle, in a knitted cover, blue with a sheep on it — lying along the mattress like a sleeping animal. I touched it. Warm. Not warm-ish, not holding some ancient heat. Warm the way a thing is warm when it was filled twenty minutes ago.

On the nightstand: a glass of water, beaded with condensation. A book, face-down to keep its place. I looked at the book before I could stop myself, because of course I did, because it was going to be something about me, everything in this house is eventually something about me.

It was a paperback romance, the kind sold at petrol stations, the kind with a windswept couple on the cover. The bookmark was a strip of paper torn from a surveyor's field notebook. My handwriting. An inventory line I'd written two days ago: *hall table, oak, water damage, value nil.* Except the words had changed. They read now:

*hall table, oak, water damage, kept anyway.*

I put the book down carefully, the way you put down something that might be asleep.

* * *

Here is what I should tell you about myself, since the house clearly already knows: I have never once in my life been expected anywhere.

This isn't self-pity; it's inventory, and inventory is my love language. I was passed between four sets of relatives between the ages of six and sixteen, and the trick to surviving that is to become furniture. Be quiet, be useful, be easy to store. Don't take up a bedroom that might be wanted later. Don't have preferences that require remembering. When you arrive somewhere, unpack into the smallest available surface and keep your coat within reach of the door. I got so good at it that even now, staying with friends, I wake at dawn and remake the sofa bed so they won't know I was ever there.

So understand what that turned-down bed did to me. Understand that a hot water bottle with a sheep on it, warming a mattress in a room that does not exist, in a house that should be empty, hit a wound I didn't know I had with a precision no person has ever managed. Some part of me — the part that was six years old and waiting to be collected — looked at that folded-back corner and thought, with a rush of relief so violent it was nearly tears:

*Oh good. Someone saved me a place.*

I sat down on the edge of the bed before I decided to. That's the honest truth. One moment I was standing, taking notes, maintaining professional distance, and the next I was sitting, and the mattress received me with a sigh, and the warmth came up through my jeans, and I had to grip the quilt to stop myself lying down.

The quilt was soft. Of course it was.

"Stop it," I told myself out loud. "You're a grown woman. You appraise buildings. Buildings don't tuck people in."

The room said nothing, which was somehow worse than an answer, because the silence had a listening quality to it. The quality of a host who has stepped out to check the oven and will return shortly.

I got up. I photographed everything — the window, the bottle, the book, the bed, the fold of the quilt, the water glass with its ring already marking the nightstand. Forty frames. In the viewfinder everything looked normal, which frightened me more than anything anomalous would have. Horror should look like horror. Comfort that shouldn't exist looks like comfort, and comfort is much harder to report to a county office.

Then I did the sensible thing, the thing any competent professional would do: I went looking for the room's walls from the outside.

* * *

The east wing gave me nothing. I paced its length with my laser measure and got numbers that matched yesterday's exactly, matched the 1974 plans, matched geometry itself. Ten feet by twelve of roof space simply refused to contain the Kindly Room — I'd started calling it that in my head within an hour, and I hated myself for it, because naming things is how you adopt them — and yet when I walked back down the corridor, the green door stood where it stood, patient as a dog by a gate.

I tried the maths again. And again. I drew the floor plan three times, and each time my pen hesitated at the same spot, wanting to add a rectangle that had no business existing, the way your tongue goes to a missing tooth.

Fine. Fine. I'm a surveyor, not a physicist. Houses settle; apparently some settle sideways.

What unsettled me more was smaller. When I finally packed up for the evening — because I am not sleeping in the magic tuck-in room, I have some standards, they're just located very far south of my curiosity — I passed the green door and saw that the light under it was on.

Warm lamplight, gold at the seam.

I hadn't left a lamp in there. There was no lamp in there. There was no electricity in the east wing; I'd checked the fuse box on day one and written *east wing: no supply, rewire essential.*

I stood in the dark corridor with my torch dying in my hand and watched the light under the door, and I thought about the hot water bottle, and the water glass, and the book with my handwriting in it, kept anyway, and I understood — really understood, down in the cellar of myself — that none of this was a trap. Traps are set by things that want to hurt you, and hurt is at least a form of attention you can fight. This was worse. This was a spare room made up for a guest. This was a place at the table. Somewhere in the bones of Hollowmere House was a hunger shaped exactly like hospitality, and it had looked at Ada Voss — invisible Ada Voss, easily-stored Ada Voss, the woman nobody had ever saved a place for —

— and it had saved her a place.

The light under the door flickered, once, like a wink, or like a fire settling in a grate it had no business having.

"I'm sleeping in the front room," I announced to the corridor, loudly, the way you talk to dogs and planning officers and other creatures you hope will respect firmness. "Do you hear me? Front room. Locked door."

The corridor held its silence, courteous as a butler.

I locked the front-room door. I put a chair under the handle, which I knew was theatre, and did it anyway, because theatre is also a kind of prayer.

And I lay there in the dark telling myself the rational things — gas leak, carbon monoxide, temporal lobe, stress, the road loops, the road loops — until I realised I could smell bread. Faint, from under the door, from everywhere. Fresh bread, rising somewhere in the house, in an oven that had no power, baked by hands that had no owner, for a breakfast I had not asked for and would, I already knew, with the weary certainty of a woman who has finally been expected somewhere,

eat.

Somewhere below me, unhurried, a kettle began to boil.
