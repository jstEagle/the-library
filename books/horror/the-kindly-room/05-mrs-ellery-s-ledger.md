## 5. Mrs. Ellery's Ledger

The village archive occupied the back room of St. Cuthbert's parish hall, which meant it smelled of old tea and older hymns, and it was kept, insofar as anyone kept it, by a woman named Mrs. Ellery.

I knew this because the librarian at the county branch had told me so on the phone that morning, in the tone people use when they're passing you along to someone they'd rather not deal with themselves. "You'll want Edith Ellery for anything before 1990," she said. "She's got the ledgers. She's got everything." A pause. "Don't mention Hollowmere House to her."

Which was, of course, exactly like telling me not to think about an elephant while handing me a banana.

I had spent the drive over composing my opening remarks. I am good at opening remarks; they are one of the many small tools of invisibility, along with sensible shoes and the ability to ask questions without ever answering any. You get a person talking about themselves or their records and you become furniture. Furniture gets told things. In thirty-one years of being passed over, promoted around, forgotten at parties, and left off guest lists, I had learned that the overlooked see everything. It's the visible people who miss the world.

So I arrived at the parish hall at ten past two with my folder of photocopies and my most unremarkable cardigan, and I found Mrs. Ellery already waiting for me, as though she'd been told.

She was small and upright in a chair behind a trestle table stacked with ledger books, her white hair pinned so precisely it looked architectural. Her eyes were pale blue and very quick, and they went through me the way I go through a house — room by room, taking inventory.

"You're the surveyor," she said. Not a question.

"Ada Voss. County appraisal office. I'm working on a property out—"

"Hollowmere House."

The elephant, mentioned for me. I blinked. "That's — yes. How did you know?"

"Because you're the third one they've sent." She folded her hands on the table. "In fifty years. And the other two didn't come here first, and neither of them finished their reports." Something moved behind her face then, something old and carefully stored, like furniture under dust sheets. "Sit down, Miss Voss. Would you like tea? Don't answer. I'll make tea. You'll want both hands free in a minute."

I sat. I did not understand what she meant by that, but I have learned that when an old woman tells you to sit, there is generally a reason measured in decades.

* * *

While the kettle grumbled in the next room, I looked at the ledgers. They were parish records, mostly — baptisms, burials, the slow arithmetic of a village that had been quietly shrinking since the war. But there were others interleaved among them: a school register, a shop account book, and one slim volume bound in green cloth with no title at all, its spine cracked into soft ridges like the knuckles of a hand.

Mrs. Ellery came back with two cups on a tray and set mine down without asking how I took it. Milk, no sugar. Correct, as it happened, which unsettled me more than if she'd gotten it wrong.

"You've been in the house," she said.

"Two nights now. It's a substantial property. Georgian core, Victorian additions. The roof is better than the county expects."

"The roof is fine. The roof was always fine." She lowered herself into her chair with the economy of someone who has rationed her movements for years. "What did the inventory say? The 1974 survey."

I hesitated. This was the part I hadn't planned to share, the part I'd been carrying around for three days like a stone in my coat pocket: eight names on the occupancy list of an empty house. But Mrs. Ellery was looking at me with those quick pale eyes, and I understood suddenly that she had not survived to her age by letting people keep their stones.

"Eight occupants," I said. "Dated October 1974. The house has been vacant since."

"And the eighth name?"

"Illegible." I heard my own voice do the thing it does — flatten, professionalize, become a door closing. "Water damage, probably."

"Mm." She drank her tea. Outside, a tractor went by on the lane and faded, and the silence after it was enormous. "My brother is on that list," she said. "Alfred Ellery. Third name down. He was forty-four years old and he went to Hollowmere House on the fourteenth of October, 1974, to help search for a lost child, and he never came out, and he has never come out, and every year on the fourteenth of October I bake a lemon cake because it was his favorite, and I eat one slice, and I give the rest to the birds." She set down her cup with a small precise click. "Now. Tell me about your mirrors."

I hadn't mentioned mirrors.

I want to be clear about that, because afterward I spent a long time turning the conversation over, looking for the place where I might have let it slip. I hadn't. I had said: eight occupants, illegible name, water damage. I had said nothing about standing in the upstairs corridor at three in the morning watching my reflection finish raising its arm a full second after I raised mine. I had said nothing about the mirror in the east bedroom where, just once, something crossed behind me that I had not yet turned to see.

But Mrs. Ellery asked about mirrors the way you ask about a wound you can see through someone's shirt.

"They lag," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "About a second."

She nodded, unsurprised, unsurprising, and opened the green ledger, and this is the point — I know it sounds impossible, but I have decided to stop apologizing in my head for the things that are true — this is the point where the room changed. Or perhaps I changed rooms. The tea went cold in my cup between one breath and the next, and the light through the parish hall window went the color of weak cider, and I was still sitting in my chair but I was also, somehow, seeing October 1974.

Her memory. She was showing me, or it was spilling out of her, or the ledger was doing what ledgers do, which is hold things. I don't know. I only know that I saw it.

* * *

A kitchen. Yellow wallpaper, sun-faded to the color of butter left out too long. A woman — young, dark-haired, flour on her forearms — laughing at something a man said, though the man was out of frame, only his voice remaining, warm and low. The kitchen belonged to Hollowmere House; I knew it the way you know a face in an old photograph, by the deep sill of the window and the way the light came down the garden path toward it. There were children somewhere in the house. You could hear them the way you hear weather in another room — a thump, a shriek of delight, running feet on stairs.

And Alfred. Alfred Ellery at forty-four, big shoulders, forearms like a farrier's, standing in that yellow kitchen holding a mug of tea and complaining cheerfully about the price of feed corn while the woman laughed again, and the whole scene had the specific golden texture of an afternoon that nobody present knows is the last of its kind.

Then the voice of the man — Merrick, some part of me supplied, the family was called Merrick — the man's voice sharpening, calling up the stairs, calling again, and the running feet stopping, and the silence arriving all at once like a dropped glass.

The boy was gone. Thomas. Six years old. Gone from a house with its doors locked against the evening, gone from a garden with walls too high for a child to climb, gone between one shriek of laughter and the next, and the woman's hands were still dusty with flour when the neighbors started arriving with torches, and Alfred put down his mug of tea — I saw him set it down, half-drunk, forever — and went out into the dark with everyone else to find a child who had not been lost but taken, though nobody knew that yet, though nobody would ever quite know it, even now.

They searched for three days. I watched it happen from above and inside at once, the way dreams work: the lanes, the quarry pond dragged with chains, the churchyard walked row by row. On the third night the searching stopped being organized and became desperate, and on the fourth day a boy from the village — I felt Mrs. Ellery's younger self flinch at his name, a boy called Prosser, dead now these thirty years — came running up the lane shouting that he'd seen lights in Hollowmere House. Lights moving. Room to room, floor to floor, like lanterns carried by searching hands.

But the Merricks weren't home. The Merricks had driven to the county town that morning to put up posters, and the house stood empty, and everyone knew it stood empty, and the lights moved anyway.

Alfred went in first. Of course he did. He was the kind of man who went in first; you could see it in how he held his shoulders. Seven of them went in behind him — I counted them as they crossed the threshold, because the memory counted them, because Mrs. Ellery had counted them ten thousand times across fifty years: Alfred, and the vicar, and Prosser's father, and two farmhands whose names slid past me, and a woman named Cathcart who taught piano, and one more, a young policeman, barely grown.

Seven. Plus the boy Thomas, who was not in the house at all — who was, though no one living knew it yet, already on the north road in the back of his aunt's borrowed car, asleep under a coat, clutching something silver against his chest.

The seven went in with their torches, and the door closed behind them — not slammed, not locked, just closed, the way a mouth closes gently around a secret — and when the Merricks came home at dusk and threw open the front door calling for their son, the house was empty. Entirely empty. No searchers, no torches, no mud on the hall floor. The yellow kitchen smelled faintly of bread.

The police came. The police searched. The dogs would not cross the gate. And in the end — I felt this arrive in Mrs. Ellery's memory with the weight of a coffin lid — in the end the file was closed with the words *no trace*, and the house was sealed, and the Merricks sold it within the year to a buyer whose solicitor's letters came from an address that didn't exist, and the village agreed, without ever saying it aloud, to agree on nothing.

Except Mrs. Ellery. Mrs. Ellery baked a lemon cake every year.

* * *

The parish hall came back. The kettle ticked as it cooled. My tea was cold and I was gripping the edge of the trestle table hard enough to hurt my fingers, and Mrs. Ellery was watching me with an expression I couldn't read until I realized I could read it perfectly, because it was the expression I wore in empty houses: professional detachment stretched over something screaming.

"You saw it," she said quietly. "You went away and came back. That's how it starts, Miss Voss. First the mirrors. Then the drifting."

"What was that?" My voice came out rough. "Was that — your memory, or—"

"It's in the ledger." She touched the green book without opening it, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Not the words. The ledger doesn't have words anymore. It used to. I wrote it all down in 1975, every detail, because I thought if it was written down it would stay put. And it stayed put for years. But sometime — I couldn't tell you when — the pages went blank, and now when I open it, it shows me instead. Shows whoever opens it, I think. Whatever they need." Her mouth twisted. "It's a very accommodating house. Even its paperwork."

"Mrs. Ellery." I made myself say it plainly, because plainness was the last tool I had left. "Your brother. Do you believe he's alive in there?"

She was quiet for a long moment. Through the window, the churchyard lay in flat grey light, rows of stones like teeth.

"I believe the house keeps what it loves," she said. "That's the horror of it, if you want the horror. Everyone expects a haunted house to be cruel. Screaming walls, blood on the ceiling, doors that bite. Hollowmere isn't like that. Hollowmere is *kind*. It laid supper for those seven men and women, I'd stake my life on it. It made up beds. It warmed the rooms. It wanted them comfortable." Her voice frayed slightly at the edges, an old tear in old cloth. "Fifty years, and I promise you they are not suffering in there. They're *content*. That's what makes it worse. Cruelty you can fight. Kindness like that — kindness that won't let go — you can only grieve it."

"Why haven't you told anyone? Why did the librarian warn me off?"

"Because the other two surveyors came asking questions, and I told them what I'm telling you, and they went out to the house anyway." She folded her hands. "One of them left a car at the gate in 1988. It's still there, under the ivy, if you look. The county wrote him off as a runaway — debts, they said. The other one finished her report, actually. Filed it and everything. Sound structure, no hazards, recommend demolition." She looked at me steadily. "Read the signature line sometime. It isn't her handwriting. It isn't anyone's handwriting. It looks like it was written by a house trying to remember what a hand is."

I thought of the inventory list. Eight occupants. The eighth name resolving, when I looked too long, into letters I refused to read.

"There's a way to end it," I said. "Isn't there. You know what it wants back."

Something flickered across her face — hope, maybe, or its exhausted cousin. "Thomas Merrick took something when he ran. Something small and silver. His mother never knew what; his father guessed and never said. The house has been holding its residents ever since, waiting for its property to come home." She leaned forward. "If you're thinking of going to Thomas Merrick, Miss Voss, he's sixty-one years old now and he lives in a care home in Northgate, and he has tried four times in his life to throw that thing in a river, and four times it has been back in his pocket by morning. And the house cannot take it from him itself, and he will not carry it back, because—" she stopped.

"Because?" I prompted, though I already felt the answer coming, the way you feel a stair that isn't there.

"Because the debt can't be paid by proxy," she said. "The house has to receive it from a hand it already holds. From someone on its list." Her pale eyes rested on me, gentle and terrible. "You said the eighth name was illegible. Water damage, you thought."

The parish hall was very quiet. Somewhere outside, the birds were getting the tail end of a lemon cake.

"Look again," Mrs. Ellery said softly. "When you're ready. Look again, and read it properly. And then decide whether you're the kind of woman who runs north on the night road like Thomas Merrick did — or the kind who walks in the front door like my Alfred, meaning to help."

She closed the green ledger and put both hands flat upon it, and for a moment — I would swear this in any court, though no court would have me — for a moment the ledger breathed, once, under her palms, like something patient turning over in its sleep.

"Tea's cold," she said briskly, and began stacking cups. "Come back Thursday. I'll show you the photographs. And Miss Voss—" I paused at the door, cardigan buttoned, invisible, professional. "Wear your own coat tomorrow. Not the one hanging in the hall at Hollowmere. I know it's warmer. I know it fits perfectly. Wear your own."

I drove back with the windows down and the autumn air scraping my face, and I did not look at the passenger seat, where a green canvas surveyor's satchel sat that I had never owned, monogrammed with initials that were almost, but not quite, mine.
