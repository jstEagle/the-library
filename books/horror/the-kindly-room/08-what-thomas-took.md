## 8. What Thomas Took

Mrs. Ellery's cottage smelled of woodsmoke and something older underneath it—camphor, maybe, or the ghost of camphor, the way old houses keep the memory of smells long after the source has gone. She'd made tea without asking whether I wanted any, which was either hospitality or a stalling tactic, and she'd set out two cups on a tray that trembled very slightly when she carried it.

I'd come to the village archive on a Tuesday morning with my folder of photographs and my professional detachment buttoned up to the chin, expecting to find dusty ledgers and a bored clerk. What I found instead was a woman of about eighty who took one look at the address written in my surveyor's hand—Hollowmere House, parish of Little Wendle—and went the color of uncooked pastry.

"You're not going in there," she'd said. Not *are you going in there*. Not even a question mark. A flat declarative sentence delivered with the certainty of someone who had watched this exact conversation play out before and knew how it ended.

"I'm appraising it," I said. "For the county."

"The county," she repeated, as if I'd said *for the devil*, though honestly the distinction may have been finer than I appreciated at the time.

Now we sat across from each other in her narrow front room, and she looked at me for a long moment before she spoke again, and when she did, her voice had changed. It was quieter. It was the voice people use in hospitals and churches and other buildings where the truth tends to arrive uninvited.

"Before I tell you anything," she said, "I want you to answer me one question honestly. Have the mirrors started lagging?"

The tea went cold in my throat. I hadn't told anyone about the mirrors. I hadn't told anyone about the names, or the Kindly Room, or the corridor of doors that hadn't existed on Friday. I had, in fact, been very carefully not telling anyone anything, because telling people things is how you end up in a paper gown being asked to count backwards from ten.

"How did you know that?" I managed.

"Because everyone knows it eventually." She folded her hands in her lap. They were beautiful hands, old and knuckled, the hands of a woman who had done actual work with them for eighty years. "My brother knew it. In the end, he knew it better than anyone."

* * *

Her name had been different then, she told me. Everyone's name had been different then, in the way that names are different when you're eleven—you wear them loosely, like a coat you expect to grow into.

"It was October of 1974," she said. "The seventeenth. I remember because it was the day after my birthday, and Thomas—" her breath caught on the name, an old snag in an old sweater, worn smooth from catching but never mended, "—Thomas had promised to take me to the pictures in Wendle St. Mary as a belated treat. He never came."

She looked at the fire, not at me. People do that when they're about to handle something fragile. They look away so the thing they're holding doesn't have to see their face.

"The Merricks lived at Hollowmere. Had done for generations—the house was older than the parish records, older than the church, probably older than the road. There were seven of them in residence that autumn. Old Mr. Merrick and his wife, their daughter Ruth and her husband, and three of the cousins who'd come to stay after the war and simply never left. And Thomas. Thomas was fourteen."

"Seven," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "You said seven."

"I did."

"And there are seven names on the 1974 inventory. Seven residents listed as present on the date of survey. The eighth entry is—" I stopped. The eighth entry was mine, but I wasn't ready to say that aloud yet, and Mrs. Ellery, mercifully, didn't push.

"Go on," I said instead.

* * *

Here is what I understood by then, though I would not have phrased it this way until later: every house keeps something. Most houses keep only heat and dust and the particular silence of rooms nobody uses. But some houses—old houses, houses that have been loved badly or loved too hard or loved by people who died in them—some houses keep more than that. They keep a center of gravity. A hearth-name. And if you take that thing away, the house doesn't die. Houses don't die. Houses *wait*.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and Mrs. Ellery would have scolded me for it. She was a great one for order. Let her tell it.

* * *

"There was a cup," she said. "A christening cup, silver, small enough to sit in the palm of your hand. It had been in the Merrick family since before anyone could say how long. Every child born at Hollowmere was baptized from it—not at the church, mind, but at the hearth itself, because the Merricks were old-fashioned in ways the vicar pretended not to notice. And engraved around the rim, in letters worn nearly smooth, was the family name. The true name. The name the house was christened with."

"Hollowmere," I said.

"That's what it's called now." She said it carefully, the way you'd set down something hot. "Names change, dear. That's rather the point. The house has been called Hollowmere since 1911, when the last of the original line married a Hollow and took his name, because that's what women did then. But the cup held the older name. The first one. The one the walls were raised under and the foundations were blessed with. A hearth-name isn't just a word. It's the house's own name for itself. It's how the house knows it's *home*."

"And Thomas stole it."

"Thomas stole it." Her jaw tightened. "He was fourteen and he was furious—he'd had a row with his father about school, about everything, the way fourteen-year-olds do—and he decided he'd run away to his aunt in Birmingham and make them all sorry. And he wanted to take something. Something that would hurt. He'd heard his grandfather talk about the cup, about how the family was nothing without it, and so on the night of the seventeenth he went down to the hearth room and he took it out of its box and he ran."

"He got away, then."

"Oh, he got away." She laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "That's the horror of it, dear. He got clean away. Two counties, then four, then a life. He never came back. Never wrote. As far as I know he's still alive somewhere, an old man now, carrying that cup like a stone in his pocket for fifty years. Because here's the thing about a theft like that—it doesn't stay stolen. It stays *carried*."

"But the others. The seven."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside a bird made a sound and stopped abruptly, as if corrected.

"When Thomas ran," she said, "the household went after him. All of them. His father first, then his mother, then Ruth and her husband, then the cousins, one after another, out the front door and down the drive and into the lane. I saw them go. I was standing at our gate—I could see the front of Hollowmere from our gate, everyone in the village could—and I saw them running, and I thought, good, they'll catch him, he'll get a hiding, and next week he'll take me to the pictures after all."

She was quiet for a while. I let her be quiet. You learn patience in empty houses; it transfers.

"They never came out," she said finally. "That's what I can't make anyone understand, all these years. They didn't die. There were no bodies, no inquest, no funeral. The police searched for a month. Dogs went in and came out whining and wouldn't go back. And the house stood there with its door open and its lamps lit—lit, dear, though the electricity had been cut off in June—and it waited, and the seven of them simply never came out. Because they weren't lost in it. They were *kept*."

* * *

I want to be precise here, because precision was my trade and it's the only courage I've ever really had: I did not believe her. Not entirely. Not in the way you believe a thing with your whole spine. Some part of me was already drafting the report—elderly local resident, folkloric embellishment, recommend further historical research—and that part of me was doing its job, keeping me safe behind glass, cataloguing the world instead of living in it.

But another part of me was thinking about the inventory list that rewrote itself. About the eighth name. About the mirror in the east bathroom that had shown me reaching for a doorhandle a full second before my hand moved, and how I had stood there watching my own reflection like a film of a woman I hadn't met yet.

And another part—the part I liked least, the part I'd spent forty years pretending wasn't there—was thinking: *seven people stayed in that house for fifty years, and not one of them ever tried to leave.*

Or perhaps they tried. Perhaps leaving simply wasn't among the available options, the way flight isn't available to a fish. Perhaps the house had never locked a single door. Perhaps it had only ever needed to make them *belong*.

I knew something about belonging. Specifically, I knew about its absence, the way you know about a missing tooth—by the shape of the gap, by the tongue returning to it again and again. I had spent my whole life being unremarkable on purpose. Passed between relatives as a child, unnamed in every household, the girl who ate quietly and packed lightly and learned to read a room faster than she could read a book. Invisibility wasn't a flaw in me. It was a skill. It was armor. And now a house with a hunger fifty years old had looked at that armor and seen through it in a single night, and built me a warm room with bread smell and lavender, and put my name on a door.

Mrs. Ellery was watching me with terrible kindness.

"You feel it already," she said. "Don't you. The wanting-to-stay."

"I don't want to stay," I said. "I want to finish the appraisal and file my report and go home to my flat where the radiator clanks and the neighbor's telly comes through the wall."

"Yes," she said gently. "That's exactly what I mean. You described your home like a list of inconveniences. The house will fix that. The house is very good at fixing that."

* * *

"Tell me how to stop it," I said. "Tell me how to give it back."

She shook her head slowly. "I've thought about it for fifty years. I wrote to Thomas once, in the eighties. One letter, no reply. I wrote again in '96. Nothing. Either he never got them or—" she spread her hands, "—he couldn't bear to. And I'll tell you the other thing I worked out, the thing that kept me up nights when I was young enough to still have nightmares worth the name. The house doesn't want revenge. Revenge would be simple. Revenge, you could appease. What it wants is its name back, because without its name it cannot let anyone go. It holds them the way a fist holds water—no, that's wrong. The way a fist holds a bird. Gently. Kindly. Forever."

"The kindly room," I said.

Her eyes came up sharp. "What did you call it?"

"There's a bedroom. Small. Warm. It's not on any floor plan. The bed's turned down. It smells of bread and lavender."

Mrs. Ellery closed her eyes. When she opened them again they were wet, and she let them be wet without apology, which is the bravest thing I saw anyone do in the whole affair, including me.

"That was the nursery," she whispered. "Thomas slept there when he was small. Oh, you poor creature. It's making you a place in the family. It does that. It starts with the room."

"Then I need to return the cup," I said. "Tonight, tomorrow, as soon as—"

"No." Her hand shot out and gripped my wrist, and for eighty years old she was strong as a gate hinge. "Listen to me. The debt can't be paid by proxy. The house won't take the cup back from a stranger's hands—it's had fifty years of strangers' hands, surveyors and constables and one dreadful man from the council in 1988 who took photographs and left with his hair white. The cup must be returned by someone the house has already claimed. Someone whose name is already on a door."

The fire settled. Outside, the light was going amber and long, and I thought absurdly of harvest moons, of the almanac page I'd glanced at in the library, of dates circled in red ink by some previous librarian with a sense of drama.

"Then I have a problem," I said, "because the man who took it won't come back."

Mrs. Ellery released my wrist and sat back, and for a moment she looked every one of her eighty years, and then some years besides that belonged to other people—to her brother, to the boy at the gate, to the whole vanished October of 1974.

"No," she agreed. "He won't come back. He's spent half a century not coming back. But he's alive, dear. I checked the records every few years, the way you check a wound. He's in a care home now, up past Ashcombe way—Merrick, Thomas Merrick, aged sixty-one. Lucid, the matron says, when she wrote back to me last spring. Lucid and frightened and never once, in all these years, able to throw that cup away."

She reached for the teapot and refilled my cup with a steady hand, hostess to the end, and said:

"So you'd best go and meet him. Before the moon turns."
