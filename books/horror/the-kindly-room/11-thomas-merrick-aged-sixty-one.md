## 11. Thomas Merrick, Aged Sixty-One

The care home was called Rosebank Lodge, which is the kind of name that promises roses and a lodge and delivers neither—just a pebbledash bungalow on the edge of a market town two counties east of Hollowmere, with a car park full of cars that never went anywhere and a noticeboard advertising a quiz night I would have bet my surveyor's license nobody won.

I'd found him the way you find anything now: badly, then suddenly. The county records had given me a Thomas Merrick born 1954, and after that it was three days of phone calls, a subscription to an ancestry site I'd cancel before the trial ended, and one very patient registrar who thought I was doing family history. In a sense I was. I just hadn't known until recently that my family might turn out to be a house.

The drive down had taken four hours, most of them spent arguing with myself in the way I do—the quiet, circular argument where one voice says *you're a professional woman following up a records discrepancy* and the other says *you're driving toward the man who did this to seven people and to you, and your mirrors have started showing you tomorrow.* I'd covered the rearview with a tea towel outside of Leeds. It didn't help. I could feel the reflection back there anyway, half a beat behind, like a passenger breathing out of time with me.

At reception, a young man with a lanyard and the unshakeable cheer of someone three weeks into the job looked me up on his screen.

"Visiting Mr. Merrick? Are you family?"

"I'm—" I considered several answers. "A relation," I said, which was true in a way I hoped he'd never have to understand. "Distant."

"Lovely. He doesn't get many. He's in the conservatory—he likes the light." The young man lowered his voice as if confiding something medical. "He's very sharp, our Thomas. You'll know what I mean when you talk to him."

I knew what he meant before we'd finished shaking hands.

Thomas Merrick sat in a wicker chair by the conservatory's fogged windows with a folded newspaper on his lap that he clearly hadn't read, because his eyes weren't on it. They were on me from the moment I came through the door. He was small the way old men get small, folded into himself, cardigan the color of weak tea—but his eyes were sixty-one years old and also about nine, and they went straight past my face to something behind me, checking the doorway, checking it twice.

"You've come from there," he said. Not a question. His voice was dry and level, a man reading out a diagnosis.

I pulled a chair over and sat, keeping the little table between us the way you keep a table between yourself and anyone holding something breakable. Because he was holding something. I'd seen it the moment I entered: a silver cup, small, christening-sized, sitting on the table beside a cold cup of tea, wrapped at its base in a handkerchief so it wouldn't touch the surface directly. He kept one finger resting against its rim the whole time we talked, the way some people keep a hand on a dog that startles easily.

"My name's Ada Voss," I said. "I'm a surveyor. I've been appraising a property called—"

"Hollowmere." He said it the way you'd say the name of a disease you'd survived, except he hadn't survived it; he'd just carried it somewhere else. "You're the one they've got now."

The word *they* did a lot of work in that sentence, and none of it was the work I wanted done. "There are records," I said, falling back on the register I use for difficult clients, "from October 1974. Seven people listed as residents of the house who were never accounted for afterward. Your mother owned the property. You were seventeen."

"I was seventeen," he agreed. "And they were not *residents*, Miss Voss. Residents can leave." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I'd seen in weeks, and I'd recently been smiled at by a corridor. "Sorry. That's rude. You've driven a long way. Would you like tea? The tea here is a war crime, but it's hot."

"No thank you." I looked at the cup under his finger. Up close I could see the engraving, worn nearly smooth, just the ghost of letters: *HOLLOWMERE*. And beneath it, deeper, older, cut by a surer hand: another word entirely, a family name I didn't recognize—*Everdene*. "That's what he took," I said. "That's the hearth-name."

Something moved through his face like weather. "Mrs. Ellery told you." He closed his eyes briefly. "Of course she did. She's been waiting fifty years for someone to come asking. She used to write to me, you know. Every Christmas, for about ten years. No return address after a while. Just the same line every year: *Bring it home, Tom.*" He turned the cup a quarter-inch, absently, the way you'd worry a loose tooth. "Then the letters stopped. I expect she decided I wasn't going to."

"Were you?"

"What do you think?" He gestured at the room—the fogged glass, the dying poinsettia, the quiz night notice visible through the door. "Look where I live. Look at what I'm holding. I'm sixty-one years old and I sleep with this thing in a sock drawer because if it's more than six feet from me I can't breathe properly. I've thrown it away eleven times. Eleven. A canal in Sheffield. A skip in Nottingham. Once I drove forty miles to bury it on the coast and I dug it back up the same afternoon because my hands started doing it without asking me." He laughed, short and awful. "It always comes back. Or I come back to it. At this point I genuinely couldn't tell you which."

"Why?" I asked. "What does it do?"

"It doesn't *do* anything. That's the horror of it. It sits there being a cup." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into the register of a man explaining fire to someone standing in smoke. "But it's a *name*, Miss Voss. Not engraved—a name. Spoken. My mother spoke it over me when I was born in that house, and the house heard it, and the house kept it, the way houses keep heat in the walls. And when I took the cup—" his hand trembled once, and stilled, "—I took the house's name too. Its own name. The one it was christened with, before anyone called it Hollowmere. Before it was ever a house at all, maybe. I don't know what it was. I don't want to know what it was."

"And without it—"

"Without it, it can't let go of anything. Including itself." He said it flatly, a fact worn smooth as the engraving. "It holds on. That's all it knows how to do now. Hold on. People, mostly. It held on to seven of them the night I ran, because they tried to stop me and the house—" he stopped. Started again. "They walked into the hall after me and the front door simply stopped being a door. Mrs. Ellery's brother was one of them. Robert. He was holding my arm. I felt the house take him out of my grip like a tide taking a shoe."

I thought of the ledger. Of eight names where seven should be. Of mine, arriving in ink like a guest expected.

"It's got me now," I said. "Partly. There's a door with my name on it. There's a room that smells of bread and lavender and has my—there are things of mine in it." I wasn't going to tell him about the mirror-me walking around unsupervised. One confession per conversation. "I need to understand how to give it back. What happens if the cup goes home?"

His finger lifted off the rim for the first time since I'd arrived, and hovered there, and settled again. "I don't know," he said. "Nobody knows. That's the whole problem. Maybe it frees everyone. Maybe it takes the house apart. Maybe—" and here his voice did something I hadn't heard it do yet, cracked down the middle like ice on a pond, "—maybe it forgets them. All seven. Fifty years of holding on, and the moment it gets its name back, it lets go, and everything it was holding falls through its hands and is gone. Not freed. *Gone.* Do you understand? I have spent my entire life unable to put this cup down, and I cannot make myself pick it up and carry it back either, because either way I finally find out what I did in 1974, and both answers are unbearable."

The conservatory was very quiet. Somewhere down the corridor a television laughed at something, tinny and far away, like a party in another life.

"Tell me about that night," I said gently. "Not the house. You. Why did you take it?"

He looked at me for a long moment, and whatever he saw in my face made him decide I'd earned the truth, or at least a piece of it.

"Because I hated it," he said simply. "The house. Everyone loved it—my mother, the village, the seven of them, they all adored Hollowmere, it was the warmest place any of us had ever been, and I was seventeen and suffocating in being *loved* by something with no face. It knew things. It put my shoes by the door before I asked. It warmed my bed. When I was sad it made the kitchen smell of the exact bread my grandmother used to bake, and I never told anyone that, because who tells you that? Who believes it? And I thought—if I take its name, it stops. It becomes just a house. Cold and ordinary and *free*, and so am I." He shook his head slowly. "I didn't understand that a name isn't a leash. It's a spine. I didn't take its freedom. I took its ability to end. Nothing that can't be named can be let go of, Miss Voss. I made it immortal by accident, and immortality made it hungry, and the hunger made it hold on, and everything it loves—" he tapped the cup once, softly, "—stays loved. Forever. Whether it wants to or not."

"That's why it wants me," I said. The words came out of me before I'd vetted them. "It doesn't want to trap me. It wants me to *belong*."

"Yes." He studied me with those double-aged eyes. "And you've been making yourself invisible your whole life, haven't you? I can see it. You sit like someone who learned early that rooms go easier on you if you're furniture." I flinched; he nodded as if I'd confirmed a diagnosis. "So did I, in my way. That's why the house got its hooks in us both. It only ever collects people who were never collected. It's not cruel, Miss Voss. God, I wish it were cruel. Cruelty you can fight. Kindness with no exit—" he spread his hands, indicating the conservatory, the care home, the fifty years, "—that's the trap that fits everyone eventually."

We sat with that. Outside, actual roses struggled along an actual trellis, doing their best to justify the signage.

"Come back with me," I said.

I hadn't planned to say it. It came out the way names come out of the house—unbidden, inevitable. But once it was out, I meant it completely.

Thomas Merrick went white under his tan, whiter than the fogged windows. His hand closed around the cup fully now, knuckles blanching, and he pushed his chair back six inches, which was as far as the wicker allowed.

"No," he said. "No. Absolutely not. You don't—you can't ask me that. If I set foot on that ground it will take my name off the cup and put it on a door. Door number eight. VOSS above me, MERRICK below. It's been saving my place for fifty years—I can feel it saving it, even here, even two counties away, some nights I wake up and I can hear a corridor measuring itself." His breath was coming fast. A nurse passing the door slowed, assessing; he waved her off with a trembling smile that cost him visibly. "I will die in this chair, in this terrible building, drinking this criminal tea, rather than walk through that gate. Do you hear me? I have made my peace with it. I am the only man in England who chose a care home *on purpose*."

"Then help me another way," I said. "Tell me everything. The rules. What it responds to. What it fears. How a name is given and how it's taken back. Because in—" I checked the window, the low gold angle of the light, and felt my stomach drop through the floor, "—in five nights, the harvest moon rises, and I am fairly sure that's a deadline, and I am the only person on earth stupid enough to be standing in the doorway when it comes due."

He stared at me. And then, horribly, he began to laugh—not the awful laugh from before but something rawer, younger, a seventeen-year-old's laugh escaping through a sixty-one-year-old's chest.

"*Stupid enough,*" he repeated, wiping his eyes. "Oh, you'll do. You'll do exactly what I couldn't." He sobered abruptly, and leaned across the little table, and for a moment his finger left the cup entirely, hovering over it between us like a man deciding whether to defuse a bomb or detonate it. "Listen to me. There's one thing I never told anyone, not Mrs. Ellery, not the priests, not the doctors. The night I ran, the house spoke to me. Out loud. One sentence, through the kitchen wall, in a voice like my grandmother's and nothing like my grandmother's. It said—" his throat worked, "—it said, *'Names are not stolen, Thomas. They are given. Bring mine back, and bring yours, and we shall see which of us is the house.'*"

The conservatory seemed to tilt slightly. Behind me, I didn't dare check, but I would have bet anything that the fogged window showed my reflection a half-second late, leaning forward, listening harder than I was.

"And you never told anyone," I managed, "because—"

"Because I've spent fifty years trying to work out," Thomas Merrick said, and his hand came down on the cup like a verdict, "whether it was a threat—or an invitation. And next week, Miss Voss, you're going to find out for both of us."

* * *

I stayed at a pub in the town that night, in a room with no mirror, having requested one specifically and paid extra for the inconvenience. I slept badly anyway. Around three in the morning I woke with the certain, physical knowledge that someone had said my name in the dark—my actual name, *Ada*, spoken kindly, the way you'd call a child in from the garden—and I lay listening to the strange silence of a room with no reflections and understood that it hadn't been the house.

It had been the cup. Forty miles away, in a sock drawer in Rosebank Lodge, silver had remembered me.

In the morning, Thomas was waiting in the conservatory before visiting hours officially began, washed, combed, wearing a tie. On the table in front of him, the cup sat unwrapped, upright, gleaming in the early light like something newly decided.

"I've been thinking," he said, before I'd even sat down. "All night. About doors." He turned the cup slowly, and the engraving caught the sun. "There are nine, you said. Eight names and yours. But there should be ten."

"Ten?"

"Mine was never on a door," he said quietly. "Because I took the house's name before it could finish writing mine. I'm not a resident, Miss Voss. I'm a debt. And debts—" he looked up at me, terrified and resolved, a boy of seventeen wearing the face of an old man, "—debts have to be delivered in person. Book me a taxi for Friday. And don't tell the nurses where we're going."
