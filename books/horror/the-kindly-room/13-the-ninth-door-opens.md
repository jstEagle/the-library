## 13. The Ninth Door Opens

I want it on the record that I tried to be reasonable about the road.

I drove to the county line. I drove past it, in fact, all the way to a service station outside Marlow where a woman sold me coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during the war and a sausage roll that had given up on flavor sometime around 1998. I sat in my car and told myself, out loud, in the tone one uses with a frightened animal or a small child, that houses do not follow people. That roads do not loop. That what I had experienced on the way *out* of Hollowmere was exhaustion, a wrong turn, and possibly a mild aneurysm.

Then I drove back, and the road let me. That was the worst part, I think. Not the looping. The permission. The house had decided I'd had enough time away, and so it opened its hand and let the tarmac run straight again, and I drove along its palm like a coin being carried back to a pocket.

The gate was open. It is always open now. I have begun to suspect it has no hinges at all, that the gate is less a barrier than a gesture, the way a host sweeps an arm toward a drawing room. *After you.*

"Absolutely not," I told it, and went in anyway.

You'll have noticed by now that I say one thing and do another with almost perfect consistency. I've made a study of it. My whole life has been a long negotiation between the sensible thing and the thing I actually do, and the sensible thing has never once won on points. It's why I'm good at my job. A surveyor's entire skill is standing in front of something alarming—a subsiding wall, a roof like a collapsed lung, a cellar full of black water—and writing *noted* in a steady hand. I have spent forty-one years writing *noted* over the top of my own life. It was only a matter of time before a house noticed.

* * *

The hall smelled different when I came in.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. Houses have smells the way people have handwriting. Hollowmere's was dust and cold ash and the green rot of damp plaster, with an undertone, lately, of bread. That had been creeping in for days—warm bread, lavender, the smell of a house that is *kept*, which is a smell no empty house has any business producing. But tonight there was something new under it. Tonight it smelled like my flat.

I don't mean it smelled nice. I mean it smelled like *my flat*. The specific cocktail of it—the lemon polish I buy because it's on offer, the coffee gone stale in the grinder, the faint electrical warmth of the radiator I never bled. That radiator smell. In a house whose boiler had been condemned before the Falklands War. I stood in the hall with my bag still on my shoulder and my keys still in my fist and I thought, with the calm of a woman who has gone somewhere past fear into a kind of administrative horror: *it's been in my flat.*

Or it hadn't. Or it didn't need to be. Both possibilities were bad. I filed them both under *noted*.

The corridor was where I'd left it, which is to say it had grown again. Nine doors now, and I counted them twice, because the first time I got eight and I know how to count; counting is the one thing I have never doubted about myself. Nine doors, each with a small brass plate screwed neatly at eye height, and I did not read the names this time. I have learned that the names read you back. I walked past seven of them with my eyes on the floorboards—good wide boards, Victorian, I could have written a lovely paragraph about them in another life—and stopped at the ninth.

The ninth door was open.

Every other night it had been shut, and I had been grateful, because a shut door is a promise that something can still be refused. This one stood open onto darkness with a strip of warm light lying across the threshold like a tongue.

"Voss," said the brass plate, in small engraved capitals. It had been there for days. But the door had never been open, and an open door with your name on it is a different animal entirely. A shut door with your name on it is a filing cabinet. An open one is an invitation, and I have spent my whole life avoiding invitations, because invitations mean someone is looking at you, and being looked at is the first step toward being *known*, and being known is the first step toward being *kept*.

I stood there for a long time. Long enough that the house, which has the patience of geology, did not so much as creak at me.

Then I went in, because I am, as established, a person who does the other thing.

* * *

The Kindly Room was finished.

That's the only word for it. For days it had been a room in progress—the bed turned down, the lamp with no bulb, the wallpaper peeling in long pale tongues. Now it was *done*, and it was done in the way that made my stomach drop through the floor and keep going, because it was done with my things.

My things. From my flat. Two hundred miles and a looping road away.

My green reading chair sat by the window with the dent in the left arm where I rest my elbow. My bookshelf stood against the wall, sagging slightly in the middle the way it always has, and the books were *my* books, in my order—alphabetical by author, then by date, because I am that kind of person and the house had clearly done its research. My blue mug was on the bedside table. Not a blue mug. *The* blue mug, with the chip on the rim that I keep meaning to throw away and never do, because it's the mug I drank tea from in my grandmother's kitchen the last winter she was alive, and I am a sentimental idiot about exactly one object and this is it.

The bed was made with my duvet cover. The one with the faded stars. There was a water glass on the table, and beside it a book, face-down, spine up, holding a place. I didn't look at which book. I couldn't. Some things you can know or you can function, and I chose function.

"You've been busy," I said to the room, because talking to it had stopped feeling ridiculous somewhere around the fourth night, and because the silence in this house is never empty. Silence here is a held breath. It waits for you to fill it.

The room did not answer, but the lamp came on. Warmly. The way lamps do in rooms where someone loves you.

I sat down on the bed—my bed, my duvet, my exact level of too-soft mattress that no hotel has ever managed and no shop has ever sold me—and I put my head in my hands, and I laughed, or something near it. Because here is the thing nobody tells you about horror: the truly frightening version doesn't come for you with teeth. It comes for you with *comfort*. It learns your mug. It learns which side of the bed you sleep on. It does the work of a hundred years of loneliness in a single afternoon, and then it waits for you to be grateful.

I was so tired of being unremarkable. I want that understood. I chose it, every day, for forty-one years—chose the small jobs, the quiet voice, the seat at the end of the table, the name nobody remembers at parties. Invisibility is a skill and I am a master of it, and masters get lonely in ways that don't have a word. And this room, this warm, bread-smelling, star-duvet room, was looking at me with all that skill and saying: *we see you. We've always seen you. Sit down. Stay.*

It would have worked on me, is the thing. If I'd come here at thirty, it would have worked in a week. If I'd come here at nineteen it would have worked in an hour.

I looked at the water glass. Condensation on the outside. Cold, fresh, poured recently, by no one, for me.

"Noted," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle of the word, and the house was kind enough, or cruel enough, not to mention it.

* * *

It was the mirror that finally did it.

There's a cheval mirror in the corner of the Kindly Room—there wasn't one before; I would have *noted* a mirror—and it stands at an angle, the way you'd position a mirror if you wanted someone to be able to see themselves from the bed. The lag had been getting worse all week. First a flicker, then a half-second, then a full second of the room moving without me, my reflection arriving late to its own life like a commuter missing a train. I had adapted to it the way you adapt to anything: badly, and then completely.

Tonight I got up to leave—because leaving the room, even to walk to the corridor, had become a nightly act of will, a small rebellion I performed like brushing teeth—and I passed the mirror, and my reflection did not pass with me.

It stayed.

I stopped. It didn't. It kept walking, at my pace, along my path, and then it turned—smoothly, without the little hitch of weight a real turn has—and it faced me, and it looked at me the way you look at yourself in a shop window when you're checking your hair. Evaluating. Pleased.

I want to describe this accurately, because I've read accounts of hauntings and they always leave out the texture. The texture was: my own face, my own tired eyes, my own hair doing the thing it does at the left temple, and none of it *mine*, all of it worn, like a coat. The reflection's expression was one I have never made in my life. It was soft. It was *content*. It was the face of a woman who belongs somewhere, and I have never once in forty-one years made that face, and seeing it on my own features was like reading my own obituary written by someone who liked me.

"Hello, Ada," my reflection said.

My voice. Warmer than I use it. I don't sound like that. I sound like a woman apologizing for taking up space in a queue.

"You've had a long day," it said. It stepped closer to the glass from its side. "You should sleep. The house has made supper. It's your favorite."

I don't have a favorite. I eat the same four meals in rotation and have done for two decades, because favorites are preferences and preferences are things people notice about you. I opened my mouth to say so and found I had nothing. Because the terrible thing, the thing I will not write in the survey and will barely admit here, is that for one full second—one whole second, the length the mirrors used to lag—I *wanted* it. Wanted the supper and the star duvet and the soft-voiced woman in the glass who was so obviously glad I existed. Forty-one years of being nobody's, and here was a room that had learned my mug.

Then the reflection reached out and touched the glass from the inside, and the glass gave.

I don't mean it broke. I mean it *gave*, the way the surface of deep water gives, and my reflection stepped through it into the room, one foot and then the other, and stood there on the rug in its stockinged feet—my stockinged feet, my darned heel—and looked at me with an expression of mild apology, the way you look when you've arrived early to your own house and let yourself in.

"You don't have to do anything," it said, and its voice was kind, and kindness from a thing wearing your face is the worst sound I have ever heard. "That's the whole point. You never have to do anything again. We've got you."

"We," I said. My voice came out flat and professional, God help me. Even at the end of the world I reach for the register. "Who is we?"

The reflection-that-was-not-me smiled, and tilted its head, and for just a moment—less than a second, a flicker, a lag running backward—it looked past me, through the open door, down the corridor of nine doors, and its expression changed into something older and wearier and sadder. Something that had been standing in a hall for fifty years, waiting.

"They want to meet you," it said. "They've been so looking forward to it. They're in the hall now. All of them." It glanced toward the window, where the light was going the color of old brass. "Three nights until the moon. They thought you'd want to know."

And it walked past me—close enough that I felt the warmth of it, the bread-and-lavender warmth, my own warmth, borrowed—and out through the door, and down the corridor, and I stood alone in my finished room with my chipped blue mug and my faded stars, listening to footsteps that were exactly my footsteps going away from me, and somewhere below, faint and patient, the front hall began to fill with the sound of people who had been waiting a very long time to say hello.
