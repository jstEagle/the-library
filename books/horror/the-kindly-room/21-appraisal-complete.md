## 21. Appraisal Complete

The county survey form is a miracle of bureaucratic optimism. It assumes, you see, that every house can be described. It gives you boxes: *Year of Construction, Number of Rooms, Structural Condition, Occupancy Status.* It does not give you a box for *the house has opinions about you,* or *one room smells like your grandmother's kitchen even though your grandmother never had a kitchen worth smelling,* or *please note the mirrors run approximately half a second behind, which I have decided to find charming.*

I sat at my kitchen table—the one in my flat, the real one, with its wobbly leg and its ring stain shaped vaguely like Portugal—and filled out the form over three evenings, because some things deserve to be done properly.

*Year of Construction:* 1841, though the foundations argue for older. The cellar walls are stone that predates the brick above them, and there's a hearth in the oldest room that was built to burn something larger than logs. I wrote 1841 because the county would not accept "sometime before anyone thought to write it down," which is the honest answer.

*Number of Rooms:* This one took a while.

The official floor plans say eight. The house, as of the night the harvest moon rose and everything changed, says nine. And then there's the Kindly Room, which appears on no plan drawn by human hands, and which I have come to think of less as a room than as a promise the house made to itself and finally got to keep.

I wrote nine. Then I sat with my pen hovering, and added a footnote in the margin, small enough that a clerk might miss it: *One additional room not accessible by corridor; warm; keeps bread fresh indefinitely; see attached addendum.*

There is no box on the form for that either. There should be.

*Structural Condition:* Sound. Genuinely, boringly sound—roof tight, joists true, damp gone from the north wall as though the house simply decided it had been embarrassing itself long enough. Mrs. Ellery, who came out to look after everything was settled, walked through the ground floor with her hand trailing along the wallpaper like she was greeting old friends, and said, "It's holding itself together for you." She meant it kindly. It still gave me a shiver, but I've decided shivers are allowed now. I've earned them.

*Occupancy Status:* Vacant.

I wrote that word and laughed until I had to put down my tea.

Vacant. As if a house could be vacant after fifty years of keeping seven people safe from itself. As if those seven—Merricks and Ellerys and a gardener named Pell whose name only survived because he'd scratched it into the pantry door sometime around 1961—hadn't stood in the hall on the last night and let go, one by one, each of them fading not into nothing but into something gentler, the way fog lifts rather than dies. As if the house weren't still humming faintly under the floorboards, the way an empty church hums, full of all the voices it has ever held.

Vacant, yes. Fine. Let the county believe what's easiest to file.

*Recommended Action:* Condemnation.

That was the pre-printed suggestion, actually—one of three checkboxes. *Condemn. Demolish. Refer for restoration grant.* I looked at the word *condemn* for a long time. It's such a certain word. Such a tidy word. A word for buildings that have failed, when really it's usually the people who failed the buildings.

Hollowmere never failed anyone. That was the whole tragedy of it. It loved too hard, the way lonely things do—it gathered people in and couldn't bear to let them go, because once, in 1974, a six-year-old boy had carried away its name in a silver cup, and a house without its name doesn't know how to open its hands. It only knows how to hold.

And I understood that. God help me, I understood it better than any surveyor should understand a building.

I left the checkbox blank.

* *

Mrs. Ellery met me at the village archive the following Tuesday, which is to say she was already there when I arrived, sitting in her usual chair by the microfiche machine with two cups of tea poured and a look on her face that said she'd known I'd come before I had.

"You're going to buy it," she said. Not a question.

"I'm going to *look at* the—" I began, and she just raised one eyebrow at me, this tiny birdlike woman who had spent fifty years being the only person in the village willing to say Hollowmere's name out loud, and I stopped pretending. "Yes. I'm going to buy it."

"Good." She pushed a cup toward me. "Because if you didn't, someone else would eventually, and they'd be terrible at it."

"At owning a house?"

"At belonging to one." She wrapped her hands around her own cup. Her brother's name—Robert Ellery, resident number four—was carved somewhere inside me now too, part of the ledger I carry. "There's a difference, Ada. You know there's a difference. You've known it since the first day you counted rooms and felt the house counting back."

She was right, which was annoying, because Mrs. Ellery is right approximately ninety percent of the time and has learned to be gracious about the other ten.

"The council will want a structural survey," she went on. "A proper one. Not whatever you scribbled on that county form."

"I did a very thorough job on that county form."

"Mm. Did you mention the room?"

I sipped my tea. "I mentioned the room."

"In how much detail?"

"In the detail of a professional surveyor protecting her credibility."

Mrs. Ellery smiled, and for a moment she looked young—or not young, exactly, but like the girl she must have been in October 1974, waiting at a gate for a brother who never came home, learning far too early that some doors close in ways that have nothing to do with locks. "Good girl," she said. "Some things aren't for the filing cabinet. Some things are for the people who'll live there."

* *

The purchase went through in November, faster than any property transaction has a legal right to go, and I choose to believe the solicitor's unusual efficiency had nothing to do with the fact that his office window faced east, toward the moor, and that he seemed eager to conclude our business before dusk. I signed. I paid. I received a set of keys that turned out to be decorative, since Hollowmere's front door has been unlocked since the night I named it, standing open to the weather like a dog that has finally been told it may get off its bed.

I closed it anyway, that first evening. Locked it, even, out of habit. Then I unlocked it again and left it on the latch, because a house called *Home* ought to have a door that opens easily, and because I am trying, deliberately, clumsily, to become the kind of person who leaves doors unlocked.

Old habits die hard. Mine died kicking.

Here is what the final survey says—I'm looking at my copy now, the one I keep in the drawer of the desk in the Kindly Room, because where else would a surveyor keep the most important document of her career:

**HOLLOWMERE HOUSE — CONDITION REPORT**
*Surveyor: A. Voss*

- Roof: sound. Slate, original, re-pointed by parties unknown during the week of 12–19 October. I did not re-point it. I have stopped asking.
- Walls: sound. Wallpaper in the east room preserved beneath later layers; children's drawings documented and framed. The boy in the drawings has been identified (see Merrick, Thomas). He visits on Sundays now, in a wheelchair, and sits in the garden while the house makes him toast. He cried the first time. We agreed not to discuss it.
- Windows: sound. All mirrors functional. Note: mirrors lag current reality by approximately half a second. Assessed as cosmetic. Assessed, further, as company.
- Doors: nine. Yes, nine. The ninth is mine. It is currently a door to a room containing a bed, a desk, a kettle, and a view of the moor that makes writing difficult in the best possible way.
- Cellar: dry. Hearth in oldest room: clean, cold, and content. Nothing burns there anymore except ordinary firewood, which is all it ever needed to be given.
- Occupancy: one. Plus, on Sundays, visitors. Plus, permanently, the sense of being expected, which I have decided to count as an amenity.
- Recommended action: none. None whatsoever. Leave it alone. Bring bread.

I filed the official copy with the county, minus the footnotes. The clerk—a young man with the untroubled eyes of someone who has never been followed through a village by the feeling of being welcomed—stamped it without comment. Somewhere in a filing cabinet in the county offices, Hollowmere House is now officially a derelict property in fair condition with an anomalous door count, purchased by a single female applicant of no particular distinction.

Of no particular distinction. I used to build that phrase like a wall around myself. Unremarkable Ada Voss, who moved through other people's houses taking measurements and leaving no fingerprints, who had gotten so good at being invisible that when a hungry house reached for her, it found almost nothing to hold onto—just a name she'd never liked using and a wound she'd hidden from herself.

The house taught me what the wound was. Naming it *Home* taught me how to stop bleeding from it.

You cannot claim a place on purpose if you've spent your whole life practicing disappearance. But you can learn. I'm learning. It turns out belonging isn't something that happens to you, like weather. It's something you do, like surveying—room by room, measurement by measurement, honest line by honest line, until the whole structure stands revealed and you sign your name at the bottom and mean it.

* *

On my first full night as owner—not appraiser, not trespasser, not reluctant guest, *owner*—I cooked supper in the kitchen, for one, and laid the table for one, and then stood there looking at the single place setting and feeling the house's attention settle on me like a hand between the shoulder blades.

"One," I said aloud. "Just me tonight. Don't make it weird."

The kettle, which had been warming itself unbidden, subsided slightly, in the manner of a large animal settling back down.

But here's the thing I've noticed, the thing I haven't told Mrs. Ellery yet, the thing I'm telling this page instead because paper keeps secrets better than people: the house isn't finished wanting. Houses like this don't stop being what they are; they just learn better manners. On quiet nights I feel it reaching—not grasping, not holding, just *reaching*, the way an old dog reaches its nose toward a new person, hopeful and restrained and a little heartbroken about having to ask.

And I've started doing something about it. Small things. I've started inviting people.

Mrs. Ellery, Thursdays, for soup. Thomas Merrick, Sundays, though his care home driver grumbles about the lane. The postman, once, when it rained—he stayed forty minutes and left looking like a man who'd been gently rearranged. Next month I'm going to invite the young couple from the farm down the road, the ones with the baby, because a house that spent fifty years starving for family shouldn't have to wait for holidays to hear a child cry in its halls.

I'm feeding it, you see. Properly, this time. Not with names taken, not with residents kept, but with the thing it was actually hungry for all along, the thing it mistook for captivity because no one ever showed it the voluntary kind:

Company.

* *

Tonight I walked the house before bed, as I do. Nine doors in the long corridor, eight of them plain and shut and sleeping. The ninth stood open, as it always does, spilling lamplight into the dark.

I paused at each of the eight and put my palm flat against the wood, the way you'd check a horse, or a fever, or a friend's forehead. Warm. Quiet. At peace. Whatever the seven are now—released, dispersed, dissolved into the light that filled the hall on the night of the harvest moon—they leave warmth behind them, the way a chair stays warm after someone gets up.

At the ninth door I stopped longer. My door. VOSS, in letters that appeared overnight after the naming and that I have decided not to sand off, because a woman who spent thirty-five years unnamed in every household she passed through has *earned* a door with her name on it, thank you very much, even if she wrote the name herself, in a manner of speaking, with a house for a pen.

Inside, the Kindly Room smelled of bread and lavender, as always. The bed was turned down. The mirror over the dresser caught me in its glass, and I watched myself watch myself, and the reflection came—half a second late, faithful as ever, patient as ever.

"Evening," I said to her.

She said it back, a beat behind, and I swear the lag has gotten warmer lately. Less like a delay and more like consideration. Like someone listening carefully before answering.

I got into bed. I pulled the quilt up. And as I lay there listening to the house breathe around me—that low, settled hum, the sound of a thing that has finally eaten—I heard it, faint and clear, from the direction of the kitchen:

The sound of a second place setting being laid at the table.

Not for a ghost. Not for a resident. The china sounded expectant. Hopeful. The way a table sounds when a house believes, against all its long experience, that tomorrow someone might come to stay.

I lay in the dark and smiled at the ceiling.

"Well," I told the house. "We'd better get more chairs."

Downstairs, softly, a drawer opened, and I could have sworn I heard it counting.
