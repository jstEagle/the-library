## 7. Six Empty Chairs

Isandro had spent twenty years learning how to sit with the dying, and the first thing he'd learned was that nobody dies well in a room that smells of pity. So he dressed the part — soft cardigan, clipboard, the laminated badge from Rosehaven he still carried out of habit — and went door to door through Hollowmere like a man taking a census of grief.

The pretense was thin. He was a hospice outreach nurse, he said, doing follow-up visits for families who'd lost children. It was even true, in its way. Nobody asked why Rosehaven's territory extended two hundred miles past its county line. Grief makes people bad at arithmetic.

He started at the top of the lane and worked down. Six families had lost children in the winter of 1994. Four still had someone living in the house. The other two houses stood with their doors painted over — painted shut, he realized on closer inspection, brushstrokes sealing the gaps like skin over a wound — and he did not knock, because you don't knock on a sealed thing.

* * *

The first house belonged to the Perrys. A woman named Greta Perry opened the door before he finished knocking, as if she'd been standing behind it all morning, waiting for someone to arrive or not arrive.

"You're from the hospice," she said. Not a question. Her eyes went to his badge and then to his face, and something flickered there — recognition, almost, the way you almost recognize a face in a crowd decades later. "You have his hands."

"I'm sorry?"

"Nothing." She stepped back to let him in. "Come in where it's warm. Though it isn't warm. Nothing's been warm here since—"

She didn't finish it. The house was immaculate in the way houses are immaculate when cleaning is the only prayer left. A boy's bedroom preserved behind a closed door, Isandro could tell without being told. A chair at the kitchen table with no one in it, pushed in neatly, kept neat for thirty years.

They sat. He asked his gentle questions — how are you sleeping, are you eating, do you have support — and she answered them the way people answer questions they've answered so long the answers have worn smooth as river stones. And then, mid-sentence, somewhere between *I manage* and *some days are harder than*, she stopped.

Her lips kept moving.

Isandro leaned forward. "...Greta? Mrs. Perry?"

She was counting. He knew the cadence instantly, the way a nurse knows a death rattle — *fourteen, thirteen, twelve* — her voice flat and unhurried, her eyes fixed on the middle distance where nothing was. She wasn't distressed. That was the worst part. She counted the way you breathe.

"Mrs. Perry, can you hear me?"

"*Nine,*" she said. "*Eight.*"

He took her wrist. Pulse steady, sixty-two, a resting heart. Her skin was cold but not clammy. She was not dying. She was *counting*, and when she reached —

"*Three. Two. One.*"

She blinked. Looked at him. Smiled pleasantly, as if resuming a conversation that had never paused.

"—harder than others," she said. "But we manage. Would you like some tea?"

Isandro's hands were not steady. Twenty years of night shifts, and his hands were not steady.

"No tea," he said. "Thank you. I should go."

At the door she caught his sleeve. Her grip was light, apologetic.

"They all do it," she said quietly. "You'll see. We all count now. We just don't say what we're counting toward."

* * *

The second house was the Aldouses'. The third was the Vessicks', where old Mr. Vessick counted backward from *thirty-one* while describing his garden, unbothered, pausing only to blow his nose. The fourth was a trailer at the village edge where a man named Corben counted from *nineteen* and then wept without sound for four minutes afterward, though he couldn't have said why, and neither could Isandro bring himself to ask.

Different numbers. All descending. He wrote them down in his notebook that night, in the rented room above the Drowned Lamb, and looked at the column:

Perry: fourteen.
Vessick: thirty-one.
Corben: nineteen.
Aldous: [she hadn't counted yet]

Four numbers, all falling, none of them reaching zero while he watched. He thought of Mr. Ellery in the counting ward, forty-one, the lights dimming nine seconds, the doors locked twice. He thought about what happened at zero.

He thought: *they aren't sick. They're scheduled.*

* * *

Mrs. Aldous received him in a sitting room dominated by six empty chairs arranged around a cold fireplace. Not one chair. Six. Small chairs, child-sized, wooden, polished with care, set in a half-circle facing the hearth as if an audience waited for a performance that had been cancelled three decades ago.

"You're the nurse," she said. She was small and straight-backed, white-haired, with the fierce economy of a woman who has decided not to fall apart and has held that decision like a plank across open water for thirty years. "Edith Aldous. Sit. Not there." She nodded at the little chairs. "Never there."

He sat in an adult chair by the window. She poured tea without asking, and her hand did not shake, and he understood this was the steadiest person in Hollowmere and possibly the most broken.

"My colleagues sent me," he began. "Aftercare for bereaved—"

"Don't insult us both," Edith said mildly. "You're not from any hospice. You're Isandro Amberdale, Maren's boy, and you've come home looking like a man checking whether his own grave is occupied."

The tea went cold in his mouth. "You know me."

"I knew your mother. I signed my name next to hers." She said it the way you report weather. Then she looked at him, really looked, and whatever she saw in his face made her set down her cup. "You don't know. You actually don't know. God help us, they didn't even tell you."

"Tell me what?"

She was quiet a long moment. Outside, the frost on the window had begun, in the last week, to form in lines — faint, regular, like ruled paper waiting for words. Isandro had seen it on every window in the village. Edith followed his gaze and her jaw tightened.

"The children," she said. "Everyone thinks they died. Drowned, frozen, taken by some madman — the story changes depending on who tells it and how much drink is in them. There was no body, you know. Not one. Six children gone in one winter and not a bone, not a shoe, not a hair ribbon. The police came and found nothing because there was nothing to find."

"What happened to them?"

"They were given away." Her voice didn't waver. That was somehow worse than wavering. "Given. Freely. By their own parents, by name, at the well beyond the churchyard. I gave my Thomas. I stood at that stones' ring and I spoke his name into the dark and I walked home and ate supper and slept, because the tall man said if we didn't, the winter would take the whole village instead. The harvests had failed six years running. People were starving, Isandro. Starving people will sign anything."

Isandro heard his own pulse. "The tall man."

"In the buttonless coat. Polite as Sunday. Never cold, never hungry, always standing just at the edge of the lamplight like he was waiting to be invited and knew he would be." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, the smile of someone remembering kindness. "He was kind. That's the devil of it. He was *kind*. He held my hand while I said the name. He said Thomas wouldn't feel a thing and he was right, because Thomas laughed on the way to the well. Thought it was a game. They all thought it was a game."

"And then?"

"And then nothing. No bodies. No funerals. Empty chairs." She gestured at the half-circle by the hearth. "And every year after, the harvests came back. Fat years. Good years. The village got fat on it, and quiet, and the doors stayed shut, and we raised our remaining children in houses where six small chairs gathered dust, and nobody ever said the word *well* aloud again except at parish meetings, in whispers, with the ledger open."

Isandro set his cup down carefully, because if he didn't he would drop it.

"Mrs. Aldous. Edith. Are the children dead?"

And here Edith Aldous finally broke — not into tears, which he'd braced for, but into something more frightening: a low, urgent laugh, hysterical in its gentleness, pressed flat between her teeth.

"Dead?" she said. "Oh, you sweet, stupid boy. If they were dead, we could bury them. We could grieve and be done and go to our graves ourselves with the account settled. Dead is *finished*. Dead is mercy." She gripped his wrist with shocking strength, her small hand clamping his forearm, and pulled him close enough that he could smell the lavender and underneath it something older, mineral, like wet stone. "The well doesn't kill what it's given. That's the whole horror of it, don't you understand? It doesn't kill. It *keeps*. My Thomas is down there right now — not drowned, not frozen, not rotted — *kept*. Nine years old. Still nine. Still singing that bloody counting song they used to sing skipping rope. Thirty years and he is still nine, and I am eighty-one, and every night I leave the window open a crack in case the cold lets him come up for air, and every morning the sill is frosted on the *inside*."

The room tilted. Isandro held onto the arm of the chair.

"Kept," he repeated. "Held."

"Held. Like money in a bank. Like meat in a larder. Like letters in a drawer nobody will ever post." She released his wrist and sat back, suddenly exhausted, suddenly ancient. "That's why we count, you know. All of us. The ones who signed. We count because the well counts, and when each of us reaches our number — everyone has a different number, mine's seven, seven nights left as far as I can reckon — the well comes to collect the interest. Us. Our names were written too, in a manner of speaking. We bought thirty good years on credit, and credit comes due."

"Then stop it. Refuse. Tear up the ledger."

"You can't tear up a ledger written in frost, love." She almost smiled. "There's one way accounts close early, and it isn't refusal. It's payment. Full payment, freely given, everything owed and then some." Her eyes went to the six small chairs, and softened, and broke at last, properly, tears finally coming, thirty years late. "If someone paid enough — if someone gave *enough* — maybe the books would balance. Maybe what's held would be let go. Maybe my Thomas could come up out of that dark and be nine years old in the sunlight for five minutes before the world remembered he doesn't exist anymore."

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, ashamed.

"But nobody pays, Isandro. Nobody ever pays enough. That's the joke. That's the whole rotten joke. We were cowards in '94 and we're cowards now, and the well knows it, and it waits, patient as frost, because—" 

She stopped. Her lips moved. *Seven,* she mouthed. *Six.*

She caught herself, pressed her knuckles to her mouth, and looked at him with sudden sharp clarity, as if seeing him for the first time.

"Why did you really come back?" she whispered. "Your mother gave your name too. Did you know that? Yours was the last one written. The ninth gift. And yet here you sit, thirty years later, whole and warm and *counting nothing*." Her eyes widened slowly. "Why is that, Isandro? Why does the ledger still show your name outstanding, after all these years? What did you do — or not do — that night at the well?"

Isandro opened his mouth.

And from the six empty chairs by the hearth, in a voice like a child's breath moving over ice, very softly, someone began to sing.

*One for the well and two for the snow,*

Edith went rigid. Isandro turned.

The chairs were empty. The chairs had always been empty.

*Three for the dark where the small ones go —*

The song trailed off unfinished, the way a child stops when called in for supper. In the silence after, the frost on the window finished writing its line, and Isandro read it upside down through his own reflection:

**5**

Edith stared at it. Then at him. Then, terribly, hopefully, at him.

"Five nights," she breathed. "It's never moved that fast before. Not in thirty years." She grabbed his sleeve again. "What did you bring back with you, Isandro Amberdale? What did you wake up?"

Outside, somewhere down the lane, a door that had been painted shut for three decades creaked — once, softly, like something clearing its throat.

Isandro did not stay to find out which one.
