## 14. Counting Backwards Together

It began at the Aldouses', though no one in Hollowmere would ever agree on that.

Mrs. Aldous was washing a single teacup when the numbers came up her throat like water finding a crack. She said "nineteen" out loud to an empty kitchen, and then "eighteen," and her hands kept washing the cup because there was nothing else for them to do. By "fourteen" she had sat down. By "eleven" she had stopped trying to stop.

Across the lane, Tam Prewitt counted from twenty-three while he fed his dog, who would not eat and would not leave the room. In the flat above the Drowned Lamb, the innkeeper counted from thirty-one into his pillow, as if the mattress could swallow the sound. Old Hollis counted from nine on his porch steps with his hat over his face, and when he reached zero he simply started again, because the well, whatever else it was, was patient about its arithmetic.

The counts were not the same number. But they were the same speed — one number every few seconds, unhurried, metronomic — and by ten o'clock that night, anyone standing in the middle of the high street could hear them overlapping through the walls of the village like bells rung slightly out of tune. A chorus of debts, all falling toward the same zero.

Isandro heard it first through the frost.

He had been awake in his rented room, watching the window where the patterns had thickened all day until the glass looked furred, alive, a throat lined with white. The columns of numbers descended there too, legible now even to him, and at 10:14 by his watch the nearest column shed its topmost figure — *forty-one* peeling away like bark — and somewhere down the street a voice said "forty" aloud, muffled by plaster and distance but unmistakable.

Then another voice. Then another.

He dressed without deciding to. Twenty years of night shifts had built a body that moved before the mind consented, and it carried him down the stairs of the Drowned Lamb past the innkeeper's door, behind which came the steady fall of numbers — *twenty-six, twenty-five* — and out into a street lit by streetlights burning at midnight as bright as noon, because Hollowmere had never learned to let its lights rest.

The cold was total. Not painful — total, like being inside something's held breath. The frost on the windows he passed wrote as he walked, columns ticking downward in time with the voices inside, so that the whole village had become one instrument and the well was playing it.

He did what he always did. He went toward the counting.

* * *

The Aldouse kitchen smelled of tea gone cold and lavender polish laid down over older smells, the way grief is lacquered over grief. Mrs. Aldous sat at her table with her hands flat on the wood, counting — *nine, eight* — and her husband stood behind her with both hands on her shoulders as if he could hold the numbers in her body by force.

"Seven," she said, and saw Isandro in the doorway, and her eyes went wet with relief and shame in the same instant. "I can't stop. I've tried biting it back. It comes up like — like being sick."

"I know." He pulled out the chair across from her and sat. "I've sat with forty people who couldn't stop. You're not doing anything wrong."

"What happens at zero?" her husband asked. His voice cracked on *zero*. "What happened at Rosehaven? You've seen it. Tell us what happens."

Isandro thought of Mr. Ellery, of the flatline tone and the nine dark seconds, of the logbook entries in his own handwriting that no one would ever be able to attribute. He thought of the truth, which was that he didn't know what waited here, only that it was worse than a monitor going quiet, and that lying to the dying or their families had never once helped anyone.

"I don't know," he said. "But I know this part. The part where someone stays."

"Six," said Mrs. Aldous.

So he stayed. He took her hand the way he took the hands of the dying — not gripping, just present, a warm weight that said *your body is still yours and I am still here.* Her fingers were freezing. He wrapped both of his around them.

Her husband paced. At "three" he began to weep without sound, which Isandro recognized as the professional cry of a man conserving himself for what came after. At "two" Mrs. Aldous looked at Isandro and said, between numbers, with terrible clarity: "We signed. All of us. We thought names were just words."

"One," she said.

The kitchen lights dimmed. Not flickered — dimmed, evenly, like a lung exhaling, and held there in a brown twilight while the frost on the window finished its column. Isandro counted the seconds against his pulse. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine.

The lights came back.

Mrs. Aldous gasped like a woman surfacing. She stared at her own hands, then at Isandro, then at her husband, and said, "It's still in me. It's wound back up. I can feel the next number waiting." And indeed, faintly, from somewhere beneath her ribs, came: "*thirty-nine.*"

A new count. A new debt. The well had closed one book and opened another.

"It's not collecting," Isandro said quietly, more to himself than to them. "It's *recounting.*"

* * *

He walked the village after that, because there was nothing else a nurse could do at scale except make rounds.

At the Prewitts' door he stood in the cold and listened to Tam count down from nineteen, and when the zero came and the lights dipped and the nine seconds passed, the next count rose in the boy's chest from thirty-five. At old Hollis's porch, Hollis counted from seven, hit zero, and laughed — a dry, awful laugh — before the new number took him. Down the lane, in a house Isandro had never entered, a family he didn't know counted in unison, three voices braided together, parents and child falling toward zero hand in hand, which was either love or arithmetic depending on how you looked at it.

Every house. Every remaining soul. Synchronized without trying, the whole village breathing numbers, and under it all, if you stood very still in the middle of the high street, you could feel the rhythm of it — the well keeping time.

His presence had done this. Odile had told him as much that afternoon, her face gray: *You reopened the account. Debts sleep until someone comes asking questions, and then everything outstanding comes due at once.* He had wanted to argue. Standing in the singing, counting dark, he found he couldn't.

He checked his watch against the frost on the inn's window. The columns there were descending faster than they had that morning. Whatever interval the well observed between numbers, it was shrinking.

* * *

Odile Crane's cottage sat at the end of Chapel Lane, and her light was the only one on the street that was off.

That frightened him more than the counting had. He knocked, softly, the way you knock on a hospice door at three a.m. — an apology made of knuckles.

"Go away," said Odile's voice. Then, underneath it, barely: "*Eleven.*"

He opened the door anyway. Some doors you knock on; some you open, because the person behind them has stopped believing anyone will come.

She was sitting on the floor beside her bed, her back against the frame, her knees drawn up, and she was counting into the dark of her own lap. No lamp. No candle. As if she'd decided that if the well wanted to read her numbers, it could do it in the dark it preferred.

"Eleven," she said. "Ten. Go *away*, Isandro."

He sat down on the floor across from her, cross-legged, close enough that his knee touched hers. In twenty years he had learned that proximity is a language, and that dying people — and people who are being died, which is different — almost never mean it when they send you away. They mean *prove I'm worth staying for.*

"Nine," she said.

"I'm staying," he said.

"Eight." Her head came up. In the light from the window — the frost writing its pale columns, giving off a glow like moonlight through wool — he saw that she had been crying for some time, and had done it efficiently, the way she seemed to do everything. "You don't understand what you're sitting next to. My count started at eleven. Everyone else got twenties, thirties. Do you know why mine is lowest?"

"Tell me."

"Because I owe the most." Seven. Six. "Thirty years of offerings, Isandro. Thirty years of keeping the account *warm*, as the Kindly Man put it, so that my name — my real debt — stayed deferred. And now the account's open, and deferrals are the first thing called in." Five. Four. "I substituted your name for my daughter's. The well knows. The well has *always* known. This isn't a countdown, it's a—"

"Three," said Isandro, with her, because sometimes the dying want company in the counting more than they want silence.

She stared at him.

"Two," they said together.

"One," said Odile alone, and closed her eyes.

The darkness deepened. Not the lights this time — there were no lights — but the dark itself gathered, leaned in, the way a room leans in around a deathbed in the last minute. The frost on Odile's window completed its column and hung there, empty below the final stroke, waiting.

Isandro counted the seconds against his pulse. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine.

And in the ninth second, in the deepest fold of the dark, he heard — or dreamed he heard — a child's voice, small and clear and unaged, coming from everywhere and nowhere, from the direction of the churchyard and from inside his own chest:

*"Mama, it's cold."*

The dark released. The ordinary night resumed, starless and cold and merely cold. Odile opened her eyes and looked at him with an expression he had seen on a hundred faces at a hundred bedsides: the look of someone who has been to the edge of the account and been sent back with the balance unchanged.

"Again?" she whispered.

"Again," he said. "New number?"

She pressed a hand to her sternum, listening inward. When she spoke, her voice was hollowed out. "Twenty-two. It gave me back *more* this time." She laughed, one broken note. "It's toying with us. Or it's — oh God, Isandro, or it's *negotiating.*"

He took her hand. Her fingers were ice; he warmed them the only way hands warm other hands, slowly, stubbornly, an act of faith in biology.

"Then we sit with it," he said. "Whatever it's negotiating, whoever it thinks it's talking to — nobody in this village counts alone tonight. That's the whole of what I know how to do. It's not much."

Odile looked at their joined hands for a long moment. Something moved through her face — guilt, gratitude, and a third thing she swallowed before he could name it.

"It's not nothing," she said finally. "Twenty years of hospice work taught you that?"

"Twenty years of hospice work taught me that everyone counts down," Isandro said. "The only variable is whether someone's holding their hand when they reach the bottom."

They sat on the floor together as her new count fell — *twenty-one, twenty* — and outside, through the frosted glass, the voices of Hollowmere rose and fell in their terrible unison, the whole village a ward now, every house a bed, every soul a patient, and Isandro Amberdale making rounds in the dark, doing the one thing he had ever been good at.

He did not yet see the figure standing at Odile's gate.

Tall. Buttonless coat. Unmoving, patient as frost, keeping an appointment made decades ago — and watching the two of them through the window with what, in anything else, might have been mistaken for tenderness.

When Isandro finally looked up, the gate was empty.

But the frost on Odile's window had begun a new column, and its topmost number was smaller than any it had written yet.

*Eighteen,* it wrote. And then, in letters finer than the rest, as if embarrassed by its own clarity: *and falling faster now.*
