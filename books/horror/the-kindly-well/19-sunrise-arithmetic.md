## 19. Sunrise Arithmetic

The frost finished its work before the sun did.

Odile Crane was standing at her kitchen window when it happened — she had not slept, had not tried to sleep, had spent the night watching the numbers descend on the glass like something being written by a patient invisible hand — and so she saw the last stroke falter. The frost had been writing a four. It stopped. The half-formed numeral hung there in the pane, silver and unfinished, and then, all at once, the entire window went clear. Every window on Crane Lane went clear. She could hear, faintly, from the houses opposite, the small collective sound of people seeing their own breath again and not knowing what to do with it.

The cold remained. But the counting had stopped.

She stood very still. Her hands were around a cup of tea she had not drunk, made at three in the morning because making tea was something hands could do when the rest of a person was busy being afraid. The tea had gone cold hours ago, and she held it anyway, the way you hold a rail.

*Zero,* she thought. *It reached zero, and the village is still here.*

Then she thought of the churchyard, and the rope, and the man who had come back after thirty years to settle a debt that was never his, and she set the cup down so hard it cracked, and she ran.

* * *

The mist in Hollowmere that morning was not ordinary mist. Odile had lived her whole life in villages that made mist, low river valleys that brewed it off the water meadows like porridge, and she knew all its textures. This was different. This rose from the ground rather than settling on it, and it came from one place only: the churchyard, the stones, the ring of worn rocks beyond the east wall where the well stood dry and patient as a held breath.

The villagers were already gathering when she arrived, coming up the lane in ones and twos, drawn the way they had been drawn every night of the past week — by the frost, by the counting, by the terrible arithmetic that had made the whole village into a single held number. Mrs. Aldous was there in her dressing gown. The Tull family, all four of them, standing in a row like fence posts. Old Garrick, who had not left his house in nine years, leaning on a stick at the lychgate. They stood at a distance from the well, the way you stand at a distance from a dog you have kicked.

And in the mist, small shapes were moving.

Odile's heart did not lurch so much as stop entirely, a held organ, a stopped clock. She had spent thirty years imagining this and thirty years training herself not to imagine it, and now that it was happening her mind offered her nothing at all, just white noise and the smell of frost and the sound — faint, thin, unmistakable — of children's voices. Not singing the counting song downward. Singing it upward. *One, two, three,* climbing like birds off a wire.

They came out of the mist in a line, the way they had gone into it, and they were unaged. That was the thing that broke the village's heart in the first ten seconds: they were nine years old. Every one of them. The winter they vanished had kept them like a pressed flower keeps a summer, and here they came out of the white, blinking at the sun as though it were a stranger, in the same coats they had been wearing in 1994, coats that were now thirty years out of fashion and thirty years unrotted.

Wren was first.

Of course Wren was first. Wren had always been first — first down the hill, first into the cold river, first to dare, first to laugh at whoever was slowest. She came out of the mist at a walk and then the walk broke into a run and then Odile's legs were carrying her too, past the lychgate, past the stones, into the churchyard grass still silvered with the vanishing frost, and her daughter — her daughter, her nine-year-old daughter, her daughter who had been nine for thirty years — slammed into her with the full weight of a child, which is to say with the full weight of the world.

"Mama," Wren said, into her coat. "It's not cold anymore."

Odile could not speak. She knelt there on the wet grass and held her daughter and felt the impossible solidity of her, the elbows and shoulder blades and bird-bone wrists of her, real, real, real, and around them the churchyard was erupting. Mrs. Aldous was on her knees with a boy named Tam in her arms, saying his name over and over like a word she'd forgotten the meaning of and was relearning. The Tulls had their girl between them, lifting her, passing her back and forth as though to prove she had weight. Garrick had dropped his stick. Someone was laughing and someone was sobbing and both sounds were coming from the same throats, and above it all the children's counting song went on, upward, unafraid, as if they had never learned the other direction.

It was, Odile would think later, the only morning of her life that was entirely perfect, and it lasted four minutes.

Because in the fifth minute, Mrs. Aldous — still holding Tam, still rocking him — lifted her head and looked past Odile's shoulder and said, in a voice raw with weeping and gratitude:

"Where is he? Where's the nurse? The one who came back for them?"

* * *

The question moved through the churchyard like a draft through a house.

They looked for him the way you look for a person you have wronged, which is to say they looked everywhere at once and nowhere properly. Isandro Amberdale had been standing at the well's mouth not twelve hours ago — Odile had seen him there, had watched him speak his name into the dark in a voice as calm as a man reading a bedtime story, had watched him swing his legs over the worn stones and lower himself down like a man getting into bed. The villagers had watched too, some of them with rope in their hands, all of them ashamed of the rope now, and none of them had moved to stop him, and he had gone down into the dry dark singing the counting song upward, and the mist had risen, and the children had come out.

He should have been there. That was the arithmetic every villager was doing in those first confused minutes: children out, so the man who bought them out must be standing somewhere, must be leaning on the churchyard wall, must be walking up the lane for a well-earned sleep. Someone said he was probably at the inn. Someone said he'd probably gone home to Rosehaven. Someone said — this was Garrick, quietly, and the words fell into the crowd like a stone into still water — *someone said the well doesn't give change.*

Odile left Wren with Mrs. Aldous — Wren, who did not want to be left, who clung and then let go because she was nine and obedient and did not know yet what she was letting her mother walk toward — and went down the lane to the Drowned Lamb.

The inn was empty at that hour, the bar stools up, the smell of old smoke and beer in the air. She went behind the bar without asking. She knew where the ledger was hidden; she had helped put it there, thirty years and a lifetime ago. She pulled it out and set it on the bar and opened it to the winter pages, and there it all was, the gifts, the livestock, the heirlooms, the names, six names in a column in six different hands, and beneath them, dated that final winter, a seventh entry in a hand she did not recognize at first and then recognized with a pain like a swallowed nail: *Isandro Amberdale. Son of Maren.*

The entry was still there. That was the first wrong thing. She had expected — she did not know what she had expected — but the name remained, ink and paper, unblotted.

The second wrong thing was the ink itself. She touched it, and it smeared. Not faded. *Smeared*, wet, as though the page had been written a moment ago and had not yet dried, and as she watched, the letters softened, ran, and slid off the page like water off glass, leaving nothing behind but the faintest shadow of a shadow, the ghost of a ghost, a place where a name had been and would not be again.

She turned the pages back. The mother's entry — Maren's entry, the smudged-out seventh name, the *Forgive me. I could not finish it either* — was gone too. The paper there was blank, but not the blank of never-written. It was the blank of unwritten, and Odile had been a parish councilwoman for long enough to know the difference, and she stood behind the bar of the Drowned Lamb with her hands shaking and understood that the well had closed its books, and a closed book keeps no names at all.

* * *

They searched, of course. Villages search; it is one of the things villages are for.

They went to the lane where his car had been parked, the hired car with the Rosehaven hospice sticker fading on the windscreen, and the lane was empty. Not empty-of-car. Empty. There was no oil stain on the gravel, no flattened grass at the verge where the tyres had rested, no mark at all, as though the car had never been. Garrick, who had lived opposite and complained about the parking every day of the past week, stood in the middle of the road turning in a slow circle and said, "I never complained about anything. Did I? I don't remember complaining about anything."

They went to his room at the inn. The innkeeper unlocked it with the master key and stood aside, and the room beyond the door was a storeroom. It had always been a storeroom; the innkeeper was sure of it, said so with the confidence of a man stating the weather. Dust sheets over furniture. Boxes of the inn's old stock. A window that had not been cleaned in years. There was no bed, no suitcase, no hospice badge, no folded coat on a chair. The innkeeper laughed, a little uneasily, and said someone must be having a joke, and then stopped laughing because no one was laughing with him.

Mrs. Aldous had a photograph. She had taken it on her phone, four days ago, of the stranger in the churchyard with his notebook, asking his gentle hospice-nurse questions, and she pulled the phone out with the certainty of a woman producing evidence and found the photograph blank. Not deleted — she had not deleted it, she had never deleted anything in her life. The image was there in the gallery, a rectangle of grey, a photograph of nothing, taken from a height that suggested a person had stood in the frame and now did not. She scrolled. Every photograph of him was the same grey. Every photograph of him was blank paper.

And the logbooks — Odile thought of this later, standing in the churchyard while the village celebrated around her and could not celebrate — the logbooks he had carried, the notes in that careful slanted hand, the questions written down about counts and ledgers and winters. She had seen them. Everyone had seen them. And now no one could say whose handwriting it was. The hand was familiar the way a voice on the wind is familiar. It was a hand that had written things, and the things remained, and the writer had been subtracted from the world so cleanly that even his own penmanship had become anonymous.

By mid-morning the village had done what villages do with unbearable things, which is to fold them away into the same drawer as the six empty chairs and the painted-over doors and the tall man in the buttonless coat. The children were back. The children were back, and they were hungry, and they were nine, and there were beds to warm and doctors to phone and a hundred small miracles of ordinary life to perform, and the human heart is a muscle that will always choose the lifting it can manage. One by one, the villagers drifted home with their children, and the churchyard emptied, and the mist burned off, and the well stood dry and silent among its worn stones, its books closed, its accounts settled.

Odile did not drift home. Odile stood at the well's edge with Wren's hand in hers — Wren, who kept asking questions in her small clear voice: *Mama, who are we thanking? Mama, everyone keeps saying thank you, who is it for?* — and Odile looked down into the dark and understood the price the way she had never understood anything in her life.

There had been a man. She could feel the shape of the absence of him, the way you feel a tooth that is no longer there. She remembered a kindness. She remembered someone holding her hand while her count ran down to eleven, to nine, to the edge of zero, someone whose voice was steady and whose hands were warm and who stayed, the way the good ones stay. She remembered being told the truth she had spent thirty years avoiding, and she remembered that it had not felt like a blow. It had felt like a door opening.

But his face — she reached for the face and the face was water. His name — she reached for the name and the name slid off her mind like frost off a warming pane. There had been a ninth gift, an unfinished one, a son of a woman named Maren, and he had climbed down into the dark so that her daughter could climb out into the light, and the well had taken his payment in the only coin it accepted: everything. Not his life. His *having been.*

"His name was—" she said aloud, to the well, to the morning, to no one, and the sentence ended where the name should have been, a door opening onto a wall.

Wren tugged her hand. "Mama? Are you sad?"

Odile looked down at her daughter — her warm, breathing, impossibly returned daughter — and found that she was both things at once, entirely and without contradiction, the way the well itself was both kind and terrible. "Yes," she said. "And no."

"Both?"

"Both," Odile said. "Come away from the stones, love. They're worn smooth from being held, and we've held them long enough."

They walked out of the churchyard together, and behind them the mist was gone, and the frost was gone, and the numbers were gone, and the doors of Hollowmere — all of them, every painted-shut door on every lane — stood open for the first time in thirty years, and no one in the village could have told you why.

* * *

That evening, in the kitchen on Crane Lane, Odile set the table for two and then, out of a habit with no root and no name she could find, set a third cup at the third place, and stood looking at it for a long moment before taking it down again.

Wren, at the table, eating her first proper meal in thirty years and tasting nothing strange in it, looked up. "Who was the third cup for, Mama?"

"I don't know," Odile said. And it was the truth, the whole truth, the complete and terrible arithmetic of it: she did not know, she would never know, and some part of her — some part that kept setting cups and waking at dawn with grief in her throat and an objectless ache where a man used to be — would spend the rest of her life not knowing.

She looked out the window at the darkening lane, at the village with its open doors, at the churchyard wall just visible beyond the houses, and she thought she saw, for half a heartbeat, at the far edge of the lamplight, a tall figure in a buttonless coat, standing patient as a held breath.

She blinked, and it was gone.

But the frost, when it came that night — the ordinary frost, the harmless frost, the frost that only makes patterns — wrote nothing on her window at all.

Except, in the very corner, small as a signature: a single number. Not a countdown.

A one.

Patient. Unhurried.

Waiting.
