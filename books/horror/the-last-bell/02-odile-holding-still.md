## 2. Odile, Holding Still

The woman was already in the house when I let myself in at eight a.m., which should have been my first clue that Hollowmere did not operate on the same rules as other properties.

She sat in the front parlor in a wingback chair positioned precisely where the morning light would strike her hands and nothing else, as if she'd calculated the angle the way I'd calculate a setback line. Ninety-one years old, if the estate file was accurate, and she looked like every year of it—skin like tissue paper over wire—but her eyes were the sharpest thing in the building. She watched me come through the door the way a hawk watches a field mouse that has made the mistake of pausing in open ground.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm early," I said. "My appointment is at nine."

"The house expected you at eight." She folded her hands. "It's been expecting you for some time."

I checked my phone out of habit—8:04—and decided not to have that conversation. "You must be Mrs. Ferrar."

"Odile." She said it like a correction of something larger than a name. "Mrs. Ferrar was my mother-in-law, and she was a horror. You'll want to measure everything yourself?"

"That's standard practice."

"Then you'll want me present for all of it."

"Also standard," I said. "Owners usually prefer to know what's being documented."

"No." She unfolded her hands and refolded them, and I noticed they were perfectly still between foldings—not fidgeting, not resting, but *held*, the way you hold a door shut against wind. "Not to know what's being documented. To make sure you document *all* of it. Every room. Every wall. Nothing skipped because it seemed unremarkable, nothing omitted because your instruments disagree with your eyes." Her gaze came up and pinned me. "Especially then."

I've appraised four hundred and eleven properties in eleven years. I have a script for eccentric owners. It involves nodding, agreeing, and doing exactly what I was going to do anyway.

"Every room," I said. "Every wall."

Something in her shoulders loosened by a millimeter. She nodded once, like a judge accepting a plea.

* * *

Her name was Odile Ferrar, and according to the file she had lived at Hollowmere House alone since 1974. The file said this without comment, the way files do, but I did the math twice: fifty years alone in a fourteen-thousand-square-foot colonial with no staff listed after 1976 and no visitors logged on any utility account since 1989. Fifty years of holding a house the size of a small hotel by herself.

Unless, I thought, watching her walk ahead of me down the east corridor at a pace that suggested she knew exactly how long each room would take, she hadn't been holding it alone.

She carried a cane she didn't use. She tapped it against each threshold as we crossed—one clean knock, like a court reporter's pen hitting paper—and waited until I'd finished my laser readings before moving to the next room. She never spoke during a measurement. That was the strangest part, at first. Most owners narrate. They tell you which walls are original, which floors were replaced after the fire of whatever-year, which ghost their grandmother saw. Odile held still and silent as a photograph while I worked, and only when I lowered the device did she tap her cane and move.

By the third room I started talking anyway, because silence plus her stare was starting to feel like an interrogation conducted in reverse.

"Load-bearing masonry throughout the first floor," I said, pinging the parlor wall. "Original plaster above. The foundation stone is local—I'd say quarried within five miles, probably before 1740."

"It was quarried from the riverbed," Odile said. "The founders pulled it up themselves. Eleven of them. They wanted the house to sit on stone that had never been part of anything else."

"Sentimental."

"Deliberate." She said it flatly, and I wrote *foundation: local stone, pre-1740* and underlined *deliberate* without knowing why.

The rooms were magnificent and wrong. I don't mean wrong the way chapter one of my report will eventually imply—I didn't understand yet what kind of wrong. I mean the ordinary appraisal-kind of wrong: proportions that didn't match the exterior footprint, window counts that varied between the north elevation and the interior plans, ceiling heights that stepped up half an inch between adjacent rooms for no structural reason anyone sane would choose. The house felt like a document that had been edited by several hands across centuries, each hand adding clauses nobody had agreed to.

"Your measurements won't sum," Odile said, when we reached the second-floor landing.

I stopped mid-stride. "Excuse me?"

"The rooms. When you add them together, the total will be larger than the parts. It always is. Don't waste a day trying to find the error. There isn't one." She tapped her cane on the landing boards. "There's just more house than house."

I laughed. I'm not proud of it—it came out too quick, the laugh you give a client who says something impossible to keep the appointment moving. But she didn't smile, and my laugh died in the hallway's cold air, and I wrote in my notes, in the margin where I put things I intend to delete later: *she knows about the discrepancy.*

Which meant she'd read my preliminary figures. Which meant someone had sent them to her. Which meant—

Or it meant she'd measured the house herself, once, decades ago, and gotten the same impossible arithmetic, and had spent fifty years sitting with a number that wouldn't close.

I preferred the second explanation. I noted that preference, too.

* * *

At noon she fed me.

I want to be clear that this was not hospitality. There was no offer, no warmth, no tea-and-crumpets performance of gracious decay. At 12:00 exactly she stood up in the kitchen—which was a museum piece, copper pots hanging over a hearth-sized fireplace, a modern refrigerator humming apologetically in a corner alcove like a guest who'd overstayed—and said, "You'll eat now," and set bread, cheese, and apples on the table in three movements, and sat across from me with nothing for herself, and watched me eat.

"Do you mind if I ask—" I began.

"Yes," she said. "But ask anyway. That's why you're here."

"Why are *you* here? The listing says the estate wants a full appraisal for probate. You're the last resident. Technically you're the asset."

"I'm aware of what I am." She turned her water glass a quarter rotation, precise as a dial. "I'm here because the last appraiser measured alone, and his report was forty pages short, and he left in the night and drove until he hit a tree outside Poughkeepsie, and the report was the only part of him that survived intact. Forty pages describing a house that doesn't exist. Rooms that aren't there. A staircase on the west side that was removed in 1911." Her eyes came up. "He measured what the house showed him instead of what the house is. I intend to prevent that."

"And how does your presence prevent it?"

"Because the house lies differently when it's observed. Like anything else." She said it the way you'd say *water is wet*, and I felt the first real cold of the day go down my spine, because she wasn't speaking metaphorically. I could hear it. Somewhere in her syntax there was a literalness so complete it circled back around to madness or truth, and I couldn't yet tell which.

"Mrs.—Odile. With respect. What do you think this house *is*?"

She considered me for a long moment. Outside, wind moved through the elms, and the light through the kitchen window shifted, and I remember thinking later that the light moved *wrong*, that it slid across the floor like something being dragged rather than something falling—but at the time I filed it under fatigue and ate my apple.

"I think," she said, "that you are the fourth appraiser to sit at this table, and the first one I've asked to stay past noon."

That stopped me. "Why?"

"Because of your hands."

"My hands."

"You write everything down. Everything. The man before you photographed; the woman before him recorded audio; the first one trusted his memory. You *write*. Pen on paper, constantly, even when the device is running. You don't trust devices, do you?" She tilted her head. "You trust the record. You believe that what is written down is safe."

I opened my mouth to deny it and found nothing there. She was right. I photograph everything and then I transcribe everything, because photographs can corrupt and recordings can fail, but ink on archival paper is the closest thing our profession has to permanence. I'd never told anyone that. I'd barely admitted it to myself.

"What did you survive?"

The question landed like a dropped tool. Not *where are you from*, not *how long have you been in the business*. *What did you survive.*

"That's not a standard intake question," I managed.

"Nothing about today is standard." She leaned forward, and for the first time I saw something underneath the flint—something tired and enormous, like bedrock showing through a lawn. "Answer it or don't. But know that the house will ask the same question, and it will not accept 'don't.'"

"There was an accident," I heard myself say, and had no idea why I was saying it. "Years ago. I don't—"

I stopped. Here is the strange thing, the thing I have written and rewritten and finally left in this account exactly as it happened: I went to answer her question, and the answer wasn't there. Not suppressed, not walled off, not the aching shape of a thing refused. Just—a smooth place. A page in my memory with nothing printed on it, the way a form looks before it's filled in. I knew there had been an accident the way you know a fact from a file. I could not access a single second of it.

"I don't remember," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I intended. "Isn't that strange. I know there was one. I can't remember it."

Odile closed her eyes. When she opened them again, they were wet, and furious, and aimed somewhere over my shoulder—at the doorway, at the hall beyond it, at the whole patient architecture of the house around us.

"Fifty years," she said softly. "Fifty years, and it's already started on you. You've been here four hours."

"Started what?"

She stood. The motion cost her—you could see the ninety-one years reassert themselves, the joints, the weight—but her voice didn't shake at all.

"Finish your lunch, Mr. Sandoval. Then we're going upstairs. There's a hallway on the third floor with seven mirrors, and I need you to do something for me."

"What?"

"Stand in front of one," she said, "and raise your right hand."

* * *

The third-floor hallway ran the length of the house, narrow, lit by two windows at either end, and lined—she hadn't exaggerated—with seven mirrors in frames of blackened walnut, each one taller than I am. Dust sheeted everything except the mirrors. The glass was immaculate. In a house where every surface wore its half-century of gray, seven rectangles of perfect clarity is not cleanliness. It's maintenance.

"Why do you want me to—"

"Hold still," Odile said. "And watch yourself. Not me. Yourself."

I raised my right hand.

The man in the mirror raised his right hand.

I lowered it. He lowered it.

"Again," Odile said. "Slower."

I raised my hand, slowly, watching the glass. And there—at the top of the motion, at the moment my fingers went still above my head—the reflection kept rising. A fraction. A stutter of movement that arrived after mine had finished, like an echo coming back from a wall farther away than the one in front of me.

I stood very still in the cold hallway, arm up, heart beginning a slow professional climb toward panic, and I did what I always do with an anomaly.

I timed it.

I took out my phone, opened the stopwatch, and raised my hand on a count of three. The reflection followed. Late. I did it ten times. Ten trials, ten lags, and when I averaged them in my head with the part of my brain that computes square footage without effort, the number came out consistent and impossible:

One second. The man in the mirror was one second behind me.

Behind me. Not ahead. Not independent. *Behind.* As though the glass weren't showing me at all—as though it were playing me back, from a recording, with a delay.

Odile watched my face do the arithmetic. She didn't look surprised. She looked like a woman standing at a graveside she'd dug herself.

"How long," I said. My voice sounded fine. Professional. I was proud of it. "How long has it been one second?"

"This morning it was a quarter," she said. "Yesterday, when the cousin visited, it was nothing at all. The lag isn't the house malfunctioning, Mr. Sandoval." She laid her still, papery hand on my sleeve, gentle as a bandage going on a wound. "The lag is the house *reaching* for you. It grows as it learns you. By the time it shows you things you haven't done yet—"

She stopped herself. Closed her mouth on the rest of it like a door.

"Things I haven't done yet," I repeated.

"Finish the measurements downstairs," Odile said, turning back toward the stairs, cane tapping the boards, one knock per step, all the way down into the dark of the house. "Do it thoroughly. Document everything."

"Everything except the mirrors?"

She paused on the landing without looking back.

"Especially the mirrors," she said. "Because tomorrow, one of them is going to show you something you haven't done. And I need to know, before it does, whether you're the kind of man who writes down what frightens him—or the kind who stops writing."

I stood alone in the hallway with seven perfect glasses and my own delayed face, and I realized I didn't know the answer either.
