## 17. The Unmaking of Marcus

I heard him before I saw him. That was the wrong order for things to happen in Hollowmere. In that house, you always saw things first—saw them a second early or a second late—and then the sound arrived like an apology.

But Marcus was humming. A low, contented hum, the kind a man makes when he's just closed a deal, and it came from behind the pantry wall, from the room that wasn't in any survey, and I stood in the dark of the kitchen with my flashlight off and understood that I had never once heard the house make a sound that wasn't mine.

The pantry door was open. It had been bricked-over-looking, painted shut, a door that answered every knock with silence, and now it swung inward on hinges that gleamed like they'd been oiled that morning. The house wanted me to follow. Or Marcus did. By then I couldn't have told you which.

I went in with the flashlight on, because whatever else I'd become in nine days at Hollowmere, I was still an appraiser, and appraisers document.

The study was exactly as I'd left it after finding it—the ledgers floor to ceiling, eleven founders' worth of handwriting climbing the walls like ivy made of ink—but the air had changed. It was warmer. Not comfortably warm. Warm the way a mouth is warm.

Marcus sat at the founders' desk with his back to me, and he was still smiling. I could hear the smile in the hum.

"Mr. Ferrar," I said. "Marcus."

He turned, and the smile was real, wide, untroubled, and it was also wrong, because half of his face was no longer entirely face. The right side of him—from temple to jaw, down into the collar of his expensive shirt—had gone soft-edged, blurred, like a photograph someone had smudged with a thumb before the ink dried. His ear was a suggestion. His hairline faded into the paper-gray air of the room as if he were being developed out of existence, one chemical bath at a time.

"You should see the numbers," he said. His voice had a paper texture to it, a dry rustle underneath the words. "Odile's estate, once we clear probate without her signature holding things up—Xanthe, do you know what this property is *worth*? Once it's documented properly? Once someone finally writes it all down?"

"I know what it costs," I said.

"That's not an answer." He laughed, and the laugh came out slightly ahead of his mouth moving. I watched it happen. The sound arrived, then the lips parted around it, a beat behind, and something in my chest folded over on itself, because I knew that lag. I'd been living inside that lag for days. The house had learned it from me, or I from it, and now it was eating him at the speed of a metronome.

"Marcus," I said, and crouched to bring myself level with him, the way you talk to a man on a ledge. "Look at your hands."

He looked. His right hand lay flat on the desk, and the fingers were legible. That's the only word I have. There were lines across them—not creases, lines of script, tiny and precise, running along each finger like the grain in wood. As I watched, a word surfaced on the back of his hand and sank again: *consideration*, in a hand I recognized as the codicil's hand, the hand that wasn't any of theirs.

"That's new," Marcus said, delighted. "That started this morning." He flexed the fingers and the word dissolved. "It doesn't hurt. You'd think it would hurt, but it's more like—" He searched for it. "Like when you've been carrying a heavy bag all day and someone takes it. Like being *handled*."

"It's writing you," I said. "You're not signing the ledger anymore. You're becoming a page."

"Well." He turned back to the ledger open in front of him, and I saw then that it was thick, impossibly thick, thicker than Odile's, though he'd been in the house less than two days. Pages upon pages of his life, in the founders' hand: the prep school, the first divorce, the offshore accounts, the way he'd practiced his mother's signature at fourteen until he could forge it better than she could write it. Every entry ended in a number. Every number was large. The house had read him fast because there hadn't been much else to read.

"Here's the thing," Marcus said conversationally, dipping a pen—I hadn't seen him pick up a pen; the pen was simply in his hand, black, wet-tipped—"here's the thing nobody understands about assets. An asset is anything that can be written down. That's it. That's the whole secret. If it can be set down on paper, it has value. And if it has value—" he tapped the ledger, and the tap sounded like a knock on a door, from the other side, "—it can be *transferred*."

"What did you sign?"

"The closing documents." He said it brightly, proudly. "All of them. Odile's been dithering for fifty years, poor thing, and the buyers want clean title, so I—well. She's ninety-one. Her hand isn't steady. I helped." He said *helped* the way other men say *hero*. "The house took it. Did you feel it take it? This morning, around six? The whole place went—"

"Warm," I said. "I felt it."

"The mirrors cleared up too. Showed me everything right on time. First time all week I've looked normal in a mirror." He gestured at his own dissolving profile with the pen. "Well. Normal-ish."

"Marcus, listen to me." I put my hand over the ledger, over the wet line of ink he was laying down, and the paper flinched under my palm—a small muscular shiver, like skin. "When the last page fills, there won't be an estate to sell. There won't be a buyer. There won't be a town. The house is detaching. You didn't close a sale, you cut a mooring line."

For one moment—one flicker, gone almost before it came—something moved behind his eyes that wasn't greed. It might have been fear. It might have been the memory of fear, filed away with everything else the house had already taken from him, a specimen pinned under glass.

Then he smiled wider, and the smile cost him. I watched the right corner of his mouth drag a half-second late, pulling against a face that was forgetting how to be pulled.

"You're jealous," he said gently, pityingly, and God help me, that was the worst thing yet—to be pitied by a man mid-dissolution, to be looked at with compassion by someone the house was drinking through a straw. "You found this room first. You read the ledgers. You could have signed years' worth of value into your name and you just—*catalogued* it. Measured it. Took photographs." He shook his head, and the shake rippled oddly across his blurring cheek. "Some people look at a gold mine and count the rocks."

"Your face is coming off," I told him. "In ink. Look at what it's writing on you. *Consideration.* That's a contract term, Marcus. You're not the signatory anymore. You're the consideration. You're what's being paid."

And he looked—at his hand, at the words surfacing and sinking, at the soft ruin of his own reflection in the brass lamp beside him—and for three full seconds he was silent, and in that silence I heard the house breathe. Not metaphorically. The walls of the study expanded and contracted, slow, patient, the respiration of a thing digesting.

"Nineteen million," Marcus said at last, softly, wonderingly. "That's what it wrote me down as. Nineteen million and change. Do you know how long I've been worth that on paper?" He laughed again, and this time the laugh came out of the ledger instead of his throat. "Nobody ever got it right before. My ex-wives lowballed me. The IRS lowballed me. But this house—" he laid his smudging hand flat on the open page, tenderly, the way you'd touch a sleeping child, "—this house *knows my value*."

I stepped back. There was nothing left to say to him. That's the truth of it, and I've had time to make peace with the truth of it: there are people you can pull out of a fire and people who will fight you for the right to burn, and Marcus had already signed himself into the flame and called it warmth.

At the door I stopped, because he said my name.

"Xanthe." The paper-rustle voice. "It asked about you."

I turned. "What did?"

"The house. While it was writing me. Between entries, it pauses—it thinks—and this morning it paused longer than usual, and I felt it wondering." He touched his temple, where the blur was thickest. "It wants to know if you'll pay the third price. It's been waiting fifty years for someone to ask it that question back."

I didn't answer. I walked out through the pantry, and behind me the humming resumed, contented, and under the humming, faintly, the scratch of a pen that never needed dipping.

* * *

Odile was dying in the front parlor, in a wingback chair positioned precisely where the afternoon light used to fall, back when the house let light be light. She had arranged herself for it the way she arranged everything—deliberately, defiantly, chin up—as if death were an inspector she intended to receive with dignity and a complete file.

She was smaller than when I'd met her nine days ago. Days had been doing decades' work on her since Marcus forged the signatures. Her hands in her lap were translucent at the fingertips, and I could see the beginnings of script beneath the skin of her wrists, faint as old scars: the house reclaiming its collateral, calling in a loan fifty years deferred.

"You found him," she said. Not a question. She'd heard the humming too, probably. The whole house probably hummed now.

"He's happy," I said. "That's the part I can't get past. He's dissolving into a ledger and he's *happy*, because it valued him correctly."

"Money is just memory that agreed to be counted," Odile said. "He spent his whole life turning himself into numbers. The house only finished the job." She coughed, a dry papery sound, and I knelt by the chair and hated how natural kneeling beside her had become, how quickly I'd gone from appraiser to deathbed attendant to—whatever I was now. Whatever the mirrors were drafting me to be.

"Can he be saved?"

"No." No hesitation, no comfort. Fifty years had sanded all the false mercy off her. "Once the pages start filling faster than the man can live them, there's no getting between them. I've seen it before. Not often. Twice." Her eyes found mine, sharp as a deed restriction even now. "Both times, the person smiled. Remember that, when you're tempted to measure a man's danger by his suffering. The house doesn't take the ones who suffer. It takes the ones who *agree*."

"Then why hasn't it taken you?"

Her mouth twitched. "Because I never agree. Fifty-one years of disagreeing, dear. It's exhausting work, but I recommend it." Then the sharpness went out of her all at once, like a tide withdrawing, and what was left was very old and very tired and, for the first time since I'd known her, frightened. "Xanthe. Come closer. I haven't got the breath for speeches."

I leaned in. She smelled of lavender and ink.

"Marcus signed the closing papers in my name. The house accepted the forgery—that's the obscenity of it, it *accepted*—because intent doesn't matter to a ledger. Only marks matter. Only what's set down." Her hand rose, trembling, and gripped my wrist with a strength that startled me. "Which means the ritual stands unfinished. Which means the hunger is loose in the house with no vessel to hold it, and it will go through this town like fire through a hayfield unless someone completes what I could not."

"The ritual needs a vessel," I said. "You said—you trapped it in yourself. Someone has to—"

"Someone has to pay the third price." Her grip tightened. "Witness. Record. Relinquish. The founders wrote it plainly enough, and every one of them flinched at the third act, and their flinching is why the thing got loose in the first place. Power over the ledger was always the ledger's power over them. They thought they were building a cage and they were building a mouth." She drew a breath that whistled. "But the ritual doesn't require a Ferrar. It doesn't require blood or birthright or any of the nonsense I hid behind for half a century. It requires a witness willing to give up the worst thing they carry. Anyone. Anyone at all."

"Odile—"

"*Anyone,*" she repeated, and her eyes bored into mine, and I understood that she knew. Of course she knew. She'd been asking me strange questions since my first hour in her house—what have you survived, what do you refuse to remember—and she had read my answers the way the house read its ledgers, completely. "You have ten seconds, Xanthe Sandoval. I've watched you circle them for nine days. Ten seconds you built your whole careful documented measured life on top of, the way men build houses over sinkholes. The house has been counting them down in its mirrors. It knows. I know. And the only question left—the only one that matters, the one I've been dying slowly for fifty years rather than answer—is whether you'll pay it."

Outside, the light was going strange. I could see it through the parlor windows: not dusk, nothing so innocent, but the glass showing streets that weren't there, lamplight on cobblestones from some century the town had forgotten on purpose, the mirrors of Hollowmere House filling up with everywhere except here. The house was stretching. Testing its new reach. Somewhere upstairs, a bell that no one had installed hung silent in its cradle, waiting.

"If I don't," I said quietly, "the town pays. Again. Another 1974. Another generation of gaps."

"If you don't, everyone pays forever," Odile said. "If you do, only you pay once." She released my wrist and settled back into the chair, and the translucence had reached her knuckles now, script swimming pale beneath the skin like fish under ice. "I won't dress it up. It is the worst trade you will ever be offered, and it is also the only honest one, because at least you'll know the price going in. Nobody told me at twenty-five. I had to find out at the threshold, and I flinched, and my flinch has been feeding on this town for half a century." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Don't be me. Being me is the punishment."

"Odile." I took her translucent hand, careful, the way you hold a page that might tear. "What happens to you? If the ritual completes?"

Something crossed her face that I had never seen there in nine days of watching her hold still against the end of the world.

Relief.

"Then I'm just a woman who dies," she whispered. "Only that. Only everything."

The house sighed around us, walls swelling, and upstairs the uninstalled bell stirred in its cradle with a sound like a held breath, and in the hidden study behind the pantry a pen scratched on, filling pages, counting down a man who had never once in his life refused to sign.

I stood up. My legs knew the way to the pantry door before I did.

Ten seconds. All I had to do was give up ten seconds.

I had been carrying them for eleven years, and I had no idea how heavy they were until I reached for the door and found my hand wouldn't stop shaking.
