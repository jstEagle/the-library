## 18. Witness

The codicil's third page had been the hardest to read, not because the ink was faded but because it was so plain. *First, witness. The vessel must see what has been taken and say it aloud, that the ledger may hold it as seen and not as kept.* No incantation. No blood. Just an instruction to walk through Hollowmere House and name what lived in its walls.

Xanthe stood in the front hall at dawn with Odile's wheelchair beside them and a legal pad on their knee, and understood that they were about to do the one thing their profession had trained them for and their whole life had taught them to avoid: look directly at what was lost, without measuring it first.

"I'll be in the parlor," Odile said. "If you need me."

"You should rest."

"I have rested for fifty years." Her hands were folded in her lap like two closed books. "Walk, appraiser. Say it out loud. It hates being seen more than anything else in this world, because seeing is the one thing it cannot do back."

* * *

Xanthe started in the parlor because it was where the mirrors were thickest, and the mirrors were where the house kept its inventory.

The glass over the mantel did not show the parlor. It showed a wedding—June light through windows that no longer existed, a bride in a dress the color of cream, a room full of people whose faces Xanthe almost recognized from the village, younger by decades, laughing at something outside the frame. The image had the grainy patience of a thing that had been waiting a long time to be looked at.

Xanthe raised the legal pad. Their hand shook, and they let it.

"June fourteenth, 1968," they said aloud, reading the date off the corner of the memory the way you'd read it off a photograph. "Eleanor Voss marries Thomas Aldous in the parlor of Hollowmere House. There are forty-one guests. The cake has three tiers and sugar lilies." They stopped. Swallowed. "I witness it. I see it. It happened."

The mirror flickered—not like a screen failing but like an eye flinching—and for half a second the wedding was gone and there was only the empty parlor, dust in the light, before the bride came back, dimmer now, like a candle burned down.

Something in the walls exhaled.

Xanthe wrote the date on the pad. Moved to the next mirror.

A funeral. Black wool, wet grass visible through the window, a coffin carried by six men whose shoulders were all wrong with grief. "November second, 1953. The burial of Marguerite Ferrar, aged sixty-one. Her daughter Odile is fourteen years old and does not cry until the house is out of sight." In the glass, the girl at the edge of the frame turned her head—as if she'd heard her own name spoken after all these years—and Xanthe felt the cost of it go through them like cold water: every word they said aloud took something. Not blood, not breath. Attention. The raw currency of being a person who notices things. Each witnessed memory left Xanthe slightly less able to remember why they'd come here, what the laser measure in their bag was for, whether they'd eaten breakfast. The house wasn't taking payment. It was taking *witness*, and witness was finite.

They kept walking.

* * *

By noon they had cleared the ground floor. Forty-one weddings, funerals, christenings, homecomings, last words. Xanthe's voice had gone hoarse and their handwriting had degraded into a surveyor's shorthand, dates and names stacked like fence posts. The mirrors were going dark one by one behind them, each emptied of its stolen scene, showing only honest reflections again—a tired person with a legal pad, nothing more.

Odile watched from the parlor doorway as Xanthe passed.

"How much of yourself is left?" she asked quietly.

"Enough."

"That's what the founders said too. On the second day." She turned her chair. "Keep going. But eat something. The ritual doesn't forgive fainting."

In the kitchen, Xanthe found bread and hard cheese and ate standing up, tasting nothing, running the list in their head. Upstairs next. Then the cellar. Then—

Then 1974.

They didn't know yet where the house kept 1974. But they knew it would be the heaviest room in the building, and they knew, with the certainty of someone who has spent a career finding the room that doesn't sum, exactly which direction it would be in.

* * *

The upstairs hallway held the small thefts. A boy's first bicycle, wobbly down a driveway. A dog dying under a porch while its family was at church. A woman telling her sister a secret and then, decades later, both of them reaching for it and finding smooth blankness where the secret used to be. Xanthe named them all. Their voice cracked on the dog. They didn't know why the dog undid them until they said the next line—"I witness it"—and felt the sentence land somewhere below language, in the place where grief keeps its unfiled papers.

The lag in the hallway mirror was still there, but changed. Their reflection no longer moved late. It moved *with* them, exactly, perfectly, and when Xanthe stopped, it stopped, and then—only once—it nodded.

Not mocking. Encouraging. Like a colleague acknowledging good work.

Xanthe did not nod back. They were afraid of what agreeing to anything in this house might sign them into.

* * *

The cellar stairs were stone and went down farther than the exterior dimensions allowed. Xanthe had measured this discrepancy on day one and filed it under "settling." Now they understood: the house grew downward the way a tree grows roots, and it stored its deepest takings deepest.

At the bottom was a door with no handle, and it was already open.

Inside, the room was not a room. It was 1974.

Not a séance, not a signing—those were upstairs, in the formal spaces, in the mirrors. This was the town itself, the year itself, rendered in slow amber: Main Street with its awnings, the diner with its pie case, the school gymnasium mid-basketball-game, the whole population of Hollowmere suspended in ordinary joy. And threaded through every scene, the same moment repeating like a stitch: a man in a gray coat standing at the crosswalk by the hardware store, turning his head, mouth open around a word—

And Odile, twenty-five, across the street, watching him, not moving.

"The eleventh of March, 1974," Xanthe said, and their voice came out steady, which surprised them, because inside they were shaking apart. "Walter Merrick steps off the curb at the corner of Main and Hale. He calls out to Odile Ferrar. She does not answer. A truck comes through the intersection. Walter Merrick dies at 4:17 in the afternoon. I witness it. I see it. It happened."

The amber shivered. The man in the gray coat turned fully around, and for the first time in fifty years, Walter Merrick looked at someone who was looking back.

"It was not your fault," Xanthe said—to the room, to the memory, to the woman sitting upstairs who could not hear them and had been punishing herself longer than Xanthe had been alive. "It was an accident. Accidents are not debts. I witness that too."

The room went dark. Not menacing dark—the dark of a drawer finally closed, of a file finally shelved. Somewhere above them, faint through the floorboards, Xanthe heard Odile make a sound they had never heard a human being make: relief and ruin in the same breath.

* * *

They climbed back into the daylight with the legal pad full and their own head strangely quiet. Too quiet. They reached for the memory of their sister's face—the one they'd been circling for days, the redacted page, the ten seconds—and found it still sealed, still theirs to give, still waiting.

One act left. One price.

In the front hall, Odile was waiting, and she was crying without any shame at all, and she said:

"Now you understand what it costs. Now write your account, appraiser. Because after that—" her eyes went to the hidden study, to the ledgers, to the blank page with Xanthe's name on it, "—after that, you pay."

Xanthe looked down at the legal pad, forty-one weddings and a funeral and a bicycle and a dog and Walter Merrick, all of it witnessed, all of it held, and understood that the house had never once tried to stop them.

It had been helping them to the table.
