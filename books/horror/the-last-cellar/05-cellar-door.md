## 5. Cellar Door

The scratch trails started in the kitchen.

Ivo found them at six in the morning, kneeling on the Holloways' linoleum with a flashlight held low and flat, the way he'd been taught to read rodent runs twenty years ago. Grease trails. Rub marks. The classic sign of a rat highway along a baseboard—except these went up.

They climbed the wall like ivy. Five parallel lines, evenly spaced, dragging a faint dark smear behind them, rising past the light switch, past the window frame, up over the door casing, and into the seam where the ceiling met the wall. From there they continued across the ceiling itself, visible only when he tilted the flashlight almost parallel to the plaster: a faint greasy road map of everywhere the thing had walked above them while they slept.

Ivo photographed all of it. He always photographed everything. That was the creed. You don't guess at an infestation; you document it, you measure it, you build the case before you build the treatment plan. His old mentor had called it pest control's first sacrament: *the evidence is the truth, and the truth is what you treat.* Ivo had taken it further than most. He kept a logbook for every job, timestamped, cross-referenced, backed up twice. He photographed his own van's odometer each morning. He had once driven forty minutes back to a job site because he couldn't remember whether he'd counted eleven droppings or twelve in a pantry corner, and he hadn't been able to sleep until he knew.

People called it obsessive. He let them. Documentation was prayer, and prayer was how he kept the world legible. A world with records was a world with rules. A world with rules was a world where things could be named, treated, closed out, filed away. He had learned that lesson young, in a house that had none of those things, and he had never unlearned it.

So he knelt on Mara Holloway's kitchen floor at dawn and did what he always did. He documented.

The grease was wrong, though. He noticed it as soon as he touched it—or rather, as soon as he decided not to touch it. Rat grease is sebum and dirt, brownish, waxy, smeared in long continuous strokes because rats press their whole flanks against surfaces as they travel. This was different. These were five discrete lines, finger-width, ending in round pads at intervals. They were handprints. Someone had pressed a hand to the wall and dragged it upward, again and again, climbing a vertical surface without any purchase, leaving prints at heights no one in this family could reach without a ladder—and there were no ladder scuffs anywhere, no dust disturbed on the tops of the door frames, nothing.

He measured the highest print. Eight feet, four inches. He wrote it down. His handwriting came out smaller than usual, cramped, like the numbers themselves were trying to hide.

Mara appeared in the doorway in a bathrobe, hair wet, watching him with the hollow look she'd worn since the first day. She didn't ask what he was doing. She'd stopped asking questions two visits ago, which told him more than any answer would have.

"The cellar," she said instead.

He looked up. "What about it?"

"You'll want to see the cellar." Her voice was flat, rehearsed, like she'd practiced saying it to herself in the mirror and still hated the words. "The scratches go down there too."

* * *

The cellar door stood at the end of a short hallway off the kitchen, under the stairs—a plank door, painted over so many times its panels were soft-edged, with a brass hasp and a padlock the size of a fist.

Padlocked from the outside. That was normal. Farmhouses lock their cellars; storm cellars, root cellars, places where children shouldn't wander. Ivo had seen a thousand locked cellar doors and thought nothing of any of them.

But the padlock hung loose in the hasp. Unlocked. And the hasp itself—the metal loop screwed into the doorframe—was smeared with fingerprints.

On the inside.

He crouched. He made himself look properly, the way he'd make himself look at anything, because looking was the job. The hasp was a flat steel plate bent into a U, bolted through the doorframe, and the inner curve of that U—the surface that faced the door, the surface that could only be touched by someone standing *behind* the closed door, reaching around its edge from the cellar side—was printed with five fingers and a thumb. Fresh. The grease still had a wet shine to it in the flashlight beam, catching the light the way fresh sealant catches light.

Someone in the cellar had been holding the hasp. Not trying to open it—the padlock wasn't even engaged. Just holding it. Feeling the shape of the hardware between their fingers, the way you'd handle something familiar. Something you'd handled for a long time.

Ivo sat back on his heels. His breath came out slow and deliberate, the way he breathed when he needed his hands steady.

"Rats don't do that," he said quietly.

"No," Mara said behind him. "They don't."

"When was this last opened?"

"Grandma locked it in—" Mara stopped. Started again. "Nobody's opened it since Grandma died. Four years. Dale keeps the key on his ring. He says it's for safety." A pause. "He says a lot of things are for safety."

Ivo pulled out his phone and photographed the hasp from three angles, then the padlock hanging open, then the door itself, top to bottom. His hands were shaking now—not badly, not enough to blur the shots, but enough that he noticed, and noticing made it worse, because his hands didn't shake. In nineteen years of crawlspaces and dead-wall voids and wasp nests the size of footballs, his hands had never shaken. Fear was a thing he'd filed away long ago, in a room he didn't visit. His body didn't shake at work. His body understood that shaking was for after.

There was no after here.

He set the phone down on the floor, screen up, recording video, propped against the baseboard so it would capture the whole door. Then he took out his logbook and wrote:

*Day 3. Cellar door. Hasp printed on interior face. Padlock disengaged but door secured by unknown means—will not force. Prints match rafter prints (see pp. 14–19): five digits, incorrect joint placement, proximal phalanges appearing to flex backward. Height consistent with subject standing on cellar floor beneath hasp, i.e., subject height approx. 7 ft if arm raised, or approx. 8+ ft if standing upright.*

He stared at what he'd written. *Subject.* He'd written *subject*, the way he'd write about a raccoon. Except raccoons didn't have phalanges. Raccoons didn't wait four years beside a locked door, holding the latch, patient as furniture.

His rational framework—the scaffolding he'd built his whole life on, every strange thing reduced to species and entry point and treatment plan—was buckling, and he could feel it buckling, and some cold clear part of him noted that too, wrote *that* down: *observer experiencing significant departure from baseline professional detachment.*

Documentation as prayer. But prayer, he was beginning to understand, runs both directions. You speak into the dark assuming the dark doesn't listen. What do you do on the morning you realize the dark has been keeping notes on you too?

* * *

He spent an hour on the door itself.

Not forcing it—he wouldn't force it, something older than professionalism told him forcing it would be a mistake, the way you don't knock on a hive—you circled it. He photographed the hinges: intact, oiled recently, though nobody claimed to have oiled them. He photographed the gap at the threshold: half an inch of darkness with a draft coming out of it, cool and mineral-smelling, like the air off a stone that had been underground a very long time. He held the flashlight to the gap and saw nothing, which was somehow worse than seeing something. Darkness that returns your inspection with interest.

The scratch trails converged here. That was the thing he kept coming back to. All night, every night, according to the pattern of grease and dust, the prints descended the walls of the house—out of the rafters, down the stairwell, along the hallway—and ended at this door. And then, on the inside of the hasp, they began again.

It wasn't trapped. He understood that with a certainty that bypassed his training entirely. Whatever moved through the rafters and left eight-foot prints on the parlor ceiling was not contained by a plank door and a brass hasp. It went where it liked. It climbed the walls of the living house and it sat below the living house and the door between was not a barrier.

It was a boundary. A courtesy. A line both sides had agreed not to cross.

And the prints on the inside of the hasp were not the marks of something trying to get out.

They were the marks of something holding the door shut—from within. Keeping itself in. Or keeping something else out. Or simply resting its hand there, night after night, feeling the vibrations of the family moving overhead, the way you'd rest your palm on a speaker cone to feel the music.

*It knows we're here,* Ivo thought, and the thought arrived with the specific gravity of fact, already documented somewhere in himself, already filed.

He filmed the hasp for four minutes. He narrated in a low voice, date, time, conditions, observations, the way he'd narrated ten thousand site videos, and halfway through he heard his own voice change—not louder, not faster, but closer, like a man leaning toward a confessional grille. He stopped the recording. Sat on the hallway floor with his back against the opposite wall, facing the door, and did not move for a long time.

The house was quiet. Mara had retreated to the kitchen; he could hear her running water she wasn't using, the small domestic noises people make when they're pretending a house is ordinary. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard ticked as the sun warmed it. Ordinary sounds. A house full of ordinary sounds, arranged around a door that nobody opened and nobody could explain, behind which something waited with its hand on the latch—not pushing, just touching. Present. Aware.

Ivo looked at his logbook. Nineteen years of logbooks filled a shelf in his apartment, ninety-odd volumes, every infestation, every carcass, every entry point sealed and signed off. Closed cases. That was the whole architecture of his life: find it, name it, end it, file it. Nothing left open. Nothing left unresolved. Because he knew—Ivo Amberdale alone among pest controllers, maybe alone among men—what it did to you, what it did to a whole life, to leave a door unopened, a thing unnamed, a cry unanswered—

He shut that thought the way you shut a drawer. Gently. All the way.

Then he opened the logbook again and wrote, in his smallest, most controlled hand:

*Treatment plan suspended pending further observation. Recommend continued monitoring. Do NOT open cellar. Do NOT remove lock. Note: subject does not appear to wish exit. Subject appears to wish attendance.*

He read the word back. *Attendance.* Like a churchgoer. Like something that came, every night, to sit in the dark under the house and hold the door, present for whatever happened above it. Listening to the family the way the family refused to listen to it.

Prayer, he thought. Documentation as prayer. But he'd had it backwards, maybe. You didn't document to keep the world at bay. You documented to get closer to it. Every photograph, every measurement, every hour crouched in front of that door with his flashlight and his shaking hands—he wasn't building a case. He was building a relationship. The thing below had been attending to this house for thirty years, and now Ivo was attending to it, and somewhere in the space between those two attentions something was being negotiated that had nothing to do with treatment plans.

His phone buzzed against the baseboard where he'd left it recording. He picked it up. Forty minutes of footage. He scrubbed to the end, to the last few seconds before he'd stopped—

And froze.

In the final frames, in the half-inch gap beneath the door, in the darkness that had returned his inspection with interest all morning, there was a reflection. Faint. Wet-shine. Five points of light catching the flashlight beam from inside the cellar, arranged in an arc.

Like fingernails. Pressed to the gap. On the other side.

Holding still. Holding the door. Holding position while he filmed—as aware of the camera as the camera was of it.

Ivo lowered the phone. Looked at the door. The door said nothing, in the way of doors.

"Okay," he said quietly. His voice sounded strange in the narrow hall, too loud and too flat at once. "Okay. I see you."

From below the floor, from behind the plank and the stone and the forty years of dark, came a sound he would spend the rest of this case trying to describe and never quite manage. Not a scratch. Not a voice. A single, slow exhalation of wood and earth, like a house settling—but settling *toward* him, if a house could lean.

That night he would go up into the attic, following the smell of old paper, looking for anything that explained a locked door and a patient hand. He didn't know that yet. Standing in the hallway with his shaking hands and his open logbook, he knew only one thing, and he wrote it down because writing it down was the only sacrament he had left:

*It is not trying to get out. It is waiting to be asked.*
