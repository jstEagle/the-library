## 13. Edith's Confession

*The following pages were found loose at the back of Edith Holloway's final journal, unbound, folded twice, and tucked beneath the false bottom of a sewing box. The handwriting deteriorates across the sequence—from the careful schoolteacher's script of her earlier years to something that looks like it was written by a hand that had forgotten what hands are for.*

---

**March 3rd, 1976**

I have not opened this book in two years. I told myself I was done writing it down, because writing it down makes it real, and there are mornings—most mornings—I can almost pretend my house is only a house.

But last night Walter's chair moved.

Not far. Six inches, maybe less, from where it stands in the parlor corner to where it stood this morning. Toward the kitchen. Toward me. And I sat at my table with my tea going cold and understood that I was not afraid of it.

That is the confession, isn't it? Not the chair. Not the thing below. The not-being-afraid.

A woman should be afraid of that. A woman who buried her husband two years ago and still keeps his chair facing the room like a sentry—a woman who hears it scrape an inch closer every harvest moon and feels *satisfaction*—that woman ought to be afraid of herself.

Let me start where I always start. At the beginning. Maybe if I write it plainly enough times, one of these tellings will finally be true.

---

**March 9th, 1976**

Walter Holloway beat me for nineteen years.

There. It is on paper. Nineteen years, and in all that time I never once said it aloud to another living soul, because who would I have said it to? The minister who played cards with him Thursdays? My sister who told me a marriage is a garden you weed yourself? The neighbors who heard him through the walls and turned their radios up?

Nineteen years. He broke my wrist the winter of '61 and told Doctor Pruitt I'd fallen on the ice, and Doctor Pruitt looked at my face while he set it—the way you look at a horse with a sore mouth—and he knew, and he said nothing, and I thanked him for his silence because silence was safer than truth. That is how they make us complicit. Not with cruelty. With convenience.

And then in October of '74 he came home drunk off Ray Tuller's still and put my head through the pantry door, and I lay on the kitchen floor with flour sifting down over me like snow, and I thought: *I am fifty-one years old and I will die in this kitchen and no one will even be surprised.*

That night I went down to the cellar.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently the only currency this house accepts. I didn't go down to the cellar to do anything. I went down because it was the only room in the house Walter never entered—he hated it, said it smelled wrong, said the cold down there got into his bones—and I needed one room that was mine. I took the lamp and Edna Marsh's old book—I'll come to the book—and I sat on the bottom step and bled onto the stone from my split lip, and I spoke out loud for the first time in nineteen years without editing myself first.

The book called it an invitation. I called it talking to myself. I know better now.

---

**March 14th, 1976**

Edna Marsh lived in the Hollis place before the Hollises, and when she died her niece sold the contents and I bought a crate of books for a dollar, mostly hymnals and seed catalogs, and one book with no title on the spine. Handwritten. Part almanac, part recipe box, part prayer book for people who had stopped believing God was listening. The pages about the cellar were written by someone named only as H., and H. wrote like a farmer writes about weather: practical, unsentimental, certain.

*What sits below has always sat below,* H. wrote. *It is older than the farm and older than the county. It does not want worship. It wants work. Give it a wrong to weigh and it will weigh it. Speak your grievance over your own blood spilled on its stone and it will hear you. But hear this: it serves the speaker, and it feeds on power, and it does not leave until it is dismissed, and dismissal costs more than most can pay.*

I read that last part sitting on the step with my blood drying on the stone, and I confess—I confess everything now, that is the whole point of this—I did not care. I was past caring. I read *it does not leave* and I thought: good.

I spoke the words H. gave. Over my own blood, as instructed. I asked it to sit in judgment. I asked it to weigh what Walter had done. And I remember the cold coming up through my shoes like the ground itself had drawn breath, and I remember thinking: *nothing will happen, you foolish old woman, you've been reading fairy stories.*

Three nights later Walter fell down the cellar stairs.

The coroner said drink. Everyone said drink. Walter drank, everyone knew it, a man falls, that's the whole story. And I stood at his funeral in my good black dress and let people squeeze my hand and say *you're free now, Edith, you're free*, and I nodded like a widow nods, and inside me there was a voice singing so loud I could barely keep my face arranged.

Here is what I have never written, not once, not anywhere: I don't know if I pushed him.

I was upstairs. I was asleep, or near it. But I heard the door open—that heavy latch, I'd know it in my grave—and I heard his boots on the top stair, and I heard him say something low, the way a man talks when someone else is in the room, and I lay in my bed and I did not get up. I could have. I heard him and I knew the stairs and I knew his state and I lay there with the blanket pulled to my chin and I *let* it happen. Whether the thing below reached up or merely waited, whether my silence was murder or only cowardice—the distinction matters to God, maybe. To me, on some nights, it doesn't matter at all.

I wanted him dead. There it is. Nineteen years of wanting, compressed into one sentence, and it fits so easily on the page, the way a knife fits easily in a hand.

---

**March 21st, 1976**

The chair appeared the first harvest moon after his death.

His chair—the ugly oak thing his father made, with the carved acorns on the backrest that he'd rest his boots against—I had put it in the parlor corner because I couldn't throw it out and couldn't sit in it. And one morning in September it was facing the room. I had left it facing the wall. I know I had, because facing the wall was the only way I could bear to look at it.

I moved it back. Next morning: facing the room again.

Then the photographs. Mara was a baby then; my son Gerald took pictures of every family gathering like his father before him, and in the developed prints there was the chair, standing in rooms where the chair had never been. In the church hall at Mara's christening. On the porch. Once, terribly, beside my bed in a photograph taken in the hospital the year my hip went.

Gerald laughed about it. *Mom, you've got a ghost furniture problem.* He moved his family to Ohio that same year, and I have often wondered if the moving was entirely about the mill closing.

I know what it is now. I have known for a long time. It sits in judgment, H. wrote, and so it sits—in his chair, wearing his absence like a coat—and it watches this house, and it weighs us.

And here is the part I cannot forgive myself for, the part that has brought me back to this journal with my hands shaking:

Every year, on the harvest moon, I open the cellar door.

I don't call it anything. I don't speak the words again. I just unbolt the padlock—which I installed myself, which I tell the neighbors is for the preserves—and I leave it unlocked for one night, and in the morning it is locked again from the inside, and the air in the house is different, cleaner, the way a room feels after a storm.

I tell myself I'm maintaining it. Feeding it, so it stays quiet. A farmer maintains his fences. That's the lie I've built my whole retirement on: that I am a caretaker performing necessary maintenance.

But maintenance doesn't require you to stand at the top of those stairs with your heart hammering and feel *glad*. Maintenance doesn't leave you humming over the dishes the next morning.

---

**April 2nd, 1976**

Dale hit his daughter yesterday.

My grandson Dale—Gerald's boy, fourteen years old and already built like his grandfather—took his belt to little Mara over a broken dish, in the yard, where the neighbors could see, and the neighbors saw, and the neighbors said nothing, and I stood at my kitchen window with a dish towel in my hands and watched, and I did nothing.

I want to write that I was paralyzed. I want to write that I'm eighty years old and frail and what could I have done. Both true. Neither is the truth.

The truth is that some cold patient part of me stood behind my own eyes and thought: *the house sees this too.*

The house sees everything. That is what I made it into. Not a refuge, not a judgment—those were the words I used, but what I actually built, with my blood and my grief and my nineteen years of swallowed screams, was a witness. Something that would watch this family forever and never look away, the way no one ever watched for me. And I have discovered, these two years, that a witness is a terrible gift to give your descendants. Because the house remembers. The house weighs. And the house has learned, from watching me, exactly which sins to feed on.

I have made my grandchildren's inheritance a courtroom, and none of them committed the crime.

---

**April 10th, 1976**

I tried to dismiss it.

There. Now it's written, and I can't take it back, and I won't soften it later.

H.'s book gives the words of dismissal on the same page as the words of invitation, and warns—H. is very clear about the warning—*dismissal requires the speaker to name her own desire. You must stand on the stone and say what you wanted, wholly, without ornament. The thing below cannot be lied to and cannot be flattered. If you ask it to leave while hiding from yourself, it will know, and it will stay, and it will be angrier for the asking.*

So on Tuesday night I took the lamp down the stairs. First time since the night I spoke the invitation. The cold came up to meet me like an old dog greeting its master, and I stood on the stone where my blood had dried to a ghost-shadow that I swear is still there, and I began the words.

*I, Edith Holloway, release what I have called—*

And I stopped.

Because the next line is: *for I spoke in grief, and did not truly wish—*

And I could not say it.

I stood on that cold stone for—I don't know how long. An hour. The lamp burned low. And the whole time, the truth sat in my chest like a stone of its own, and the truth is this: I *did* wish it. I wished Walter dead with the whole of my heart, nineteen years deep, and when he died I was glad, and I am glad still, and every morning I wake in this house he never touched me in and I think: *this is mine, I earned this, I paid for this in blood,* and that gladness is the realest feeling I have ever had.

To dismiss it, I would have to kneel on that stone and say: *I lied. I didn't want justice. I wanted him dead and I wanted to be glad.* I would have to make my nineteen years into a lie. I would have to tell the dark that my suffering was just weather, that my survival meant nothing, that the only sin in this house was mine.

I blew out the lamp and went upstairs.

And I have not been back down since. And the padlock is on the door. And every harvest moon I unlock it for one night like a woman leaving milk out for something she's too ashamed to name.

They will say, whoever finds this—they will say *she could have stopped it and chose not to.* And they will be right. Hear me: they will be right, and I knew they would be right, and I chose anyway. Because the alternative was to stand in the wreckage of my own life and admit that I built it on a murder I didn't have to lift a finger for.

Justice curdles. No one tells you that. Justice kept too long in the dark turns to something else—something that starts as your servant and ends as your habit, and then your heirloom, and then your god. I wanted a judge. I got a habit. And habits are passed down like chin shapes and tempers, and my grandson Dale has his grandfather's hands, and the house is watching, and I have taught it what to watch *for*.

---

**April 17th, 1976**

My hands are bad now. The doctor says age; I say the house takes the steadiness out of them a little more each year, the way it takes an inch of floor from the chair each autumn. Look at this page. Look what my confession has become—spiders walking through spilled ink.

Mara was here Sunday. She is eleven and she stands in the parlor doorway looking at the chair, and she asked me, *Grandma, why does Grandpa's chair watch us?*

Out of the mouths of children. She sees it. Of course she sees it; children see everything until we teach them blindness, and I will not teach her blindness, that at least I can refuse.

I told her: *It's waiting for something, sweetheart.*

She said: *Waiting for what?*

And I said: *For someone to tell the truth.*

I have had two years to tell the truth. Two years of harvest moons, twenty-four months of unlocked doors, and I have walked to the top of those stairs a hundred times and come back a hundred times, because the truth is a cellar too, and I am too old and too tired and too much a coward to climb down into myself.

So I leave this page for whoever comes after. For Mara, maybe, if she's the one who finds it, and God forgive me, I hope it's her—she has her grandmother's eyes and none of our cruelty, and if anyone in this line can stand on that stone and speak honestly, it's the child.

Or perhaps it will be a stranger. Someone hired to chase the scratching in the walls, some careful man with a clipboard and a flashlight, poking through my attic, finding this box. If that's you—if you're reading this with dust on your sleeves and my family's secrets in your hands—then listen to an old woman who ran out of time:

Don't do what I did. Don't call it and then flatter yourself about why. Don't mistake relief for righteousness. And whatever you decide—banish it, feed it, take it into yourself—decide it standing in the truth, because the thing below has never once been fooled, and it has forty years now of listening to this family lie, and it is *hungry* for something clean.

The moon fills in three weeks. The chair has moved nine inches since March.

I am going to unlock the door tonight. One last time. Not to maintain it. Not to feed it.

Because I think, at the end, I would rather it weighed me than spared me.

*—E.H.*

* * *

The page ended there, the ink trailing off in a long wavering line, as though the pen had been set down mid-stroke and never picked up again.

Ivo Amberdale sat on the attic floor with the sewing box open in his lap and the harvest moon three weeks from full, and he read the last line four times, and then he looked at the photograph in his other hand—the parlor, the corner, the empty chair facing the room—and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Edith Holloway had never climbed back down those stairs at all.

And neither, he realized, hearing the faint, patient scrape drift up through the floorboards, had the thing that was waiting for someone to tell the truth.

Three weeks. And the house had already started choosing who it would ask first.
