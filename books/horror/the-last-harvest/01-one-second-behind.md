## 1. One Second Behind

The sign at the edge of town said WELCOME TO HOLLOW CREEK, POP. 1,204, and beneath it someone had painted a smaller line in careful white letters: *A Good Place to Grow.*

Ines Thornbury read it twice, because the second time he noticed the paint was fresh. Not the sign itself—the correction. The population number had been painted over an older figure, and the older figure was still faintly legible if you knew to look: 1,215.

Eleven people, gone from the count. He would think about that later, much later, and understand it had been the first thing the town ever told him about itself. But that afternoon, driving his overloaded Corolla down a main street lined with shuttered storefronts and stubbornly blooming marigolds, he only thought: small towns always fudge their numbers. Everyone wants to seem fuller than they are.

He was thirty-four years old and arriving with everything he owned in boxes that smelled like the storage unit they'd spent two years in. The teaching post at Hollow Creek Elementary was a step down from the university adjunct work he'd been cobbling together in the city—less money, less prestige, a one-year contract for a fourth-and-fifth combined class of what the superintendent's letter cheerfully called "a manageable fourteen students." Fourteen students. In the city he'd taught classes of thirty-one in rooms built for twenty-five. A manageable fourteen sounded like a vacation.

It sounded like a place where nothing had ever happened.

The school itself sat back from the road behind a chain-link fence, its brick face softened by ivy that someone clearly tended rather than simply tolerated. A flagpole stood in the front yard, straight and gleaming, flying a flag so new it still held its creases. Ines slowed as he passed, some instinct making him catalog the building—the tall windows, the dark basement-level windows near the foundation, the way none of the classroom windows on the east side had curtains or blinds or anything at all behind the glass.

Empty rooms, probably. Budget cuts. He'd ask about it Monday.

The house was on Corliss Street, three blocks off the main drag, and it was the kind of house that made you understand why people stayed in dying towns even as the towns died around them: solid bones, deep porch, a yard big enough to get lost in. His landlord, a man named Dill who had shaken his hand with the crushing sincerity of someone raised to trust handshakes, had told him it belonged to the old school caretaker, a Mr. Aldous Wren, who had "kept the place up till his health went."

"He was here fifty years," Dill said, standing on the porch while Ines fumbled the key. "Wren. Longer'n most folks been alive. You'll find things around the house he left. Just—leave 'em be, mostly. He liked his order."

"Was he the caretaker at the school too?"

Dill's smile arrived a half-second before the rest of his face agreed to it. "Long time ago now. Well." He clapped Ines on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him slightly. "You'll want to get settled before dark. We're early risers out here."

Ines watched him walk down the drive and turn onto the sidewalk without looking back, which struck him as odd only because everyone else he'd met in Hollow Creek had looked back. The woman at the diner. The kid pumping gas. They all did this thing where they watched him go, not rudely, just thoroughly, the way you'd watch weather come in.

The house felt watched too. He noticed it the moment the door closed behind him—not sinister exactly, more like the sensation of walking into a room where a conversation has just stopped. The air had that quality. Attentive. He stood in the front hall with his keys in his fist and listened to the house listen to him.

"Dust," he said aloud, to hear a voice. "Old houses hold dust and it holds heat and heat moves. That's all."

His voice came back to him off the walls a little too flatly, as if the rooms were upholstered in something soft and absorbent, and he decided not to think about that either.

* * *

Unpacking took until dusk. He found Mr. Wren's order everywhere: hooks labeled in faded pencil, a broom closet arranged by bristle stiffness, kitchen drawers where every utensil lay parallel to its neighbors like spoons in a case. In the bathroom medicine cabinet, behind a bottle of iodine so old the label had gone the color of tea, he found a comb wrapped in wax paper and a small tin of mints that had fused into a single mint-colored stone.

He should have found it charming. Instead each small evidence of the dead man's tidiness landed on him with a weight he couldn't name, and by the time he'd made the bed in the master bedroom—a bed that came with the house, frame and mattress both, which he'd have refused anywhere else—he was too tired to refuse anything.

He ate takeout containers on the porch steps and watched the cornfields north of town go from gold to gray to black. There were no streetlights past the last house. The dark out there was total and textured, and once—just once—he thought he saw a shape move through the corn at a distance no person could walk at, faster than any person, keeping pace with something.

A truck, he decided. Headlights sweeping across stalks.

There were no headlights.

He went inside and locked the door and told himself the lock was habit, not fear.

* * *

The bathroom was upstairs, small and white-tiled, with a mirror over the sink that had a hairline crack running through one corner like a river on a map. He brushed his teeth under the yellow flicker of a bulb that needed replacing, and it was somewhere between the toothpaste and the rinsing that he noticed it.

Noticed is too strong a word. What happened first was a feeling: a wrongness in his peripheral vision, the sense that his reflection was somehow *late*. He turned his head sharply to the left and caught—what? Nothing. His own tired face, the cracked corner of glass, the towel rack behind his shoulder.

But then he did it again, slower, watching deliberately, and there it was. A lag. Tiny. Measurable only because he was measuring. When he lifted his right hand, the reflection's hand rose a beat afterward—a fraction of a second, less than a heartbeat, but distinct, the way a dubbed film goes subtly out of sync until you can't unsee it.

He put the toothbrush down. Stood very still. The reflection stood very still, and he could not tell anymore whether it was standing still *with* him or a moment after.

"Exhaustion," he said to the mirror. His mouth moved; the reflected mouth moved; if there was a delay, it hid inside the word itself.

He leaned close. His breath fogged the glass, and in the fog he saw the whole room ghosted and warped, and for one irrational instant the room in the mirror seemed deeper than the room behind him—as if the reflection showed not the bathroom but a longer version of it, stretching back past the wall into darkness the bathroom didn't have.

Then the fog faded and it was just a mirror again, old and spotted, holding his face the way mirrors do.

"You've been driving for nine hours," he told himself. "You slept four hours last night. Your brain is doing what brains do."

This was true. It was comforting. It was also, he would remember, the exact shape of a lie: true facts, arranged to say something false.

He clicked off the light. In the sudden dark, before his eyes adjusted, the mirror held a faint gray rectangle of remembered light, and he had the crawling, unspeakable impression that the rectangle lingered a moment after the rest of the room had gone black—that the mirror kept the light a heartbeat longer than the light deserved, the way a struck bell keeps ringing after the hammer has moved away.

He did not look back at it. He walked down the hall to the bedroom, closed the door, and lay down on Mr. Wren's bed in Mr. Wren's house in a town that had quietly repainted its own numbers, and he fell asleep faster than he expected, dreamlessly, like something set down gently into deep water.

* * *

He woke once, near three in the morning, to a sound from downstairs.

Not a sound, precisely. A rhythm. The tick of the hallway clock—which he hadn't wound, which he wasn't sure could be wound—marking seconds. Except the clock was in the hall below, and the sound was coming from the bathroom, and when he lay listening he understood that what he heard was not ticking at all but tapping: fingernail, or fingertip, or something with neither, tapping against glass. Tap. Tap. Tap. Patient as a metronome. Keeping time with something.

With his pulse, he realized. It was keeping time with his pulse.

He lay in the dark and his heart sped up and the tapping sped up with it, perfectly, faithfully, and then—this was the part he would never tell anyone, the part he barely admitted to himself—it stopped. All at once. The way a person stops talking when they realize you can hear them.

Ines lay awake until the window went gray, and in the morning the daylight made the night ridiculous. Sunlight poured through the bathroom window and lay across the tile in warm honest bars. The mirror was just a mirror. The tap had been the house settling, pipes contracting, the clock, the wind, exhaustion, exhaustion, exhaustion.

He showered, dressed, and stood a moment at the sink, and on impulse he raised his right hand quickly, testing, the way you'd test a bruise.

The reflection followed.

Perfectly. Immediately. Exactly with him, all the way up, all the way down.

"See?" he said to his grinning morning self. "Nothing."

And the reflection grinned with him—or it grinned a moment after him, a fraction of a heartbeat, a sliver of time so thin you could only see it if you already believed it was there—and Ines lowered his hand, and the reflection lowered its hand, and outside the window the cornfields stood silent in the sun, eleven stalks taller than all the others, though he didn't notice that either.

Not yet.

He picked up his lesson plans, locked the door of the house that watched him go, and drove toward Hollow Creek Elementary, where Room 4 waited behind its clean glass with no curtains, no blinds, and no children—and where, on the roster waiting in his mailbox, eleven names had been struck through in ink so old it had gone brown, as if the paper itself had bled.

He hadn't seen them yet.

By tonight, he wouldn't be able to stop seeing them.
