## 7. Mara's Second Sight

Mara Voss knew the whispering had started before the new teacher came, but she'd never told anyone, because telling was how you got looked at funny, and being looked at funny in Hollow Creek meant your mother started crying at night and your father stopped eating dinner with you.

It began with spoons.

That was the thing nobody would believe. Not mirrors—everyone knew about mirrors, everyone had a story about a mirror doing something wrong, though nobody ever said what. Spoons were worse because spoons were everywhere and nobody watched them. Mara had been six, sitting in the kitchen with her oatmeal, when she noticed the bowl of her spoon wasn't showing her face right. It showed her face, but it showed it slow. She'd wiggle her nose and the spoon-Mara would wiggle hers a half-beat later, like an echo that hadn't decided yet whether to come back.

She dropped the spoon. Her mother asked what was wrong and Mara said *nothing* and her mother said *good girl* and something about the way she said it made Mara understand, even at six, that *nothing* was the correct answer to every question in Hollow Creek.

The whispering came later. Or maybe it had always been there and she'd only just gotten old enough to hear it, the way you can't hear a dog whistle until your ears are the right age and then suddenly the whole world is full of thin silver screaming.

*Eleven voices,* is what she counted the first time she really listened. She was nine. She'd counted them three times on three different days to be sure, and it was eleven every time, and eleven was a number she recognized without knowing from where, the way you recognize a smell from a house you've never visited.

They didn't say words at first. They said sounds. The sound of chalk on a board. The sound of small shoes on stairs going down. The sound of breathing in a room where breathing shouldn't echo, but did.

*

By the time Mr. Thornbury arrived in September, Mara had rules.

Rule one: never listen near water. Water held reflections sideways and loose, and the voices came through it scrambled and mean, like they were drunk or drowning, which maybe they were.

Rule two: never listen at night in the bathroom, no matter how much the mirror seemed to want her to look. Looking was listening's older brother, and once they were together you couldn't separate them again.

Rule three: if a voice ever said her name, she was allowed to tell one person. Just one. She'd picked the person years ago without knowing why—a shape of a person, someone patient, someone who asked questions other people flinched from—and when Mr. Thornbury stood at the front of the class on his first day and wrote his name on the board in careful, slanted letters, Mara felt the shape click into place like a bone setting.

He was the one. She didn't know how she knew. Knowing things without knowing how was becoming a specialty.

*

The trouble was that the voices had opinions about him too.

She heard it first in the classroom window on his third day. October light coming through the glass all gold and dusty, everyone reading quietly, and Mara staring out at the parking lot while pretending to read page forty-two for the fourth time. And there, under the wind, under the furnace hum, under everything: a voice saying a word over and over, soft as breath on cold glass.

*Thornbury. Thornbury. Thornbury.*

Not her name. His.

She sat up so fast her chair squeaked and Mr. Thornbury looked over and smiled at her—that smile he had, tired but real, like a lamp someone actually bothered to turn on—and Mara's chest did something complicated, because the voice in the window kept saying his name the way you'd say the name of somebody you were waiting for. Somebody expected. Somebody *wanted.*

She looked at the window. Just the ordinary reflection: desks, kids, the flag outside hanging limp. But underneath the ordinary, the way a photograph has things underneath it if you hold it to the light, she saw the classroom again, and it wasn't this classroom. Desks in different rows. A clock with hands stopped at 3:14. And shapes sitting very still in the seats, shapes like children but too smooth, like drawings of children done by someone who'd forgotten what children looked like.

One of them turned its head toward her.

Mara read page forty-two. She read it so hard her eyes hurt.

*

That night she tested things, because that was another thing about her—she couldn't leave a wound alone, she had to press it.

Her mother's good serving spoons, laid out on the counter for tomorrow's church potluck: silent. Polished to a shine and completely, insultingly silent. But the dented teaspoon she liked, the one with the worn spot where the silver had rubbed thin—there it was. Faint. Eleven heartbeats' worth of murmur, and threaded through it, still, patient:

*Thornbury.*

She checked the toaster. Nothing. The kettle, its curved belly holding a warped little world: nothing. The window over the sink, dark now, holding the kitchen inside itself like a jar: *Thornbury, Thornbury*, and beneath it, new tonight, a second word, spoken by a smaller voice, a girl-voice with gravel in it:

*Mara.*

Rule three activated. One person. She'd already chosen.

But not yet. Not until she understood more, because you couldn't bring someone a puzzle with pieces missing; her dad always said that about work orders, and it seemed like it should apply to hauntings too.

*

The worst discovery came on Sunday.

Church was the one place Mara couldn't escape, and she'd stopped fighting it, and instead she'd learned to catalog. Stained glass was safe—the colors broke reflections into useless confetti. The baptismal font was not safe, but it was covered, and covering worked, mostly, the way a bandage works on a bite. The danger was people. People's eyes.

She'd known for a year that eyes could carry it. Her own eyes, sometimes, in the morning, before she was fully awake—she'd catch movement in her own pupils that didn't match the room. But she'd assumed it was only her, because she was the weird one, the quiet one, the one who drew classrooms that didn't exist.

Then Mrs. Aldous turned around in the pew to pass the collection plate and smiled at Mara, and in the black of Mrs. Aldous's pupils, small and upside down and perfectly clear, Mara saw a boy.

Not reflected. Reflected was the room, the hats, the preacher's flailing hand. This boy was *in* the eye, standing in it like it was a doorway, and he was looking out at Mara with an expression she'd never seen on a living face: hope stretched so thin it had gone transparent.

His mouth moved. No sound crossed the distance between eye and ear—it wasn't that kind of traveling—but she could read lips, a little, from watching TV with the volume down after bedtime, and the boy's mouth made the same two syllables the windows and spoons had been making all week.

*Thorn-bur-y.*

Mara passed the plate. Her hands didn't shake, which surprised her. Inside she was shaking like a paint mixer, but her hands had learned something her insides hadn't: that in Hollow Creek, the surface stays calm no matter what's underneath. Maybe that was the town's whole religion, really. Keep the surface calm. Feed the surface. Never mind what the surface was floating on.

After the service, walking out past the graveyard with her parents, she counted headstones without meaning to. Old names. Voss, Aldous, Crane, Mercer, Thornbury—no, wait, no Thornburys, the Thornburys had all left or died out, her grandmother said, back before Mara was born. Back around—

Back around 1974.

Mara stopped walking. Her mother tugged her arm. *Come on, daydreamer.* And Mara came on, daydreamer, while behind her eyes the arithmetic finished itself: the voices had started getting loud right around when the new teacher arrived. The voices said his name like an appointment. And his family name fit a hole in the graveyard the way a key fits a lock you've never seen but dreamed about.

Whatever was waiting in the glass, it had been waiting for *him.*

*

Tuesday she stayed late for reading help, which was code for *Mara reads above grade level and needs somewhere to be until five*, and when the last bus pulled away she found herself walking past Room 4.

Room 4 was sealed. Everyone knew that the way everyone knew not to swim in the quarry. The door had a metal cover screwed over the handle, painted the same beige as everything, so that if you didn't know, you'd think it was a closet. But kids knew. Kids always know. There was a whole mythology about it—flood damage, asbestos, a teacher who went crazy—but the versions never matched, and mismatched stories were another Hollow Creek specialty. When a town lies to itself, it can't keep the lie consistent. That was how you knew.

Mr. Thornbury was standing in front of the door. Not knocking. Just standing, the way you stand in front of a painting, except his face wasn't admiring. It was doing the thing faces do at funerals.

Mara almost walked past. That was the smart move. Rule four, unwritten until now: never let an adult see you seeing.

But then he turned, and caught her, and instead of the practiced smile the other teachers wore like dentures, he gave her a small honest shrug, and said, "You ever wonder what's behind there?"

And Mara, who had spent her whole life giving the correct answer of *nothing*, heard herself say: "Every day."

Something moved between them then—not visible, not touchable, but real, the way the space between two magnets is real. He studied her a moment longer, and she watched him decide something, and she thought: *he's lonely here. He's lonely the way I'm lonely. The town does that to people who notice.*

"Shouldn't you be at pickup?" he asked gently.

"Shouldn't you be at the faculty meeting?" she returned, and he laughed—an actual laugh, startled out of him—and the sound of it carried down the hall and came back off the trophy case glass, and Mara heard, threaded faintly under the echo, eleven voices go quiet at once. Listening. Waiting.

They wanted him. All of them. And wanting, in Mara's experience of the glass, was never gentle.

*

She told him on Thursday.

Not because she planned to. Because the voices escalated, and escalation demanded action, and rule three had a deadline built into it that she'd never written down but always felt: tell them before they start saying things you can't repeat.

It happened at lunch. She'd brought soup in a thermos, and she'd forgotten about thermoses, thermoses were steel and curved and basically a spoon's evil cousin, and she poured it and the broth went still and there they were, all eleven, clearer than she'd ever seen them, standing in a row in the little brown circle like a choir photographed for a church bulletin. And the girl with gravel in her voice—the one who'd said *Mara*—looked up out of the soup and said, clear as a bell across a field:

*"Tell him we remember him. Tell him we remember his grandfather's lantern."*

Mara put the lid on the thermos. Walked it to the trash. Stood there breathing.

Then she waited for the recess bell, and found Mr. Thornbury erasing the board, and said, "Can I tell you something weird," and he set down the eraser and gave her his whole attention, which was a thing adults almost never did, and that alone nearly undid her.

"I hear them," she said. "In the glass. Windows and spoons and my mom's eyes and today my soup. Eleven of them. They're kids. And for the last week they've been saying your name." She made herself keep going, because stopping was how you lost your nerve forever. "Today one of them told me to tell you—they remember your grandfather's lantern."

The eraser dust hung in the light between them. Mr. Thornbury had gone a color Mara associated with milk left out.

"My grandfather," he said carefully, "lived in Ohio."

"Did he?" Mara said. "Or did he live here, in the caretaker's house, the one you're renting?"

The silence lasted long enough for the hallway clock to tick eight times. She counted. Counting was how she stayed brave.

"Mara," he finally said, "how long have you been hearing this?"

"Since I was six. The spoons first." She hugged her backpack straps. "I have rules about it. Don't feel bad that you don't. Nobody here has rules. That's the problem with this town. Everybody agreed to forget, so nobody learned how to *handle* it." She hesitated, then gave him the last piece, the one that had been burning a hole in her since Sunday. "There's something else. In the glass, sometimes, there's a woman. Older than the kids. A teacher, I think. She stands apart from them, near a desk, and she never says anything. She just mouths the same thing over and over." Mara made her mouth do the shape she'd memorized. "*Don't end up like me.*"

Mr. Thornbury sat down slowly in a student desk far too small for him, and for a moment he looked less like a teacher than like a man reading his own obituary in a language he almost spoke.

"They know my name," he said. It wasn't a question.

"They've known it since you drove into town," Mara said. "I think—" and here was the thing she'd been circling for days, the thing that had finally pushed her past rule three, "—I think they've been waiting fifty years for somebody whose family was *there*. Somebody who owes them. And I think the glass doesn't care whether you personally did anything wrong. Glass keeps accounts, not excuses."

He looked at her then—really looked, the way you look at a person and revise everything you assumed—and whatever he saw made him nod slowly.

"Why are you telling me?" he asked. "You could get in trouble. Your parents—"

"My parents signed whatever everybody signed," Mara said flatly. "Everybody here signed something, even if it was just agreeing to shut up at dinner. I'm not signing." She shifted her weight. "Also, they said your name. And before that, for years, they said mine. Nobody else ever says my name in the glass. Just those two names. Yours and mine." She shrugged, aiming for casual and missing by a mile. "So whatever this is, it's got us both in it. Seems stupid to be in it separately."

For the first time since she'd met him, Mr. Thornbury smiled with his whole face, tired and scared and grateful all mixed together, like weather.

"Okay," he said. "Together, then. But Mara—if the voices start saying *your* name differently. If they stop sounding like waiting and start sounding like *calling*—you come to me immediately. Promise me."

"I promise," Mara said, and meant it, and hated how easily she meant it, because promises to adults in this town had a history of curdling.

She was almost to the door when the voice came—not through any glass at all this time, but straight into her ear, close as a secret, a small boy's voice with river stones in it:

*"Tell him the moon sets early on harvest night. Tell him we only get the one."*

Mara turned around. Mr. Thornbury was watching her face, and whatever he saw there made him stand up fast.

"What is it? What did they say?"

Mara swallowed. Outside the window, far over the corn, the October sun was already leaning west, and somewhere beyond the edge of town, the harvest moon was fattening toward full.

"They said," she said, "that we're running out of time."
