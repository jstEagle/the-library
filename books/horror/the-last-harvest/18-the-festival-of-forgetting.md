## 18. The Festival of Forgetting

**Ines**

The moon was already bleeding when he reached the square.

It hung low over the grain elevator, swollen and orange, the color of something that had been drowned and pulled back up. Ines kept to the edge of the crowd, the tin box pressed flat against his ribs beneath his coat, and watched Hollow Creek assemble itself for the oldest lie in town.

They'd strung lights between the poles of the basketball hoops. A stage of plywood pallets stood where the dunk tank went in June. Folding chairs in crooked rows, maybe three hundred people, and every one of them wearing the same expression he'd first seen on the faces in the 1974 photograph: pleasant, attentive, and empty as a scraped bowl.

The band played something old. Not old like a folk song—old like a hymn that had outlived its god. The melody kept circling back on itself, never resolving, and Ines realized after a full verse that it wasn't supposed to. It was a lullaby built to never end, because the children it was sung to were never supposed to wake up.

He found a place near the cider stand, half in shadow, and let the crowd close around him like water. Nobody looked at him. That was the strange mercy of the last two weeks—being forgotten made you invisible. The butcher who'd chased him out of his own rented house on Tuesday would have walked past him now without a flicker. The school secretary had asked him, twice in one day, if he was lost.

*They know your name now,* Mara had said, and she'd been wrong in only one detail. They knew it the way you know a stain on a ceiling—something to be painted over.

On the stage, Mayor Aldous Cray stepped up to the microphone. He looked smaller than he had in his office, when he'd gripped Ines's wrist and begged him to understand. Smaller and older, like a man wearing a suit that had been let out too many times.

"Friends," Cray said. "Neighbors. Family."

The word *family* landed on the crowd like a hand on a shoulder. Three hundred people exhaled at once.

"Tonight we give thanks for the harvest. Tonight we give back what the land gives us, and—" a pause, rehearsed down to the half-second, "—and what the land asks of us. As it has always asked. As our grandparents asked. As our grandchildren will ask."

Ines's grandfather had stood on that same stage. He'd seen it in the mirror, in the scene that played behind the glass like a film no one had developed for fifty years: lantern light in a cellar, eleven small chairs, and the faces of the founding families arranged in a half circle, watching. His grandfather's face among them. His grandfather's hands holding the ledger open while the caretaker wrote the entries.

*Deliveries. Eleven, complete.*

The band played the circling hymn again. Ines checked the sky.

The moon sat maybe two handspans above the elevator. It would touch the treeline in an hour, maybe less. Behind it, somewhere out past the school, the black glass in the cellar was filling with light—he could feel it the way you feel weather coming in a bad tooth. The eleven were pressing their palms to the wall. He could almost hear them.

Not almost. He could hear them. Faint, through the crowd noise, through the band, through the plywood and the cider and the three hundred empty faces: eleven voices saying one word over and over, in the patient way children repeat things when the adults aren't listening.

*Before. Before. Before.*

He touched the tin box through his coat. Eleven birth certificates, soft as cloth from fifty years in the dark, and the twelfth page with his family's name on it, the page that proved the Thornburys hadn't just watched. They'd signed.

"Before the blessing," Cray said, "we welcome the offering."

The crowd turned as one body.

Ines's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

They were bringing Mara up the center aisle.

* * *

**Mara**

They had told her it was an honor.

Her mother had braided her hair that afternoon with shaking hands, and her father had knelt in front of her and said, *You're the strongest one, Mara-bug. The others were too old. You're just right.* And she had looked at his face and understood, the way she understood the whispers in the windows and the spoons and her mother's eyes, that he believed it. That was the worst part of Hollow Creek. Not the lying. The believing.

They walked her up the center aisle between two rows of folding chairs, and the whole town turned to watch her, and she did what she'd been practicing in the bathroom mirror for three days, which was to look at nobody.

But she looked at everybody, too, the way you can without moving your eyes. She found her grandmother's empty chair—Grandma had died in March, but her chair was there anyway, because in Hollow Creek you kept the seats of the dead. She found Mrs. Pelletier from the library, who had slipped Ines the photograph, sitting very straight with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral she approved of. She found the deputy who had told her father *it's for the good of the town, kid* in a voice meant to be kind.

And she found Mr. Thornbury.

He was standing by the cider stand with his collar up, and he was so still that if you didn't know to look you'd think he was a post or a shadow. But she knew to look. She'd known since the day she drew the sealed classroom and couldn't stop drawing it, since the day the eleven started saying his name through the glass in her mom's jewelry box.

*Ines. Ines. Ines.* They said it like they were learning it. Like it was a seed they were planting in the dark.

The men walked her up the stage steps. Mayor Cray took her hand. His palm was damp and cold, like a fish, and when he smiled down at her his eyes were wet.

"What's your name, sweetheart?" he asked, loud, for the crowd.

"Mara Voss," she said, because lying was their job, not hers.

"Mara Voss," Cray repeated, and the crowd murmured it back like a grace before meat, and Mara felt the name leave her body a little. That was how it worked. She'd figured it out from the whispers. Every time they said your name in the right way, at the right hour, over the right circle, a little of you went into the sound, and the sound went into the ground, and the ground kept it.

They were going to plant her.

*Not yet,* she told the eleven, inside her head, the way she talked to them now. *Not yet, not yet, hold on.*

The smallest one—she thought of her as Penny, because that was the name that came back strongest through the glass, Penelope Ann Reave, born 1963—answered in the voice like a cold spoon against your teeth:

*The moon is low. The moon is low. Is he there?*

Mara looked at the cider stand. At the still shadow with the collar up.

*He's here,* she said. *Hold on.*

* * *

**Ines**

They put Mara at the center of a chalk circle.

He saw it from thirty yards and his blood went to ice water: the same circle, the same geometry, that he'd found in the cellar under the sealed classroom. Eleven small chairs had been carried up onto the stage and arranged around it, facing inward, empty. The town had kept them. Fifty years, and they'd kept the chairs in a barn somewhere, dusted them, waited for the harvest when the land would ask again.

Cray raised his arms. The band stopped mid-phrase, the circling hymn cut off like a snapped thread, and in the silence Ines heard the whole town breathing.

"The land gives," Cray said.

"The land gives," three hundred voices answered.

"The land takes."

"The land takes."

"We do not ask why."

The pause stretched. Ines's heart slammed against the tin box. *We do not ask why.* That was the whole town, the whole fifty years, the whole rotten covenant in five words. His grandfather had probably stood in this crowd saying it. Saying it while the lanterns went down the cellar stairs.

"We do not ask why," the crowd said, softer.

Ines began to move.

Not toward the stage—toward the edge of the square, where the generator cables ran, where the floodlights that lit the stage were daisy-chained off a single box. He'd watched the setup that afternoon from the school roof, doing the one thing he was still good at, the thing nobody forgot to let a stranger do: standing around looking like he belonged. The box had a plywood cover. The cover had no lock.

He didn't touch it. Not yet. Timing was everything now; the moon was a handspan above the trees and falling.

Because here was the arithmetic he'd done all day, walking the perimeter of the town like a ghost auditing its own funeral: the ritual needed the moon. The erasure was a transaction, and the setting harvest moon was the closing of the bank. Cray had said it in his office with tears in his eyes—*it has to be tonight, it has to be before it sets, or the ground goes hungry and takes on its own terms.* The town had one window. One hour, maybe less.

And Ines had one job in that hour: to be louder than three hundred people.

He drifted along the back of the crowd, and the crowd let him, because the crowd had no idea he existed. He passed the deputy. Passed Mrs. Pelletier, whose eyes flicked to him—she still half-saw him, the librarian with the archive fire in her past, the one person who'd kept a photograph when the county burned the rest—and she looked away fast, hands tightening in her lap. Not betrayal. Fear. He'd stopped taking it personally around the time his own bank called him *sir* and asked if he was an authorized user.

On the stage, Cray knelt beside Mara and produced something from his coat.

A knife? Ines's whole body clenched—but no. A book. A ledger, old and swollen, and Ines knew it even from thirty yards, knew the water-stained cover, because he'd burned its twin two nights ago in a barrel behind the school and watched the crops die by morning.

The original. Or a copy. The *book of giving*, Cray had called it once, in a moment of drunken honesty Ines had never been meant to hear. The book where the town wrote down what it owed and what it paid.

Cray opened it on his knees. He dipped a pen. And he began, in a voice pitched to carry, to read.

"In the year of our town, one hundred and eleven, we gave thanks for the harvest, and the harvest was plentiful, and we gave back—"

And here it came. Ines knew what came next. He'd read the folklore until his eyes burned; he knew the shape of the rite. The mayor would read the names of the givers, the founding families, the covenant—*Thornbury, Cray, Voss, Pelletier, Reave*—and each name spoken aloud would seal the bargain tighter, and then he would take the child's name, the name her parents had already surrendered, and write it in the book, and speak it into the circle, and the ground would drink it, and by morning no one in Hollow Creek would remember there had ever been a girl named Mara Voss. There would be a chair in a classroom with nobody in it, and nobody would wonder why.

The same trick they'd pulled in 1974. The same trick, one more time, forever, because the land always asked again.

"—and we give back, gladly, the child Mara—"

*Now.*

Ines hit the generator box with his shoulder, tore the plywood cover off, and ripped the main cable free.

The stage went dark.

The floodlights died, the string lights died, the whole square dropped into orange moonlight and sudden enormous shadow, and three hundred people made a sound he'd never heard a crowd make—not a scream, not a gasp, but a single collective intake of breath, like one animal.

And in the dark, Ines walked toward the stage carrying the tin box, and he began, quietly at first, to say the names.

* * *

**Mara**

The dark was the best thing that had ever happened to her.

She'd been sitting in the circle with her hands in her lap, being good, being *just right*, and the mayor's pen had been scratching, and the eleven had been screaming through the ground under her knees—not words anymore, just a sound like wind through a keyhole—and then the lights died and everything stopped.

In the dark, nobody knew where the circle was. The chalk was still there but the dark made it nothing, made it a story, and Mara did the thing she'd promised herself she'd do if she got one single chance.

She stepped out of the circle.

One step. Her sneaker came down on plywood, outside the chalk line, and the wind-through-the-keyhole sound under the ground changed—it went from screaming to something like a held breath.

Then a voice in the dark, coming closer, saying names.

She knew the voice. She'd know it anywhere, through any glass, through the ground, through her own teeth. It was Mr. Thornbury, and he wasn't whispering the way the town whispered names, the way you whisper so the ground won't hear. He was saying them *out loud*. Full voice. Getting louder.

"Penelope Ann Reave. Thomas Reave. Dorothy Voss—"

The crowd made a sound like it had been struck.

"—Samuel Ostrander. Ruth Ostrander. James—"

"Stop him," the mayor's voice said, high and cracked, and it wasn't a mayor's voice anymore, it was just an old man's voice, scared in the dark. "Somebody stop him, the moon—the moon is—"

Flashlights clicked on. A dozen beams swung around, wild, and one of them caught Mr. Thornbury crossing the square with the tin box open under his arm, white papers flashing, and he didn't run and he didn't hide. He walked straight through the light like he'd decided the light couldn't hurt him anymore.

"—James Ellery Cray," he said, and the mayor sat down hard on the stage.

Mara started toward him and her father's hand closed on her arm.

"Mara—*Mara*—" he said, and his voice broke in the middle of her name, and she looked up at him and saw something terrible and wonderful: he was trying to remember her. She could see him trying. His eyes were going over her face like a man reading a letter in a language he used to speak, and the moonlight was sliding off her like water off wax, because the circle had already started taking her, and he was *losing* her, right there, holding onto her arm.

"Daddy," she said. "Listen to him. Just listen."

And down at the end of the square, over the treeline, the moon touched the top of the tallest oak.

It had maybe twenty minutes left.

Ines Thornbury stepped up onto the stage, into the flashlight beams, papers in his hands, and the whole town of Hollow Creek turned to look at the man they had spent two weeks forgetting, and could not, quite, look away from.

He looked down at Mara first. Just for a second. And he smiled—not a brave smile, not a teacher smile, a real one, scared and certain at the same time—and then he looked up at the crowd, and at the moon, and he lifted the first page of the tin box into the light.

"Her name is Penelope Ann Reave," he said. "And she was born on the fourth of June, 1963, and you took her."

The ground under the square began, very softly, to hum.
