## 19. Speak Their Names

The moon was already bleeding down toward the treeline when I reached the edge of the crowd.

It hung low and swollen over the cornfields, the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges, and every face below it was turned up toward the stage where Mayor Aldous Prewitt stood with his arms raised like a man conducting weather. Behind him, the harvest queen's throne sat empty—except it wasn't empty anymore. Mara sat in it, small and straight-backed, her hands folded in her lap like a child at a piano recital. They'd dressed her in white. They'd braided her hair with wheat.

She wasn't crying. That was the worst of it. She'd told me once that she cried when she was scared, but what she felt now was something past fear, something older, and she had chosen to carry it without tears because she'd seen me do the same.

The tin box was under my coat, strapped against my chest with the belt from my bathrobe, because I hadn't owned anything else that would hold. Eleven birth certificates, soft as cloth from fifty years in the dark. And the twelfth page—the one with my family's name on it—I'd left at the house, weighted under the ledger's ashes on the kitchen table, so that if they took me tonight, someone might someday find it and understand that the Thornburys had been in the cellar too, holding lanterns.

Prewitt was speaking. His voice carried the way voices do in towns built for listening.

"—and as the moon sets, we give back to the earth what the earth has given us, and we give thanks for our abundance, our health, our children—" He faltered, just slightly, on *children*, and the falter rippled through the crowd like wind through wheat. "—our prosperity, which has never failed us, which will never fail us, so long as we remember what must be forgotten."

*What must be forgotten.* Fifty years of saying it until it sounded like scripture.

I moved through them. Nobody looked at me. That should have been a mercy, but I knew better now; the not-looking was the mechanism itself, the town's attention sliding off me like water off waxed paper. A woman I'd bought eggs from three weeks running stared directly through my chest. A man whose name I'd never learned turned his shoulder into my path and didn't flinch when I passed through the space he'd tried to occupy. I was becoming a rumor in real time. By morning there would be no paycheck to bounce, no landlord who remembered renting to anyone, no university that had ever issued me a degree. The erasure was patient. It only needed the moon to finish setting.

Mara saw me before anyone else did. Of course she did. Her eyes found mine across forty feet of strangers and held on, and her chin lifted—not a nod, nothing so dangerous—a small adjustment, the way you straighten your back when the hard part is coming. *I'm ready,* she was saying. *Do it.*

I started toward the stage.

* * *

They caught me ten feet from the steps, and it wasn't violent, which somehow made it worse. Hands simply arrived—on my shoulders, my elbows, the box against my ribs—and steered me sideways with the gentle, terrible competence of people who have done this before. Maybe many times. Maybe once every generation, when someone remembered too much.

"Mr. Thornbury." Prewitt had come down off the stage. Up close, in the lantern light, he looked less like a mayor than like a man holding shut a door with his whole body. "Don't do this."

"You know I'm going to."

"I know." He said it with something close to tenderness. "I've known since the day you came asking about the file. And I want you to understand—I'm not threatening you. There's no need. You're doing this to yourself. If you say those names, you don't just unmake us. You unmake everything we built on top of them. The hospital. The school roof. The wells that never went dry while every farm around us burned. My granddaughter's surgery. All of it stands on that silence, and silence is load-bearing, son. Pull it out and the whole house comes down on everyone inside it. Including you."

"Including Mara," I said.

His jaw worked. "She was going to be given to them," he admitted, and the crowd around us made no sound at all, because they all knew, because they had all agreed, because that was what Hollow Creek did—it agreed. "One every fifty years. It's how the bargain renews. She'd have gone into the glass and the town would have another half century, and in fifty years nobody would remember her either, and it would be *kind*, by then. Forgetting is kind. That's the mercy we built. That's the only mercy there is."

"The eleven in the glass would disagree."

"The eleven aren't *anything*," he snapped, and for one second the mask tore and I saw underneath: not a monster, an accountant. A man balancing a ledger written by his grandfather's grandfather. "That's the entire point. They were made into nothing so that we could be something. Every town does it. We're just honest enough to keep the receipts."

He nodded, and the hands tightened, and I understood that they weren't going to hurt me. They were going to hold me, gently, until the moon set, and then I would simply be a man who had come to a festival and stood in a crowd and could not afterward be picked out of any photograph. The teacher before me had probably been held exactly this way. Politely. By neighbors.

But they were holding my arms, and the box was strapped to my chest, and I had spent two weeks learning the names by heart because I had always known paper could be taken from me.

I began to speak.

* * *

"Alice Ruth Wexler."

The first name came out of me like something that had been waiting behind my teeth for fifty years to get through.

The effect was immediate and physical. Every head within earshot snapped toward me—not toward my voice, I realized, but toward the sound itself, the way dogs turn at a frequency humans can't hear. Because the name was audible to them in a way my voice hadn't been. The name cut through the erasure like light through a crack. Somewhere behind me, a window shattered.

"Dorothy Mae Ellingham."

A woman screamed. Not in pain—in recognition. She pressed both hands over her mouth and screamed again into them, and I knew her, I'd seen her at the market, and I understood that Dorothy Mae Ellingham had been her sister, or her cousin, or her friend, and that for fifty years the grief had lived in her body with no name attached, a wound that couldn't close because it couldn't remember what had cut it.

"Thomas James Okafor."

The lanterns guttered. All of them, all down the square, flames bending sideways though there was no wind, and above us the setting moon seemed to swell, to lean, to *listen*.

"Edith Louise Carrow."

I kept walking forward as I spoke, and the hands on my arms loosened, because the men holding me were turning their heads toward the storefronts, toward the dark windows of the hardware store and the bank and the diner, and the windows were filling with light. Not reflecting the lanterns. Generating it. A pale, sourceless glow rising in every pane of glass in Hollow Creek, and behind the glow, shapes. Small shapes. Pressing palms flat against the inside of the world.

"Henry Paul Voss."

Glass cracked somewhere in a long diagonal run, like ice giving way on a pond in spring. Then another pane. Then a dozen, a chorus of fracture singing through the square, and none of the glass fell. It bulged outward instead, softly, the way fabric does when a hand pushes it from behind.

"Samuel Robert Finch."

Prewitt grabbed my collar. "Stop," he whispered, and his whisper was the most honest thing he'd ever said to me, because it wasn't a command. It was begging. "Please. You don't know what's on the other side of that glass after fifty years. You don't know what comes out."

"No," I said. "But I know what went in."

"Margaret Anne Hollis."

The black glass wall—I could feel it even here, even blocks away from the school, the way you feel a tooth ache in your jaw. It was thinning. The whole night was thinning, reality going translucent at the edges, and through the translucence I heard them. Not words. A hum. Eleven voices that had spent five decades learning to be one voice, rising like a chord held too long.

"Charles Edwin Marsh."

The crowd was breaking apart, not fleeing—there was nowhere to flee—but scattering like a dropped handful of marbles, adults stumbling away from windows, away from car windshields, away from their own reflections in the chrome of pickup bumpers. A man fell to his knees in front of the diner window and clawed at his own face, and I heard him say, over and over, "I held the lantern, I held the lantern, I held the lantern—"

"Frances Irene Delgado."

The moon touched the treeline. Half its disc still showed, a sliver of borrowed light, and I had four names left and maybe ninety seconds, and my voice was tearing, and I had never in my life wanted to finish anything so badly.

"Peter John Ashford."

The first mirror burst.

Not the windows—the great ornate mirror from the town hall, hauled out every festival to stand at the edge of the square as tradition demanded, though no one could say why. It exploded outward in a bloom of silver light, and out of the light stepped a shape that made the whole square go silent.

She was eleven years old and she was sixty-one years old, both at once, and neither, and the truth of her was some third thing that my eyes refused to hold. Alice Ruth Wexler walked out of the broken mirror onto the packed dirt of the square, and with every step she aged, decades pouring through her body like water through a sieve—girl, girl, girl, young woman, woman, gray-templed, bent—and then, impossibly, she settled. She settled at last into the age she should have been, the age stolen from her, a woman of sixty-one standing in the town that had erased her, looking down at her own weathered hands with an expression I will carry into whatever waits for me after this life.

Wonder. Just wonder. At having hands again. At having *years* again.

"Ruth Ellen Pryce," I shouted, voice gone to gravel.

The bank window burst. Thomas James Okafor came through it in a flood of light, aging as he crossed the threshold, and behind him Samuel Robert Finch, and Margaret Anne Hollis, and the diner's plate glass gave way in a single ringing note and three more silhouettes stepped out of the brightness, and the square filled with the sound of fifty years arriving all at once—joints settling, breath drawn deep, voices cracking on their first word in half a century.

"Joseph Daniel Reyes."

Two left. The moon was a fingernail above the trees.

"Walter Hugh Brennan."

One. The crowd had stopped screaming. That was the thing no one would believe later—they had stopped screaming and gone utterly still, the whole town, watching the dead come home, and the faces I could see in the lantern light were not horrified. They were *starving*. Fifty years of not-looking breaking all at once, everyone gorging on the sight of what they'd refused to see, weeping without knowing why, touching their own mouths as if checking whether they were allowed to speak.

I looked at the last page in my mind. I had read it a hundred times. I had dreamed it.

"Lillian Grace Thornbury."

My grandfather's sister. The twelfth grave under the flagpole. The reason my family's name was in the ledger, in the cellar, in the lantern light of 1974. Lillian Grace Thornbury, who was not among the eleven, because she had not been taken—she had been *given*, by her own family, the year the bargain was struck, the founding payment, the seed corn. The other eleven had followed because the first had worked.

The last mirror in the square—the one in the barbershop window, untouched until now—filled with light so bright it threw shadows backward, and a figure stepped through, and she was old, older than the others, because she had waited longer, since 1954, since the beginning, and when she turned her face to the crowd she wore my cheekbones.

She looked at me. She looked at me the way you look at a door you knocked on for seventy years.

"Thank you," said Lillian Grace Thornbury, in a voice like dry leaves, and then, to the frozen town, to the kneeling mayor, to the founders' descendants with lantern-oil still in their family stories: "Now look at us. All of you. Look, and say what you see."

And the moon slipped below the treeline, and every mirror in Hollow Creek went dark at once—

—and the eleven turned, together, toward the crowd, and began to walk.
