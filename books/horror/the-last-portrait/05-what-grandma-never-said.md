## 5. What Grandma Never Said

The desk had been locked for as long as Astrid could remember, which was itself a kind of information. Children notice what adults guard. She'd been six the first time she asked about it—a rolltop desk of dark walnut squatting in the corner of grandma's study like a toad that had learned patience—and grandma had said, *That's where I keep the accounts,* and smiled, and Astrid had understood even then that the smile was a door closing.

*Accounts.* Grandma had kept accounts her whole life. Accounts of eggs traded with the Hendersons, accounts of who owed whom a casserole after a funeral, accounts of every dollar Tomas and Astrid had ever been slipped on birthdays. Her ledgers were famous in the family, neat columns in a hand so precise it looked printed. When she died, the accountant's discipline was the thing people mentioned first at the reception, right after her pies.

So it made a certain terrible sense that the desk would be locked, and that the key would be nowhere obvious, and that Astrid would spend two days of sorting before she thought to look inside the hollow brass finial of the desk lamp, because grandma never hid anything where a person wouldn't think to be clever enough to find it. Grandma believed in rewarding attention. That was practically her religion: pay attention, and you will be paid.

The key fit. Of course it fit.

Astrid sat down in the study with the late afternoon coming slantwise through the curtains, dust turning gold above the carpet, and rolled the top back.

The first thing she saw was paper. Not money—paper. Reams of it, decades of it, stacked in careful bundles tied with kitchen twine: grocery lists, water bills from three different decades, the program from grandpa's funeral, Astrid's own third-grade spelling bee certificate. Grandma had kept everything. Astrid had always found this endearing, an old woman's sentimentality. Sitting there now, with the drawer open and the house silent around her except for the small settling sounds old houses make when they're being watched, she felt the tenderness curdle into something else. This wasn't sentimentality. Sentiment doesn't organize by year.

Beneath the paper, flat against the bottom of the drawer, wrapped in a flour sack towel gone soft with age, was a ledger.

It was smaller than her other ones—schoolhouse-sized, black cloth boards worn through to gray cardboard at the corners. The spine was cracked white. On the cover, in that printed-looking hand, was written:

**ACCOUNTS — 1961**

And beneath that, smaller:

*to the Well.*

Astrid didn't open it right away. She held it on her knees and listened to the house. Down the hall, Tomas was running tape over boxes, the shriek-and-rip of it rhythmic, ordinary, human. Outside, the dry well stood capped under its stone in the browned grass, and she caught herself listening for it anyway—for water sounds, for dripping, for anything—though she knew better than anyone that the well had been dry since before she was born. Dry wells don't drip. Dry wells are just holes.

She opened the ledger.

The first entry was dated June 14th, 1961, and read, in full:

*Paid: one sack flour (half). Reason: asking.*

Asking. Asking for what? Astrid turned the page. The entries came steadily after that, two or three a week through June, then daily through July, each one in the same immaculate hand, each one addressed to no recipient but the one named on the cover. The payments were small and strange and got stranger as the summer went on:

*Paid: three coins (silver). Reason: rain not come.*

*Paid: bread ends, seven days' worth. Reason: the garden.*

*Paid: hair, cut and burned. Reason: asking again.*

*Paid: blood, four drops, thumb. Reason: the cows.*

Blood-typed notes. Astrid had seen the phrase in her own mind before she saw it on the page—the entries shifted partway through July, the handwriting tightening, the ink going brownish in a way that ink shouldn't, and some entries were no longer written in ink at all. They were pressed. Dotted into the paper in rows of tiny punctures, like something had bitten the ledger and left words behind. She ran her fingertip over one and felt the faint ridges of scar tissue in the page.

*Paid: blood, a saucerful. Reason: Ada is sick.*

There it was. Ada. Great-aunt Ada, who existed in the family as a name attached to nothing—a face in two photographs, a rumor of a man in Baton Rouge she'd supposedly gone off with, a silence shaped exactly like a person. Astrid had grown up understanding that you did not ask about Ada the way you did not ask about the stone on the well: not because it was forbidden, exactly, but because the not-asking was load-bearing. Pull at it and something above you might come down.

Grandma had been nineteen in the summer of 1961. Nineteen years old, writing accounts to a hole in the ground, paying it flour and silver and her own blood, and the reasons column told the whole story without ever once telling it: *rain not come. the garden. the cows. Ada is sick. Ada won't eat. Ada talks at night. Ada went out to the well and we heard her talking.*

Astrid turned pages faster. The August entries were daily now, sometimes twice a day, the handwriting beginning to fray at the edges like rope under strain:

*Paid: the good mirror. Reason: it watches me.*

*Paid: Sunday dress. Reason: it knows my size.*

*Paid: nothing today. Reason: it says nothing is also payment.*

Her mouth had gone dry. She was aware of the light failing in the room, the gold dust gone to gray, aware that she hadn't turned on a lamp, aware that some animal part of her had decided that light falling on the desk was fine but light summoned by her hand would constitute a request, and requests, apparently, were how all this started.

The last page of August was dated August 30th, 1961. It read:

*Paid: —*

The reason column said only: *rain.*

One word. And below it, in a different pressure of pen, harder, dug in, almost torn through the paper:

*PAID IN FULL. DO NOT REOPEN THIS ACCOUNT.*

Below that, at the very bottom of the page, in handwriting Astrid recognized with a lurch because it was still alive in her memory—looser, younger, shaking—grandma had written a single line that was not an entry at all:

*Forgive me. It was hungry and we fed it and now it knows our table.*

Astrid closed the ledger.

She sat for a long time in the darkening study with her hands flat on the cover, feeling the cloth boards warm under her palms as if the book had been sitting in the sun, though the room was cool. Betrayal was not the right word and she kept reaching for it anyway, because grief wants simple words. Grandma had lied to her—or rather, had built an entire architecture around the lie: the sealed well, the untouchable stone, the family silence, sixty years of *we don't discuss that side of things*, all of it masonry laid brick by brick over a summer when a starving family had paid tribute to a hole in the ground until the hole paid them back in rain and took a sister as change.

And Astrid had loved her. That was the part that hurt in a place betrayal couldn't reach. She had loved her, and the love was real, and the love had been spent partly on a fiction—which meant some portion of every kindness, every pie, every birthday dollar, had been interest paid on a debt Astrid had never known she was carrying.

Or—she made herself consider it, because she was a documentarian, because documentation was a kind of prayer and prayers should be honest—maybe it was the opposite. Maybe the ledgers, the lists, the ferocious record-keeping of a whole life, had been penance. Maybe grandma had spent sixty years writing everything down because once, in the worst summer of her life, she had written things down *to something*, and the habit had never unclenched.

*A seat forever.* The phrase surfaced from nowhere and Astrid flinched, because it arrived with a taste, faint and specific: well water, metallic, cold. She had never drunk well water from this property. She was almost sure she hadn't.

Down the hall, the tape gun had stopped.

"Tess?" Tomas called out—no. "Astrid?" His voice had a strange flatness to it, the tone people use when they're trying not to scare someone and failing. "Can you come here a second?"

She put the ledger in her bag. Not in a drawer, not back in the desk—her bag, against her body, where she could feel its weight. Some instinct older than reasoning told her that this account was not closed, whatever the last page claimed, and that she did not want it out of arm's reach tonight.

In the hallway, the house smelled faintly of ash, though nothing was burning.

Tomas was standing in the front room with the photograph albums open on the floor around him, and he didn't turn around when she came in. He was looking at the wall of framed pictures grandma had never taken down, and his hand was raised halfway toward one of them, hovering, not quite touching.

"Astrid," he said quietly. "When you sorted the study. Did you move any of these frames?"

"No."

"Okay." He swallowed. "Okay. Because there's a chair in this one." He pointed at the wedding photo, 1959, the whole family arranged on the lawn. "And there's a chair in that one." The christening, 1963. "And I have been through every picture in this house, and there is a chair in *all of them*, and I need you to tell me you've noticed it too, because if I'm the only one seeing it—"

"I see it," Astrid said.

In the photographs, plain as furniture, stood a wooden chair that matched nothing else in any of the rooms. In the oldest pictures it stood far back, near the treeline, easy to mistake for a shadow. In the newest ones—the Christmas photo from last December, grandma laughing in her cardigan—it stood just inside the doorway.

Closer every year. Patient as an entry in a ledger.

Astrid's bag, with the ledger inside, felt suddenly warm against her hip, like something sleeping there had turned over.

"We're documenting it," she said. "All of it. Starting tonight."

* * *

They worked past midnight, Astrid photographing every image while Tomas logged positions in a fresh notebook—her notebook, the hospice one, the ritual journal, because it was the only paper in the house she trusted herself to write straight in. Chair near the treeline, 1959. Chair at the fence, 1971. Chair on the porch, 1998. Chair in the hallway, 2019. Chair at the edge of the kitchen, last December.

At 2 a.m., Tomas fell asleep in the armchair mid-sentence, pencil still in his hand.

At 3:07, Astrid, alone at the kitchen table with the albums spread out like a patient chart, heard—from deep under the floorboards, from somewhere below the foundation, below the dry capped well, below anything with a right to hold water—a single drop fall.

Then another.

Counting.

She wrote the time in the notebook with a steady hand, because her hands had learned steadiness in rooms where people died, and underneath it she wrote the only entry the night allowed her:

*The account is not closed. Something has begun making payments back.*
