## 11. Invited Guests

The light began to fail at 4:40 in the afternoon, the way it does in late November—not a sunset so much as a surrender, the sky giving up its gray in slow increments until the windows of grandma's house held nothing but the reflection of the kitchen behind me. I had been sitting at the table for three hours with the ledger open in front of me, and I had not turned on a single lamp. I told myself it was because I wanted to watch the yard. The truth was that I was afraid of what the light switch might sound like in the silence, and of what might answer it.

The chair was at the table.

It had been at the table since morning, since I'd come down and found it drawn up to the fourth place—the place that had always been grandma's, facing the window, its back to the door. It was the chair from the photographs, the one that had spent sixty years creeping closer in the background of every family image, and now it had arrived. It sat there with a terrible ordinariness, pine wood gone honey-colored with age, a hairline crack running down one back slat. It did not look like a threat. It looked like furniture that had finally been allowed to do its job.

I had not touched it. I had made coffee at the counter with my back to it, and I could feel it the way you feel someone standing behind you in an elevator—not a presence exactly, but a rearrangement of the air, a claim on the room's attention.

Tomas had gone to the hardware store in town. I had let him go. He needed to be somewhere with fluorescent lighting and a man named Dale who talked about gutter guards, and I needed—I told myself—an hour alone with the ledger. What I actually needed was to stop being brave in front of my brother, because bravery is a performance, and performances require an audience, and alone in the house I could finally let my hands shake.

They were shaking now. The ledger's pages trembled like something breathing under them.

*To the Well,* the entries said. *To the Well. To the Well.* Flour in the hungry years. Coins in the lean ones. And in 1961, the summer the rain stopped, the handwriting changed—tighter, pressed hard enough to tear the paper—*Paid in full, God forgive us. Paid in full.*

Paid with what, Grandma. The question had been sitting in my chest all day like a stone I'd swallowed.

The dusk came down over the yard, and the well out back became a darker circle in the darkening grass, and that was when the first of them knocked.

Not at the door. At the *window*—a soft, courteous double tap, knuckle on glass, the way family announces itself when the door is unlocked and they don't want to presume. I turned and Uncle Henri was standing on the back porch.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was always my defense, and because no one will believe me otherwise. Uncle Henri had been dead for eleven years. I had stood at his graveside in a green coat, and Tomas had cried, and the casket had gone down into ground I could have told you the composition of. He was standing on the porch in the coat he'd been buried in, and he looked—this is the part that undid me—he looked *well*. Restored. His face full where the cancer had hollowed it, his hands unspotted, his eyes clear and brown and patient. He looked like the photograph from Tomas's graduation, the good year, the year before.

He did not try the door. He stood on the porch with his hands folded, and through the glass he said, in a voice muffled but unmistakably his, the voice that had taught me to fish off the pier at Lacassine:

"Astrid. May I come in?"

Everything in my body said *yes*. That is the thing I need you to understand, the thing I could not have understood from the outside, from the safe side of any of this. The dead do not arrive looking like horror. They arrive looking like *homecoming*. Every instinct I had, every warm memory, every grief I had never finished grieving leaned toward that door like a plant toward light. My hand was on the deadbolt before I knew I'd moved it.

I thought of the ledger. *To the Well.* I thought of the chair at the table. I thought: hospitality is a door, and a door opens both ways.

"No," I said, to the glass, to my dead uncle. "I'm sorry. No."

He didn't argue. He didn't look hurt, which was worse. He nodded, slow and gracious, the way you nod when a host tells you the table isn't ready, and he said, "Of course, sweetheart. Whenever you're ready. We're not going anywhere."

And he walked—not faded, *walked*, unhurried, his footsteps creaking the porch boards—around the side of the house, out of sight.

I stood with my hand still on the deadbolt for a long time. Then I made the mistake of turning around.

The chair had moved. It was no longer at the fourth place. It was one seat closer to mine.

* * *

They came one at a time, all through the falling dark, and each one knocked.

Great-aunt Sylvie at the front door, who had died before I was born, so that the face I knew only from a sepia photograph now breathed fog onto the storm glass and asked in a voice like dry leaves, "Astrid, chère, do you know my name?" And I did know it—Sylvie Oyelaran, born 1889, kept the bees, buried two husbands—and I opened my mouth to say it, because what harm could there be in a name, in remembering, and then I caught myself, because the ledger had taught me that in this family nothing was given freely and nothing was asked without a price. "You know I do," I said instead, which was true and also not an answer, and her shadow on the frosted glass stood there a moment longer, head tilted, and then withdrew.

Cousin Danny at the kitchen door, seventeen forever, drowned in the bayou in 1974, asking if I remembered the summer he taught me to skip stones. I did remember. I remembered it with my whole chest, and I said, "I remember, Danny," before I could stop myself, and the moment the words left me the kitchen lights flickered—every bulb dimming and surging like a held breath—and the chair moved again, another place closer, its legs making no sound on the floor at all, which was somehow more obscene than any sound would have been.

I sat down across from it and wrote in my notebook, because documentation was the only prayer I had left: *They want to be remembered. Saying yes moves the chair. Saying nothing moves the chair. Refusing outright—unknown. They are polite. That is the worst part. They are so polite.*

Because they were. That was the weapon in it, I understood by then. Not menace—*manners*. The thing at the well had been invited once, sixty years ago, by people too hungry and too desperate to know what they were opening the door to, and it had learned its manners from us. It had learned that this family said yes to family. It had learned that we fed people who knocked. It had learned that the cruelest thing you can do to people like us is to stand at our threshold looking like everyone we've ever buried and *wait*, because the waiting does the work, because love is a lever long enough to move anyone.

Grandma's sister Ada had walked into that well in August of 1961, and whatever had taken her had spent sixty years learning to knock.

* * *

At some point I lit every lamp in the kitchen and the hallway, all of them, a fortress of yellow light, and I called Tomas. He answered on the first ring, and I heard the hardware store behind him, the beep of a scanner, Dale's voice mid-sentence about composite shingles, and I almost wept at the sound of the ordinary world.

"Tomas. Come home. Come home now, and don't—"

"Astrid." His voice dropped, went careful. "What happened?"

"The chair is at the table. It's at the table, and it's moving, and they're—" I stopped. How did you say it? *Our dead relatives are queueing at the doors like it's Thanksgiving?* "They're knocking, Tomas. One at a time. They want me to say they're remembered."

Silence on the line. Then, very quietly: "And what happens when you do?"

"The chair gets closer to mine."

"And what happens when it *reaches* your chair, Astrid?"

I looked at it. Six feet away now. Close enough that I could see the crack in the back slat, close enough that I could see—this I had not seen before, and it raised the hair on my arms—that someone had carved a small, crude letter *A* into the wood, low on the leg, at the height of a child's hand. Ada. Ada had carved her initial into this chair when she was small, in a kitchen sixty years gone, and the chair had kept it the way the house kept everything, the way the family kept everything, buried and preserved and waiting.

"I don't know," I said. "But I don't think I want to be sitting in it when it finds out."

"Lock the doors. Don't answer anyone. I'm twenty minutes out."

He hung up, and I did lock the doors, all four of them, and then I stood in the middle of the kitchen with my back to the stove like a woman guarding a hearth in a story older than stories, and I waited for the next knock.

It came at the window over the sink, and this time I knew the voice before I turned around, because I had spent my whole life listening for it in the voices of other women, in my own voice on bad days, in the counting of a dying man at the hospice—*ninety, eighty-nine, eighty-eight*—the numbers falling like water.

"Open the door, Astrid."

Grandma.

She stood in the yard in her blue housecoat, the one with the faded poppies, the one she'd worn every morning of my childhood, and the dusk did not touch her. She looked the way she had in the hospital at the end, except the confusion was gone from her face, and what was in its place was worse than confusion. It was apology. It was a woman who had done a terrible arithmetic long ago and had come back to watch the sum come due.

"Mémé," I said, and my voice broke on it. "Mémé, what did you do?"

"Open the door and I'll tell you everything."

"No." My hand found the counter and gripped it. "You'll tell me through the glass."

Something moved behind her eyes—not anger. Fear. That was the detail I would carry out of this house on my back if I carried nothing else: my grandmother, dead and standing in the dark, was *afraid*, and her fear was not of me.

"Astrid, listen to me," she said, low and fast, glancing once over her shoulder at the well, at the dark circle of it. "Whatever sits down in that chair tonight—and something *will* sit down in it—you do not tell it it's remembered. You do not tell any of them. Do you hear me? A name given is a seat given. That's how it works. That's how it's *always* worked—"

"Then tell me what it is. Tell me what you paid."

Her face did something I had never seen it do. It crumpled. Sixty years of the family's founding silence, the silence I had grown up inside like a wall inside a wall, and it came apart in one second on the far side of a pane of glass.

"I was hungry," she said. "We were all so hungry, and Ada—Ada was the hungriest, and she loved us more than she loved herself, and that was the whole of it, Astrid, that was the entire sin. We let her. We *let* her, and then we never said her name again, because—" She stopped. Her head turned toward the well again, sharply, like a deer catching a scent, and when she looked back at me her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "It's coming up the porch. I have to go. Baby, whatever happens, whatever sits down—*the truth is the only thing it can't digest*. Do you understand me? It's been fed on silence for sixty years, and silence is the only—"

The porch boards creaked.

Grandma was gone. Not walked away—*gone*, between one blink and the next, the yard holding nothing but the dark and the well and the first fat drops of nothing, of no rain, of the dry air lying about itself.

And the chair was beside me.

I had not seen it move. I had been looking directly at the window, at her, and the chair had crossed the last six feet of the kitchen without a sound, without a flicker, and now it stood at my shoulder, close enough to touch, close enough that I could smell it—old pine, and beneath the pine something mineral and cold, the smell of the inside of the well, of stone that had never fully dried.

I backed away until the stove stopped me. The chair did not follow. It simply stood there, at my place at the table, angled toward my own empty chair like a guest waiting to be introduced.

Headlights swept the front window. Tomas's truck, turning into the drive, and I have never in my life been so grateful to see a vehicle. I was moving toward the front door when the last knock of the evening came—not at a door, not at a window, but on the *table itself*, a single polite rap of knuckle on wood, from the chair.

From the empty chair.

And then, from somewhere beneath the floor, beneath the foundation, beneath the dry and capped and stone-sealed well, in a voice like water finding a crack in a wall, patient and unhurried and terribly, terribly familiar—because it was grandma's voice, because it had always been grandma's voice, because it had been counting down through the walls of the dying for as long as I'd been keeping my notebook:

"Seven," it said.

The headlights swung across the yard. Tomas's boots hit the gravel.

"Six."
