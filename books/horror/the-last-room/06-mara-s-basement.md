## 6. Mara's Basement

*Ivo*

Mara Voss answered the door in scrubs the color of old dishwater, a lanyard still around her neck like she'd forgotten to take it off three shifts ago. She looked at me the way people look at a stray that's followed them home—annoyed, but already calculating whether there's food in the house.

"You're the one who's been calling," she said.

"I've called twice."

"Twice is 'been calling.' I work nights. Everything past two calls is an ambush." She stepped back from the doorway without inviting me in, which I took as either hospitality or surrender. "You have ten minutes. Then I sleep until my body files a formal complaint."

Her house sat at the end of Corben Street, the last one before the road gave up and became gravel and then became nothing. It was the kind of house that had been added onto so many times it no longer had a shape, just opinions—a porch here, a sunroom there, a chimney rising out of what appeared to be a closet. The foundation stones were older than everything above them, black with moisture, and as I stepped inside I felt it: that faint sub-audible hum I'd spent three years telling myself was water pressure.

Under her floorboards. Under her kitchen. Under her whole life.

She caught me looking down. Of course she did.

"They're loud in March," she said, conversationally, like we were discussing weather. "And around the equinoxes. And when it rains for more than four days." She hung the lanyard on a hook by the door. "I stopped keeping track of when they *aren't* loud. It was a shorter list."

My throat did something complicated. "You hear them too."

"Hear them? Honey, I grew up with them." She walked into the kitchen and put a kettle on, which struck me as the most aggressively normal thing anyone in Hollowmere had done in my presence, possibly ever. "My grandmother used to say the house was digesting. Sit down. You look like you haven't eaten since the last time you were underground, and don't ask how I know you go underground. This town has one grocery store and a gossip economy."

I sat. The chair hummed, very faintly, through its legs. Or I imagined it did. Three years of rationalizing had made me fluent in both languages—the sound and its denial—and lately the denial had started sounding thin, like a coat worn past its season.

"Mara," I said. "Why did you answer the phone?"

She didn't turn around. The kettle ticked as it heated. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed registers—nurse-flat, careful, the voice you use on families.

"Because you said the word 'founders' and then you said the name 'Blackwood,' and my grandmother told me, when I was nine years old and crying about the noises, that if anyone ever came asking about the founders, I was to listen to them." She turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "She also said I should probably charge them for tea. So. Tea's a dollar."

* * *

*Mara*

The Blackwood kid—I'd decided within thirty seconds that "they" was going to take practice and I was going to do it anyway because my grandmother raised me better than to be lazy about people—sat at my kitchen table holding a mug of chamomile like it might be wired to explode, and talked about tunnels.

Not the way the men at the hardware store talk about tunnels, all bravado and flashlight brands. Ivo Blackwood talked about them the way I talk about my dying patients. With inventory. With respect. With the particular exhaustion of someone who has been keeping records nobody asked for because somebody has to.

"—and the east branch dead-ends at dressed stone that isn't on any survey after 1891, and the acoustic profile of the central shaft changes every spring, and I know how that sounds—"

"It sounds like my childhood," I said.

They stopped. People always stop when you say things like that; they've rehearsed their strangeness so long they forget other people might have rehearsed theirs.

"I used to lie on my bedroom floor with a glass," I told them. "Ear pressed to it, glass pressed to the floorboard. My grandmother found me doing it once and didn't laugh. That's the part I remember most—not the laughing. She just knelt down and put her own ear to the boards and listened with me for a while. Then she said, 'It's not for us to answer yet.'"

"Yet," Ivo repeated.

"My grandmother was very big on *yet*. She was a strange woman. Everyone loved her and no one visited her twice." I got up, because if I kept sitting there I was going to say something true too fast, and truth needs a runway. "Come downstairs. There's something you should see, and I'd rather show you than explain it, because explaining it makes me sound like the kind of person who hears noises under her house."

"You are the kind of person who hears noises under her house."

"Yes, but I'm also a hospice nurse with a master's degree, so people have to be polite about it."

The basement stairs were steep and smelled of cold stone and lavender sachets, my grandmother's war against the damp, fought to a draw over forty years. I hit the light. One bulb, bare, swinging slightly on its cord though there was no draft down there. There never was a draft down there. I'd stopped noticing the swing years ago the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat, and watching Ivo notice it—watching their eyes track it with the grim satisfaction of a scientist confirming a hypothesis—made me feel suddenly, absurdly defensive of my basement.

"Don't judge the house," I said. "It's old. Old houses fidget."

"I'm not judging it." They stood at the bottom of the stairs, head tilted, listening. Their whole body had gone quiet in a way I recognized from my work—the stillness of someone giving a dying thing their full attention. "It's louder here. Under this room. Like—" They frowned, hunting for the word.

"Like breathing," I said. "But slower than breathing. Like something breathing *for* two."

They looked at me. In the bare bulb light their face was all angles and exhaustion, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because there are moments when two people realize they've been alone in the same room their entire lives, just at different addresses, and the realizing doesn't fit into sentences.

Then Ivo said, "Your grandmother left you something, didn't she. That's why you really let me in."

* * *

*Ivo*

The trunk was cedar-lined and smelled of camphor and time. Mara knelt beside it with the unhurried competence of someone who opened it often—maybe every year, maybe every equinox, maybe whenever the house got loud enough to make her lonely—and lifted out a journal bound in gray leather gone soft as cloth.

"She died when I was twenty-two. Left me the house, the debt, and this." Mara held it a moment before opening it, the way you hold a hand. "I read it cover to cover twice and then I put it away for six years, because reading it made the noises worse. Not louder. Worse. Like they knew I'd been reading about them."

"That's not possible," I said automatically. Three years of reflex.

"No," Mara agreed mildly. "Probably not." And the way she said it—granting me the lie like a nurse granting a patient their dignity—was somehow more devastating than arguing would have been.

She opened the journal on the workbench. The handwriting inside was small and upright, a schoolteacher's hand, and it began conventionally enough: recipes, rainfall, the price of feed. Then, dated October 1891, the entries changed.

*The wall is finished and the names are cut. E. says the words must be spoken by blood, all four lines together, or the ring is only a ring.*

*J. Blackwood has begun to doubt. He asks what we are keeping and I tell him it does not matter. A lock does not need to understand the thief.*

*The well is dry now. Dry is not empty. I have written what I could bear to write and burned what I could not. If you are reading this, child, the burning was the mercy.*

I read it three times. The bulb swung. Somewhere beneath our feet, patient as geology, the house breathed for two.

"The four lines," I said. My voice came out sanded down. "Voss. Okafor. Crane. Blackwood. Those are the names in the county archive—the 1891 survey that stops at a wall that isn't there anymore."

"And those are the four families that stayed in Hollowmere longest," Mara said. "Everyone else left or died out. Us four, we thinned but we didn't break." She turned a page, and I saw that the last third of the journal was different—denser, stranger, columns of words arranged like masonry, phrases that repeated with variations, a liturgy wearing the costume of notes. "This part isn't diary. This is rite. Or half of one. Pages are missing—torn out, carefully, at the spine. Someone wanted it incomplete."

"Your grandmother?"

"Or her mother. Or whoever decided that some doors should only open halfway." Mara closed the journal gently. "There's a rite in here, Ivo. I've read it a hundred times and I can't tell if it's meant to seal something or feed it. The words work for both, if you squint. Maybe that's the design. Maybe that's the trap."

The hum rose—or I thought it rose, or I wanted it to have risen, because wanting had become indistinguishable from hearing somewhere in the last three years and Wren's arrival had burned the difference off like fog in sun.

"Mara," I said. "Something spoke to me. In the tunnels. Not pipes. Not settling stone. Something that knows voices—wore my mother's voice like a coat. And there's a well down there that's supposed to be dry, and it's filling back up, and there's a carving of names on the ring and mine is being added to it letter by letter."

Most people would have laughed. Most people would have reached for the phone, for the polite fiction of concern. Mara Voss set her palm flat against the workbench, the way you check a patient's forehead, and said:

"How far along is your name?"

* * *

*Mara*

We stayed down there until the light through the window wells went amber and then gone. Ivo spread their tunnel maps across my workbench—hand-drawn, obsessively annotated, three years of a life folded into graph paper—and I brought up the journal and we laid the century between them like setting a fracture.

Here was where my grandmother wrote *the ring is only a ring*. Here was where Ivo's map showed a chamber their pen had circled twice and labeled, in small defeated letters, *shape wrong, measurements won't close*. Here was where the rite-words began, and here—God help us—was where the map showed the well.

Dry on paper. Humming under my floorboards.

"We need the others," Ivo said at some point, not looking up. "The twins, the Crane man. Four bloodlines. That's what the journal says and what the archive says and apparently what the architecture says."

"Ellery Crane is dying. I know because I'm his hospice nurse, which I probably should have mentioned an hour ago, and which means I can get you into that house tonight if you want."

Ivo looked up. For the first time since they'd arrived, they smiled—not much, just the corner of their mouth, the expression of someone discovering that the world contains one other person who speaks their language.

"You're the judge's nurse."

"I'm everyone's nurse. It's a small town and everyone in it is dying at various speeds." I closed the journal and held it against my chest. "Ivo. Why you? Out of all of us—why did it come to your porch?"

They were quiet a long time. The bulb swung. Below us, the slow double-breath rose and fell, rose and fell, and I realized I had stopped hearing it as noise sometime in the last hour and started hearing it as waiting.

"When I was seven," Ivo said finally, "I had an imaginary friend named Wren. And three weeks ago Wren was sitting on my porch, aged not a day, and it told me it had been waiting since I stopped believing." A breath. "So I don't know why me, Mara. But I know it wasn't random. Nothing about this town is random. It's just old, and patient, and it keeps books."

I thought of my grandmother kneeling on these same floorboards, ear to the wood, saying *it's not for us to answer yet.*

"Well," I said, and stood, and tucked the journal under my arm like a weapon or a lantern. "Then it's about time somebody answered back. Get your coat. Crane's window is evenings, and he's a mean old man who'll pretend he doesn't know what we're talking about right up until the moment he tells us everything."

Ivo gathered their maps with hands that weren't quite steady, and paused at the bottom of the basement stairs, looking back at the workbench, the trunk, the swinging bulb.

"Mara. Thank you. For—for answering the phone."

"Don't thank me yet." I killed the light behind us, and in the dark, for just a heartbeat, the breathing below seemed to quicken—as if it had heard us, as if it had plans of its own. "Thank me when we're still alive on Thursday."
