## 7. The Twins Refuse

The Okafor twins lived in a house that looked like it had been designed by someone who hated both houses and people: a flat-roofed box on Delmore Street with a gravel yard, a dead car under a tarp, and a porch light that buzzed like it was considering giving up. Two mailboxes. One said D. OKAFOR. The other said R. OKAFOR. Both had cobwebs in them, which told me either the twins didn't get mail or they'd stopped believing in it.

I stood on the sidewalk for a long time, holding my folder of photocopied archives like a summons.

Here's what nobody tells you about asking strangers to believe in an apocalyptic well beneath their hometown: you rehearse the speech in the car, and the speech is good, the speech is *great*, the speech has a logical arc and a moment of vulnerability placed exactly where a speechwriter would put it—and then the door opens and every word evaporates and you're left standing there like a census taker who's wandered into the wrong century.

I knocked anyway. Three years of documenting tunnels teaches you that knocking is just a sound you make until something answers.

Footsteps inside. A pause. Then the door opened maybe four inches, secured by a chain, and one brown eye regarded me through the gap with the enthusiasm of a customs officer.

"No."

"I haven't—"

"You're going to say Hollowmere. You're going to say tunnels. You're going to say some word like *ritual* or *seal* or *descendants*, and I am going to close this door, and we are both going to pretend this never happened." The eye blinked. "Save us the time."

"Dee?" I guessed.

The eye narrowed. "Rafe."

"Rafe," I said, "I know about the dreams."

The chain did not come off. But it also did not rattle, which I chose to interpret as interest. Behind the door, somewhere deeper in the house, another set of footsteps approached, unhurried, and a voice—lower, calmer, the voice of someone who had already decided how the next five minutes would go—said, "Who is it?"

"Nobody," said Rafe through the gap. "A historian."

"I'm not a historian," I said. "I'm Ivo Blackwood."

And here is the thing I have learned about names in Hollowmere: they are less like labels and more like keys left in locks by careless ancestors. The silence behind the door changed texture. It went from *annoyance* to *attention*, and attention, in my experience, is always the first symptom of trouble.

The chain slid off. The door opened all the way, and Dee Okafor stood in front of me in a flannel shirt and socks, arms crossed, looking at me the way you look at weather you've been warned about.

"Blackwood," she said. "Like the surveyor."

"Great-great-grandfather, give or take."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she stepped aside—not an invitation so much as a concession—and said, "You've got ten minutes, and Rafe doesn't want to hear any of it."

* * *

Inside, the house was aggressively normal. Couch with a throw blanket. A wall of framed photos of two identical children aging in parallel, like a time-lapse study conducted by someone with excellent intentions. A kitchen you could smell coffee in from the living room. On the fridge, a magnet shaped like Ohio held up a dentist appointment card from eight months ago.

Rafe sat on the couch with his knees drawn up, a mug of untouched coffee in his hands, staring at a point on the carpet roughly six inches in front of his feet. He was thinner than his sister. He had the look of a man who had recently become reacquainted with the concept of sleep and found it hostile.

"Ten minutes," Dee repeated, leaning against the kitchen doorway.

I laid out the folder on the coffee table. The 1891 survey. The founders' names. The page where the county engineer's neat hand simply stopped, mid-sentence, at the edge of a wall that no longer existed. I kept my voice level. I did not mention Wren. There are things you can say to strangers and things you cannot, and "my imaginary friend came back and told me the town is built on a plugged drain to somewhere worse" falls firmly in the second category, possibly permanently.

"The founders of Hollowmere built the tunnels as containment," I said. "Not drainage. Not mining. Containment. Four families. Voss, Okafor, Crane, Blackwood. The ritual needs descendants—all four lines—to hold. And it's failing. The well is refilling."

Silence. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere outside, a dog expressed opinions.

"That's a story," Dee said finally. Not cruelly. Carefully, the way you handle something that might be sharp.

"It's a map," I said. "It's a survey. It's a hundred and thirty years of records that all stop at the same place. I'm not asking you to believe in ghosts. I'm asking you to believe in paperwork."

That got almost a smile out of her. Almost. It died quickly, though, when Rafe spoke without lifting his eyes from the carpet.

"How deep does the water go?"

Dee went very still.

"What?" I said.

"The water." His voice was flat, recitative, like he was reading from notes he hated. "In your maps. How deep does the flooding go? In the lower sections. Past the third junction."

I hadn't shown him the maps. They were still in the folder, closed. My skin performed a small, cold migration.

"There isn't flooding yet," I said slowly. "There will be. When the well refills, the lower tunnels go first. Why would you ask me that?"

Rafe's jaw worked. He looked at his sister, and whatever passed between them was old and load-bearing, and I watched Dee make a decision with her whole body—a decision that looked, from the outside, exactly like a shrug.

"He's been dreaming," she said. "For about six weeks now. Water. Tunnels. A—" She stopped herself. Recalibrated. "He doesn't sleep much anymore."

"Dreams aren't evidence," Rafe said, to the carpet.

"No," I agreed. "But shared architecture is. Tell me about the third junction."

"There is no third junction," Dee said sharply. "There's nothing. He dreams about water in a hole. People dream about water. It's a very common anxiety, actually, there are studies—"

"Dee." Rafe's voice cracked like dry wood. "Show her the drawing."

"There is no drawing."

"*Dee.*"

And then we all sat in the buzzing quiet while Dee Okafor stood in her own kitchen doorway and visibly lost an argument she'd apparently been having with herself for weeks. Loyalty, I thought, watching her face. That's the thing nobody warns you about loyalty. It doesn't break cleanly. It bends, and bending hurts more than breaking, because you can feel it happening.

She left the room. Came back with a spiral notebook, school-supply aisle cheap, its cover soft from handling. She put it on the coffee table next to my folder and did not open it. Rafe reached over and opened it himself, and turned pages until he found what he was looking for, and turned the notebook around to face me.

Pencil. Smudged. Drawn from memory, obviously, but drawn *precisely*—the obsessive precision of someone who has seen a thing so many times it has worn a groove in them. A tunnel cross-section. A junction where three passages met. And beyond it, rendered in careful hatching, water. Rising water, marked with dates in the margin. Six weeks of dates. The waterline climbing, day by day, in a place Rafe Okafor had never been.

Because I had never published my maps. Nobody had. They lived in a binder in my apartment and in my head, and the third junction—the one I'd named myself, arbitrarily, because it was the third junction I'd catalogued—was in none of them.

"This is my map," I said. My voice came out strange. "This is the third junction. I named it that. There's no record. There's no way—"

"I know," Rafe said. He finally looked up at me, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who has stopped expecting anyone to fix anything. "I know there's no way. Do you think I don't know there's no way?"

"Rafe." Dee's voice had gone thin. "Don't."

"We moved away when we were nine," he said, ignoring her, talking faster now, a dam developing hairline fractures. "We never came back except for funerals. I don't dream about Hollowmere. I dreamed about *this*. Before you ever knocked on that door. Every night. The water goes down past the junction and there's a room and there's a well and there's something in the well that knows my name, and last Tuesday—" He stopped. Swallowed. Last Tuesday, apparently, required gathering himself. "Last Tuesday it used Mom's voice. And it asked me why I was fighting it, when it only wanted to show me the rest of the map."

The refrigerator hummed. The dog outside had found religion or lost it; hard to tell.

"You're fighting it," I said quietly. "That matters. Whatever else is true—you're still fighting it. That's why you haven't slept. That's why you're sitting here instead of walking to the tunnels yourself."

Something flickered across Rafe's face that I recognized, horribly, because I'd seen it in my own bathroom mirror for three years: the expression of a person who has been explaining away sounds and is running out of explanations. Denial is exhausting work. It's a second job you never applied for.

Dee took the notebook back. Closed it. Held it against her chest like a shield, or a wound dressing.

"Get out," she said.

"Dee—"

"Not you." She wasn't looking at me. She was looking at her brother, and her voice broke in the middle of the sentence and she let it. "Both of you. Both of you sit there talking about wells and voices like it's *weather*, like it's something we can just—know about. If it's real, if any of this is real, then Mom's stories were real, and Grandma's rules were real, and everything we laughed about when we left this town—" Her breath hitched. "I need everyone to get out of my house so I can fall apart in private. That's not a metaphor. Out."

* * *

I stood on the porch with my folder, listening to the door shut behind me with the finality of a vault. Not slammed. Worse than slammed. Closed *deliberately*, the way you close a door on a room you've decided not to think about.

The porch light buzzed. The dead car sat under its tarp. Somewhere in the house, muffled, I heard Dee's voice rise and crack, and Rafe's answer too low to parse, and I stood there feeling like a man who'd kicked over a rock and found not ugliness underneath but something alive and suffering, which is a different moral problem entirely.

I was halfway down the walk when the front door opened again.

Just Dee. Eyes red-rimmed, jaw set, the notebook nowhere in sight. She came down the steps fast, like she was afraid of being talked out of it, and pressed something into my hand—small, cold, heavier than its size suggested.

A key. Brass, old, blackened in the grooves. Stamped on the bow was a sigil I knew intimately: the same interlocking mark carved above the tunnel entrance, the same mark on the founders' chamber lintel in Mara's grandmother's journal sketches. The Okafor line's contribution to the lock, or the lock's claim on the Okafors—I couldn't tell which, and suspected the distinction didn't exist.

"My grandmother's," Dee said. "She kept it in a tin with her sewing stuff. Told us it was to 'the old cellar.' We thought she meant the house in Hollowmere." Her laugh came out wrong. "We sold that house when she died. This didn't open anything in it. We tried. Rafe tried every lock in the place when he was fourteen, because Rafe tries every lock in every place."

"Dee, why—"

"Because my brother is dreaming your map," she said, "and because you're the first person who walked up to that door with answers instead of questions, and because—" She stopped. Looked back at her own front door, at the shape of her twin behind the frosted glass, motionless. "Because I don't know who I'm supposed to be loyal to anymore. Him or the truth. And I guess I'm finding out that picking one feels like betraying the other either way."

She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angry at them.

"If this is real—if the water in his head is real—then it's rising in him too, isn't it. Whatever's down there, it's not waiting for the well. It's already got a beachhead." Her voice dropped. "So find out what this opens. Find out fast. Because Rafe is losing, and I can't fight something I can't see, and if you come back here with a way to make it stop—" She jabbed a finger at me, and her hand was shaking, and she didn't try to hide it. "Then you knock on this door again. And this time I'll open it before you finish knocking."

She went back inside. The door closed. Gently, this time, which somehow felt louder.

I stood in the gravel yard turning the key over in my fingers. It was warm from her hand. The sigil caught the porch light and threw it back dull and yellow, like something preserved.

Three bloodlines accounted for, then: mine by birth, Voss by journal, Crane by confession waiting in a dying man's throat. And now brass in my palm, stamped with the fourth name, handed to me through a crack in a divided heart by a woman who had chosen, for tonight at least, to bet on the truth over her brother's peace.

Behind the frosted glass, the shape of Rafe still hadn't moved.

And as I walked back to my car, the key heavy in my pocket, I could have sworn—though I would not write this in the field notes, not yet, not until I understood it—that from somewhere beneath Delmore Street, faint and patient and unmistakably pleased, came the sound of water finding a new channel.
