## 21. The New Sound

The first thing I noticed about Hollowmere after the water settled was the quiet. Not silence—Hollowmere has never managed true silence, not in my lifetime—but the kind of quiet that comes after a long argument ends. The house still creaked at night. The pipes still knocked their little Morse code of air and pressure. But underneath all that, where there had been something else for as long as I could remember—a listening, a leaning-in, a pressure against the ear like a hand cupped to catch what you said about it—there was nothing.

Nothing is a strange thing to grieve.

I want to be honest here, because Ivo taught me that much if nothing else: for about four days after we came up out of the tunnels, soaked and half-deaf and missing one person who had become more family to me than anyone sharing my blood, I missed it. The hum. The attention. I'd lived my whole life under it the way you live under weather, and when it lifted I felt exposed, like a snail with its shell cracked off. I lay in bed in my grandmother's house—the house I'd inherited along with her journal and her secrets—and I listened to ordinary nothing and felt like a word that had lost its meaning.

Then Crane died, peaceful as Sunday, and Rafe slept through a night without screaming, and Dee laughed at something I said—an actual laugh, startled out of her like a bird—and I decided that exposure was survivable. That maybe it was even the point.

Ivo would have made some joke about it. "Congratulations on your promotion from haunted to merely sad," they'd have said, and then immediately ruined the joke by meaning something tender underneath it. That was Ivo's whole method: bury the sincerity so deep under irony you needed a map to find it. They left me maps. It's how I found everything.

* * *

Weeks passed. October came down over Hollowmere like a lid, the way it always does, the maples going the color of old bruises. The town did its best impression of normalcy. The diner reopened on reduced hours because Doreen's hip couldn't take full ones. The county finally sent someone out to look at the sinkhole on Mill Street, and the county someone stood at the edge of it for eleven minutes, took three photographs, and drove away, which is the most honest work any government employee has ever done.

The tunnels were sealed. Officially, structurally, permanently—concrete poured down the access shafts by a contractor from two towns over who asked no questions because we paid him not to, in cash, out of an envelope Dee handled with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had spent years managing other people's uncomfortable money. Rafe watched the pour with his arms crossed and his jaw set, and when the last truck rumbled away he said, "Good," in a voice like a door closing, and none of us corrected him. Some mercies are better left unexamined.

I went back to work. Hospice doesn't pause for apocalypse; death keeps office hours even when the dead keep other ones. My patients that month were Mrs. Aldous, who was ninety-four and mostly interested in whether the Cubs had won, and a man named Peter Voss—no relation, though he liked to claim one whenever the morphine loosened him up—who told me every visit that he'd once seen a ghost in the Kettle Creek churchyard and every visit I told him that sounded about right for Kettle Creek.

It was good work. It was the work I'd always said it was, before I learned otherwise: sitting with people while they leave, holding the door open, not flinching. I had flinched plenty in the tunnels. I figured I'd earned the right to stop.

And Hollowmere felt lighter. That's the only word for it. Lighter, like a room after a heavy piece of furniture has been carried out—you notice the space more than the absence. People walked faster. Strangers made eye contact. The pastor's sermons got shorter, which everyone agreed was a miracle in itself.

Dee called every Sunday. Rafe had moved into her place in Columbus—"temporarily," he said, in month five of temporarily—and she'd report on his sleep, his appetite, his slow reassembly. "He dreamed about the lake last night," she told me once. "Just the lake. Just a lake." She said *just* like it was a sacrament.

"He'll keep getting better," I said.

"I know." A pause. "Do you? Actually know?"

"No," I admitted. "But I believe it, and I've decided belief counts."

She laughed again, that startled-bird laugh. "Mara Voss, hospice nurse, theologian."

"Somebody has to be. Everyone qualified is dead or fictional."

We hung up and I sat in my grandmother's kitchen with tea going cold in front of me, and I thought about how strange it was that this—phone calls, cold tea, jokes across the distance—was what we'd built out of all that horror. Found family, Ivo had called it once, back when saying it out loud was still dangerous, back when naming things felt like giving them power. I'd rolled my eyes. Now I kept the phrase the way you keep a pressed flower, flat and fragile and proof that something had bloomed.

* * *

The sound started on a Tuesday.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only inheritance I trust anymore. Tuesday, the second week of November. I remember because I'd just come home from Mrs. Aldous's—she'd had a bad night, confused, calling for a sister who died in 1961—and I was standing in my kitchen in my coat, keys still in hand, when I heard it.

Under the floorboards. Under the floorboards and under the crawlspace and under the foundation stone my grandmother's father laid in 1912. A sound like—

Like tapping. No. Like knocking. No, neither. Those words are too friendly, too human. It was more like the memory of knocking, the way a bell keeps ringing after it's been struck, except nothing had struck anything. It was faint. It was rhythmic. And it was patient—that's the part I can't explain properly, the part that made me stand in my kitchen with my keys in my fist for ten full minutes. Sounds don't have intentions. This one did. It wasn't asking to be let in. It wasn't threatening. It was simply announcing, at long intervals, that it was there, and that it could wait, and that waiting cost it nothing.

I knew what it wasn't. It wasn't pipes—I'd spent thirty years learning my house's plumbing vocabulary, and this wasn't in it. It wasn't settling stone; I'd spent three months learning the tunnels' vocabulary too, in the worst possible graduate seminar ever convened. And it wasn't the thing from the well, because the thing from the well was behind forty yards of concrete and a seal renewed in blood and willingness, and because when it spoke, your mother's voice came out of it, and this sound had no voice at all.

It was smaller than that. Quieter. Almost polite.

I put my keys down very carefully, the way you do when a large dog is watching you, and I made dinner, and I ate it, and the sound tapped its patient intervals beneath my feet the whole time, and I did not go down to the crawlspace.

I want credit for that. Nobody's giving it out, but I want it noted somewhere.

* * *

Here's what I did instead, over the following week, while the sound kept its slow time below: I read Ivo's maps.

They're in a box under my bed now, the good ones, the final surveys annotated in Ivo's small slanted handwriting—elevations, flow rates, structural notes, and, in the margins, the running commentary that was pure Ivo: *"acoustics here suggest hollow but probe says solid — note for future: trust the probe, distrust the hollow"* and *"found a boot. 1920s? left it. some things deserve their rest"* and, near the chamber, in handwriting that had begun to shake, *"it knows our names. it has always known our names."*

That last annotation stops mid-sentence. Everything of Ivo's stops mid-sentence now. I keep the maps anyway. Grief is just love with nowhere to go, and the maps gave mine somewhere to sit.

I spread them across the kitchen table on Thursday night and checked, with a ruler and a pencil and the grim thoroughness of a woman avoiding her own basement, whether the sound under my floorboards matched anything Ivo had charted. There was a spur tunnel—one of the dozens Ivo never fully explored, marked with a dashed line and a question mark—running roughly northeast from the main gallery. Northeast. Toward my street. Toward, possibly, under, my grandmother's house, whose foundation stone predated the town's official records by nine years, because my grandmother's father had been one of the people who didn't bother with official records.

The dashed line stopped about sixty feet short of my property line. Question mark. Ivo's pencil hovering at the edge of the known world.

I sat there a long time with my finger on the question mark, and beneath my feet, faint and patient, the sound kept its rhythm, and I understood—with the clarity that only comes at midnight, at a kitchen table, alone with the dead's handwriting—that I could find out. That was the terrible gift of everything we'd been through: I now knew how to find out. I had a key with a founder's sigil. I had a journal with a partial rite. I had a phone number in Columbus belonging to a woman who would come back without asking why. I had skills, maps, a flashlight with fresh batteries, and a town so relieved to be unhaunted that nobody would stop me.

Sixty feet of unknown between Ivo's last pencil mark and whatever was politely knocking under my kitchen.

I folded the maps. I put them back in the box. I went to bed.

* * *

You should understand what choosing not to investigate costs a person like me. I'm a nurse. Curiosity isn't my flaw, it's my profession—we're trained to assess, to check, to lift the bandage and look. Every instinct I have says *know*. Know the pressure, know the dosage, know the prognosis. Knowledge is how I've kept people alive and how I've kept myself sane through thirty years of other people's endings.

And Ivo—God, Ivo. Ivo spent three years rationalizing sounds in the dark because not-knowing was unbearable, and then spent the rest of their life learning that knowing was worse, and then paid for the knowing with everything. If I went down there, if I followed the dashed line past the question mark, I wouldn't be honoring their curiosity. I'd be undoing their sacrifice. Ivo bought this town's ignorance with their life. The least I can do is live in it.

Besides—and this is the part I've only admitted to myself, here, in the privacy of my own head, where honesty goes to avoid witnesses—it wasn't malevolent. I keep coming back to that. I know what malevolent sounds like. I've heard it wear my grandmother's voice and offer me things in exchange for names. This was nothing like that. This was... small. Faint. Patient the way a cat is patient at a closed door—not demanding, not desperate, just present, just certain that doors open eventually.

Almost, if I'm being honest—and apparently I'm being honest tonight, so let's finish the job—almost gentle.

Wren had been gentle too. Wren had sat on a porch for twenty years, aged not a day, waiting for a person who'd stopped believing, and had never once forced the issue. Keepers send fragments out when they can. Ivo had told me that, or Wren had, or I'd understood it in the chamber with water rising and no time to ask follow-up questions. Fragments. Pieces of the keeper, sent outward, to watch, to wait, to knock softly on the world from underneath.

Ivo was in the well. Ivo was the well's keeper, willing and living, sealed in stone and name and choice.

And something under my floorboards was knocking.

I did the math on that exactly once, standing in the shower with the water too hot, and then I put the math away in the same drawer where I keep my grandmother's journal and the envelope of cash and every other thing I've decided to carry without opening. Because here's the thing about the drawer: it holds. Here's the thing about doors: they stay shut if you choose it. Ivo chose the well. I can choose the floorboard. We each get one door, maybe, in a life, and I'd already gone through mine.

Some nights, though. Some nights I lie awake and listen to it—fainter now, or maybe I'm just learning to hear around it—and I talk to it. Quietly. Into my pillow, like a teenager, like a fool.

"I'm not opening it," I tell it. "Whatever you are. Whoever you are. I'm not opening it, and I'm not coming down, and I'm not finishing anybody's sentences."

It taps its patient rhythm, and it doesn't argue, and somehow that's the worst part, or the best part. I haven't decided.

* * *

December came early that year. First snow on the fourth, wet and heavy, bending the pines along the creek. Dee sent a photo of Rafe shoveling her sidewalk, actually shoveling, upright and scowling at the sky like a man with a future he resented slightly less each day. I taped it inside the cupboard door where I keep things I need to see at low moments, next to a napkin Ivo had doodled a map of the diner on, with a comment about the coffee being structurally unsound.

Mrs. Aldous died on the ninth, mid-morning, holding my hand and complaining about the Cubs, which is the best exit I've witnessed in fifteen years of witnessing exits. I sat with her afterward for the required interval, doing the paperwork of departure, and when I finally stepped outside the light was doing that thing winter light does in Hollowmere—coming down clean and hard and silver, rinsing the streets, making even the shuttered buildings look less abandoned than merely resting.

The town felt lighter. I noticed it again, walking to my car. Lighter. Like something had been carried out.

Under my boots, beneath the frozen ground, beneath the frost-heaved pavement of Birch Street, faint as a pulse taken through a coat sleeve, something tapped. Twice. A pause. Twice.

Not malevolent. Patient. Willing to wait as long as waiting takes.

I got in my car. I turned the heater on. And I did not look back at my house, where the sound would be waiting for me under the floorboards when I got home, keeping its soft time, keeping its counsel, keeping—though I chose not to confirm it, and will never confirm it, and will carry that unconfirmed thing to my own mid-morning ending someday—keeping watch.

Because here is what I know, and what I've decided is enough: doors stay closed by choice, and choices are the only inheritance worth leaving. Ivo left me theirs. I intend to leave mine intact, unopened, and annotated in the margins with a single line, in my own handwriting, for whoever finds the maps after me:

*Some knocks are answers. You don't have to open the door to be kind to them.*

I wrote it that night, at the kitchen table, while the sound kept time beneath my feet like a heart learning a new body. Then I turned off the light, and went to bed, and slept—really slept—for the first time since the water settled.

In the morning there was snow on the porch step, undisturbed, unmarked, and the sound under the floorboards had changed its rhythm.

Three taps now. A pause. Three.

Like someone practicing a greeting.
