## 16. The Second Behind the Wall

The lag had been growing all week, and by Thursday night it was long enough to hold a person.

Sebastian discovered this the way she discovered most things at Hollowmere: by accident, in the middle of a ritual she'd performed so many times it had worn a groove into her. She stood at the bathroom mirror at 11:40 p.m., toothbrush in hand, watching her reflection brush its teeth one second behind her — no, longer now, two seconds, two and a half — and she thought, not for the first time, *I should be used to this by now.*

She was not used to it. You did not get used to your own face arriving late.

But tonight something was different. Tonight, when she set the toothbrush down and lifted her hand to touch the glass, her reflection did not follow. It kept brushing. And in the gap between Sebastian's raised hand and the mirror's delayed answer, there was a seam.

It's hard to describe a seam in glass. It looked like the moment a film projector changes reels — that flicker of blankness nobody is supposed to notice, the frame where nothing is projected because the reel is turning over. Except this flicker didn't pass. It stayed. A vertical line of gray, faint as breath on cold glass, running from the top of the mirror to the bottom, right through the center of her delayed reflection's forehead.

Her hand was already moving toward it before her mind finished deciding. That was the thing about Hollowmere — it made curiosity feel less like a choice and more like gravity. Her mother would have said: *write it down first.* Sebastian's ledger sat on the sink's edge, open, pen clipped to the cover. She wrote, in her small careful hand:

*11:42 p.m. Seam visible during lag. Appears only in the gap between my movement and the reflection's. Testing contact.*

Then she touched it.

The glass did not feel like glass. It felt like the surface of very still water that has been holding still for a very long time — cool, taut, patient. Her fingertip sank through without resistance, without splash, and the seam widened around her wrist like an eye opening, and the bathroom fell away behind her the way a dream falls away when you finally notice you're dreaming.

* * *

She stepped out of the mirror into a corridor.

Not her corridor. Not any corridor that belonged to the waking school. This one stretched in both directions farther than Hollowmere had walls to contain, and every surface of it was wrong in a way her eyes kept trying to correct and failing. The wainscoting was oak, but the grain of the wood moved, slowly, like the rings of a tree deciding where to grow next. The sconces along the wall held flames that did not flicker — each flame was a perfect frozen teardrop of light, and when Sebastian looked closely she saw that inside each flame was a tiny image: a classroom, a desk, a child's raised hand. The floor beneath her feet was polished stone, and in its black shine she could see reflections of things that were not above her — a ceiling of open sky, stars arranged in the pattern of a school crest.

And the air. God, the air. It tasted like the last day of summer vacation, like pencil shavings and cut grass and the specific sweetness of a September that hasn't turned yet. It tasted like being eleven years old and believing the year ahead of you would be kind.

"Hello?" she said, and her voice did something impossible: it arrived before she finished saying it. The word *hello* echoed back to her from both ends of the corridor simultaneously, overlapping itself, as if sound here could not decide which direction time ran and had simply chosen all of them.

That was when she understood what this place was.

*The second.* The lag. All those weeks of mirrors showing her yesterday's gesture, yesterday's smile — she had assumed the delay was empty space, dead air between broadcast and reception. It wasn't. The second was a place. It had always been a place, and she was standing in it.

Time pooled here like water in a cellar after rain. She could see it — actually see it — gathering in the low places of the corridor, ankle-deep and silvered, moving with a slowness that made glaciers look hasty. Where it pooled thickest, the past lay on the surface like leaves: a chalk eraser felt clapping dust into sunlight; a bell ringing in 1974; a girl's laugh, preserved whole, rising off the surface and dissolving before it could finish.

Sebastian took out her ledger. Her hands were shaking, but her handwriting, trained by thirty years of grief-driven discipline, came out level.

*Entered the lag at 11:43 p.m. (local). Interior does not correspond to any physical space in the academy. Time present in liquid state. Light sources contain images. Sound precedes speech. I am writing this to prove to myself later that I was lucid.*

She looked up from the page, and that was when she saw them.

* * *

They were standing at the far end of the corridor, and they were children.

Eleven of them. They stood in a loose half-circle, facing her, wearing the Hollowmere uniform as it must have looked fifty years ago — charcoal blazers, maroon ties, the girls' skirts pressed sharp as folded letters. They were arranged with the careful asymmetry of a class photograph, and Sebastian understood with a lurch that they were arranged exactly as the razored-out photo in the 1974 yearbook would have arranged them, that this was the composition the scissors had destroyed, reassembled here in the one place the archive couldn't reach.

They were smiling.

Every one of them. Eleven smiles, wide and white and unwavering, held with the fixed endurance of children told to hold still for the camera. But it was the quality of the smiles that made Sebastian's stomach turn over — not the width of them, though the width was terrible, stretching nearly to the ears on the smallest boy — but their *duration*. No living smile lasts. A real smile trembles, tires, drifts, breaks and reforms. These smiles had been held for fifty years. They had gone past expression into architecture. They were load-bearing.

"Hello," Sebastian said again, and this time she let the word come out slow, the way she spoke to frightened animals, to grieving families at her mother's funeral, to anyone standing at the edge of something.

None of them answered. But the girl at the center — small, dark-haired, a ribbon in her hair that even now Sebastian recognized from the attendance slips, from marginalia, from the name she had written in her own ledger more times than any other name — the girl tilted her head, and her smile widened by a single millimeter, and somewhere in the walls of the corridor something exhaled.

Wren Halloway.

"You're Wren," Sebastian said. "Wren Halloway. You were in Room 12. Your teacher was Mr. Aldous Pryce. You liked — " she searched the notes in her head, the marginalia she'd spent weeks excavating — "you liked drawing horses. In the margin of your arithmetic book, someone wrote *Wren draws better than she divides.*"

The smile did not move. But Wren's eyes — her eyes were the only part of her face that was still doing anything human — her eyes went bright, and wet, and for one instant Sebastian saw behind the smile a girl drowning in her own face, hands pressed against the inside of the glass of her expression, mouthing something.

Mouthing: *don't look too long.*

Then the smile reasserted itself, smooth as water closing over a dropped stone, and Wren Halloway was just another smiling child in a frozen corridor, and Sebastian's heart was hammering so hard she could hear it arrive before it beat.

*Don't look too long.*

She wrote it down. Standing there, in the pooled silver time, with eleven smiling corpses-who-were-not-corpse watching her, she opened her ledger and wrote: *Wren tried to warn me. Don't look too long. Why? What happens if I do?*

She found out almost immediately, because she had already done it.

Looking away from Wren, she realized the corridor had changed. The far wall was nearer. Or she was nearer to it. Between one heartbeat and the next — except heartbeats didn't work properly here, she could feel hers stuttering like a record skipping — the distance between herself and the eleven had halved, and she had not walked. Something had subtracted the space while she wasn't watching, the way the school subtracted names from ledgers, the way it subtracted classes from memory. Here, attention was distance. Looking was traveling. And she had been looking too long.

The smallest boy, front row, gap-toothed, his smile straining at the corners of his mouth like a button about to pop off a coat — he was close enough now that she could see the fine detail of him. The cowlick at his crown. The scab on his knee, decades old and never healed, because healing required time and time here was pooled on the floor and none of it belonged to them. His blazer pocket held a folded paper airplane, yellowed, never thrown.

His name surfaced in her memory unbidden, from an attendance slip: *Tomas Ellery.*

"Hi, Tomas," she whispered.

His smile widened. All eleven smiles widened, in perfect unison, a wave of widening rolling down the half-circle like wind through wheat, and the walls of the corridor drank the motion in, and the flames in the sconces showed her new images: her own cottage, her own bed, her own bathroom mirror — seen from *behind*, from inside the glass, looking out at a woman who was not currently standing there. The corridor was learning her home. The corridor was learning where she lived.

*Violation.* The word arrived in her mind with total clarity, cold as well-water. This was what Mrs. Okonkwo's warnings had been circling all along, what the rules about gifts and goodbyes and smiling back had been built to prevent. This place didn't attack. It *incorporated*. It learned you the way ivy learns a wall, patiently, lovingly, cell by cell, until there was no telling which of you was holding which up. Every fact she gave these children — every name recalled, every kindness offered — was a thread pulled out of her own fabric and woven into theirs.

And the worst part — the part she would write down later and then sit staring at for an hour — the worst part was that it felt wonderful. Belonging poured off the eleven of them like heat off a stove. Fifty years of shared attention had made them a family of a density no living family achieves, and standing near them was like standing near a fire in winter, and some starving cellar-deep part of Sebastian — the part that had been alone since her mother died, the part that kept notebooks because paper was the only company that never left — that part leaned toward the warmth and wanted in.

She caught herself mid-lean. Physically caught herself, one hand slamming flat against the moving-grain wainscoting, stopping her own body's drift toward the half-circle like stopping a sleepwalker at a window.

"No," she said aloud. To herself. To the corridor. "No. I know what this is. This is the bait. This is how it gets teachers. It doesn't drag you — it *invites* you, and it makes the invitation feel like coming home."

The eleven smiled. Somewhere beneath the floor, beneath the pooled time, something vast shifted its weight, the way a sleeper turns over, and Sebastian felt the shift through the soles of her shoes and understood that the children were not the entity. The children were the *pantry*. The thing that lived here was older and lower and hungrier, and it was listening to her refusal with the patience of a creature that had eaten eleven generations of refusals.

* * *

She needed to leave. She knew that with the same animal certainty you know a staircase is dark. But leaving, she suspected, was not as simple as turning around — in a place where attention was distance, where sound preceded speech, where the exit might be wherever the corridor decided exits were — leaving required a trick.

She thought about the seam. She had entered through a gap — the flicker between her movement and her reflection's. The lag was a door, and doors swing both ways, but only during the lag. Which meant she needed to find the edge of this second. The place where it ended and the real world resumed.

She began walking — deliberately, counting her steps aloud, writing each number in the ledger as she spoke it, anchoring herself in sequence the way her mother had taught her to anchor herself in grief: *one thing, then the next thing, then the next.* At step forty, the sconce-flames stopped showing her cottage and started showing the bathroom mirror again, live, real-time: her own reflection standing in her own bathroom, toothbrush in hand, waiting. Waiting for her. The lag worked both directions. Out there, her body's double was holding her place.

At step sixty-three, she saw the edge.

It looked like the rim of a soap bubble viewed from inside — a curve of thinning light where the corridor's impossible geometry simply gave up, and beyond it she could see her bathroom tiles, warped slightly, like a painting of a bathroom rather than the bathroom itself. Between her and the edge, the pooled time lay deepest, waist-high silver, thick with sunken yesterdays.

She also saw, at the edge, standing in the shallows, Wren Halloway.

The other ten remained in their photograph formation at the corridor's end. Wren had left it. Wren was standing between Sebastian and the way out, smiling that fifty-year smile, and her eyes were bright and wet and screaming.

Sebastian stopped. Every survival instinct she owned told her to stop, and she stopped, and she raised her ledger like a shield, and she said, clearly, in the voice she used for reading aloud:

"Wren Halloway. Born March 3rd, 1961. Drew horses in the margins. Was very brave."

The smile cracked.

It was tiny — a tremor at the left corner, a fracture no wider than a hairline crack in teacup — but it was real, it was *hers*, and through the crack Sebastian saw the girl again, the drowning girl, and heard her voice arrive before she spoke it, thin and rusted from disuse:

*"You said my name right."*

"I write everything down," Sebastian said. "Names most of all. Nobody gets forgotten in my books. Not ever."

Something in the corridor recoiled. The pooled time rippled outward from Wren in a ring, and the sconce-flames guttered — actually guttered, the first imperfection the light had shown — and for a moment the whole second seemed to hold its breath around the small cracked smile of a dead girl who had just been remembered correctly.

Then the moment passed. The smiles reassembled. The corridor smoothed itself like a bed being made. But Wren stepped aside. She stepped aside from the edge, clearing the way out, and as Sebastian waded through the silver shallows toward the thinning light, Wren's voice followed her, arriving before it was spoken, so that Sebastian heard the words before Wren's lips moved, and the words were:

*"Come back. Please. But come back careful. He counts the visits."*

*He.*

Sebastian plunged through the seam.

* * *

She came out of the mirror the way a swimmer comes up from deep water — gasping, arms wide, knocking the toothbrush cup off the sink. The bathroom was her bathroom. The clock on the shelf read 11:44 p.m. Two minutes. She had been gone two minutes, or the second had spent two minutes of her, or both. Her reflection stood in the glass, on time, pale, matching her gasp for gasp, and for the first time since she'd arrived at Hollowmere the synchronization of her own face made her want to weep.

She sat down on the closed lid of the toilet and wrote until her hand cramped.

*The lag is a place. The second is inhabited. The eleven are there, intact, smiling, arranged as they were photographed. The smiles are maintained, not felt — I believe they are being maintained FOR something, or BY something. Attention = distance there. It learns you when you look at it. It feels like belonging. That is the trap. Wren can crack. Names help. She said "he counts the visits." There is a HE. There is someone keeping count.*

She stopped. Looked at the last line. Read it three times.

Somewhere below her cottage — below the foundations, below the frost line, down where the founders' ledger said the covenant fed — something that counted was adding one to a tally that had been running for a hundred years, and the newest mark on the tally was hers.

Sebastian capped her pen, held the ledger against her chest like a talisman, and did not sleep.

In the morning, she would go find Mrs. Okonkwo, and she would ask her, point-blank, what happens to the people who visit the second and come back — and whether anyone had ever come back twice.

Because she already knew she was going back. Wren had asked her to. And Sebastian Delacroix had never once, in her entire documented life, left a request like that unanswered.
