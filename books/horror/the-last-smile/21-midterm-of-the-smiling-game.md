## 21. Midterm of the Smiling Game

The idea came to her the way most dangerous ideas do: quietly, at three in the morning, while she was pretending to sleep.

Sebastian lay in bed with the lamp on and the mirror in the bathroom covered by a bath towel she'd pinned at all four corners. The lag had settled at seven seconds now — long enough that when she brushed her teeth, she could watch a stranger finish the motion after she'd stopped, a woman with her face who moved like film run through water. She had learned not to look. She had also learned that not looking was its own kind of surrender.

So instead she looked at the ledger.

It sat on the nightstand where it always did, the Hollowmere ledger, separate from her mother's notebooks, bound in green cloth she'd bought at a stationer's in town because Mrs. Okonkwo had told her: *never write in their paper, girl. Only yours.* The book was nearly half full now — attendance anomalies, marginalia transcriptions, the eleven names reconstructed from slips and erasures, Okonkwo's rules copied out in her careful hand:

*Never smile back.*
*Never accept a gift.*
*Never say goodbye.*

And beneath them, dated last Tuesday, in ink that had appeared without her writing it:

*We are tired of smiling.*

She had told no one. Not Voss, whose kindness was a hinge on which something terrible swung. Not Okonkwo, who had gone silent since the night Sebastian saved her, as if grief were a debt being repaid in installments of silence. The message had simply been there one morning, pressed into the page between two of her own entries, in handwriting that belonged to another century — looped, pencil-faint, the letters of a child taught penmanship by nuns or by fear.

*We are tired of smiling.*

It should have terrified her. It had, for a day and a half. But terror, Sebastian had discovered, was just information arriving too fast, and her whole life had been an education in slowing information down until it could be held. Her mother's voice, memory-soft: *If you can write it down, Basty, then it's real, and if it's real, then it can be known, and if it can be known, it isn't the dark anymore.*

They had written to her. Which meant they could be written to.

Which meant — and here was the dangerous part, the part that made her sit up in the cold room with her heart going like a trapped bird — which meant they could still be taught.

* * *

She spent Thursday preparing the way she'd once prepared for her first classroom observation, back when teaching was only teaching: coffee gone cold, notes cross-referenced, a lesson plan drafted three times. Except this lesson plan was written entirely in pencil, on loose pages she would burn afterward, because Okonkwo's lore had a corollary Sebastian had deduced herself: whatever was written in ink, the house could read. Pencil was a whisper. Ink was a bell.

The test, when she finished drafting it, fit on a single page.

Not arithmetic. Not history. She had thought about what a midterm should measure, and the answer had arrived with the terrible clarity of things that are obvious only after you've survived them. A midterm measured whether the students had absorbed the material. So the question was: what material?

She wrote eleven questions. One for each name.

1. What is your full name? Write it the way your mother said it.
2. What did you want to be, before?
3. Who taught you the smiling game? (You may describe them without drawing them.)
4. What is the last thing you remember being sad about?
5. If sadness were allowed, what would you cry about first?
6. Describe your favorite meal. Be specific. Hunger is a kind of honesty.
7. What season is it inside the second?
8. Do you know what happened to you? Answer yes or no, and then answer honestly.
9. What do you miss?
10. If you could stop smiling for one minute, what would your face do instead?
11. Wren Halloway: why did you write to me?

She stared at the eleventh question a long time. Then she added, beneath it, in smaller letters:

*Extra credit, for everyone: You are allowed to stop. I am telling you this the way a teacher tells a student the exam is open-book. This is permission. Take it.*

She folded the page into her ledger, between the entry for October 14th and the rules, and went to school through rain that fell like static.

* * *

Room 12 at eight-fifteen was full of morning light and eleven students who did not exist.

That was the thing no one warned you about — how ordinary they looked. How *right*. They sat in their desks with spines straight and hands folded, uniforms pressed, faces turned toward her with an attention so complete it felt like weather. Alice Pemberton chewed the end of her braid. Thomas Merrick had a ink stain on his left cuff that Sebastian had catalogued weeks ago and never seen change. And Wren Halloway sat third from the window, small and sharp-eyed, her hair ribboned in yellow, watching Sebastian with the particular patience of someone waiting for a train they know is coming.

"Good morning," Sebastian said.

"Good morning, Miss Delacroix," eleven voices answered, in unison so perfect it made her teeth ache. Fifty years of practice, she supposed. Or fifty years of something worse than practice.

She took attendance. She always took attendance; it felt like holding a rope over a drop. Eleven names, eleven voices, each one bright and glad and wrong in a way she couldn't have described to a living soul. When she reached Wren, the girl answered "Present" a half-beat late, and her eyes flicked — quickly, deliberately — toward Sebastian's satchel, where the ledger lived.

Sebastian understood.

At nine o'clock she announced a surprise examination. Her colleagues, she knew, saw nothing unusual in Room 12 — saw, perhaps, an empty classroom and a new teacher talking to chalk dust, though none of them ever said so, and the school's collective blindness had long since stopped surprising her. But the students heard her. The students *straightened*. And when she said the word *examination*, something moved through the room like wind through wheat — eleven bodies rippling with an emotion they hadn't been permitted in decades, and Sebastian realized with a lurch that it was dread. Real, human, childish dread. The dread of a test.

Good, she thought, and hated herself a little. Fear meant they believed this mattered. Fear meant they were still students, and students could learn.

"I'm handing out the papers now," she said, and took nothing from her satchel at all.

Instead she opened the ledger flat on her desk, found the folded page, and read the first question aloud into the sunlit, impossible room.

"What is your full name? Write it the way your mother said it."

For a moment, nothing. The light lay across the desks like a held breath. Then Wren Halloway smiled — that smile, the too-wide one, the one that had haunted Sebastian's mirrors since October — and said, politely, "May we use our own paper, Miss?"

"You may use any paper you like," Sebastian said. "But I'll only be able to read what appears in mine."

Another silence. Longer. Somewhere in the walls — or behind the walls, in the pooled and frozen second where the corridor waited — something shifted its weight. Sebastian kept her face level. *Never smile back.* She let her expression settle into the neutral mask of every teacher who has ever watched a class struggle with question one.

Then, on the open page of her ledger, between her own penciled lines, a word began to appear.

It came the way frost comes on glass — not written so much as *remembered into being*, gray strokes surfacing from the paper's grain. Sebastian's breath went thin. She had seen the message from Wren before, had read *we are tired of smiling* forty times, but seeing it happen, watching the handwriting assemble itself letter by letter in 1974's careful cursive, was different. It was like watching a hand reach up out of a lake.

*Wren Miriam Halloway,* the page said. *Mum called me Ren-Ren when I was small and Wrennie when I'd skinned my knee. Nobody calls me anything now except "smile."*

Sebastian read it twice. Then she said, aloud, steady as she could make her voice, "Thank you, Wren. Question two."

By the time the first bell rang, six answers had surfaced in the ledger, and Sebastian had read every one of them aloud to a classroom of children who had died before she was born, and had not cried, and had not smiled, and had kept her voice level the way you keep a candle cupped against wind.

* * *

Question four broke her.

*What is the last thing you remember being sad about?*

The answers came slowly for that one, surfacing one at a time across the afternoon, as if the question had to travel a long way down to find them. Alice Pemberton wrote: *My dog, Biscuit. He got hit on the Mill Road and Dad said we'd get another but we never did and I wasn't allowed to cry at supper.* Thomas Merrick wrote: *When my brother went to work in Coventry and the house went quiet.* Daniel Okafor wrote: *Nothing. We weren't allowed. That's the answer, miss. The last sad thing is that there wasn't a last sad thing.*

And Wren Halloway wrote: *I don't remember. I think they took it out the same day they put the smile in. But there's a shape where it used to be, and the shape hurts worse than crying ever did.*

Sebastian read that one standing at the window, her back to the class, because her face had begun to do something she couldn't permit it to do in front of them — not grief, exactly, and not a smile either, but the place where the two met, the trembling border country. Behind her, the room was silent in a way it had never been silent. No shuffling. No pens. Eleven children sitting with the sound of their own interior lives opening like doors that had been painted shut for fifty years.

"Miss?" Wren's voice, small. "Are we in trouble?"

Sebastian turned around. Eleven faces watched her, and here was the miracle, here was the thing she would later write down three times to be sure she hadn't invented it: the smiles were *flickering*. Not gone — God, not gone, the habit was fifty years deep and probably load-bearing — but stuttering, like lights on a failing circuit. Alice Pemberton's mouth had slipped sideways into something confused and human. Thomas Merrick was frowning, actually frowning, his brow creased as though the expression were new shoes he hadn't broken in.

"No," Sebastian said. "No one is in trouble. This is the opposite of trouble." She walked back to her desk and laid her hand flat on the ledger, over the ghost-gray writing. "This is what school is actually for. Not the smiling game. Not — " she caught herself before saying *what they taught you*, because naming the founders aloud in this room felt like striking a match in a gas leak. "Not performing. School is where you go to find out what's true, including what's true inside you. And what's true inside you right now, all of you, is that you are tired. And tiredness is allowed. Tiredness is *honest*."

"The game says—" began Alice, and stopped, because she didn't seem to know what the game said. That was the horror under the horror, Sebastian thought: they didn't even know the rules they were dying of. The game had been installed so deep it wore the costume of nature.

"The game," Sebastian said carefully, "is a rule someone else wrote. And I am the teacher now. In this room, my rules supersede." She picked up the chalk. On the board, in her plain modern hand, she wrote: **RULE ONE OF THIS CLASSROOM: YOU DO NOT HAVE TO SMILE.** She underlined it twice. "Copy that into yourselves however you copy things. That's the only material on today's exam that matters."

For a moment, nothing. Rain ticked against the windows. Then Wren Halloway laughed — a tiny, cracked, astonished sound, nothing like the silver bells of the recitation Sebastian sometimes heard through the walls at night — and clapped both hands over her own mouth as if she'd committed a crime.

"That's not in the game," Wren whispered through her fingers.

"No," Sebastian agreed. "It's better than the game."

* * *

She gave them the rest of the week.

There was no other way to say it, no professional vocabulary for it: she gave them the rest of the week. Every morning she read questions from the ledger and every afternoon the answers surfaced, and by Friday the book had become something no ledger in the history of accounting had ever been — a confessional, a census of the lost, a conversation conducted in frost. She learned that Ruth Calloway wanted to be a vet and had once bottle-fed a lamb with a dropper. She learned that Peter Vance missed the smell of his grandmother's kitchen, bread and coal smoke, and that he could still summon it if he tried, and that trying made him feel *guilty*, and Sebastian wrote in the margin: *guilt about joy is also grief, Peter. Grieve that too.* She learned that the season inside the second was neither winter nor autumn but the color of held breath, a permanent gray noon, and that they had been there — they estimated, they guessed, time pooled strangely — for what felt like both an afternoon and a geological age.

And on Friday evening, alone in her cottage with the towel pinned over the bathroom mirror and the lamp burning against the early dark, Sebastian turned to the final question and found that Wren had already answered it.

*Why did I write to you?*

The handwriting was different this time. Shakier. Pressed hard enough that the pencil had torn the paper's skin, and beneath the words Sebastian could see the faint impression of earlier drafts, erased and rewritten, a palimpsest of courage.

*I wrote because you're the first one who looked at us like we were people instead of a job. The others smiled at us. Smiling is how they feed us to it, miss, I understand that now, I've had fifty years to understand it. But you wrote us down. You put our names in a book with your own hand. Do you know what that's like, when nobody has said your name out loud since 1974? It's like being handed back your own skeleton. You didn't save us. You just refused to forget us, and that turns out to be the same door.*

*Here is what I know that you don't yet, and I'm sorry to tell it to you on a Friday:*

*The centennial is coming. Voss will ask you to sign. When he does, don't listen to his reasons, because his reasons are true and that's what makes them a trap. The thing under the school doesn't want your death. Death is nothing to it. It wants your consent. It has always wanted consent — that's the whole architecture, that's why the founders built it with invitations and receptions and smiling games, because a meal that walks willingly to the table keeps the hunger sweet.*

*We were eleven consents, miss. Children asked if they wanted to belong forever, and we said yes, because we were ten years old and belonging was the whole world.*

*Don't say yes.*

*And don't say no the way they expect, either. There's a third answer. We've been working on it. All eleven of us, all week, in the gray noon. Alice cried first — she'll pretend it was Thomas but it was Alice. And when she cried, the corridor got warmer. Just a degree. Just enough to notice.*

*We are tired of smiling, Miss Delacroix. Teach us the rest.*

Sebastian sat for a long time with the lamp hissing and the rain on the glass and the words graying themselves into permanence on the page. Then she did what she had been built to do, what her mother had built her to do, the only sacrament she had ever trusted: she reached for her pen, uncapped it, and beneath Wren's answer she wrote, in ink, in her own hand, witnessed by no one:

*Received. Understood. Lesson plan revised.*

*Monday, we begin Unit Two: How to Stop.*

Outside, the towel over the bathroom mirror shifted, very slightly, as if the air behind it had drawn a breath — and somewhere below the school, below the foundations, below the gray noon where eleven children were learning, one degree at a time, the temperature of grief, something old and patient and hungry felt its meal begin to move, and for the first time in a hundred years, grew afraid.
