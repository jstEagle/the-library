## 29. Voss Learns the Truth

Edmund Voss had not slept in the cottage in thirty years, but he still dreamed of it.

He dreamed of Room 12 as it had been in 1974, before the razored page, before the doctored ledgers — before he understood what his grandfather's school actually was. In the dream, Marguerite Okonkwo sat by the window with her chin on her fist, and she was laughing at something Wren Halloway had whispered, and the afternoon light came through the glass like honey poured slow, and Edmund was seventeen and certain that this was the whole of what happiness would ever be. He woke from that dream every morning at 4:17 a.m., precisely, because the thing beneath Hollowmere kept its accounts to the minute, and it did not like him dreaming of her unsmiling.

He was awake now, of course. He was always awake now. He stood at the window of the headmaster's residence with a glass of watered whisky he hadn't touched, watching the rain come down over the quadrangle in long gray sheets, and he thought: *two days*.

Two days until the centennial reception. Two days until Delacroix either signed or didn't. Two days until the hunger beneath the foundations was fed or went unfed, and everything — the school, the town, the careful machinery of forgetting that four generations of Voss men had oiled with their silence — either continued or collapsed.

He told himself he wanted her to sign. He told himself it was mercy. He had been telling himself that for so long the words had worn smooth as river stones, and when he held them up to the light they no longer reflected anything at all.

The knock came at eleven minutes past midnight.

Voss knew who it was before he opened the door. There was only one person left at Hollowmere who knocked like that — three sharp raps, a pause, then one more, as if the last knock were an afterthought, an amendment, a *and also, I am not afraid of you*. Sebastian Delacroix had been knocking like that for weeks.

He opened the door and she stood in the rain-hissed dark holding a ledger against her chest the way other people held children, or wounds.

"Headmaster," she said. "I need to show you something. And you need to be sitting down."

"I'm quite all right standing."

"You won't be." Her voice was flat and steady and terribly kind. "Please, Mr. Voss. I'm not here to fight you. I'm here because you're the only one left who remembers her face."

The rain filled the silence between them. After a moment, Voss stepped back and let her in.

* * *

She would not show him at first. That was the cruelty of it, or the mercy — he could never afterward decide which. She sat across from him in his study, among the leather spines and the portrait of his grandfather Aldous Voss gazing down with painted benevolence, and she made him talk first.

"Tell me about her," Sebastian said. "Not the version you tell the trustees. The true one."

"There is no true one anymore," Voss said. "That's rather the point of everything."

"There's always a true one. It just gets buried under the official one. My mother taught me that." She set the ledger on the desk between them but kept her hand flat upon its cover. "Marguerite Okonkwo. What was she like?"

And God help him, he talked. He talked because no one had asked him that question in fifty years, because the answer had been locked in his chest so long it had grown around his ribs like a tree growing around a fence. He told her about the way Marguerite chewed her pencil when she was working through a proof. About how she'd once climbed out the dormitory window in February to prove the ivy was strong enough, and fallen into a snowdrift, and laughed so hard the matron couldn't punish her. About how she wanted to be an engineer — *an engineer*, in 1974, when the guidance counselor had suggested secretarial school and Marguerite had told the man, politely, that he could keep his suggestions somewhere the sun didn't reach.

"She was going to build bridges," Voss said. His voice had gone strange to his own ears. "That's what she said. Bridges, because everyone else built walls."

Sebastian nodded slowly. "Wren Halloway wrote something similar. In her essay. About the smiling game — she said Marguerite was the best at it, and that it made her sad, because Marguerite only smiled like that for other people's benefit. Never for herself."

Something turned over in Voss's chest, cold and enormous.

"What essay?"

"The one she submitted last week. Dated 1974." Sebastian lifted her hand from the ledger at last. "Mr. Voss, I'm going to show you her face now. Not the way the school keeps her. The way she is. And I need you to promise me — I need your word — that whatever happens, you will not smile at her. Whatever you feel, however much you want to comfort her, you will not smile. Do you understand? A smile is an invitation. It's how they keep them. It's how they've always kept them."

"That's superstition."

"It's grammar," Sebastian said. "It's the grammar of the thing that lives under your school. You've spoken it fluently your whole life. Now I'm asking you to stop."

He should have refused. Fifty years of duty said refuse; four generations of Voss men said refuse; the smooth-worn stones of *it is mercy, it is mercy* said refuse. But he was tired in a way that sleep had never touched, and outside the rain was coming down like the sky trying to remember how to weep, and he heard himself say:

"All right. I give you my word."

Sebastian opened the ledger.

What happened next did not happen in his study. He understood that immediately, the way you understand you are dreaming. The lamplight thickened. The rain went silent — not faded, *silenced*, as if a hand had closed over the world's mouth. The air took on the quality of water, of deep water, pressure without weight. And the mirror above his grandfather's fireplace — a mirror that had hung there since 1874, that had watched three generations of Voss men practice their rehearsed smiles — began to lag.

He saw it happen. He saw Sebastian raise her hand and the reflection follow a full second behind, then two, then five, the gap widening like a wound opening. The surface of the mirror stopped being a surface. It became a corridor.

And in the corridor stood Marguerite.

She was sixteen forever. That was the first obscenity of it — that the girl was exactly as she had been, the same cropped hair, the same ink-stained fingers, the same cardigan with the loose button she'd never let anyone sew. But she was standing in a posture no living girl would hold: perfectly upright, hands folded, facing forward, and her face —

Her face was smiling.

It was not a smile. Voss had seen smiles his whole life; he had manufactured ten thousand of them for donors and trustees and frightened new teachers; he knew the species entire. This was not that. This was a smile stretched past its architecture, held past endurance, a smile maintained by something that did not understand faces except as instruments. Her eyes were open and dry and fixed on nothing, and the corners of her mouth were pulled up as if by hooks, and she was beautiful and she was wrong and she was *his*, the girl who fell into snowdrifts, wearing joy like a muzzle.

"Marguerite," he said.

Nothing. No flicker. The reflection-She did not turn. The corridor held her the way amber holds an insect — perfectly, permanently, without malice and without mercy.

"She can't hear you yet," Sebastian said quietly. "They're deep in it. But they're not gone, Mr. Voss. That's what no one understands. They're not gone. Watch."

Sebastian leaned toward the mirror and spoke in a clear, level voice, the voice of a teacher taking attendance, and she said:

"Marguerite Okonkwo. You climbed out the dormitory window in February of 1974 to prove the ivy would hold. It didn't. You fell into the snowdrift by the chapel wall, and you laughed, and Mrs. Garrett couldn't punish you because half the class had followed you out. You wanted to build bridges. You told the guidance counselor he could keep his suggestions where the sun didn't reach."

The smile flickered.

It was small — a stutter, a skipped frame, the way a film jumps when the reel snags. But Voss saw it. He saw the hooks loosen by a single degree, and in that degree something moved behind Marguerite's dry open eyes, something that had been waiting fifty years in the dark for permission.

"No," Voss whispered. "No, no —"

"Marguerite," Sebastian said, gentler now. "You died in the winter of 1974. All eleven of you did. The school fed you to something old, and it has been wearing your happiness ever since. You have been smiling for fifty-one years. You are allowed to stop."

And Marguerite Okonkwe — sixteen forever, engineer of bridges — began to cry.

There was no sound. That was somehow worse than sound. Her face simply *broke*, the way ice breaks on a river in spring, all at once and irreversibly, the smile collapsing inward into a grief so total that her features seemed to rearrange themselves around it, and tears ran down the frozen corridor's version of her cheeks, and her mouth opened in a shape that was not a smile, that was the opposite of a smile, that was the oldest human shape there is, and Voss felt fifty-one years of careful, complicit, well-oiled silence go out of him like heat out of an open door.

He was on his knees. He did not remember getting there. His forehead nearly touched the cold hearthstone, and the sounds coming out of him were not dignified, were not headmasterly, were the sounds of a boy of seventeen losing the girl by the window all over again — except this time there was no ledger to doctor, no page to razor out, no grandfather's hand on his shoulder saying *this is how it must be, Edmund, this is how it has always been.*

"She's in pain," he gasped. "You're causing her pain —"

"I'm causing her *grief*," Sebastian said. "They're not the same thing. Pain is what the thing below uses. Grief is what love turns into when it has nowhere to go. She has fifty-one years of it backed up, Mr. Voss. Every smile she was forced to wear has been storing it. Let her spend it."

In the mirror, Marguerite wept, and the corridor around her seemed to shudder, and far off — impossibly far off, down through the foundations, down through the century of fed and fattened years — something vast turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake hungry.

Then the mirror snapped shut. Lag gone. Surface plain. Rain audible again. The study was only a study, smelling of pipe tobacco and dust, and Sebastian was closing the ledger with hands that shook only slightly, and Voss knelt on the floor of the life he had built out of lies and wept like something newly born.

* * *

Later — he did not know how much later; the clocks in the residence had never kept honest time since his grandfather's day — they sat on opposite sides of the cold fireplace, and Voss told her everything.

He told her about 1975, the year after, when he was eighteen and grieving and the school offered him a scholarship he hadn't applied for, and his grandfather had taken him down beneath the east wing to see the founders' ledger, and explained the covenant in the calm voice of a man discussing endowments. One smile per generation. One teacher who would stand before the children and mean it, or learn to mean it, or be remade until they did. In exchange: the school thrived. The town thrived. Nothing sickened, nothing failed, nothing *grieved* beyond a decent interval.

"They sold the town's sorrow," Voss said. "That's what nobody understood. Hollowmere didn't just take the children. It took mourning itself. Funerals here lasted an hour. Widows remarried in a month. We called it resilience." He laughed, and the laugh was a terrible thing. "We were a town that had forgotten how to cry, and we called it resilience."

"And you cooperated," Sebastian said. Not accusing. Just placing the stone on the scale.

"I survived," Voss said. "I was twenty-two and they put me in front of a classroom of empty desks and told me to smile, and I smiled, and the thing below let me live, and I told myself for thirty years that survival was the same thing as virtue." He looked at her. "You've read my predecessors' files. The teacher from '98. The one from 2011. You know what happens to the ones who resist alone."

"I know."

"So why?" His voice cracked on it. "Why show me this? Why break me? You need me locked and loyal for two more days. Shattering me serves nothing."

Sebastian was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked against the window.

"Because you loved her," she said finally. "Fifty-one years, and you never once tried to see her again — because seeing her meant knowing. But you kept her photo in your desk drawer, turned facedown, and I found it, and I noticed you never threw it away. Love like that doesn't die, Mr. Voss. It just goes underground and waits for someone to dig." She met his eyes. "I'm not breaking you. I'm digging."

Voss looked into the dead fireplace for a long time. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — emptied out, scoured, the voice of a man who has burned his furniture for warmth and now sees the room clearly for the first time.

"The centennial hall," he said. "The doors are warded. Founder's work — iron keys cut in 1874, and the locks don't answer to force or to theft. They answer to the sitting headmaster's intent. To my *consent*." He rose, joints cracking, an old man suddenly, fifty-one years arriving all at once in his face. "Friday night, when the town fills that hall and the eleven chairs sit empty at the front, the entity will expect me to present you. To offer you. That presentation is the hinge of the whole covenant — it has been since my grandfather's generation."

"And if the headmaster declines to present?"

"Then the invitation lapses." He said it like a man testing whether a bridge would hold. "But it needs to lapse *cleanly*. If the hall stays sealed, the thing feeds on anticipation alone — it can stretch the night for years inside those walls. The doors have to be open. The town has to see. Whatever you're planning, Miss Delacroix — and I know you're planning, you're the most documented woman I've ever met — you'll need that hall open, and every mirror in it uncovered, and me standing beside you when you refuse."

"You understand what that costs you," Sebastian said. "The pact, the school, your name. Everything your family built."

"My family built a slaughterhouse with a chapel on top," Voss said. "Let it cost."

He crossed to the cabinet by the window — the tall iron-banded cabinet that the trustees believed held the school's founding charters — and took from it two keys. They were black with age, longer than a hand, their teeth cut in patterns that hurt to look at directly, and when he lifted them the rain outside went briefly, utterly silent, as if the thing beneath the school had heard the sound of metal it had not heard in a hundred years and did not like it.

Voss weighed them in his palm. Then he held them out to Sebastian.

"The east door and the west," he said. "Unlock them Friday at dusk, before the guests arrive, and leave them open. I'll handle the trustees, the town council, the program. As far as anyone knows, the centennial proceeds exactly as planned." His mouth twisted into something that was almost, for the first time in decades, a real expression — grim and grieving and free. "And at the appointed hour, when they call the next teacher up to smile for the children —"

"You'll introduce me anyway," Sebastian said, taking the keys. Their cold sank straight through her skin. "And I'll walk to the front carrying my ledger. And I will teach."

"Teach what?"

"Grief," she said simply. "Lesson one: the names of the dead. Lesson two: how they died. Lesson three—" her voice caught, just once, the first crack he had ever seen in her, "—lesson three is that stopping smiling isn't dying. It's the beginning of coming home."

Voss nodded slowly. At the door, as she was leaving, he stopped her.

"Miss Delacroix. The face you showed me. Will she — after Friday — will she know? That I waited? That I never—"

Sebastian paused in the doorway, rain-light silvering her shoulders.

"I think," she said, "that grief is the only message that gets through. Tell her Friday. With your whole face. Not a smile." 

She went out into the dark.

Voss stood alone in his study, beneath his grandfather's painted benevolence, and for the first time in fifty-one years he did not practice his smile. Instead he took the photograph from his desk drawer, turned it faceup at last, and set it on the mantel where the mirror could see it — a boy and a girl by a dormitory window, laughing, unguarded, alive.

Far below the foundations, something vast and hungry felt the hinge of its century begin, very slowly, to turn.

And in the town of Hollowmere, every clock struck one — and then, softly, wrongly, struck it again.
