## 32. The Smile They Want

The hall smelled of lilies and floor wax, and both were wrong.

Sebastian knew the scent of lilies — her mother had grown them along the south fence of the house on Marrow Street, and every August the whole yard had gone white and heavy with them, so that grief and summer were braided together in her memory forever. But Hollowmere's centennial hall had no windows that opened, no garden beyond its stone, and no reason on earth for lilies. The smell was a set dressing. It was a scent chosen by something that had studied her grief the way a forger studies a signature.

The floor wax was worse. It smelled like her mother's hallway on Sunday mornings, when the light came through the frosted pane and the whole house felt scrubbed and holy and safe. Sebastian stood at the edge of the crowd with her ledger held against her chest like a breastplate, and she let the smell wash over her, and she hated herself for the small, traitorous warmth it produced.

The town had come. That was the first thing she'd understood when she walked in — the whole town, or enough of it: the butcher's wife in her good coat, the two councilmen who never attended school functions, families she'd only seen as shapes behind lit windows on her walk from the cottage. They sat in folding rows facing the stage, and on the stage stood eleven chairs.

Empty chairs. Polished. Arranged in a gentle arc, like a class photo waiting to be taken.

Voss stood at the podium in his best gray suit, and his smile had finally, at last, reached his eyes. That was how she knew the end had truly begun. For eight weeks she had watched that smile sit on his face like a mask strapped on by someone else, and now it lived there, warm and genuine and terrible, because Voss believed — had always believed — that what came next was mercy.

"—and so, on our hundredth year," he was saying, his voice carrying that practiced schoolmaster's cadence, "we welcome a new member into the long tradition of Hollowmere. Miss Sebastian Delacroix, our newest teacher, who has already shown such devotion to our students. Miss Delacroix — smile for the children."

The room turned toward her. Two hundred faces. She counted them out of habit — counting, always counting, her mother's litany running under everything: *write it down, write it down, what is written cannot be unwritten* — and the count came back wrong. Two hundred faces, and not one of them blinked at the same time.

She walked down the aisle. Her legs carried her because she had decided they would, and because the plan was in her pocket, written in pencil, and because Wren had asked to be forgotten properly and forgetting required a witness. But her body was not calm. Her heart was a fist beating on a locked door, and every step toward the stage felt like walking into deepening water.

The lilies thickened. The wax smell thickened. And then, as she reached the front row, the lights overhead dimmed by a single degree — a dimming so gentle that no one else in the hall so much as shifted — and the mirrors changed.

There were no mirrors in the centennial hall. She had checked. She had checked twice, with her ledger open, on Thursday, because she was Sebastian Delacroix and checking twice was the whole architecture of her survival. And yet along the back wall, behind the folding chairs, where the honor boards should have been, there now stood a row of tall mirrors in dark frames, and every one of them was lagging.

Not by a second. Not by five. The reflections in those mirrors were so far behind that they showed the hall as it had been minutes ago — showed her own entrance, showed Voss mid-sentence at an earlier point in his speech — and threaded through the lag, moving at the wrong speed, were figures.

Children. Eleven of them, faint as breath on glass, walking the reflected aisles in their 1974 uniforms, smiling.

*Don't look*, said the rules in her ledger, in her own handwriting, in the warm ink that had never faded: *never smile back, never accept a gift, never say goodbye.* But the rules had not anticipated this, because the rules assumed the entity would come at her sideways, through lag and suggestion and the polite machinery of the school. It had not occurred to her — how could it have — that it would simply come out and *speak*.

"Sebastian."

Her mother's voice.

It came from everywhere and nowhere, from the lilies and the wax and the space just behind her left ear, and it was perfect. Not approximate. Not the flattened memory her own mind had been degrading for three years, the way all memories degrade, the voice losing its grain and its humor and its specific tiredness at the end of long days. This was her mother's voice with every frequency intact, warm and dry and faintly amused, the voice that had said *write it down, love, the world will try to tell you it didn't happen* — and Sebastian's whole body turned toward it like a plant toward light.

"You always hated crowds," the voice said, gentle. "You get that from me. Come here. Let me look at you."

At the front of the hall, the eleven empty chairs were no longer empty.

They filled the way a photograph develops — not movement but arrival, the children simply *there*, seated in their arc, hands folded, faces turned toward her. And they were smiling. They had always been smiling; that was the horror she had carried since the frozen corridor behind the mirror, the horror of fifty years of joy with nothing under it. But the smiles had changed. They were softer now. Warmer. They were smiles she recognized, because she had spent weeks teaching them, in the illicit classes held in the second behind the wall, to let their faces fall — and the thing wearing them had studied every lesson.

It was showing her the smiles it had taken from her. It was wearing her own curriculum as a mask.

"Miss Delacroix." Voss extended his hand toward the chairs, toward the children, his voice thick with something that was not performance. "You see? They came. They always come, for the renewal. Fifty years I have set these chairs, and fifty years they have sat in them, and I have never once —" his voice caught, actually caught, and Sebastian understood with a lurch that everything he'd confessed to her in the recorded conversation was true, all of it, the love and the cowardice and the hope worn smooth as river stone — "I have never once been allowed this close."

*Don't answer him*, she told herself. *Don't answer anything. You are here to witness, and to speak the names, and to refuse.*

But her mother's voice was still speaking, and it had moved to the place where her resolve kept its house.

"You're so thin," it said. "You don't eat when you grieve. You never have. You think I don't know? You think I'm not — " a pause, delicate as a held breath — "I'm *proud* of you, Sebastian. What you've done here. What you've written down. No one else could have found them. No one else could have given them back their names."

And God help her, the warmth was real. That was the trap's true engineering — not the voice, not the lilies, but the warmth, which was not manufactured at all. The entity had found the warmth already in her and was simply redirecting it, the way a thief redirects a river. Because she *was* proud, somewhere under the terror. She had found eleven names the school had spent fifty years razoring out of its own face, and she had taught a dead girl to write an essay, and she had torn a page out of a covenant to save an old woman who had spent forty years cleaning around a secret. That pride was hers. It was real. And the thing at the front of the hall was holding it out to her like a hand extended across a table.

"Sit with us," said Wren Halloway.

Sebastian's breath stopped.

Wren had risen from the third chair. She stood in her gray uniform with her dark hair loose — no ribbon; Sebastian had refused the ribbon; the ribbon was still in her desk drawer wrapped in paper like evidence — and her smile was Wren's smile, the real one, the crooked one, the one that had cracked for the first time in the frozen corridor when Sebastian had said *your mother kept the house on Fell Street until 1989, she never stopped setting your place at the table.*

"You found me," Wren said. "Out of everyone, in fifty years, you *found* me." She took a step forward, off the stage, into the aisle, close enough that Sebastian could see the exact gray of her eyes, and the terrible thing — the thing Sebastian knew she would carry to her grave whether she survived this night or not — was that it was not horror anymore. Standing in front of Wren Halloway felt like standing in front of a student who had finally raised her hand. It felt like the whole reason she had ever set foot in a classroom.

"You could stay," Wren said softly. Around her, the other ten were rising too, coming down into the aisle, and the crowd in the folding chairs watched with slack, joyful faces, and Voss wept openly at the podium, and the mirrors along the back wall showed it all happening a half-minute late, over and over, a memory being rehearsed. "You could stay and we'd be your class. Not the class that vanished. *Your* class. You'd never lose another one. You'd never walk into an empty room again. You'd never —"

"— be alone," said her mother's voice, and now it came from the mirrors, from all of them at once, from every lagging reflection, and the hall's lights dimmed another degree, and the lilies were so thick she could taste them. "That's all this has ever been, love. That's all it ever offered anyone. Not a monster. A family that doesn't die. You've been so lonely. Since me. You write everything down because you're afraid that if you stop, there'll be nothing left holding the world together, and you'd be in it alone."

The truth of it went through her like a blade through wet paper.

She *was* afraid of that. She had been afraid of it since the hospital corridor, since the notebook she'd kept at her mother's bedside recording every medication, every breath rate, every hour, as if documentation were a rope she could tie around the world to keep it from sliding away. The compulsion was grief. She knew that. She had always known that. And now something in the dark had read her whole life back to her in her mother's voice and offered to take the weight of it — offered her a room that was never empty, a class that never graduated and never left, eleven faces that would smile at her across the desks of Room 12 until the sun burned out.

The chair at the center of the arc was the largest. It had not been there before. It sat at the center like a throne's modest cousin, wooden and plain, and it was the only chair on the stage that faced *outward* — facing her, facing the town, facing the future — and she understood what it was for. One willing smile. A century of feeding. A family that never leaves.

"Say yes," whispered the eleven, in eleven voices, and under the eleven voices her mother's voice, and under her mother's voice something older that had no voice at all, only hunger, vast and patient and *tired*, and Sebastian felt her own mouth begin — horribly, gently, without her permission — to turn up at the corners.

*Never smile back.*

She caught it. Barely. She caught the smile the way you catch a falling glass — late, clumsy, with the shock of it jangling up her arm — and she held her face still with both hands, actually pressed her palms against her own cheeks in front of two hundred people, and the crowd murmured its soft approving murmur, because to them she was only a nervous teacher at a ceremony, and the murmur was the kindest sound in the hall and the most damning.

*Write it down.*

Her hands were shaking so badly she nearly dropped the ledger getting it open. But the pencil was in the spine where she always kept it, and the page fell open to the plan written in pencil, and she looked at her own handwriting — her own, the one thing in this hall the entity could not wear, could not forge, could not offer back to her — and she held on to it like a rail over a drop.

They were close now. All of them. The eleven stood in a half-circle around her, and the warmth pouring off them was almost unbearable, because it was not the entity's warmth, it was *theirs* — Wren's crooked hope, the tall boy Aldous's shy diligence, the twins' matched mischief — everything Sebastian had spent weeks coaxing back out of them in the corridor behind the glass, everything she had loved, gathered up and held out as bait by something that had never loved anything in a hundred years.

"You can stop writing now," her mother's voice said, and it was so tender. "You can put the book down, love. You're safe. You're *home*. You never have to hold the world together again."

And Sebastian Delacroix, standing in the smell of lilies with her dead mother's voice in her ears and a dead girl's smile three feet from her face, felt the temptation crest over her like a wave — felt it in her knees, in her throat, in the animal part of her that had been cold and lonely for three years — and she let it crest, and she did not fight it, because fighting it would have meant pretending she didn't want it, and lying was the entity's language, not hers.

Instead she said, quietly, in a voice that carried further than it should have in that hushed hall:

"I know. That's what makes it a trap."

The hall went still.

"I *want* it," she said, and her voice cracked, and she let it crack, because her mother had taught her that what is written cannot be unwritten and what is spoken cannot be taken back, and she wanted this said, here, in front of the town and the mirrors and the hungry dark. "I want my mother's voice. I want to walk into Room 12 and see all of you, every morning, forever. I have wanted it since the first night I heard you reciting through the wall. If I said yes right now, it wouldn't even be a sacrifice. That's the whole trick. That's what it's been doing for a hundred years — finding the ones who are lonely enough that the cage looks like a home, and calling it mercy." She looked at Voss. He was staring at her with his smile gone, finally, utterly gone. "It's not mercy. It never was. It's a mouth, and it's asking us to feed it, and it's using the people we love as the spoon."

The mirrors along the back wall shivered.

Not shattered — shivered, all at once, like the surface of a struck pond, and in the shivering the lag *stuttered*, the reflections jumping, and for half a second the hall saw itself truly: the crowd's slack faces slackening further into confusion, the eleven children flickering like bad film, and beneath Wren's smile, for half a second, something else — something gaunt and vast and patient turning over in the dark under the school, disturbed, *offended*, a thing that had offered its finest gift and been declined.

"You would grieve us," Wren said. Her smile held, but her voice — Wren's true voice, the one from the corridor, the one that had said *we are tired of smiling* — came through underneath it like a pulse under skin. "You'd rather grieve us than keep us."

"I'd rather you grieve *yourselves*," Sebastian said. "That's the only way any of us leaves this room free."

The lilies wilted. She watched it happen — the scent dying in the air all at once, like a candle pinched out, and the smell that came up behind it was cold stone and old water and something faintly, unmistakably like an empty stomach. The hall's lights steadied. The crowd shifted, murmuring, unsure what they were waiting for. And the eleven children stood in their half-circle around her, smiling their borrowed smiles, and behind the smiles — visible now, because she had named the trick and naming is a kind of light — she could see them straining. Ten children and one girl with loose dark hair, pushing against the inside of their own faces like hands against a locked window.

*Hold on*, she wanted to say. *Hold on, hold on, one more lesson.*

She did not say it. Saying it was goodbye, and the rules were the rules, and she had written them herself in ink that never faded.

Voss stepped back from the podium. His face was gray. His hand went to the renewal document lying on the lectern — the covenant's newest page, her name already inked in handwriting that was not hers — and he looked at it, and then at the flickering children, and then at her, and Sebastian watched the last fifty years of his certainty die in his eyes.

"Miss Delacroix," he said hoarsely. "The document. It requires — it has always required — a signature freely given, before the town, before the chairs. If you will not sign —"

"Then it lapses," Sebastian said. "At sunrise. You told me that yourself, in October, when you still thought I'd say yes. The invitation needs a host. No host, no pact. No pact —" she turned to face the eleven, to face the dark behind their smiles, and she lifted the ledger open in both hands like a lamp — "no *meal*."

The thing beneath the school finally stopped pretending to be a family.

It came up through the floor of the hall the way cold comes up through stone — not seen, but *felt*, a pressure change, a dropping of the air, and every mirror in the room went black at once, and the eleven empty chairs scraped backward across the stage as if shoved, and the last thing Sebastian saw of Wren before the dark took the shape of the room was the girl's smile, still fixed, still frozen — and beneath it, in her eyes, a fierce and unmistakable nod.

*One more lesson*, the nod said. *I remember.*

The lights went out. Somewhere in the blackness, two hundred people began, very softly, to scream — and beneath their screaming, beneath the cold, beneath the hunger, Sebastian Delacroix opened her ledger to the page of names, found her pencil, and began, in the dark, to speak the first of the eleven aloud.
