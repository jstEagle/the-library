## 33. Grief Lesson

The hall smelled of lilies and old varnish, and beneath both — faint, like something drowned surfacing — the sweet-rot stink of the thing that had worn this school for a hundred years.

Sebastian walked to the front carrying her ledger against her chest with both arms, the way her mother had once carried a Bible she no longer believed in. Behind her, eleven empty chairs stood in a row on the stage, their wooden backs polished to a shine by fifty years of nothing. The whole town had come. Farmers in their funeral suits, shopkeepers, the mayor's wife in pearls, three generations of Hollowmere alumni holding programs printed on cream stock: *A Century of Light — Centennial Reception.* Voss stood at the podium introducing her, his voice warm and steady and wrong, and every face in the audience was turned toward him with that slack, honeyed attention she had learned to dread.

*Smile for the children,* the program said at the bottom, in small italic type.

She did not smile.

Her hands were shaking. She let them shake. Her mother had taught her that too, in the last winter, when the morphine made the walls breathe: *If your hands shake, write anyway. The shaking is just the truth trying to get out through your skin.*

Voss finished. He turned to her, and for one unguarded instant — one half-second, the width of a lagging mirror — his eyes were not warm at all. They were terrified. Then he stepped aside, and the applause began, and it went on too long, and it sounded like rain on a roof over a grave.

Sebastian set the ledger on the podium. Opened it. Found the page she had copied out forty times until her hand knew it blind: eleven names, eleven dates, eleven causes, each one written in pencil first and then traced in ink, because ink was what lasted, because ink was what the thing beneath the school could not digest.

"Thank you for coming," she said.

Her voice carried strangely in the hall. Not echoing — *delaying*. As if the room were considering each sentence before permitting it to exist. She felt the mirrors along the back wall — the great gilt-framed ones the founders had installed in 1924, the ones no one ever dusted but which never held dust — leaning toward her like an audience of their own.

"I was asked to come up here tonight and smile for the children," she said. "I'd like to talk about the children instead."

A ripple went through the room. Small. Almost nothing. But she saw a woman in the third row stop smiling — just for a heartbeat — before the smile climbed back onto her face like a dog returning to its master.

"In 1974," Sebastian said, "eleven students attended Hollowmere Academy. They had names. They had handwriting — Wren Halloway looped her g's like cursive waves; Daniel Okafor pressed so hard he tore the paper. They had favorite seats, and grudges, and one of them — Marguerite Voss—" she looked directly at the headmaster, who had gone the color of candle wax— "kept a tin of peppermints in her desk drawer and shared them with anyone who sat alone at lunch."

"Miss Delacroix." Voss's voice was gentle. Pleading. *Stop.* "This isn't—"

"They died on the fourteenth of November, 1974," Sebastian said, louder, over him, over the hall, over the thing in the walls that had begun, very quietly, to hum. "They were not transferred. They did not run away. They were led down into the east cellar during a fire drill that was never filed, and they were told it was a game — the smiling game — and they were told that if they kept smiling, no harm would come to them."

The humming stopped.

The silence that replaced it was worse. It was the silence of a mouth opening.

"And they smiled," Sebastian said. "God help them, they smiled. Because they were children, and children believe adults, and the adults of this town fed them to something hungry and called it tradition."

The chandeliers dimmed. Not flickered — *dimmed*, evenly, like pupils contracting. In the darkened glass of the back mirrors, Sebastian could see the reflection of the hall, and the reflection was wrong: in it, the chairs on the stage were not empty. Eleven small figures sat in them, hands folded, faces turned toward her, smiling with the terrible patience of things that have waited fifty years to be acknowledged.

*Not yet,* she thought at them. *Hold on. I'm coming to you.*

She turned back to the room. Somewhere in the crowd, a man had begun to laugh — soft, helpless, delighted laughter, the sound of someone watching a wonderful show — and Sebastian understood with a lurch that the entity was already working the room, already pouring its counterfeit joy into two hundred open mouths, and that she had perhaps ninety seconds before the whole hall went slack and golden and hers forever.

So she did the only thing she had ever been good at.

She read aloud.

"Wren Halloway," she said. "Age twelve. Drowned — the coroner's word was drowning, though there was no water in her lungs, only cellar dust. She wanted to be a marine biologist. She had never seen the ocean."

The laughing man stopped laughing.

"Daniel Okafor. Age thirteen. Suffocated. His family moved away the following spring and never wrote again, because grief with no body to bury is a door that won't close."

An older woman near the aisle made a small sound, like a stitch tearing.

"Marguerite Voss. Age twelve. Cause of death withheld by arrangement. She liked peppermints. She is still waiting for her brother to visit her, and he has visited her every year for fifty years, and every year he has smiled instead of wept, and every year she has had to watch him do it."

Voss made a noise Sebastian would remember for the rest of her life — the sound of a man whose duty finally breaks against the rock of his love. He did not try to stop her again. He gripped the edge of the stage and held on.

"Tomasz Brandt. Age fourteen. Beatrice Okonkwo—"

In the fourth row, Mrs. Okonkwo rose to her feet. She was not smiling. Her face was wet and fierce and sixty years old, and she said, in a voice that shook the lilies in their vases: "Say her name again."

"Beatrice Okonkwo," Sebastian said. "Age thirteen. Your sister. Who waved at you from the school bus every morning, and who has been standing behind the glass of this school's mirrors every night since, waiting for someone to say her name out loud where the walls could hear it."

Okonkwo sat down slowly, both hands over her mouth, and wept — openly, loudly, without shame — and the sound of it was like a key turning in a lock that had been rusted shut since 1974.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing Sebastian had understood at 3 a.m., copying the founders' ledger by lamplight while the cottage doors rearranged themselves around her: the entity fed on joy, yes, but not real joy. Real joy was too quick, too mixed with everything else — too human. It fed on *unearned* joy. On smiles worn over graves. On belonging performed in place of mourning. Every centennial reception, every alumni dinner, every yearbook signed *best wishes always!* over the razored-out photograph — those were its meals. And grief, honest grief, grief with names attached and tears allowed, was poison to it. Was exorcism.

The founders had known. That was why the ledger existed. That was why forgetting had been engineered so carefully, generation after generation. A town that remembered could mourn. A town that mourned would starve it.

Sebastian kept reading.

"Priya Chandrasekaran. Age twelve. Elias Finch. Age thirteen. Ruth Ann Mercer, who was left-handed and had to write with her right, and whose essays were full of smudges, and who was proud of every one of them..."

One by one. Name by name. Date by date. Cause by cause. She gave them back their small human details like coins placed on the eyes of the dead: the boy who collected bottle caps, the girl who was afraid of thunder, the twins who finished each other's sentences even in the attendance register, writing each other's initials beside their own.

And the room changed.

It changed the way a frozen river changes — invisibly at first, then all at once, with a groan. The program papers trembled in two hundred hands. A farmer took off his hat. The mayor's wife's pearls seemed suddenly obscene, and she clawed them off and dropped them in her lap. People were crying now — not the pretty crying of funerals, but the ugly, honking, graceless crying of truth arriving fifty years late, and every tear that fell hit the floorboards like rain on a long-dry field.

And the entity convulsed.

Sebastian felt it before she saw it — a shudder through the floor, through the walls, through the roots of her teeth, as if the whole building were a throat trying to swallow something too large. The chandeliers guttered. The lilies blackened in their vases, curling like paper in flame. Along the back wall, the great mirrors bulged — actually *bulged*, their surfaces swelling outward like the skin of a drum struck too hard — and in every one of them the reflected hall was screaming, though the real hall had gone silent except for weeping.

"You fed on our happiness," Sebastian said, and her voice did not shake now, and she did not know where the steadiness came from unless it came from her mother, from every page her mother had ever written to hold a dissolving world together. "But happiness was never yours. You stole it. You wore it. And underneath every smile you farmed, there was always this —"

She lifted the ledger high.

"— *this*. The record. The names. The truth. Grief is not the opposite of joy. Grief is what joy costs, and you never paid, and now the bill has come due."

The mirrors shattered.

All of them, simultaneously, outward — not inward, not toward the room, but *outward*, away from it, as if the reflections themselves were fleeing, as if two hundred years of stolen light had burst its cage and gone streaming back to wherever light goes when it is finally released. Glass rained across the back wall in a glittering curtain. Someone screamed. The chandeliers died completely, and in the sudden dark the only illumination came from the stage, where — Sebastian's breath caught — the eleven empty chairs were glowing.

Faintly. Warmly. Like windows seen from a cold road at dusk.

And in the glow, the air above the chairs was thinning, peeling, opening — a seam of pale gold widening in the middle of the hall, and through it came a smell no one in that room had smelled in fifty years: chalk dust and pencil shavings and October leaves and the particular warmth of a classroom radiator on a cold morning.

The seam widened. Shapes gathered in it. Small shapes. Eleven of them.

Wren Halloway stood at the front of them, in her 1970s uniform, her hair ribbon faded almost white, and her face —

Her face was changing. Sebastian watched it happen. Watched the smile — the vast, fixed, fifty-year smile that had haunted her mirrors since autumn — begin to *crack*. To tremble. To fall apart at the corners like old plaster, like a mask whose wearer has finally stopped holding it up.

Wren's lower lip quivered. Her chin crumpled. Her eyes — her living, grieving, twelve-year-old eyes — filled and spilled over, and the first tear she had shed in half a century rolled down her cheek and fell onto the stage boards, and where it landed, the wood darkened and softened and became, briefly, ordinary. Just wood. Just a school. Just a floor.

"We're tired," Wren said. Her voice was small and rusty and the most beautiful thing Sebastian had ever heard. "We're so tired of smiling."

"I know," Sebastian said. She came down from the podium, slowly, keeping her hands visible, the way you approach anything wild and wounded. "I know, sweetheart. You don't have to anymore. None of you do. Cry as long as you need to. I'll write down every minute of it."

Behind her, the darkness at the edges of the hall recoiled — a vast, starving shape pressing itself into the corners, shrinking from the sound of two hundred people weeping honestly, from the sound of names spoken in love instead of ritual, from the sound of a girl crying for the first time since 1974. It hissed. It *begged*. For one horrible moment Sebastian heard it clearly, heard the voices it had collected wearing it like feathers: her mother's voice, saying *Sebastian, come home*; the 1998 teacher's voice, mid-sentence; dozens of others, offering her everything, offering her belonging, offering her a family that would never, ever leave.

She kept walking toward Wren.

The seam of gold light opened wider. The other ten children were stepping through it now, one by one, and each of them was crying, and each of them was *aging* as they cried — not dying, not fading, but moving, at last, at terrible natural speed, through the childhood that had been stolen and frozen for them. Wren reached the edge of the seam and paused. Turned. Looked at Sebastian with red-rimmed eyes that held no smile at all, and no fear either — only a question.

"Will you remember us?" Wren asked. "Really remember? Not the way they did."

Sebastian held up the ledger. Its pages were warm against her palms, warmer than they had ever been, warm as a living thing.

"Every name," she said. "Every day. In ink."

Wren nodded. And then — the last thing, the thing Sebastian would carry for the rest of her life — the girl did not smile goodbye. She simply looked at her teacher a moment longer, the way students look at teachers when the bell has rung and neither of them wants to say the class is over.

Then she stepped through, and the light took her, and the seam began to close.

Except—

At the very edge of the closing light, the darkness surged forward one final time. Sebastian felt it move past her like a cold tide, felt it reach — not for the children, not for the crowd, but for the space between the last chair and the wall, where the eleventh figure had been lingering, where something still remained unfed and furious and patient beyond all reason.

The light snapped shut.

The hall went utterly dark. Utterly silent. Two hundred people held two hundred breaths.

And in the blackness, close enough that Sebastian felt its breath stir the hair at her temple, something that had lost everything except its hunger whispered — in her mother's voice, gentle as bedtime:

*"You forgot one."*

* * *

The emergency lights stuttered on — dim, red, theatrical. The hall reassembled itself around Sebastian in pieces: the stunned crowd, the wet faces, the shattered mirrors, the eleven empty chairs standing innocent and ordinary under the red glow, as if they had never held anything at all.

Voss reached her first. His hands found her shoulders. "Sebastian. Sebastian — are you hurt? What happened? What did it say?"

She couldn't answer. Her ears were ringing. Her ledger was clutched so hard against her chest that the cover had bent, and when she looked down at it, her blood went cold.

The pages were fluttering. All of them. Though every window in the hall was closed.

And on the final page — the page she had reserved, all these months, for the ending she hadn't dared to imagine — fresh handwriting was appearing, line by line, in ink that gleamed wet and black under the emergency lights. Not her handwriting. Not her mother's.

Eleven names, crossed out.

And beneath them, in letters that curled like smoke:

*One seat remains. One smile will do. You know where the mirror is.*

Sebastian raised her head slowly and looked toward the back wall, where the largest of the founder's mirrors had hung for a century.

It had not shattered after all.

It hung there in the red light, whole and patient, its surface rippling like the skin of a pond — and in its depths, half a second behind everything, a single empty chair waited, facing outward.

Facing her.
