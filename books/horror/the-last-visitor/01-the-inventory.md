## 1. The Inventory

The taxi would not come all the way up the drive.

Elodie Fairweather stood at the iron gate with their satchel of instruments — the laser measure, the clipboard, the little brass calipers their mentor had pressed into their hands a decade ago with the words *numbers don't lie, but houses do* — and looked down the long gravel throat of the drive toward Fairweather House. October had stripped the elms to their knuckles. The light came low and amber through them, and the house stood at the end of it like something patient.

They had not been here since they were nine years old. They remembered almost nothing: a porch that smelled of camphor, a grandmother whose hands were cold as tap water, a sensation of being measured by every room they entered and found wanting. The family had stopped inviting them after that. Or perhaps the family had never really started. Elodie had grown up in the gaps between Fairweather gatherings, seeing photographs of cousins at holidays they were never told about, hearing secondhand of weddings and christenings as though news of weather in another country. They had learned early to make themselves small enough to be overlooked, and then had learned, later and more painfully, that no amount of smallness was required. The Fairweathers simply did not look.

And now the house was theirs. All of it. Every room they'd never seen.

The gate swung open before they touched it.

Elodie stood very still for a moment, telling themselves about wind, about old hinges, about the physics of wrought iron. Then they walked up the drive with their boots crunching deliberately loud, because silence in an empty place is a kind of invitation, and they did not intend to accept anything today except measurements.

The front door was unlocked. It opened onto warmth.

That was the first wrong thing, and it was so domestic, so gentle, that Elodie nearly forgave it. Heat rolled out to meet them, woodsmoke and beeswax and something faintly sweet underneath, like apples going soft in a bowl. The hall stretched ahead, papered in faded rose damask, lit by afternoon sun through tall windows. Dust motes drifted in the light — ordinary, honest dust motes — but there was no dust anywhere else. The floorboards gleamed. A coat hung on the hook by the door, wool and moth-eaten, and when Elodie touched its sleeve it was dry, weightless, a coat that had been brushed and aired for no one.

Grandmother Maren had died in March. Seven months ago. Elodie had learned of it from a solicitor's letter, which was fitting; the Fairweathers communicated through paperwork the way other families communicated through touch. Seven months of autumn rain and wind and birds through broken panes, and yet the house sat here warm as a held breath, waiting.

*Houses hold heat,* Elodie told themselves, writing it on the clipboard anyway because writing things down makes them smaller. *Old houses especially. Stone holds heat like a grudge.*

They began in the hall, because you begin where you enter. They photographed the staircase, the newel post worn pale by generations of palms, the portrait above the landing — Maren herself at forty, painted with the particular severity of women who have outlived their appetites. Elodie worked the way they always worked: methodically, aloud, narrating to the empty rooms because the sound of their own voice kept the other sounds quiet.

"Ground floor, east wing. Drawing room, twelve feet by eighteen."

The drawing room was perfect. That was the word that kept arriving and refusing to leave. Not clean — perfect. The fire in the grate had burned down to a soft orange bed of coals, as though someone had stepped out to fetch more wood and would return any moment. Two teacups sat on the sideboard. Elodie touched one. Warm.

They set it down carefully, the way one sets down a sleeping animal, and went back to the hall, and told themselves the chimney drew warm air strangely in old houses, and did not believe a word of it.

* * *

By late afternoon they had measured eleven rooms and found the blueprints disagreeing with the house in only one place, which should have been reassuring and was instead the worst thing yet.

The west corridor ran thirty-two feet on the architect's plan, ending in a wall. In the house, it ran forty-one feet, ending in a door.

Elodie stood before the door for a long time. It was oak, unremarkable, fitted into the plasterwork as naturally as if it had always been there — and that was precisely what disturbed them. There was no seam, no scar, no evidence of renovation. The wallpaper flowed across the frame uninterrupted, roses blooming straight over the jamb as though the paper had grown there, around the door, in deference to it. Houses are honest in their alterations. Walls remember being cut. This wall remembered nothing.

They tried the handle. Locked. They pressed their ear to the wood and heard — or thought they heard, or chose to think they heard — nothing at all, which was somehow worse than breathing, worse than movement. A room that made no sound. A room that kept itself secret even from the house's other secrets.

On the blueprint, in the margin, in pencil gone silver with age, someone had written: *not to be counted.*

The handwriting was Maren's. Elodie recognized it from the birthday card she had sent once, when Elodie turned sixteen, the only card in twenty-nine years: stiff loops, letters leaning away from each other like strangers at a bus stop.

*Not to be counted.* As if rooms were sheep. As if the house might otherwise tally them.

Elodie wrote *door, west corridor, unlisted* on the inventory sheet, and their pen hesitated over the column marked *condition,* and finally they wrote: *waiting.*

They didn't know why they wrote that. They crossed it out. The ink stayed.

* * *

The photographs were in the long gallery off the library, and there were more of them than Elodie expected, decades of Fairweathers arranged in gilt frames like specimens. Here was the family at Christmas 1987, all sharp chins and matching sweaters. Here a wedding on the lawn, a christening, a Fourth of July picnic with a croquet mallet frozen mid-swing. Elodie moved along the wall cataloguing faces, most of them strangers, a few half-remembered: Aunt Vesper, Uncle Theo, cousin Marisol as a round-faced child glaring at the camera with magnificent contempt.

It took them three passes down the gallery to see it.

In the oldest photograph — 1961, the whole clan posed on these very steps — there was a chair beside the family. A plain wooden chair, standing slightly apart, angled toward the group. No one sat in it. No one looked at it. It was simply there, the way a chair is there, unremarkable, until you realize it has no business being there at all.

Elodie checked the next photograph. Christmas 1987. Chair. The wedding, 1994. Chair, nearer now, drawn up closer to the bride's family, close enough that its armrest overlapped the flower bed. The christening. The picnic. Chair, chair, chair, and in each image it had moved a little closer, a guest edging incrementally toward inclusion, and in none of them did anyone glance at it, and that was the detail that turned Elodie's mouth dry. Because families notice empty chairs. Families grieve them, argue over them, feel their absence at the table. But these Fairweathers looked past it with the smooth, practiced blindness of people who have agreed not to see something —

— the way they had agreed not to see Elodie, all those years. The way a family can make a person into an empty chair without ever saying a word.

Their skin prickled. The gallery was warm, the light honeyed, the coals of some far-off fire still breathing somewhere in the walls, and Elodie felt suddenly, unmistakably *watched* — not by anything hostile, which they could have borne, but by something attentive. Something taking notes. Something that had noticed the extra body in its halls today and was adjusting its arrangements accordingly.

They turned. The corridor was empty. The rose damask glowed. Somewhere below, a clock ticked with the patience of geology.

At the end of the gallery hung the newest photograph, dated last spring — a final gathering, taken weeks before Maren died. Eleven Fairweathers on the steps, squinting into the sun. And beside them, close now, close enough that its wooden arm nearly touched Maren's sleeve, stood the chair.

But this time, the chair was not empty.

Something sat in it. Not a figure, exactly — a suggestion of one, a softness in the air shaped like shoulders, like a bowed head, rendered in the photograph as a faint blur, the way moving things blur. The way a person blurs when they are almost, but not quite, gone.

Elodie fumbled out their phone and photographed the picture, and when they checked the screen, their own reflection stared back at them from the glass of the gallery wall behind the camera's view — and beside their reflection, unmistakable, patient, angled toward them —

was the chair.

Outside, the first breath of true cold came down off the hills, and against the kitchen window, thin as spider silk, the frost began to write.
