## 4. Edith's Arithmetic

Edith Marlowe had always been the kind of woman who could make an apology feel like an accusation and a compliment feel like a diagnosis. Nineteen years away from her, and my first full sentence in her house was still somehow a confession.

"You look terrible," she said, from the wingback chair by the window, "which means you've been eating like a resident instead of a doctor."

"Nurse," I said. "And you look—"

"Dying. Yes. We've established that. Sit down before I have to watch you hover any longer; it's making me tired, and I'm rationing my energy for things that matter." She gestured at the chair opposite hers with a hand that was all knuckle and papery skin, an IV line taped along the forearm beneath it. "You've gotten tall. That's unfortunate. I liked being able to look down at you."

I sat. The chair knew my weight the way old chairs do, with a sigh of recognition, and something in my chest did the same thing. Nineteen years. I'd told myself on the drive up that this would be professional — a nurse taking a case, a favor owed to the woman who'd taken him in. But sitting there in the front room of the Marlowe house, with its smell of cedar and camphor and the particular dust of a place that has stopped pretending anyone will clean it properly, professionalism lasted about four seconds.

"You signed yourself into hospice," I said. "You could have gone to St. Brigid's. Anywhere. You asked for me by name."

"I asked for competence," she said. "Your name was attached to it. Regrettably."

But her eyes — gray going milky at the rims, the irises still sharp as awls — held mine a moment too long, and I understood that we were both performing, and that she was better at it than I was. She had been performing since 1974, though I didn't know that yet. All I knew then was that Edith Marlowe, who had raised me on porridge and discipline and an unshakable conviction that feelings were private property, was dying, and had summoned me across three hundred miles to witness it, and would rather chew glass than say why.

"So," I said, because someone had to break first and it wasn't going to be her. "Let's talk about your care plan."

"Let's not."

"Edith."

"Ivo." She matched my tone exactly, which was a skill of hers, mirroring people back at themselves until they heard how they sounded. Then she relented, the way she always relented — one inch, grudgingly, as if conceding territory in a war only she was fighting. "Fine. We'll do your little plan. But first you'll tell me what number you're on."

I blinked. "What?"

"The counting. You know what I mean. Don't give me that face; I've known about the counting since before you were born. Every dying person in this town does it. My mother counted down from sixty-one for eleven days. Lark—" She stopped. Something crossed her face like a bird's shadow over a window, quick and gone. "Never mind Lark. What's yours? What number did they give you?"

I thought of Mr. Aldous, three weeks ago, reaching zero mid-syllable with his hand outstretched toward something I hadn't seen. I thought of the chart I kept in my head, the numbers no two patients shared, the way they never explained them and never needed to. I had built a whole career on not thinking about those things.

"Three hundred and ninety-one," I said, and then heard myself, and felt the floor tilt slightly under the chair. Because I hadn't meant to answer. Because I hadn't known I had an answer until it came out of my mouth, smooth and certain, the way you recite your own birthday.

Edith nodded slowly, unsurprised, and that frightened me more than the number had.

"Four hundred," she said. "I started nine days ago. Nine down already. I intend to be insufferable about every single one of them."

* * *

The care plan, such as it was, took shape over the following days, and it was shaped entirely around her arithmetic. Edith treated her count the way other patients treated pain — something to be managed, reported, worked with rather than against. She woke at six, took her medications without complaint (a first, in my experience; the dying hoard their grievances like coins), and spent her mornings in the garden chair on the porch with a blanket over her knees, watching the street through eyes that catalogued everything and forgave nothing.

At night she counted. Not aloud, mostly. I could see it happening behind her face when I checked her vitals — the small muscular movement of her lips, the pause at certain numbers. Three-eighty made her frown. Three-seventy-six made her laugh, once, a short bark of a sound, and refuse to explain it. When I asked what happened at zero, she looked at me with an expression I couldn't read and said, "Arrival," and changed the subject to whether I was eating enough vegetables, which in Edith's language was an expression of profound love.

I should note here that I am, by training and temperament, a rationalist. Hospice work will either make you one or break you, and I had chosen the former with the enthusiasm of a man nailing shut a door he doesn't want to look at. Terminal lucidity. Hypoxia. The brain tidying itself before the lights go out, sorting drawers, saying strange things. I had explanations for everything, filed and cross-referenced, and I reached for them now the way a man reaches for a banister on a dark staircase.

The problem was Wren.

Wren sat on the porch railing most mornings, feathered and patient, his head tilting at angles that a neck shouldn't permit, watching Edith count with the attentive expression of a man listening to a clock. He had not spoken to me since *you're late*, and I had not spoken to him at all, because speaking to him would require deciding what he was, and I was not prepared to hold that meeting.

Edith, apparently, had other plans.

On my fourth morning, she waited until I'd finished her blood pressure, then said, conversationally, "He's still there, isn't he. On the rail."

My stethoscope went cold against my own suddenly damp palm. "I don't know what you—"

"Don't insult us both, boy. I may be ninety-one and leaking, but I put that thing on this porch myself, forty years ago, and I know exactly what sits on my railing." She fixed me with the look she'd used on me at age eleven, when I'd lied about breaking the greenhouse pane and she'd let me dig my own hole for a full minute before lowering me into it. "Two rules. You'll follow them or you'll pack your bag, and I'll die alone out of spite, which I am fully capable of doing."

"Rules," I managed.

"One." She raised a finger, trembling slightly, steady at its purpose. "Never answer him aloud. Not a word, not a nod you mean, not so much as a hum along with whatever he's doing. He asks you things — he will ask you things, that's what he's *for* — and you keep your mouth shut and your mind your own. Answering is agreeing. Agreeing is signing. Do you understand me?"

"That's not — Edith, he's not real. He was—"

"He was *assigned*," she snapped, and the word cracked through the room like ice settling on a pond, and for one disorienting second I saw that she wasn't frightened of Wren at all. She was frightened *for* me, with the specific exhausted terror of a woman who has watched this exact mistake get made before. "Whether he's real is a question for philosophers. Whether he's *patient* is a question with a known answer. Rule two: there is a ledger in the cellar. A book, green cover, brass corners, behind the false panel where the preserves used to be. You will not read it. You will not touch it. You will not go into that cellar at all, in fact, if you can help it — the stairs are bad and the air is worse. Promise me."

"Edith, if there's a document down there, legally—"

"*Promise me.*"

And here is the thing about love, which nobody tells you when you're young: it doesn't make you brave. It makes you *compliant*. It hands you a leash and calls it devotion. I looked at this woman who had fed me and scolded me and driven me to the bus depot at seventeen with fifty dollars sewn into my jacket lining and not one word of sentiment, who was now counting down from four hundred with her spine straight as a fence post, and I promised her. Both rules. Out loud, even, because she demanded it aloud, and I understood dimly that words were currency here, that in this house things said were things *owed*.

She relaxed by perhaps five percent, which for Edith was effusive gratitude.

"Good," she said. "Now make me tea. And don't stand near the window while it steeps. He likes to watch people think."

* * *

That night I lay in my childhood bed — narrower than I remembered, the ceiling closer, the water stain above the window still shaped like a running dog — and listened to Hollowmere.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last furniture I had left. At first it was nothing. Crickets. The creak of the house cooling. Somewhere down the hill, a dog with opinions. Ordinary night sounds, the kind I'd slept through for nineteen years in cities that never fully went quiet.

Then, around what I judged to be two in the morning, I noticed that the crickets had organized.

Not stopped — organized. Their chirping had found a common interval, a shared pulse, and underneath it there was something else, something sustained, a tone with no beginning that I could identify. I got up and stood at the window. The town lay below in its grid of streetlights, the church steeple black against the stars, and the sound rose out of it — out of the houses, I realized, out of the ground between the houses — a single low note, held, patient, the way a congregation holds a chord past the point where the organist has stopped.

It was not unpleasant. That was the worst part. It was warm. It was the sound of being expected.

I stood at that window for I don't know how long, telling myself furnace, telling myself wind through wires, telling myself the whole rational litany, and the note held steady beneath every explanation like a floor beneath a rug. And then, without deciding to, I found I had opened my mouth, and that some part of my throat wanted — *wanted*, with a pull like gravity — to match it. To hum along. To add my small voice to the town's one long syllable.

From the hallway, faint but perfectly clear, came Edith's voice, dry as paper:

"Don't you dare."

I closed my mouth. The wanting drained out of me like water from a pulled plug, leaving me shaking and absurd and wide awake. Down the hall, her door stayed shut. She hadn't gotten up. She'd simply heard me — heard the intention in me, the way she'd heard everything about me for forty years — and spoken through the wall the way she spoke through everything: directly, and just in time.

I got back into bed. The note went on below the window, unhurried, unoffended. It could wait. It had, I understood without knowing how I understood, been waiting a very long time, and it was very good at it.

Somewhere on the porch railing, feathers shifting in the dark, something settled more comfortably.

Three hundred and eighty-four, I thought, before I could stop myself, and did not know whose number it was.
