## 14. The Papers Unsigned

Edith Marlowe had spent ninety-one years refusing to do things on other people's schedules, and dying was not going to be the exception.

I could chart her decline the way I'd charted a hundred patients before her — the appetite going first, then the walks to the window shortening from twenty minutes to ten to a kind of ceremonial leaning against the sill, as if the glass itself were a friend she was saying goodbye to in installments. Her hands, which had kneaded bread and pulled weeds and once, according to town legend, slapped a county sheriff for impertinence, now trembled so badly she needed two tries to lift her teacup. She hated the trembling more than the dying. The dying, she said, was just arithmetic. The trembling was indignity.

She was still counting. Down from 400, steady as a metronome with a grudge. When I took her vitals in the mornings she would murmur the numbers under her breath — *three hundred eighty-two, three hundred eighty-one* — and sometimes, when she caught me listening, she'd give me a look that said *yes, I know what it means, and no, you don't get to ask yet.*

The lawyer came on a Thursday.

His name was Prosser, and he had the specific pallor of a man who billed by the hour and resented every minute he couldn't. He arrived in a car too clean for Hollowmere's gravel roads, carrying a leather portfolio that he set on Edith's kitchen table with the reverence of a priest laying out relics.

"Mrs. Marlowe," he said, "the estate documents are ready for your signature whenever you feel up to it."

"I'll never feel up to it," Edith said pleasantly. "That's rather the point of dying."

Prosser smiled the smile of a man who had heard worse and billed for it. "Of course. But your niece in Corvallis has been asking after the timeline, and the probate court does prefer—"

"My niece," Edith said, "has asked after me exactly twice in nineteen years, and both times it was about the timeline." She turned to me. "Ivo, be a dear and get Mr. Prosser some coffee. The good stuff, not the jar."

I got the coffee. It bought us four minutes of silence, during which Prosser arranged his papers into a fan of imminent decisions: the deed, the accounts, the medical directive, and at the bottom, a single page that looked no different from the others except that my skin prickled when I stood near it, the way air prickles before lightning.

I knew what it was now. Wren had told me, in his patient, wrong-jointed way, perched on the porch rail like a heron that had read too much: *The signature isn't ink, Ivo. It never was. Seven names, seven deaths, each one renewing the hymn. Your grandfather tore his line out and the town has been running on fumes ever since — on Edith, mostly, and on how long she can refuse.*

The seventh signature. The last founder. Edith had held Hollowmere together for fifty years by the simple, monstrous act of not signing, of dying slowly enough to keep the pen hovering above the page. And here was Prosser, bless his billable heart, delivering the pen.

"Mr. Kavanagh," Prosser said, catching me staring at the bottom sheet. "You're named in here as executor. You should probably review—"

"He has reviewed," Edith said. "He reviews everything. It's his most annoying quality."

"It's true," I said. "I once read a cereal box twice."

Edith laughed, and the laugh cost her something — I saw it leave her body in a long shudder — but she waved off my hand before I could reach her. That was the thing about Edith. She treated her own frailty like an uninvited guest: acknowledged, seated in the corner, strictly forbidden to speak.

Prosser left the portfolio on the table. "No rush," he said, in the tone of a man who meant *considerable rush*. "But Mrs. Marlowe, I'd be negligent if I didn't note that patients in your condition sometimes lose testamentary capacity, and the court takes a dim view of—"

"You may note," Edith said, "that I have been of sound mind since 1934, which is longer than you've been alive, and I intend to remain insufferably lucid until the job is done." She smiled at him with all her teeth. "Whenever I decide the job is done."

Prosser gathered his coat. At the door he paused beside me and lowered his voice. "Between us? She should sign soon. Whatever she's waiting for — it isn't coming."

"She's waiting for nothing," I said. "She's waiting for the right moment. There's a difference."

He gave me a look I'd learn to recognize later — the look of a man who suspects everyone in a small town knows something he doesn't — and drove away trailing dust.

* * *

That night I sat at the kitchen table with the portfolio open in front of me, because apparently I had become the kind of person who disobeyed explicit deathbed instructions as a hobby. Edith had only ever given me two rules: never answer Wren aloud, and never read the ledger in the cellar. I had broken the second within a week. The first I'd kept, barely, through an act of will that felt like holding a door shut against wind.

The papers were ordinary. That was the horror of them — they were genuinely, boringly ordinary. Property descriptions. Beneficiary designations. A clause about the church roof fund that made me almost smile; Edith had left money to fix the roof of the building where Pastor Grell wanted me run out of town. And there, at the end of the final page, a signature line with her name printed above it, and beneath it, in smaller print, a line I hadn't noticed before:

*Signed, witnessed, and sealed, that the work may continue.*

Not standard probate language. Not anything a lawyer drafts. Someone had slipped that phrase into the document generations ago, and every lawyer since had copied the template forward without reading it, the way we all copy things forward, the way congregations sing hymns without hearing them.

I put my finger on the line and felt it — faintly, unmistakably — a hum. The same note the whole town sang in its sleep. The paper was a door lying flat, and her name was the key, and underneath the floorboards something shifted its weight, attentive, patient, *hungry* in the way that waiting things are hungry.

"Don't," Edith said from the doorway.

I hadn't heard her come down the hall. She stood in her nightgown with one hand braced on the frame, and for a moment she looked less like a dying woman than like a general inspecting a battlefield she'd already memorized.

"I wasn't going to sign it," I said. "It's your name."

"No," she said. "You were going to hide it. I can always tell with you, Ivo. You have your grandfather's face and your mother's stubbornness and your own particular talent for doing the wrong thing for the right reason." She crossed the room slowly, lowering herself into the chair across from me like a ship taking on water in a controlled fashion. "Put it back in the folder."

"If you sign this, the binding breaks. Everything Wren told me, everything in the ledger — the seventh signature releases it. Grell's congregation is already singing it wrong on purpose. If you add your name—"

"I know what my name does." Her voice didn't rise. It did something worse; it went quiet and old, older than the house. "I've known since 1974. I watched six people sign their names and their deaths into that book, and I watched your grandfather stand up in front of all of us and say *no*, and I have spent fifty years deciding which of them was right, and I will tell you what I decided, Ivo: they were all wrong, and so am I, and it doesn't matter, because being wrong doesn't stop the clock."

"Then let me finish it. Lark's verse. The hymn can be completed instead of broken — Wren remembers pieces of it, and the ledger has the rest. If I can reconstruct it before—"

"Before what?" She smiled, tired and terrible. "Before I die? Child, I'm counting down from four hundred. Do you know why I chose such a large number? Because I needed room. Room to hold the door. Every day I don't sign is a day the thing below stays bound, and every day costs me, and I have paid gladly, but I am nearly out of coins." She laid her trembling hand flat on the table, next to the portfolio, not touching it. "And here's what you don't understand yet. If I die unsigned, it doesn't stay shut either. An unbroken chain still rusts through. The founders' bargain fails either way. The only question is whether it fails screaming or fails singing."

"That's not true," I said. "Wren said—"

"Wren says what Lark taught him to say." Something moved behind her eyes — grief, maybe, or its fossil record. "Lark believed there was another way. Lark always believed there was another way. And look where believing got her. Scratched out of a photograph. Erased from every record in this town, including, I'm ashamed to say, mine, because I was a coward in 1974 and I have been paying interest on that cowardice ever since." She pushed herself up, hand over hand along the table's edge. "Put the papers back, Ivo. Please. Let an old woman keep her own counsel a few days longer."

I put the papers back. It felt like loading a gun and handing it to someone I loved.

* * *

Prosser returned Monday, and this time he brought a witness.

Her name was Ms. Okafor, a notary from the county seat, brisk and competent, with a rolling case of stamps and seals. They arrived together at ten in the morning, and I understood immediately that this was a coordinated operation — that somewhere between Thursday and Monday, Edith's niece in Corvallis had made a phone call, and the machinery of probate had begun to turn, indifferent as weather.

Edith received them in the parlor, propped in her good chair, dressed as though for church. She had put on lipstick. I recognized the gesture for what it was: armor, ceremony, defiance. She intended to look like herself when she did whatever she was going to do.

"Mrs. Marlowe," Prosser began, "we've prepared everything for today. If you're feeling well enough—"

"I'm feeling exactly well enough," Edith said. "Which is to say poorly, but lucidly. Bring your papers."

My chest went tight. Nineteen years of loving this woman funneled into a single point of pressure behind my sternum. I thought of the ledger in the cellar, seven names and a torn line. I thought of the shape I'd seen through the floorboards, vast and listening. I thought of Wren on the porch rail, saying *a volunteer cannot substitute for an heir* — no wait, that was later, that was the bargain, that was the fine print I hadn't read yet. What I thought then was simpler: *she's going to sign it right now, in front of witnesses, and there is nothing I can do that wouldn't be a betrayal of her.*

Unless betrayal was the point.

"Actually," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved, "there's a problem with the documents."

Prosser turned. Ms. Okafor turned. Edith did not turn, but I felt her attention swing onto me like a searchlight.

"A problem," Prosser repeated.

"The property description on page three." I picked up the deed and held it with the confidence of a man who had, forty minutes earlier, Googled *how to challenge a property description* on a hospice Wi-Fi connection. "The parcel boundaries reference the 1968 survey, but there was a boundary adjustment in 1981 — the well easement, when the county rerouted the water line. If she signs with the outdated description, the whole conveyance could be challenged. Her niece could spend years in litigation."

Prosser frowned and took the deed. He was, to his credit, actually reading it, which meant he hadn't read it closely before, which meant the template had indeed been copied forward by lawyers who skimmed, generation after generation, like a congregation mumbling a hymn. "This is... possibly an issue," he admitted.

"It's definitely an issue," I said. "You'd want a corrected survey before execution. Otherwise you're inviting a contest."

Ms. Okafor said, "A corrected survey could take weeks."

"Yes," I said. "It could."

Silence. Then Edith laughed — a real laugh, sudden and barking, that dissolved into coughing, and while she coughed I knelt beside her chair and held her hand and she gripped it hard enough to hurt, and in that grip was a message I chose to read as *you magnificent little liar* and chose not to examine further.

"Well," Prosser said, repacking his portfolio with the resigned efficiency of a man whose afternoon had just evaporated. "We'll order the survey. Mrs. Marlowe, I apologize for the delay. These things happen."

"They do," Edith said, wiping her eyes. "They happen constantly. It's practically their defining feature."

At the door, Prosser shook my hand. His grip lingered half a second past professional. "Good catch," he said. "Most executors wouldn't have noticed."

"Most executors," I said, "aren't paying attention."

He paused. For a moment I thought he was going to say something else — something about the phrase at the bottom of the final page, maybe, something about how many times he'd copied that template without reading it — but then Ms. Okafor called his name from the car, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

* * *

That evening I found Edith on the porch, wrapped in the quilt her mother had sewn, watching the light go amber over the hills. Wren was nowhere visible, which meant he was everywhere visible, the way he'd been all week — a feather on the windowsill, a shadow with too many joints at the edge of the yard, a presence like a held breath.

"You lied to a lawyer," Edith said, "in my parlor, with a notary present. To buy me time."

"I did."

"That was either very loyal or very presumptuous."

"Knowing you, I assumed it was both."

She patted the bench beside her. I sat. For a while neither of us spoke, and the town hummed its single note below us, and above us the first star came out like a pinhole in dark cloth.

"How long did you buy?" she asked finally.

"A few weeks. Maybe less, if Prosser finds a fast surveyor."

"And what happens in those weeks?"

"I finish the verse," I said. "All of it. Lark's verse. And then, when your count reaches zero, Hollowmere sings something new instead of something old, and nobody has to sign anything, ever again."

Edith was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had gone soft in a way I'd only heard once before, the night she first told me about my parents.

"Your grandfather said almost exactly that," she said. "Standing in the church, in front of all of us, with the ledger open on the altar. He said, *there's another way, I can hear it.* And then he walked out, and three days later he drowned in a pond two feet deep, and I have spent fifty years wondering whether he found the other way or whether the other way found him." She turned her head and looked at me, and her eyes were clear and frightened and fierce. "Don't drown, Ivo. Whatever you find down there — whatever you think you hear — come back up."

"I'll come back up," I said.

Because I believed it. That was the terrible part. I believed it completely, right up until the morning the lights failed across Hollowmere and I looked down through the floorboards and understood, finally and fully, what *come back up* was going to cost.

But that was the next day. That night there was only the porch, and the quilt, and Edith's hand in mine, and beneath us the slow patient hum of a town holding its breath around a woman who refused, on principle, to die on anyone's schedule but her own.

Inside, on the kitchen table, the portfolio waited with its pages in order and its single false line of scripture, and upstairs in the spare room I could feel Wren awake, watching, counting alongside Edith in a voice I wasn't allowed to answer.

Three hundred seventy-four, said the house, in the voice of everything sleeping.

Three hundred seventy-three.
