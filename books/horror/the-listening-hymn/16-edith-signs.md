## 16. Edith Signs

I have outlived every person who ever told me the truth, which is either a testament to my constitution or an indictment of my company. Probably both. Lark would have said something kinder and meant it less.

Ivo sleeps in the blue room now, the one that used to be his, back when he was small enough to believe a feathered thing on the porch was a game and not a posting. He sleeps like the dying do — all at once, completely, as though sleep were a country he'd been granted emergency visa to. I stood in his doorway for a long while tonight just watching him breathe. Nineteen years I waited for him to come home, and now that he's here, I find I mostly want him asleep. There's a joke in that. Lark would have found it. I never learned how to be funny about the things that matter; I only learned how to be stubborn about them, which is funnier, if you're the sort who laughs at funerals.

The papers are on the kitchen table where Mr. Voss left them, squared to the table's edge because Voss is the kind of man who squares things when he's frightened and doesn't know it. Estate documents, he calls them. Power of attorney, medical directive, the disposition of the Marlowe holdings. Fifty years of lawyers' language wrapped around one line of ink at the bottom of the last page, and only three people in Hollowmere know what that line really is. Two of them are dead. One of them is me, and I am working on it.

Ivo thinks he's beaten me. The dear boy forged a delay — a phone call to Voss about some discrepancy in the parcel descriptions, a request for "clarified survey language," stalling tactics dressed up as diligence. He thinks I didn't notice. He thinks a woman who has spent half a century holding a town together with her signature and her pulse wouldn't recognize a stall when she saw one. I raised him better than that, or worse, depending on how you look at it, and I have looked at it both ways tonight with a glass of the good sherry I've been saving for an occasion, on the theory that this is one.

Here is what Ivo doesn't understand, because he is young and because Wren has filled his head with poetry: the old bargain works. It has worked for fifty years. Ugly, yes. Cruel, yes, in the way that a tourniquet is cruel. But nobody in Hollowmere has gone into the ground wrong since 1974. Nobody's grandmother has come back wearing their voice. Nobody has heard their dead husband scratching at the storm cellar door asking to be let in, asking, asking, in a voice like wet gravel, until they opened it, because love makes you stupid, and the thing below knows exactly how stupid love makes you.

I know. I opened it. That's a story for another glass of sherry, or no story at all, which is what I've made it for fifty years.

* * *

Let me tell you what pride is, since everyone gets it wrong. Pride isn't thinking well of yourself. Pride is refusing to be spared. My sister had pride too, but hers ran the other direction — she wanted to be spared so badly she wrote a whole verse about it, and look where that got her: underneath everything, listening forever, the eighth founder, the one we don't name except when we're counting, which is always.

Lark wrote the freeing verse. I've known that since we were girls sharing a bed in this house, her feet cold against my calves, her lips moving in her sleep, composing. She hummed it once, whole, at the kitchen table, and the sherry in our mother's glass went still as a photograph, and our father took the hymnal off the shelf and burned it in the yard and said, *never again*, and Lark said, "But it's finished," and he said, "That's what frightens me."

She was right and he was right and both of them are dead and the verse is still true, sitting in the ledger like a splinter, waiting for someone brave enough or foolish enough to sing it. Ivo wants to be that someone. I can see it in him — the same set to the jaw his grandfather had, the same terrible Kavanagh certainty that being right is the same as being safe.

It isn't. I have the paperwork to prove it.

Because here is the arithmetic Ivo hasn't done, won't do, can't do: if the binding breaks and the dead go free, fine, beautiful, hallelujah. But the release requires a listener, and the listeners don't get to retire. His grandfather refused to sign in '74 and the town paid for it in ways I will not write down even here, even drunk, even alone. And if Ivo sings Lark's verse and takes the listening himself — which is what Wren's bargain amounts to, whatever soft feathers Wren wraps it in — then my boy becomes what Lark became. Present. Faithful. No longer entirely human. Forever, or whatever word we use down there for forever.

He'll do it, too. That's the worst part. He'll do it out of loyalty to me, and I will not be the reason a twenty-eight-year-old nurse spends eternity under his hometown. I buried his parents. I signed his school forms. I taught him to drive in the church parking lot and lied to him about his grandfather with a straight face for nineteen years. I have earned the right to make this decision without his permission, and I intend to exercise it the way I've exercised everything else in my life: stubbornly, alone, and slightly ahead of schedule.

The old bargain needs a seventh signer. The chain broke in '74 and has been held together since by nothing but me dying slowly enough to be inconvenient about it. Every year I don't sign, the fraying gets worse — Aldous counting down to zero in a hospice wing two towns over, the lights failing, the hum getting ideas above its station. I have held this town together with a pen I refused to pick up. There's a joke there too. Everything important in my life has been a joke I only understood afterward.

So. Tonight.

* * *

The kitchen at three in the morning is the honest room in any house. Daylight kitchens perform; midnight kitchens confess. I sat down at my own table with the sherry and the papers and the fountain pen — the Marlowe pen, the one seven hands have held before mine, though the records say six, because we erased Lark so thoroughly that even the erasure got erased — and I read the last page the way you read a letter from someone you loved who has died. Slowly. Twice. Looking up between lines to give yourself time to lie to yourself.

*In witness whereof, the undersigned...*

Fifty years I kept those words unsigned. Fifty years of headaches and lawyer visits and the slow, deliberate business of dying at the pace of continental drift, purely out of spite toward a piece of paper. Pastor Grell thinks I'm a saint. Voss thinks I'm sentimental. Ivo thinks I'm afraid. None of them guessed the truth, which is that I am the most selfish woman in Hollowmere, because I wanted more time. Not for myself. For the town. For the arrangement. Because the arrangement, whatever it cost, kept the dark where the dark belongs, and I am too old to gamble with the dark on the strength of a dead girl's unfinished song.

It isn't unfinished anymore, says the part of me that sounds like Ivo. Wren remembers it. The ledger holds it. He could sing it tomorrow.

And if he falters? If he sings it wrong, one note off, the way Grell's congregation sang it deliberately wrong last week outside his door — did he tell you about that, Edith, did he mention the mob at midnight, the hymn bent out of true like a wheel about to come off? If he falters, the thing below doesn't get freed gently. It gets freed hungry. And the first mouth in its path will be mine, and the second will be his, and the third will be every soul in Hollowmere who ever hummed along without knowing why.

No. I did not survive 1974 to die of optimism.

I uncapped the pen.

The house knew. I'll say that for it — the house has always known things before I do, floorboards ticking like a clock winding backward, the cellar door breathing cold air up through its seams. Somewhere beneath my kitchen, beneath the foundation my great-grandfather poured, something vast turned its attention upward like a dog hearing its name, and the hum — the hum I've lived with so long I'd mistaken it for silence — changed.

Not louder. Deeper. A second note slid in under the first, the way a hand slides under a falling body, and the two notes leaned against each other and held. A chord. The first chord Hollowmere has heard in fifty years, rising up through the soles of my feet, through the chair, through the sherry in my glass, which trembled in rings like a struck bell.

"Oh, hush," I told it. "You'll wake the boy."

It didn't hush. Nothing down there has ever hushed when told; that's rather the whole problem. But it quieted to something like attention, the way a congregation quiets when the organist finally sits down on the bench, and I understood that it had been waiting for this sound — my decision, translated into pressure and ink — for half a century. It wasn't menacing. That was the horror of it. It sounded grateful.

I put the pen to the paper at the line marked with the small penciled x that Voss uses, the x that means *here*, the x that means *you*, and my hand did not shake, which disappointed me somehow. I'd earned a shaking hand. Fifty years of refusal should buy a woman at least a tremor.

*Edith Marlowe.*

My name went down wet and black and final. The letters looked like strangers. Then, quickly, before I could think better of it — and thinking better of it has never once been my gift — I signed the date, and the date was today, and today was the day, and it was done.

The chord beneath the house swelled and resolved, three notes now, four, stacking themselves like voices finding each other in the dark of a church, and I sat very still in my kitchen at three in the morning and listened to the town's oldest sin harmonize, and I thought: *Lark, you difficult girl, I hope you can't hear this.*

But of course she can hear it. She hears everything. That's the whole of her punishment and the whole of her job.

* * *

I gave myself until the sherry was gone to feel it. Grief, relief, terror — the usual trio at my age; they travel together like old women at market. What surprised me was the fourth thing, the one I hadn't budgeted for: tenderness. Sitting there with an empty glass and a signed page, I felt tender toward the whole ridiculous town — toward Grell and his wrong-keyed congregation, toward Voss squaring his papers, toward Aldous reaching zero in his rented bed, toward the boy asleep upstairs who is going to hate me, properly hate me, maybe for a year, maybe forever, and who will be alive to do it, which is the entire point.

That's the cruelty of it, if you're keeping score, and I know you are. The kindest thing I ever did for anyone was the cruelest thing I ever did to him. I chose the town over the truth. I chose the tourniquet over the surgery. I chose the devil whose terms I knew over the angel whose terms I didn't, and I did it in the middle of the night, alone, with a fountain pen, like all the great betrayals of history.

I climbed the stairs slower than I have in years. Not weakness. Ceremony. At the blue room door I looked in at Ivo — sprawled, young, one arm hanging off the mattress the way he slept at nine — and I whispered, because Wren has taught me caution about speaking aloud in this house:

"You'll forgive me or you won't. Either way, you'll live. That's more than your grandfather managed, and more than Lark, and more than me, come morning or near enough."

He stirred. Frowned at something in his dream. Counting, probably. They all count at the end, the ones who know, and lately I've caught myself doing it too — backwards from four hundred, the founders' number, one number for each soul the old bargain bought safe. Three hundred and ninety-one tonight. I have time. A little. The signing buys days, not hours, but the chord is rising and the thing below is waking and even a tourniquet only holds if the limb is still attached.

Downstairs, the papers lay on the kitchen table, my name drying black at the bottom of the last page, and the hum rose through the floorboards in a chord so full and patient it sounded almost like forgiveness.

Almost.

I closed the blue room door softly behind me and went to wait for morning, counting.
