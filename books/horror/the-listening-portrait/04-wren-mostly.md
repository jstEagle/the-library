## 4. Wren, Mostly

The light went strange at dusk.

Felix noticed it first in the hallway mirror—the one with the hairline crack running through the silver like a river on an old map—where the reflection of the landing held a second longer than it should have, the way a bell holds its note after you've struck it. They had spent the whole day testing mirrors. Kitchen spoon, bathroom glass, the dark rectangle of the television when it was off. All of them lagged now, all of them a heartbeat behind, and Felix had learned to stop looking directly into them the way you learn to stop touching a bruise: not because it stops hurting, but because you get tired of proving that it does.

So they were watching the window instead when the light changed.

It wasn't sunset. Sunset was gold going to ash. This was the color of the inside of a closed eyelid—a warm, arterial dimness that seeped up from the ground rather than down from the sky, pooling in the garden like something spilled. The birch tree stood in it without casting a shadow. And standing beneath the birch, where no one had been standing a moment before, was Wren.

Felix's body reacted before their mind could intervene. The teacup—they'd been holding a teacup all day for something to do with their hands—slipped and shattered against the floorboards, and Felix was moving, down the stairs two at a time, wrenching open the front door with a cry half-swallowed in their throat.

"Wren—"

The figure turned.

It was Wren. That was the unbearable thing. It was Wren exactly as Wren had been at twenty-six, before the years and the distance and the slow estrangement had worn them down to phone calls twice a year and then to nothing at all. The same narrow shoulders. The same cowlick at the crown that never lay flat no matter how Wren fought it. The same way of tilting the head slightly left when listening, as if sound arrived better from that angle.

But the dusk-light didn't touch them right. It went *through* them, or around them, the way water goes around a stone, and where their shadow should have stretched long across the grass there was only more of that eyelid-dark, seamless.

"Felix." The voice was Wren's voice, pitched low and warm, with the little rasp at the back of it that came from too many years of whispering jokes during sermons. "You dropped your cup."

"You're dead," Felix said. Their own voice sounded very small against all that darkening air. "I buried you yesterday. I watched them—I watched the box go into the ground."

"I know," said the thing wearing their twin, and it smiled, and the smile was Wren's smile, crooked on the right side first. "That was awkward, wasn't it. Standing there while they put me down. You kept checking your phone."

Felix's breath caught. Because that was true. That was a true, private, shameful thing—standing at the graveside, numb, glancing at a screen because grief was too large to look at unassisted and the phone was a comfortable peephole. No one had seen that. No one living.

"How do you know that?"

"I was there." Wren stepped closer, and the darkness moved with them like a hem. "I'm always here, Lix. I've always been here. You just stopped listening."

*Don't call me that.* The protest rose and died. Because Wren had called them that—only Wren, ever—and hearing it now was like pressing a tongue to the socket of a lost tooth. Ache and familiarity in the same instant.

"I don't understand what you are," Felix managed.

"Neither do I, entirely." And there was something almost disarming in the honesty, a shrug so perfectly Wren's—the elbows out, the palms up—that Felix's chest seized with longing so sharp it was indistinguishable from nausea. "I remember being him. Most of him. Some parts come back when I need them and some parts are just... gone, like rooms in a house I can't find the door to anymore. But I remember you." The head tilted left. Listening. "I remember the summer we were nine. The creek behind the Hendersons' field. You pushed me in."

"You pushed *me* in," Felix said automatically, and then hated themselves for the correction, for the intimacy of it, for playing along.

"I pushed you in first. You pushed me in *back*. There's a difference." The smile again. "You held my hand under the water until I promised to stop dunking you. You held on even after I promised. You held on so long our fingers went pruny together. Do you remember?"

Felix did remember. God help them, they remembered everything—the green smell of the creek, the cold shock of it, Wren's laughter bubbling up through the water in silver strings. And the thing on the lawn knew it, knew it in detail no stranger could guess and no ghost should keep, and Felix understood with a lurching, sick clarity that this was precisely the problem. Whatever stood under the birch tree had been fed on those memories for seven years while Felix stayed away. It had grown fat on the family's discarded past. It knew the shape of Wren the way a well-worn glove knows a hand.

"The sheet music," Felix said. "In Wren's room. The handwriting keeps changing."

Something flickered across the borrowed face—not guilt, nothing so human, but a kind of careful stillness, like a heron deciding whether the fish has seen it.

"You found it," Wren said softly. "Good. I hoped you would. That's actually why I'm here." They took another step, close enough now that Felix could see the eyes properly, and made themselves look, made themselves search for the wrongness, and found it only in the smallest thing: Wren's eyes had always been restless, always darting, alive to every movement. These eyes did not move at all. They were fixed on Felix with the patient attention of something that had waited a very long time and intended to wait longer if necessary. "I can't finish it, Lix. The song. Mother's song. I have most of it—it's in the walls, it's in the floors, I hear it all the time—but the last verse is..." The hands came up, Wren's hands, and pressed against Wren's temples in Wren's old gesture of frustration, and Felix's heart broke a little more. "It's gone. It's like a word on the tip of the tongue. I can feel the shape of it. I just need someone who remembers it *whole*. Someone who heard it the way I did."

"I don't remember it," Felix lied.

The stillness deepened. For a moment the dusk itself seemed to lean in.

"You hummed a bar of it yesterday," Wren said gently. "In my room. Third bar, after the part that goes up. I felt it like sunlight through a window." The tenderness in the voice was the worst part. It was real tenderness, or something so perfectly shaped like tenderness that no instrument Felix owned could tell the difference. "I've been waiting for that my whole death, Lix. One note. That's all it took, and I could finally come to the door."

*Come to the door.* As if the house were theirs. As if they had been outside it all this time, waiting politely on the step.

Felix became aware that they were humming.

They hadn't decided to. The melody had simply risen in them, drawn up by proximity the way water climbs a wick—the third bar, the one that goes up, Wren's favorite part, the part they used to harmonize under while their mother played and pretended not to notice. Three notes. Four. Their mouth was already shaping the fifth—

And the thing under the birch opened its mouth with them.

Not singing. *Receiving.* Its lips parted, its throat worked, and Felix saw—or thought they saw, in the last of that eyelid light—something behind the teeth that was not a tongue but a darkness with depth to it, a corridor of dark, and the notes Felix was humming were falling into it like coins into a well, and somewhere far below, something was drinking.

Felix bit down on the melody. Hard. Clamped their jaw shut so hard their molars sang.

The silence rang.

"Don't stop," Wren whispered, and the tenderness curdled by one degree, the way milk turns—you couldn't say what changed, only that it was no longer safe. "Please. I'm so tired of being mostly. Don't you want me whole?"

Every instinct Felix possessed screamed the same word, and the word was *no*, and yet underneath the scream ran a quieter current, treacherous and warm: *this is the closest you will ever come to having your twin back.* Seven years of silence between them, and now here was Wren's voice asking for help, Wren's hands reaching, Wren's crooked smile waiting to be answered. To refuse was to bury them a second time. To refuse was to choose the grave over the ghost.

"I can't," Felix said. Their voice cracked down the middle. "Whatever you are—I can't."

The thing regarded them for a long moment. Then, horribly, it nodded—with understanding, with patience, with the awful gentleness of a parent watching a child refuse medicine.

"That's alright," it said. "You'll sing eventually. Everyone sings eventually. Mrs. Okonkwo next door is already learning it in her sleep." It began to turn away, the darkness folding around it like a coat. Over its shoulder, in Wren's voice, light as a joke between bunks: "Ask her about 1974 sometime. Ask her why she never says her brother's name."

Then the dusk swallowed it—not fading, not walking off, simply *closing*, the way a book closes—and the ordinary evening rushed back in, blue and cold and honest, and the birch tree cast its shadow again, and there was nothing on the lawn but dew beginning to form.

Felix stood on the doorstep for a long time, shaking.

Inside, they did not turn on any lights. They climbed to Wren's room, took the sheet music from the desk, and sat cross-legged on the dusty floor with it spread before them like a map of a country they'd sworn never to enter again. The changing handwriting crawled across the staves—Mother's neat slant dissolving into a cramped, spidery script, which dissolved into something older, looped, almost medieval—and now, by lamplight, Felix saw what exhaustion had hidden from them before: the final verse wasn't missing at all. It was there, fully written, in every hand at once.

But someone had tried to scratch it out. Repeatedly. The paper was worn thin with erasure, and beneath the newest layer of ink, in their mother's writing, so faint it might have been written in water, was a single line:

*If it asks you to help it remember, it is not remembering. It is borrowing.*

Downstairs, in the walls, very softly, the house began to hum the third bar—the one that goes up.

And two streets away, in a small brick house with lace curtains, Mrs. Okonkwo woke from a dream of her brother, sat upright in bed, and said his name aloud for the first time in fifty years.
