## 10. A Second Burial

The house had gone quiet the way a bell goes quiet—not silent, exactly, but finished. Felix noticed it first in the mornings, waking without the sensation of being listened to, the walls holding their plaster and their secrets alike. Three weeks since the mirrors had shattered mid-reflection, three weeks since the melody had collapsed into six wrong voices singing six different wrong songs, and still Felix sometimes stood in doorways waiting for the hum to start again.

It didn't. The house only breathed now, the ordinary breathing of old wood expanding in the sun, contracting in the dark. Honest sounds. Living sounds.

The mirrors were honest too. Felix had replaced them one by one—cheap glass from the hardware store on Marsh Road, nothing ornate—and every morning they showed exactly what they should: a tired face, a body that had lost weight it couldn't afford to lose, eyes that looked less invisible than they used to. When Felix raised a hand, the reflection raised its hand at the same time. Not a beat behind. Not a heartbeat early. Just glass doing what glass does.

Felix had learned to be grateful for such small fidelity.

* * *

They buried Wren on a Thursday in late October, when the light came slantwise through the elms and turned the cemetery grass the color of old brass.

The first funeral, seven years ago—or rather, three weeks ago, though grief kept its own calendar—had been a performance. Felix remembered standing at the graveside feeling nothing but the wind, watching mourners they barely knew say kind things about a twin they'd abandoned to distance and unanswered calls. Wren had died alone in this house, and Felix had spent seven years not knowing, not visiting, not listening. That was the word that kept surfacing now. *Listening.* Their whole life had been an exercise in not listening—to Mother's warnings, to Wren's letters, to the low persistent song beneath everything.

This burial was different. This was for the real Wren—the one who'd hidden frogs in the piano bench and cried at the endings of books and once, aged nine, told Felix that if they ever got separated in a crowd, Felix should just stand still, because Wren would always find them. Wren had found them, in the end. In the worst possible way. But they'd found them.

Felix dug the grave themselves. Mrs. Okonkwo had offered to arrange it properly, but Felix needed their hands in the dirt, needed the blisters, needed the weight of the shovel the way a penance needs its weight. What remained of Wren fit in a cedar box not much bigger than a bread loaf—the entity had worn the shape of their twin like a coat, and when the melody broke, the coat had fallen empty, leaving behind only what was truly Wren's: bone fragments, a lock of hair still stubbornly red, the silver whistle they'd carried everywhere as children and which Felix had never once seen during those terrible dusk visits.

The whistle went into the box last. Felix held it a long moment before letting go, thumb tracing the dented mouthpiece where Wren had dropped it off the orchard wall in 1994 and sworn never to tell Mother.

"I'm sorry I didn't stand still," Felix said. "I'm sorry I ran so far you had to come find me."

The wind moved through the elms. It was only wind. Felix had gotten very good lately at telling the difference, and very grateful for the practice.

Mrs. Okonkwo stood at the edge of the plot with her hat in her hands, her gray braids catching the light. Dev hovered beside her, seventeen and hollow-eyed and alive, glancing every few seconds at his own shadow as if confirming it still belonged to him. Behind them came Mr. Aldous Reyes, the retired choir director, upright and precise even in mourning, and Silas Marr, the fisherman, who had given his voice to anchor a boy he'd known for four days and who communicated now in a small notebook he kept in his breast pocket. He wrote something and showed it to Felix:

*She would have liked the light today.*

"She would have hated the fuss," Felix said, and Silas's crinkled smile said he agreed.

They lowered the box together. Felix refused help with the shoveling, but the others stayed until the mound was shaped and settled, and then Dev did something none of them expected: he began to sing.

Felix's whole body flinched. Mrs. Okonkwo's hand shot out—but it wasn't the lullaby. It was something else entirely, something with a gospel backbone and a minor-key ache, a song about rivers and crossing over, and Dev sang it rough and off-key and entirely his own, and after a bar Mrs. Okonkwo joined him, and then Reyes, finding harmonies with a conductor's instinct, and Silas mouthing the words silently, giving the rhythm with his hands against his thighs.

Felix stood over their twin's grave and wept—openly, ugly, honestly—for the first time since childhood. Not because of the song. Because of the people singing it. Because Wren had spent their whole life trying to teach Felix that belonging wasn't something you earned by disappearing gracefully; it was something you walked into, muddy-handed and wrong-footed, and let hold you up.

When the song ended, Felix spoke the words they hadn't managed at the first funeral.

"Goodbye, Wren. I mean it this time."

* * *

The house took some getting used to, emptied of its tenant.

Felix had expected to leave. Had assumed, all through those final weeks, that survival meant selling the place to some unsuspecting family or letting it rot into the hillside. But standing in Wren's room the night after the second burial, sorting sheet music that no longer changed handwriting mid-phrase, Felix understood that leaving was just another form of hiding, and hiding was how the thing had gotten in. Invisible people left vacancies. The house needed someone who stayed awake in it.

So Felix stayed. Not as a prisoner—as a keeper.

The journals went back on Mother's shelf, annotated now in Felix's own hand: dates, cross-references, the names of every listener the song had marked across four generations. Reyes had helped reconstruct the melody's full architecture from memory and manuscript, and together they'd compiled what Felix thought of privately as the anti-hymnal—a complete notation of the summons, every verse, every variation, bound in gray cloth and kept in a locked drawer.

Not to sing. Never to sing. But to know.

"You can't avoid a landmine you don't know the location of," Reyes had said, tapping the manuscript with his baton-hand. "Ignorance isn't innocence. Ignorance is just an open door."

Once a month, they gathered. Mrs. Okonkwo brought jollof rice and stories of her brother Samuel, whose name she'd finally spoken aloud after fifty years of silence, and who deserved, she said, to be remembered as a boy who loved kites rather than a cautionary tale. Dev came after school, doing better now that his reflection obeyed him again, though he still slept with the lights on and had asked Felix, seriously, to teach him "which notes never to sing"—so Felix did, walking him through the melody phrase by phrase the way you'd teach a child to identify snakes: this one harmless, this one will kill you, see the difference, see it forever. Silas came when the fishing allowed, writing observations in his careful hand, and it was Silas who noticed, in the third month, that the lullaby's power seemed to live in its intervals—that sung correctly, the gaps between notes pulled like a current, and sung wrongly, the current simply stopped.

"That's why it worked," Felix said, the night they worked it out, sitting around the kitchen table under a bulb that flickered only because the wiring was bad, gloriously, mundanely bad. "Six people singing it six wrong ways. We didn't fight it. We made it impossible."

Silas wrote: *Wrongness is a kind of armor.*

Felix had the sentence framed eventually. It hung in the front hall, where the big mirror used to be.

* * *

Winter came, and the house held.

Felix fixed the porch rail, re-glazed the south windows, planted bulbs along the walk that wouldn't show themselves until spring—acts of faith in a future the house had never been permitted before. Neighbors who had once looked through Felix now nodded, waved, stopped to chat about the weather and the state of the road. Mrs. Okonkwo's granddaughter came by on Saturdays to help in the garden, chattering about school, utterly unhaunted, and Felix found that being seen—plainly, casually, without awe or dread—was a pleasure that never quite wore out.

Some nights, Felix still woke at 3 a.m. with a bar of melody caught in the throat, sweet and coaxing, and had to get up and drink water and say their own name aloud until it passed. Some nights the silence felt enormous, almost hostile, and Felix missed—even guiltily, even horribly—the sense of being accompanied. Grief, Reyes told them, does that. It fills the space a haunting leaves the way scar tissue fills a wound. You don't miss the wound. You miss not being empty.

On the first of December, Felix walked the house room by room, the way Mother's journal said a keeper should, checking the mirrors, checking the walls, listening for anything that breathed out of rhythm. Everything held. In Wren's room, dust motes turned in the window light, ordinary as anything. On the desk lay the sheet music, and beside it the silver whistle—which Felix had taken back from the grave, unable to explain why except that some things belong to the living who remember, not the ground that keeps them.

Felix picked it up. Turned it over. And on an impulse that felt like Wren's own mischief, set it to their lips and blew—not the lullaby, never the lullaby, but three bright, tuneless, joyful notes, the kind of sound a child makes on a summer afternoon for no reason at all.

The notes rang down the hallway and faded into the honest silence.

And somewhere below them—faint, brief, unmistakable—something answered. Not a voice. Not a hum. Just the house itself, settling around the sound like a dog circling twice before lying down at its master's feet. Content. Watchful. Home.

Felix smiled, wiped their eyes, and went downstairs to put the kettle on, leaving the door open behind them—for the listeners who would come at dusk, for the family that had chosen them, for whatever winters the house still had left to keep watch through, together.
