## 1. Eleven Names

The bus let Yara off at the edge of Marrow Creek with two suitcases, a box of books, and the particular lightness of a person who has finally stopped running from something and started running toward it instead. The driver—a heavyset woman with a face like a folded map—pulled the door open, looked past Yara at the empty road in both directions, and said, "You'll want to call ahead for a taxi out here. Nobody comes by regular."

"I'm staying," Yara said. "I took the teaching post."

The woman's expression did something small and complicated, like a window shade being drawn halfway down. "Marrow Creek Primary," she said. Not a question. "Well." She handed down the second suitcase as though it were heavier than it was. "Good luck with that."

Then she was gone, and the diesel smell of her departure hung in the air over the creek itself—which was less a creek than a suggestion of one, a silver thread running through tall grass gone gold at the tips—and Yara stood on the shoulder of Route 9 with their whole life in their hands and felt, for the first time in months, exactly right.

They had wanted this. They had wanted it so badly they'd stopped telling people about it, the way you stop talking about a wish because saying it aloud feels like spending it. A real classroom. A real town, small enough to learn by heart. And Odette—Grandma Odette, who had raised them through the long gray years after Yara's mother died, who wrote letters in looping fountain-pen script and signed them *all my love, always*—Odette was here. That was the true gravity of Marrow Creek. Everything else was scenery.

Yara walked the last mile into town because there was no one to drive them and because they wanted to arrive on foot, the way you arrive in a place you intend to belong to.

*

Marrow Creek announced itself gradually, the way weather does. First the water tower, rust-brown, its lettering faded to a ghost: MARROW CREEK, EST. 1891. Then a feed store, a church with a steeple like a raised finger, a diner called The Griddle whose windows steamed even in September. A hardware store. A library built of gray stone with ivy climbing one corner like something patient. And everywhere, everywhere, the trees—oaks and cottonwoods planted in rows along every street, so uniform and old that they seemed less like landscaping than like witnesses.

The school sat at the town's center the way a heart sits in a body. It was a redbrick building, two stories, with a bell cupola and windows so tall and dark they reflected the sky back wrong, slightly too blue, slightly too still. A marquee sign out front read WELCOME BACK STUDENTS! FIRST DAY SEPT. 3 in removable black letters, and beneath that, in smaller letters someone had neglected to remove: HARVEST FESTIVAL OCT. 26 — ALL FAMILIES.

Yara stood across the street for a full minute, just looking. Their chest did the thing it did—the warm expanding thing, the thing that had gotten them through four years of night classes and substitute assignments in schools where the radiators screamed and the textbooks predated the moon landing. *This one,* the feeling said. *This is yours.*

Later, Yara would remember standing there. Later, they would wonder why they hadn't noticed that not one child was visible anywhere near the building, though summer was ending and the afternoon was mild and golden. Later—but for now, the light was kind, and kindness is very good at hiding things.

*

Odette's house stood on Larch Street at the end where the pavement gave up and became gravel. It was narrow and white-clapboarded, with a porch swing that had been broken since before Yara was born and a garden that Odette kept in ferocious order despite being seventy-nine. The door opened before Yara reached the steps.

"You walked," Odette said. It wasn't an accusation or a question. She said it the way she said everything, in a voice like low music, and then she came down the steps and held Yara's face in both hands and looked at them for a long time with eyes the same hazel as Yara's own, and whatever she saw made her exhale through her nose, once, like a horse deciding to accept a rider.

"Hi, Grandma."

"You're thin."

"I'm fine."

"You're thin and you walked nine miles with luggage, which proves my point." But she was smiling, and she pulled them into a hug that smelled of lavender soap and woodsmoke and the faint mineral smell of the well water everyone in Marrow Creek drank without thinking about it. "Come in. I made soup. Don't argue."

Inside, the house was exactly as Yara remembered it from childhood visits that had grown rarer and rarer as the years went on—visits Yara now understood, with a small cold stone settling behind their sternum, they should have made more of. The same floral wallpaper in the parlor, sun-faded to whispers of pink. The same clock ticking in the hall, a little off-tempo, always had been. The same kitchen with its yellow curtains and its enormous cast-iron stove that Odette refused to replace because, she said, a stove should have opinions.

But there was something else too, some quality Yara couldn't name until they'd been inside ten minutes and then could name all at once: the house was quiet in a way houses aren't usually quiet. Not peaceful-quiet. Held-quiet. The silence of a room where someone has just stopped speaking.

"Your room's upstairs," Odette said, stirring soup she didn't need to stir. "I aired it out. There's a draft off the creek in the evenings; you'll want the heavier blanket."

"Grandma." Yara set down their bags. "Thank you. For—this. For letting me land here."

Odette's spoon paused. Just for a moment. A hitch, like a needle skipping. "Where else would you land?" she said, and turned to smile at them, and the smile was warm and real and arrived perhaps half a second too late, like thunder after lightning.

*

Unpacking took until dusk. Yara's room was under the eaves, a narrow room with a dormer window looking east over the garden toward the darkening line of trees beyond the town. They hung clothes in a wardrobe that smelled of cedar and decades. They stacked books on the sill. They set the framed photograph of their mother on the nightstand—her only photograph, really, taken the year before she left Marrow Creek forever, laughing at something outside the frame, twenty-six years old and already carrying whatever it was she carried.

Yara touched the frame's edge, the way they always did. "Made it, Mom," they said quietly, to no one, to her. "We're home."

Downstairs, floorboards spoke under Odette's feet, moving room to room, closing curtains against the dark. Yara heard her pause somewhere below—pause longer than curtain-closing required—and then continue, and told themselves they hadn't heard it at all.

It was while hauling the last box upstairs that Yara found the photo.

It had slipped behind the hall linen closet, wedged between the wall and a stack of winter blankets that smelled of mothballs and time. Yara only found it because the box caught the closet door and swung it wide, and the flashlight they'd brought up caught a glint of glass. They knelt, expecting dust or dead flies or nothing, and drew out a picture frame, walnut, heavy, its backing paper torn at one corner.

A class photograph. Sepia-toned at the edges, the color shifted toward amber the way old photographs go, like tea steeping too long. Across the bottom, in white paint-pen letters gone slightly yellow: **MARROW CREEK PRIMARY — ROOM 4 — MRS. HALLORAN — 1974.**

Yara carried it to the dormer window where the last light came through, and looked.

Eleven children stood and sat arranged on the front steps of the school—the same school, unmistakably, though younger, its brick brighter, its windows reflecting a different sky. Behind them, half-visible in the doorway, a teacher's shape: Mrs. Halloran, presumably, her face soft with blur.

Eleven children. Yara counted twice, because counting is what teachers do, the way carpenters measure. Eleven faces squinting into a sun that must have been behind the photographer. Eleven smiles of varying sincerity—the front row grinning wide, the back row wearing the strained politeness children wear when told to hold still.

And one boy, third from the left in the back row, who wasn't smiling at all.

He was small and dark-haired, and he looked directly into the camera with an expression that on a child reads as nothing much—tiredness, maybe, or shyness—but which in the amber wash of the old print, in the failing light of Yara's new room, looked like something else entirely. Like a child listening to something behind him. Something in the doorway beside the blurred teacher. Something the camera had almost caught.

Yara turned the frame over. Someone had written on the backing paper in pencil, in a hand that was not Odette's looping script but smaller, tighter, hurried:

*Room 4, fall '74. Full class.*

*Full class.* An odd thing to write. Who labels a class photo *full*, unless—

Unless there was a version of events in which it wasn't.

"Supper!" Odette called up the stairs, and Yara started hard enough that the frame nearly slipped from their hands. Their heart ticked fast, absurdly, the way it does after a dream you can't quite keep hold of. They laughed at themselves—a short, embarrassed sound in the empty room—and set the photograph upright on the dresser, facing out, the eleven faces catching the last of the light.

Outside the dormer window, the trees beyond the garden had gone black against a bruised-purple sky, and somewhere far off—so far it might have been imagination, might have been traffic on Route 9, might have been anything—there was a sound. Low. Steady. Below hearing and above feeling, sitting in the strange register where your body notices before your mind agrees to.

Yara listened for three seconds. Four. Five.

It stopped.

"Coming!" they called, and went down to supper, and the soup was good, and Odette asked about the bus ride and the classroom and whether Yara had met the principal yet (not yet; Monday), and the evening filled up with ordinary warmth the way a cup fills with water, and if there was a hairline crack somewhere in the cup, neither of them mentioned it.

But later—much later, when the house had gone held-quiet again and Yara lay in the narrow bed under the heavy blanket listening to the draft breathe at the window—they thought about the photograph once more. About eleven names on a class roster for a school that, according to everything they'd read while preparing their lesson plans, had never enrolled more than ten students in any given year in its history.

That wasn't true, of course. They hadn't read any such thing. They hadn't read anything about Marrow Creek's enrollment at all.

The thought simply arrived, fully formed, the way certain thoughts do at 2 a.m.—uninvited, unexplained, wearing the confidence of memory—and it lay down beside Yara in the dark and stayed there, patient as the trees, until sleep finally took them near dawn.

On the dresser, eleven faces smiled into a sun that had set fifty years ago.

Third from the left, the small dark-haired boy went on listening.
