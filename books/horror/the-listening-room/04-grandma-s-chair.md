## 4. Grandma's Chair

The house smelled of cardamom and old wood when Yara came home from their first day at Marrow Creek Primary, and for a little while—maybe an hour, maybe less—the wrongness of the schoolhouse seemed like something they might have imagined.

Grandma Odette was in the kitchen, sleeves rolled to the elbow, kneading dough on the flour-dusted table the way she had kneaded it in every kitchen Yara had ever known her to occupy. The radio murmured low—a station that played nothing but fiddle music and weather reports—and the late afternoon light came through the window over the sink in a long amber bar that turned the rising steam golden. It was, Yara thought, the kind of scene a person carries with them into old age. The kind of scene you build your whole idea of *home* out of.

"You look pale," Odette said without turning around. She always knew who had entered a room before she looked; she claimed she could tell by the way the floorboards greeted them.

"Long day." Yara dropped their bag on the bench by the door and washed their hands at the basin, watching the flour dust drift in the light. "The kids are good. There's one girl, Pru, who—" They stopped. They weren't sure how to say the rest. *There's one girl who writes me notes about the thing under the floor.* "Who's very observant."

"Mm." Odette folded the dough over itself, pressed, turned it. Her forearms were thin but strong, corded with seventy years of work. "Observant children grow into either poets or troublemakers. Which are you feeding them toward?"

"Poets. Obviously."

"Obviously." A small smile, there and gone. "Supper's in an hour. Go put your things away. And Yara—" now she did turn, and the light caught the fine white hairs at her temple, made her eyes briefly unreadable, two dark coins, "—how many desks did they give you?"

The question landed strangely, like a stone dropped down a well whose depth Yara hadn't suspected.

"Eleven," they said. "Why?"

"No reason." She turned back to the dough. The folding, pressing, turning resumed, a little faster than before. "Go on, then."

Yara went. But climbing the narrow stairs to their room, they found themselves counting without meaning to. Ten names on the roster. Eleven desks. Eleven faces in a photograph taken twenty-nine years before any of those children were born. The numbers kept arranging themselves in their head like birds on a wire, all facing the same direction, waiting for something.

* * *

That evening, after supper—rabbit stew, dense with root vegetables from Odette's garden—Yara sat at the little desk under their window and began unpacking the last of their boxes. Books first, then papers, then the shoebox of photographs they'd brought from the apartment they'd given up in the city: prints from the past three years, mostly unsorted, most of them taken on film because Yara liked the way film remembered things differently than memory did. Slower. More honest about what it chose to keep.

They were looking for a picture of their mother to prop on the shelf—they'd been meaning to do it since they arrived, and had been putting it off for reasons they didn't examine—and so they began sorting through the box properly for the first time in months.

The photographs were arranged loosely by year. Three autumns ago: the city, the old apartment, friends crowded onto a fire escape. Two winters ago: snow on a university quad, breath-fog, someone laughing mid-frame. Last spring: the coast, gulls, a pier going gray into the water.

And then the pictures of this house.

Odette had invited them to visit Marrow Creek twice before Yara took the job here—once two springs ago, once last Christmas—and both times Yara had taken photographs, because that was what they did, because the camera was the organ through which they understood anything they wanted to keep. There were perhaps thirty prints from those visits. Most of them were of Odette herself: in the garden, on the porch, laughing at something off-frame. And a fair number were of the kitchen, because the kitchen was the heart of the house, the warm amber room where everything good happened.

Yara laid them out on the desk in rows, the way they'd learned to lay out evidence, though at the time they'd have said *memories*, and it took several minutes of idle shuffling before they noticed.

There was a chair against the wall.

In every photograph.

Not a chair being sat in. Not a chair pulled up to the table. A wooden chair—high-backed, dark, plain, the wood gone silver-gray with age—standing against the wall beneath the window, beside the cabinet where Odette kept the good dishes. Facing outward. Facing the room.

Yara frowned and picked up the nearest print, holding it under the lamp.

Two springs ago, April, the visit when the plum trees had been blooming. There was Odette at the stove, mid-laugh, wooden spoon raised. There was the window, the amber light, the flour canister, the dish cabinet. And there, against the wall, the chair.

Yara had no memory of that chair.

They picked up another print. Last Christmas. Snow at the window, Odette's hands wrapped around a mug, steam rising. The same wall. The same cabinet. The same chair, standing in exactly the same place, at exactly the same angle, as if it had not moved so much as an inch in eight months. As if it had been nailed there. As if it were part of the house.

They went through the rest. Thirty-one photographs of the kitchen across two visits. In thirty-one photographs, the chair stood against the wall. Same position. Same angle. Same stillness. In some frames it was partially cropped, half-swallowed by the edge of the picture, but it was always there, always facing the room, always empty.

"Okay," Yara said aloud, to the empty bedroom, because saying things aloud sometimes broke spells. "You're tired. You're seeing patterns."

But their hands had gone cold, and they knew—knew the way you know a floor is rotten before your foot goes through—that they were not seeing patterns. They had stood in that kitchen for hours across two visits. They could reconstruct it in their mind cabinet by cabinet, jar by jar. There had been no chair against that wall. There had been the radiator, and the basket of onions, and Odette's reading glasses folded on the windowsill above the sink. No chair. Never a chair.

They got up and went downstairs.

Odette was in the front room in her armchair, mending a shirt by lamplight, the needle flashing in and out of the fabric with the patience of water wearing stone. The television was off. The house ticked and settled around her the way old houses do, breathing through its joists.

"Grandma."

"Mm?"

"When I visited in April. And at Christmas." Yara held the stack of photographs behind their back, which was absurd, childish, and which they did anyway. "Was there a chair against the kitchen wall? Under the window? A wooden one, high-backed?"

The needle stopped.

It stopped so completely, so suddenly, that the silence it left behind seemed louder than the sound had been. Odette did not look up. For three full seconds she sat perfectly still, thread taut between fabric and spool, and Yara watched the color leave her face the way water leaves a sink—not all at once but steadily, inevitably, drawn down somewhere below.

"Why do you ask?" Her voice was level. Too level. The voice of a woman walking across ice, testing each step before committing her weight.

"I'm asking if there was a chair."

"There is no chair against my kitchen wall." Still she did not look up. The needle trembled, a hair's width, and was stilled. "There has never been a chair against my kitchen wall."

"That's what I thought." Yara brought the photographs forward and laid them on the arm of her chair, one atop another, the topmost showing the amber kitchen, the laughing woman, the spoon, the chair. "Because they're in all of them. Every picture I've ever taken in this house. Every single one. It's—it's in the exact same spot, Grandma. Like it never moves. Like it's *waiting*—"

"*Give them to me.*"

Odette's hand shot out faster than Yara would have believed possible, closing on the stack with a grip that made the prints crumple slightly at the edges. Now she looked up, and her eyes were not the dark warm coins Yara had known all their life. They were wide, and wet, and fixed on the photograph with an expression Yara had no name for—the expression, they thought later, of a woman looking at a bill come due decades early.

"Where else," Odette said. It wasn't quite a question. "Where else does it appear?"

"It doesn't. Just the kitchen. Just that wall." Yara heard their own voice go thin. "Grandma, what is it? Whose chair is it?"

For a moment heaving silence filled the room. The lamp hissed faintly. Somewhere outside, far off across the fields, a dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

Then Odette stood, gathered the photographs to her chest like a wounded bird, and walked into the kitchen.

Yara followed.

She set the prints facedown on the counter, all of them, smoothing each one flat with both hands as though calming animals. Then she opened the drawer beside the stove—the junk drawer, the drawer of string and batteries and dead pens—and took out the matches. The long wooden ones she used for the stove, though the stove was gas and needed only a turn of the dial.

"Grandma—"

"Hold this." She thrust the iron skillet into Yara's hands. Into it, one by one, she placed the photographs, facedown, and struck a match, and touched it to the corner of the topmost print.

The photo curled and browned and caught. Amber light, laughing woman, wooden spoon—and the chair, the chair, the chair—wrinkled into black lace and came apart in flakes.

"Grandma, *stop*—" Yara reached for her wrist and she shook them off without looking, striking another match, feeding the fire print by print. The smell of burning gloss and chemicals filled the kitchen, acrid, chemical-sweet, wrong in a room that smelled of cardamom and bread. "Those are pictures of *you*. Of us. Of Mom's—there's hardly anything left of her, you can't just—"

"They aren't pictures anymore." Odette's voice had dropped to almost nothing. She spoke to the flames, or to the chair that wasn't there. "Not since it started standing in them. You don't keep a window open once something's learned to look through it."

"What does that mean? What's *looking*?"

Another match. Another curl of black. Ash rose and hung in the air between them, fine and gray, settling on the flour-dusted table, on Odette's rolled sleeves, in Yara's hair. Through the window over the sink the night pressed close, moonless, the fields invisible, the dark absolute.

"Thirty-one years I've lived in this house," Odette said quietly, feeding the fire. "And in thirty-one years that chair has never once been in my kitchen. Do you understand what I'm telling you, Yara? It isn't coming *into* the photographs. The photographs are coming to *it*. Something has decided my kitchen belongs in its room, and it is arranging the furniture accordingly."

The last print caught. Yara watched their mother's Christmas—Odette's mittened hands, the mug, the steam—shrink to embers in the skillet. Their throat ached. Their hands wouldn't stop shaking, and they gripped the iron handle tighter to hide it.

"Whose chair is it?" they asked again, softer now.

Odette emptied the ashes into the sink and ran the water until they vanished, and stood with her back to Yara for a long time, watching the gray swirl down the drain. When she finally spoke, her voice was steady again, and that steadiness frightened Yara more than the trembling had.

"It's late. You have school tomorrow." She turned, and the warmth was back in her face like a lamp relit, and it was the most terrible performance Yara had ever witnessed, because it was flawless. "I'll make pancakes. You liked pancakes when you were small."

"Grandma."

"Pancakes," she said, "and you'll tell me about your students. All ten of them."

*Ten,* Yara thought. *I told you eleven.*

But they didn't say it. They stood in the ruined-smelling kitchen while Odette measured flour with steady hands, and they understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water poured down the back of the neck, that the woman stirring the batter was not making breakfast. She was building a wall, brick by brick, between Yara and something she had spent fifty years refusing to name.

Later, upstairs, brushing their teeth, Yara caught their own reflection in the bathroom mirror and had the sudden, irrational urge to check behind it—to look, the way you look behind a door, the way you look under a bed—for a high-backed wooden chair, standing patient against the wall, facing the room.

They laughed at themselves. The laugh came out wrong.

Back in their room, they booted their laptop and opened the folder of digital photos—the ones they'd taken on their phone during the Christmas visit, before they'd switched back to film. They scrolled quickly, telling themselves they weren't going to look, knowing they were going to look.

Kitchen. Window. Cabinet. Odette's smile.

No chair.

Relief flooded through them, warm and foolish—

—and then they noticed the date stamp. They scrolled back. Checked the file properties. The phone photos were from December. The film prints were from December too. Same visit. Same week. Same kitchen.

One camera had seen the chair. One had not.

Yara sat very still in the dark, listening to the house breathe, and thought about Pru's note folded in their bag downstairs. *It likes when you listen.* Thought about eleven desks and ten names. Thought about a thing that arranged itself into some pictures and not others, choosing its witnesses, choosing its doors.

Downstairs, faintly, the kitchen clock chimed midnight.

And beneath the chime—so soft Yara might have invented it, so soft they would spend the next week trying to decide whether they had—came a second sound. A low, sustained tone, rising up through the floorboards, through the foundations, through fifty years of packed earth.

The house was humming.

And somewhere below it, patient as furniture, something was keeping time.
