## 13. Voss's Offer

The note appeared in my mailbox on a Tuesday morning, folded into thirds and sealed with a wax stamp I'd never seen before—a stylized ear, pressed into deep green wax. Inside, in handwriting so neat it looked typeset:

*Ms. Almeida—Please come by my office at your earliest convenience. There is a matter of some delicacy I'd like to discuss. —Principal Voss*

I stood in the faculty parking lot with the note in my hand and the October wind pulling at my coat, and I understood, in the way you understand a thing before you can name it, that this was not about curriculum.

The school had been watching me. Or rather, the town had been watching me through the school, the way light comes through a curtain—diffuse, patient, everywhere at once. Ruth's warning from weeks ago still sat in my chest like a swallowed stone: *Ask about the harvest fund.* I had asked. I had asked too many questions of too many people, and now the questions had ripened into something the town needed to deal with.

I tucked the note into my bag, next to Pru's drawing—the one with twelve figures, ten children, me, and the tall shape by the door labeled THE LISTENER—and walked inside.

* * *

Voss's office smelled of apples and furniture polish. It always did, that particular sweetness, like something left to rot under a layer of varnish. He had a bowl of fresh apples on his desk that no one ever ate, and every morning they were replaced with new ones, so that the fruit was always perfect, always just past the point where anyone would want to bite into it.

"Yara." He rose from behind his desk with both hands extended, as if greeting a relative at a funeral. "Thank you for coming. Sit, sit. Can I get you coffee? Tea? Odette's family drinks tea, if I remember right."

"You remember right," I said, and didn't sit.

He sat instead, folding himself back down into his leather chair with the ease of a man who had done this many times before. That was the thing about Voss—I'd realized it slowly over the autumn, the way you realize a room is cold only after you've been shivering for an hour. Everything he did was practiced. His warmth was a technique. His smile arrived a half-second before his eyes agreed to it.

"I'll be direct with you," he said, "because I respect you, and because I think you're the kind of person who appreciates directness."

I said nothing. The hum was faintly present today, even up here on the second floor, even through the walls and the carpet and the closed door. Or maybe I imagined it. Lately I couldn't tell the difference between what I heard and what I'd been taught to hear.

"You're an excellent teacher," Voss continued. "Genuinely. Your reading scores are already the best in the district, and it's October. The children adore you. Pru especially—" he paused there, delicately, the way a man steps over a crack in the sidewalk, "—though of course Pru has been out ill."

"She's been out eleven days," I said. "Nobody will tell me what's wrong with her."

"Children get sick. It's the season." He waved this away like smoke. "My point is, Marrow Creek has been fortunate in its teachers over the years. Fortunate, and—let us say—selective. We look for people who fit. And when someone doesn't quite fit, well." He opened a drawer and drew out a folder, cream-colored, unmarked. He laid it on the desk between us but did not push it toward me. "We try to make the parting gentle."

I looked at the folder. "You're firing me?"

"Firing you? Goodness, no." He laughed, and the laugh was genuine, which somehow made it worse. "Yara. We're offering you a transfer. A position in Millbrook—newer building, higher pay, and a signing bonus that I think you'll find... substantial." He turned the folder around and slid it across the desk.

Inside was a letter of recommendation so glowing it read like a eulogy for a saint, a transfer agreement pre-filled with my name, and a figure at the bottom that made my breath catch. Three months' salary. As a bonus. For leaving.

"This is a lot of money for a teacher who 'fits,'" I said.

"It's a lot of money for a teacher we'd hate to lose," Voss said smoothly. "And we would hate to lose you. But we'd hate more to see you unhappy here. Marrow Creek isn't for everyone. Some people find it..." He searched for the word, savoring the search. "...confining."

"The eleventh desk," I said.

His smile didn't move, but something behind it did—a shutter closing, a lamp carried into another room.

"I'm sorry?"

"In my classroom. There are eleven desks. My roster lists ten students. When I asked facilities about it, they told me the desk count was correct. Eleven desks, ten students, no discrepancy. I'm asking you directly, Principal Voss: whose desk is the eleventh?"

For a long moment the only sound was the radiator ticking and, beneath it, beneath everything, that low tone I had stopped being able to unhear. Voss removed his glasses and cleaned them on his tie, unhurried, giving himself time to become the version of himself he needed to be.

"You know," he said, putting the glasses back on, "you're not the first teacher to ask that question. In 1981, a young man named Daniel Osei taught fourth grade. Bright fellow. Asked about the desks in his first month. By Christmas he'd taken a position in Ohio and a bonus very similar to the one in that folder. In 1993, Margaret Ilves—she kept a journal, apparently, though she was sensible enough to leave it behind. And in 2009—" he smiled again, warmly, terribly, "—well. You knew Ruth Ellery came from somewhere else, didn't you? She asked her questions too. She got her offer. She declined it, actually, the first time. And then one morning she woke up and found that everyone in town had simply... stopped seeing her. Not cruelly. Nothing was ever done *to* her. But invitations stopped coming. Conversations ended when she entered rooms. Her car tires were always soft in the mornings. Small things. Accumulating things. By spring she signed the paper, took the money, moved two towns over—and then, wouldn't you know it, she came back. Asked to come back. Accepted things as they are." He spread his hands. "People do, eventually. It's easier."

The threat was so beautifully wrapped I almost admired it. No raised voice. No explicit menace. Just a man describing, in the tone of a golf scorecard, how a town digests the people who ask questions.

"And if I don't sign?" I asked.

Voss sighed, and for just a moment—for a flicker, a single frame of film—he looked tired. Old. Like a man who had been holding up a ceiling for so long he'd forgotten there was anything above it.

"Then you stay," he said quietly. "And you keep teaching, and you keep counting, and you keep hearing what you hear. And Yara—I say this with real affection—you have no idea what you're standing in. You think you've found something. A secret. A wrongness. You think the town is hiding a crime, and maybe it is, and maybe you even imagine you could expose it, fix it, make it right. But what's under this school isn't a crime. Crimes have perpetrators and victims and solutions. What's under this school is an arrangement. Arrangements don't end. They only change who keeps them." He tapped the folder once, gently. "Take the money. Teach somewhere the floors don't hum. Have a life. Please."

It was the *please* that nearly undid me. Because it was sincere. Somewhere under all that polish, Voss meant it. He wasn't threatening me; he was trying to save me, the way you'd pull a child back from a well without explaining what's at the bottom, because explaining would mean looking down.

I thought of Pru's drawing. I thought of the tall shape by the door. I thought of Tomas Reyes, the name that existed nowhere except in a photograph and a gap in the records, a boy erased so thoroughly that even the paper trail had been taught to forget him.

"No," I said.

Voss closed his eyes briefly. "Yara—"

"No. I'm not taking the transfer. I'm not taking the bonus. And I'm not going to stop asking questions." I picked up the folder and set it back on his desk, aligning its edges with his blotter, because my hands wanted something orderly to do. "Whatever arrangement this town made, it was made with a child's life. Somebody's son. Somebody's student. And every year since, somebody's child. You can buy off teachers, Principal Voss. Apparently you've been doing it for fifty years. But you can't buy off the truth, and you can't buy off me."

He studied me for a long moment, and I watched him decide something. I don't know what. Maybe whether to warn me further. Maybe whether to call someone. Maybe nothing at all—maybe just deciding to let the machinery do what machinery does.

"All right," he said finally, softly. "All right. The offer stands until Friday. Take the weekend. Think about your grandmother. Think about what she'd want." 

That landed harder than any threat. He saw it hit. He didn't gloat. He simply nodded, once, and returned to his papers, and I walked out of the office with my spine straight and my heart going like a trapped bird.

In the hallway, the hum rose to meet me, warm through the floorboards, like something below turning over in its sleep.

* * *

I didn't go straight home. I drove out past the feed store and the shuttered cannery to the county road, parked on the shoulder, and called Ruth from my cell phone, because I didn't trust the landlines anymore—they had a way of carrying faint sounds underneath conversations, a way of breathing.

She answered on the second ring. "He offered you the folder."

"How did you—"

"He offers everyone the folder. Did you take it?"

"No."

A long silence on the line. Wind moved through the dead corn in the field beside me, making a sound like whispering, like a classroom full of students murmuring answers.

"Then you need to hear what happened after I refused," Ruth said. "Not to scare you. To prepare you. Because it starts small, Yara. It starts with kindness, and then it starts with absence, and then—" she stopped. Started again. "Check your porch tonight. And check it before dark, not after. And whatever you find, don't bring it inside."

"What will I find?"

"I found a crow," she said. "In 2009. On my doorstep, arranged neatly, wings folded, like it was sleeping. Except crows don't sleep on their backs with their feet crossed." Her voice went thin. "It's not a threat, exactly. It's a message. It means: *we see you, and the thing below sees you, and the town has decided to let it.* After the bird, the tires. After the tires, the letters. After the letters—" 

"After the letters, what?"

Another silence. Longer.

"After the letters, they come for your documentation," she said. "Everything you've written down. Every proof. Because proof is the only weapon that works on them, and they know it better than anyone. Get copies, Yara. Get copies and get them out of your house tonight. And then—" her voice dropped, and I heard in it the sound of a woman talking herself into courage she'd spent fifteen years avoiding, "—then call me. Whatever happens next, call me. I owe you that much. I owed the last one that much too."

"The last one?"

But she'd already hung up.

* * *

I drove home the long way, watching my mirrors, and the sky over Marrow Creek was the color of a bruise going yellow at the edges. Harvest moon soon. Four days, according to the almanac I'd started keeping hidden in my car. The fields were bare and black, and the mountains stood against the last light like the shoulders of something enormous hunched over the valley, listening down into it.

At home, Grandma Odette was in the kitchen, sitting in her chair by the window, hands folded in her lap, staring at nothing. She'd been doing that more lately—sitting very still, as if listening to something far away, or waiting for something near. The clocks were all running again since the night they'd stopped at 3:11, but she wound them twice a day anyway, obsessively, as if time in this house needed constant supervision to keep from slipping.

"There's stew," she said, without turning. "Eat it while it's hot."

"Odette." I sat down across from her. "Voss offered me money to leave today. A lot of money. And when I refused, he told me to think about what you'd want."

Her hands tightened in her lap. The knuckles went white as river stones.

"And what did you tell him?" she asked.

"I told him no."

"Stupid girl." But her voice cracked on it, and she reached across the table and gripped my wrist, hard, her fingers cold and strong. "Listen to me. Listen. When I was seventeen, I thought refusing was brave too. I thought love was enough to stand on. And I learned—" she stopped, jaw working, eyes bright and terrible in the lamplight. "I learned that some doors don't care how brave you are. They only care whether you're willing. Do you understand? Bravery is nothing. Willingness is everything. And that thing under the school has been waiting fifty years to find out how willing you are."

"Willing to do what?" I asked. "Odette. *Willing to do what?*"

She let go of my wrist and turned back to the window, to the darkening yard, and would not answer. Outside, the first stars were coming out over the tree line, and the wind had gone completely still, the way it does before snow, or before something else.

* * *

I worked until midnight making copies. Photographs of the ledger pages Ruth had shown me, photographs of the 1974 class photo with its eleven smiling faces, scans of the enrollment records with Tomas Reyes's ghost-entry, Pru's drawing flattened between the pages of a phone book and photographed from four angles. I mailed one set to myself at a PO box in Millbrook—the irony of that address not lost on me—and hid another set inside the hollowed-out spine of a dictionary on Odette's shelf, wedged between *quandary* and *quietus*, which felt appropriate.

Then I slept badly, in the shallow water-surface way I'd slept all autumn, and woke before dawn to a sound on the porch.

Not a knock. Not footsteps. Something softer—a weight settling, the way a bird settles onto a branch. I lay in the grey half-light and listened to the house breathe around me, and then I got up, and took the fireplace poker from beside the hearth, and opened the front door.

The sparrow lay on the welcome mat, centered, precise.

Dead—but not broken. No blood, no marks. Its wings were folded against its body in perfect symmetry, its small gray head tucked, its yellow feet crossed neatly at the ankles, as if it had lain down deliberately, arranged itself, and waited. Around it, drawn on the boards of the porch in something dark—soil, maybe, or ash—was a circle. And inside the circle, written small and careful in the same dark substance, in handwriting I recognized with a lurch that turned my stomach to ice:

My own handwriting. My own slanting script, the one that had been changing lately on my lesson plans, drifting toward someone else's angles. It said:

*TEN MORE DAYS.*

I stood on the threshold in my nightgown, the poker loose in my hand, and counted backward despite myself. From today. Ten days. Past the harvest moon. Past everything.

The sparrow's eye caught the dawn light and held it, round and black and fixed, and for one vertiginous moment I had the absolute certainty—not a thought, a certainty—that it was not looking at nothing. That behind that tiny dead face, something patient and enormous was looking at me, the way you look through a keyhole.

*I see you,* the arrangement said, in soil and ash and small gray feathers. *The town sees you. And the thing below has begun to count.*

I did not bring the sparrow inside. I buried it at the edge of the yard, under the bare apple tree, and filled in the earth with my hands, and washed them until they stung.

When I came back inside, Odette was standing at the kitchen window, looking out at the fresh-turned dirt by the apple tree. She had a suitcase packed and waiting by the door.

"We leave tonight," she said. "Both of us. Before moonrise."

And downstairs, faint but unmistakable, rising up through the floorboards of my grandmother's house—through ground that should never have known that sound—the humming began.
