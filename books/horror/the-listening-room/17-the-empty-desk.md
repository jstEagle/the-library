## 17. The Empty Desk

Pru came back on a Tuesday, the way weather comes back—without apology, without explanation, filling a space that had learned to live without her.

Yara saw her first from the window of Room 4, standing at the far edge of the schoolyard where the fence met the tree line, where the morning fog hadn't yet burned off. She was small against all that gray, in her yellow raincoat, her backpack hanging off one shoulder like an afterthought. For one absurd, swelling moment Yara felt something close to joy. Pru was back. Pru was safe. Whatever had taken her—a family emergency, an illness, some ordinary catastrophe Yara's mind had refused to supply because it kept offering worse ones—was over.

Then Yara noticed that Pru wasn't moving.

She stood at the fence line with the stillness of furniture, of things placed rather than arrived, and the fog moved around her instead of past her, curling at the hem of her coat as if reluctant to touch it. The other children streamed by her toward the doors, and not one of them looked at her. Not one of them slowed. They parted around her the way water parts around a post, without thought, without seeing, and Yara understood with a cold sliding feeling in their stomach that this was not new to them. The children had known she would come back today. The children had perhaps always known.

Yara's coffee went cold in their hand before they could drink it.

* * *

By the time Yara reached the classroom, Pru was already seated—at the eleventh desk.

Not her own desk, third row by the window, where her pencil shavings used to collect in a little gray nest she'd never clean up. She sat at the eleventh desk, the one no roster claimed, the one that had stood empty since Yara's first day, the one Mr. Okafor had warned them never to count aloud. It had been empty for six weeks. It was not empty now.

Pru sat there with her hands folded on the desktop, her coat still buttoned to the throat though the room was warm, and she was facing forward—not toward the board, not toward Yara, but slightly off-axis, toward the corner of the room where the wall met the floor, as if listening to something being said very quietly beneath the linoleum.

"Good morning, Pru," Yara said, and hated how carefully they said it, how each word was set down like a foot on river ice.

Pru turned her head. That was the wrong part—the turn itself. It was smooth and continuous and too slow, a head rotating on a neck like a door swinging on oiled hinges, and when her face came around to meet Yara's, her eyes were Pru's eyes, brown and bright and eight years old, but the *attention* behind them belonged to something else. Something that looked out through her the way light looks out through a window it did not build.

"Good morning, Miss Almeida," Pru said.

Except it wasn't only Pru's voice. The words came out doubled, layered, Pru's high clear voice with another voice underneath it speaking in perfect unison, a voice lower and flatter and older, the way two instruments an octave apart can play the same note and make a chord that sounds like neither. Yara had heard choirs. They had heard unison singing in churches, in school assemblies, in the recordings of their own students' concerts. This was not that. This was one mouth producing two throats.

The other children filed in and took their seats. None of them glanced at the eleventh desk. Yara watched them, waiting for someone to notice, someone to whisper, someone to do what children do when something is wrong—and no one did. Marcus hung his jacket on his hook. Soleil sharpened her pencil with the little crank sharpener she loved. Dev took his seat and opened his math book to the dog-eared page he'd been stuck on for a week. The classroom performed its morning exactly as it always did, and the eleventh desk sat occupied among them like a stone in a stream, and the water of the ordinary day simply flowed around it.

Yara took attendance. Their hand shook, and they let it shake; hiding it felt suddenly dangerous, as if the thing in the room rewarded concealment.

"Aiden."

"Here."

"Bianca."

"Here."

They read down the roster, ten names, ten voices answering, and then—because they were tired, because they were frightened, because some stubborn documentary instinct in them demanded a complete record even now—they paused at the bottom of the sheet, at the blank line after the tenth name, and said nothing, and the silence stretched, and Pru raised her hand.

Yara called on her. What else could they do?

"Present," Pru said, in the doubled voice, and smiled, and the smile was Pru's smile, gap-toothed, the smile Yara had seen a hundred times, and it was the most terrible thing Yara had ever seen, because it fit perfectly and meant nothing.

* * *

Silent reading. Twenty minutes of enforced quiet, the room filled only with the sound of pages turning and the radiator ticking and, beneath everything, so low you could convince yourself it was the pipes, the hum.

Yara walked the aisles the way they always did, checking comprehension, straightening spines, and when they passed the eleventh desk they made themselves look down, casually, the way a teacher looks at any student's book.

Pru's book was open to a page in the middle. Her finger rested under the first line. But her eyes weren't on the page. They were closed. And her lips were moving.

Yara bent closer, telling themselves they were checking on a student who might be unwell, and heard it.

"...esier, esier, esier..."

A whisper. Barely voiced. Backward.

Yara knew the names. Of course they knew the names—they had spent weeks pulling them out of ledgers and archives and the stubborn memory of a town that wanted to forget them. Reyes. Corliss. Whitlock. Okafor. Ellery. Voss. Names given to the Listener across fifty years of harvests, eleven names struck through in Ruth's ledger, names that had been spoken once, willingly or otherwise, and then erased from every record the town could reach.

Pru was reciting them backward. From the most recent to the oldest, walking the ledger in reverse, syllable by inverted syllable, and the doubled voice made each name sound like it was being unwound rather than spoken, unmade rather than remembered.

Yara straightened up slowly. Pru's eyes stayed closed. Her finger stayed under the line of text, tracking nothing.

And Yara understood—or began to understand, in the wordless way the body understands before the mind consents—that this was not possession the way movies showed it, not a hijacking, not a demon wearing a girl like a suit. This was *listening*. Pru was doing what the room below the school did. She was receiving. And whatever spoke through her was running the ledger backward for a reason: it was checking. Counting its holdings. Confirming that every name it had been given was still where it had put them.

Eleven names. Eleven chairs below the foundations. And now, apparently, a twelfth being measured for size.

Yara backed away down the aisle, one step at a time, and Pru's lips kept moving, and the hum beneath the floorboards deepened by a fraction of a tone, the way a held breath deepens before speech.

* * *

At recess Yara found the playground empty of answers. They stood at the window instead, watching the yard, and saw Pru sitting alone on the bench by the fence—the same fence, the same fog-line from the morning—while the other children played around her. Not with her. Around her. A circle of games that simply didn't include her coordinates anymore.

Ruth Ellery appeared at Yara's shoulder. Yara hadn't heard the door open.

"That's not her," Ruth said quietly. It wasn't a question and it wasn't quite a statement. It was a woman naming a thing so she wouldn't have to describe it.

"She came back," Yara said. "After three weeks. Nobody called. Nobody reported her absent. I checked—her file says she was never gone. The attendance records show her present every single day." Yara laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, thin and glassy. "She's been marked present for three weeks, Ruth. In a room where nobody saw her."

"The records here don't record," Ruth said. "They *agree*. There's a difference." She was gripping her own elbow, hard, the way people hold themselves together. "My grandmother used to say the creek keeps books better than the county does."

"It's using her to count," Yara said. "It's reading the ledger through her. Backward. Like it's—" They stopped, because saying it made it real, and real was a country they were already living in. "Like it's taking inventory."

Ruth was silent a long moment. Below them, Pru turned her head—all the way around, smoothly, without moving her shoulders—and looked up at the window where they stood. At Yara. She smiled the gap-toothed smile and waved, a small cheerful wave, fingers opening and closing, and the doubled voice carried up through glass and distance and forty feet of autumn air as clearly as if her mouth were pressed to the pane:

"You forgot to mark me present, Miss Almeida."

Ruth made a sound Yara had never heard a grown woman make, somewhere between a gasp and a swallowed prayer.

"I have to call her parents," Yara said. Their voice sounded far away to them, a voice heard through a wall. "I have to call someone. Child services. A doctor. Someone who—"

"And say what?" Ruth asked, and it was gentle, which made it cruel. "That a child speaks in two voices? That she sits at a desk that isn't on any roster? They'll take her away, Yara. To a hospital, maybe. To doctors who'll write things down about her in files that will follow her forever. Or—" Ruth's voice dropped. "Or the council hears about it first, and then she stops coming back at all. Like the others. Like Tomas."

The name landed between them and neither picked it up.

"There has to be something between doing nothing and destroying her," Yara said.

"There is," Ruth said. "There's the well. There's the moon. There's nine days." She looked at her watch, then closed her eyes briefly, as if the numbers had personally offended her. "Nine days until the harvest moon, Yara. It's never done this before. Taken a living child into itself like a—it's never needed a *puppet* before. Something's changed. Something's making it impatient."

"Or someone is," Yara said, and thought of Elder Corliss's smooth face, of the offer that had come wrapped in generosity, of Pru's name spoken in a room full of men who calculated children the way farmers calculate seed corn.

Ruth left. Yara stayed at the window until the bell rang, watching a small figure in a yellow coat sit motionless on a bench while autumn happened around her.

* * *

The afternoon was worse, because the afternoon required teaching.

Yara stood at the board and wrote the date and drew the reading groups on the rotation chart and asked questions and received answers, and all the while the eleventh desk sat in their peripheral vision like a wound they couldn't stop probing. Pru participated. That was the horror of it—she *participated*. Her hand went up. Her answers were correct. When Yara called on her, she read aloud fluently, in her own single voice, and for whole minutes at a time Yara could almost believe the morning had been a trick of exhaustion and dread, that grief and insomnia had finally finished the job of unhinging them.

Then, mid-lesson, mid-sentence, Pru would stop. Her head would tilt—that slow hinged tilt—and her lips would move, and the second voice would surface underneath hers like something rising through dark water, and she would whisper a fragment of a name, backward, a syllable or two, *silo-hcroc*, *kcoltihw*, and then she would blink and continue reading exactly where she'd left off, and the class would not react, because the class had decided long ago, in some animal consensus Yara would never be party to, that the eleventh desk was not to be seen.

At 2:40, during art, the children drew pictures. Free draw, crayons and paper, the noise and chaos of twenty-six children making things. Yara collected the drawings at the end, and most were the usual inventory of eight-year-old interior life: dogs, houses, rockets, a surprisingly detailed excavator.

Pru's drawing was of the classroom.

She had drawn it from above, the way children do, every desk visible, every figure facing the reader. Ten children at ten desks. Yara at the front, holding a book. And at the eleventh desk, drawn in careful purple crayon, a figure wearing a coat—but not Pru's yellow coat. A long dark coat, buttoned to the throat, and above the collar of it, where a face should be, Pru had drawn nothing at all. Just paper. Just blankness, colored over so many times the wax had built up into a smooth dark shine.

Underneath, in Pru's careful eight-year-old letters: **SHE IS WEARING MY COAT AT NIGHT.**

Yara looked up. Pru was washing her hands at the sink, humming a skipping rhyme, in one voice, sweet and ordinary, and the hum threaded seamlessly down into the floorboards and became, for three seconds, indistinguishable from the hum beneath the school, and then separated again, a child's tune once more.

Yara folded the drawing and put it inside their copy of *The Westing Game*, in the faculty bookshelf, behind the others. Evidence. Prayer. Both.

* * *

Yara drove home in the blue hour with the windows cracked because the car smelled wrong to them—too warm, too breathed-in—and the radio found only static until they crossed the creek bridge, when it abruptly cleared, as if the town's borders were also the signal's.

Grandma Odette was in the kitchen when Yara came in, and one look at Yara's face stopped her hands over the potatoes she was peeling.

"Pru came back today," Yara said.

Odette went still. Not pale—Yara had seen her pale, in the chapter of this story where the photographs grew extra chairs. This was different. This was the stillness of a woman hearing a clock strike an hour she had spent fifty years pretending wouldn't come.

"Where is she sitting?" Odette asked.

Yara told her. Odette set down the peeler with exaggerated care, the way you set down something when you don't trust your own hands, and she looked out the kitchen window at the darkening yard, and she said, "In my day, when it took a seat, we had until the moon. The moon was the deadline. The moon was *mercy*." Her voice frayed on the last word. "It's never reached into a living child before. Never worn one like—"

She stopped. Yara waited. Outside, the wind moved through the bare maples, and somewhere down the street a dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

"Unless," Odette said slowly, "it isn't reaching. Unless it's *answering*. Someone offered it something, Yara. Someone went to it early, hungry, ahead of the moon, and put a name in its ear. And it's trying the name on." She turned from the window, and her eyes were wet and furious and terrified, all three at once, a storm in a small face. "You recorded Corliss. You told me. Where is the recording?"

Yara's blood went cold in a slow tide, from scalp to sternum.

"In my notebook," they said. "With everything else. In my bag."

"Go and look," Odette said. "Right now. Go and look and tell me it's there."

Yara went up the stairs two at a time, down the hall, to the room that had been theirs for two months, to the bag beside the desk where they kept the notebooks, the photographs, the phone with the recording of Elder Corliss's voice saying *give it the girl's name and walk away rich*—

The bag was open.

Yara had left it zipped. Yara always left it zipped; they were compulsive about it, documenting even their own habits. The bag stood open like a mouth, and inside, the notebooks were gone. All of them. Every page of documentation, every attendance anomaly, every photograph of bleeding ink and tally marks and the warm bricked door. The phone lay where they'd left it, but when Yara thumbed it awake, the recording was gone from the voice memos, deleted so cleanly there wasn't even a ghost of it in the recently-deleted folder, as if it had never existed, as if the town's records had *agreed*.

Everything gone. Everything except—

Yara stood in the middle of the room, breathing, and thought of the faculty bookshelf, and *The Westing Game*, and a folded drawing hidden behind the paperbacks in a classroom nine days from the harvest moon.

Downstairs, Odette was calling their name, and her voice had the doubled quality of fear, one voice and the echo of an older one, and outside, far off across the fields, so faint it might have been the wind or might have been pipes or might have been eleven throats and one more learning its part, something beneath Marrow Creek began—very softly, very patiently—to sing.
