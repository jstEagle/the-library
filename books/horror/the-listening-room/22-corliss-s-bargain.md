## 22. Corliss's Bargain

The note came the way everything came to me in Marrow Creek: quietly, and with someone else's handwriting.

It was tucked under my windshield wiper in the school parking lot, a square of heavy cream paper, folded once. *Mr. Almeida. A word at your convenience. The feed store on Route 9 closes at six. — E.C.*

No threat in it. No warmth either. Just the flat confidence of a man who had never once been told no by anyone who mattered.

I should have thrown it away. I want that on the record, wherever records go after this one—into whatever drawer, whatever ledger, whatever listening room receives them. I should have driven home, locked Odette's crooked door, and let Elder Corliss stand in an empty parking lot wondering where his leverage had gone.

But Ruth's ledger was still folded against my ribs, inside my coat, where I'd carried it for three days like a second heartbeat. Eleven names struck through. One blank line waiting. And Pru hadn't come back to school since the morning she'd recited those names backward in a voice that wasn't entirely hers, her mouth moving half a beat out of sync with the words, like a film dubbed badly into its own language.

So at ten minutes past six, when the lights in Hollis Corliss's feed store went dark except for one lamp behind the counter, I pushed open the door he had left, pointedly, unlocked.

* * *

The store smelled of grain dust and creosote and something older underneath, something mineral, like the air in a cave that has never seen weather. Sacks of seed corn were stacked to the ceiling in the back. Horse tack hung from pegs along one wall, bridles dangling slack-jawed. And everywhere, on every surface—the counter, the nail bins, the windowsills—there were photographs.

Framed photographs of Marrow Creek. The school in 1952. The harvest festival in 1968, paper lanterns strung between poles. A groundbreaking ceremony, men in fedoras holding silver shovels over a rectangle of raw earth. In every single photograph, the same faces recurred, aging and unaging: broad, satisfied, land-rich faces. Founding-family faces. And in the corner of each frame, small as a signature, sat a brass plaque I hadn't noticed at first:

*EST. 1911. THE COVENANT HOLDS.*

Corliss stood behind the counter with his sleeves rolled, forearms like fence posts, silver hair cropped close to a skull that seemed built for butting through objections. He looked exactly like what he was: a man who had inherited not just money but certainty, three generations deep, the way other people inherit eye color.

"Yara Almeida." He said my name carefully, tasting it, the way you'd test a strange fruit. "Your grandmother's name was Odette Vann. Before she married. You knew that?"

"I know a lot of things now."

"I imagine you do." He gestured at the stool across the counter from him, an old milking stool, worn smooth. I didn't sit. He didn't seem offended. "You've been busy. Notebooks. Photographs. That little recorder you carry in your jacket pocket—you think nobody notices? Mr. Okafor noticed. Ruth Ellery noticed. Half the fourth grade could draw your phone from memory."

The recorder. My hand moved toward my pocket before I could stop it, and something flickered across his face—not anger. Amusement. The pleasure of a man watching a mouse find the cheese.

"Sit down, teacher," he said, softer now. "Let's talk about what happens next."

I sat. Not because he asked. Because I wanted my hands below the counter line, where they could work.

* * *

He talked for eleven minutes. I know because I counted, and because later, when I played it back, the recording ran eleven minutes and four seconds, and I have not yet decided whether those four seconds mean anything. Everything means something here. That is the first lesson Marrow Creek teaches and the last one it lets you forget.

"You're a good teacher," he began. "That's not flattery. Voss says your lesson plans are the best the district's seen in years. Your students read above level. Even the Reyes boy—" he caught himself, a hairline crack in the polish, smoothed instantly, "—even your difficult students are coming along. The town appreciates good teachers. We've had them before. We keep them, when we can."

"When you can," I repeated.

"We keep the ones who understand the arrangement." He laced his fingers together on the counter, thick knuckles like knots in rope. "You've found the ledger. Of course you have. Ruth always struck me as the sentimental type. So you know the shape of it. Every year, a name. Every year, the town prospers. No droughts worth mentioning. No bank failures. The mill stayed open through two recessions while towns twice our size went under. My grandfather signed the first covenant with a handshake and a prayer, and my father kept it, and I have kept it, and it has kept us." He spread his hands, encompassing the store, the town beyond it, the dark coming down over the fields. "Prosperity, Mr. Almeida. Real prosperity. You've seen it. You've lived in it these three months. Have you ever eaten better? Slept deeper? The apples here taste like apples did when you were a child. There's a reason."

"There's a reason," I agreed. "It's under the gym."

His jaw tightened, then released, deliberately, like a man setting down a weight he'd rather throw. "And there it is. The modern vocabulary. Under the gym. As if it were a furnace problem." He leaned forward. "Here is what I called you here to say. The council has voted—three to one, with two abstentions, and yes, I know which way your new friends abstained—to make you an offer. Formally. Generously. Once."

The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the walls of the old store, pipes ticked as they cooled, and I thought: even the buildings here hum. Even the wood. What did we build all of this on top of?

"A full year's salary," Corliss said. "Paid immediately, in cash, from the discretionary fund—which is real, and large, and has bought quiet from teachers before, though I'll deny saying so. Plus a settlement. Enough to buy a house outright anywhere you like. Portland. Seattle. Anywhere the ground stays still." He slid an envelope across the counter. It was heavy. It made a sound no envelope should make, a soft slither of many bills. "In exchange: you stop documenting. You surrender what Ruth gave you. And you leave before the moon fills out. Those are the terms. All of them. There is no negotiation on the third point."

"And if I don't leave?"

Corliss smiled. It was the worst thing I'd seen all week, and I had seen a child speak in two voices.

"Then the offer expires, and the council proceeds as it always proceeds. The fund finds a name. The name goes down. The spring comes up sweet. And Marrow Creek forgets you by planting season." He tilted his head. "Unless—"

There it was. I'd been waiting for the *unless* the way you wait for the drop on a rollercoaster, stomach already knowing.

"—unless you'd rather be part of the solution than a footnote to it." He reached beneath the counter and brought up a folder, thin, official-looking, a school district letterhead I recognized. "A recommendation. Glowing. Signed by me, countersigned by Voss. Any post in the state, yours for the asking. And one more item." He laid a single sheet of paper beside the folder, and turned it so I could read it.

It was a list. One name long.

*Pruett Marsh.*

"They'll take her," Corliss said, gently, horribly, the way you'd offer a grieving family condolences and a casserole in the same breath. "The Listener doesn't care about bloodlines—that's romantic nonsense. It cares about weight. About fit. A child who already hears it, who already sits close to the eleventh desk, who already talks to it in her head—" he tapped the name once, "—she's not a loss to it. She's a homecoming. And the town gets another fifty years. Your grandmother dies in her bed. Your students grow up rich in the only way this valley knows how. And you walk away whole, funded, and free, having solved the problem better than any of us could. Everyone will call you wise."

The store was very quiet. Grain dust drifted through the lamplight like slow snow.

"You're asking me to give you a child," I said.

"I'm asking you to do arithmetic." For the first time, something moved behind his eyes that wasn't amusement—something tired, and old, and frightened, quickly buried. "One child who was always going to hear it anyway, against a town of four hundred souls. Against your grandmother, who won't survive another winter of this, and we both know she's been writing letters to people who died years ago. Against yourself." He straightened. "The council doesn't need your consent, Mr. Almeida. The lot can fall however lots fall. But a willing hand makes the taking cleaner—it always has—and a clean taking spares everyone pain. Including her. Especially her. Think of it as hospice."

I thought of Pru's drawing. Twelve figures. Ten children, me, and the tall shape by the door, labeled in her careful, wobbling capitals: THE LISTENER. She had drawn herself looking directly out of the page, at whoever held the picture. At me. *Keep this,* her eyes said. *Remember me right.*

I thought of Tomas Reyes, enrolled for three days in October 1974, erased from every archive because he was convenient. Because no one would come asking. Because his name weighed nothing to anyone who mattered.

And I understood, finally, standing at that counter with the envelope slithering softly between us, that Elder Corliss was not offering me a bargain.

He was offering me a mirror. And in it, wearing his face, I saw exactly what I would become if I said yes.

* * *

"No," I said.

I said it quietly. I had expected to shout it. But it came out low and level, and it surprised both of us, how much room such a small word could fill.

Corliss exhaled through his nose. "Pity."

"The answer's no. It's no tonight, and it'll be no on the harvest moon, and if you come near Pruett Marsh with that list again, I will burn this town's pretty secret down to the foundation stones. I'll mail the ledger to every newspaper in the state. I'll stand in the schoolyard and read the names until my voice gives out. Do you understand me? I am not a teacher you can buy. I'm a witness. And witnesses don't sign your covenants."

Something shifted in his posture—a settling, like a house adjusting on its foundations. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped its charm entirely, and what was underneath was worse: administrative calm. Procedural. The voice of a man reading terms aloud at a foreclosure.

"Then you should know the council's fallback position." He slid the envelope back toward himself, unhurried. "If the lot falls badly this year—if the debt comes due and goes unpaid—the covenant doesn't simply lapse. Covenants collect. Interest, Mr. Almeida. Fifty-one years of interest, compounded. My grandfather's generation understood that. They wrote it into the founding charter, in ink that isn't ink anymore." He looked at me with something almost like pity, and pity from this man was the most frightening thing in the room. "You've been reading our records. Then you've read what happened in 1938, the one year the taking failed. Ask your grandmother about the winter of '38. Ask her why the church register for that year is burned, and why the oldest families don't say the word *February* out loud."

The lamp flickered. Just once. Just enough.

"You have until the moon sets," Corliss said. "They all do. Even the girl." He picked up a pencil and, with terrible casualness, wrote a small check mark beside Pruett Marsh's name, then drew a single line through it—struck through, the way Ruth's ledger struck its names, the way a gravedigger might mark a plot reserved. "Consider your position, teacher. Consider whose name is easier to lose."

I walked out into the dark with my heart going like a trapped bird and my hand closed around my phone in my pocket, and only when I was three fields away, standing under the cold beginning bulk of the waxing moon, did I let myself shake.

Because I had recorded all of it. Every word. The offer, the envelope, the name on the list, the check mark, the threat dressed as bookkeeping. Eleven minutes and four seconds of Elder Hollis Corliss proposing, in his own voice, in his own store, the sale of a nine-year-old girl.

They had stolen my notebooks. They had taken my photographs, my documentation, my evidence—everything I'd built to protect myself and Pru and the memory of Tomas Reyes. They thought they had disarmed me.

They had taught me the lesson instead: paper burns. Ink bleeds. Ledgers get surrendered or seized.

But a voice—*their* voice, offered freely, recorded willingly—is a different kind of document. It lives in the air and in the machine and in me, all three at once, and there is no envelope fat enough to buy it back.

I played it once more under the moon, Corliss's baritone murmuring *consider whose name is easier to lose*, and I felt the fury in my chest stop churning and go still, and cold, and clear, the way water does right before it freezes solid.

Strategy, now. Not panic. Strategy.

Tomorrow I would find Odette and ask her about the winter of 1938. Tomorrow I would put the recording somewhere no council could reach—in three places, maybe four, in voices other than mine. And tomorrow I would start teaching my students something no one had asked me to teach: how to remember out loud, in chorus, so that no seizure, no bribe, no struck-through name could ever make them forget.

The moon was rising fuller over Marrow Creek, five days from harvest, and far beneath my feet—beneath the road, beneath the fields, beneath the sleeping town—I felt, or imagined I felt, the faint familiar hum change key.

Not hungry.

Attentive.

As if something down there in the dark had heard the word *no* spoken in a feed store on Route 9, and was waiting, with infinite patience, to learn what I would do next.
