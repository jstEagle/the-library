## 28. Naming the Dead

The gymnasium smelled of floor wax and old varnish, the way it always did before an assembly—two hundred bodies' worth of anticipation pressed into a room built for half that. Yara stood backstage near the equipment cage, holding a single sheet of paper in both hands, and watched the folding chairs fill.

They had come. That was the thing that surprised them most. When they'd requested the assembly slot from Principal Voss—"a heritage presentation," they'd called it, and Voss had smiled his practiced smile and signed the form without reading it, because he believed Yara had already been bought or broken—the request had gone home in Friday folders to every family in Marrow Creek. *Marrow Creek Primary Honors Its Founding Families.* The council had even approved it. Elder Corliss himself had been quoted in the newsletter: *"A celebration of our town's long memory."*

Yara intended to give them exactly that.

*Long memory.* They had no idea how long.

Backstage, Ruth Ellery stood with her arms crossed tight, her cardigan buttoned wrong. She hadn't recanted—not fully, not yet—but she hadn't spoken to Yara in three days either, and when their eyes met now she looked away toward the bleachers where her own students sat.

"You don't have to do this," Yara said quietly.

"I know." Ruth's voice was flat as slate. "That's not why I'm here."

"Then why?"

Ruth was quiet for a long moment. Down on the floor, Mr. Okafor dragged the last of the folding chairs into rows, moving with the deliberate slowness of a man who wanted very much to be somewhere else but refused to leave. His left eye was still swollen faintly yellow at the edge—a souvenir, Yara suspected, from wherever the council had kept him.

"Because my grandmother is on that list," Ruth said finally. "Not as a bargain-maker. As a *classmate*. 1974. She died when I was nine, and nobody ever told me she'd spent her last years asking questions about a boy who disappeared." She swallowed. "If you're going to read them, someone should be standing here who isn't afraid of you."

"I'm afraid of me," Yara said.

"That makes two of us."

* * *

Voss introduced them at 10:15 exactly, his voice warm and resonant through the PA system, all civic pride and handshake confidence. "Teachers like Yara Almeida are why Marrow Creek remains the finest small community in the county. Please welcome them."

Applause, scattered and polite. Parents in quilted vests and work jackets. Children cross-legged on the gym floor in the front rows, Pru among them—or what remained of Pru. She sat perfectly still between two classmates, her hands folded in her lap, her eyes fixed on Yara with an attention that had nothing childlike left in it. Since her return, she spoke only in that doubled voice, only after dark, only names. But today, in daylight, she simply watched. Waiting, Yara thought, for her own name to be said back to her.

Yara walked to the microphone. Adjusted it. Let the silence stretch until the shuffling stopped.

"When Principal Voss asked me to prepare a presentation about Marrow Creek's history," they began, "I went looking for records. Enrollment ledgers. Photographs. Attendance sheets. The ordinary paperwork of an ordinary school." A pause. "What I found instead was a photograph from 1974. A class photo, taken on these grounds, of a fourth-grade class. It shows eleven children."

A ripple of unease moved through the adult rows. Someone coughed. Near the back, a man in a flannel shirt—Yara recognized him vaguely from the hardware store—shifted his weight as though the folding chair had grown teeth.

"The enrollment ledger for that same class lists ten students." Yara held up the photocopy, let it catch the light. "Ten names. Ten attendance records. Ten report cards filed and archived. And yet here are eleven faces, smiling, on picture day, October 1974." They set the paper down on the podium. "I've spent this semester trying to learn the eleventh child's name. Because every child has a name. Every child deserves one. Even—*especially*—the ones we've agreed to forget."

"Is this appropriate?" a woman called out, halfway down the middle section. Her voice cracked on the word *appropriate*, which told Yara everything—that she knew, that she'd always known, that the knowing had been sitting in her chest for decades like a stone swallowed whole.

"It's appropriate," Yara said, "for the same reason funerals are appropriate. Because something here was never buried properly."

Elder Corliss rose from the front row of adults. He was seventy-something and built like fence post oak, and he did not look at Yara as he turned to face the room. "This presentation was approved as a *heritage celebration*," he said, his voice carrying the easy authority of fifty years of being obeyed. "I suggest we return to that."

"Sit down, Wendell," said a woman in the third row. Yara didn't know her name. Her hands were shaking, but her voice wasn't.

Corliss looked at her. Looked at the room. Something passed across the assembled parents—not agreement, not yet, but a loosening, a hairline fracture running through forty years of shared silence—and Corliss sat back down with his jaw working like he was chewing gravel.

Yara took a breath. Beneath the floorboards, so faint that only someone who had spent months learning to hear it could find it, the hum began to rise. Not threatening. *Attentive.*

Pru tilted her head, like a bird listening for worms under snow.

"There were eleven children in that photograph," Yara said. "I'm going to read you their names. All of them. In the order they appear on the 1974 class roster—which, I should say, exists in exactly one copy, hidden in a library book where someone hoped it would stay forgotten." They unfolded the sheet. Their hands were steady. They had rehearsed this at Odette's kitchen table at midnight, over and over, while the clocks ticked and the extra chair stood against the wall and neither of them looked at it.

"Margaret Corliss."

In the front row, a heavyset man in his fifties made a sound like he'd been struck. Margaret Corliss. His mother's name. He would have known her as a woman who never came to town meetings, who kept her curtains drawn every October.

"Dale Hutchins."

An older couple near the bleachers clasped hands. Dale Hutchins—there was a Dale Hutchins buried in the churchyard, dead at nineteen in a truck accident, and his mother had never once, in thirty years of widowhood, spoken his childhood name aloud.

"Susan Okafor."

Mr. Okafor, standing by the equipment cage, closed his eyes. His father had bricked the door. His aunt had been taken before that—or after; the order hardly mattered anymore. His shoulders shook once, and stilled.

"Peter Voss."

Principal Voss, seated at the side of the stage, went the color of uncooked dough. His grandfather's name. His family had run this school for three generations, and he had signed the harvest fund disbursements every October with a fountain pen he kept in his top drawer, and he had never once asked himself what the money was *for*.

"Ellery Anne Boyd."

Ruth made a small noise behind her hand. Not her grandmother's surname—but her grandmother's maiden name, the one on the birth certificate, the one that had been changed within a year, quietly, the way things were changed in Marrow Creek.

"Jonah Reyes."

A murmur ran through the room. *Reyes.* The name was a live wire. Migrant families still came through the valley every autumn, still picked apples along the ridge, and everyone knew that name the way you know a scar—without wanting to remember how you got it.

"Thomas Aldous Pruett."

Pru's grandmother, in the fourth row, began quietly to weep. Pru did not turn around. Pru was watching Yara with those still, borrowed eyes, and her lips were moving—silent, synchronized, saying each name a half-second before Yara spoke it, as if she were reading ahead from some page only she could see.

"Lena Marsh."

"Hattie Boone."

"Samuel Corliss, Junior."

And then Yara paused. The gymnasium had gone absolutely silent except for the weeping, scattered now like rain across the rows, and beneath it all the hum had swelled to something almost melodic, almost tender, a lullaby pitched below hearing.

Yara looked up from the page and found Corliss staring at them with an expression that was no longer anger. It was pleading. *Don't,* the expression said. *Not that one. Anything but that one.*

Yara had spent weeks hating this man. Standing here, they found the hatred had burned down to something colder and more useful: clarity.

"And the eleventh child," Yara said, "the one whose face appears in the photograph but whose name appears nowhere—in no ledger, no archive, no grave, no memory anyone in this town has been willing to keep alive for fifty years—was named **Tomas Reyes**."

The hum stopped.

Not faded. *Stopped.* Cut off mid-breath like a throat closed by a hand, and the silence that rushed into its place was so total, so physical, that several people gasped. It pressed against the eardrums. It filled the gymnasium the way water fills a glass. Two hundred people sat inside a silence that had weight and temperature and intention, and in that silence Yara heard—everyone heard—a single sound from beneath the floor, deep under the foundations, deeper than pipes, deeper than bedrock:

A long, slow exhalation. Like something setting down a burden carried for fifty years.

One second. Five. Ten. Twenty. The clock above the scoreboard ticked audibly, each tick a small detonation. Thirty seconds. Forty. A woman in the back was whispering *oh God, oh God* into her hands. Fifty seconds. Pru's lips had stopped moving. For the first time since her return, her face was entirely her own—wide-eyed, terrified, *awake*—and she mouthed one word at Yara:

*More.*

Fifty-eight. Fifty-nine. Sixty.

On the sixtieth second, the hum resumed—but different. Softer. Thinner. Like a radio station drifting out of range, like a voice growing hoarse from disuse. And Yara understood, with a certainty that settled into their bones like cold water, what had just happened.

It had heard its own name. Tomas's name. Spoken freely, publicly, over the ground where he'd been stolen. For one full minute, the Listener had stopped listening for what it was owed—

Because for one full minute, it had finally been given something else.

* * *

The assembly dissolved into chaos that wore the costume of order. Voss took the microphone and thanked everyone for coming in a voice that cracked twice; parents gathered their children with the frantic gentleness of people herding animals before a flood. The woman who had told Corliss to sit down stood in the aisle holding her coat without putting it on, staring at the floor as if she could see through it. The heavyset man—Margaret Corliss's son—walked straight up to Yara at the edge of the stage, red-eyed, and gripped their hand hard enough to hurt.

"My mother used to stand at the window every October," he said. "Every October, all night. We thought it was grief for my father." His voice broke. "It wasn't, was it."

"No," Yara said gently. "I don't think it was."

He nodded, wiped his face with his sleeve, and walked out into the bright cold morning like a man leaving a courtroom.

Corliss did not speak to anyone. He rose during the final applause, straightened his jacket with two sharp tugs, and marched up the center aisle with his spine rigid and his face gray, and just before the doors he stopped—stopped dead, as if the floor itself had snagged him—and looked back at the gymnasium, at the children, at the floor beneath the children, and Yara saw his mouth move around words too quiet to carry. Whether it was a curse or a prayer, they couldn't tell. Then he shoved through the double doors and was gone, and the doors swung shut behind him with a boom that echoed like a lid closing.

Ruth appeared at Yara's elbow. Her cardigan was still buttoned wrong. Her eyes were wet and furious.

"Fifty years," she said. "Fifty years, and it took one substitute teacher with a piece of paper." She laughed, a broken sound. "You know what the worst part is? Half those people in there felt *relief*. You could see it. Like a splinter coming out."

"They'll feel worse before they feel better."

"Oh, I know." Ruth's laugh died. She glanced toward the doors Corliss had gone through. "Wendell won't take this lying down. This was his whole architecture, Yara. His family's name is woven through every lie in that ledger. Men like him don't grieve. They *retaliate*."

"I know."

"Do you?" Ruth caught Yara's arm, lowered her voice. "The moon's nearly full. Whatever he does next, he'll do it fast, and he'll do it in the dark, and he will tell himself it's for the good of the town while he's doing it. Get your documentation somewhere safe. Get your grandmother somewhere safe. And Yara—" her grip tightened— "*don't sleep alone.*"

She released them and walked away, and Yara stood alone at the edge of the emptying stage, the sheet of names still in their hand, slightly damp now where their palms had sweated through it.

Down on the gym floor, Mr. Okafor was stacking chairs. He worked alone, methodically, and when he reached the eleventh desk—the one that had stood unclaimed since Yara's first day, the one no roster explained—he paused, laid his palm flat on its surface the way you'd touch a sleeping child's forehead, and then folded it up and carried it, gently, to the storage closet.

The hum beneath the floor had thinned to almost nothing. Almost a memory.

But as Yara gathered their notes and walked out through the locker corridor toward the parking lot, they passed the bricked-over door beneath the gym stairs—the warm one, the one with the tally marks—and stopped.

The mortar was cool for the first time since they'd found it.

And scratched beside the eleven tallies, in mortar-dust still fresh enough to smudge, was a twelfth mark. Not cut deep like the others. Drawn light. Tentative. The way a child practices writing their own name before they're sure anyone will want to read it.

Yara stood in the dim corridor a long time, listening to the building breathe, and understood that whatever came next—Corliss, the council, the retaliation Ruth had promised—it would come tonight.

Above them, through the high windows, the afternoon sun was already leaning west, and the moon was waiting behind it, one night shy of full.
