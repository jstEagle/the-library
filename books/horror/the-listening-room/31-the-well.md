## 31. The Well

They left the house at half past four, while the light still held some amber in it, because Odette insisted they walk out to the school in daylight and stand at the well before dusk made it into something else.

"You want to know what you're walking toward," she said, buttoning her coat with hands that would not quite steady. "You want your eyes to have met it once, kindly, before you meet it again in the dark."

Yara had argued—briefly, uselessly—that there was no reason to go early, that they should conserve themselves for tomorrow's descent, and Odette had looked at them the way she had looked at them their whole life: the look that said *I buried a husband, I raised your mother, I burned photographs of my own kitchen, do not tell me about reasons.*

So they walked.

Okafor came with them, carrying his father's blueprint rolled in a cardboard tube like a diploma he was ashamed of. He had not spoken much since returning two nights ago with a split lip and bruised wrists and the rolled paper under his arm, but his silence had changed quality. It was no longer the silence of a man keeping a secret. It was the silence of a man who has set something heavy down and is listening to see if the floor holds.

The town was quiet in the way Marrow Creek was always quiet on harvest eve—not empty quiet, but *held* quiet, the quiet of a congregation between hymns. Porch lights were already on though the sun hadn't set. Yara counted them without meaning to. Every house on Alder Street had one lit. Every house except the Corliss place, which stood dark behind its iron fence like a pulled tooth.

"They're all home," Odette murmured, following Yara's gaze. "They're all waiting too. They just won't say what for."

The school rose at the end of the lane, brick gone soft-edged in the failing light, its windows catching the last of the sun so that the whole building seemed to glow faintly from within, like a lantern with something alive inside it. The hum was audible from fifty yards out. Yara had learned to live inside it these past weeks the way you learn to live near train tracks—but tonight it was different. Tonight it wasn't a hum. It was closer to breathing. Long, slow, patient inhalations that seemed to come up through the ground itself, through the soles of their shoes, through the bones of their ankles.

"It knows we're coming," Okafor said. Not a question.

"Of course it knows," Odette said. "It's been knowing for fifty years."

* * *

The well sat behind the gymnasium, past the dead garden where the playground gave way to weeds, in a shallow bowl of ground the town had never built on. Yara understood why now. You don't build over a mouth.

It was older than the school—older than anything else in Marrow Creek, probably. A ring of fieldstone, mortared and remortared across generations, waist-high, with a wooden frame above it and a crank whose handle had been worn to a shine by hands long dead. The stones were furred with moss on the north side. On the south side, facing the school, the mortar was cracked in a pattern that didn't look like settling. It looked like claw-marks. Or like something that had pushed, once, very hard, from below.

Odette stopped ten feet away and would come no closer. She stood with her arms wrapped around herself, a small woman grown smaller, and Yara saw her lips moving—not praying, exactly. Reciting. The rules, maybe. The terms. *A name returned willingly, spoken over the theft-site, before the moon sets.* She had been reciting them for three days like a woman rehearsing testimony.

Okafor went to the well's edge first. He moved carefully, the way you'd approach a dog of unknown temper, and leaned over the rim just far enough to look down.

"Well," he said, and then again, softer, as if correcting himself: "*Well.*"

"What?" Yara crossed to him.

He stepped back to give them room, and Yara looked down into the dark.

The well was deep—deeper than any well had a right to be behind an elementary school. The flashlight beam Okafor clicked on fell down a stone throat and did not find bottom; it found instead a curve of wall, then darkness, then nothing, the light simply giving up the way sound gives up in snow. But it was the rope that stopped Yara's breath.

The rope hung down the center of the shaft, thick hemp, old, and it was *frayed*. Not worn through from age or weather—the fraying started six feet below the rim and continued downward, fibers splayed outward, shredded, *pushed apart from the inside*. As if something climbing up had gripped it with more strength than hemp could bear. As if something had climbed—how far? Yara leaned closer, heart slamming, and followed the damage down by flashlight: frayed at six feet, frayed at ten, frayed at fifteen—and then, at roughly twenty feet down, the fibers changed. Below that point the rope was intact. Whole. Untouched.

Something had climbed partway up. And then it had gone back down.

"Why would it stop?" Yara whispered. Their voice fell into the shaft and did not echo. The well ate it.

Okafor's jaw worked. "Because it doesn't take," he said finally. "That's the thing nobody says out loud, the thing my father couldn't make himself say even at the end. It never takes. It never climbs out and drags anyone anywhere. It climbs up to the edge of the world and it *waits* to be let the rest of the way. Fifty years, and it's never once taken a step it wasn't invited to take." He laughed, a terrible small sound with no humor in it. "We built a whole town on top of a thing that only ever wanted to be asked."

"That's not mercy," Odette said from behind them. Her voice was thin and hard. "Don't you dare call that mercy. That's worse. That's making us do it ourselves and calling it our idea."

"I know," Okafor said quietly. "I know, Mrs. Almeida. My father knew too. That's why he drank."

The sun touched the tree line while they stood there, and the light went from amber to copper to a bruised violet, and the well's shadow lengthened across the dead garden until it pointed at the school like a finger.

* * *

They did what they had come to do, which was mostly to look, and looking took longer than any of them expected, because none of them could stop.

Yara circled the well twice, photographing it—old habit, the documentation reflex that had become prayer without their noticing. The camera's flash lit the interior in stroboscopic bursts, and in those frozen instants Yara saw things the continuous dark hid: a ledge of stone partway down, wide enough for a person to stand on. Scratches in the wall below it, deliberate ones, arranged in rows. Tally marks. Dozens of them. More than eleven. More than fifty years' worth.

Counting what? Yara wondered, and then decided they did not want the answer tonight. Some answers were doors best left shut until morning.

Odette laid her palm flat on the well's rim, on the cold stone, and kept it there for a long moment, eyes closed. When she opened them they were wet.

"My mother brought me here when I was nine," she said. "Told me never to lean over. Never to drop anything in. Never to say my name near it." A pause. "She never told me why. And I hated her a little, my whole life, for all the things she wouldn't say. And then I grew up and became her." She looked at Yara, and the love in her face was so naked it hurt. "I'm sorry. For all of it. For 1974 most of all, but for every year after, every year I chose the quiet lie over the loud truth. If tomorrow goes wrong—if it takes you instead of taking my apology—"

"It won't," Yara said.

"You can't know that."

"No," Yara admitted. "But I know this: Tomas Reyes waited fifty years in a room under a school, and nobody came. Nobody said his name. Nobody even remembered he'd existed. If the price of fixing that is my fear, then the price is cheap, Grandma. The price is nothing."

Odette pressed her hand to her mouth. Behind her, Okafor turned away and studied the gymnasium wall as if it were fascinating, giving the two of them the privacy of his turned back.

Dusk settled fully. The first star came out over the school's roofline, and beneath it, faint as a pulse under skin, the hum deepened.

* * *

It was Okafor who heard it first.

"Stop," he said. "Both of you. Listen."

They listened. Crickets. Wind in the dead garden stalks. The distant tick of the school's cooling boiler. And underneath it all, rising up through the well's open throat like smoke:

Singing.

A child's voice—or many children's voices braided into one, the way Pru's voice had been braided with the other thing's voice in the classroom. It was a skipping rhyme, the kind chanted over hopscotch, the kind every generation learns without being taught. Yara recognized the tune from their own childhood, from playgrounds a thousand miles from Marrow Creek, which meant the rhyme was older than the town, older than the bargain, maybe older than the well. The words drifted up thin and broken:

*One for the sorrow, two for the mow,*
*three for the name we never told, so—*

The verse stopped. Not faded. Stopped, mid-line, the way a record stops, and the silence that followed was worse than the singing, because in the silence Yara could hear the rope creaking. Gently. Rhythmically. Swaying, though there was no wind down a stone shaft.

As if something had just let go of it.

Odette made a sound low in her throat. Okafor's hand found the crank handle and gripped it, knuckles white, though cranking the bucket up would mean winding the frayed rope through his own fingers, and all three of them knew, without discussing it, that they were not going to touch the rope. The rope belonged to the thing below now. It had climbed it. It had held it. It had left its grip written in the ruined fibers like a signature.

"We should go back," Odette said. "Get off this ground before full dark."

"In a minute." Yara knelt at the rim, close enough to feel the well's breath on their face—warm air rising, smelling of chalk dust and pond water and something sweeter underneath, something like apples left too long in a cellar. "Tomas," they said quietly, into the dark. Just the name. No ritual, no ceremony. The way you'd say a name across a classroom, taking attendance.

The humming stopped.

For three heartbeats the whole night held its breath—the crickets, the wind, the boiler, everything—and then, from twenty feet down, from the depth where the fraying ended and the untouched rope began, came a sound Yara would carry for the rest of their life however long that turned out to be.

A small voice. Clear. Unbraided, unblended, singular—a boy's voice, alone, speaking for the first time in fifty years:

"*You said it right.*"

Then, softer, almost shy, the way a child asks a question they've been holding all day:

"*Are you coming down?*"

Yara's eyes burned. "Tomorrow," they said. "I'm bringing your name back tomorrow. Your real name, in your real records, and your family's picture. I promise."

Silence. Then the humming resumed, but changed—it was lighter now, quicker, and Yara realized with a jolt that it sounded like someone humming while they worked, while they tidied, while they *prepared a room for a guest.*

Okafor helped Yara to their feet. Odette was already retreating up the slope, not running—she would never run—but walking with the urgent dignity of a woman who has spent fifty years keeping promises to the wrong things and will not break the one she's finally made to the right thing.

They walked back through the dead garden as the last light died. At the edge of the playground Yara turned for one final look.

The well stood black against the violet sky. And hanging above it, just clearing the eastern tree line, swollen and low and the color of a fresh scar, the harvest moon had begun to rise—one night early, huge with anticipation, already pulling at the tide of the earth.

Behind them, faint but unmistakable, carried up the well and across the dead garden, eleven voices began to sing the skipping rhyme again from the beginning.

And this time, Yara noticed, there was a twelfth voice learning the words.
