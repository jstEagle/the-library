## 2. Wren on the Porch

The first frost came on a Thursday, and Felix knew it before she opened her eyes.

Not because of the cold — the cottage's baseboard heaters had been fighting a losing war all week, ticking and groaning like old men asked to carry furniture. She knew it the way she'd known things as a child, in that pre-rational register she'd spent thirty years training out of herself: a pressure behind the sternum, a hush. The particular silence of a world holding its breath.

She got up anyway. Made coffee. Stood at the kitchen window with her hands around the mug and watched the light come up gray over the fields, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the season, just the altitude, just the fact that she'd moved three thousand miles to a town where the grocery store closed at five and the church bell rang the hour whether anyone wanted it or not.

Rationalism was her armor. She'd built it piece by piece through graduate school, through her mother's long illness, through every moment when believing something too hard had hurt her. Cause, effect, evidence. A ledger was a ledger. A dead radiator was a dead radiator. A town that lost children was a town with bad roads and worse cell service, and grief made people strange about it, that was all.

She repeated this to herself while she graded her sophomores' essays on *Wuthering Heights*, most of which argued that Heathcliff was misunderstood. She repeated it while the light thickened and thinned again, while the afternoon went blue at the edges, while the temperature outside dropped past numbers she'd only ever seen on weather apps for places she'd never lived.

At 4:47, Mrs. Aldous stopped by.

Felix heard the car first — the principal drove an enormous Buick that sounded like a barge docking — and opened the door before the knock. Aldous stood on the step holding a covered casserole dish, silver hair pinned back, face arranged into its usual expression of competent kindness.

"Beef and barley," she said. "It's tradition. First frost, every new teacher gets fed."

"That's kind of you."

"It's practical." Aldous handed over the dish but didn't let go right away. Her grip was dry and strong. "Felix. I want you to hear this from me and not from the diner gossip. Tonight will be cold. Colder than you're used to. Keep your doors locked, keep your curtains drawn after dark, and if you hear anything outside — anything at all — don't open it."

Felix laughed, because the alternative was asking what she meant. "Bears?"

"Something like bears." Aldous released the dish. For a moment her eyes went somewhere else — past Felix, into the house, or into some other house entirely, some other year — and then came back. "You'll do fine. You seem like a woman who keeps her doors shut."

"I keep my windows open," Felix said. "For the writing."

Something flickered across Aldous's face. Not quite disapproval. Recognition, maybe. "Well," she said. "Mind how you use them."

Then she was gone, the Buick grinding away down the lane, and Felix stood in her doorway with a casserole and a warning, feeling like she'd been handed both halves of a sentence whose beginning someone had torn off.

* * *

Night came early and absolute.

By six the dark was total, pressing against the glass, and the frost began. Felix watched it happen from her desk by the window — watched the fern-patterns climb the pane from the corners inward, delicate as lace, patient as handwriting. She'd seen frost before, obviously. But this was different. This frost didn't spread randomly. It grew in spirals, in branching lines that forked and forked again, and there was something about the rhythm of it, the *cadence*, that snagged at a memory she couldn't reach.

She wrote until nine. Then the heater died — a final clank, a rattle, silence — and the cold came into the house like it had been waiting for permission.

Felix dug out sweaters, found the breaker box, discovered nothing wrong with it. The temperature kept falling. By ten her breath was visible indoors, and the frost on the window had reached the center of the glass, and she stood looking at it with her coffee gone cold in her hand and thought: *that looks like letters.*

Not did look. Did not look. She corrected herself firmly. Frost looked like feathers, like ferns, like nothing at all. It did not look like letters, and even if it had, letters were what human brains did — pattern-matching machines, meaning-hungry, desperate to find faces in wallpaper and words in static. She'd taught a whole unit on pareidolia. She could diagnose herself perfectly well.

The knock came at eleven.

Three soft raps. Polite. Almost shy.

Felix went still. The cottage sat alone at the end of Larkspur Lane; the nearest neighbor was a quarter mile off; no one walked these roads at night in this cold without reason. Aldous's voice was in her head — *don't open it* — reasonable, warm, and somehow exactly wrong, the way a lie is wrong when it's wearing true clothes.

Another knock. And then, muffled through the door, a voice she knew.

"Felix? It's me."

Her body reacted before her mind did — a full-body jolt, coffee sloshing, heart slamming. Because she knew that voice. She would have known it underwater, under anesthesia, under thirty years of deliberate forgetting. It was high and clear and slightly hoarse at the edges, a child's voice with an old soul's patience wound through it, and the last time she'd heard it she was seven years old, sitting cross-legged in a closet in Ohio, talking to nobody.

Talking to Wren.

"No," Felix said aloud, to the empty room. "No, absolutely not."

The voice came again, closer to the door now, gentle. "It's cold out here, Feffy. I've been waiting such a long time."

*Feffy.* Only one creature on earth had ever called her that — a mispronunciation of her own name that she'd found embarrassing at six and sacred by eight, a secret name that existed nowhere except between her and the friend she'd invented, or who had invented himself, or who had simply arrived one summer afternoon in the hedge behind her grandmother's garden and refused, for four years, to leave.

Felix crossed the room without deciding to. Her hand was on the latch. Some part of her stood back and observed, clinically, taking notes: *subject exhibits dissociative episode following isolation, stress, sleep deprivation; subject is opening her door at eleven p.m. in subzero temperatures to speak with a hallucination; subject is, notably, still doing it.*

She opened the door.

The cold hit her like a wall of water. Beyond the porch light's yellow cone the yard ran white and glittering to the treeline, and the frost hung in the air itself, tiny suspended stars, and standing on her front step — small, slight, hands folded, head tilted — was Wren.

Unchanged.

That was the thing her mind kept circling and refusing to land on. Unchanged. He was maybe eight years old, the way he'd always been maybe eight years old — bird-boned, dark-haired, eyes the gray-green of shallow water over stone. He wore the same coat he'd always worn in her imagination, a little wool thing with horn buttons, buttoned wrong the way he'd always buttoned it wrong, top button in the third hole, because Wren had never once in his existence done anything in the correct order. His breath did not fog in the cold. She noticed that. She noticed it and filed it away in the part of her brain that was already screaming.

"Hi, Feffy," Wren said, and smiled, and the smile was the listening smile, the one that made you feel like whatever you said next would be the most important thing anyone had ever said, and thirty years collapsed like a folding chair.

"You're not real," Felix said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Armor working. "I made you up. I was a lonely kid and I invented you and I grew up and I stopped. That's the whole story."

"You did make me up," Wren agreed pleasantly. "Or you remembered me. It's hard to say which comes first." He rocked up onto his toes, peering past her into the cottage with frank curiosity. "Oh, you have *books*. You always said you'd have books everywhere. Did I tell you that? That you'd be a writer?"

"You didn't tell me anything. You weren't real. Real people can't—"

"Can't wait?" Wren said. "Can't stay the same? Can't come back?" He considered each item, ticking them off with one small finger. "No. I suppose they can't."

The wind moved through the yard and did not move him. Snowflakes drifted down and passed, she was almost sure, *through* him, or landed on his shoulders without melting and without weight, and Felix stood in her doorway with her rationalism clutched around her like a coat that had suddenly developed holes.

"Why are you here?" she asked.

"Because you are." He said it simply, the way children state enormous things. "You came back to where I could find you."

"I didn't come *back*. I've never been here before in my life."

Wren tilted his head, and for a moment — just a moment — the delight in his face went somewhere older and sadder, a depth passing under ice. "Haven't you?" he said softly. "Ask your grandmother's house. Ask the ledger. Ask the frost what it writes." Then the brightness returned, seamless, total, and he beamed up at her. "Anyway. I'm here now. I'll be around. I'm very good at waiting — you remember."

"I remember you disappeared," Felix said. "When I was eleven. I stopped believing and you were just — gone. Kids do that. They grow out of it."

Something flickered in his expression again. Hurt, quickly folded away. "You stopped *listening*," he said. "It's different." He stepped backward off the porch, down onto the snow, and his feet left no prints — she saw it, she *saw* it, the unbroken white, and some final load-bearing wall inside her skepticism cracked clean through. "Lock your door tonight, Feffy. Not because of me. Never because of me."

"Wren—" Her voice cracked on the name, thirty years of it held in her mouth. "What happens on frost nights? What does the frost want?"

He was walking backward into the dark now, unhurried, hands in his pockets, the horn buttons catching the porch light. When he answered, his voice carried impossibly clearly across the frozen yard, the way sound carries over snow.

"The same thing it's always wanted, Feffy. To be *counted*." A pause. The smile, faint and terrible, hanging in the cold. "And Hollowmere has always been so good at counting."

Then the dark took him — not gradually, not at the treeline, but all at once, mid-step, like a candle pinched out — and Felix stood alone on her porch with her heart going like a trapped bird and the frost climbing the doorframe beside her shoulder in slow, deliberate spirals.

She locked the door. She checked it twice. She did not sleep.

And in the morning, on the frosted glass of the kitchen window, in lines no melt and no science could explain, someone had written a single word in the ice:

*SOON.*
