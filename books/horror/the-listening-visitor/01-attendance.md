## 1. Attendance

The bus let me off at the bottom of Hollowmere Hill because the driver said the road up was "not for buses," and I walked the rest with my suitcase wheels rattling over gravel, past hedgerows gone feral, past a sign that read HOLLOWMERE PRIMARY — EST. 1904 in paint so faded the numbers looked like they were trying to escape. The school sat at the top like something that had been set down there by someone who meant to come back for it later and never did. Grey stone. Tall windows. A bell tower with no bell in it, just an empty arch where a bell should hang, and I remember thinking: that's odd, a school without its voice.

I want to be honest in these pages. That's the whole point of them. So I'll say it plainly: on my first morning, standing in the road with my suitcase and my notebooks and my careful plans, I felt hopeful. Genuinely hopeful. A new town, a new classroom, twenty-six children whose names I hadn't learned yet — all of it unwritten, all of it mine to record from the first line. I had survived worse places than this. I had survived myself, which is harder.

I keep notebooks. Have done since I was nine years old, since the year my mother stopped being reliably present even when she was in the room. If I write everything down — every roll call, every silence, every strange detail — then nothing can dissolve on me. Nothing can transfer away in the night without leaving a record. The notebooks are not a hobby. They are a load-bearing wall.

So: Notebook One, page one, written that evening in the flat above the old post office that the school had arranged for me.

*Arrived 8:40 a.m. Weather grey, wind from the east. School larger than expected; grounds well kept except behind gymnasium (see below). Met headmaster Mr. Vane — warm handshake, warm smile, eyes that went somewhere else mid-conversation, twice.*

I'll get to Vane later. First the classroom, because the classroom came first, and because what happened there set the shape of everything after.

* * *

Room 4 smelled of chalk dust and floor wax and, underneath both, faintly of damp — the smell of a building that has been rained into for a century and has made its peace with it. My predecessor's things were still on the walls. Paper flowers, hand-cut, sagging slightly. A poster of the water cycle with a coffee ring on the ocean. And in the corner by the window, a desk.

An empty desk. Not abandoned-empty — *kept*-empty. It had been dusted. Someone had been dusting around it, carefully, the way you'd work around a shrine or a wound. The chair was tucked in. On the desktop, centered, lay a single pencil, sharpened, unused.

"Whose desk is that?" I asked the caretaker who'd shown me up — a round, apologetic man named Tullis who spoke mostly in shrugs.

He shrugged. Then he said, "Spare."

"It's got a pencil on it."

"Spare pencil."

"You've dusted it."

"We dust everything." He said it quickly, and then he left, and his footsteps down the corridor were faster than his footsteps coming up had been. I wrote that down too. Footstep tempo is data. Everything is data. That's the whole religion of it.

At nine o'clock my class came in — Year Five, twenty-five children, and I counted twice because the staff list had said twenty-six. Twenty-five children filed in and sat down, and one by one they looked at the empty desk by the window, and one by one they looked away, in a sequence so smooth and so practiced it could only have been rehearsed. Or inherited. Children inherit things like that the way they inherit eye color.

I took attendance. I do love attendance; it's the most honest ritual in teaching. Every name spoken aloud, every voice answering back, present or absent, accounted for. I called the roll and twenty-five voices answered, some bright, some mumbled, one little girl named Priya who answered in a whisper as though her name might break if she said it too loud.

Then I reached the end of the list, and there was a gap in it — a space between *Hollis* and *Iqbal* where a name had been typed and then struck through, not deleted. Struck through. You delete a typo. You strike through a name.

"Is anyone missing?" I asked. "Anyone who usually sits here?" I touched the empty desk's edge.

Twenty-five children looked at me with the flat, patient expressions of people waiting for weather to pass.

"That's nobody's desk," said a boy near the front. Marcus Hollis, per my list. He said it kindly, which was somehow worse. "It's just always there."

* * *

I found the well on my second day, behind the gymnasium, and I want to record exactly what I saw before the memory of it starts editing itself, which memories do, especially the ones that itch.

The gym is a low brick building at the north edge of the grounds, and behind it the playing field runs out toward a stand of ash trees. About forty meters short of the trees, the grass changes — grows greener, thicker, wronger, a circle of it maybe ten meters across, like the ground there is being fed something. In the middle of the circle stands a stone wellhead, low and round, older than the school, older than anything else on the property. And around the wellhead, wrapped in three concentric rings of chain-link fence, topped with coiled razor wire, is an enclosure no more than two meters square.

A well. Fenced like a prison yard. For a well.

There was a laminated sign zip-tied to the fence. MAINTENANCE ACCESS — STAFF ONLY. Below it, smaller, in different lettering, newer: DO NOT ENTER AT NIGHT. No explanation of what would happen if you did. No signature. Just the imperative, hanging there, doing its small bureaucratic work of forbidding without informing.

I stood at the fence and took notes. I noted the greenness of the grass. I noted that the fence posts were new but the concrete footings were old — the enclosure had been rebuilt at least once, recently, by someone taking it seriously. I noted that there was no rope, no bucket, no crank. The wellhead's mouth was covered with a steel plate bolted down with six bolts, and every bolt gleamed, freshly turned.

And I noted the sound. This is the part I almost didn't write down, and I'm writing it now precisely because I almost didn't. Standing there, notebook open, I heard — very faintly, from under the steel plate, from under however many meters of stone and dark water — a sound like humming. One note, held. Low. Steady. The kind of note a person hums while working, absent-minded, contented.

Wells don't hum. Wells gurgle, drip, echo. They don't hold a note.

I told myself it was wind in the fence wire. Chain-link sings in wind; I know this; every child who ever ran a stick along a fence knows this. But the wind was coming from the east and the hum was coming from below, and the two sounds did not match, and I stood there for four full minutes listening to them disagree before I made myself walk away.

In the notebook that evening: *Well behind gym. Bolted, fenced, signed. Humming? (wind — probably wind). Return with better ears.*

Better ears. As if this were a birdwatching expedition. As if the worst thing I might catch out here was a rare warbler.

* * *

The week assembled itself into routines, and routines are how I hold my shape, so I leaned into them hard. Up at six. Notebook review over tea. Walk up the hill at eight. Attendance at nine — always attendance first, names spoken aloud into the room like small anchors dropped one by one. Lessons. Lunch duty, where I learned the children's alliances and feuds the way you learn a country's map. Home by four. Notes until dark.

And I began collecting the silences. That sounds mad written down, but it's accurate: there are silences in that school that have texture, weight, direction. When I asked about my predecessor — Ms. Ferrier, whose paper flowers still sagged above me — the staffroom went quiet in a way that wasn't embarrassment. Embarrassment shuffles. This didn't shuffle. Four teachers in three years, Tullis had let slip on day one, before catching himself and shrugging it into nothing. Transfers, he said. All transfers. But when I asked Odile Marchand, the school nurse, a sharp-eyed woman with silver streaks in her dark hair who struck me as the only adult in the building who looked directly at things, she said, "Transfers," in a tone that put quotation marks around the word so firmly I could hear them click.

Odile watched me. I noticed it on day three — noticed being noticed. She didn't ask questions, but she positioned herself near me at odd moments, in corridors, at the kettle, the way you'd position yourself near a structure you suspected was settling.

On Friday, Mr. Vane stopped by Room 4. He filled the doorway the way headmasters are built to do — tall, silver-templed, hands clasped warmly behind his back. He praised my first week. He asked how I was settling. His eyes, while his mouth discussed the curriculum, moved once to the empty desk by the window and then away again, quickly, the way you look away from a scar on someone's face.

"And Ms. Ferrier," I said, smiling, keeping it light, keeping it administrative. "Did she leave anything I should know about? Lesson plans, seating notes?"

"She transferred suddenly," Vane said. "Family matter. She was very fond of that desk arrangement." A pause, half a heartbeat too long. "We all miss her."

*We all.* Spoken smoothly. Spoken like a man reciting lines he'd had occasion to recite before. Three times before, at least, if Tullis's arithmetic held.

That evening I sat in my flat with the window open to the east wind and wrote for two hours. Roll call records. The struck-through name. The dusted desk. The well and its six bright bolts and its impossible held note. The quotation marks around *transfers*. I wrote until the pages looked less like a teacher's journal and more like the opening file of an investigation, and then I sat back and read it all over, and I felt the thing I have felt only a few times in my life: the specific cold that comes when cautious hope curdles.

Because here is what I knew by Friday night, in the plain language I try to use in these pages:

Something in that school is being managed. Not maintained — *managed*, the way you manage a fire, the way you manage an animal. The desk is managed. The name is managed. The well is managed with bolts and fences and signs, and the staff's silence is managed with warmth, with Vane's warm warm warmth laid over everything like fresh snow over a dump site.

And here is what I didn't yet know, what I couldn't have known:

That Friday night, in the basement of Hollowmere Primary, a machine was running. An old machine, older than me, older than the digital age, with reels and needles and a patience I couldn't imagine. It had been recording the staff bedrooms for years — for wellness research, the paperwork said. It had recorded Ms. Ferrier. It had recorded the three before her. And on Monday morning, Odile Marchand was going to decide, for reasons of her own, that the new teacher who took too many notes ought to hear what was on those tapes.

She was going to play me a voice answering questions at three in the morning, in a register no throat should make.

But that is Monday. This is Friday. And tonight the east wind is up, and somewhere down the hill, behind the gymnasium, under six bright bolts and a steel plate, something is holding its one low note — patient, contented, waiting to see whether the new teacher keeps a diary.

It does. I mean — I do.

I keep notebooks.
