## 2. The Sleep Lab

The nurse's office smelled of eucalyptus and old paper, and Odile Ferreira smelled of both, plus something underneath — a mineral tang, like water that had been sitting in copper pipes too long. She was a broad, deliberate woman with reading glasses pushed up into gray-streaked hair and the unhurried hands of someone who had bandaged a thousand scraped knees and no longer believed any of them were emergencies.

"You're the new one," she said, not looking up from a form. "Montrose. Year Six."

"That's me." I stood in the doorway with my notebook against my chest like a shield. I'd already filled four pages that morning: the layout of the staff room, the exact wording of Mr. Vane's welcome ("We're so glad you've come to us, Yara. We've needed someone steady."), the fact that three separate teachers had used the word *steady* within an hour of meeting me.

"Steady" was not a word people chose at random for a stranger. It was a word people chose for a replacement.

Odile finally looked up, took me in — the notebook, the ink on my left ring finger, the way I hadn't blinked enough — and something shifted behind her eyes. Recognition, maybe. Or resignation.

"You document things," she said.

"I keep notes. For myself. It helps me—" I almost said *stay together*, which was true and also more than I wanted a stranger to know on my first day. "It helps me think."

"Hm." She set down her pen. "Come with me, then. If you're going to write everything down, you should write down the right things."

* * *

I expected her to lead me to the medical supply cupboard or the records room. Instead she took me past it, past the boiler room door with its yellowed asbestos warning, down a corridor I would have sworn wasn't on the floor plan Vane had given me. The walls here were older — actual plaster over brick, painted institutional green up to shoulder height and cream above, the paint gone the color of weak tea. The corridor sloped gently downward, so gradually that I only noticed because my ears popped.

"How far under the school are we?" I asked.

"Under the playground, mostly," Odile said. "This wing predates the rest. They built the school around it in 'sixty-one rather than demolish it. Cheaper." A pause. "Or so the story goes."

"What's the other story?"

She didn't answer. At the end of the corridor was a steel door, unmarked except for a laminated sign in cheerful comic sans: **WELLNESS RESEARCH — STAFF ONLY**. Someone had drawn a smiley face next to the word *wellness*. The smiley face had been there long enough for its ink to fade to gray.

Odile produced a key from the lanyard around her neck — one key among many, but this one older than the others, brass gone soft-edged with handling — and unlocked the door.

"This is the sleep lab," she said.

* * *

The room beyond was larger than I'd expected and colder than it should have been. Racks of equipment lined the walls: polygraph machines with their pens long since dried out, an EEG rig coiled with cabling like sleeping snakes, reel-to-reel tape decks stacked three high, and — incongruously modern — a shelf of small digital recorders, each labeled in Odile's blocky handwriting with names and dates. *R. ASHWORTH — RM 4. T. OKONKWO — ANNEX. D. PELL — FLAT B.*

And above all of it, running along the ceiling, a junction box with perhaps twenty cables feeding out through a bored hole in the wall, disappearing upward into the fabric of the school.

"Those go where?" I asked, though I already knew.

"The staff bedrooms," Odile said. "Every one. Has been since before I arrived, and I arrived nineteen years ago." She said it flatly, the way you'd report the weather. "Officially it's a wellness study. Sleep quality, stress markers, that sort of thing. Funded by a grant that nobody can produce paperwork for. Every few years a new headmaster rediscovers it, decides it's either scandalous or brilliant, and keeps it going."

"And the teachers know?"

"The teachers sign a consent form in their induction pack. Page forty-one. Nobody reads page forty-one." She looked at me steadily. "Did you read your induction pack, Miss Montrose?"

I thought of the box still sitting unpacked in my flat above the village shop. "No."

"No," she agreed. "So yours is recording you now. Room 3. It started when you signed the lease."

My hand went, without permission, to my notebook. Odile watched the gesture and nodded as if I'd answered a question correctly.

"There it is," she said softly. "You want to write this down. Go ahead. Everyone who finds out wants to write it down. That's the interesting part."

"What do you mean, everyone?"

She turned to the shelf of digital recorders and ran her finger along them without touching. "Ashworth kept a journal. Okonkwo kept a journal. Pell kept a journal. Detailed ones. Obsessive ones. You'd recognize the handwriting style — small, fast, pressing hard enough to emboss the next page." Her finger stopped. "They all found this room within their first month. And they all started writing more after they found it. Not less."

"Why would finding out make them write *more*?"

"Because," Odile said, "once you know someone is listening, you start performing. And once you start performing, you need an audience you control." She lifted one recorder off the shelf — *R. ASHWORTH — RM 4*, dated fourteen months ago — and held it between us like a specimen. "Do you want to hear what listening sounds like?"

I should have said no. I want to be honest about that, even if honesty is the one thing I can't afford anymore. Every instinct I'd built my careful life on said *no, put it down, go back upstairs, teach fractions, keep your head down.* But there is a particular kind of hunger in people like me — people who document, who catalog, who press flowers between pages — and it is not curiosity exactly. It's the belief that if you can just get the whole thing written down, pinned, labeled, it can't hurt you.

I said, "Yes."

* * *

Odile connected the recorder to an old amplifier with a frayed cloth-covered cable, and we sat in folding chairs facing each other across a workbench scarred with solder burns. She pressed play.

For a while, nothing. Room tone. The faint hiss of a microphone doing its patient work. Then a bed creaking — Ashworth turning over — and the small domestic sounds of a person failing to sleep: a sigh, the click of a lamp, bedsprings again.

Then Ruth Ashworth spoke.

I knew her name by then, of course. There was a mug in the staff room with her name on it that nobody used, and a timetable in the office with her name crossed out in correction fluid, and a classroom — mine now — with a desk in the corner that no child sat at and no teacher claimed. But hearing her voice was different. Hearing her voice made her a person. She sounded tired in the specific way of someone who has been tired for months and has stopped expecting anything else.

*"Are you there?"* she whispered. The timestamp in the corner of the little display read **03:12**.

Silence. Long enough that I glanced at Odile. Odile didn't glance back. She was watching the recorder.

*"I know you're there. You're always there after midnight. Are you the one taking them?"*

Another silence. And then the answer came.

I have tried, in the weeks since, to describe that sound, and every description fails, but I'll record my failures anyway because failures are data. It was a voice. It was not a throat. It had the texture of a voice heard through several feet of packed earth — muffled, resonant, arriving slightly *after* itself, as if the sound had to travel a distance to reach the microphone even though the microphone was inches from Ruth's pillow. It was low, but not a man's low; it was low the way a pipe is low, or a well. And it was answering her questions in order, patiently, the way a tutor answers a slow student.

*"Yes,"* it said. *"I am here. I am always here. I do not take. I am given."*

Ruth's breathing changed. Faster. But — and this is the part I have replayed forty times, the part that keeps me awake — she did not sound afraid. She sounded *lit up*. She sounded like I feel when a sentence finally comes out right on the page.

*"Given by whom? Who gives them to you?"*

A pause, longer than the others. When the voice returned, it seemed pleased. *"Ask me another."*

*"Why do they call it transferring? Where do they actually go?"*

*"Down,"* the voice said, and the word had weight, had *depth*, the audio equivalent of looking over a ledge. *"Down is not far. Down is only far enough."*

*"Far enough for what?"*

But here the recording degraded — or something interfered with it. The hiss swelled, and beneath the hiss I caught something else, something rhythmic, almost melodic, a pattern of sound that my brain kept trying to resolve into a tune and kept failing, the way an eye tries to focus on something too close. Ruth's voice came through it, thinner now:

*"What is that? Is that a song? Whose song is— "*

Odile stopped the tape.

The silence in the lab afterward was enormous. My ears rang with the absence. I realized I had been leaning forward hard enough that the workbench edge had printed a line across my forearms, and that my pen was in my hand and moving — I'd written nearly a full page without noticing, my shorthand, tight and fast, pressing hard enough to emboss the next page.

Odile looked at my notebook. Then at me. Then at the notebook again.

"Ashworth's looked like that too," she said quietly. "By the end."

"By the end of what?"

"By the end of the month." She stood, wound the cable with practiced loops, and set the recorder back in its place among the others. There were more of them than I'd counted at first. Dozens. Years of them. "The grant ended for her in March. That's what Vane calls it. The grant ending." She turned to face me fully, and for the first time the nurse's calm cracked along a fault line I could see all the way down. "Yara. Listen to me, because I will only say this once, and then I will deny saying it, because I have a daughter in Year Four and a mortgage and I am a coward in all the ordinary ways. That machine in your bedroom is not studying you. It is *introducing* you. And the thing downstairs—" she gestured vaguely, not at the floor of the lab but deeper, through it, through the earth itself, "—it likes the ones who ask questions. It likes the ones who write it down. You are its favorite kind of person, and you have been here nine hours."

I looked down at my notebook. At the page I'd filled without thinking. At the last line, which I had no memory of writing, in my own unmistakable hand:

*Down is only far enough.*

"I wrote that," I said. "That's my handwriting. I wrote that while it was talking."

"I know," Odile said. "They all did."

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. I sat up in Room 3 with every light burning and my notebook open, and I told myself the sensible things: suggestion, infrasound, a colleague's elaborate prank, my own exhausted brain stitching meaning out of static. I wrote the sensible things down. Sensible things fill pages easily.

At 3:07 a.m., the small green light on the smoke detector above my door — the one I'd assumed was a smoke detector — pulsed twice, like a blink, and went dark.

And somewhere below me, below the floor, below the foundations, patient as groundwater, something waited to see what I would ask.

I held my pen over the page until dawn, and I asked it nothing.

But I have never been good at keeping quiet, and the morning brought Bess Okafor banging on my classroom door with a ring of keys and a look on her face that said she knew exactly what I'd heard — and that she'd been waiting years for someone new to hear it too.
