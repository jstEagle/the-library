## 6. Vane's Hymn

I told myself I was going to his office for the staff roster. That was the lie I chose, and I chose it carefully, because a lie with paperwork attached is much easier to carry up three flights of stairs than the truth, which was: *I want to see your face when I say the word well.*

The corridor outside the headmaster's office smelled of floor wax and something underneath it — older, sweeter, like water that has stood too long in a dark place. The school clock read 4:40. In forty minutes the building would empty and Hollowmere would belong to whatever it belonged to after midnight, but at 4:40 it still belonged to the living, mostly.

His door was open.

That should have been my first warning. Mr. Vane kept his door closed the way other men kept their wallets close — out of habit, out of ownership. Today it stood wide, and from inside came a sound I had heard only once before, on Odile's basement tapes, and never hoped to hear again while standing upright in daylight.

Singing.

* * *

He was at the window with his back to me, and he was not singing the way a man sings in the shower or the way a choir sings on Sunday. He sang the way a bell rings — as if the sound were happening *to* him, moving through him, using his chest for its own purposes. It took me four bars to recognize the melody, and when I did, my hand found the doorframe and held on.

It was the skipping song. Bess's song. The one the children chanted at the chain-link fence without knowing what they were feeding.

Three verses. He sang all three, and then he went back to the first, over and over, circling, refusing — or unable — to go where the song wanted to end.

"Mr. Vane," I said.

He didn't stop. His shoulders rose and fell with the tune, and I watched, with a clarity that felt like falling, the way the skin at the back of his neck moved. Not like skin moves. Like water moves when something beneath it is rising.

"Mr. Vane." Louder.

The song stopped mid-word. The silence afterward had texture, weight, the silence of a room holding its breath around us both. When he turned, I understood why he'd left the door open. A man in his condition doesn't hide. A man in his condition has passed the stage where hiding matters.

His eyes were wrong. Not black, not filmed — *deep*. As though someone had taken his irises and pressed them gently inward, like thumbs into dough, and what looked back at me now came from further down than eyes are meant to reach. His hands, folded neatly in front of him, had gone the grey-green of pond stones. And when he smiled — warm, paternal, the smile that had welcomed me on my first morning — the smile was the last living thing about him.

"Miss Montrose." My name in his mouth sounded rehearsed. Everything about him sounded rehearsed now, a headmaster costume worn by something still learning the part. "Come in. Please. I've been hoping you would."

"You've been singing the children's song."

"The children's song." He tasted the phrase. "Yes. Isn't it funny how things come round? I taught it to them myself, years ago. Thought if they carried it lightly — skipping rope, playground noise — it would stay light. Stay harmless." He gestured to the chair across from his desk. I did not sit. "You've been to the well."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it. I set my notebook on the edge of his desk instead — deliberately, the way you'd lay down a card. "Four staff in three years. Each one recorded asking questions after midnight. Each one gone within the month. And each file signed off — closed, transferred, resolved — by you."

Something moved behind his face. Grief, maybe. Or the memory of grief, kept like a pressed flower.

"I wondered which of them would figure it out first," he said. "Odile knows half. Bess knows more than her mother ever wanted her to. But you—" he tilted his head, and the motion had too many joints in it, "—you write everything down. Ruth did that too. Lists, observations, little prayers made of ink." His voice softened into something terrible and tender. "She thought documentation would save her. She thought if she could just get the pattern onto paper, the paper would argue for her."

"What happened to Ruth?"

"She listened too well." He said it simply, the way you'd report weather. "That's all any of this has ever been, Miss Montrose. Listening. The well sings; someone hears; someone wonders. Curiosity is the hook. It always has been. I simply try to make sure the hook lands in adults."

* * *

He walked to the window while he talked, and I noticed then that he'd drawn the blind halfway, and that beyond it, past the car park, the chain-link cage around the well caught the late sun and threw it back dull, like old coins.

"Do you know what this school was before it was a school?" he asked. "A workhouse. Before that, a parish poorhouse. Children died here, Miss Montrose. Dozens of them, over a century, of fevers and famines and things the ledgers don't bother naming. They went down the well. Not bodies — the well wasn't for bodies. They went down the way you'd put coins in a slot. Payment. The thing below kept the rest safe. Kept the fields wet, the winters mild, the dreams quiet. A parish arrangement. Very old-fashioned. Very local."

"And when the workhouse closed?"

"The arrangement didn't. That's the trouble with arrangements. They outlive everyone who understands them." He turned from the window. "I found out in my first year as headmaster. Found the ledgers in the cellar, found the last entry, found the date it was due. You cannot imagine what it is to read a debt with your school's name on it and a due date of spring."

"So you paid it."

"With what?" For a moment the old Vane surfaced — tired, decent, appalled at himself. "Money means nothing to it. Prayers mean nothing. It wants voices. It wants people who have learned the song, who have asked their questions, who have leaned over the edge of themselves far enough to fall in. Adults. Grown, finished, childless or near enough. People whose absence the world absorbs without breaking." He spread his stone-grey hands. "Teachers. Caretakers. Me, eventually. I have been choosing, Miss Montrose, for eleven years. Do you know what that does to a soul? Every September I look at my staff list and I think: *who can we spare.*"

"You call that mercy."

"I call it arithmetic." No anger. That was the worst of it — no anger at all. "The alternative is children. The alternative is a classroom like yours, thirty small throats, all of them already chanting the verses without knowing why the chant feels good in the chest. If the well goes hungry, it stops being patient. It starts taking what walks past its fence." He stepped closer, and I smelled the standing-water sweetness on him, strong now, coming up through his pores like sap. "Every adult I have fed it bought a year of childhoods. Eleven years. Two hundred and twenty children who slept safely, learned their letters, grew up, moved away, had children of their own. Weigh that against four names on a memorial wall that will never exist."

"That's not weighing," I said. My voice shook and I let it. "That's spending."

"Yes," he said, almost gratefully. "Exactly. Spending. And every spender believes, right up until the end, that the next purchase will be the last one needed. I believed it eleven times." He looked at his hands. "I don't believe it anymore. That's why I'm telling you the truth. That's why the door was open."

* * *

He sat down behind his desk then, heavily, the way an old man sits, and for a moment he was only a tired administrator in a cheap suit, and I nearly pitied him. Nearly.

"The song has a fourth verse," he said. "Ruth knew it. The others learned pieces. I have known it for eleven years and I have never once sung it, because the fourth verse isn't words, Miss Montrose. It's consent. It's the moment the melody turns around and asks *will you*, and waits for your throat to answer." His deep-pressed eyes fixed on mine. "When you sing it, you stop being a person who lives near the well and become a person who belongs to it. Your body stays up here for a while — teaching, attending, signing forms. But the best of you goes down, and comes up only when it's called, and calls others in turn. That's the office I hold. Headmaster is just what the town sees."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I am nearly spent." He said it without drama, the way you'd admit a low fuel gauge. "Look at me. Another month, perhaps two, and there won't be enough of me up here to keep the arrangement running, and the well will start shopping on its own, and it has never once chosen an adult when it could reach a child." He opened a drawer — slowly, ceremonially — and took out a slim ledger, bound in green cloth gone soft with handling. He laid it on the desk between us. Inside, I knew without looking, were names. Dates. Entries in a careful hand going back eleven years.

And blank pages after.

"I'm not asking you to push anyone," he said gently. "I'm asking you to inherit. Learn the verse. Hold the office. Choose the way I chose — carefully, mercifully, adults only, as few as possible. Be the shepherd." His ruined eyes glistened. "Or refuse, and let it be a wolf. Those are the only two shapes this job comes in. There is no third option where nobody goes down the well. I searched for it for eleven years. It isn't there."

The clock ticked. Somewhere below us, faint through the floorboards, I could have sworn I heard water moving.

"There's always a third option," I said. "You just haven't met the people who'd build it with you."

His smile returned — patient, pitying, the smile of a man watching a new teacher make her first mistake. "Then you'd better hurry," he said. "Because the well has started refilling early, Miss Montrose. I felt it this morning. Something down there is counting the days." He slid the ledger toward me. "And it isn't counting adults."
