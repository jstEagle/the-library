## 7. What the Tape Said Back

The first time it happened, I almost didn't notice.

That's the part I keep returning to — not the horror of it, but the ease. The way my own voice could slide out of a speaker and into the room and be mistaken, for whole seconds, for me.

It was Tuesday. I know because I date everything now; the notebook has become a second skeleton, something to hang myself on. I'd been replaying the night's recording at my kitchen table, headphones on, tea going cold beside me, listening to six hours of my own breathing compressed into static and small sounds. The creak of the bedframe at 1:14. A car passing at 2:40. My voice, thick with sleep, murmuring something at 3:03 that I couldn't make out.

I rewound it. Turned the gain up. Played it again.

*"What do you want from the well?"*

My voice. Unmistakably mine — the slight flattening of my vowels, the way I swallow the ends of questions like my mother did. Except I had never asked that question. Not aloud, not asleep, not ever. And beneath it, faint as a finger dragged along wet glass, came the answer:

*"Its verse back."*

I sat very still. The tea had a skin on it. Outside, someone's dog was barking in the flat, patient rhythm of a town that had learned not to look at Hollowmere too long.

I told myself: sleep-talking. People say strange things in their sleep. People ask strange questions. It was coincidence that this one fit so neatly against the thing Odile's tapes had caught three years running, the thing that had preceded four disappearances like a cough precedes a fever.

I wrote it down anyway. That's what I do. I write it down and the writing makes it smaller, makes it a specimen pinned under glass instead of a hand around my throat.

*Oct 14. Recorder captured my voice asking a question I have no memory of asking. Answer audible beneath it. See attached transcript.*

Then I went to bed, and I slept, and in the morning there was dirt under my fingernails again, and I decided — deliberately, the way you decide to keep your eyes shut during a frightening film — not to think about where it came from.

* * *

By Friday, the tapes had stopped pretending to be mine alone.

I'd taken to reviewing them at school, in my classroom after hours, because the flat felt wrong now — because the walls of my bedroom had begun to feel like the inside of an ear. I sat at the small desk with its child-sized chair digging into my knees and listened through October 16th.

At 12:51 a.m., my voice said: *"Tell me about Ruth."*

I hadn't asked about Ruth. I'd thought about her — everyone at Hollowmere thinks about Ruth eventually, the teacher whose classroom is now mine, whose name appears in the staff ledger and then simply stops, mid-term, like a sentence cut off by a door. But thinking isn't asking. Thinking stays behind the teeth.

And the answer came in *my* voice too. Both sides of the conversation. Me asking, me answering, a duet performed entirely by one throat that wasn't in the room.

*"Ruth liked the window seat. She kept her pens in a jam jar. She hummed when she marked books."*

*"Did she go down?"*

*"She went down singing."*

I pulled the headphones off so fast they cracked against the desk. The classroom was dark except for the exit sign's green bleed. Twenty-eight desks faced me, twenty-seven occupied by day, and the twenty-eighth — Ruth's, the empty one, the one nobody would explain — seemed in that moment less empty than all the others. Less vacant. More like a held breath.

I should have stopped listening then. I want to be honest in these pages, even if honesty indicts me: I put the headphones back on. Because the voice knew things. Because it said *jam jar* and *window seat* and I checked the next morning and there was a jam jar on the windowsill, stained purple at the bottom, and no one had mentioned it to me, and knowing felt like power, and power feels like safety when you are alone in a building that eats people.

That's how it works. I understand that now. It doesn't drag you. It *informs* you. It makes itself useful until usefulness becomes dependence becomes invitation.

* * *

Saturday, the recordings began using my memories.

I need to describe this carefully, because describing it is the only defense I have left, and even that may be exactly what it wants.

The tape from Friday night — reviewed Saturday afternoon — contained, at 3:33 a.m., a sound I recognized before I understood it: my mother's kitchen. The particular squeak of the screen door at our house in Aba, the rain-barrel drip, the way she sang low when she thought no one was listening — half-words, Yoruba lullabies worn smooth as river stones. All of it captured on a microphone sitting in a bedroom four thousand miles from anywhere she'd ever stood.

Then my voice, gentle, coaxing, the way you talk to a child or a frightened animal:

*"You miss her."*

*"Say the verse. She used to sing it to you. You've known it since you were small."*

I don't remember turning off the recorder. I remember the taste of copper and realizing I'd bitten my lip. I remember standing in the middle of my flat with my hands pressed over my ears as though the tape were still playing, as though sound could get into me through the skin.

Because here is the thing I have not written down until this moment, here in the safest margin of this page: I *do* know a verse. Three verses, actually — Bess taught me the skipping song last week at the well, the one the children chant, the one her mother sang. But the voice on the tape hadn't described Bess's song. It had described a song from *before*. From my childhood. From a kitchen in another country, sung by a woman who has been dead six years.

There is no version of this in which the visitor learned that from my recordings. There is only the version where it learned it from *me* — from whatever I carry into sleep, from the dirt under my nails, from the walks I don't remember taking.

* * *

Sunday night, Odile found me at the gate.

This account comes partly from her, because I have gaps in it — hours missing like teeth knocked out of a jaw. What I have is this: I went to bed at eleven. I woke at some black hour standing barefoot on the frost-stiff grass inside the school grounds, my hand on the chain-link fence around the well, both hands, fingers hooked through the diamonds, and the fence was humming. Not vibrating. Humming. A note held low and patient, and underneath it, threaded through it, a melody rising toward me like water rising in a throat —

— and arms around my waist, hauling me backward, and Odile's voice in my ear saying my name, just my name, over and over, the way you'd call someone back across a wide cold river.

She walked me to the nurse's office wrapped in a blanket from the supply closet and made me drink something awful and sweet. She didn't ask questions at first. She sat across from me under the flickering fluorescent tube with her big capable hands flat on her knees, watching me the way you watch a fever chart.

"Your feet," she said finally. "Look at your feet."

They were black to the ankle. Not dirty — *black*, packed soil, well-soil, the wet dark earth that lives at the bottom of things. And my palms, when I turned them over, carried the same stain in the shape of the fence's diamond pattern, printed into the skin like a brand.

"I was asleep," I said.

"You were singing," she said.

The word landed in my chest like a stone dropped down a shaft. I waited for it to hit bottom. It didn't. It's still falling.

"I wasn't — Odile, I would know—"

"You were singing the skipping song," she said, "and then you started on a fourth verse, and I have never heard a fourth verse, Yara, and neither has anyone alive, and you were singing it in a voice that was yours and also—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. This woman who has held broken bones steady and talked parents through the worst nights of their lives could not finish the sentence.

"And also not," I finished for her.

She nodded once, slow.

We sat there while the fluorescent light ticked. Then she got up, locked the office door, and wheeled out the old tape machine — the sleep-lab deck, the one that runs every night in the basement below the staff rooms, below my room. She threaded a reel with hands that weren't quite steady.

"This is from last night," she said. "Three a.m. onward."

She pressed play.

My voice filled the little room. But it wasn't talking to the visitor anymore. It wasn't answering questions. It was *giving* — reciting my life in a flat, obedient monotone, item by item, like inventory being read off to a customs officer. My mother's name. The name of the street I grew up on. The color of the dress I wore to her funeral. The exact words I said at the graveside, which I have never spoken aloud to another living person, which exist nowhere outside the sealed rooms of my skull.

And between each item, faintly, from somewhere deeper in the recording than a microphone should reach, something made a sound of satisfaction. Not speech. Not breath. The sound a lock makes when the right key turns halfway.

Odile stopped the tape.

"It's learning you," she said. "Not your secrets. *You.* The shape of you. It's fitting itself into the space where you are, and when it fits—" She looked at her hands. "Ruth's tape sounded like this, the week before. I've listened to it a hundred times trying to understand. It starts as questions and answers. Then it starts as inventory. Then—"

"Then what?"

"Then there's only one voice on the tape," she said. "And it isn't hers anymore. But it thinks it is."

* * *

I am writing this in the nurse's office at dawn, Monday, because I will not sleep in my room again, and because Odile is making tea and Bess is due any minute — Bess, who begged me to stop digging, who was right, who I owe an apology shaped like the truth.

Here is the truth, then, set down while I still recognize the hand holding the pen:

The documentation was never a prayer. It was a mouth. Every question I asked into the dark, every fact I fed the page, every night I lay awake cataloguing the wrongness — I wasn't studying the visitor. I was *tutoring* it. Lesson by lesson, night by night, I have been teaching something ancient and patient how to be Yara Montrose, and it is nearly fluent, and last night it tried to walk me home to the well wearing my body like a borrowed coat.

The notebook is still my skeleton. But skeletons can be worn by other things.

Bess just knocked. Through the frosted glass of the office door I can see her small shape, and beside her, impossibly, another — taller, wavering, edges bleeding into the corridor light like ink in water.

Odile sets down the teapot. Her face has gone the color of paper.

"That," she says quietly, "is Ruth."
