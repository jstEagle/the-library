## 1. The House That Counts Its Doors

The bus to Hollowmere runs once a day, and it runs late, and the driver—a man with a face like a folded map—told me twice that I'd be getting off at "the old stop," as though there were any other. I said yes. He nodded. Then he said it again, in exactly the same tone, with exactly the same pause before *stop*, and I decided not to answer, because some part of me understood we were having a different conversation than the one I thought we were having.

Twenty years since I'd been back. Twenty years since my grandmother died and the cottage went to solicitors and dust, and here I was, a folklorist with a grant from a university that had misspelled my name on the award letter, come home to record songs. Hollowmere had songs. Everyone said so. Songs nobody outside the valley had ever heard, sung by people who, according to every archive I could find, had never been recorded singing them twice.

I should have asked more questions about that. In my defense, I was thirty-four and flattered that anyone still remembered Hollowmere remembered anything.

* * *

The cottage smelled of cold ash and lavender gone to powder. My grandmother's chair still sat by the window, angled toward the lane, as if she'd only stepped out to see who was coming. I set my bags down in the hall and stood there a long moment, listening to the house settle around me—not creaking, exactly. More like clearing its throat.

There were eleven doors inside the cottage. I know because I counted them that first night, standing in the kitchen with a mug of tea I'd forgotten to drink, counting doors like a child counts sheep. Front door, back door, pantry, three bedrooms, bathroom, two cupboards under the stairs—one of which opened onto nothing but brick, which is its own kind of joke—and the little door to the cellar, and the door of the hall closet.

Eleven.

The next morning I counted again. Eleven. The night after that, ten—I couldn't find the second cupboard door, just a smooth stretch of wallpaper where it had been—and then thirteen the following evening, when I discovered a narrow door behind the coat rack that I would have sworn on my grandmother's Bible hadn't been there at noon. It opened onto a space barely wider than itself, smelling of well water and old wool, and contained nothing but a single wooden peg, worn glossy, as if something had hung there for years.

I started keeping a tally on the back of my field notebook. Doors: 11, 11, 10, 13, 12, 14. I told myself it was data. Folklorists love thresholds; liminality is practically our brand. I did not tell myself that no house gains doors, or that I had begun checking them before bed the way other people check locks, or that on the fourteenth-door night I lay awake until dawn listening for footsteps that never came, which was somehow worse than footsteps.

* * *

The village itself was worse, in the way that a familiar face on a stranger is worse than an unfamiliar one. Everything sat where memory had left it—the crooked signpost, the green with its leaning maypole, the church whose bell rope had rotted through sometime during my adolescence and never been replaced. But the people moved through it all slightly wrong. Not wrong the way actors are wrong. Wrong the way dreams are wrong, where everyone knows their lines but no one quite looks at each other.

They knew my name. That unsettled me more than it should have. A woman selling eggs called me "little Seb" without looking up from her till. The postman handed me a bundle addressed to *Miss S. Almeida, the Ashgrove cottage*, though I'd told no one I was coming except the letting agent, who was, I gathered, also the butcher, also the man who fixed roofs, also possibly the entire parish council.

"You'll want to talk to Marta," the postman said, tucking my mail under his arm instead of giving it to me. "About the songs."

"I will, actually. Where do I find her?"

He looked at me then, properly, for the first time, and his eyes were the flat grey of the sky over the mere. "You'll find her," he said, "when she's ready. She's always ready before you are." And he walked off down the lane with my letters, whistling—three notes, rising, cut off clean, like a song that had been interrupted on purpose.

* * *

My first real interview was Edith Parr, seventy-something, keeper of the village shop, and the closest thing Hollowmere had to a welcome committee. She made me tea in her back room among sacks of flour and jars of preserves labeled in handwriting that wasn't hers, and when I explained my project—archival recording, preservation, the vanishing oral traditions of the valley—she smiled the way you smile at a child announcing they'll catch the moon in a bucket.

"We've got songs," she agreed. "Always have done."

"Would you sing one for me? Just into the recorder. It doesn't have to be whole. A verse is plenty."

She looked at the machine on the table between us—my good Sony, tape reels turning patiently behind their little windows—with an expression I couldn't read. Not fear. Something more like courtesy extended toward a dangerous animal.

"One verse," she said. "Once."

"Of course."

And she sang.

I want to describe it properly, because everything that happened afterward happened because of how it sounded. It was a simple melody, pentatonic, the kind of tune that seems older than language, about water and waiting—I caught fragments: *down where the water listens, down where the dark remembers*—and her voice was thin and cracked with age but utterly certain, the way voices are when they've sung a thing so many times the singing has become involuntary, like breathing. Thirty seconds. Maybe forty. Then she stopped, mid-phrase, on a note that wanted resolving and didn't get it, and pressed her lips together like a woman closing a gate.

"That's all I can give you," she said.

"It was beautiful. Could you do the next verse? Or even just—"

"No."

It wasn't unkind. That was the strange thing. She refilled my tea, asked after my mother, sent me off with a jar of damson jam, and was warm as bread the entire time, right up until the moment I mentioned singing again, at which point she became a closed door in a cardigan.

* * *

That night, in the cottage, I played the recording back.

I do this with every field session; it's discipline, not superstition. You hear things on playback you miss live—the second voice in a round, the drone note under a lullaby, the child giggling off-mic. So I sat in my grandmother's chair with headphones on, in a house with fourteen doors (I'd counted), and I listened to Edith Parr sing about the water that listens.

Her verse ended. I knew it ended; I'd watched her mouth close. But the tape kept going.

Another voice came in underneath where her last note faded—a low hum first, then words, or almost words, syllables shaped like the ones Edith had sung but deeper, wetter, as if someone were singing the same verse from underwater, or from the bottom of a very long throat. It sang the verse again. All of it. Every line Edith had given me, repeated back in that drowned register, and then it continued—into the second verse, the one Edith hadn't sung, the one I'd never heard—clear as a bell, patient as tide.

I ripped the headphones off. The cottage was silent. Fourteen doors, all shut, all where they'd been at dusk.

I played it again. Edith's voice, thin and certain. Silence. Silence. Silence—

The second voice came in exactly where it had before. Same pitch. Same pause. Same impossible continuation into the verse I didn't know.

Here is what I did not do: I did not play it a third time. Some instinct older than my training put its hand flat on mine and stopped me, and I sat in the dark with the tape reels stilled and my heart going like a bird in a box, understanding two things with total clarity.

First: repetition is not neutral. Somewhere in the architecture of this valley, saying a thing twice is a knock on a door.

Second: whatever had answered Edith's verse on my tape had been waiting twenty years for someone to bring a recorder to Hollowmere.

Or—worse, and I only admitted this to myself much later—it hadn't been waiting at all. It had simply been listening, all this time, to a house that keeps gaining doors, in a village that refuses to sing twice, for the one person foolish enough to press record.

I wrote in my notebook, in handwriting less steady than I'd like: *Day 3. First recording obtained. Subject: E.P., b. 1949. One verse, unrepeated. Anomaly on playback — second voice, unidentified, continues past source material. Do not play back after dark.*

Then, because I was tired and homesick for a home that had stopped existing, I added: *Wren would have loved this.*

I don't know why I wrote that. I haven't thought that name in twenty years.

The tally on the back page, when I checked it before bed, read fifteen.
