## 8. Down Where the Water Listens

The rope was Marta's idea, though she refused to say so out loud. She simply appeared at my door on the morning after the doors multiplied—appeared with a coil of hemp over one shoulder and a storm lantern in her hand, looking like a woman who had decided that if I was going to be an idiot, I would at least be a well-equipped idiot.

"You'll want to go down," she said. Not a question. She'd been watching me count doors through the window, I think. Watching me stand in the hallway at three in the morning with my notebook, tallying doorways where there had been four yesterday and eleven today, all of them closed, all of them humming faintly, like throats clearing behind wallpaper.

"Yes," I said.

"Then you'll want the rope." She set it down on my step with the finality of a woman laying out a body. "And you'll want to know this before you go: whatever you see down there, it isn't dead. None of it. That's the part people get wrong, when they get it wrong. The Well doesn't kill what it keeps."

"What does it do?"

Marta looked past me, into the cottage, at the eleventh door. Her mouth did something complicated.

"It listens back," she said, and left before I could ask her anything else, which was, by then, her signature move.

* * *

The Listening Well sits in the square like a wound that healed over wrong. I'd walked past it a dozen times since arriving, telling myself it was just a well—stone lip, mossy bucket, the particular smell of deep cold water that every village well has. But standing over it now, at dusk, with the rope anchored to the old oak and the lantern hissing beside me, I could admit what I'd been refusing to admit: the well was *breathing*. Slowly. In and out. The air rising from it was warm, and it moved in a rhythm suspiciously close to sleep.

I am a folklorist. I have spent my life among stories about holes in the world. There is always a hole in the world, in these stories, and there is always someone who cannot stop themselves from leaning over it.

I leaned over it.

"Hello," I said, because apparently I am also the kind of person who greets things.

The dark below swallowed the word. And then—and I want to be precise here, because precision is the only weapon I have left—the dark gave something back. Not an echo. An echo would have been comforting. This was my own voice, returned slightly *late*, as though someone far down had heard "hello" and found it interesting enough to try on.

I climbed over the lip.

The shaft was wider than it looked from above—wide enough that my shoulders never brushed stone, which felt less like architecture and more like accommodation. The rope paid out in long, easy lengths, as if the well were reeling me down gently rather than letting me fall. The lantern swung shadows across the walls, and the walls were not plain. They were carved. Rows and rows of small figures, mouths open, arranged in spirals going down and down, and every spiral was a verse. I knew them by shape before I knew them by sight. I had been transcribing those melodies for two weeks from mouths that clamped shut the moment I asked for a second pass.

The villagers hadn't been refusing me. They'd been quoting the walls.

Halfway down, the water sound began—not splashing, nothing so honest. It was the sound of water *listening*, if such a thing can be imagined: a hush with attention in it. And threaded through the hush, faintly, a hum. Six voices. Children's voices. Singing the first verse of the Well Song in a round that never ended, never repeated quite exactly, each cycle slightly changed, the way a song changes when it's being *kept* rather than sung.

My hands went cold on the rope. Because I knew that round. I had sung it. In 1998, in the photograph, with five other children whose names I'd spent the last week digging out of parish records like splinters.

The rope ran out.

I expected a floor of water. What I got was a floor of stone, dry as bone, and a corridor leading away from the shaft's base—an impossibility I accepted with the numb professionalism of someone who has stopped expecting villages to make sense. The lantern showed me walls again, more carvings, and then the corridor opened into a chamber, and the chamber—

God help me. The chamber was full of people.

They stood in a ring around a pool of black water, spaced evenly, like guests at a ceremony no one had told them had started. There were perhaps twenty of them. Men and women in clothes spanning forty years—a man in a 1970s sheepskin coat, a girl in a Hollowmere Primary sweatshirt, old Tom Ellery, who had gone missing the winter before last and whose wife still set a place for him at table. They stood perfectly still. Their chests rose and fell. Their eyes were open.

Their mouths were open too—all of them mid-note, mid-verse, frozen not in terror but in *concentration*, the way singers look when they're holding a difficult harmony. And their eyes tracked me. All of them, all at once, as I stepped into the ring's edge, twenty pairs of eyes sliding toward me with the smooth synchrony of weathervanes finding wind.

"Tom?" I said, because he was closest, and because saying a name makes a thing either more real or less, and I needed one of those desperately. "Mr. Ellery? It's Sebastian. Sebastian Almeida."

His eyes crinkled. Almost a smile. His mouth stayed open on its held note, and from somewhere inside him—deep in the chest, beneath the silence—came a thread of sound. Not words. A hum. The third verse. He was singing it *at* me, silently, his whole body a struck bell that couldn't ring.

"They can't stop," said a voice behind me, young and familiar and impossible. "That's what keeping means, Seb. You don't get to put the song down."

Wren stood at the chamber's far side, half in shadow, unchanged, twenty years old and eight years old all at once. He didn't come closer. He looked tired in a way that children aren't supposed to look tired.

"You shouldn't be here," I whispered.

"I know." He hugged himself. "But you keep coming closer to the truth, and the truth keeps coming closer to you, and eventually those two things meet, and it's always—" he gestured at the ring of silent singers, "—here."

I should have run. Instead, I did the scholar thing. I raised the lantern and looked properly at the faces, cataloguing, because cataloguing is how I keep from screaming. A woman in her thirties with a gap between her front teeth. A teenager with a chipped tooth and a Walkman clipped to his belt. And then, near the back of the ring, a face that stopped my heart with a lurch of pure vertigo.

She was maybe thirty-five. Tall. Dark-haired, streaked early with grey. She wore a field jacket with a recorder's lanyard around her neck and a notebook in her pocket, and her mouth was open on a note, and her eyes—her eyes were mine. Not similar. Mine. The same crooked left eyelid, the same way of looking at the world slightly off-center, as if listening for something behind it.

"Marta said the Well saves places," Wren said quietly, watching me look at her. "For the ones it wants most. It's been saving yours a long time, Seb. Since you were little. Since you sang loudest."

"That's not—that isn't *me*."

"It's who you'd be," Wren said. "In a few years. If you stay. It doesn't take you all at once. It takes you the way you take a song—one verse at a time. First you stop singing twice. Then you stop singing new songs. Then one year at the annual singing, your mouth opens, and—" He shrugged, small and miserable. "You're very good at harmonizing. You always were. That's why it likes you."

The woman who might have me for a face shifted her gaze, slow as syrup, until she was looking directly at me. And in her open mouth, in that held, silent note, I heard—I swear I heard—a melody I didn't know yet but recognized anyway, the way you recognize a room from a dream. A fourth verse. A fifth. Something after that, something the tapes skipped and the villagers wouldn't give and the walls only hinted at in spirals of small carved mouths.

The last verse. She was holding it for me. Saving it, the way the Well saved everything it heard.

"Don't listen," Wren begged. His voice cracked, and for one moment he sounded exactly eight years old. "Seb, please. If you learn it from her, you'll owe it a song back. That's how it works. That's how it's *always* worked. Just—come up with me. Come sing with me instead. Like we used to. Just us. I'll keep you safe. I've kept you safe for twenty years, haven't I?"

And there it was, finally, laid bare on the wet stone between us: my oldest friend, offering me the choice he'd probably been sent to offer, or maybe—this was the horror of it, the unbearable part—maybe offering it out of pure, selfish, lonely love, unable to tell the difference anymore between keeping me and keeping me *kept*.

Behind him, the woman with my eyes held her note. Around her, twenty throats held theirs. Below us all, the black water listened, patient as archives, and I understood at last what the Well actually was.

It wasn't a monster. It was a collection. And collections, I know this better than anyone, are built by people who cannot bear to let a song end.

The annual singing was tomorrow night.

I took out my recorder, and pressed it against my chest, and began to climb.
