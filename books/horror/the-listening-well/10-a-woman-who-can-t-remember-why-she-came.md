## 10. A Woman Who Can't Remember Why She Came

The tape was warm in my hand, which made no sense. Machines don't hold heat like that. But it had been sitting in my pocket all through the singing, pressed against my hip while the whole village hummed around me in the dark, and when I finally took it out on the walk down to the well, it felt less like a cassette and more like a small animal that had decided to trust me.

Wren walked beside me. He had stopped begging somewhere behind the churchyard, and now he just walked, hands in the pockets of the coat he'd worn for twenty years without wearing out, and I understood that this was its own kind of pleading. Silence from Wren was louder than anything he'd ever said.

"You could still stop," he offered, at the lychgate.

"I know."

"You could sing it with me instead. Just once. The way we used to." His voice did something young and cracked at the end of it. "You'd forget all this anyway. That's what she does. You'd wake up tomorrow and it would be a nice dream you had about a village."

"That's not forgetting," I said. "That's being eaten politely."

He laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, and for a second he was seven years old again, laughing at my terrible jokes under the yew tree while my grandmother's tape recorder turned and turned. Then his face settled back into that terrible agelessness, and I kept walking.

The well stood open in the moonlight. Someone had removed the cover — Marta, I supposed, or the village itself, which by now amounted to the same thing. The water below was black and still and attentive. I want to say it looked like a mirror, but mirrors only show you yourself. This looked like an ear.

I threaded the tape into my grandmother's recorder. My hands were steadier than they had any right to be.

"Last chance," Wren whispered.

"Last verse," I corrected him, and pressed play.

* * *

What came out of the little speaker was everything I had refused to give with my own mouth. The collected voices sang the forbidden verse — the one no archive held, the one every tape skipped over, the one the Well had been keeping for itself like a coin under a tongue. It wasn't beautiful. That surprised me. I'd expected beauty; what I got was something older and plainer, a work-song, a lullaby, the kind of tune you'd hum to keep something asleep rather than to charm it awake. Six voices, maybe seven, braided together, and underneath them a sound I recognized from thirty seconds of every corrupted recording I owned: water, listening.

I held the recorder out over the shaft.

The Well heard itself. There is no other way to describe what happened. The song went down into the dark and the dark went quiet in a way that was different from before — not the silence of attention but the silence of something finally full, or finally finished. The water shivered once, like a struck bell deciding not to ring.

And then the voices came apart.

They rose out of the well the way heat rises off summer stone — not bodies, not ghosts, just voice-stuff, unraveling upward into the cold air, and each thread of them, as it thinned, seemed to sigh. I heard the woman who might have been me go last. She didn't say goodbye. She sang her half-line of the verse one final time, softly, directly at me, and there was such relief in it, such plain human gratitude, that I sat down hard on the well's stone lip and cried like a child.

"Wren," I managed.

He was already fading. Not dramatically — no light, no wind, none of the theater you'd expect. He simply became less certain, the way a face becomes less certain when you try to recall it years later. His edges went soft. His coat went gray.

"Thank you," he said. And then, because he was Wren, and had always been Wren: "You were the best thing I ever pretended to be."

"Don't," I said. "Don't do the goodbye speech."

"It's not a speech. It's a duet part. You're just not singing." He smiled, and it was my smile, the one I'd invented for him out of my own mouth when I was small. "That's the whole point. That's how you save everyone. Even me. *Especially* me."

"Wren—"

"Forget me," he said gently. "It's the kindest thing anyone's ever done."

And he was gone — not vanished, *resolved*, the way a chord resolves, leaving you unable to remember what the tension sounded like.

The memories started going almost immediately. I felt them leave the way you feel warmth leave a bath: gradually, then all at once. A cottage with too many doors. A porch in late summer light. A name — his name — sliding off something in my chest like water off glass. I grabbed at them and they came away in my hands like wet paper, legible for one heartbeat, blank the next. I remember thinking, quite clearly, *so this is the price*, and then I remember thinking, *what price?*, and then I remember standing up beside a well in a village I couldn't quite place, holding a tape recorder, wondering why my face was wet.

* * *

Later — though "later" is a generous word for what followed — a woman came walking up the lane out of Hollowmere carrying a canvas bag of field equipment and a single cassette she did not recognize.

She was tall, mid-thirties, with the particular posture of someone who has spent years leaning toward other people's voices. Her name, according to the tag on her bag, was S. Almeida. She had driven here — the car was parked by the green — though the reason for the trip had gone strangely absent from her mind, the way a word goes absent on the tip of the tongue. Folk songs, she supposed. Fieldwork. Something about this village. It had seemed important enough to drive four hours for, and now it was nothing at all, a blank page where an intention should be.

The villagers were friendly in an odd, distracted way, like people waking from a shared nap. An old woman named Marta pressed bread into her hands and told her the road back was better than the road here, which was true, and also told her, gripping her wrist briefly, "Whatever's on that tape — don't listen to it twice." The woman laughed, assuming local color. Marta did not laugh.

She walked the village once, because she was thorough. She noted the well at its center, capped now with a new iron lid, and felt — she would have said *nothing*, if pressed, but it wasn't nothing — a faint ache, like a room she'd been happy in, demolished before she ever saw it. On the green, children were singing a skipping rhyme. None of them would sing it twice. When she asked why, they looked at her with round, untroubled eyes and said, "You only need to sing it once," as if this were the most obvious law in the world, and perhaps by then it was.

In her bag, among labeled tapes and notebooks in her own handwriting — handwriting she trusted more than she trusted the notebooks' contents, which described interviews she could not recall conducting with people whose names meant nothing to her — there was one unlabeled cassette. No date. No title. Just a small paper sticker, peeling, on which someone had written in pencil, in a child's careful capitals:

**FOR WHEN YOU FORGET WHY.**

She played it on the drive home, once, windows down, hedges flickering past.

It was thirty seconds of well water. Underneath the water, so faint she had to turn the volume all the way up at a red light, two voices were singing together — a child's voice and another voice, neither quite either, both unmistakably laughing between the lines of some tune she almost knew. It should have been eerie. It was, a little. But mostly it made her cry, quietly, at a red light in a village she was already forgetting, for reasons she could not have written down if you'd asked her, and she did not ask herself, because she was a folklorist, and folklorists know better than anyone that some songs survive precisely by refusing to explain themselves.

At the edge of Hollowmere, a sign read LEAVE WHAT YOU CARRIED. Someone had scratched beneath it, recently, in a different hand: *or keep one small thing.*

She kept the tape.

She never knew why. But on certain evenings — and this is the last thing anyone recorded of her — she would take it out, look at the penciled capitals, and feel an absence shaped exactly like a friend, and she would put it back in the drawer, unplayed, next to the door key of a cottage she had sold without ever remembering owning.

Some wells, after all, are fed by what we carry away.

Hers, at least, would never run dry.
