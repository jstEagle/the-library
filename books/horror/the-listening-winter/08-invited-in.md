## 8. Invited In

The bell had been ringing for two hours when I stopped trying to count the intervals.

That's the thing about sound in an enclosed space—it stops being information and becomes weather. You can't reason with weather. You can only decide what you're going to do while it happens to you. I had decided, after exhausting the other options, to sit with my back against the altar and my arms around my knees, like a child told to wait in the hall, and to think very carefully about nothing at all.

I was not good at nothing. I want that on the record. I have built an entire life on the principle that every phenomenon has a mechanism, and the mechanism was currently ringing a bronze bell that had no rope, no clapper I could see from where I sat, and no business existing at all, and I could not find the mechanism, and the not-finding was starting to feel less like a gap in my knowledge and more like a gap in the world.

The rockfall had sealed the drain shaft at 4:40 in the afternoon. I know because I checked my watch obsessively, the way you check a wound. Forty tons of chalk and flint, packed with a neatness no collapse produces. Collapses are chaotic. This had been *arranged*. Somewhere above me, in the dying light, someone—or something—had closed a door.

And the moon. I'd seen it through the grating at the top of the shaft before the grating stopped mattering, a fat red disc climbing the sky hours before it had any right to exist, swollen like an infection. Harvest moon, the old man on the green had said. Be indoors by dusk Friday.

It was Friday. I was indoors. I was also, by any reasonable definition of the word, *inside the house*.

* * *

The ringing changed at some point—I couldn't say when, only that I surfaced from my own spiraling thoughts and realized the intervals had stretched. Longer silences between the tolls. As if something were listening between them. As if the bell were less a sound than a *question*, and something in the dark below the nave was considering its answer.

I stood up. My legs had gone stiff and cold; the undercroft held its chill the way a bank vault holds paper. My headlamp swept the nave—pews of blackened oak, the tallow-thick altar, the walls furred with a pale growth I'd been telling myself all day was calcium deposit.

"Hello," I said. My voice came out smaller than I'd budgeted for. "I know you're there."

The bell tolled. Once. Softly. The way you'd clear your throat.

"I'm a surveyor," I said, and then, because that was absurd, "I'm a cartographer. I map things. Drainage anomalies, mostly. I don't— I didn't come for you. I didn't know you were here. Nobody told me you were here."

The silence that followed had texture. It's the only word I have. It pressed against my skin like the moment before a storm breaks, that greenish pressure, and in the middle of it I heard—no. *Felt* is more accurate, but felt isn't right either. It arrived the way a thought arrives, fully formed, wearing my own voice:

*You knew.*

"I didn't."

*You knew something was wrong the moment you saw the salt. You catalogued it. Doorway, doorway, doorway. You told yourself it was folk practice, agricultural superstition, a preservation habit from hard winters. You told yourself a lot of things. You're telling yourself things right now.*

"Okay." I put my hands up, palms out, to an empty nave. "Okay. You're— what, infrasound? Resonant frequencies inducing auditory hallucination? Temporal lobe—"

*Say the rest of it.*

The voice was patient. That was the worst part. It wasn't mocking. It had all the time in the world, and it was letting me run through my litany like a child reciting a spell against the dark, and it was going to wait until I ran out.

"Temporal lobe epilepsy," I said. "Focal seizures. Auditory aura, sense of presence, it's extremely well documented, it's called a—"

*A felt presence.* The voice turned the words over gently, like a jeweler examining a stone for flaws. *You've read the papers. You've always read the papers. You read them at three in the morning after the dreams, looking for the mechanism that would make the dreams harmless. You never found it. You kept looking. Do you know why you kept looking?*

"Because the alternative is—"

*Because you were afraid the dreams were memories.*

I stopped talking.

Here's what I have never told anyone, not my thesis supervisor, not the two therapists I cycled through in my twenties, not the woman I almost married before I chose the tunnels instead. When I was six years old, in the house in Roubaix, I used to wake at night certain that something was standing at the foot of my bed listening. Not watching—my grandmother's house had plenty of watching things, the ordinary furniture-shadows of childhood. *Listening*. Leaning toward me the way you lean toward a radio when the signal's failing, trying to catch the words. And in the morning there would be a fine grey dust on the floorboards between the bed and the door, in a line, straight as a surveyor's string, and my grandmother would sweep it up without comment and put salt down that night, and I would sleep.

I had filed it. I file everything. Six-year-olds dream; dust is dust; grandmothers are superstitious; the file closes. That is what a rational mind *is*—a filing system with good locks.

The presence opened the file.

*Roubaix,* it said, in my own voice, in my own memory's voice, warm and small and terrified. *Rue des Fabriques. The house with the leaning chimney. You called it the listening man because you didn't have the word for what it was. Your grandmother had the word. She'd had it her whole life. That's why the salt. That's why she never let you sleep with your window open in harvest season. That's why, when the family asked her why she was leaving the north, she said the winters were getting too hard to pay for.*

"Stop," I said.

*You thought she meant money.*

"I said *stop*."

*She meant this,* the presence said, and the nave went quiet in a way that was not an absence of sound but a presence of attention, the whole black architecture of the place leaning toward me like that shape at the foot of the bed, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like a bone breaking, that I had not been followed here. I had been *expected*. Not today. Not this year. My grandmother had walked out of the listening country sixty years ago, and whatever kept the books had never closed her account, and the debt had sat in a ledger gathering interest until a surveyor with a drainage contract and a headlamp came looking for the mechanism.

There is no mechanism. I want to say that plainly, to whoever reads this, if anyone reads this. I spent my whole life believing the universe was machinery, that terror was just misread data, that if you could map a thing you could master it. I was wrong in the specific way that matters: the universe is not machinery. It is *attentive*. There is a difference between a room and an ear, and I was standing in an ear.

* * *

It didn't stop. That's the other thing. It had no reason to stop, and it didn't.

It went through the files. All of them. Not in order—there was no order, no chronology, just the terrible associative shuffle of a mind that knows you better than you know yourself, because it *is* you, or it wears you, or it has been keeping your thoughts the way a bank keeps deposits.

It gave me back the night in the Karst when I left Tomáš at the junction. He'd wanted to push on into the unmapped gallery; I'd wanted to turn back; I'd won the vote because I was lead and he was junior, and the gallery flooded two hours later and they found him eleven days after that, and at the funeral his mother had held my hand and said *you knew, you felt it, that's why you turned back, God gave you the feeling*—and I had smiled and let her believe it because the truth was so much worse. The truth was that I'd felt nothing. I'd turned back because I was tired and the beer at the village inn was good and I wanted my bed. I had let a grieving woman build a cathedral of meaning on the foundation of my laziness, and I had never once, in nine years, told anyone, because the confession served no one and cost me everything.

The presence said it back to me in my own voice, in the exact tone I'd used at the junction—*we'll come back for it next season*—and then it said the other thing, the thing under the thing, the sentence I had never let reach language: *and you were relieved, when they found the body, that it wasn't you.*

"That's not—" My voice cracked. "That's not what I—"

*It is. You've thought it at three in the morning for nine years. You've tried not to think it and the trying is how I know. Do you understand? I am not reading your mind. I am the part of your mind you locked the door on. I am every thought you've ever swallowed. I have been keeping them for you. I keep everyone's.*

The bell tolled, somewhere above in the dark, and the toll moved through the floor and up through my boots and I realized my knees had given and I was on the stone, hands flat, like a man praying who has forgotten how the words go.

*The villagers think I'm hunger,* the presence said, almost conversationally now, almost kind. *Hunger is simpler. Hunger they can feed. But I don't eat what they give me, Xanthe. I listen to it. Do you know what a child's whole unlived life sounds like, heard all at once? Every thought they never got to think? The village calls it an invitation. It's a recital. And then the winter is quiet, because I am full of listening, and full is the only mercy I have.*

"You're lying," I whispered. "You're— whatever you are, you want me compliant, you want me—"

*I want you to stop narrating,* it said, and for the first time there was something in the voice that was not my voice, something underneath, vast and patient and old as the chalk, the way the sea is under a recording of the sea. *I want you to stop filing. I want one hour of your unguarded mind. That is all an invitation is. That is all it has ever been. The children were easier. Their doors were never locked.*

I pressed my forehead against the cold stone. Above me the bell tolled, and tolled, patient as tide, and somewhere in the tolling I could hear—this is going to sound like madness, and I have stopped caring what things sound like—the spaces between the notes filling with voices. Thin. Small. A century of them. Not screaming. *Reciting.* Saying everything they'd never gotten to say, all at once, forever, into an ear the size of a parish.

"Then take it," I heard myself say. "The hour. Take it and let me go."

The silence that followed had a different texture than the others. Almost gentle.

*That is not mine to give,* it said. *You were not invited by me, Xanthe Joubert. You were invited by men. The invitation is theirs to answer, and the answering has terms, and the terms have a keeper, and the keeper's chair has been empty for thirty years, which is why I have grown so very, very attentive. Ask the woman who sells rope. She knows about empty chairs.*

"Maren," I said. "Maren Ostrow."

*Ask her what she was meant to carry down here in the winter of '94, and what she did instead, and what this village has lost every year since, field by field, child by child, because she would not sit in the chair. Ask her. She is coming down to you now, against the council's orders, with a lantern and a lie in her mouth. I can hear her heartbeat on the stairs. I can hear everyone's heartbeat. That is what I am. I am the winter, and the winter hears everything, and the only question that has ever mattered in Hollowmere is who is willing to sit still and listen back.*

The bell tolled. The red moonlight—impossible, underground, coming through stone that had no windows—poured down the nave like something spilled.

And far above me, faint but unmistakable, boots on chalk stairs, and a woman's voice calling my name in a whisper, the way you call a name when you're afraid of who else might answer.

The presence withdrew—not gone, never gone, just folded back into the walls, into the silence, into the fine grey dust on the floorboards—and left one last thought behind it, planted in my mind like a bulb in winter ground, already growing:

*She will tell you the terms. The terms are true. But ask her, before you agree to anything, who taught the village that a stranger is cheaper than a daughter—and then ask yourself why the salt packet she gave you was opened before she put it in your pack.*
