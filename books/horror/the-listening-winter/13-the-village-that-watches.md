## 13. The Village That Watches

I want to tell you that I climbed out of the ground with dignity. That I emerged from the earth like a diver breaching a surface, blinking and brave, ready to face whatever Hollowmere had waiting for me.

What actually happened is that I came up the last three meters of the drain on my hands and knees, one arm dead from where the rockfall had pinned me, the ledger wrapped in Maren's oilcloth and strapped to my back like a hump I'd grown overnight, and I vomited into the frost before I was fully upright. Twice. The second time was mostly bile and something that might have been gratitude.

The sky over Hollowmere was the color of dishwater going grey at the edges, which meant dawn, which meant I had made it. Barely. By the terms of a bargain I still only half understood, I had made it.

And then I looked up, and understood that making it out of the ground and making it out of Hollowmere were two very different propositions.

They were all there. Every one of them.

The whole village stood in the street outside the churchyard wall — the lane, the green, the road that ran down toward the drowned mere — arranged not in a crowd but in a *line*, or rather in ranks, the way you'd stand for a photograph if the photographer had asked everyone to look like they were attending their own funeral. Forty-some people. Fifty. I counted without meaning to, the way you count exits in an unfamiliar room. Old men with their caps in their hands. Women in coats buttoned to the throat despite the fact that it wasn't cold, not really, not yet. Children held by the sleeve, kept at the front rank like ballast, or bait, or both.

Nobody spoke. Nobody moved. Nobody was even shivering, and I remember noticing that, noticing it the way you notice a wrong note in a hymn — the whole village standing stock-still in the pre-dawn chill, breath barely fogging, as though they'd been out there long enough for the cold to stop mattering. As though they'd been waiting since before dawn. Since before I started climbing.

Since, possibly, since the moment the bell began to ring below the earth, and every soul in Hollowmere had walked out of their salted doorways and taken up their positions, the way a congregation takes up pews.

"Good morning," I said, because apparently my survival instinct had been left behind in the tunnels along with most of my blood volume and my entire belief system.

No one answered. A dog somewhere behind the houses started barking and was silenced so fast, so completely, that I felt the silence more than heard it. Someone had picked that dog up. Someone had decided that this morning, of all mornings, Hollowmere would be quiet enough to hear what came up out of the ground.

I got my feet under me properly. My left boot was full of something I preferred not to inventory. The ledger's weight against my spine was the only warm thing about me, and I mean that literally — the oilcloth had gone soft and faintly damp against my back, as though the book were sweating, as though it had been carried up out of the dark and didn't care for it any more than I did.

Maren Ostrow's shop stood at the far end of the line, shuttered, its doorway conspicuously bare. The salt line that had been there when I arrived — the one she'd pretended not to have drawn while refusing to sell me rope — had been swept away. Every salt line on the street had been swept away. I could see the pale ghost-traces of them, arcs and bars of disturbed grit at every threshold, erased in the night.

They had opened their doors. All of them. They had unmade their protections and come out to stand witness, because today was the day the debt came due, and you don't watch the collection through a window.

*Well,* said the small clear voice in the back of my skull that had somehow survived everything the thing below had done to it. *At least they're honest now.*

Corvin Hale stepped forward.

He was exactly what he had been when I'd met him two days ago, shaking my hand at the church door with the grip of a man testing fruit: tall, silver-templed, dressed in a good coat, wearing the expression of a man who has spent his whole life being reasonable and has never once enjoyed it. But the reasonableness was gone now, or rather it had been set aside, folded and put away like Sunday clothes after the service, and what was underneath was simpler and much older.

"The ledger," Hale said.

Not *good morning*. Not *you survived, how remarkable*. Not even *who are you, stranger, and what have you done*. Just the noun, stripped of grammar, the way you'd speak to a delivery man.

"You'll want to introduce yourself first," I said. "It's traditional."

Behind him, nobody laughed. Behind him, fifty faces watched me with the flat patience of livestock watching weather. One of the children — a girl of maybe eight, red-mittened, her chin tucked into her mother's coat — was looking at me with an expression I recognized, because I'd worn it myself once, in a tunnel, reading a ledger that already knew my name. It was the look of someone who has been told a story about a monster and has just realized the story was a manual.

Hale crossed the churchyard gate. He did it carefully, stepping over the swept-away trace of the salt line as though it were still there, as though the habit of protection were itself a kind of piety. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and lamp oil, and beneath that, faintly, of tallow — the same black, sweetish reek that coated the altar in the nave below, the smell I had been breathing for six hours and would probably be smelling for the rest of my life.

"You went down," he said. "You read it."

"I read some of it." I shifted the pack higher on my shoulder. "I'm given to understand the rest of it reads me."

Something moved through the ranks then — not a murmur, nothing so warm as a murmur, but a shifting, a redistribution of weight, the way a congregation shifts when the sermon turns toward them personally. Hale's jaw tightened. Whatever he'd expected me to be — frightened, broken, grateful to be alive, grateful to be *spared*, because I understood now that being alive this morning had been someone's decision, someone's arithmetic — I was not being it.

"The ledger belongs to Hollowmere," he said. "It has belonged to Hollowmere for a hundred and fourteen years. You have no right to it."

"That's interesting," I said, "because according to the ledger, Hollowmere doesn't belong to Hollowmere. It belongs to whatever's under the church, and the rent's been paid in children." I let that sit. I watched it land. I watched the faces in the front row do the terrible thing faces do when a truth they've always known is spoken aloud in daylight for the first time — the flinch, the settling, the quick sideways glance at the neighbors to see who else flinched. "So we can argue about rights, Mr. Hale. Or we can argue about what's actually in the book. Your choice. But I've been underground all night listening to something recite my worst thoughts back to me in my own voice, and I am *not* in the mood."

The silence that followed had texture. It had grain. Somewhere in the middle distance the mere lapped at its shores with a sound like slow applause.

"You don't understand what you're holding," Hale said, and here his voice changed, dropped its official register, became almost — almost — human. Pleading, nearly. "That book isn't evidence. It isn't a weapon. It's a *contract*, and contracts cut both ways. If you open it in front of these people—"

"They already know, Corvin."

Maren Ostrow came out of the crowd the way water comes through a cracked wall — no drama, just pressure finding its path. She was in her shop apron, of all things, as though she'd been interrupted mid-morning, as though this were any Friday and she'd simply stepped out for air. She planted herself between Hale and me with the unhurried finality of a woman who had clearly rehearsed this stance in her head for thirty years and was furious at herself for taking three of them to use it.

"Maren," Hale said, and his voice did something complicated — warning and weariness and, underneath, a genuine grief, the grief of a man watching the last person who might have stood with him choose the other side of the room. "Go home."

"I've been home," Maren said. "Home is why I'm here." She turned her head just enough to look at me, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "You look terrible."

"I've had a night."

"I know. I heard it." And I understood that she meant she had heard the bell, all of them had heard the bell, ringing down there under the village hour after hour while the thing below learned my name syllable by syllable, and that none of them had slept, and that some of them — I saw it flicker across a few of the older faces, guilt with nowhere to put itself — had lain awake hoping the sound would stop, which could only mean one of two things, and neither of them was mercy.

"The council voted," Hale said quietly. "You weren't there."

"No," Maren agreed. "I was digging my son's spade out of the rockfall you made, metaphorically speaking, by keeping my mouth shut about it for two days." She said it loudly. She said it to the whole street. "He sealed the drain Thursday night. Before the moon rose. Before anything was owed. He put a stranger underground and collapsed the way out behind her, and every one of you knew, and every one of you let him, because it was easier than thinking about your own kitchen tables."

A woman near the front — broad-faced, hands raw with farm work — made a small sound and put her hand over her child's ear, and then, visibly ashamed, took it away again. The child hadn't needed protecting. The child had heard worse at bedtime, probably. The child had probably been told the story as a comfort: *the village endures, the village always endures, that's what the dark is for.*

"There are terms," Hale said, and now his voice was rising, cracking along old seams. "There have always been terms, and the terms have kept us alive through winters that killed every valley around us. Through the blight. Through 'sixty-three, when the snow stood taller than the church door. You want to talk about children? Talk about the children who lived, Maren. Talk about every grandchild on this street who exists because somebody, once, had the stomach to pay what was owed—"

"With other people's children."

"With *what was accepted*!" And there it was, finally, the mask off entirely, the reasonable man burned away, and what stood in the churchyard gate was a hundred years of inherited arithmetic wearing a good coat. "Nothing was ever taken. Nothing was ever stolen. It was *invited*, freely, by parents who chose it, and the thing honors its word, it has *always* honored its word, and if this—" his hand slashed toward me, toward the lump of oilcloth on my back, "—if this outsider breaks the covenant out of ignorance or spite or whatever self-righteousness she's carrying up out of that hole, then come January there will be no salt in the world thick enough, and every one of you will watch your own hearths go out, and it will be on her head, and on yours, Maren, *yours*—"

"It's on everybody's head," Maren said. "It's been on everybody's head since 1911. That's rather the point of the book."

And then, softly, so that only the front ranks could hear, so that it traveled backward through the crowd the way cold travels through a floor:

"Read it to them, Xanthe."

I unslung the pack. My hands were shaking — cold, exhaustion, and yes, fear, plenty of it, but underneath the fear something steadier, something that had been growing in me all night in the dark while a presence beyond my comprehension recited my private shames back to me in my own voice: the stubborn, stupid, lifelong conviction of a nonbeliever confronted with undeniable proof, which is not faith at all but *auditing*. Somebody had cooked these books for a century. Somebody had to say the numbers out loud.

I unwrapped the oilcloth. The ledger lay in my arms, heavy as a sleeping animal, its cover dark with age and something darker with use. The morning had brightened another degree; the sun was coming up behind the eastern ridge, and its first light came down the street low and level, touching the front rank of villagers first, then the second, gilding them one by one like figures on a clock.

I opened it to the ribbon Maren had tied there, to the page I'd marked in the archive by lamplight with her hand steady on mine.

"Nineteen eleven," I read. "Harvest moon. *Given willingly: Tomas Ostrow, aged nine.*"

Maren didn't move. But the woman with the raw hands made the small sound again, and this time she didn't take her hand away from her child's ear, and down the street, one by one, the villagers of Hollowmere stopped looking at me and started looking at each other.

"*Nineteen twelve. Given willingly: Edith Crane, aged seven.*"

"Stop," Hale said.

"*Nineteen thirteen—*"

"I said *stop*—" He took a step forward, and Maren stepped with him, lateral, cutting the angle, and I saw that she had positioned us deliberately, that she had been positioning us since she walked out of the crowd, that the shopkeeper who wouldn't sell rope had spent her whole life learning exactly where to stand.

"Hale," she said, and used his name like a hand on his chest. "Look at them. Really look. You can take the book off her. You can probably take the book off me. And then what? You'll stand in front of fifty people who've just heard their grandparents' names read out like a butcher's tally, and you'll tell them the winter's coming, and the debt's due, and the stranger got away — and you'll ask them whose kitchen table it comes to this year." She shook her head slowly. "You've bent the terms for thirty years, Corvin. Everyone knows. Everyone's always known. The book knows. The thing under the church *knows*. And this morning it rang its bell until sunrise for a stranger, which it has never done, not once, not in living memory — and either that means nothing, or it means everything, and either way, hiding the book won't save your daughter."

At the mention of the daughter — Ilsa, seventeen, I'd seen her once through a window, carrying water — Hale went very still. Stillness of that particular quality. The stillness of a man standing on ice and hearing it begin to speak.

And that was when the bell rang.

Not the bell below. The bell above — the empty tower at our backs, the frame that had held nothing since 1911, the bronze that was supposedly melted for war metal and shipped away and gone. From inside the tower came a single stroke, deep and round and unmistakably metallic, resonating out over the frozen fields, and every head in Hollowmere turned toward it, and every face in Hollowmere went the color of the frost.

Because the empty frame had rung.

Because somewhere under our feet, its twin was answering — or perhaps it was the other way around, perhaps it had always been the other way around, perhaps the two bells had never been separate instruments at all but one voice split across two worlds, and this morning, with the ledger open in a stranger's arms and the covenant fraying thread by thread, the voice had decided to speak.

The toll faded. The echoes went out over the mere and did not come back.

In the silence afterward, Corvin Hale looked at the ledger in my arms, and then at the tower, and then at his daughter's empty window, and I watched him arrive at the understanding that the arithmetic had changed — that the variables had slipped out of his column, all of them, in a single morning.

"Give me the book," he said, but his voice had lost its floor, and we both heard it.

"No," I said, and turned the page.
