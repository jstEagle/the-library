## 16. What It Wants

The mill had been dead longer than I'd been alive, which by Hollowmere's standards practically made it a spring chicken.

Ilsa and I came at it from the stream side, through a stand of birches so pale they looked like they'd been bleached for a funeral. She ran the way you run when you've done it before—low, quiet, no wasted breath—and I ran the way you run when your entire athletic history consists of sprinting for buses. By the time we reached the mill's broken skirt of stone, my lungs were filing formal complaints and my ribs were reminding me, in detail, about the rockfall.

"Inside," she whispered.

"It's a ruin."

"It's a ruin with walls. Walls matter tonight." She said it with the flat certainty of someone reciting catechism, and I suppose in Hollowmere they were the same thing.

We went in through what had once been the loading arch. The floor was flagstone and rotting chaff, and above us the great beam of the axle still hung in its cradle, black with a century of damp, like the rib of something enormous that had lain down here to die and changed its mind halfway. Moonlight came through the collapsed roof in slanted bars—red moonlight, fat and wrong, the harvest moon that had risen three hours early and hadn't had the decency to look embarrassed about it.

Ilsa pressed her back to the wall beneath the window and pulled me down beside her. "It follows the salt," she said. "The lines. Where the salt is whole, it can't cross. Where it's broken—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. We'd both seen the field behind us, the long pale seam of scattered salt where the fence line used to run, looking less like a boundary now than like a trail of breadcrumbs leading away from a house nobody wanted us to find again.

"So we stay put," I said. "We wait for dawn. Dawn is the deadline. Maren said—"

"Maren says a lot of things." Ilsa's voice cracked on it, not from fear exactly but from something older and more tired than fear. "She's been saying things for thirty years to keep people from asking others."

"That sounds like a conversation for after sunrise."

"There might not be an after sunrise." She looked at me then, and up close her eyes were her father's eyes, which must have been a horrible thing to inherit, except that hers were full of something his weren't. "You don't understand what it is yet. None of us do. We know the rules. We don't know *it*."

"I'm getting a crash course."

Outside, the wind stopped.

Not died down. Stopped, all at once, the way a record stops when the needle lifts—the birches frozen mid-shiver, the stream suddenly silent as if it had been listening too and didn't want to interrupt. The silence had texture. It had weight. It leaned against the mill walls like a tide against a seawall.

Ilsa went white. "It found the break."

* * *

Here is what I want to say about being cornered by something that has spent a century eating invitations: it wasn't loud. That's the part no one tells you. You spend your whole life assuming horror arrives with volume—with roars and thunder and the orchestra hitting the sting. But this came in quietly, the way cold comes under a door.

The red light in the roof gaps dimmed, though the moon itself didn't move. Shadows deepened in the corners of the mill until the corners stopped being corners and started being suggestions of elsewhere. And the air filled with a sensation I can only describe as *attention*—the feeling of being read, thoroughly, by something patient enough to take its time.

Then it spoke. And by spoke, I mean it did the thing it had been doing since the nave, but closer now, intimate, inside the architecture of my own skull rather than merely echoing off it.

**YOU RAN.**

"Yeah," I said, because apparently my survival instinct had been replaced by sarcasm somewhere around the rockfall. "Shocking development. Cornered person runs. Write it up."

Ilsa grabbed my arm. "Don't answer it. It uses what you give it."

**IT USES WHAT YOU ARE,** the presence said, gently, almost kindly, which was infinitely worse. **THE GIRL IS RIGHT. AND YOU HAVE BEEN GIVING US YOURSELF SINCE THE CHURCH.**

The shadows moved—not toward us, just rearranged, the way furniture rearranges in a dream. And then the voice began doing the thing I had hoped never to hear again: it started repeating me back to myself.

**YOU WERE EIGHT. YOUR FATHER SAID GOD WAS A STORY FOR PEOPLE AFRAID OF THE DARK, AND YOU BELIEVED HIM BECAUSE BELIEVING HIM MEANT BELIEVING HE WAS BRAVE INSTEAD OF AFRAID.**

"Stop," I said.

**YOU HAVE NOT PRAYED ONCE TONIGHT. NOT EVEN ACCIDENTALLY. NOT EVEN THE WAY PEOPLE SAY OH GOD WHEN THEY FALL DOWN STAIRS. THAT IS A KIND OF DISCIPLINE. WE ADMIRE DISCIPLINE.**

"I said stop—"

**YOU ARE NOT AFRAID OF DYING. YOU CHECKED, IN THE TUNNELS, WHILE THE ROCKFALL CAME DOWN. YOU WERE AFRAID OF DYING WITHOUT UNDERSTANDING. THAT IS THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN YOU AND THEM.**

Ilsa was staring at me. "Is it—is it really saying those things?"

"Yes." My voice came out sanded flat. "It's been doing it since the nave. Every thought I've ever tried not to think. It keeps receipts."

**WE KEEP EVERYTHING,** it said. **THAT IS WHAT NO ONE IN THIS VILLAGE HAS EVER UNDERSTOOD. THEY THINK WE ARE HUNGER. HUNGER IS ONLY THE SHAPE WE MAKE SO THEY CAN SURVIVE KNOWING US.**

The temperature dropped. My breath came out silver. Somewhere above us, faint and impossible, I heard the bell—a single soft toll, carried down through forty feet of earth and half a mile of night, the lower bell ringing to an empty tower, or maybe not empty, maybe resonant, maybe the two halves of a split instrument remembering each other across the dark.

And the entity paused. Listened upward. Something in its attention turned, briefly, homesick.

"What are you?" I asked. And this time I asked it the way you ask anything that matters—quietly, and meaning it.

The silence stretched. Then:

**WE ARE THE LISTENING.**

"That's not an answer."

**IT IS THE ONLY ANSWER THERE HAS EVER BEEN. YOUR VILLAGES BUILT A CHURCH OVER US BECAUSE BUILDING OVER IS WHAT PEOPLE DO WITH THINGS THEY CANNOT NAME. THEN THEIR HARVEST FAILED AND THEIR CHILDREN STARVED, AND ONE NIGHT A MOTHER CAME DOWN INTO THE DARK AND DID NOT ASK US TO LEAVE. SHE ASKED US TO LISTEN TO HER GRIEF. SO WE DID. IT WAS THE FIRST THING ANYONE HAD GIVEN US FREELY.**

The voice softened further, and I hated how much it sounded like grief itself, how convincing, how *reasonable*.

**A GRIEF GIVEN FREELY CAN BE HELD. A HELD THING SUSTAINS US. AND A SUSTAINED THING CAN GIVE BACK. WE GAVE THEM WINTERS THEY COULD SURVIVE. WE GAVE THEM SPRINGS. ALL WE ASKED IS THAT SOMEONE STAY BELOW AND KEEP THE DOOR OPEN. A KEEPER. A HAND ON THE BELL.**

"A keeper," I repeated. "That's what the bell is for."

**THE BELL IS THE INVITATION MADE BRONZE. RUNG BY A WILLING VOICE, IT OPENS THE DOOR WIDE ENOUGH FOR GIVING. RUNG BY NOTHING, THE DOOR NARROWS. AND A NARROW DOOR—** the attention contracted around us, sudden and vast, the listening becoming appetite in a single breath— **A NARROW DOOR MAKES US TAKE.**

Ilsa's hand found mine in the dark. Her fingers were ice.

"Hale said you take children," she said, and her voice shook but held. "The ledger. Nineteen eleven. They gave you children."

**THEY GAVE. WE RECEIVED. THERE IS A DIFFERENCE, BUT WE UNDERSTAND IF THE DISTINCTION COMFORTS NO ONE.** A pause, and something almost like weariness moved through the dark. **WE DID NOT ASK FOR CHILDREN. WE ASKED FOR A KEEPER. THE VILLAGE DECIDED CHILDREN WERE CHEAPER. THE VILLAGE HAS BEEN DECIDING THINGS WERE CHEAPER FOR A HUNDRED YEARS, AND EACH TIME, THE DEBT GREW, BECAUSE A THING GIVEN UNWILLINGLY FEEDS THE TAKING PART OF US AND STARVES THE REST.**

"And the rest is starving now," I said. Understanding arrived the way cold water arrives—all at once, everywhere, no mercy in it. "That's why the fields are dying. Why the bell frame upstairs is empty. Why the winters are getting worse. You're not getting stronger. You're getting *hungrier*, because every keeper for a century has bent the terms, and every bent term feeds the wrong half of you."

**YES.**

"And if there's no keeper at all—if the invitation goes unanswered tonight—"

**THEN WE STOP HOLDING.** The words fell into the silence like stones into a well. **EVERYTHING WE HAVE CONTAINED FOR A CENTURY COMES UP AT ONCE. NOT OUT OF CRUELTY. OUT OF STRUCTURE. A DAM DOES NOT HATE THE VALLEY. IT SIMPLY FAILS.**

I looked at Ilsa. She looked at me. Between us, unspoken, sat the same arithmetic: a village full of children, a night full of hunger, and one stranger crouched in a ruined mill who had walked into this story believing the worst thing under Hollowmere was bad drainage.

"You want me," I said slowly. "Not my body. Not my blood. You want someone willing. Someone who rings the bell and holds the door and *stays*. That's the price. That's what it's actually been, all along, under everything Hale and his council have layered on top of it."

**YES.**

"And if I say no?"

**THEN WE WILL TAKE UNTIL SOMEONE SAYS YES. WE ALWAYS HAVE. THE DIFFERENCE IS THAT THIS TIME, THE TAKING WOULD NOT STOP AT ONE.**

The red moonlight shifted through the broken roof. Far away—or not far away at all; distance had stopped meaning anything—I heard dogs begin to howl in the village, and then stop all together, mid-note, as if a hand had closed over the sound.

Ilsa was crying without noise, the way you learn to cry in a house like hers. "They'll seal you back in," she whispered. "My father. He'll vote it. He already voted it. He'll put you below and tell everyone it's done and ring nothing, because he thinks the terms are a cage instead of a covenant, he's never understood—"

"Ilsa."

"—and you'd be down there forever, alone, with *that*—"

"*Ilsa.*" I squeezed her hand. "Breathe."

Because here was the thing, the terrible clarity of it, arriving in me like the toll of a bell I hadn't struck yet: I had spent thirty years being proud of not believing in anything. And now something ancient was offering me a bargain, and the bargain was honest—more honest than any contract I'd ever signed, more honest than Hale, more honest than the pretty lies villages tell themselves to sleep at night. It wanted a keeper. It had always wanted a keeper. Everything else—the children, the harvests, the salt, the fear—was scaffolding the living had built around a simple, awful truth: *somebody has to hold the door.*

The only question left was whether somebody would hold it willingly, or whether the door would come off its hinges.

"The ledger," I said to the dark. "The book in the undercroft. My name's already in it, isn't it. The ink was waiting."

**THE BOOK RECORDS WHAT THE HOUSE EXPECTS. THE HOUSE HAS EXPECTED YOU SINCE YOU STEPPED OFF THE BUS AND READ THE EMPTY BELL FRAME AND THOUGHT: *THAT'S NOT VANDALISM. THAT'S AMNESIA.***

I laughed. I couldn't help it—a short, cracked, unhinged little laugh that echoed wrong in the mill, and the entity let it echo, patient as winter.

"If I do this," I said, "if I go down there and ring that bell—it doesn't end. You know that. I know that. It's not a rescue, it's a job. Forever."

**FOREVER IS LONGER THAN YOU IMAGINE AND SHORTER THAN YOU FEAR. AND YOU WILL NOT BE ALONE. WE ARE EXCELLENT COMPANY, FOR THOSE WHO CAN STAND BEING KNOWN.**

"That's the most sinister sales pitch I've ever heard."

**WE HAVE HAD A CENTURY TO PRACTICE.**

Above us, through the broken roof, the harvest moon cleared the last of the birches and flooded the ruin with red. And in that light I saw them—faint, at the edge of the loading arch, shapes standing in the field beyond. Not the entity. People. Villagers, dozens of them, motionless, faces turned toward the mill, holding lanterns whose flames burned straight and colorless as needles.

Hale had gathered his flock. And they had come not to save me, and not to mourn me, but to watch—to see whether the stranger would walk into the ground willingly, or whether they would have to carry me.

"They're coming," Ilsa breathed.

"No," I said, standing, my ribs screaming, my breath silvering the air. "They're *waiting*. There's a difference, and tonight I finally understand all of them."

I looked at the shapes in the field, and past them, toward the village, toward the church tower standing black against the red moon with its empty frame like a held breath—and below it, far below, the other bell, waiting a hundred years for a hand that chose it.

"Take me to the church," I told Ilsa. "Both of them. Before the moon peaks."

**YES,** said the listening dark, softly, hungrily, hopefully. **YES.**
