## 18. Council of Knives

I want to say I walked back into Hollowmere like a conquering hero, ledger under my arm, truth blazing in my free hand like a torch. What actually happened is that I limped up the cart road at half past six in the morning, smelling of tallow and tunnel water, with a gash on my forearm I'd bandaged using a page of parish records because irony was the only thing I had left in abundance.

The village was already awake. Of course it was. Half of it had probably never slept.

They were waiting in the square—not gathered so much as *arranged*, the way iron filings arrange around a magnet you can't see. Maren stood near the well with her arms folded. Ilsa Hale hovered at the edge of her father's porch, wearing the expression of someone who has recently done something unforgivable and would do it again. And Corvin Hale stood front and center, hands clasped behind his back like a man greeting a delayed train rather than a dead woman walking.

"You're supposed to be below," he said.

"Your rockfall had other opinions." I held up the ledger. "And so do I."

* * *

Maren had rung the upper church bell to call them in—actually rung it, first time in years by the sound of it, a cracked and rusty complaint that nevertheless carried across every rooftop in Hollowmere. The nave filled with families. Farmers, shopkeepers, the schoolteacher, two brothers who ran the garage on the far side of the green. Children too, which surprised me until I understood: there was no one left to watch them. Every adult this village had was in this room, and every one of them knew why.

I set the ledger on the altar rail. My hands were shaking. I'd like to tell you it was from cold.

"This book," I said, "goes back to 1911. It records every harvest invitation for a hundred and fourteen years. Names. Dates. Ages."

"Put it away," Hale said quietly.

"The early entries are children, Mr. Hale. The youngest was four."

The silence that followed wasn't the silence of shock. That's what I want you to understand, sitting wherever you are, in whatever warm room you've got. It wasn't shock. It was the silence of people hearing out loud the thing they had all agreed never to say aloud, and discovering that saying it changed nothing—that the words went into the air and came back down as weather, and the weather was the same weather it had always been.

Edith Marlowe, ninety if she was a day, spoke from the third pew. "We know what's in that book, girl. We buried most of it."

"Then bury it properly," I said. "Because it isn't just names. It's arithmetic." I opened the ledger to the pages I'd marked in the archive, my thumbnail still black from the tunnels. "Look at the intervals. 1911 through 1930, an invitation every year. Then gaps—two years, three, five. You know why? Because the hunger grew slower when they fed it children. Small offerings, frequent. Then somewhere around the fifties somebody decided children were off the menu, and the invitations got bigger and rarer, and the winters got worse anyway, and the fields started dying. You didn't reform the bargain. You just found a way to pay less often for more."

"That's not—" someone began.

"And then there's this." I turned to the last written entry before mine. "Thomas Ostrow. 1994. Age nineteen. Except Thomas Ostrow didn't die in 1994, did he? He moved to Leeds. He sends Christmas cards. I checked the parish register on my way through—the burial record is in a different hand than the rest of the book. Fresher ink. Someone wrote him into the ledger who was never taken at all."

Every head in the church turned toward Corvin Hale with the slow, synchronized dread of a field of sunflowers following a dying sun.

Hale didn't flinch. I'll give him that. He came down the aisle like a man walking to collect a delivery, and he looked at me with something almost like pity.

"You've been below," he said. "You've heard it. You know what happens if we don't answer. So ask yourself, stranger—what exactly do you think your book is going to buy us? Mercy?"

"I think it's going to buy you the truth about what you've been doing."

"We know what we've been doing." His voice didn't rise. That was the worst part; it never rose. "That's the whole point of a covenant. Everyone knows. Everyone agrees. Everyone carries it together, so no one has to carry it alone. And now you want to hand each family their own private piece of the weight and call it honesty." He gestured at the pews. "Ask them if they want it."

Nobody asked. Nobody answered. The church creaked in the wind like a ship deciding whether to go down.

* * *

It was Edith Marlowe who broke first, and she broke sideways, which is how old villages break.

"My mother gave my brother," she said, to no one, to the rafters. "Famine winter. She used to say his name in her sleep until the day she died, and I used to think it was grief, but it wasn't, was it. It was *terms*. She was keeping the terms. You don't stop saying the name or it forgets the arrangement—" She stopped, pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

"It doesn't work like that," Hale said.

"How would you know?" Edith's voice came up off the floor like something exhumed. "How would *you* know, Corvin, you who've never given anything but other people's—"

"Careful."

"—other people's *children*!" It tore out of her, and then the church tore with it.

I have seen bar fights. I have seen a planning committee meeting in a Welsh village over a proposed phone mast, which is worse. But I had never seen a community come apart at a seam a century old, and I am here to tell you that it does not come apart loudly at first. It comes apart in accusations pitched low, in chairs turning, in families rotating physically away from other families like magnets flipping poles. The garage brothers squared off against the schoolteacher, whose grandfather's name sat three entries from theirs. A woman I didn't know said the word *Ostrow* like a curse and Maren said *say it to my face* and for one bright second I thought the whole thing would stay verbal, and then—

Then Hale moved.

Not at me. At the ledger. He crossed the last three feet of aisle with a speed that made nonsense of his age, and his hand closed on the book, and I did the only intelligent thing I have ever done under pressure: I let go.

He staggered back with the ledger in both hands, off balance, triumphant—and the church went very quiet, because everyone had just watched their elder steal the evidence, and watching a thing and admitting you've watched it are different animals entirely.

"Give it back, Da," Ilsa said.

"Ilsa—"

"You wrote Tommy Ostrow in yourself. Didn't you." It wasn't a question. She came down the aisle slowly, and I saw now where her steadiness came from; it was his, inverted. "When Grandad was keeper. You bent the terms before I could even walk. Every year since, you've been spending down a debt you refinanced in secret, and this year the payment comes due and it's—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through— "it's me. Or the stranger. And you picked the stranger because she's nobody. Because nobody will come looking."

"There is no one coming to look," Hale said. "That is precisely the point of Hollowmere."

"No," I said, and took a step forward, and every face came back to me, and I realized with a lurch that I had an audience, that I had maybe thirty seconds of an audience, and that I had spent my whole life being believed by strangers and never once by a room. "There's me. There's the survey filed with the county council. There's a colleague who expects a report by Monday. You should have let the rockfall finish the job, Mr. Hale, because I'm the loudest thing that's ever happened to this village, and I'm standing in your church holding nothing at all, and you still look frightened."

He did look frightened. Not of me. Of the ledger in his hands, which he was gripping hard enough to bend the boards.

"Read the last page," I said softly. "Go on. Read what's written there."

His jaw worked. He opened the book. And I watched Corvin Hale read his own daughter's name—Ilsa Hale, harvest moon, age eighteen—in ink that had been waiting in the ledger since before either of us drew breath, in handwriting that matched none of the keepers, in a script that was not quite human and had never needed to be.

The ledger hit the stone floor. He dropped it like it had bitten him, which, in fairness, it had.

And underneath the church—under the flagstones, under the crypt, under the second nave that most of these people pretended not to remember existed—a bell tolled once. Soft. Patient. The sound of something checking its watch.

The congregation came apart for good then, all at once, a hundred and forty years of shared silence shattering into shouting and weeping and the scrape of benches, and above the noise Hale's voice cut clean as a knife off a strop:

"Seal the doors. Nobody leaves. If it can't be answered by sunrise, it takes what it wants—and I'll be damned before it starts with mine."

I looked down at the fallen ledger, open on the stones between us all, its blank final page drinking the candlelight.

So much for exposing the truth.

Now I just had to survive the meeting.
