## 20. Invitation Answered

The rope was where Maren said it would be, coiled on the stone lip of the bell frame like something left out for me. Like an offering. I suppose that's what it was.

I want to tell you that I descended those stairs with courage. That my hands were steady and my mind clear and that some part of me had made peace with what I was about to do. But the truth is uglier and more honest: I went down because the alternative was worse. Because the thing in the dark had already learned the shape of my name from the inside of my skull, and because somewhere above me, a village full of people who had spent a century feeding children to a listening dark were waiting to see if a stranger would do what none of them ever could.

The lower nave was waiting for me too. It always is, I think. That's the whole horror of Hollowmere — nothing here waits *patiently*. It waits hungrily.

The tallow-black altar caught the light of my headlamp and threw it back wrong, the way deep water throws back light. The bronze bell hung above it in its frame of black oak, and even before I touched it I could feel it — not hear it, *feel* it — a low vibration in the floor, in my teeth, in the wet meat of my lungs. It had been ringing all night, on and off, patient as tide. And each time it rang, something below answered. Something that was no longer below at all.

Because it was here. In the room with me. In the walls. In the space between my ribs where I keep the things I don't say out loud.

*You came back,* it said, and it didn't use sound. It used memory. My mother's voice, the way she said my name when I was small and feverish. *You always come back. You came back to your father's house after he died, just to stand in his kitchen. You came back to the tunnels under Marseille after they collapsed. You are a creature that returns to the places that hurt you.*

"That's not—" I started, and stopped, because arguing with it was exactly what it wanted. Every thought I gave it, it wore. Every denial, it dressed itself in. I had learned that much in the hours since the rockfall: this thing did not feed on fear or flesh or faith. It fed on *attention*. On being heard. A hundred years of invitations, and every single one of them had been, at bottom, someone agreeing to listen.

So I didn't listen. I climbed.

The bell frame was old oak gone soft as bread, and the wheel beside it was crusted with a century of dust except for one arc, polished smooth by hands. Keeper's hands. Generations of them. I looped Maren's rope through the wheel's iron cleat and wrapped it twice around my forearm, and the cold of the bronze came up through the rope like the cold of a hand.

*I know what you're doing,* it said, gentle now, almost tender. It had found my father's voice too. Of course it had. *You think ringing the bell binds me. You read it in their ledger, in their frightened little marginalia. But Xanthe. Xanthe, look at me.*

I looked. God help me, I looked — because it asked in my grandmother's voice, dead eleven years, and there is a limit to what a person can refuse.

It wasn't a shape. It was the absence of one, a standing column of *listening*, and where it stood the air bent inward like heat-haze, like the world leaning close to eavesdrop. And in the middle of it, held up like a lantern, like a gift, was every thought I had ever tried not to think. The night in Lisbon I'd walked past a man having a seizure in a doorway and told myself someone else would call for help. The relief — the actual, shameful *relief* — when my father's will turned out to leave everything to my brother. The way I'd felt, three days ago, standing in Maren Ostrow's shop: *these people deserve whatever lives under their church.*

*There,* it said, warm as a bath. *That one. You still believe that one. Shall I tell you what else you believe?*

"No," I said, and pulled the rope.

The bell moved. The clapper struck. And the sound —

I have been in earthquakes. I have stood next to a train derailment. I have never heard anything like that bell. Not because it was loud, though it was loud enough to crack the tallow on the altar in long black veins, but because the sound went *through* me rather than past me, and in the half-second of its decay I felt every bone in my body hum at a frequency that was somehow also a word. Not a word in any language. A word in the language of contracts. Of terms. Of *binding*.

And above me — far above, up through the rock and the roots and the ruined undercroft and the dead church with its empty tower — something answered.

A hum. Low, vast, resonant, coming down through the stone itself, and I understood then what I should have understood in the archive with Maren, what the ledger had been trying to tell me in its crabbed century-old hand: the two bells had been cast as one. One instrument, split in 1911, half melted for war metal and half buried, and the empty tower above wasn't empty at all. It was the other half of the mouth. The bell below spoke, and the tower above *resonated*, and between them — stretched across the whole dark spine of Hollowmere — the covenant had a voice again.

The entity screamed.

Not with sound. With *absence* of sound — the nave went silent so completely that I heard my own blood, heard the wet click of my own eyes moving, and in that silence the presence convulsed, the heat-haze of it whipping like smoke in a draft, and it said, in a hundred voices at once, in the voices of everyone it had ever taken:

*WHO RINGS.*

"Xanthe Joubert," I said. My voice came out wrecked and small and it was mine. "Surveyor. Nonbeliever. Stranger." I pulled the rope again, and the bell tolled, and the tower above hummed, and the entity recoiled from the sound like a hand from a stove. "That's who rings."

*You do not know the terms—*

"I read the ledger." Another pull. Toll. Hum. The veins in the altar's tallow glowed faintly now, or maybe that was my vision going. "Every entry. Nineteen-eleven to last harvest. Names, dates, moons. I know what you've been fed. I know what they promised you." I wrapped the rope tighter, set my feet against the wheel's frame, and made myself look directly into the place where its face would be, if hunger had a face. "And I know what they never wrote down, because they were too cowardly to say it plainly. So I'll say it. Here are my terms."

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. The whole nave leaned in.

"You get one invitation per harvest," I said. "One. As written. Not Hale's arithmetic, not the council's quiet additions, not the children whose names got smaller and smaller in the ledger until they were just initials. One invitation, freely given, by the keeper — and the keeper is me now, and I am telling you the invitation is *me*. My attention. My listening. My name in your book." I hauled the rope back for another stroke. "In exchange, Hollowmere survives the winter. All of it. The fields, the children, the old ones, the liars — especially the liars, because God knows this village runs on them. And you take nothing else. No thoughts dragged out of sleeping heads. No voices in the drains. No answering the bell with anything but silence unless I ring first. Those are the terms of the house. Ring once to accept."

For a long moment, nothing. The entity hung in the dark of the nave, and I felt it considering — actually considering, the way a flood considers a levee — and I understood with total clarity that it could simply refuse. That it could take me apart thought by thought, wear my mother's face while it did it, and then go up into the village and collect a century of arrears.

Then, from above, faint and impossible, the empty tower rang.

Once.

The resonance came down through the stone and met the tolling of the bell below and where the two sounds crossed, in the air between me and the listening dark, something closed. I can't describe it better than that. A door shutting. A knot drawing tight. I felt it settle over the entity like a net over a bird, and the presence shrank, and shrank, and drew itself down toward the altar, down into the black tallow, down into the floor, and the last thing it said — and it said it in *my* voice, which I suppose was its idea of a signature —

*Paid until harvest, keeper.*

And then the nave was just a nave. Cold stone, dead air, a bell swinging itself slowly still above a cracked altar. My legs went out from under me and I sat down hard on the frozen ground and laughed, or sobbed, or both, the rope still wrapped around my forearm, the skin beneath it bruised purple in a band that I would carry for the rest of my life. Which, I understood dimly, might be a very long time indeed. Keepers, the ledger implied, were not granted quick exits.

I looked at my hands. They were shaking. They were also, I noticed with a strange detached calm, still holding the rope, and would probably be holding ropes for the rest of my life, and there was a version of me — the old version, the Lisbon version, the version who walked past people in doorways — that would have found that funny.

The new version climbed the stairs.

* * *

Coming up through the undercroft, I passed the alcove where the ledger had lived, and I stopped, because the ledger was open. I had left it closed. I had left it in the village archive, upstairs, locked, with Maren.

But there it sat on its stone shelf, open to the newest page, and in ink that glistened wet as a fresh wound, below a century of names, a new entry had written itself:

*Xanthe Joubert. Harvest moon, the year the stranger rang. Invitation given freely. Terms renewed.*

And beneath that, in smaller letters, in handwriting I recognized as my own:

*One per harvest. Nothing else. Witnessed by the bells.*

I hadn't written that. Or — I corrected myself, standing in the cold with my heart slamming — I hadn't written it *yet*. Somewhere ahead of me, in the life I'd just signed for, there was a night when I would sit at that shelf with a pen and copy out the terms in my own hand, because that is what keepers do, and the book knew it, and the book was patient.

I closed it gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child, and carried it up into the dawn.

* * *

The sun was coming up over the eastern ridge when I emerged from the drain mouth behind the church, and the whole village was in the street. Every soul. Silent. Watching. Hale at the front with his face gray as ash, Maren beside him with her jaw set like she'd argued her way down here through a wall of men, and behind them the families, the farmers, the children — the children, alive, all of them, blinking in the early light with no idea how close the arithmetic had come to including them.

They saw the ledger under my arm. They saw the rope-burn on my forearm. And one by one, without a word being spoken, they did the thing I would later understand they had done only twice before in a hundred years: they bowed their heads.

All except Corvin Hale.

He stepped forward, and his eyes went from the ledger to my face, and I watched him do the math — the same math his grandfather had done, and his father, the eternal village arithmetic of *who pays* — and when he spoke, his voice was hoarse.

"The terms," he said. "What did you give it?"

"One invitation," I said. "Mine. Freely given. And nothing else — ever — from anyone. That's the covenant now. That's what I rang into it."

Something moved behind his eyes that I couldn't name. Relief, maybe. Or grief. Or the particular despair of a man whose careful machinery has just been replaced by something worse: honesty.

"And when the next harvest comes," he said quietly, "and the one after? When you're old, keeper? When you're *gone*?"

I looked past him, at the children in the street, at Ilsa standing slightly apart from her father with her chin up and her eyes red-rimmed and fierce, and I said the truest thing I have ever said in my life:

"Then that's a problem for the page after this one."

Behind him, Maren exhaled like a woman surfacing from deep water. And high above us, in the empty tower of the dead church, in the windless morning air, the bell that wasn't there hummed — soft, brief, unmistakable.

Acknowledgment.

Or appetite.

I tightened my grip on the ledger and went to find out which.
