## 8. Counting Doors

The Floor stopped pretending to be architecture at 3:14 in the morning.

I know because I was counting. That's what I do. That's what I've always done, since I was a child pressing my palm to warm steel, since the blackout, since the first time I learned that a corridor unwatched is a corridor unmade. I count doors. I mark lights. I annotate. And at 3:14, standing in the founders' antechamber with Aldous Vell's blank eyes on me and the Floor's bargain hanging in the air like breath in cold weather, I felt the whole structure shudder — not like an earthquake, but like a throat clearing.

Then the walls began to move with intent.

Not the lazy rearrangement I'd been navigating for two days, the slow shuffle of rooms trading places when I wasn't looking. This was purposeful. The corridor outside the antechamber stretched, contracted, and tilted upward, and I understood with a clarity that felt like falling: it wasn't trying to trap me anymore.

It was trying to leave.

"The crew," I said. "The excavation site. There are eleven people up there, Aldous. There's a whole city up there."

Aldous didn't answer. He hadn't answered in hours. He sat in the corner of the ritual chamber with his hands flat on his knees, mouthing numbers — door numbers, floor numbers, the serial numbers of his own dissolving thoughts — and when I grabbed his shoulder he said, "It counts better than you," in a voice like paper tearing.

"Then I'll count better than it."

I left him there. I've hated myself for it since, but I left him, because the Floor was rising and I was the only person in Vell who knew the geometry of the thing chasing them. I clipped my headlamp on, checked my backup, checked my backup's backup, and stepped into a corridor that was no longer pretending to be a corridor at all.

* * *

Here is what I know about the Floor, and what I knew then, in the way you only know things when your life depends on the knowing:

It is not a place. It is a hunger wearing the shape of a place. The founders didn't build it; they caught it, and a caught thing needs a cage, and a cage made of brick is just a suggestion. So they made the cage out of something stronger. They made it out of a mind. A living mapmaker's memory — the one material the Floor cannot digest, because memory is the act of insisting a thing happened, and the Floor is the act of insisting nothing is fixed.

Every door in Vell was opening inward.

I found the first one forty feet up the rising corridor — a door that shouldn't have existed, standing free in a wall of raw earth, and it opened as I watched, swinging inward with the courtesy of a hotel porter. Beyond it: not a room. A street. Marlowe Street, the actual Marlowe Street, four levels above the foundry, seen through a doorway standing in the dirt like the mouth of a well. I could see the all-night bakery. I could see the fog. And I could see, drifting out of the bakery's open front door, a darkness that moved like water finding a drain.

The Floor was growing doors. It was threading itself into the city the way roots thread into a foundation — patiently, invisibly, until the day the building leans.

I marked it. Red grease pencil, on the doorframe, in my shorthand: *M-ST, 3:31, OPENS IN*. My hands were shaking. I marked it anyway.

Because that's the thing. That's the whole thing. The Floor can rearrange stone, can furnish rooms with my childhood, can keep an old man alive for eighty years as a lock and scrape him hollow doing it. But it cannot hold a fact that someone insists on. My maps are not records. They are arguments. Every line I draw is me telling the Floor: *you were here, like this, at this time, and I will not agree to your version.*

It hates that. It hates me for it.

Good.

* * *

I ran the corridors like a surveyor running a gun line, and the corridors fought me the whole way up.

Stairs that gained landings as I climbed. Landings that lost doors. A hallway that repeated itself three times, and each time I walked it I counted the doors aloud — *four, five, six, seven* — and each time the count came out different, and each time I wrote the discrepancy down, because a discrepancy is a fingerprint. The Floor could shuffle its rooms but it couldn't stop me from noticing the shuffle, and every notice was a nail.

The third repetition, the hallway gave up and became a different hallway. I heard it change — an actual sound, like a deck being riffled — and when I turned the corner I was looking at the crew's break trailer, the real one, seen through a doorway standing in the foundry's sub-basement wall.

Eleven people. I counted them. Reyes on the radio, not talking to anyone, just holding the handset like a rosary. Okafor asleep sitting up. The night cook staring at a door in the trailer's far wall that had not been there at dinner — a door with no frame, no hinges, standing an inch off the floor, opening inward, and behind it a darkness that had texture, that had *patience*.

"The door's a lie!" I shouted through the gap, and my voice came out wrecked. "Don't touch it! It opens inward because there's nothing on the other side — it's a mouth, not a door!"

Reyes looked up. Looked at me standing in a wall that had no reason to contain me. And then — I will remember this as long as I remember anything — he looked at the door, and he picked up Okafor's coffee, and he threw it through the opening.

The darkness drank it without a sound. No splash. No hiss. The coffee just stopped being coffee.

Reyes got everyone out of the trailer in ninety seconds. I counted. Ninety seconds, eleven people, and the door swung wider behind them like something unhurried, like something that knew there would be other doors, in other walls, all over the city, and that the city was full of people who had never once in their lives counted anything.

* * *

Above ground, Vell was starting to remember.

I felt it before I saw it — a pressure change, the specific wrongness of a city's attention turning. Through the rising corridors, through the doors opening inward on streets I'd mapped a hundred times, I could hear it: car alarms in patterns too regular to be random. A church bell ringing nine times at 3:50 in the morning. And voices — dozens of voices, on porches, at windows, in the fog, all saying the same words in the flat tone of people reading from a prompt only they can see:

*"The Ninth Floor. The Ninth Floor. There were nine floors."*

The founders had excised the Floor from every blueprint, every deed, every portrait and parish record. But they'd excised it from the city's memory too, and a memory is not a document. A memory can be given back. The Floor was giving itself back, door by door, street by street, and every citizen of Vell was waking up with a floor plan in their head that they'd never drawn and couldn't refuse.

I passed a doorway onto Candlemaker's Row and saw an old woman standing in her nightgown in the fog, drawing on her own front door with a crayon — drawing corridors, doorways, a staircase going down — with the sure, sick fluency of someone copying from memory. She looked at me through the gap and said, "You're the mapmaker," and it wasn't a question, and it wasn't comfort, and I ran.

* * *

The last stretch was the worst, because the Floor stopped fighting me and started *helping*.

Corridors straightened when I ran them. Doors opened before I reached them. Stairs delivered me upward with the smooth efficiency of a thing that wanted me somewhere — and I knew exactly where. The ritual chamber. The half-finished rite. The final hand. The Floor had spent two days trying to lose me in its geometry and had finally done the math: better to have me finish the ritual than to rise into a city with no lock at all. Or so it wanted me to believe. Or so I told myself, running, because the alternative was to stop running.

I counted anyway. I counted every door — *one, two, three, the door that opens on Marlowe, four, five, the door that opens on the bakery, six* — and I marked every light, and I annotated every discrepancy, and with every mark I felt the Floor flinch. It was subtle, the flinch. A corridor hesitating before it shifted. A door taking a half-second longer to open. But it was there, and it was mine.

My obsession. My stupid, lifelong, ruinous obsession. The thing that had cost me every friendship, every relationship, every year above ground. The thing Marguerite Vell had counted on when she'd hired me, because a marked mapmaker is a hungry mapmaker, and hunger is a leash.

She'd been wrong about one thing. Hunger is also a whetstone.

The Floor could digest fear. It had eaten Aldous's fear for eighty years, course by course, until there was nothing left but numbers. It could digest memory — that was the whole ritual, that was the whole horror of it, memory was its food and its building material both. But it could not digest the act of *noticing*. It could not digest a mind that met every rearrangement with a pencil and a count and the flat, stubborn refusal to be confused.

I was not food. I was a survey. And surveys get finished.

* * *

The ritual chamber door was open when I reached it. Of course it was. It opened inward.

Inside, the founders' half-finished rite covered the walls in lines that hurt to look at — the Floor's true geometry, the shape of the caught thing, drawn by eight generations of hands and left one wall short of complete. Aldous sat where I'd left him, mouthing numbers, and as I crossed the threshold he stopped. Looked at me. And for one second — one second only — something surfaced in those scraped-hollow eyes, something that might have been the man he'd been, and he said:

"It's counting your footsteps. It's been counting them since the door. It knows how many you have left."

Behind me, down the corridor I'd just climbed, I heard my own footsteps coming back to me. Counted back in a voice like paper tearing. One. Two. Three.

I picked up the chalk.

The wall was waiting. The city above was remembering. And I had maybe a hundred steps' worth of time to draw the thing below us exactly, perfectly, from memory — knowing what the ritual would demand as payment, knowing what I'd have to feed it, knowing I would hand over my mother's face and call it a fair trade.

The footsteps reached the doorway.

The Ninth Ritual begins where the counting ends.
