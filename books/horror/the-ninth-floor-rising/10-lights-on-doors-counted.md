## 10. Lights On, Doors Counted

The morning I walked out of the Foundry site, the sun was doing something ordinary and obscene — sitting fat and gold over the cranes, gilding the chain-link, warming the mud. A workman in a hi-vis vest nodded at me like I was nobody, which, as far as he knew, I was.

The door was gone.

I stood in the excavation pit where it had been — that warm steel slab bolted shut from the inside — and found only a wall of poured concrete, curing gray, unremarkable, already tagged with a surveyor's chalk number. *B-14.* The crew moved around me with the easy boredom of men who had never found anything stranger than a pipe. One of them complained about his ex-wife. Another was eating a sandwich with the crusts cut off. Nobody mentioned a door. Nobody mentioned the three weeks the site had sat empty, the foreman who'd vanished, the blackout that had swallowed the whole east grid.

I asked the sandwich man if he'd ever seen a steel door down there.

He looked at me like I'd asked if he'd ever seen a whale in the break room. "Door? Nah. It's all slab and rebar. Been slab and rebar since we broke ground."

* * *

Marguerite Vell paid me in silence, which is to say she paid me in full and said almost nothing at all.

Her office on the top floor of the Vell building smelled of paper and cold tea. She sat behind a desk older than either of us, and she counted out the final installment in a cashier's check and one envelope of cash, the way you'd pay a debt you didn't want a record of. Her hands were steady. Her eyes weren't. They kept going to the window behind me, to the city, as if she expected it to answer her.

"You walked out," she said.

"I walked out."

"And the Floor?"

I opened my mouth to answer and found the shape of the answer and nothing inside it. It was like reaching into a pocket you knew had a key and finding the lining sewn shut. "The Floor," I said, "is handled."

She studied me for a long moment. Whatever she was looking for — grief, madness, the scraped-blank stare of a hollow cartographer — she didn't find it, and that seemed to frighten her more.

"You don't remember," she said. Not a question.

"I remember the contract. The survey. The door." I ticked them off. "I don't remember what came after the door."

Marguerite closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was eighty years older and looked relieved about it. "Then it took its payment. That's how it's supposed to work." She slid the check across the desk. "Don't come back, Ms. Coldharbour. Don't map anything under the old city. Take the money and survey bridges. Bridges are honest."

At the elevator, she stopped me with one word.

"Oscar."

I turned.

"Whatever you gave it," she said, "it's keeping it safe. That's the horror of the thing. It doesn't destroy. It *keeps.*"

The elevator doors closed on her face, and I rode down thirty floors trying to remember what I'd given away, and finding, each time I reached for it, a smooth white wall where a door should be.

* * *

Weeks passed. I did what I always did, because whatever else had been taken, the habit remained, rooted deeper than memory.

I kept my lights on. Every room in my apartment, every hour, even sleeping — I'd wake at three in the morning and the whole place would be burning with lamplight, and I'd lie there with my heart hammering and not know why, only that the dark had a texture I refused to think about. I counted doors. On my street there were eleven between my building and the corner. I counted them every morning and every night, and if a new one appeared — a construction hoarding, a boarded shopfront — I stood in front of it until my pulse came down, and then I mapped it. Drew it. Annotated it. Filed it.

I didn't know why the habit mattered. That was the worst part. The rituals had survived; the reasons had not. It was like watching my hands do surgery with no medical training — precise, certain, and utterly unexplained. I kept a notebook by my bed. The first page said, in my own handwriting: *You wrote this before. You don't remember writing it. Trust the handwriting.*

I trusted the handwriting.

I went back to work. Small contracts. Storm drains off Corvess Street, a culvert under the rail cut, the kind of jobs that paid in invoices and never in nightmares. I mapped them clean and came home clean, and every time I descended a ladder into the dark, I turned my light on before my feet left the top rung, and I counted the doors at the bottom, and I never, ever let the dark count my footsteps back to me.

I didn't know why that rule existed.

I followed it anyway.

* * *

The map appeared on a Tuesday.

I came home from the culvert job, wet to the knee, and found it on my wall — the big blank wall above my drafting table that had been, that morning, blank. I was sure it had been blank. I would have sworn to it.

It wasn't blank now.

Pinned there was a sheet of drafting film, and on it, in my own hand — my lettering, my line weights, my little annotation flags, the whole private grammar of a lifetime's obsession — was a map of tunnels I had never surveyed.

They ran beneath the old city, deeper than the storm system, deeper than the Foundry. They didn't correspond to anything in the archives, because I checked, that same night, pulling every plan the records office would give me and holding them up to the light. The tunnels on my wall matched nothing. They matched themselves. They were confident. They were *finished* — surveyed to the millimeter, every junction numbered, every door counted, and there were so many doors, dozens of them, each one marked with my small careful notation for *opens inward.*

At the center of the map, drawn in a hand that was mine and not mine, was a chamber. And beneath the chamber, in my own lettering, a single line of annotation:

*The lock holds. The record keeps. — O.C.*

I sat down on the floor of my apartment with every light burning and looked at the map for a long time.

Somewhere below the city, in a dark I had paid more than I could name never to see again, something was keeping a perfect record of everything I'd given away. My mother's face. The reason I explored at all. The shape of the answer I'd carried to Marguerite's question. It kept them the way a wall keeps its bricks — and apparently, some nights, it lent them back.

I got up. I took a fresh sheet of drafting film, clipped it to my board, and began copying the map — because that was the discipline, that was the ritual, that was the thing my hands knew how to do even when my mind had been sewn shut. Count. Annotate. Never let a space go unmapped.

Halfway through copying, I stopped.

There was a new corridor on the original. It hadn't been there when I sat down. It branched off the central chamber and ran west, under the river, and at its end was a door I had not drawn, annotated in my own handwriting with a notation I did not recognize and understood completely:

*This one opens outward.*

I turned on every light in the apartment. All of them. Then I picked up my pen, and — because the handwriting is mine, and I trust the handwriting — I began to map it.
