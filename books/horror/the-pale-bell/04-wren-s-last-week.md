## 4. Wren's Last Week

### Seven Days Before

Wren Calloway was nine years old and she could hold her breath for forty-one seconds.

She knew this because she had tested it in the bathtub on Tuesday, cheeks puffed, eyes squeezed shut against the steam, counting the seconds in her head the way her mother counted when she baked bread—one-Mississippi, two-Mississippi—until her lungs burned like she'd swallowed a match. Forty-one. She wrote it on the fogged mirror with one finger: **41**. Then she drew a smiley face under it, because numbers alone looked lonely.

"Are you drowning yourself again?" her mother called from the hallway.

"Practicing," Wren said, and came up gasping and grinning, water sheeting off her braids.

The reservoir was three fields past the back fence, past the rusted gate with its sign so weathered it read only *NO SWIMMING* if you already knew what it said, which everyone did, which was why everyone swam there. The old church grounds ran down to the water on the far side—a gray stone building that wasn't a church anymore but something with antennas, though nobody Wren knew had ever been inside. Her grandmother said the place had a well under it deeper than anyone had ever measured, and that the bell they'd taken out of its tower in 1951 had never rung right afterward, had gone pale-sounding, like a voice heard through a wall.

"Bells don't sound pale," Wren had said.

"You'll understand when you're older," her grandmother said, which was the sentence adults used when they didn't want to explain things, or when they couldn't.

That first day of her last week, Wren rode her bike down the gravel lane with the wind making flags of her ears, and she stopped at the fence line and looked at the water. It lay flat and green-gold in the late light, holding the sky the way a mirror holds a face—faithfully, but reversed. Somewhere under all that calm were the drowned foundations of the old churchyard, stones and coffins relocated long ago, except everyone said not everything had been moved. Everyone said some things were too heavy to move, or too unwilling.

Wren was not afraid of the reservoir. She wanted to say that clearly, later, to whoever might be keeping track: *I was not afraid.* She loved it the way you love anything that lets you be weightless. She loved the way sound changed underwater—the muffled boom of her own heartbeat, the way her voice came back to her strange and slow, as if the water were considering her words before returning them.

She dipped a toe in from the flat rock at the swimming hole's edge and shivered happily.

Forty-one seconds. By Sunday, she promised herself, she'd make fifty.

* * *

### Six Days Before

Her mother set her plate at her usual place, then stood looking at it a moment too long, as if checking the setting against some list only she could see. Fork on the left. Milk in the jelly glass with the blue rim. Napkin folded into a triangle because triangles were better than squares, Wren had decided this years ago and her mother had simply agreed, the way mothers agree to small laws.

"You're quiet," her mother said.

"I'm thinking."

"About?"

"The church by the water." Wren pushed peas into a fortification around her mashed potatoes. "Grandma says there's a well under it. A really deep one."

"There's a lot of things under a lot of places," her mother said. "Eat your peas."

"But what if the well is connected to the reservoir? Underneath. Like tunnels. Like the whole town is sitting on top of—" she made an explosion gesture with her fork, flinging a pea onto the tablecloth, "—water."

"Then we'd all be drinking church," her mother said, deadpan, and Wren laughed so hard milk nearly came out of her nose, and her mother laughed too, and for a moment the kitchen was so warm and ordinary that neither of them noticed how the light through the window had gone the color of pond water, green-gold and holding still.

Later, in bed, Wren heard it for the first time.

A hum. Not from outside—from below, or inside the walls, or inside her own ears; she couldn't tell. It was very faint, a single sustained tone like someone far away singing one note and refusing to stop. She lay with her ear pressed to the pillow and listened until the tone seemed to listen back, and then, embarrassed by the thought, she rolled over and went to sleep.

In the morning she remembered the hum the way you remember a dream about a door: certain it happened, unable to find it anywhere.

* * *

### Five Days Before

Thursday was library day. Wren checked out three books: one about mermaids (for fun), one about cave diving (for ambition), and one about local history (because the librarian, Ms. Okafor, had said, "If you're going to keep asking about that church, child, read this and stop asking me").

The book was thin and smelled of dust and glue. It said the church had been built in 1888 over a natural spring, that the spring had been enclosed into a well, that the congregation had believed the well was bottomless and holy and hungry, in roughly that order of enthusiasm. It said a bell had been cast for the tower using metal that included—Wren read this paragraph four times—*bronze recovered from the well itself*, dredged up in 1902 by men who should have known better. The bell had sounded beautiful, the book said. The book said it had sounded *pale*. The book used that word twice, italicized both times, as if the author had been startled by his own pen.

There was a photograph. The church in its full dress, steeple sharp against a sky gone white with overexposure, and beneath the photograph a caption: *The Pale Bell, so named for its unusual timbre. Removed 1951. The well was sealed the following spring.*

Sealed, Wren thought. Not filled. Sealed.

She asked Ms. Okafor if she could photocopy the page. Ms. Okafor looked at the page, then at Wren, for slightly too long.

"What do you want it for?"

"A school project."

"What school project?"

Wren blinked. Adults usually didn't audit lies that small. "The history of our town," she said, which was even true.

Ms. Okafor photocopied the page. As the machine's green light swept across the glass, Wren thought she heard—very faintly, under the machine's hum—a single sustained tone, like a note held by someone who refused to stop. She looked at Ms. Okafor. Ms. Okafor was looking at the copier too, with an expression Wren couldn't name, an expression like a woman hearing a word in a language she'd sworn she'd forgotten.

"Take your books home before dark," Ms. Okafor said.

It was barely four o'clock. Outside, the light was already going the color of pond water.

* * *

### Four Days Before

Friday night her mother worked late, so Wren stayed at her grandmother's house, the small one with the aluminum awnings that ticked in any wind at all. Grandma made cocoa the real way, with a saucepan, and let Wren have the marshmallows that had survived to the bottom of the bag, the ones compressed into dense sweet pebbles.

"Tell me about the bell," Wren said, because Grandma told it best, with her hands.

Grandma's hands went still around her own mug. That was the first strange thing. Grandma always talked with her hands; her stories were half pantomime. But now she set the mug down and looked out the dark window toward the water she couldn't possibly see, and said, "Who've you been talking to?"

"Nobody. I read about it."

"Reading's talking," Grandma said. "Everything's talking, if you listen. That's the trouble." She picked the mug back up. "All right. You want the story, you get the story.

"They pulled the bell out in '51 because it wouldn't stop ringing. Not loud—you'd hardly call it ringing. More like... remembering. Some nights the whole town would wake at three in the morning with that tone in their ears, one note, held, like something under the ground humming to itself. And people started doing things in their sleep. Walking down to the water. Standing in their yards with their mouths moving. My own mother—" Grandma stopped. Restarted. "My own mother hummed it for a year before she passed. Doctors said it was nothing. It wasn't nothing. When they took the bell away, it stopped. Mostly."

"Mostly?"

Grandma drank her cocoa. Over the rim her eyes were steady and old and sorry. "Mostly," she said again. "Now drink yours before it skins over."

That night at Grandma's house, Wren dreamed of water. In the dream she was not drowning; she was being *sung to*. The water was warm and dark and patient, and somewhere beneath her, vast and gentle, something was learning a song one note at a time, and each note it learned, it learned in a voice she almost recognized. In the dream she wasn't afraid. In the dream she answered.

She woke with the taste of lake water in her mouth, though she hadn't been near the water all day.

* * *

### Three Days Before

Saturday. The last perfect day, though no one knew to count it.

Wren and Dev Chaudhary biked out to the swimming hole with towels rolled like bandoliers and a bag of salt-and-vinegar chips sweating in the sun. Dev was her best friend in the specific way that best friends are best in summer: co-conspirator, competitor, witness. They jumped off the flat rock maybe thirty times, refining technique. Dev did a cannonball that soaked a heron into flight. Wren did her silent entry, the one she'd practiced, knife-clean, barely a ripple.

"Forty-one seconds," she announced, treading water. "That's my record. Today I'm beating it."

"You're gonna pop a lung."

"Worth it."

She went under.

And the water was different today. She noticed it immediately, the way you notice a room has been rearranged while you were out. Warmer at depth, where it should have been colder. Darker in a way that wasn't shadow—it was more like the light was being *kept*, held close, rationed. And the sound. Sound underwater was always muffled, always slow, but today the muffling had a shape. Today the silence between her heartbeat and her ears felt occupied.

She dove anyway. Down along the rock face where the cold veins used to run, down to where the green-gold light went thin, down until her ears pressed and the world above became a wobbling coin of sun. Forty-one seconds came and went. Fifty. She hung there in the darkening water, lungs beginning their slow argument, and she listened, because the tone was there again, the single held note, and this close to the bottom it wasn't faint anymore. It was everywhere. It was the water itself, vibrating. It was coming up through her skin, through her teeth, through the soft bones behind her ears.

And underneath the note—underneath, the way a foundation is underneath a house—there was something else. Not a second sound. A second *listener*. The sensation of being attended to, thoroughly and tenderly, the way you attend to a candle flame cupped in your hands against the wind.

Fifty-eight seconds. Her chest seized. She kicked for the surface and broke through into sunlight and air, gasping, laughing, alive.

"Fifty-eight!" she shouted at Dev. "Fifty-eight seconds!"

Dev whooped. The heron came back. They ate chips on the hot rock until their fingers were white with salt, and Wren did not mention the listener, because she didn't have words for it yet, and because some part of her understood—even at nine, especially at nine—that words are invitations.

They biked home racing the sunset and she won by half a length and her mother was on the porch with her arms crossed pretending to be mad about the time, and Wren flew into the hug at full speed, knocking the pretend-mad clean out of her.

"Smells like lake," her mother said into her hair.

"Smells like victory," Wren said.

Her mother held on a beat longer than usual. Mothers do that sometimes, without knowing why, the way animals know weather.

* * *

### Two Days Before

Sunday. Church-adjacent, though the Calloways weren't churchgoers; her mother called Sunday "the day we sleep in and feel guilty about it." But Wren woke at three in the morning with the tone in her ears, clear as a struck glass, and lay in the dark listening to it resolve, note by note, into something almost melodic. Almost a phrase. Like a sentence being assembled one word at a time by someone speaking very slowly, or very carefully, or in a language they were only just learning.

She got up and drank water from the tap and the tapwater tasted faintly of stone and something older than stone, mineral and deep, and she stood at the kitchen window in her pajamas looking east toward the reservoir she couldn't see, and she thought: *it knows my name.*

Not in a scary way. That was the thing she would never be able to explain. It knew her name the way the reservoir knew her weight when she floated—completely, and without opinion. Being known by it felt like being held. It felt like the jelly glass with the blue rim, set at her place every night, waiting.

She went back to bed. On the edge of sleep, the tone spoke—not words, but the space where words would go—and she hummed back, one note, just to be polite.

The note came back changed. Warmer. Pleased.

She slept like a stone at the bottom of a well.

* * *

### One Day Before

Monday. Homework on the porch, popsicle dripping neon down her wrist, her mother watering tomatoes and narrating their problems like a soap opera ("This one's given up, look at it, dramatic little thing"). Ordinary. Sacred, though no one there knew the word applied.

"Mom. Have you ever heard of the Pale Bell Hour?"

Her mother's watering can paused mid-tilt. "Where'd you hear that?"

"Kids at school. It's a radio show? Really late at night. People call in."

"It's nonsense," her mother said, too quickly, and resumed watering. "Old radio stuff. Nobody listens to radio at three in the morning except people who shouldn't be awake."

"What kind of show is it?"

"A sad one, probably." Her mother crouched, pinched a hornworm off a tomato stem, flicked it into the grass. "Wrenny. Why are you asking?"

"Just heard the name." Wren shrugged, licked her wrist. Inside her chest, faint as a memory of a memory, the tone hummed its held note, and she realized with a small cold surprise that she hadn't heard it all day. All day it had been quiet. All day the water had kept its distance, and she had felt the absence like a missing tooth, probing the gap with her tongue, wanting it filled.

"Can I go to the swimming hole tomorrow after school? Last swim before it gets cold."

Her mother considered. The light was going gold and long across the yard. Somewhere down the street a dog was barking at nothing, at something, at the space between.

"One hour," her mother said. "Home by six. Straight to the rock, no exploring the far shore."

"No exploring," Wren promised, crossing her heart, meaning most of it.

That night her mother set her place at dinner. Fork on the left. Milk in the blue-rimmed glass. Napkin in a triangle. And when Wren had gone up to brush her teeth, her mother stood at the empty chair a moment too long, one hand resting on its back, as if steadying it, or herself, against something that hadn't happened yet but was already leaning on the house.

* * *

### The Day

Tuesday came up overcast, the sky the color of dishwater, the air thick and close, pressure dropping the way it does before weather changes its mind. At school the lights buzzed at a frequency that made Wren's fillings ache. At lunch the milk tasted of minerals, of deep places. Dev said, "Swimming's off, right? Looks like rain."

"One hour," Wren said. "Mom said one hour. It's not even raining."

It wasn't. That was the strangest part, the part the searchers would puzzle over later: the sky held. The storm sat on the horizon all afternoon like a guest who wouldn't come in, and the reservoir lay flat and green-gold and patient, holding the gray sky faithfully, but reversed.

Wren got to the flat rock at ten past five. She left her shoes side by side, laces tucked in, because she was not raised in a barn. She left her backpack against the fence post. She waded in to her knees and the water was warm—too warm, bathwater warm, and she thought of the bathtub, of the number written in fog, of forty-one.

She dove.

The water received her the way it always did, and also not. The warmth went all the way down now. The light was being kept. And the tone was there, waiting, no longer distant, no longer a hum through walls—it rose to meet her like arms, and it was singing, actually singing, a phrase nearly finished, and the voice it sang in was—

Hers. Almost hers. Hers the way a reflection is her face: faithful, but reversed.

She should have surfaced then. Later—in whatever came later—she would want to say that clearly, to whoever kept track: *I meant to surface.* But the song was so nearly complete, and it had been practicing so long, and it opened for her like a door opening onto warmth, and she was nine, and she had spent her whole short life loving the weightless dark, and love, as certain old men would learn much too late, opens vessels widest.

She swam down. Past the rock face. Past the cold veins that weren't cold anymore. Down to where the green-gold went out entirely and the coin of sun above shrank to a pale ring, and her lungs began their final argument, and the song wrapped around her, complete now, finished, learned, and she thought with the last bright coin of her attention: *so that's the last verse.*

Under the water, in the flooded dark beneath the old church grounds, in the sealed well that was not so separate from the reservoir after all, something heard a child finish its sentence. Something rang, once, faint and pale, a bell-tone that traveled up through sixty feet of water and out into the overcast evening, unheard by anyone—almost. Far off, in a decommissioned church with antennas on its roof, every phone in the building gave a single soft ring, though no lines were connected, and in the studio a man named Jonah Fairweather looked up from his notes and frowned at a silence he could not explain.

The water closed. The sky finally broke, rain arriving too late to matter, needling the flat green-gold surface into static.

At six o'clock, her mother stepped onto the porch and looked east toward the water she couldn't see, and waited, and the waiting had no end in it.

Seven days later, at three in the morning, a low-power radio signal carried a small voice into the dark:

*"Hi. Is this the Pale Bell Hour? Do you hear us—or do you just answer?"*
