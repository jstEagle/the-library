## 22. Caller, You're On the Air

The morning show was called *Wake Up, Harborview*, which Jonah had always privately thought was a threat rather than a greeting.

It ran from six to ten, all traffic and weather and cheerful sponsor reads for the garden center on Route 9. There was a co-host now, a sunny young man named Petey who laughed at his own jokes with the unselfconscious delight of a golden retriever, and there was a sound board with exactly none of the strange old routing patched into it, and there was coffee that tasted like coffee instead of like the long dark hours of the world. It was, by every measurable standard, a good job. A normal job. The kind of job Jonah had once imagined wanting, back when they still believed their life would settle into something with edges.

Jonah could not remember applying for it.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, losing a piece of your memory: the shape of the hole didn't show. Ruth had sat them down in her office—the office with the new blinds, the old desk—and told them gently, in the tone people use for delivering diagnoses, that there had been an accident, an electrical fire scare in the old building, a night Jonah doesn't need to think about anymore. That the doctors said memory sometimes came back and sometimes didn't, and either way was survivable. That the daytime slot was open if Jonah wanted it. That she'd understand if they wanted to leave Harborview entirely.

Jonah had stayed. They couldn't have said why. When Ruth asked, Jonah heard themselves answer, "The light's good here in the mornings," which was true and also felt like reading someone else's diary entry aloud.

There were other holes, smaller ones, that Jonah had learned to walk around the way you learn to step over a loose stair. They owned a cassette player they had no memory of buying; it sat on the shelf above the television, unplugged, and Jonah dusted it without ever once thinking about why they kept it. They woke at 3 A.M. Every night. Not startled, not from dreams—they dreamed nothing at all, or nothing that survived the climb to consciousness—but simply awake, eyes open in the dark, with a feeling like a word caught behind the teeth. Some nights the feeling had a color. Most nights it had a weight, as though something small were sitting on the sternum, patient, waiting to be remembered into being.

They kept a notebook by the bed because the doctor suggested it. In three months it held eleven entries, all in Jonah's handwriting, all meaningless:

*Ask Ruth about the key.*

*The bell is —* (the sentence simply stopped)

*A name. I'm holding a name. Whose?*

And, written so hard the pen had torn the page:

*DON'T SING IT.*

Jonah did not sing. They'd noticed it around the second week—had caught themselves humming along with Petey's terrible morning playlist and stopping mid-note with their heart slamming, hands cold, no idea why. Now when music came on they went quiet as a held breath. Petey teased them about it. *You're the only person in America who won't sing happy birthday,* he said, and laughed, and Jonah smiled and let him believe it was a quirk, because the alternative was trying to explain a fear that had no face and no story attached to it, just a shape, like a shadow cast by something standing outside the frame of everything they knew.

* * *

Autumn came to Harborview the way it always did, gold going to rust going to bare branches scratching at gray sky. The reservoir dropped with the season, exposing pale margins of mud and stone, and people walked their dogs along the new shoreline and remarked on how low the water was, how strange, how it seemed lower than anyone could remember it being, as if the whole lake had exhaled.

Jonah drove past it one Sunday and pulled over without deciding to. Stood at the guardrail looking down at the wide wet scar of the drawdown. Somewhere under all that flat gray water was a place where a girl had drowned, and Jonah knew this the way they knew the town's other facts, from news archives and gossip, but knowing it produced no feeling at all, and the absence of feeling frightened them more than any feeling could have. It was like pressing a thumb into a bruise and finding smooth, unmarked skin. Something should hurt here. Something had been amputated, and the nerves hadn't gotten the memo.

A woman was walking the shoreline below with a small dog, and as Jonah watched she stopped, bent, and picked something out of the mud—a stone, maybe, or a toy—and stood holding it against her chest for a long time before putting it in her coat pocket. She looked up at the road once. Her face was ordinary and grief-worn and familiar in a way Jonah couldn't place, the way a stranger can be familiar because you've passed them a thousand times in a town this size. She raised one hand—not quite a wave, more like a salute, or an acknowledgment across a distance neither of them could name—and Jonah, feeling absurd, raised theirs back.

In the car afterward, Jonah sat with both hands on the wheel and cried for four minutes without knowing why, and then stopped, wiped their face, and drove home, and by the time they pulled into the driveway the crying was already becoming a rumor even to themselves.

* * *

The nights got longer, and the 3 A.M. awakenings got heavier, until some mornings Jonah came to work with the specific exhaustion of someone who has been carrying furniture all night in a house they can't see.

"You look like hell," Ruth said, kindly, sliding a coffee across the desk. She was older than Jonah had first guessed—there was a fragility in her wrists now, a paperiness—and she watched Jonah with a vigilance that never quite resolved into anything. Like a lighthouse keeper watching a horizon out of habit long after the last ship is due.

"I keep waking up," Jonah said. "Three o'clock. On the dot."

"Body clock," Ruth said. "Shift workers get it. Your blood remembers the schedule even when you don't." And then, too quickly, too smoothly: "You could take something for it. Melatonin. Or—" she paused, and Jonah saw her decide against whatever the rest of the sentence was. "Or you could just get up. Sometimes fighting it's worse than riding it out."

So some nights Jonah rode it out. Made tea. Sat in the kitchen while the house ticked and settled. Read half a page of whatever novel was on the nightstand and retained none of it. The word-on-the-tip-of-the-tongue ache was worst at those hours, a pressure behind the breastbone, a sense of an appointment being missed. Once, half-asleep at the table, Jonah heard—clearly, unmistakably, in the space between one tick of the refrigerator and the next—a child laugh. Bright, gap-toothed, delighted. Jonah sat bolt upright. The house was silent. The street was silent. But the laugh left a warmth behind it, the way a hand leaves warmth on a shoulder after it's gone, and Jonah sat in the dark with their palm pressed flat over their own sternum and thought: *I know that laugh. I know that laugh, I've earned that laugh, we used to—*

Used to what?

Nothing. There was nothing. There was only the ache, and the tea going cold, and the clock crawling toward a dawn that never brought the answer with it.

* * *

It happened on a Tuesday in November, the kind of raw gray Tuesday where the rain can't decide whether to commit.

Jonah had slept badly even by the standards of the season, and at 2:47 A.M. gave up entirely, dressed in the dark, and got in the car. No destination. Just the need to be moving, to be somewhere other than inside their own skull. They drove the empty streets on autopilot and were not surprised—though some distant part of them registered the surprise they *should* have felt—to find the car idling in the gravel lot behind the old church on Bellwether Lane.

The station had moved downtown two years ago, Petey had mentioned once, into a strip mall between a dentist and a nail salon. This building had been sold, resold, rezoned; there was a permit notice stapled to the boarded front door announcing its conversion into condos that would never be built. It stood black against the blacker sky, its little steeple a silhouette like a finger raised to its lips.

Jonah got out of the car. The rain had stopped. Everything smelled of wet stone and dead leaves and, faintly, impossibly, of lake water.

The side door—the one by the old steps, the one Jonah's hands found without searching—was unlocked. Of course it was unlocked. Jonah's body moved through the dark interior with the horrible fluency of habit, past the sanctuary, down the narrow hall, into the room that had once been a studio and was now just a room: bare floorboards, a dangling bulb, dust sheets over shapes that might have been furniture or might have been nothing. But in the corner stood the old transmitter rack, unplugged, cobwebbed, tagged with a bright orange sticker from some municipal inventory: *DECOMMISSIONED — DO NOT OPERATE.*

Jonah knelt in front of it. Their hands were steady. That was the strangest part—how steady they were, how this felt less like trespassing in a condemned building at three in the morning and more like coming home to a house they'd only forgotten they owned. They plugged the rack into the wall outlet that still lived, apparently, on some ancient separate circuit, because the needles on the dead dials shivered and rose like something inhaling. They flipped the switches in an order they had never known and knew perfectly. The tubes warmed with a low amber glow and a hum—not electrical, or not only electrical; a hum with a shape to it, a hum that was almost, almost a note—

*Don't sing it,* said the torn notebook page, in their own handwriting, in a voice of pure warning.

"I'm not singing," Jonah said aloud, to the empty room, to whoever had written the note. "I'm just listening."

They took off their headphones—old ones, hanging on a nail, foam crumbling—and pressed one earcup to their ear.

Static.

Just static, at first. The white noise of a dead frequency, the sound of nothing broadcasting to no one. Jonah sat down on the dusty floor with their back against the transmitter rack, letting the static wash over them, and found—to their mild, detached astonishment—that it was comforting. It sounded like rain on a roof. It sounded like the ocean heard from underwater. It sounded like a room full of people breathing quietly, waiting their turn.

Minutes passed. Maybe more than minutes. The amber dials glowed. Somewhere above, wind worried the boarded windows with small patient fingers.

Then the static changed.

It thinned. Parted. And through the gap came a sound so small Jonah almost missed it—a click, the click of a phone line picking up, the most mundane sound in the world, the sound of a call connecting. Jonah's heart began to pound with a rhythm that felt older than their body, a rhythm their blood remembered even when they didn't.

And then a voice. Small. Young. Careful, the way children are careful when they're afraid the magic will break if they're loud.

"Hi." A pause. A breath drawn in a place where breath shouldn't be possible. "Do you remember me?"

The studio was silent except for the carrier hum and the far-off wind. Dust drifted through the amber light. Jonah sat perfectly still, and somewhere very deep—somewhere beneath the missing months, beneath the smooth unbruised skin where the wound should have been, in the flooded cellar of the self where all the given-up things had gone—something turned over. Something that had been sleeping with its name held tight in its fist.

"I don't," Jonah heard themselves say, and their voice cracked clean down the middle. "I don't remember you. I'm sorry. I don't—I can't—"

"That's okay," the voice said, and it was smiling; you could hear a smile like you could hear a bell. "Ruth said maybe you wouldn't. She said it might be easier this way. But I've been calling every night for a hundred nights, and tonight you came, so I thought—" The voice steadied itself, gathering courage the way children do, from somewhere bottomless. "I thought I'd try. Just once. Out loud."

"Who are you?" Jonah whispered. Tears were already running down their face, hot and fast and utterly unmotivated, grief arriving like weather from a country they'd never visited. "Why am I crying? Who are you?"

"You cry every time," the voice said gently. "You don't remember, but you cry. That's how I know it's really you." A small shuffle, the sound of someone settling in, getting comfortable, home at last. "My name is Wren. I used to call your show. You used to put me on the air, and we'd talk about homework, and my mom's meatloaf, and whether ghosts get bored. You said I had radio hair." A giggle, wet at the edges. "I don't have hair anymore. It's fine. It's actually kind of nice. Less to dry."

Jonah made a sound that was not quite laughter and not quite sobbing and pressed the earcup harder against their ear as if they could hold the voice in place by pressure alone.

"I can't stay long," Wren said. "The water's getting shallower every night—that's good, that means it's working, that means you did it, even if you don't remember doing it. Pretty soon I'll be somewhere the signal doesn't reach. But I wanted—" The breath again, that impossible breath. "I wanted you to know it was worth it. Whatever it cost. I heard the bell ring, the real ring, the clean one, and everything got quiet and bright, and I wasn't scared. I want you to know that. In case you're ever sad about the parts you lost. They bought something good."

"What did I lose?" Jonah asked. "Please. Tell me what I lost."

"You gave up remembering us," Wren said softly. "All of us. Three years of us. So the well could fill and everyone could go home." A pause, and when she spoke again her voice had gone shy, the way it must have been when she was alive and small in a kitchen with a mother who set her place at dinner. "But you kept one thing. You cheated a little. Dell says it's technically allowed. You gave up the memories but you wouldn't give up the love, because love isn't a memory, it's a—it's a muscle, or a bell, or—" she laughed at herself, "—I'm seven, I don't have the words. You just wouldn't let go of it. So it's still in there. That's why you wake up at three. That's why you cry. That's the ache. It's me. It's all of us. We're not gone, Jonah. We're just on the tip of your tongue."

Outside, far off across the dark town, a church bell rang—one clean stroke, pale and silver, though no church in Harborview rang at this hour, though no church in Harborview had a bell at all anymore. The dials on the transmitter glowed a shade brighter. The static breathed.

"Caller," Wren said, and her voice was warm and bright and fading even as it smiled, going thin as starlight, going gentle as deep water, "you're on the air."

And Jonah Fairweather, kneeling in the dust of a decommissioned studio with tears streaming down a face that remembered nothing and loved everything, leaned toward the microphone they had switched on without deciding to, and answered.
