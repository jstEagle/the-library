## 5. Uncle Emeka Doesn't Answer

The phone rang eleven times before Uncle Emeka picked up, and in those eleven rings I catalogued everything I knew about the sound of a man deciding whether to answer.

I know phones the way some people know weather. I've recorded hundreds of calls — my own outgoing voicemails, interviews with old-timers for the tunnel project, Pip's late-night voice memos that are half research and half insomnia. A ring has a texture. Eleven rings means the caller is being weighed against whatever else is happening in the room on the other end. It means *I hear you, but I'm not sure I want to be heard by you.*

"Juniper." His voice came out flat, like something set down carefully so it wouldn't spill.

"Uncle Emeka. You didn't answer yesterday either."

"I was busy."

"You're never busy. You retired twice just to prove nobody could make you do it again."

A silence. In it I heard something I couldn't place at first — a faint rhythmic sound behind his breathing, soft as a broom against a floor. Then he moved the phone, or turned his head, and it was gone.

"How's Grandma Ada?" he asked instead of answering me, which was its own kind of answer.

"That's why I'm calling. She's fine. She's better than fine, which is part of what worries me. Listen — I need to ask you something strange, and I need you to answer it straight, because I promise you it will sound less strange than what's actually going on."

"Juniper—"

"When did your hallway get longer?"

The line went so quiet I checked the screen to see if the call had dropped. It hadn't. Somewhere in Lagos-by-way-of-Baltimore, my uncle was holding a phone and not breathing into it.

"Who told you about the hallway," he said, and it wasn't a question. It was the tone of a man who has just discovered the water rising around his ankles is coming from inside the boat.

* * *

Nobody told me. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. Nobody told me anything; I'd assembled it the way I assemble everything, from fragments, from the residue people leave without meaning to.

It started three days after Grandma Ada showed me the albums — after the chair in every photograph, closer each decade, patient as interest accruing on a debt. I'd gone home and done what I always do when the world misbehaves: I documented. I laid out my notes on the kitchen table under the good lamp, the one with the warm bulb, and I made a list of everyone whose name appeared in those photographs. Ada. Emeka. Ruthie. Bea — Beatrice, Emeka's daughter, thirty-one, works nights at a hospital lab, sends me memes at 2 a.m. because we are the two nocturnal creatures of the family. And then the dead ones, the ones whose names were already ink-dry: Grandpa Solomon, great-aunts, cousins lost to ordinary time.

Then I called them. All of them. The living ones first, obviously, but I'll admit here, in the privacy of my own record, that I also called the number listed for Great-Aunt Ngozi, who died in 2009, just to see if anyone would pick up. Someone should document that no one did. Someone should document that I sat there listening to a dead woman's ringtone echo through an empty wire, and that I said, out loud, to no one: *"Good. Good that it's empty."*

Because by then I understood that the opposite of empty was exactly what we couldn't afford.

Ruthie answered on the second ring and was her usual self, which is to say she treated my questions like a personal attack. "Why are you asking about the apartment, Juniper? Why are you asking about hallways? Did Ada put you up to this? That woman—" and then a door slammed somewhere on her end, or maybe it was nearer than that, maybe it was inside the call itself, and she hung up. But before she hung up, I caught it: her breathing had that same careful quality. The breathing of someone moving through a space they no longer trust.

Bea answered on the first ring, at 3:40 in the morning, wide awake.

"Cousin," she said, "I was literally about to call you."

That sentence still sits wrong in my chest. Not *I was thinking of you.* Not *what a coincidence.* She was *about to call.* People say things like that all the time and mean nothing by it. But Bea doesn't say filler words. Bea is a lab tech; she reports findings.

"What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong. That's the thing. Everything is normal except—" I heard her sit down, heard the creak of her kitchen chair, and underneath that, faintly, that same rhythm I'd heard behind Emeka's breathing. Soft. Regular. Like knuckles on wood. Like someone counting on their fingers, very slowly, forever.

"Except what, Bee."

"There's a sound in my walls." Her voice did something I'd never heard it do — it apologized for itself. "It started Tuesday. It's not pipes. I know pipes. It's not settling, it's not mice, it's not the neighbor's TV through the shared wall. It's—" she exhaled. "It's counting, Juniper. Don't laugh."

"I'm not laughing."

"It counts to a number and then it starts over. Same number every time. I counted along with it last night — don't look at me like that, I know how that sounds, I could hear you looking — and it goes to forty-four. Forty-four and then silence, and then it begins again. And here's the part I can't make fit anywhere: forty-four is the number of steps from my front door to my bedroom door. I measured. At four in the morning. With a tape measure. Because apparently I've become the kind of person who measures her hallway at 4 a.m., and honestly? The measuring made it feel better. The counting stopped while I was doing it. It started again the second I stopped."

I wrote it all down. Of course I wrote it down. My hand was steady and my handwriting was neat and none of that reflected anything happening inside me, which was a full-scale evacuation of calm.

"Bee, I need you to do something for me. Tonight, tomorrow, whenever. Take photos of your apartment. Every room, every wall, especially the wall where the counting is. Timestamped. Send them to me."

"Why?"

"Because I think something is taking inventory," I said, "and I want our inventory to be bigger than theirs."

She laughed — a short, scared bark of a laugh — and said, "You've been spending too much time in storm drains," and agreed, because Bea loves me, and because underneath the joke she was as frightened as I was, and fear recognizes its own dialect.

* * *

Emeka finally talked. It took forty minutes of me asking small questions and waiting, the way you wait at a burrow mouth for a shy animal, before he gave me the whole of it, and when he did, it came out in one long exhale like he'd been holding it since Tuesday.

"You have to understand, the hallway was never long. Twenty-two feet. I paced it off when I bought the place — twenty-two feet, I remember because I wanted to know if my recliner would fit at the far end with room to walk past. Twenty-two feet for nineteen years. And now it isn't." He paused, and I heard him turn from the phone, and I heard that soft rhythmic sound again, closer now, and this time I identified it: fingernails, tapping a wall. Or a wall, tapping back.

"How long is it now, Uncle Emeka?"

"That's the thing. That's the thing that's making me feel like I'm losing my mind, Juniper, so you tell me if this sounds like losing my mind." His voice dropped, went confidential, went ashamed. "It depends on when you measure it. In the morning it's twenty-two feet. By evening it's more. Last night I walked it at midnight and I counted sixty steps, and I walk it slow, and my steps are not small. This morning it was twenty-two feet again. Except—" and here his voice actually broke, a small dry crack, "except the closet door that used to be halfway down. It's not halfway anymore. It's near the end. The hallway is the same length and the door has moved, and both of those cannot be true, and I stood there this morning with my coffee getting cold looking at that door like it owed me an explanation."

"Did you take pictures?"

"A picture of what? A door? Juniper, I'm sixty-eight years old, I'm not going to stand in my own hallway photographing a door like some ghost-chaser—"

"Uncle Emeka." I kept my voice level, gentle, the voice I use with spooked witnesses and spooked raccoons. "Please. Take the pictures. Every day, same time, same spot, same framing. Date-stamp them. Will you do that for me?"

He grumbled. He asked twice more if Ada had put me up to this. And then, quietly, in the voice of a man confessing to a priest who happens to be his niece's generation: "There's something else. The smell. Down at the far end, past where the door used to be. Wet stone. Like a cellar. Juniper, I live on the fourth floor. There is no cellar under my hallway. There is no cellar under this building at all, I checked the plans when I bought it."

Wet stone. I knew that smell. I'd been breathing it for weeks in the drains below the city, in the corridor that bent wrong, in the doorway that appeared in every photograph whether I photographed it or not.

"Keep the pictures coming," I said. "And Emeka — don't walk to the end of the hallway at night. Whatever's down there, let it stay unmeasured."

"That's easy for you to say."

"No," I said. "It really isn't."

* * *

I spread it all out on the kitchen table at dawn, because that's when the truth gets its best light.

Printouts of Bea's photos, timestamped across five nights. Emeka's daily door pictures, seven of them, the door visibly migrating toward the end of the hall like a slow animal heading home. My own notes. The scan of Grandma Ada's albums, the chair advancing decade by decade. And beneath all of it, taped together from six sheets of printer paper, my map of the unmapped tunnels — the survey that isn't, the corridor that bends wrong, the extra doorway that develops into every photograph I take down there.

I stood over the table with a cup of tea going cold and did what I do. I looked for the pattern. Documentation is a prayer, and prayer is mostly just paying attention until something answers.

First I marked Bea's apartment. Then Emeka's. Then Ruthie's — because even though she'd hung up on me, her building was in the family constellation, and I'd gotten enough from her two minutes of hostility to know her hallway mattered too. Then Ada's house. Then, hesitating, my own apartment, because if this thing was counting my family, I had no reason to believe I was exempt, and indeed my own hallway had developed, sometime in the last week, a faint smell of wet stone that I had been diligently not thinking about.

Five points on the city map. Five homes of five Oyelarans, scattered across nine miles of Baltimore.

I connected them.

My pen stopped moving before my brain admitted why. I stared at the shape the lines made — a long irregular corridor of a shape, branching once near Ruthie's, narrowing toward Ada's house, and at the center of it, where the lines converged like threads drawn into a knot, sat the storm drain access where I'd found the corridor that bends wrong.

It wasn't a pattern *on* the map. It was the map. The family wasn't near the tunnels. The family was the tunnels' outline — their homes were the walls, their lives the rooms, and we had all been living, our whole lives, inside the footprint of a cellar that was still being built. Still growing. Room by room, relative by relative, the way Bea's counting climbed and Emeka's hallway stretched and the chair in the photographs drew closer and closer to the people it was set out for.

The Cellar wasn't under the city.

The city was above the Cellar. Barely.

I took a photograph of the map. When it developed, there was an extra line on it — thin, dark, new — running from the central junction straight to Ada's house, thicker than all the others, and at the end of it, where her bedroom would be, a small pale rectangle.

A chair, seen from above.

My hands were shaking so badly the second shot blurred. I didn't take a third. Some things you document; some things you refuse to witness into existence. I was only beginning to learn the difference, and the tuition was due immediately, because my phone lit up on the table beside the map.

Bea. Calling at 5:12 in the morning, which she never does, which she would never do.

And when I answered, she wasn't crying, which was worse. She was whispering, fast and precise, a lab tech reporting a finding:

"Juniper. It stopped counting to forty-four. It's counting to forty-five now."
