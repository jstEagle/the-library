## 7. Down Where the City Keeps Its Receipts

*Juniper*

The manhole cover came up easier than it should have. That was the first wrong thing.

Iron that old — stamped 1954, furred with sixty years of rust and road grime — fights you. It argues. This one lifted like a lid off a pot someone had been keeping warm for us, and the breath that rose out of the dark was warm too, warm in a way storm drains never are. Warm like a kitchen. Warm like bread.

Marisol crouched at the lip of the opening and looked at me. "You feel that?"

"Yeah."

"Say it out loud anyway. I want it on record that we both felt it before we went down."

"Warm air," I said. "From a drain. In February."

Deacon snorted behind us, checking the straps on his pack. "Or somebody's boiler's venting wrong and you two are about to write a very boring incident report." But he said it the way he always said his skeptic lines — a little too fast, a little too loud, like he was trying to drown something out. Pip stood last on the ladder rungs, hood up, tablet strapped to their chest in its padded harness, and didn't say anything at all. Pip not saying anything was louder than Deacon's whole routine.

I went first. I always go first; Marisol says it's because I don't trust anyone else to see what I see, and she's right, and I've stopped apologizing for it.

The ladder dropped maybe eight meters into a junction chamber, brick-barreled, older than the stamped concrete the city uses now. My boots found standing water only ankle-deep, and it wasn't cold either. My headlamp swept the walls and found what I'd found three weeks ago on my solo survey: the corridor that bent wrong. Not collapsed, not blocked — *bent*, the way a sentence bends when someone has edited it. The official survey maps showed this junction terminating in a sump. The sump was here. It just also had a doorway in it, dry-floored, framed in wood that had no business being below the water table, and beyond it a hallway running at an angle that made my inner ear file a complaint.

"You're sure about the family angle," Marisol said, dropping down beside me. She'd brought her good camera tonight, the film one, which she almost never did. "The reports matching the tunnel shape."

I'd plotted them on my kitchen table two nights ago: Uncle Emeka's hallway growing longer in the Bronx. Cousin Bea hearing counting through her wall in Yonkers. Aunt Ruthie's basement in Mount Vernon where, she'd admitted over the phone in a voice like she was confessing to a crime, the door to the cellar steps had started sticking *from the inside*. When I drew lines between the addresses and overlaid my survey of the unmapped corridors, the fit wasn't approximate. It was exact. The tunnels under the city were shaped like the scatter pattern of my family.

"Sure as I can be," I said.

"And your grandma's house sits—" Deacon swung down, landing with a splash, "—where?"

"At the center." I said it flat, because if I let any other tone in, the fear would ride along with it. "Everything radiates from Ada's house."

Pip reached the bottom last and stood there breathing hard, hood shadowing their face. Then they pointed past me, down the corridor that bent wrong.

"It's different from your photos," they said quietly.

I raised my camera — the digital one, the one I document everything with, the one that is half prayer and half armor — and took a shot of the corridor. Checked the screen.

In the photograph, the corridor was longer. There was a door in it that hadn't been there when I lifted the viewfinder. A wooden door, paneled, painted the faded green of every kitchen cabinet Ada had ever owned.

I lowered the camera. Looked with my eyes. No door.

Raised it again. The door was closer.

"Okay," Marisol said, in the voice she used for talking people off ledges, which in our line of work was a professional skill. "Okay. So the camera sees ahead. We knew that from your shots. We treat the photos as forecast, not fact. Nobody goes anywhere without checking the frame first. Agreed?"

Nobody argued. That was the thing about my crew — the found family, the underside people, the ones who climb into the city's forgotten organs for the pure joy of knowing them — we had rules the way other families had recipes. Check the frame. Never split up unless the space splits you. Leave marks. Tell someone when you're scared, even if especially then.

We told each other we were scared. Then we walked toward the green door, because it was between us and the answers, and because the photograph showed it open.

* * *

*Marisol*

Here's what nobody tells you about exploring with Juniper Oyelaran: they narrate. Low, constant, into their recorder or just into the air — dimensions, materials, water lines, the date, the time, who's present, who's scared. At first I thought it was quirk. Then I learned it was liturgy. Juniper documents the way other people pray, continuously, in case the world tries to take anything back. They've never told me the whole story of why. I know there was a fire. I know there are years missing. I know that when Juniper says *"recording"* in that flat little voice, it means *this happened, this happened, this happened, and no one gets to erase it.*

So when we stepped through the green door and Juniper's narration stopped — clean stop, mid-word — I knew we'd crossed into somewhere that ate records.

The room on the other side was a kitchen.

Not a modern kitchen. A 1960s kitchen, linoleum floor cracked in a starburst pattern, enamel table with chrome legs, a refrigerator humming — actually humming, actually cold when I put my hand on it, which is impossible, we are forty feet under the Bronx in February. There was a window over the sink. Through it: daylight. Gray, soft, ordinary daylight, falling on a backyard with a clothesline.

"That's not possible," Deacon said. His voice had gone thin. "That's not—"

"It's her kitchen." Juniper's voice came back online, but rough, like a machine restarting. "Ada's. This is the kitchen she grew up in. I've seen photos. The starburst crack in the linoleum — she has a photo of herself, maybe six years old, sitting right there on that floor while her mother rolled dough."

I looked at the floor. There was a faint discoloration in the linoleum, the ghost-shape of a child who had sat in one spot so many times the sun and the knees had worn it in.

"Her mother's kitchen," Juniper corrected themselves softly. "This is Ada's childhood kitchen. Preserved. Down here. Under the city."

Pip had gone to the window. I watched them look out at the daylight and the clothesline, and I watched them do the thing I do when I'm trying not to panic: catalog. "Curtains match the period. View consistent with a yard in— could be anywhere. Light source untraceable." They turned. "There's no bulb in that fixture. The light isn't coming from anywhere."

The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere in its depths, ice shifted.

And then, because none of us could help ourselves, because curiosity is the engine that drives all of us idiots, Deacon opened it.

Inside were jars. Dozens of them, shelf after shelf, home-canned in the old style — but the labels weren't peaches or tomatoes. Each jar held a photograph, submerged in clear fluid, and each photograph showed a room. Empty rooms, mostly. Hallways. Staircases. One showed a kitchen exactly like this one, except the table had a chair at it that didn't belong, plain wood, straight-backed, facing the camera like it was posing.

Juniper made a sound I'd never heard from them before. Small. Like a hinge.

"That's the chair," they said. "From the albums. From Grandma's photographs. Every decade, another photo, and the chair's closer."

I took the jar Deacon handed me and looked at the photograph inside, and the photograph looked back, and I want to be honest in whatever record survives of this night: I felt it looking. Not the image. The *thing that kept the image.*

"We should go," I said.

"In a minute," Juniper said, and raised their camera at the far wall, where a doorway led onward, and took a picture, and checked it, and I saw their face do something complicated.

"What does it show?" Pip asked.

"The next room," Juniper said. "But there's someone in it. Standing with their back to us. Wearing Emeka's jacket."

* * *

*Juniper*

My uncle Emeka has worn the same style of jacket for thirty years — brown corduroy, elbow patches, a habit he picked up teaching and never dropped. The figure in the photograph wore it perfectly. The posture was even right: the slight forward lean of a man who spent decades leaning over students' desks.

The figure in the actual room, when we reached it, was gone. Of course it was. The photographs here show what will happen next, and next had already happened by the time we arrived, or would happen later, or was happening in some tense English doesn't have a name for.

The room was Emeka's first apartment. I knew it the way I knew the kitchen — from stories, from the one surviving snapshot of him at twenty-three, young and furious and brilliant, standing in front of a bookshelf he'd built himself from planks and cinderblocks. The bookshelf was here. The cinderblocks were here. On the desk sat a typewriter with a page still in it, and I didn't read the page, because some part of me understood that reading another person's unwritten letter in a place like this was a violation I couldn't take back.

"This is a museum," Marisol said behind me. Her voice had changed since the kitchen. Harder. Angrier. Marisol's anger is a load-bearing structure; I've seen her carry entire expeditions on it. "No. It's worse than a museum. Museums have labels. This is a *collection*. Somebody collected my— your family's rooms. Kept them. Furnished them."

"Why?" Deacon asked. He was standing in the middle of the room turning slowly, and I realized he was counting exits out of habit, and realizing there were more doors than there should be. "Why keep a grad student's apartment? What's the point?"

"Receipts," Pip said.

We all looked at them. Pip stood in the doorway with their tablet glowing, though there was no signal down here, no network, nothing for it to reach. They were reading their own notes, the folklore archive they'd spent the week digging through — county histories, WPA interviews, a 1931 pamphlet about "subterranean folk practices" that read like a madman's tax code.

"The Cellar counts people," Pip said. "That's the through-line in every source. It counts, it records, it keeps. And what does any system that keeps accounts keep?" They looked around the room, at the typewriter, the bookshelf, the brown corduroy ghost of my uncle's young life. "Receipts. Proof of transaction. These rooms aren't decoration. They're documentation. The Cellar documented my— your family, Juniper. Decades ago. It kept the evidence of them the way you keep evidence."

The word landed in me like a stone in a well. Documentation. My whole life built on it. My whole faith. And here it was, turned inside out, hung with trophies: someone else had been documenting my family all along, patiently, lovingly even, the way I documented the city. With the same tenderness. With the same terrible completeness.

I raised my camera because raising the camera is what I do when I can't breathe, and photographed the typewriter.

When I checked the screen, the page in the typewriter was no longer blank. Words had appeared, typed, in a font that didn't exist:

*HE IS ALMOST COUNTED. THE HALLWAY GROWS BECAUSE HE WALKS IT. TELL HIM TO STOP WALKING.*

I showed the others. Marisol read it twice. "Emeka," she said. "It's talking about Emeka. Right now. Tonight."

"He said the hallway was longer," I whispered. "On the phone. Longer than it was. I thought he was — I thought it was a figure of speech."

"He walks it every night," Pip said quietly. "You told me. To check the mail. Three in the morning, he paces, he told you he can't sleep since the calls started."

The refrigerator hummed in the other room. Or a refrigerator. Somewhere. The building hummed, the way a house hums when it's thinking about you.

"We call him," I said. "Right now. We go up, we get signal, we call Emeka and tell him to stay in his bedroom until sunrise. We tell him to sleep in the kitchen. Anywhere but the hallway."

"And then?" Deacon asked. And it was a fair question, the question underneath every other question, and none of us answered it, because the answer was *and then we come back down, because Bea is already gone into a wall, and Ada's count has begun, and the only way out of a ledger is through it.*

Marisol touched my shoulder. "One more room," she said. "Then we surface and make calls. Agreed? One more."

I agreed. I want it on the record that I agreed, and that agreeing was the second-worst decision of my life, and that I would make it again, because the third room was where they kept the photographs that develop instantly, and one of them was going to show me the future, and the future was wearing my face.

* * *

*Marisol*

The third room was small and white and wrong in a way I still can't fully articulate. The others were furnished — lived-in, loved-in, stolen. This one was bare. White plaster, white floor, a single wooden chair in the center under a light with no fixture. The extra chair. The chair from the photographs, close enough now that I could see the grain, the old varnish, a scratch on one leg shaped like a crescent moon.

On the chair lay a stack of photographs, face down.

"Don't," Deacon said.

"They develop instantly," Juniper said. Their voice had gone somewhere past fear into that calm I hate, the calm they wear like borrowed clothing. "Pip's research. The rooms show what will happen next. If these are face down, they're waiting to be read."

"Juniper." I put my hand on their arm. Under the sleeve, their pulse was going like a bird's. "Whose room is this?"

They looked at the chair. At the crescent-moon scratch. At the white walls with nothing on them, no receipts, no collection, no history — a room with no past, only appointments.

"Mine," they said. "It hasn't been filled yet. It's waiting for me to live in it."

Pip made a noise. Deacon swore, low and heartfelt. And I did the thing I'll defend to the end: I reached past Juniper and flipped the top photograph myself, because if someone had to see it, better me than them, better me with my anger and my thick skin and my twenty years of crawling through the dark.

The photograph showed this room. The white room. Juniper sat in the chair, hands folded, looking directly into the camera with an expression of perfect peace. Behind them, filling the doorway, stood a tall figure in a long coat whose face was a blank where a face should be — not blurred, not shadowed, simply *unfinished*, like a form awaiting information.

Stamped across the bottom corner, in neat red ink, was tomorrow's date.

Underneath the stack, I could feel more photographs. Many more. A whole future, printed and waiting, patient as paperwork.

"Surface," I said. "Now. Everyone. We call Emeka, we call Ada, we wake up Ruthie if we have to. Tonight. Because this place isn't hunting us." I looked at the red ink drying into permanence on the photograph in my hand. "It's *scheduling* us."

Behind us, as we filed out — I was last, I checked, I always check — the light above the empty chair flickered once, politely, like a throat being cleared.

And in the dark of the corridor beyond, faint but unmistakable, something began to count.
