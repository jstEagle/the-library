## 4. Odile's Ledger

**Ines**

The kitchen smelled like lemon cleaner and old wood, the way it always had, and Ines hated how fast their body remembered it. Twenty years of apartments with gas stoves and dead roaches in the light fixtures, and one hour back in this house and their shoulders already knew where the low doorframe was.

Odile set a mug of coffee in front of them without asking how they took it. Black. She remembered too. That was the problem with sisters—you could leave for two decades and they still had your whole configuration memorized.

"You timed it," Odile said. Not a question.

"One second." Ines wrapped both hands around the mug. "Hallway mirror. I dropped a pen cap in front of it. The reflection let go a full second after I did."

"And you didn't run."

"I'm a pest controller, Odile. Running is what the pests do. I'm the one who shows up."

Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile. "You used to be funnier."

"You used to answer your phone."

The flicker died. Good. Ines hadn't come home to be forgiven; they'd come home because of the voicemail, ninety seconds of Odile's voice pitched carefully flat, the way she'd learned to talk when Theo was within earshot: *Come home. The house is doing it again. Bring whatever you use on the impossible ones.*

*Again.* That word had done more damage than anything else in the message. *Again* meant there was a before. A before Ines had spent twenty years professionally not thinking about.

"Show me," Ines said.

Odile left the room. Came back carrying a composition notebook, the kind that cost a dollar fifty and smelled like pencil shavings forever. Its cover was soft with age, spine cracked white, corners rounded from years of being carried in backpacks and desk drawers and, judging by the water stain on the back cover, at least one escape through rain.

She put it on the table between them like it might bite.

"What is this?"

"Records." She sat down across from Ines, arms folded, and Ines recognized the posture—she'd been doing it since she was nine, bracing for them to laugh at her. "Dates. Times. What the lag was. I started keeping it when I was eleven."

Ines looked at the notebook and felt something cold walk up their spine on small feet.

"Eleven," they said. "You were eleven, and you were timing mirrors."

"Somebody had to. You were busy deciding none of it was happening."

There it was. Twenty years compressed into one sentence, delivered without heat, which made it worse. Ines picked up the notebook because their hands needed something to do that wasn't flinching.

The earliest entries were in round, careful child's handwriting:

*June 3. Kitchen mirror. Slow again. Counted 1-2-3-4-5 before it caught up. Told Mom. She said stop making things up.*

*June 6. Same. Maybe slower? Hard to tell.*

*June 9. Definitely slower. Used the metronome from school. It's like the glass is thinking about it first.*

Page after page. Years of pages. The handwriting changed—cursive arrived, then slanted, then hardened into the tight adult script Odile still used on grocery lists. There were gaps, sometimes months long, and Ines understood those gaps without asking. Those were the times Odile had tried to quit. Tried to be normal. And then some morning the hallway mirror would lag and she'd pick the pen back up, because someone had to, and everyone else in this house had decided the house was fine.

"How often?" Ines asked, flipping forward.

"It comes in cycles. Roughly every three nights it gets worse. Doubles, most times. One second becomes two, two becomes four." She tapped a page near the middle. "Then it plateaus, or it resets, or—" she hesitated, "—or something interrupts it. It's never reached past eight seconds. Not once, in thirty years of records. Something always stops it before sixteen."

"What stops it?"

"I don't know." Her voice did something complicated. "That's the part I need you for."

Ines turned pages faster now, hunting. Near the back, the entries stopped being measurements and started being quotations. Their grandmother's name appeared—Marguerite, dead eleven years, the last person who'd lived here alone—and beside her name, transcribed in Odile's careful hand, fragments from a diary Ines had never known existed.

*Grandma M's diary, March 1988: "The count stands at four tonight. I sang to it and it rested. They cannot take what has not finished learning them. Do not let it finish learning you."*

Another entry, older, undated: *"It counts in doubles. Four, then eight, then it wants sixteen. The count is how it learns. Every second of lag is a second of us it keeps."*

Ines read the phrase twice. Three times.

"The count," they said aloud.

"That's what she called it. Never explained. I've got maybe forty pages of her diary copied out and she uses the phrase like I should already know it. Like everyone in this family should already know it." Odile's jaw tightened. "Maybe we were supposed to. Maybe that's the inheritance nobody put in the will."

Ines closed the notebook slowly. The kitchen clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled the way floorboards do, except Ines no longer fully trusted the way floorboards do.

"Every three nights it doubles," they said. "When did this cycle start?"

"Nine days ago. One second." Odile pulled out her phone, showed a calendar app dense with color-coded notes. "Three nights later, two seconds. Three nights after that—" she looked at Ines, and for the first time since they'd walked in, her composure cracked enough to show the fear underneath, "—last night, Theo and I both timed it. Four seconds."

Four. Which meant three nights from now, six. Then twelve. Then—

"Twenty-four," Ines said. "And then forty-eight."

"If nothing interrupts it." Odie leaned forward. "Nothing ever has. In thirty years. So either Grandma Marguerite was lying in her own diary, or somebody in this family has been interrupting the count my entire life, and I want to know who, and I want to know how, and I want to know why nobody told me."

The question landed between them like a dropped knife. Because Ines didn't know. But Ines knew someone who might have—the grandmother whose room they'd slept in as a child, who'd hummed strange hymns while braiding their hair, who'd once gripped Ines's wrist hard enough to bruise and whispered, *if the glass ever gets slow, you come find me before you sleep.*

They'd filed it under *grandmothers are weird* and buried it under twenty years of professional skepticism.

"Her room," Ines said, standing. "Her actual room. There might be more than what you copied."

"There's an attic, too. Boxes nobody's opened since the funeral." Odile rose, tucked the ledger under her arm like a sidearm. "You take the attic. I'll take her room. And Ines—"

They paused in the doorway.

"When you find whatever she left, don't decide it's nothing. Not this time. I can't carry this alone anymore, and Theo—" her voice caught, just barely, "—Theo hears things at night. Through his wall. He won't tell me what."

Ines looked at their sister—fifty-one years old, gray coming in at her temples, holding a child's notebook against her chest like a shield—and felt twenty years of distance collapse into something closer to shame.

"We'll find out together," Ines said. "All of it. I promise."

Odile nodded once, unconvinced but willing to spend the promise anyway, and headed for the stairs.

Ines stood alone in the kitchen a moment longer. On the table, the ledger lay open to its final written page. At the bottom, beneath last night's entry—*4 sec. Both confirmed.*—someone had written a single line in fresh ink, in handwriting that was definitely Odile's and definitely not:

*It knows you're home now.*

***

**Odile**

Her mother's room—no. Her grandmother's room. Odile still made the mistake after all these years, reaching for the wrong name the way you reach for a light switch in a house where the switches moved.

The room was exactly as Marguerite had left it: bed made with hospital corners, lace curtains drawn, a smell of lavender and dust that refused to fade no matter how many windows Odile opened. Eleven years, and the room kept her like a jar keeps a firefly.

Odile knelt at the closet, pulled out the cedar chest, and began going through it properly for the first time. Not grieving-daughter rummaging. Searching. The ledger had taught her how to search: systematically, patiently, writing everything down.

Photographs. A christening gown. Letters tied with ribbon—her mother's hand, mostly, mundane bills and birthday cards. And at the bottom, wrapped in a pillowcase, a book.

Not the diary—that was in Odile's nightstand, had been since the funeral, smuggled out before relatives could start dividing the estate. This was smaller. A hymnal, black cloth covers worn soft at the edges. Odile opened it and her stomach dropped through the floor.

Every hymn was annotated. Marguerite's spidery hand filled the margins: dates, tallies, warnings. And on the flyleaf, underlined twice:

*The count is not a punishment. It is a hunger learning our shape. Sing off-key and starve it.*

Downstairs, faintly, she heard Ines climbing toward the attic. Above her, through the ceiling, Theo's floor creaked—he was supposed to be at practice, but he'd been skipping practice for two weeks, and he'd stopped sleeping, and he'd started standing in front of his bedroom mirror with his arms crossed like he was daring it.

Odile held the hymnal to her chest and felt the full weight of the ledger in her other hand—thirty years of data, and she'd never once thought to look for the instruction manual, because the instruction manual was music, and music was the thing this family did instead of talking.

"Ines," she called, loud enough to carry up through the floor. "Get down here. She left us sheet music."

A pause. Then Ines's voice, muffled by plaster and eleven years of silence between siblings, but steady:

"On my way. And Odile—check the date on the flyleaf. I found her diary box up here, and the last entry isn't from eleven years ago."

Odile's hands went cold around the hymnal.

"The last entry is dated three days ago," Ines said. "The night the count hit four."
