## 9. Packing in Silence

Odile packed the way her mother had taught her to pack for hurricanes: essentials first, sentiment last, and never let the children see you count what you're leaving behind.

She started in Theo's room because it was the smallest and because she could not start in her own. She pulled a duffel from under his bed and worked through it with brisk hands—socks, chargers, the hoodie he'd slept in for three years straight, the chipped retainer case he swore he didn't need anymore. Theo sat on the edge of his mattress watching her, dark-circled, hollowed out by nights of not sleeping, and said nothing. That was its own kind of alarm. Two weeks ago he would have complained about every item she touched. Now he just watched her fold his life into a bag like it was something that could be folded.

"Leave the posters," he said finally.

"They'll wrinkle."

"Mom." His voice cracked on it, adolescent and raw. "Just leave them."

She left them. The band posters, the star chart he'd made at nine, the pencil marks on the doorframe tracking his height in her handwriting—*Theo, age 6; Theo, age 8; Theo, age 11*—all of it staying behind in a room where the wall by his headboard hummed at three in the morning. She zipped the duffel and did not look at the doorframe again, because if she looked at it she would have to think about who would stand here next, some other family, some other child, marking their heights against wood that had learned to listen.

Downstairs, Ines was in the kitchen with a legal pad, writing in that tight vertical hand Odile remembered from childhood grocery lists. *Water. Flashlights. Gas can.* Practical things. Pest-controller things. Their duffel was already by the back door, packed since dawn, as if they'd been waiting twenty years for permission to leave this house and had decided not to waste a minute of it.

"We should be on the road by two," Ines said without looking up. "I want daylight for the first leg. Marisol's route takes us through—"

"I'm not going."

The legal pad stopped moving. Ines looked up, and Odile watched them do the thing they always did—the slow blink, the recalibration, the professional composure sliding over whatever they actually felt like a tarp over firewood. Twenty years of practice at not being rattled. Well. Odile had practiced too.

"Ines—"

"You're not serious." They set down the pen with exaggerated care, the way you set down something when you don't trust your hands. "Odile. We spent all night going through Grandmother's ledger. You showed me yourself. The lag doubles every three nights. It's at four seconds now. By my math we have—"

"Six nights before the count completes. I know. I've known longer than you have." Odile took the duffel off her shoulder and set it down on the kitchen tile, deliberately, so Ines could see it was not going anywhere. "And I'm telling you we are not all getting in that car."

* * *

Here is what Odile had never told anyone, not Ines, not her late husband, not Theo: she had tried to leave once already.

She was nineteen. It was the summer after Grandmother's funeral, the summer the mirrors in the east hallway went quiet—which everyone in the family understood, without discussing it, to mean *satisfied*. Odile had packed a single bag and walked out the front door with her mother's car keys and gotten as far as the county line before the radio started playing the hymn. Not a recording. Not a station. Just the hymn, rising out of the static, hummed in a voice she recognized as her grandmother's, and then layered under it, faintly, voices she didn't recognize but somehow knew were hers—hers older, hers younger, hers unfinished.

She had turned around. She told herself it was because the car was low on gas. She told herself that story for twenty-two years.

The deed was in the fireproof box in her closet, and she had read it more times than she'd read any book. *Montrose, et al.* Her name on it. Ines's name on it, though Ines hadn't set foot in the house for two decades and probably thought of themselves as having escaped it. And above both names, in the chain of title, a century of Montroses stretching back to a surveyor's error that had given the family the land in the first place—a mistake, everyone said, a clerical fluke. Odile had come to believe there were no mistakes in how this family came to hold this ground. Only terms.

Because here was the other thing she knew, the thing the ledger taught her if you read past the numbers: the house had been patient. Every record, every doubling of the lag, every entry in Grandmother's cramped hand—it all moved slowly. Generations slowly. The Choir learned the way water learns stone. And the only variable that ever changed the pace, the one thing Grandmother noted with something close to relief, was this: *the count holds while the line holds the land.*

They owned the house. As long as a Montrose held the deed, the Choir waited. Polite. Almost courtly. Learning, yes—but learning the way a guest learns the layout of a home they intend to keep visiting, not the way a thief cases a house.

Break the chain of ownership, and Odile genuinely believed—believed down in the marrow where belief stops being a choice—that patience would end. That the learning would accelerate. That whatever came next would not wait for six nights or sixty seconds but would simply arrive, and follow, and find them wherever the deed's protection no longer reached.

So when Ines said *we leave today*, what Odile heard was: *we break the only covenant keeping this thing civil, and we run, and we hope it doesn't chase.*

She had heard the hymn on the radio once. She knew how chasing sounded.

* * *

"It's a piece of paper," Ines said. They were standing in the kitchen, and the afternoon light came through the window behind them, and Odile noticed—she couldn't help noticing—that Ines's reflection in the glass over the sink was still holding the pen. Four seconds now. Four seconds of lag, and the reflection kept working after the hand stopped, finishing its little motions like an echo with an agenda. Ines had stopped looking at reflective surfaces whenever possible. Odile had caught them shaving by feel that morning, eyes shut, jaw tilted by memory alone.

"It's the only piece of paper that matters," Odile said. "You want to talk evidence? Fine. Let's do evidence, since that's your language. Grandmother's ledger, 1961: lag reaches eight seconds, family considers selling to the Hendrickses. Entry reads—and I memorized it, Ines, I've had thirty years to memorize it—'*Told them no. The count prefers a settled house. Do not unsettle it.*' Three days later the lag drops back to four. Drops, Ines. It has only ever gone backward one time in ninety years of records, and it was the week after we almost sold."

"That's correlation."

"Yes! Yes, it is. You know what else is? Everything. Every single thing happening in this house is correlation, because we cannot run a controlled study on the thing in our mirrors. So we go with the data we have, and the data says the deed keeps it patient, and you want me to sign away the one lever we have ever had." Odile heard her own voice climbing and pressed it flat. "You've been gone twenty years. You got to be rational from a distance. I stayed. I raised my son inside this ledger. So don't stand in my kitchen and tell me what the evidence allows."

"My kitchen too," Ines said quietly.

It landed harder than any shout. Odile turned away and busied her hands with the duffel she'd already decided not to carry, repacking what didn't need repacking.

"Theo hears it humming through his wall," Ines went on. "His reflection mouths words, Odile. Words. It's rehearsing him. And your plan is to keep him here, on the property, inside the count, because a document might be slowing it down?"

"My plan is to not make it faster."

"And then what? We hold the deed forever? You and me and Theo, rotating through this house like sentries until one of us dies and the other two inherit the problem?" Ines came around the counter, and up close Odile could see the exhaustion carved under their eyes, the same family exhaustion, the same Montrose face. "That's not duty. That's a hostage exchange with no end date."

"You don't get to define duty," Odile said. "You left."

The words came out with twenty years of rust on them, and she watched them hit. Ines went very still, the way people go still around wasps.

"There it is," Ines said.

"No. Don't. Don't make this about—"

"You've been saving that one since I was eighteen. Go ahead. Spend it." They spread their hands. "Yes, Odile. I left. I left because every adult in this house told me the sounds were pipes and the handprints were condensation and the hymn was a wind chime, and I was sixteen and I believed them because believing them was survivable. Then I grew up and found out the adults weren't lying to protect me. They were lying because they'd decided someone had to stay, and it wasn't going to be them, and the lie was cheaper than the truth. So yes. I left. And you stayed. And I have spent twenty years paying for that in voicemails you don't return, so if you want to bill me for it, fine, invoice me. But do not dress up keeping my nephew in a haunted house as virtue."

The silence afterward had texture. Odile stood in it with her hands flat on the counter, breathing, and became aware of a small sound: Theo, on the stairs, halfway between hiding and intervening, frozen with his bag over one shoulder. How long had he been there. How much had he heard.

"Mom?" he said.

* * *

They fought for another hour, but it was a different fight after that—quieter, circular, the kind siblings fall into when the real argument has no resolution and the substitute argument at least fills the room. Odile cited the deed. Ines cited the ledger. Odile cited 1987 and the Vane family, and Ines countered that Marisol Vane was alive, wasn't she, alive and three states away, which proved flight was possible. Odile said alive wasn't the same as whole, and Ines said nothing was whole in this house either, and they were both right, which was the worst outcome available.

By five o'clock they had achieved a stalemate disguised as compromise: the car would stay packed in the driveway. Theo's bags loaded. Ines would spend one more night verifying the lag—one more data point, they said, one more entry—and they would leave tomorrow if Odile still refused to transfer the deed to a trust, to a lawyer, to anyone, so that technically a Montrose still held the land while the flesh-and-blood Montroses ran.

It was a bad plan. Both of them knew it was a bad plan. It was the kind of plan you agree to so that everyone gets to sleep angry instead of awake.

Odile cooked dinner because cooking was the only prayer she trusted. She stood at the stove stirring a pot that had fed four generations of Montroses, and she did not look at the window over the sink, and she definitely did not look at the hallway mirror, where earlier that day she had caught her reflection standing with its arms folded when her own arms were busy with laundry—four full seconds of a woman in the glass judging her, wearing her tired face, patient as a creditor.

Theo ate without tasting anything. Ines washed dishes, which was their old childhood chore, and the domesticity of it almost broke her—the sight of her sibling at her mother's sink, sleeves pushed up, doing the small eternal work of a family that might have four nights left.

Afterward, Theo went up without being told. At the bottom of the stairs he paused.

"If we stay because of the paper," he said—not whining, not arguing, just asking, in the flat careful voice of a kid who has stopped sleeping—"does the paper hear it too?"

Neither of them answered him. There was no answer that wasn't a lie.

* * *

Eleven p.m. The house had gone into its nighttime register—the settling groans, the wind finding the eaves, and underneath it, so faint you could pretend, the beginning of the hum. Odile lay in bed listening to it and hating herself for knowing its melody.

Her phone lit up. A text from Ines, sent from the room across the hall:

*Lag checked at 10:40. Still four seconds. But Odile — my reflection put the dish away before I did tonight. It's ahead now. It's not echoing anymore. It's leading.*

Odile stared at the screen until it dimmed.

Then, downstairs, faintly, through two floors of old wood, she heard the piano in the parlor play a single note.

No one had touched that piano in eleven years.

She was moving before she finished deciding to move, down the stairs in the dark, and Ines's door opened as she passed it, and they came down together without a word, and stood together in the parlor doorway looking at the piano, silent now, its lid closed, its bench neatly tucked in.

On the music stand, though the lid had been shut, lay sheet music Odile had never seen before. Old paper. Handwritten. And across the top, in ink gone brown, in handwriting she knew better than her own—

her grandmother's, and beneath it, fresher, in a hand she knew even better than that:

Ines's.

One word, underlined twice: *Tomorrow.*

Behind them, in the dark hallway mirror, four seconds late, two figures appeared in the glass—standing exactly where they stood, facing exactly where they faced.

Except the reflections were smiling.
