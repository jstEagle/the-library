## 1. The Extra Chair

The house smelled like my grandmother had just stepped out to buy milk, which was impossible, because my grandmother had been dead for eleven days and had not bought milk since 1997, when she declared the dairy industry a scam and started making her own yogurt in a jar on the windowsill. The jar was still there. The yogurt inside it had achieved a state of being I preferred not to think about.

I stood in the kitchen doorway with two suitcases and a box of books and told myself, the way I always tell myself, that there was an explanation. Old houses hold smells the way old coats hold smoke. Grief makes noses sentimental. Any of a dozen reasonable things.

I have been explaining things away for thirty-four years. It's practically a career. When I was nine and heard someone walking around upstairs in our apartment — we lived on the top floor — I decided it was the pipes. When I was fourteen and heard humming through the floorboards of my mother's house, low and sweet and patient, I decided it was the neighbor's radio. When the humming stopped whenever I held my breath, I decided that was coincidence, because the alternative was a thing with lungs, and I wasn't ready for a world with those in it.

So: the smell was the house remembering her. Fine. Houses are allowed to remember.

* * *

Hollow Creek School has one hundred and forty students, a boiler that sounds like it's clearing its throat before telling a joke, and, as of this September, three empty desks. Marisol Vega, gone fourteen months ago. Danny Okafor, gone eight months. And now Petey Lindqvist, gone three weeks, whose desk still had a half-carved eraser on it when I took over his old classroom.

Nobody says *missing* anymore. They say *gone*, the way you'd say a tooth went. The sheriff's office put up flyers with faces slightly too cheerful for the purpose, and the town folded them into its silence like receipts into a pocket. At the staff meeting, Principal Auerbach asked us to "keep routines normal," which is what people say when they mean *do not look directly at it*.

I teach sixth grade English. On my first Friday, a girl named Wren stayed behind after class to sharpen her pencil for four full minutes. She didn't need to. She just wanted the noise, I think — the small mechanical certainty of it. When she finally left, she hummed something under her breath, six or seven notes, looping.

I knew the tune.

That is a strange sentence to write, so let me be precise about how strange it is. I recognized the melody the way you recognize your own name said by a stranger. It was the floorboard song from my childhood, the one I'd filed under *neighbor's radio* at fourteen and never thought about again, except that apparently I had thought about it every day of my life, the way you don't think about a splinter until someone touches it.

"Where did you learn that?" I asked.

Wren stopped in the doorway. She looked at me with the flat, careful expression kids wear when an adult asks the wrong question correctly.

"It was already in my head," she said. "It's always been in my head." Then she left, and the hallway swallowed her, and I sat alone in a classroom with a dead boy's eraser and explained to myself that lots of folk tunes sound alike. That children pick things up. That memory is unreliable.

I am very good at explaining. You have no idea. I could explain the ocean out from under a drowning man.

* * *

The photographs were in the cedar chest at the top of the stairs, wrapped in a pillowcase embroidered with strawberries. Grandma Odette kept everything; she was the family archivist, the keeper of birthdays and grudges alike, and there were decades of her here — sepia squares and faded Kodachrome and the orange-tinted seventies, all of it documenting a life I only got the edges of.

Mostly they were pictures of the kitchen. That was her throne room: the yellow wallpaper, the wood stove, the table where she'd taught me to make biscuits and to lie politely to census takers. In photo after photo, there she was — younger, then older, then old — flour on her hands, squinting at whoever held the camera.

I noticed the chair on the second evening, going through the pile with tea going cold beside me.

Against the far wall of the kitchen, in every photograph after 1981, stood a wooden chair. Plain, dark, high-backed, pushed against the wallpaper between the pantry door and the window. Empty in every single image.

Here's the thing about that wall: I spent whole summers in this house. I know that kitchen the way you know your own mouth. There was no chair against that wall. There was a coat hook, and later a calendar from the feed store, and once a poster of a horse that grandma hated and refused to remove out of spite toward whoever gave it to her. But no chair. Never a chair.

I laid the photos out across the living room floor in chronological order, because that's what rational people do, and rationality is my native language. 1978: no chair. 1979: no chair. 1980: no chair. 1981 — a birthday, judging by the cake, a cake shaped lopsidedly like a barn — and there it stands. Dark wood, high back, empty. 1982: there. 1985: there. 1994: there, and now I could see the grain of the wood even in the blurry print, the way one leg sat a hair crooked, the way the seat caught the window light.

Every image after 1981. As if the camera had finally agreed to see something that had been standing there all along.

I did what any sensible person does. I Googled "pareidolia," "photographic artifacts," "film defects," and "grief hallucinations," and I read until midnight, and I found explanations that would satisfy a person who wanted to be satisfied. I am usually that person. That night, for the first time, I couldn't quite make myself one.

Because here is what explanation can't touch: in the last photograph, taken three years ago at Christmas, grandma is looking at the chair.

Not at the camera. Her head is turned, her eyes are on that empty seat, and her expression is one I'd never seen on her face in life — patient, and sad, and waiting, like a woman listening for footsteps.

And here is the other thing, the thing I keep turning over like a stone with something underneath it: 1981 was the year the well went dry. I found that in her letters, or rather I found the edge of it — a note in her handwriting tucked into a recipe box, dated that October: *The water's gone quiet. Good. Let it stay quiet.*

Let what stay quiet?

I sat on the living room floor surrounded by forty years of a dead woman's Tuesdays, and I felt the first hairline crack run through the load-bearing wall of my sanity, the one labeled *there is always an explanation*. Through the crack came a draft that smelled like well water and sounded, faintly, from somewhere below the floors — below the cellar, below the foundation, below anything with a right to exist — like humming.

Six notes. Looping. Patient.

I did not hum along. Some animal part of me understood, without evidence, without reason, that this was the one rule: whatever happens, however lovely it sounds, you do not sing along.

In the morning I packed the photographs back into the chest, all but the last one, which I propped on the bookshelf facing the room — grandma, watching her empty chair, keeping watch over me. I told myself it was sentiment. I told myself a lot of things.

Then I went to school, and Wren raised her hand, and asked if she could stay in at recess and hum, because "it likes it when I practice."

I said yes, because I didn't know what else to say, and spent the rest of the day listening to a twelve-year-old perfect a song that something beneath my grandmother's house had been teaching this town's children for longer than I've been alive.

That night, I started digging through the recipe box for the rest of grandma's letters.
