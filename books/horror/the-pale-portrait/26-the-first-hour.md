## 26. The First Hour

I barricaded the cottage the way you'd board up a boat in a storm — badly, quickly, with whatever was nearest.

The kitchen table went against the back door first, because the back door was the one that had stood open all night without my opening it. Then the dresser. Then Edith's old steamer trunk from the attic, which was heavier than it had any right to be and smelled of river water when I dragged it, though there was no river within a mile of Marrowfen. I wedged a chair under the front door's handle — an ordinary chair, pine, one of four — and then I stood in the middle of my grandmother's kitchen with chalk dust on my hands and realized what I'd done.

I'd used a chair to hold the door.

I took it out and put it in the garden. It felt like the correct instinct. It also felt like admitting I understood the rules.

* * *

The clock in the hall said nine minutes past eleven. The sky through the kitchen window said nothing at all — no stars, no moon, just a low grey ceiling of cloud the color of dishwater, pressing down on the lane like a lid.

Down the lane, the villagers were still in their gardens.

I could see them from the window over the sink: Mrs. Aldery with her hands folded; the vicar standing at his gate with his collar pale in the dark; two men whose names I'd never learned, sitting on a bench they had carried out of their house for the purpose. They weren't watching me. That was the worst of it. They were facing me, all of them, the way an audience faces a stage before the lights go down — patient, settled, coats buttoned against the damp — but their eyes were aimed somewhere over my head, or into the middle distance, or nowhere. As if I were not the performance. As if I were only the interval.

Bram was not among them. I hadn't seen him since dusk, when he'd stood at my gate and said, "Don't open for anything that sounds like someone you love," and then walked back up the lane with his shoulders set like a man walking into weather. His light was on, three doors down. Or it had been. Now every house on the lane was dark except mine, and mine was dark too, because I'd decided an hour ago that I didn't want to see the rooms behind me reflected in the glass.

So I sat at the kitchen table with a candle, a pot of cold tea, and forty years of my grandmother's notebooks stacked in front of me like sandbags, and I watched the photographs.

They were spread across the table in rows. I'd taken them out of the albums deliberately, all of them, every picture the chair had ever entered — Edith as a young woman on the seafront at Whitby; Edith's mother outside a bakery long demolished; a wedding, a christening, a group of women with washing-line arms squinting into a sun that no longer existed anywhere but in silvered paper. In every one, the chair. Plain wood, straight-backed, pulled to the center of the frame as if the photographer had asked everyone to gather round it.

For most of the evening it had been empty.

At half past eleven — the hall clock said half past eleven, though I want to be clear from the start that the hall clock and I were no longer on speaking terms about time — the chair stopped being empty.

It didn't happen the way things happen in photographs. There was no flicker, no moment I could have caught if I'd blinked differently. I looked down at the row of pictures after pouring tea I wasn't going to drink, and the figure was simply *there*, seated in the chair in every photograph at once, the way the chair itself had always been in every photograph at once, as if it had never been anywhere else and I had merely been slow to see.

I will describe it as best I can, knowing description is already a kind of surrender.

It was more absence than shape. Imagine the outline a person leaves on a bed after they get up — the impression, not the body. It sat the way people sit when they are being polite: hands presumably folded, spine courteous, head inclined very slightly toward the camera. Toward me. In the Whitby photograph it faced left, where Edith stood laughing at something off-frame. In the wedding picture it faced right, toward the bride. In each image it oriented itself toward whoever in that photograph had been most alive.

In the newest one — the one taken last week, before I came, of the cottage garden — there was no one else in the frame at all.

It faced straight out.

* * *

I did the sensible thing first, because sensible is what folklorists do when the material turns on them: I documented it.

I wrote the time. I wrote *figure present in 31 of 31 photographs*. I wrote *seated, stationary, orientation variable*. My handwriting came out small and neat, the way it does when the alternative is shaking. Then I turned the photographs face-down, one by one, and told myself that paper has no power, that this is silver gelatin and card stock, that a thing cannot sit inside a picture any more than a song can bleed.

Then I turned them back over, because not looking felt worse, and by then I needed to know whether it had moved.

It hadn't. That was the thing. It did not approach. It did not lean, or rise, or grow. It sat in its chair in thirty-one small paper worlds and waited with the terrible patience of furniture, and I understood — sitting across the candle from it, close enough that the flame guttered when I breathed — that the waiting was the whole of its method. A hundred years of it. It had waited out famines and funerals and eleven singers, and it could wait out one more night, and one more after that, and it would never once need to raise what it kept where a voice would go.

The clock said twelve.

I looked away to write the hour, and when I looked back, the candle had burned down an inch and a half.

I checked. I'm not proud of how fast I checked — knocked the notebook off the table getting up, held the candle stub against the hall clock's face like a torch. Twelve-oh-four. Four minutes for an inch and a half of wax. I timed it again, properly, watching the flame: another inch and a half in four minutes. The candle was fine. The candle was behaving exactly as candles behave.

The clock was not.

By one o'clock by the hall clock, the candle had burned to a puddle and I had lit a second one, and the second one burned at the same honest rate while the hands crawled like things wading upstream. I put my ear to the clock case. The pendulum swung. Tick, tick, steady as a metronome, a sound I had fallen asleep to my whole childhood in this house. But the swing was slow — slower than a pendulum has any business being, each arc taking its sweet time, each tick arriving late, a clock ticking in honey.

Outside, the fog had come up off the fen and swallowed the gardens. I couldn't see the villagers anymore. I could hear them, though — not talking, not moving. Just the sound a crowd makes when it holds its breath together, which is no sound at all, which is a shape cut out of the night's noise, a silence with edges, stretching from Bram's gate to the vicarage wall.

I sang to myself to fill it. Badly. "Green Grow the Rushes" and then, when that ran out, the harvest song, verses one through three, the ones that were allowed. My own voice sounded thin and wrong in the boarded-up kitchen, and on the third verse I heard — not heard, *felt*, the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder — attention settle on me like a hand on the back of the neck.

I stopped singing.

In the photographs, the figure tilted its head. All thirty-one of them, in unison, the way a roomful of mirrors tilts when you tilt.

"Right," I said aloud, to it, to the kitchen, to nobody. "You don't get requests."

My voice cracked on *requests*, which rather undercut the line.

* * *

Somewhere past two — past two by a clock I no longer trusted, in a night that had begun to feel less like a duration and more like a place — I made the mistake of going upstairs.

I told myself it was for the blankets, and that was even true; the kitchen had gone cold in a way fire couldn't fix, a cold that came up through the floorboards like water through a hull. But the truth is I wanted the photographs out of my sightline, and I took them with me anyway, all thirty-one, because leaving them alone downstairs felt like leaving a child with a stranger. I stacked them in Edith's biscuit tin and carried the tin up with one arm and the candle with the other, and I did not look at the landing mirror, and I did not look at the landing mirror, and I looked at the landing mirror.

The chair was in it. Empty, angled toward the stairs, waiting for whoever came up next. Just the chair. I counted that as a mercy and hated myself for grading on that curve.

In Edith's bedroom I built a nest of blankets on her old bed and sat with my back against the headboard and the biscuit tin on my knees, and I read her notebooks by candlelight, not for information — I had the information, God help me, I had all of it, the famine and the threshold and the eleven names and the twelfth written in her hand, *Noor Coldharbour* — but for her voice. Because the Guest had her voice now, had used it, had offered me bargains in it, and I wanted the real one back. I wanted the marginalia that complained about wet boots and the notes that said *Agnes flat again, bless her* and the crossed-out fourth verse struck through eleven times like a woman beating at a door with her fists.

Halfway through the 1971 notebook I found a page I hadn't seen before, pressed between two others so the ink had offset faintly onto the facing sheet. Not a field note. A letter, unsent, addressed to no one.

*M.,* it began. *If you are reading this then either I failed or I am dead, and I find I no longer know which would be which.*

*I have tried to write down what it is like, the waiting, so that you will know it isn't hunger, exactly. It doesn't want to eat you. It wants you to stop resisting being waited for. Do you understand the difference? Hunger takes. Waiting persuades. By morning you will find yourself thinking the chair looks comfortable. You will think: it's only sitting. You will think: everyone else got to rest eventually. When those thoughts come, say your own name. Say it out loud, ugly and loud, until the thoughts shut up. Your name is the one thing it can't pronounce yet. Don't give it practice.*

*— E.*

I read it three times. Then I said my name out loud, ugly and loud, and the candle flame stood up straight as a soldier, and downstairs — muffled by a door and a dresser and a steamer trunk that smelled of river water — something shifted its weight in a wooden seat.

Not approaching. Settling. The way you settle in when the film has started and you intend to stay to the end.

* * *

The hours after that blur, and I'll set down what I can of them in order, because order is a rope and I was determined not to let go.

Three o'clock by the hall clock: the fog pressed against the windows so hard it seemed to have mass, and the pane bulged inward slightly, like a drum skin touched from the other side. I checked the photographs. The figure had not moved. I checked again twenty minutes later. It had not moved. I began checking every few minutes, compulsively, lifting the tin lid like a woman checking a cake, and every time: the same courteous posture, the same inclination of the absent head, the same nothing, and the nothing grew louder than anything in the house.

Four o'clock: I heard Agnes's voice in the garden, humming the fourth verse, and I pressed my hands over my ears and said my name until the humming stopped, and it stopped mid-phrase, politely, the way a caller stops when they realize you're not coming to the door.

Five o'clock: the candle problem became unavoidable. I had six left. At the current rate — the true rate, the candle rate, not the clock rate — I had perhaps five hours of light, and the night outside showed no intention whatsoever of ending. I stood at the window and searched the east for the least grey suggestion of dawn and found the same dishwater ceiling, seamless, committed. Somewhere in the last hour the clouds had stopped moving. I watched them for a long time to be sure. They were not moving. The world had begun holding still around the house, the way the villagers held still in their gardens, everything waiting on the same appointment.

Five-thirty: I caught myself thinking the chair looked comfortable.

I said my name until my throat was raw.

Six o'clock: I went down to the kitchen for water and found the photographs I'd left face-down on the table — the ones from the city, my flat, my life, smuggled into the pile somehow, or always part of it — and in every one the chair now stood before a lit hearth. Firelight moved in the images. Actual movement, actual flicker, orange light breathing on walls in pictures taken in daylight decades ago. The convergence Edith's ledger had promised, the single night all the photographs were traveling toward. The anniversary. Tonight. This night, this endless, unmoving night, the one the whole century had been rowing toward with me in the bow.

And in the photograph of my own kitchen — my real kitchen, three hundred miles south, with its chipped blue mugs and the postcard on the fridge — the chair had been drawn out from the table. Pulled back, the way you pull a chair for a guest. Facing the doorway.

Facing anyone who might walk in.

I filled a glass of water with steady hands, drank it, and laughed once, briefly, at the absurdity of being thirsty — the body insisting on its animal business while the soul stood trial upstairs — and the laugh came out wrong in the silent house, too loud, and in thirty-one photographs the figure's head lifted, very slightly, like a dog hearing its name.

"No," I told it. "That's not yours either."

Seven o'clock. Eight. The pendulum swung. The candles shrank. The fog leaned. Once, from the direction of Bram's house, I heard a man's voice saying numbers — counting, tallying aloud, *eleven, twelve, thirteen* — ragged and stubborn, a lighthouse of arithmetic, and I clung to the sound of it like a handhold, because it meant Bram was awake, Bram was holding, Bram was paying whatever price the truth charges per hour, and I was not alone in the dark even if I was alone in the house.

Nine o'clock by a clock that had told me nine o'clock twice already tonight.

I sat on Edith's bed with the tin in my lap and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding. If the night wouldn't end, the candles would. When the candles went, the photographs would be the only light in the house — thirty-one small hearths, all burning, all of them his. And the figure had not approached, not once, not in all these hours, because it had never needed to approach. It had only ever needed to wait longer than I could stay awake, longer than the candles, longer than the night, and the night, I understood at last, was not passing over the house.

The night was pooling here. Gathering. Being kept, like a cellar, like a larder, like a jar of something salted down against winter.

The hall clock struck ten — I counted the strokes, ten of them, unhurried, each one falling into the silence like a stone into a well that never quite reached bottom — and in the biscuit tin, in thirty-one photographs, the figure in the chair raised one hand and rested it, gently, on the arm of the chair beside it.

On the empty chair beside it.

The one it had pulled out for me.
