## 34. The Fourth Verse

The door was not locked.

Noor had barricaded it with the kitchen table, with Edith's cedar chest, with every chair in the cottage except the one that mattered, and still, when she put her hand to the latch, none of it resisted her. The furniture moved aside like water. It was as if the house had only ever been holding the door shut against her leaving — and now that she meant to go out, it stood down.

The night outside was the color of wet slate. No stars. The lane ran from her gate to the village green and it was lined with people.

They had come at three o'clock, the vicar first, then the others, filing out of their houses with their doors standing open behind them — every door in Marrowfen open, she saw now, a whole village unbuttoned, offered up. They stood in their gardens and along the verges in their nightclothes and coats thrown over, and they did not shout. That was the worst of it. A mob screams. This was quieter than a funeral. They simply watched her windows, and when she appeared at the glass they inclined their heads, politely, terribly, the way you nod to someone being led to the scaffold on your behalf.

Bram Tull stood between them and her gate. He had been there for hours; she could see it in the set of his shoulders, in the chalk dust ground into the knees of his trousers. In one hand he held a stub of chalk worn round as a river stone. His lips were moving. She realized he was counting — doors, tallies, verses, anything with an order to it — counting aloud under his breath the way a man grips a rope.

"Noor." His voice carried down the lane without effort, as though the night itself had agreed to let it through. "You don't have to open anything. Whatever's at your door, it can't come in unless it's asked, and it can't be asked unless you sing, and you don't ever have to sing."

"I know," she said.

She came down the hall. The photographs were on the walls around her, dozens of them now, Edith's and her own and ones she had no memory of taking, and in every single frame the chair stood at the center of the room, hearth-lit, and beside the chair stood the Guest.

It was more absence than shape. Her eyes kept trying to find edges and failing, the way you fail to look directly at the space between two thoughts. But it had posture. It had *manners*. One long nothing of an arm rested on the chair's back, and the chair was pulled out from the table, angled toward the front door, angled toward her, the way a host angles a seat for the guest of honor who has finally arrived.

*Come sit,* the dead had sung at her windows all night, in Agnes's voice, in the washerwoman's voice, in a young woman's voice that made Bram bleed at the nose across the lane. *The fire is lit. The verse is finished. Come sit.*

Noor stopped at the threshold and looked at the thing in her hallway, and it looked back — she felt the looking, a pressure like deep water, patient as geology.

"You've been waiting a hundred years," she said. "What's one more hour."

It didn't answer. It never answered in its own voice. But the hearth in the photographs flared, all of them at once, and the night outside grew a shade darker, and somewhere down the lane a child began, softly, helplessly, to hum the harvest tune.

* * *

She went past Bram at the gate and he caught her arm.

"Whatever you're doing—"

"What I'm doing is what should have been done forty years ago." She looked at him. His face was gray, bloodied at the nostril, sixty years old and scared as a boy. "Bram. If this works, there's something you need to know before it does. Because afterward you might not—"

"Don't." His grip tightened. "Don't tell me things I won't get to keep. That's crueler than the rest of it."

"It's the price," she said. "I want someone to know the price while I'm still here to pay it."

He stared at her, and she watched him understand — watched it land in him like cold water — because Bram Tull had spent his whole life keeping accounts, and he could read a ledger faster than any man in the fen.

"No," he said. "No, there has to be another—"

"There isn't. There's only the debt or the debtor." She pried his fingers loose, gently, one by one. "Keep the notebook. Tell it wrong if you have to. Just tell it."

Then she walked down the lane between the gardens of Marrowfen, and the villagers drew back from her as she passed, not in fear exactly — in *recoil*, the way grass leans away from something about to fall on it. The vicar stepped forward once, hands raised, smiling his smooth rehearsed smile, and said, "Miss Coldharbour, whatever you believe happened in 1923, I assure you the parish record shows—"

"The parish record shows what the parish paid for," Noor said, and did not stop walking.

Behind her she heard Bram begin to speak. Not counting this time. Reciting. The true history, the ugly one, aloud, in a voice that cracked and steadied and cracked again: *"In the winter of 1923 this village starved, and my great-grandfather's generation invited something over the threshold to end it, and it fed the fields and it fed on the singers, eleven of them across a hundred years, and we knew, we always knew, and we sang everything but the fourth verse because the fourth verse names the listener—"*

Good, she thought. Let them hear it. Let truth stand guard over him the way it had all night.

The Guest was waiting at the green.

It had come out ahead of her — or it had always been there, folded into the dark between the war memorial and the empty bandstand, and only now consented to take up room. The chair was with it. Of course the chair was with it: plain wood, no ornament, older than the memorial, older than the famine, sitting on the frost-stiff grass as though it had grown there. Pulled out. Facing her.

Around the green, in a wide ring, the village stood. Every soul in Marrowfen. She counted without meaning to — old habit, Edith's habit — and got the number she'd known she'd get. The number from the ledger. Twelve households' worth, plus herself.

Plus the Guest.

"Edith?" she said quietly, testing, one last time.

The absence shaped itself, briefly, into something almost tender. And in her head, in her grandmother's voice — warm, dry, the voice that had read her *The Owl Service* by torchlight — it said:

*You came. I hoped you would. I feared you would. Both, love. Both, since the day you were born listening.*

"That's not her."

*Nothing here is anyone. That is rather the point.* The voice sighed, and the sigh moved the fog. *But I keep what I am given, and I was given her faithfully, for forty years, and so I sound like her. Sit, Noor Coldharbour. The night will hold. The sun will wait. It has waited for me before.*

"No," Noor said. "It hasn't. Not once. Not in a hundred years. You've taken eleven singers and every time the village bought you off with silence, and silence isn't a bargain, it's just a slower way of saying yes." Her breath smoked in the cold. "My grandmother chose silence. I understand why. I've spent thirty-six hours understanding why. But she also wrote it all down. Every verse. Every name. Every cost. She couldn't break the pact and she couldn't bear it, so she left me the one thing stronger than either."

*And what is that?*

"A song sung twice."

The ring of villagers stirred. Somewhere a woman began to weep without noise.

Noor took Edith's final notebook from inside her coat. Its cover was soft as cloth from a century of handling. She had read it until the pages felt like her own skin. The fourth verse was there in Edith's collected notation — not the garbled half-heard version the fiddler had choked on in the pub, not the tune Agnes hummed in her sleep, but the full notation, checked against three sources, against the archive, against the dead washerwoman's lullaby, against the eleventh entry in the county records. Every note accounted for. Every word.

The verse that names the listener.

She had thought, in the small hours, about running again. About the city, the train, the flat where the chair sat at her kitchen table in photographs she'd burned twice. But Bram had been right, in his gruff ledger-keeper's way: *it doesn't chase, it waits where you're going*, and there was nowhere she could go that wasn't already furnished.

There was only forward. There had only ever been forward, for a Coldharbour who listens.

"Noor." Bram had broken from his recitation, had crossed half the green before two farmers caught his arms. His voice tore. "Girl — listen to me — you don't know what it takes, you don't know what—"

"Yes I do." She turned her head and found his eyes and held them. "Chapter and verse. I know exactly what it takes."

And because he had earned it, and because someone in that ring deserved to hear it said plainly, she said it louder:

"It takes the singer! It unwrites what the singer most truly holds — and what I most truly hold is myself, do you understand, it will take my name and my face and my place in every photograph and every memory, and you'll all go home at sunrise free, and none of you will know why, and none of you will know me!" Her voice rang off the cottages. "That's the price. That's the whole price. And I'm paying it, because a hundred years of you lot paying it in other people's children is enough."

Silence. Real silence, the fen kind, vast and listening.

Then the vicar, softly, horribly reasonable: "Miss Coldharbour, no one is asking you to—"

"You are," Noor said. "That's exactly what you're doing. You walked here at three in the morning to ask me. Politely. Terribly. Go home."

Nobody went home.

She opened the notebook. Her hands were steady. She noticed that, and was grateful, and knew the steadiness wasn't hers alone — it was Edith's, lent down through the paper, through the notation, through thirty years of a woman copying out a verse and striking it through, eleven times, each strike a wound, each copy an act of hope. *So someone after me can finish it.*

"Grandmother," Noor said, to the air, to the absence, to whatever of Edith Ravensworth was still anywhere at all, "if any part of you is in this — don't fight it. Please. You kept me in the world forty years. Let me spend that."

The Guest made no move to stop her.

That was the detail she would have written down, if there had been time to write anything down: it did not stop her. It straightened, the way a listener straightens, the way a hungry man straightens when the meal is finally served. It had waited a century for a Coldharbour to sing the fourth verse aloud, and it was not going to interrupt the singing.

It wanted to be named. Naming was the oldest hunger there was.

Noor lifted her face to the lightless sky, filled her lungs with fen-cold air, and sang.

* * *

The fourth verse, complete, in Edith's collected notation:

It was not a long verse. That was the trick of it, the horror of it — four lines, a melody simple as a gate swinging. Any child could carry it. A child had carried it, once, humming in her sleep for eighty years.

*"Who walks the fen when the reeds hang low,*
*who counts the doors where the tallies grow,*
*who sits unsung at every board —*
*the listener, the listener, the Hollow Guest our silence stored."*

The name went out over Marrowfen like a struck bell.

The village flinched as if struck. All of them, at once, a single convulsion — the vicar staggered, the farmers dropped Bram's arms, a woman near the well pressed both hands over her mouth as though to take the words back by force. Because the verse named the listener, and the listener was not only the Guest. The verse named everyone who had ever heard the songs and refused to sing them twice. It named the whole ring of them. It named a century.

And the Guest —

The Guest *arrived*. That was the only word. For a hundred years it had been a presence, a pressure, a chair in a photograph, and now, named, it became a fact. The fog pulled inward toward it like a tide turning. The dark bent around it. She could see it fully for the first time and understood why no singer had ever managed to describe it: it was shaped like the space a person leaves. Like the outline of everyone the village had fed to it, eleven silhouettes folded into one courtesy, wearing Edith's patience and Agnes's hum and the washerwoman's wet red hands.

Its mouth, when it opened, was the shape of a door.

"*Coldharbour,*" it said, in its own voice at last, and its own voice was every voice it had ever borrowed, layered, harmonized, a choir of the taken. "*Named. Named and naming. Do you know what you have done, little singer?*"

"I've ended the lease," Noor said. Her voice shook now; the steadiness had gone where it needed to go, into the verse, and what was left was just a woman, freezing, terrified, magnificent with fear. "The pact is dissolved. You're not invited anymore. You're not *anything* anymore. You're a story with the terms read out loud, and stories can't keep house."

"*The pact dissolves,*" it agreed, and the agreement was worse than any threat. "*All debts are called in. All accounts settled. And yours, Noor Coldharbour, comes due at the last note.*"

"I know."

"*Hold the note, then,*" said the Hollow Guest, with something horribly like respect. "*See how long you can hold what you most truly hold.*"

It reached for her — not with hands, with *unwriting*, with the slow erasure she had felt gathering at the edges of her all night — and Noor Coldharbour did the bravest and simplest thing anyone in Marrowfen had done in a hundred years.

She sang the fourth verse again.

Twice. Freely. The taboo broken past mending, the name rung out a second time, and this time the village did not flinch — this time, she heard, faintly, raggedly, Bram Tull's cracked voice joining her on the second line, and then, unbelievably, one other voice, and then another, the whole cursed congregation of Marrowfen stumbling through the forbidden verse like children learning to walk, singing the listener's name into the open air where it could finally, finally die.

The Guest screamed without sound. The photographs, all of them, everywhere in the village, bleached white as bone.

And Noor felt herself begin to go — gently, thoroughly, like snow off a roof in morning sun — and gripped the last note with both hands, and held on.

* * *

The first thing to fade was her reflection in the dark cottage windows ringing the green. Then the chalk marks on her doors, lifting away like breath off glass. Then — she watched it happen, watched with the fierce attention of a folklorist to the end — the memory of her in Bram Tull's face, his expression smoothing from grief into blankness, into a man standing in a field at dawn wondering why he was crying.

*Tell it wrong if you have to,* she had said. *Just tell it.*

The sky in the east had gone the color of a bruise healing. Sunrise was coming. It was actually coming, for the first time in a night that had refused to end.

Noor held the note.

She thought of Edith, and found — this was the mercy, this was the terrible mercy — that she could not. The shape of her grandmother slid through her fingers like water, like the Guest itself, and Noor understood that she was now the only vessel left in all the world for both of them, and that the vessel was emptying.

So she did the last thing a Coldharbour who listens could do.

She began, silently, inside the fading citadel of herself, to write it all down — every verse, every name, every cost, the famine and the pact and the eleven singers and the twelfth entry and the woman who struck the verse through eleven times out of love — writing it into the only place that would hold, the place where she was still, for a few minutes more, the sole keeper of the true account of Marrowfen.

The sun broke the horizon.

The chair on the green was empty, and then it was not there at all, and then there had never been a chair, and the grass was only grass, and the village of Marrowfen stood blinking in honest daylight, free, and could not have said from what.

Only one man among them was crying, and could not say why.

And only one notebook, in a cottage that belonged to no one he could name, still held the beginning of the sentence: *Her name was Noor.*
