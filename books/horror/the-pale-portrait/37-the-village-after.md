## 37. The Village After

The morning after the night that would not end, Marrowfen woke to birdsong.

I stood at the cottage window and listened to it the way you listen to a language you almost remember — each note familiar, each one slightly wrong, as if the birds had learned their songs from a recording played at the wrong speed. The fog had lifted for the first time since I arrived. The lane lay clean and pale in the early light, doors standing open all down its length, and the villagers came out of their houses slowly, blinking, like people surfacing from a shared dream none of them could quite describe.

Nobody spoke. They just stood in their gardens, in their nightclothes, looking at one another across the low stone walls, and I understood that they were doing what I was doing: taking inventory. Counting what remained.

The chair was gone. I had checked every photograph in Edith's cottage before dawn — the ones that hadn't bleached white, which were fewer than I'd hoped — and there was no chair in any of them. No hearth-fire burning out of season. No hand on an armrest. No silhouette angled toward the camera with terrible patience. Just kitchens and weddings and christenings and a woman on a doorstep with a washing basket, alive in silver gelatin, her face turned toward whatever she'd been looking at when the shutter fell.

The washerwoman. I still didn't know her name. That was the pact's last cruelty, or maybe just its arithmetic: it had taken her before I thought to ask.

* * *

By noon, the singing started.

It began, as these things do in Marrowfen, at the pub — though the pub wasn't open, and wouldn't be for hours. Old Tolly Fenn simply walked up to the door, set down the bucket he was carrying, and sang the harvest song from the first verse through to the fourth without stopping, his voice cracked and thin and steady as a plumb line.

*And who will hear the listener listening,*
*who will name the name behind the door—*

I heard it from the cottage, half a mile off, because sound carries strangely over waterlogged ground, and because I think — I know now — that the song wanted to be heard. It had spent a century muffled. It came out of Tolly like water out of a cracked dam.

Nothing happened. That was the miracle of it, and the horror. He sang the fourth verse aloud in broad daylight, the verse that names the listener, the verse eleven singers had died for knowing, and the ground did not open, and the fields did not blacken, and no figure stepped out of any doorway wearing courtesy like a coat. The verse was only a verse. It had always been only a verse. The power had never lived in the words; it had lived in the silence around them, in the century of held breath, and now the breath was let out and the words were just words again, light as chaff.

By evening, everyone in Marrowfen knew the whole song. Children sang it skipping rope. A man painted the fourth verse on the gable end of the old mill in letters two feet high, and nobody stopped him, and nobody repainted it. Agnes Pike's grandniece — a girl of perhaps nineteen who had never once looked me in the eye before — knocked on my door to ask if I knew any other songs the old people used to hold back, because her mum said if this one could be sung, maybe the others could too, and she wanted to write them all down before they went.

I gave her everything. Edith's notebooks, my recordings, the transcriptions I'd made in the first weeks when I still believed this was research. She took them with both hands, the way you take something hot.

"You'll want credit," she said. "In the collection. For the finding."

"No," I said, and heard how strange my own voice sounded, already thinner than it should be. "Just put Edith Coldharbour. She found them first."

"Who?"

* * *

That was the first time. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left, and it is going faster than I expected.

She said *who*, but not the way you say *who* when you've never heard a name. She said it the way you say *who* when someone has asked you to repeat yourself — a small snag, a momentary static, quickly smoothed over. She wrote *Edith Coldharbour* in her notebook without hesitation, because I spelled it for her, and then she looked at the page as if the letters might rearrange themselves into something she'd understand better.

"They were her notebooks," I said. "Your grandmother's neighbor. The folklorist."

"Oh," she said, warming. "Old Mrs. — the cottage lady. Of course." And she smiled, and meant it, and carried the notebooks away under her arm like a rescued child, and I stood in my doorway watching her go and felt the first true cold of the new arrangement settle into my chest.

The Guest had unwritten what I most truly held. I had assumed — God help me, I had assumed it would take Edith, that the price would be her memory, her photographs, her place in the village's telling. But standing in that doorway, watching a girl carry my grandmother's life away without knowing whose it was, I understood the shape of the transaction at last. It hadn't taken Edith from the world. It had taken *me* from the story. Every story. Starting with the one about last night.

Because here is what the villagers remembered of the anniversary: nothing, and then something, and then less. By the second day they remembered that a strange night had happened, long and lit and full of waiting, and that it had ended, and that the songs were free. By the third day they remembered that someone had ended it. By the fifth day, the someone had become "the folklorist" — a role, not a person, a function like the tallies had been a function. By the end of the week, the vicar preached a sermon about deliverance in which the deliverance arrived, apparently, on its own, an act of grace, and the congregation nodded along, and I sat in the back pew and felt the word *folklorist* slide off the world like rain off slate.

Bram remembered. Bram was the exception, and I will come to him, because he is the reason any of this is being written down.

* * *

But first, the liberation, because I don't want to give the wrong proportions. The liberation was real. It was enormous. It was worth it, and I need to write that down while I can still mean it.

You have to understand what a century of silence does to a village. It gets into the mortar of the houses. It gets into the way people look at one another — that flicker of calculation, that constant quiet audit of *what can be said to this one*. Marrowfen had been polite the way a hostage is polite. And now the hostages had walked out of the bank, and the strangeness of walking upright after so long crouched made them laugh at nothing, weep at nothing, sing at everything.

They sang at the washerwoman's funeral — a proper funeral, finally, a month late, once the fear had lifted enough to let grief in. All four verses, and then two more verses nobody had sung in living memory, recovered from a girl's memory of her grandmother humming, and then a chorus that turned out to have a counterpoint. The vicar didn't call it a weak heart. He called her by name — Maren Slee, her name was Maren Slee, I finally asked and someone finally answered, easily, gladly, because names cost nothing now — and he said she had died holding a door shut so that others could learn to open theirs, and whether or not that was literally true, it was true enough, and Maren's sister wept openly in the front row, and no one watched anyone else while they did it.

Agnes Pike's grave got a stone with the fourth verse carved into it, the line that names the listener, because Agnes had hummed it in her sleep for eighty years and the village decided she'd earned the right to have it said over her awake.

The archive girl — her name was Wren, Wren Oakes, she'd come from the county records office — catalogued Edith's notebooks and pronounced them the most complete body of collected song from the fen country in existence. "Whoever this Coldharbour was," she told the pub, "she had an ear like a net." And forty people drank to Mrs. Coldharbour, whoever she was, and the toast landed on me like snow falling on a fire.

I sat in the corner with my pint going flat and felt myself go out of the room by degrees. Not dramatically. Nothing about this has been dramatic. It was more like watching a tide withdraw from a stretch of sand — each retelling of the anniversary night pulled a little more of me out with it. The stranger who broke the pact became the folklorist became the woman who stayed became, eventually, in the version told at the harvest fair, *someone from away who helped*, and the barman who told it that way glanced around the room as if checking whether anyone would correct him, and no one did, and neither did I, because I wasn't sure anymore that correction was possible. You cannot testify to your own erasure. The witness stand dissolves under you.

There was one moment — I want to record this one exactly, because it frightened me more than the Guest ever did. At the fair, a child, maybe seven, tugged my sleeve and asked if I was the lady from the photograph. I said which photograph, and she pointed at the noticeboard outside the church, where the vicar had pinned a picture of the village from 1984, and there in the back row was a young woman with dark hair and my grandmother's jaw, and the caption read *Village visitors, harvest 1984*, and I was in it, I was plainly in it, and the child said, "Mum says nobody knows who that is."

"I know who that is," I said.

"Do you?" said the child, with the terrible directness of children, and ran off.

* * *

The songs lost their power the way ice loses its grip on a pond — not all at once, but edge by edge, in the warm places first. Wren Oakes recorded Tolly Fenn singing the harvest song twice through, back to back, deliberately, as a test, and the second rendition differed from the first only in that his voice gave out on the high note. She played both recordings for the pub. Nothing happened. People clapped. Someone bought Tolly a whisky. The taboo was dead, and its corpse was ordinary, and that ordinariness was the whole victory.

Other things loosened too. The chalk tallies vanished from the doors — not smeared, not erased, just *gone*, the way a scar goes when the wound was never really there. Bram told me later he'd walked the lane the morning after and found every lintel bare, and had stood in front of his own door with his hand raised to check marks that weren't needed anymore, and had laughed until he cried, and wasn't sure which of the two it mostly was.

The drowned part of the village — the old Marrowfen under the fen water, the streets the maps stopped showing — gave up a bell. Fishermen hauled it up in their nets, green with a century of silt, and when they cleaned it they found the founder's mark and a date, and the church hung it in the tower, and when it rang for the first time the sound carried across the water and every dog in the parish answered it, and I stood on the bank with my hands over my ears and my eyes streaming and couldn't have told you whether I was grieving or rejoicing, and I have decided, writing this, that it was both, and that both is what freedom costs, and that I would pay it again.

I would pay it again. Hold on to that sentence. Whatever else thins out of these pages, hold on to that one.

* * *

The fading has a rhythm, and I've learned its rhythm the way I once learned the songs: by attention, by repetition, by sitting with it until it gives up its structure.

In the mornings I am most myself. Names hold. My own reflection holds — though I've noticed the mirror takes a half-second longer to find me than it used, a small lag, a search engine returning fewer results. In the evenings I fray. Sounds reach me late. Yesterday the postman handed me a letter addressed to *The Occupant, Hollow Cottage* — Hollow Cottage, which has been Edith's house and then my house for sixty years, renamed overnight by some consensus I was never consulted on — and I read my own address as a stranger would, and felt the ground tilt.

People are kind to me. That's the worst part, or nearly. They're kind the way you're kind to a lodger, a visitor, a nice woman staying the summer. Wren brings me bread. The vicar asks after my health. Children wave. None of them can tell me why I'm here, and increasingly, I think, none of them can quite see why I'm here either — I'm becoming a question the village has stopped asking, furniture in the story, a chair pulled to the center of the frame.

A chair. Yes. Even now, even unmade, the Guest leaves its grammar everywhere. Perhaps that's all any haunting ever was: a way of arranging people around an absence.

Except Bram. Bram arranges himself around a presence, and the presence is me, and he fights for it daily, visibly, like a man bailing a boat with his hat.

He says my name every time we meet — full name, both barrels, *Noor Coldharbour*, as if the saying of it were a tally, a chalk mark renewed against the forgetting. He tells the story of the anniversary night in the pub, every week, and every week he puts me back in it, larger, refusing the tide. "The folklorist's granddaughter," he says. "Noor. She opened the door herself. Nobody made her. She walked past all of us and sang the verse, and it cost her—" and here he always stops, because the cost is the thing that slides, the cost is the part the telling won't hold, and I watch the whole room try to remember what he means and fail, kindly, collectively, like a congregation missing a cue.

Last night he walked me home from the pub — my home, the cottage, though the lane signs have begun to disagree — and at the gate he took my hand in both of his, work-rough and shaking slightly, and said, "Tell me something only you'd know. Something to hold onto. Because I can feel it going, Noor. I can feel the shape of you getting simpler. Like a coin worn smooth."

I told him about Edith teaching me to whistle through a blade of grass, badly, both of us laughing. I told him about the smell of her attic — dust and paper and lamp oil. I told him the last thing she ever said to me on the phone: *"Don't come to Marrowfen, love. There's nothing here for you but the past, and the past doesn't share."*

Bram repeated it back to me three times, word for word, like a man learning a rope pattern. Then he said, "Tomorrow I'll ask you again. And the day after. Until one of us runs out of days."

"And when I run out?" I asked. "When I can't tell you anything only I'd know?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Down the lane, behind him, the new bell rang the hour, and the sound went out over the water where the old village slept, and for a second I swear the water rang back.

"Then I'll tell *you*," he said. "Every day. Your name, her name, the grass-whistle, all of it. I'll be your memory if you'll be mine. That's the bargain now. That's the only pact left in Marrowfen, and I'm keeping it."

I went inside. I lit the lamp. I opened Edith's final notebook to the first blank page — the pages after her last entry, the space she left, whether she knew she was leaving it or not — and I dipped the pen, and my hand hesitated over the paper longer than it ever has.

Because here is what I saw, in the lamplight, in the margin, faint as breath on glass: handwriting that was not mine and was not Edith's. Small, patient, courteous letters, in ink the color of shallow water, and the letters said:

*Write it while you can. I am not the only thing that was named tonight.*

*Something else came loose when the pact dissolved. It has been waiting under the songs for a hundred years, patient as I was, and it does not want a chair.*

*It wants the one who remembers.*
