## 40. A Song Sung Twice

*The following pages are in Noor Coldharbour's hand, written in the last week of her presence, in Edith's notebook, in the cottage at Marrowfen. They begin on a page already half-filled with her grandmother's notation — a harvest tune, verses one through three, the fourth struck through eleven times. Noor wrote around the strike-throughs. She said later — said once, said only to Bram — that it felt like writing in the margins of a conversation she had finally been allowed to join.*

*This is what she wrote.*

---

If you are reading this, you count doors.

That's not a threat anymore. I want you to understand that first, because everything I was told when I arrived here was delivered in the grammar of threat, and it took me a whole year to learn that the grammar was the trap. The tallies, the unsung verses, the polite refusals — none of it was ever protection. It was a language of appeasement, and appeasement is just fear with good manners. What I'm writing now is a different language. I hope it holds.

My name is Noor Coldharbour. I was a folklorist. I came to Marrowfen in the fog with a recording kit and a head full of method — document, don't intervene; record, don't repeat — and I broke every rule I'd been taught, and it cost everything, and I would do it again tomorrow, and I want to tell you why, while I still have a tomorrow in which to tell it.

I am going away. Not dying — I need you to hear the difference, because the village will want to call it dying, will want to file it under the old arithmetic where a singer is taken and a debt is paid. It isn't that. I was not taken. I gave. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole story.

The Guest is gone. I named it — sang the fourth verse complete, at sunrise, on the anniversary, with the whole village watching from behind me like a congregation at a hanging — and when it was named it could no longer be fed, because you cannot feed a thing you can see. It unmade the pact. And unmaking has a price, and the price was me. Not my life. My *place*. My name, my face, my seat at tables, my line in photographs. The thing I most truly held, and what I most truly held, it turns out, was not my grandmother — I thought it would be Edith, I braced for that, I sang anyway — it was myself. The self that listens. The folklorist's self, the one that stands at the edge of other people's songs and writes them down. That's what I am. That's what's being unwritten.

I have maybe a week of being written at all.

So here is what I know, and I'm putting it down plainly, because Edith put it down in riddles for forty years and the riddles killed Agnes Pike and the washerwoman whose name I am already losing — I can feel the shape of her going, a warm place in my memory cooling — and I will not have riddles kill anyone else.

One: the famine was real. In the winter of 1891 the village starved, and my great-great-grandmother sang something over her threshold, and the fields came back, and so did the dead, walking politely, asking to sit. Two: the thing she invited was not evil. I want that recorded too. It was *hungry*, and it was *lonesome*, and it had been singing its own song into the dark for longer than there were villages to hear it, and when Edith's grandmother sang back, it thought it had found a friend. It has been waiting a century for someone to finish the conversation. Every death in this village's ledger was a misunderstanding conducted with perfect courtesy. Three: the tallies were never a ward. They were a *reminder* — a way of counting what the village owed, disguised as a way of counting what the village feared. Bram Tull understands this better than anyone now. Ask him. He will tell you the ugly version, and the ugly version is the true one, and the true one is the only thing that ever held it off.

Four: the fourth verse. I'm going to write it out. Complete. Unstruck. Edith struck it through eleven times and I have sat with this page trying to understand why, and I think I finally do, and it is the hardest thing I have ever had to write, so I will write it slowly.

She struck it through because she loved me.

She learned what naming cost — she read the same ledger I read, in the same archive, on the same kind of desperate night — and she chose. She chose silence. She chose to keep the pact alive, to keep the Guest waiting, to let it take a singer every generation, rather than sing the verse and lose me. She was a coward and she was the bravest woman I ever knew and both of those are true and I refuse to choose between them. She sat in the chair herself, once, forty years ago, smiling, paid in full, and she came out the other side and spent her whole life keeping the debt from reaching me, and the way she kept it was to make sure no Coldharbour ever listened again.

And then she died and left me the house.

I have laughed about that. I have cried about it. Mostly I have sat in her kitchen at the table where the chair used to stand and I have understood that love is not always a protection. Sometimes love is the trap. She built the trap out of love, plank by plank, and it was a good trap, and it almost worked, and the only reason it failed is that I am my grandmother's granddaughter and I listened anyway.

So: the fourth verse. It's on the next page, in her notation, with my corrections. Sing it twice if you need to. Sing it a hundred times. It has no power anymore except the power of a true thing said out loud, which is the only power that was ever worth having. It names the listener. That's all the taboo ever was — the verse describes the thing at the edge of the hearing, the hungry lonesome thing, and names it *friend*, and asks it in, and the asking is what bound us, because an invitation freely given cannot be freely taken back. Except it can. I took it back. I stood in the doorway at sunrise and I sang the verse *backward in intent* — not inviting, but releasing — and the Guest looked at me with whatever a thing like that uses for eyes, and I swear on Edith's grave that what it did was nod. Like a guest finally, finally allowed to go home.

The chair is gone from every photograph in Marrowfen. I checked. I spent a whole day checking, photograph by photograph, Edith's albums and the vicar's parish records and Agnes's shoebox of funeral pictures, and there is not one chair in any of them, and the strangest part is how ordinary they all look now. Just people. Just kitchens and weddings and a dog on a step. I sat on the floor of the front room surrounded by a century of photographs and I cried, and I don't know if I cried from grief or relief or the sheer exhausting ordinariness of images that only show what was there.

Here is what will happen to me, so you are not surprised. The village will forget me first — I can feel it happening already, the washerwoman's funeral, the way the vicar's voice slid past my name last Sunday like a stone skipping off water. Then the photographs will go, then the notebook entries in other hands, then, I think, Edith's memory of me, which is the one that hurts, because Edith is gone and I am her last living thought of herself as someone loved. Then it will be Bram, holding on with both hands, saying my name like a tally every morning at my door. He told me he'd do that. He said, *I counted doors for forty years out of guilt, I can count one name out of love.* Bram Tull, who I arrived in this village distrusting, who turned out to be the only honest man in it.

And then it will be this notebook. And that's enough. That's more than the eleven singers got — eleven names in a ledger, blank entries, chalk on doors. I get a book. I get my own hand. I get to say what I said and why I said it and what it cost, in my own words, for as long as paper lasts.

What do we owe the dead and the forgotten? I used to think the answer was remembrance, and I have learned, at the very end of myself, that the answer is *witness*. Remembrance is a gift we give and can't always keep. Witness is a thing we do, and having done it, it's done. I witnessed the famine ledger and the eleven names and the fourth verse and the chair and the night that would not end, and I sang, and the singing is in the world now, outside me, unowned. You can't unwrite a song that everyone knows. The Guest taught me that. Maybe that's the one good thing it taught me.

Sing the songs. All the verses. Twice, if you like. Three times. Teach them to children who ask annoying questions. When someone comes to your door with a recording kit and a head full of method, let them in, and make them tea, and sing until their batteries die, and then sing a cappella.

And if you find a chair in a photograph that shouldn't be there — if the pictures start making room for someone — don't chalk your doors. Don't count. Open the door, and say the true thing, whatever the true thing is, out loud, in your own voice.

It's the only invitation that ever mattered.

— Noor Coldharbour, Marrowfen, in the last week of her being written

*Below, in a different hand — broader, slower, a working man's hand that has held chalk for forty years — the page continues.*

---

* * *

Bram Tull writes this, and he writes it slow, because his eyes are bad and his hand is worse and because he wants to get it right, and getting it right is the whole of what's left to him now.

Three weeks since the sunrise. The village is strange in a way I never expected: it's *light*. Not the weather — the weather's the same grey it's been a hundred years. The people. I walked down the lane on Sunday and old Marta Ellery was teaching the harvest song to her granddaughter on the step, all four verses, loud, wrong in places, and nobody flinched, and the girl asked what the fourth verse meant and Marta said *it means you listen*, and that was it. That was all. A hundred years of silence and it comes down to a child being told to listen.

The vicar has rewritten the parish history again — third version I've seen — and this one's nearly honest, which for him is a miracle. There's a famine, and a song, and a cost, and he's even left a blank line in the memorial roll where a name should go. He doesn't know whose. Nobody does. He thinks the line is for one of the eleven. I know better. I stand next to that blank line sometimes and I say the name under my breath, and I'll keep saying it as long as my mouth works, because someone has to, and I'm the only one left who can.

That's the hard part, and I'll say it plain because she said things plain and she'd want this plain: I'm the only one. I look at her photograph — the one I took of her the morning after, standing in the garden with fog in her hair, laughing at something I'd said — and I see her, clear as Sunday, and everyone else who looks at that photograph sees an empty garden. I've shown it to people. I've watched their eyes slide off the space where she stands. It's like watching water refuse to wet a stone. And I can't decide if it's the worst thing that ever happened to me or the most important job I've ever been given, and I've decided it's both, and I've decided to stop deciding.

The cottage is mine now, or nobody's, which amounts to the same thing. I keep it. I air the rooms. I've left her notebook on the kitchen table where she wrote the last of it, and I've left the fourth verse page open, because it seems right that a true thing should sit in the open in that house from now on. The tallies by the doors I've washed off. All of them. Took me an afternoon and a bucket of water and I stood back at the end and looked at the clean doorframes and I wept like a child. Forty years of chalk. My sister's door was the first one I ever counted. I was eleven.

She'd have told me to stop crying and start writing. So.

* * *

The traveler came on a Tuesday, in the fog, the way folklorists do.

I saw her from the garden — a young woman, city boots, a canvas bag with a recording kit strapped to it, standing at the bend in the lane where the village first shows itself and most visitors first think about turning back. She didn't turn back. She stood there a long moment, looking at the houses, and then she looked at the cottage, at Edith's cottage, at the cottage where I was standing like a fool with a hoe in my hand, and she started walking toward it, and I knew — the way you know weather coming — that I knew what she was.

She knocked. Of course she knocked. They always knock.

I opened the door before the second knock, which surprised her, and I looked at her, and she looked at me, and she said, "I'm sorry to trouble you — I'm collecting songs. Local songs. I understand Marrowfen has some that are... unusual." And her voice did the thing their voices all do, the careful thing, the thing that means she's already heard a rumor and is pretending she hasn't.

And I stood in the doorway of that house, in the doorway where a woman sang a hungry lonesome thing over the threshold a hundred and thirty years ago, and where another woman stood at sunrise and sang the same song backward until the world came loose, and I made a choice that took me no time at all, because it had been made for me, in a notebook, in a hand I'd know anywhere.

"Come in," I said. "But first — you'll want the fourth verse. They'll all stop at three, and they'll all have their reasons, and the reasons are rubbish. So you'll want it from me, and I'll give it to you, and I'll give it to you twice, so you get it right."

She blinked. "Twice?"

"Twice," I said. "It's a song. Songs are meant to be sung twice. That's how you know they're songs and not wounds."

And I sang it. There in the doorway, loud enough for the lane to hear, in Edith's notation, complete, the verse that names the listener — and I sang it again, and my voice cracked on the high line both times, and the second time I wasn't singing it for her. I was singing it for the blank line in the memorial roll. I was singing it for eleven names in a ledger and a woman at a doorstep at dawn and a sister whose door I counted for forty years. I was singing it for the folklorist standing in the garden behind the traveler, in the fog, in the photograph only I can see, and I felt — I will swear this on anything you like — I felt the song *land*, somewhere, the way a tally lands. Counted. Received.

The traveler had her recorder out before I finished the second pass. Her hands were shaking. "What is it?" she said. "What does it mean?"

"It means you listen," I said, which is the true answer, and stepped back from the door. "Come in. I'll make tea. And then I'm going to tell you about a famine, and a ledger, and a woman who loved her granddaughter enough to damn a village for forty years, and a woman who loved a village enough to be forgotten by it. It'll take all day. It might take longer. I've got time."

She came in. She hung her coat on the hook like she'd been doing it for years. And the kitchen — I noticed this, I'll always notice this — the kitchen had one chair at the table, pulled out slightly, the way chairs are, and it was just a chair. Wood and wear and nothing else. It has been just a chair every day since.

The chair stays empty. That's how I keep the house. Not with chalk. With that.

* * *

*The notebook ends here, except for one line, in the traveler's hand, added recently, in pencil, lightly — the way you write when you're not sure you have the right to.*

*I asked Bram who wrote the pages before his. He looked at me a long time and said a name I've never heard anywhere else, a name that isn't in the parish roll or the county archive or any photograph in this village. He says it every morning at the cottage door, like a count. Like a tally. Like a prayer that someone, somewhere, can still hear it.*

*I've started saying it too.*
