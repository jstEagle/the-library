## 5. Grandmother's Verse

The attic stairs were the eleventh door, and Ansel hated them for it.

He had counted again last night—eleven doors in a house built with ten—and then he had stood in the upstairs hall with his palm flat against the attic door's wood until the grain seemed to warm under his hand, like skin. He hadn't opened it. He'd gone back to bed and lain awake listening to the house breathe, if houses breathed, if that sound was breath and not something moving through the walls on all fours.

In the morning he opened it.

The stairs went up farther than they should have. He counted the steps out of habit now—counting was the only prayer he trusted anymore—and got nineteen going up. When he looked back down, he couldn't see the bottom. Just dark, patient as held water.

The attic was long and low, running the full spine of the house, lit by three dormer windows furred with dust. The light came down in slanted columns and hung there, thick as syrup, and motes turned in it slowly, not drifting so much as circling, as if each column of light were its own small weather system with its own loyalties.

His grandmother's things were everywhere. Not stored—arranged. Chairs faced one another across a dead hearth as though conversation had only just ended. A child's rocking horse stood mid-rock, frozen at the top of its arc. On a writing desk, a fountain pen lay uncapped beside a page of handwriting that stopped mid-word.

Ansel didn't touch any of it. He walked the center aisle between the furniture like a man walking a church aisle, and at the far end, under the last window, he found the trunk.

It was cedar, older than anything else up here, older maybe than the house, and it was unlocked. Inside: reel-to-reel tapes in labeled boxes, dozens of them, and a portable recorder with a leather case gone stiff as bark. The labels were in her handwriting—the sharp, slanted script he knew from birthday cards, from the flyleaf of every book she'd ever given him.

*Twins, age 4 — song, first attempt.*
*Twins, age 5 — song, second attempt.*
*Twins, age 6 — song, third attempt.*
*Twins, age 7 — DO NOT FINISH.*

He sat down on the floor with the boxes spread around him and read that last label four times. *DO NOT FINISH.* Not "incomplete." Not "unfinished." An instruction. She had written it to herself, or to whoever found them, and she had underlined it twice.

He threaded the first tape anyway. His hands were steady in the way hands go steady when they've stopped asking permission from the rest of you.

A click. Hiss. Then her voice filled the attic, and Ansel was six years old again and safe, which was the cruelest thing the tape could have done.

"—all right, my darlings, sit still, sit *still*. Hands in laps. This is an old song. Older than this house, which is saying something. You'll learn it because you're Montroses, and Montroses keep things."

"Keep what?" That was Aurel. He could tell by the gap where a front tooth should have been. He could tell by everything; he would have known that voice underwater, in ash, in his own mouth.

"Never you mind what. Sing when I sing. Stop when I stop. And Aurel—" a pause, a smile you could hear, "—no harmonizing. Not yet."

Then she sang.

Ansel had forgotten the lullaby. He was certain he had. But the melody rose off the tape and met something already waiting inside him, some groove worn into him before memory started recording, and his lips moved with it. He clamped his jaw shut. The tune kept going without him, patient, pleased.

Her voice was low and sure through the first verse, warmer through the second. The words were simple—a house, a door, a sleeper who must not wake, a keeper who must not sleep. Children's-rhyme logic, the kind of nonsense that means nothing until it means everything:

*Hush now, hush, the pale room's deep,*
*and what I keep I mean to keep.*
*One door shut and one door sworn,*
*I'll hold the line till morning's born—*

She stopped there. Mid-phrase. The tape hissed for three seconds, four, and then her voice came back smaller.

"That's as far as we go today."

"But it doesn't end," said the other child. Him. His own voice, four years old, thin and tinny and already stubborn. "Songs end."

"This one doesn't," she said. "This one *stops*. There's a difference, and someday you'll thank me for knowing it."

* * *

He played the second tape. Then the third. Each year the twins' voices deepened by a shade, and each year the song grew by nothing. First verse, second verse, stop. Always stop. By the third tape the stopping had a rhythm to it, a flinch, and Ansel began to hear what lived underneath it: she wasn't teaching them a song. She was teaching them where the song ended and making the ending more familiar than the rest.

Training. That's what it was. Not music lessons. Evacuation drills.

On the fourth tape—the one marked *DO NOT FINISH*, age seven—he almost turned it off. His thumb hovered over the button for a long time. The attic light had gone amber around him; he'd been sitting on the floor for hours and hadn't noticed.

He pressed play.

"Today," his grandmother said, "we're going to talk about the part I don't teach you."

"Why not?" Aurel.

"Because knowing a thing is half of doing it, and I don't want you doing it."

"What happens if we sing it whole?" Ansel's voice. His own small voice asked it lightly, the way children ask about death, about God, about the dark under the bed—all the questions that are actually enormous, asked in the tone reserved for whether they can have dessert.

The silence on the tape lasted long enough that he checked the counter. Eleven seconds. He could hear her breathing. He could hear, faintly, beneath the hiss, something else—a low tone, sustained, that might have been wind against a window or might have been the recorder itself straining toward a sound it wanted to catch.

"Then the house keeps you," she said.

Not a story voice. Not a warning voice. Flat. Factual. The voice she'd used to tell them their father wasn't coming home.

"It keeps the singer?" Aurel again, and there was fascination in it, seven years old and already leaning toward the edge instead of away.

"It keeps whatever finishes it." A chair creaked; she must have leaned forward. "Listen to me. Both of you. This house is a lock, and the song is the key, and a key is no good until it's whole. Every verse you learn makes you more of the key. That can't be helped—you're Montroses, it's in your blood like your eye color. But the last verse—" her voice caught, actually caught, and steadied itself with visible effort, "—the last verse isn't sung. It's *given*. Someone has to want to finish it. The song finds the wanting. Do you understand? It will come to you dressed up as love. It will come to you dressed up as mercy. It will sound exactly like the right thing to do."

"And if we never finish it?" Ansel, again. Even at four, even at seven, even now: always the other question. Always the door nobody else thought to check.

"Then the house stays locked," she said, "and the keeping passes down, and one day you'll be sitting where I'm sitting with two children of your own, lying to them gently, and you'll understand why I'm lying to you now."

"Lying about what?"

Another silence. Longer. And then, so quietly the microphone nearly lost it:

"About how many verses there are."

The tape ran on into hiss. Ansel let it run. He was aware of his own heartbeat, of the dust turning in its slow orbits, of the rocking horse at the far end of the attic which—he told himself it was the settling of old wood—was no longer at the top of its arc.

There was one more box in the trunk. Unlabeled. He'd saved it for last without deciding to, the way you save the worst room in a house tour.

Inside was a single reel, and wrapped around it, a note in her handwriting, dated thirty years ago:

*Aurel asked me today what's behind the cellar wall. I told him nothing. He smiled at me the way his grandfather used to smile, and I understood that I am not raising two boys. I am raising one boy and one key, and I no longer know which is which.*

*If you're hearing this, one of them finished it.*

Ansel sat very still.

Below him, through the floorboards, through the floors and floors of the house, faint but unmistakable, rising up the stairwell like heat: humming.

Someone was singing the lullaby downstairs. The first verse. The second. Stopping.

And then—not stopping.
