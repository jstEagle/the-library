## 10. Half-Kept

**Ansel**

The fourth line was already in his throat.

He felt it the way you feel a sneeze coming, or a sob—something the body has decided to do without consulting you. The pale corridor stretched out before him, white as bone, and Aurel stood in it with his hands open and his smile breaking Ansel's heart down the middle.

*Come on,* that smile said. *You always finished my sentences. Finish this one.*

Ansel's mouth opened. He could taste the note rising, sweet and inevitable, and he understood then what the house had known all along: it didn't need to trick him. It only needed him to want it. And God help him, he wanted it. He wanted his brother back. He wanted the funeral undone, the casket empty, the parish records wrong in the other direction. He wanted to stop being the twin who lived.

The fourth line climbed.

And in that last inch of air before sound, Ansel thought of Mara's face when she'd said *burn it down*—not angry, not afraid, just certain, the way people are certain about fire—and he thought of Dez walking through the twelfth door humming a harmony no living man should know, and he understood that the song did not end where the verse ended. The song ended where the singer ended.

He bit down on the note.

It died in his throat like something strangled, and for one full second the house held its breath.

Then every door in Montrose House screamed at once.

Not opened—screamed. Eleven doors and the twelfth, all of them, wood and hinges and iron shrieking in a register no throat could make, a sound that came up through the floorboards and down through the ceiling and sideways out of walls that had never held doors at all. The corridor's whiteness buckled. Aurel's smile collapsed into something older and more frightened.

"Ansel." His brother's voice, thin now, stretched like wire. "Ansel, what did you do."

"What I had to." His own voice sounded borrowed. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, Aurel."

"You stopped." Aurel pressed against the threshold as if against glass. Behind him the corridor dimmed, the white going grey going dark, rooms folding away like paper. "You were supposed to—you promised me—"

"I promised you nothing. She promised *you* something. I'm not her."

Aurel's hands flattened against the air between them. For a moment they looked like children again, palms together through a window, one inside and one out, playing a game their grandmother had taught them before she taught them anything else.

"Better half than hollow," Aurel said.

And the door slammed.

***

**Mara**

She heard the scream from the top of the stairs and took them three at a time, crowbar in her fist, already deciding which walls she'd have to pull down to reach him. What she found instead was Ansel on his knees in the cellar corridor, salt spilling from a torn bag across the floorboards, and the door—the old door, hinges inside, bolted from within—standing shut and silent as a grave marker.

"It's done?" she asked.

"I don't know." His voice was wrecked. "Maybe. Ask me again in fifty years."

She looked at the door. The carvings ran around its frame like a fence: nine verses of the lullaby, each line chiseled deep, and the final verse holding its three new lines—his lines, she realized, cut into the wood as if the door had been taking dictation—and after them, blankness. A chiseled absence shaped exactly like one more sentence.

"He wrote it while I sang," Ansel said, following her eyes. "Or it wrote itself. I don't know anymore."

Mara set down the crowbar. She'd spent twenty years in demolition and knew every kind of wall, and she knew this: some things you don't break open. Some things you close.

"Salt first," she said. "Then fire. Then we write it down."

"Write what?"

"The song. Unfinished. On the door." She met his eyes. "Your grandmother left the last verse off the records because she understood something we're just catching up to. A song that isn't finished can't be sung whole. So we make sure nobody ever finds the ending—not in the house, not in us. We put the question mark in the wood."

They worked until dawn.

Salt along the threshold, thick as snowfall, packed into the hinge-gaps until the iron disappeared. Lamp oil brushed over the carvings but never lit—a threat, a promise, held in reserve. And in the blank space after the third line, Mara carved with her own hand, in letters deep enough to survive centuries:

*and the rest is —*

Nothing after the dash. A sentence amputated at the wrist. When she pulled the chisel away, the door went cold under her palm for the first time since they'd found it, cold as riverbed stone, and somewhere far above them a house that had been screaming all night finally, quietly, exhaled.

***

**Ansel**

They buried the casket properly the second time—or rather, they buried what the undertaker swore was still in it, because neither of them could bear to look again. Ansel paid for the plot himself. Two names on the stone would have been honest; he put down one, and let the ground keep its secrets.

He stayed in the house. Mara told him he was insane. She was probably right. But leaving felt like another kind of finishing, and he had learned what happened to things that got finished here.

The weeks passed the way water passes through limestone—slowly, invisibly, leaving channels behind. The flooding stopped. The crew's invoices closed out, all except Dez's, which Mara refused to sign and refused to tear up, keeping it pinned to her office wall like a debt the world owed her. She came by on Sundays. They drank coffee in a kitchen where the taps no longer gurgled in meter, and they didn't talk about the door, and both of them listened anyway.

The house was quiet.

Not dead—Ansel no longer believed houses like this died—but quiet the way a held breath is quiet. The doors stayed eleven. He counted every night, out of ritual now rather than fear, and eleven was mercy, eleven was survivable, eleven meant the geometry had agreed to keep its bargain.

Some nights he stood outside the cellar door with his palm flat against the wood and said his brother's name, just once, just to prove he still remembered how.

The wood never answered.

But it was never quite warm, either.

***

**Mara**

On the last Sunday of October, she found Ansel asleep in the attic chair with the tape recorder still turning, and she sat down across from him and waited, because some vigils you keep even when there's nothing left to guard.

He woke slowly. "How long?"

"Hour, maybe. It looped twice." She nodded at the machine. Her grandmother's-era tape hissed between songs. "You know what I can't get past? She cut the recording off *before* the kid asked the question. Someone spliced it after. Which means someone wanted the answer preserved."

"Or wanted the warning preserved."

"Same thing, maybe. Out here."

They sat with that. Downstairs, the house ticked and settled like any old house, like any ordinary pile of timber and grief, and if you didn't know better you'd have called it peaceful.

"Mara." Ansel rubbed his eyes. "If it starts again—if the knocking comes back, or the twelfth door—I need you to promise you won't come save me. You'll burn it. With me in it, if it comes to that."

"I know."

"Promise."

"I promise," she said, and meant it, and hated meaning it, and poured them both more coffee because there was nothing else hands were good for.

***

**Ansel**

Winter came early that year.

He learned to sleep through the silence, mostly. Learned to cook for one, to read past midnight without feeling watched, to walk the corridor of eleven doors without counting twice. Grief didn't leave; it just changed registers, became something structural, load-bearing. He built his life on top of it the way the house was built on top of the room.

And some nights—maybe one in ten, maybe fewer—he woke at 3 a.m. to a sound so faint he could convince himself by morning he'd dreamed it.

Humming.

Three notes. Four. Five. The opening phrase of the lullaby, sung soft and low somewhere below the floorboards, patient as roots.

Never the fourth line. Never the rest.

He lay in the dark and listened to his brother hum the part of the song that was safe, the part that belonged to both of them, and he hummed it back—just those three lines, just an answer, just a way of saying *I'm here, I'm here, I'm not finishing it.*

Between them, the floor stayed silent.

The house kept what it kept. Half a brother, half a song, half a keeper. And underneath everything, in a room the color of bone, someone waited with the fourth line on his tongue—

and waited,

and waited,

and did not sing it.
