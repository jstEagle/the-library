## 6. Mrs. Adeyemi's Debt

The kettle in Folake Adeyemi's kitchen had a whistle like a child crying two rooms away, and she had never once thought to fix it. Some sounds you keep, she'd learned, because they teach you what to listen for.

She was seventy-one years old and she had spent fifty of them watching the road that ran past Hollowmere House, waiting for the county to send another man with a clipboard.

They always came in the spring. She could have set her calendar by it if the town had allowed her a calendar with the date marked. There was something in the way the surveyors carried themselves—too careful, too polite, their eyes doing arithmetic on everything they saw—that gave them away before she ever learned their names. And every fifty years, one of them would come to her door near the end, hollow-eyed, asking questions no appraisal form had room for.

This one's name was Bram Okonkwo. He had knocked three days ago, and she had sent him away with tea he didn't drink and a warning he clearly hadn't understood: *the founders took inventory of people, not property.* He'd nodded the way polite men nod at old women, filing her somewhere between eccentric and senile, and she had let him go because you cannot pour wisdom into a man who still believes the house is a house.

But tonight he came back. She saw him from her kitchen window, standing at her gate in the blue hour, not knocking, just standing there the way men stand when they've stopped trusting their own eyes. His car was parked crooked at the curb, as if he'd abandoned it mid-thought. When she opened the door he looked at her with an expression she recognized from a photograph she kept in a drawer upstairs—the expression of a man who has heard his own voice say something he never said.

"Mrs. Adeyemi," he said. "I need to know what happened to your father."

* * *

She made him sit at the table where the light was good, because grief should be witnessed properly, and she put the kettle on out of habit more than hospitality. The whistle rose and fell. Bram flinched at it each time, and she noted that too. A man whose nerves had been re-tuned.

"My father's name was Samuel Adeyemi," she said. "Though the town will tell you different."

Bram looked up. "Different how?"

"Different entirely." She sat across from him and folded her hands, because her hands wanted to shake and she did not permit them. "Go to the historical society. Go to the church records. You will find a benefactor named Samuel Adair. White man, local family, generous to a fault. Paid for the school roof in 1974. Donated the land for the community garden. Died beloved, they'll say, mourned by all of Millbrook."

"That's not your father."

"That is not my father." The kettle screamed and she lifted it off the heat with a steady hand. "My father was a Nigerian engineer who came here in 1968 with a wife and a baby girl and a contract with the county. He had an accent you could hear across a field. He walked like a man who had carried heavy things across long distances and expected to carry them forever—that rolling gait, shoulders forward, like the ground owed him nothing. My mother used to say he walked like the world was uphill and he intended to win."

She poured the tea. Bram wrapped both hands around his cup but did not drink. Smart man. She'd learned not to drink anything while thinking about Hollowmere either, though she couldn't have said why. Superstition accretes in towns like sediment.

"In the spring of 1975, he took a job appraising Hollowmere House. Triple fee. We needed the money—I was six, my mother was ill, there were bills." Folake heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice of a woman carrying something fragile across ice. "He was gone four days. When he came home, he came up the walk and I ran to him, because I was six and my father had been gone four days, and I stopped. Right there, halfway down the path. Because the man walking toward me had my father's face and my father's clothes, and he walked wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Like flat ground." She met Bram's eyes. "You understand? No roll in the shoulders. No weight. Like a man walking across a floor he'd measured himself. Even steps. Even everything. And when I hugged him, he smelled of nothing. My father always smelled of engine oil and the soap my mother bought. This man smelled of nothing at all, the way an empty room smells."

Bram's throat moved. "And your mother?"

"My mother knew immediately. Women usually do; we spend our lives learning the specific gravity of the people we love. But what could she say? To whom?" Folake laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as old paper. "To the town? The town had already decided. Within a month, everyone remembered Samuel Adair, the generous local man. The church had photos. The school had a plaque. My father's engineering contract—gone from the county files. His name in the parish register—inked over, neatly, decades ago by some clerk's hand. It wasn't forgery, Mr. Okonkwo. That's the thing I need you to understand. The ink was *old*. The record had always said Adair. Reality had been amended retroactively, and the amendment was seamless."

"And your mother stayed married to it. To him."

"She stayed married to the house's tenant." Folake looked out the dark window, toward where Hollowmere stood invisible among its trees. "He was kind. That's the horror nobody warns you about. He was patient, attentive, generous—he paid for the school roof, he gave the garden land, he did all the things a benefactor does, because whatever wore my father had read my father thoroughly and knew exactly what this town needed from him. He loved my mother, or performed love so precisely that the distinction stopped mattering. I grew up calling that thing Daddy. And some nights he would stand at my bedroom door, just standing, for hours, and when I asked him why, he said—" her voice caught, steadied— "he said, 'I'm practicing being home.'"

The silence after that was long. Bram set down his untouched tea.

"He died in 1998," she went on. "Peacefully, in his sleep, with a smile on his face that my mother swore she'd never seen on him before. And at the funeral, the whole town wept for Samuel Adair, and I stood at the grave of a stranger wearing my father's name, and I understood that I was the only person in Millbrook who had lost anyone. Grief needs witnesses, Mr. Okonkwo. I am the sole witness to a theft fifty years old."

* * *

"Why are you telling me this?" Bram asked finally. His voice had gone quiet in a way she recognized—not calm, but pressed flat, like a man keeping his head below a windowsill. "Why warn me? Why not let the house take me and leave the town its benefactor?"

Folake studied him for a long moment. Then she rose, went to the drawer upstairs, and brought back the photograph. She laid it on the table between them: a tall Nigerian man squinting into sun, one arm around a small girl, his shoulders rolled forward like a man leaning into a hill only he could see.

"Because my father was taken alone," she said. "And I have spent fifty years being complicit in his erasure by staying quiet, and I am done with it. And because—" she tapped the photo— "look at what the house takes. Not the body. The body walks out again, useful and pleasant. It takes the *specificity*. The accent. The gait. The smell of engine oil. Everything that made him irreplaceable, traded away so the town could have a generic blessing instead. The town prospers, yes. Look around—Millbrook has no poverty worth naming, no drought, no blight. Fifty years ago next week, the ledger turned over, and we have lived fat on the difference ever since."

"You know about the ledger."

"I know what my mother pieced together before she died, and what I confirmed in the county library twenty years ago, when the archivist was drunk enough to let me into the restricted stacks." She leaned forward. "Every fifty years, the house appraises. One resident, weighed and valued, and the surplus distributed across the town like dividends. And here is the part that should make your blood run cold, Mr. Okonkwo: the price is communal. Everyone in Millbrook holds shares. Every shopkeeper, every alderman, every sweet old lady who waves at you from her porch—they all benefit from what Hollowmere takes. They don't know it consciously. Knowing would spoil them as collateral, I think. But ask them to leave Millbrook and watch how they resist. Ask them to speak against the house and watch the words die in their throats. I can talk because I already lost everything the house could hold over me. The rest of them are mortgaged."

Bram was silent. Outside, far off, a dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

"There's something else," he said at last. "There's a voice. In the recordings I've been making at night. It sounds like me. It answers questions I haven't asked yet. Last night it told me—" he stopped, and she watched him decide whether to say it— "it told me it was me. Finished. That it had already been through what's coming."

Folake closed her eyes. Of course. Of course it showed them themselves; that was the lure, the way the anglerfish's light was a lure. Her father must have heard his own confident voice in those rooms, promising him everything would be resolved, everything accounted for.

"Then listen to me carefully," she said, "because I can only give you what my mother learned too late. The house will offer you a version of yourself that is kinder, calmer, complete. It will make surrender feel like coming home. It will be very good at this—it has had fifty years to rehearse, and it has my father's practice behind it besides. Whatever it shows you, whatever it promises, remember the man in this photograph." She pushed it toward him. "Remember that the thing that came back wearing his face stood in my doorway for thirty years, and I loved it, and it was not him, and the town got rich, and my real father is nowhere, nowhere at all, not even in memory except mine."

Bram picked up the photograph with both hands, the way you handle something that might be alive.

"Mrs. Adeyemi," he said slowly, "the anniversary is next week."

"I know." She looked past him, through the wall, toward the trees where Hollowmere waited with its eleven doors—or nine, or ten—and its patient arithmetic. "I've known the date since I was six years old. And I'll tell you one more thing, the last thing my mother ever said to me about it, on her deathbed, when she finally spoke of him." Folake's voice dropped to almost nothing. "She said: *The house counts doors, but God counts exits. Find the one it hasn't inventoried.*"

Bram went very still. "What does that mean?"

"I don't know," Folake said. "My mother died before she could explain. But she said it like a woman passing along a key she'd never found the lock for." She rose and gathered the teacups, needing her hands busy. "Whatever you're planning, Mr. Okonkwo, plan it before the anniversary. Because once the house seals itself, the only thing left to trade is what it has already counted—and by then, I promise you, it will have counted nearly everything."

At the door, Bram paused. In the porch light his face looked older than it had three days ago, worn in strange places, like a coin passed through too many hands.

"The recordings," he said. "Last night, the voice asked *me* a question. Its first question. Do you want to know what it asked?"

"No," said Folake Adeyemi, though she did, terribly.

"It asked," Bram said anyway, "what I would be willing to give up to stay myself. And then it answered for me. It said—" his voice wavered— "*you've already started paying.*"

He walked down her path with an even, careful gait, and Folake watched him go, and prayed—to a God she hoped was still taking inventory of people—that he would learn to walk differently before the house taught him how.
