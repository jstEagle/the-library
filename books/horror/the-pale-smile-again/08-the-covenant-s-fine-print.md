## 8. The Covenant's Fine Print

The county library closed at five, but Mrs. Adeyemi had a key, and she turned it in the lock at half past nine with the unhurried certainty of a woman who had decided that some things should not wait for morning.

"Local history room," she said. "Bottom of the stairs. The lights are on a timer. You'll have until they go out."

"How long is that?"

"Long enough." She handed him a flashlight anyway, its weight cold and honest in his palm. "And Bram—" She paused, and in the dim vestibule her face was all shadow except for her eyes, which held the particular grief of someone who has already watched this happen once. "Read to the end. Whatever you do, read to the end. My father stopped halfway through."

She did not say what happened to him because of it. She didn't need to.

* * *

The local history room smelled of dust and binding glue and the faint chemical sweetness of old microfilm. Shelves of ledgers stood against the walls like a jury. Bram found Hollowmere in the third one he opened—not by title, but by feel; the book was colder than the others, and when he set it on the reading table, the lamp above him flickered once, like a throat clearing.

The ledger was bound in gray leather gone soft as skin. The first pages were mundane: land grants, timber surveys, the founding families' names written in a copperplate so fine it looked engraved rather than written—Hollowmere, Vane, Okonkwo-adjacent names he didn't recognize, the whole town's ancestry laid out in ink. He read quickly, impatiently, a surveyor skimming for load-bearing walls.

Then he reached the first entry dated fifty years after the founding, and his hands went still.

*Appraised: Elias Vane. Assessed value: considerable. Difference distributed per covenant. Town prospered.*

There was no explanation of what *difference* meant. There didn't need to be. Bram had spent two weeks inside a house that gained a foot whenever he blinked, and he understood, with the sick clarity of a man reading his own autopsy, that the difference was a person. That the town's prosperity was measured out of someone, the way you might measure flour from a sack, and that the sack did not survive the baking.

He turned the pages. Every fifty years, the same entry, in the same hand or hands so similar it made no difference. Names he half-knew from the cemetery on the hill. *Assessed value: moderate. Difference sufficient.* Once, chillingly: *Assessed value: insufficient. Covenant deferred. Hard winter followed.*

The house had appraised them all, and the town had eaten the proceeds.

And then the last entry. Dated fifty years ago—next week.

*Appraised: Solomon Adeyemi. Assessed value: exceptional. Ritual completed. House sustained. Town prospered. Note: resident retained. Resident integrated.*

Bram sat back. The timer light buzzed overhead. *Resident retained. Resident integrated.* Not killed. Worse than killed—kept, folded into the house like a letter into an envelope, his value extracted and his remainder left walking around wearing his coat and his gait and his name, which was exactly what Mrs. Adeyemi remembered: a father who came home moving like someone else.

His own name was not in the ledger yet. But there were blank ruled lines waiting, and the next date was circled in pencil, faint as a breath.

* * *

The final pages were different. The paper changed—older stock, heavier, the kind that takes ink slowly and gives it up never. The handwriting changed too, loosening from copperplate into something more hurried, more human, as if the last founder had written these pages knowing no one would read them for fifty years and hoping, perhaps, that someone would.

Bram read by flashlight when the timer light died, which it did at eleven sharp, plunging the room into a darkness that felt attentive rather than empty.

*The covenant admits of two completions,* the founder had written. *This we did not understand at first, and it cost us Elias.*

*A covenant is a sentence spoken by two parties. The house speaks its half—the appraisal, the taking. The resident must speak the other half, and the resident's half can be spoken forward or backward.*

*Spoken forward, the ritual completes as designed. The resident consents—consent being nothing more than staying past the appointed hour, sleeping under the roof, answering the questions the house asks in the dark. The house takes the whole of him: memory, manner, voice, the particular way he holds a cup. What remains walks out and lives a long life in the town's warm gratitude, hollowed, a benefactor-shaped absence. This is what happened to Solomon Adeyemi. This is what will happen to whoever sleeps there on the anniversary.*

*But a sentence can be unsaid.*

Bram's flashlight trembled slightly. He steadied it against the table, and the wood was cold enough to feel like company.

*Spoken backward, the ritual unbinds. The house releases the resident, and the covenant fails, and the town loses what the town has been spending for a hundred and fifty years without once asking where it came from. But reversal is not free. Nothing in this house is free. The house has already begun to appraise him—it has been appraising since the moment he measured his first wall. It holds his value already, the way a bank holds a deposit. To walk out unbound, he must surrender what the house has appraised.*

*Not himself. Not his memories entire. Only this: every memory he holds of the house itself.*

Bram read the line three times. His breath fogged in the unheated room, though it was summer.

*The rooms he measured. The doors that counted wrong. The voice in the night recordings—every word of it, every question it asked, every answer it stole. The kitchen it built for him. The hallway from his dream. All of it, surrendered. He will wake on the porch with a week missing from his mind, smooth-edged, like a tooth pulled clean. He will not know why the town looks at him strangely. He will not know what he paid. And the house, unbound, will forget itself, and the town will forget its prosperity, gradually, painlessly, the way a dream fades—and they will forget him too, because he was part of the transaction, and forgotten transactions leave no creditors.*

*This is the mercy and the cruelty of it. Completion leaves a man alive and empty. Reversal leaves a man whole and ignorant. I could not choose for Elias. I write this so that whoever comes next may choose for himself, with open eyes, before the house chooses for him.*

*One caution. The unbinding must be spoken aloud, in the house, on the appointed night, in the resident's own voice—and the second voice will fight him for it. It is not a ghost. Understand this. It is not the founder, not the house, not any dead thing. It is him. It is the man he would be if he stayed—the finished version, the integrated version, the version the house has been drafting since he first walked the rooms with his tape measure. It wants completion because completion is its birth. If he falters, if he hesitates even a syllable, it will speak the ritual forward in his timbre, and the house will accept it, because the house cannot tell the difference between him and the thing wearing his voice.*

*It can tell the difference. He must simply mean it more.*

The last lines were cramped, pressed hard into the page:

*I have watched the house for fifty years from the outside, an old man now, keeping this ledger. Some nights I think I should burn it. But the covenant renews whether or not anyone reads these words, and I find I would rather the next man choose badly with open eyes than sleepwalk into the envelope.*

*Whoever you are: the house knows your childhood. It has been furnishing itself with you. Do not mistake being known for being loved.*

*—A. Vane*

* * *

Bram sat in the dark for a long time after the last word, the flashlight cooling in his grip, the ledger breathing its old-paper smell into the silence.

Outside the window, across the square, Hollowmere's silhouette stood against the stars, and he could have sworn—one window, third floor, the room that hadn't existed on his floor plan—was lit.

They want everything, he thought. The house wants the whole of me, and the voice wants to be born, and the town wants its prosperity, and nobody—not one soul in this valley—wants me to walk away whole except me.

And the price of whole was a blank week. A pulled tooth. He would forget the doors that counted wrong, the voice that wore him like a suit, Mrs. Adeyemi's grief, the ledger, the fear, the strange terrible intimacy of being known completely by something hungry. He would forget the only week in his life he had ever mattered to anything.

He found, examining himself honestly, that part of him wanted to keep it. Even the horror. Especially the horror. It was his.

That, he realized, was how the house won. It let you love the trap because the trap was made of you.

Bram closed the ledger, and in the dark his own voice answered him from very close—too close, close enough that he felt breath on his ear, though the room was empty and the door still locked:

"You've read to the end," it said gently, in his timbre, with his mother's cadence. "Now you know what it costs. Sleep on it. We have six days."

"And then?" Bram asked the darkness, hating how steady he made his voice sound.

"And then," said the second voice, warm as a hand on his shoulder, "we settle accounts."
