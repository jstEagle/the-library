## 6. What the Dead Want

The board lit up at 11:47 p.m., four minutes before I'd have called it a slow night.

I'd been doing this long enough to read a phone line before I answered it. Line two had a quality I'd learned to dread — not the dead-air hiss of a prankster working up nerve, but that thick, underwater stillness, like someone breathing through wet cloth. Twenty years of Hollow Hour nights had taught me every texture of silence. This one had teeth.

"Xanthe Amberdale," I said, because saying my own name was easier than saying *who's calling*, and because the ritual of it — the little liturgy of the show — was the last comfortable thing left in my life. "You're on The Hollow Hour."

The voice that came back was not one voice.

That's the only way I can describe it, and I've spent weeks trying to find a better way and failing. It sounded like a choir recorded in a stairwell, seven or eight voices stacked so precisely they occupied the same instant, the same pitch, the same breath. When they said my name it didn't echo. It *doubled*.

*Xanthe Amberdale.*

I looked at the caller ID. It read all sevens. Not 777-7777 — the full number, every digit, area code and all. My hand went to the fader before my brain caught up and told it to stop.

"You're going to want to leave the line open," Marisol said from the doorway of the booth.

She was holding two mugs of the terrible station coffee, and she set them down very slowly, the way you move when you don't trust your own hands. She'd been staying late all week. Neither of us had named why. We were two people standing in a burning house agreeing not to mention the smoke.

"Listeners," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved, "we've got an unusual caller tonight."

*We know what we are,* the chorus said. *Do you?*

I should have cut the mic. Any sane man would have cut the mic. But twenty years of explaining away the dead had built something in me like a scar over a nerve — touch it and I flinched, but I couldn't stop touching it. And underneath the scar, in the place where Xanthe Amberdale kept the truth he never said out loud, I already knew who was calling.

"Who am I talking to?" I asked.

*Everyone,* they said. *No one. The ones under the square. The ones in the water. The ones you apologized for.* A pause with weight in it, the kind of pause a preacher leaves so the congregation can feel the sentence land. *You have been taking our calls for twenty years, keeper's son. You have been very good at not hearing us. Tonight we need you to hear.*

The ON AIR sign buzzed faintly overhead. Somewhere out in the dark, I knew, there were insomniacs and truckers and grieving widows with their dials tuned to 1340 AM, hearing whatever came out of this booth. I glanced at the modulation meter. It barely twitched when the voices spoke. As if the sound wasn't traveling through the air at all — as if it was arriving some other way, directly, like a thought pressed against glass.

"What do you want?" I said.

*What we have always wanted. What your family promised.*

"My family—" I started, and stopped, because my mother's attic was sitting in the back of my mind like a stone I'd swallowed. *The door must never be opened from either side.* I hadn't told anyone about the ledger. Not even Marisol. Especially not Marisol.

*Listen carefully,* the voices said, and for the first time they separated slightly — I could hear individual threads in the weave now, a man, a woman, a child, the drowned boy from Tuesday night, the woman who'd wanted her plants watered. *There are seven. Blood of the founders, scattered in the town like seed. Seven names, seven lines, seven debts. Gather them. Before the equinox — nine nights from now — gather them before the door and speak the Rite of Sealing as it was written. Speak it whole. Speak it true. If you do, the seal holds another hundred years, and we stay where we are, and your town keeps its sleep.*

"And if we don't?"

The pause this time was longer. When the voices came back they had dropped the choir effect, and what remained was almost gentle, which was infinitely worse.

*Then we finish opening it ourselves. And what comes through will not be us.*

Marisol made a small sound. I looked over. She was standing rigid by the coffee mugs, both hands flat on the desk, staring at the monitor like it had spoken to her personally.

"You hear them," I said. It wasn't a question. Her face answered anyway.

"Every word," she whispered. "Xanthe, the meter isn't moving. They're not coming through the phone. They're coming through *you*." She swallowed. "And maybe through me. God help me, I hope it's just through me being near you."

*Seven names,* the voices repeated, patient as tide. *Would you have them, keeper's son?*

My mouth was dry enough to crack. Every instinct I'd polished over two decades — the instinct that had let me smile through calls from the drowned and call it grief, call it hoax, call it static — screamed at me to say no. To keep the burden exactly where it had always been: mine alone, deniable, manageable. That was the family trade, after all. The ledger in my mother's attic hadn't just been a record. It had been a job description. *Amberdales keep the explanations.* We were the town's official liars, appointed in 1893, salaried in silence.

But nine nights. And a door sealed from the inside, rusted shut, that a crew with diamond saws couldn't mark.

"Give me the names," I said.

* * *

They gave them slowly, one at a time, and I wrote them down on the pad I used for listener callbacks, my handwriting degrading with each line until the last name looked like it had been written during an earthquake.

*Ruth Calloway. Descendant of Ezekiel Marsh, first sheriff.* I knew the name — Ruth Calloway, retired, lived out past the reservoir, still carried herself like she could arrest you with a look.

*Denny Okafor. Descendant of Silas Marsh, undertaker.* The funeral home on Vell Street. I'd broadcast his father's commercials for years.

*Priya Nandakumar. Descendant of Abigail Crane, schoolmistress.* Sixteen years old, if I remembered right. My stomach turned over.

*Harlan Voss. Descendant of Thomas Voss, quarrymaster.*

*Edie Lark. Descendant of Mercy Lark, midwife.*

*Theo Brandt. Descendant of—* and here the voices did something strange, a hitch, a stutter in the chorus, like a record skipping over a groove, *—descendant of the seventh founder. The one whose name was removed.*

"There were six founders," I said. "Every history book in town says six. The plaque on the square says six."

*Your books were written by keepers,* they said. *Ask yourself why there is room for seven chairs in the photograph, and only six men standing.*

I thought of the photo in the Historical Society's case — I'd walked past it a thousand times without looking. Seven chairs. I'd have sworn on my mother's grave there were six. The voices waited while that settled into me, cold and heavy, like silt finding bottom.

*And the seventh,* they said, *is you.*

The pen stopped moving.

"No," I said. "No, the Amberdales weren't founders. The Amberdales were — we were the help. The ritual-keepers. My mother's ledger—"

*Was half a book,* the voices said. *As your name is half a name. Ask what the family did before they kept the door. Ask what they did to make it necessary.* A breath, or the memory of one. *Amberdale. Am-bear-dale. Say it slowly, keeper. Hear what your ancestors hid inside it.*

I didn't say it. I sat in my chair with the pad in front of me and seven names on it and the eighth implied, and outside the booth window the parking lot lights hummed their sodium hum, and everything ordinary continued being ordinary, which suddenly felt like the most obscene thing in the world.

"Why tell us?" Marisol asked. She'd come around the desk and stood beside me, close enough that her sleeve touched mine. She spoke toward the microphone, toward the phone line, toward whatever was listening. "Twenty years he's taken your calls and done nothing. Why now? Why give him the rite?"

*Because the digging stopped,* the voices said. *Because the door heard shovels for the first time in a hundred years, and it woke, and waking, it began to pull. What waits behind it does not want the seal renewed. It wants the door open, and it has begun to spend its strength on that alone. It sent a whisper to a lonely boy once. It will send more. It is patient, and it lies beautifully.* Another pause. *We are dead. We cannot gather descendants. We cannot hold a page steady or speak a word aloud in the world of breath. Only the living can renew the seal. So we ask the living.*

"And if we refuse?" I asked. "If we decide you're a hoax, a mass hallucination, a very elaborate prank — if we do what I've done for twenty years and explain it away?"

The chorus laughed. There's no other word for it. Seven throats laughing in perfect unison, soft and terrible, like wind through a keyhole.

*Then on the equinox, host,* they said, *you may explain away whatever walks out of the square. You have always been good at explanations. See how far they carry.*

Then the line clicked, and the caller ID cleared, and the modulation meter sat flat and dead as a coin on a corpse's eye.

* * *

For a long moment neither of us moved. The studio clock ticked. The coffee went cold in its mugs. Somewhere in the building the overnight HVAC cycled on with its familiar asthmatic wheeze, bless it, the most beautiful mundane sound I'd ever heard.

"We're live," Marisol finally said. "We're still live. You never took us off the air."

"I know."

"Xanthe. If they weren't coming through the phone — if the listeners heard nothing but static — then for the last ten minutes this station has been broadcasting a man talking to himself." She pulled out her phone, thumbed through something, and held it up. The station's stream chat, which on a good night might hold eleven insomniacs. It was scrolling fast.

*anyone else hearing static*
*static here*
*is the show ok? sounds like a modem dying*
*xanthe you there?*

"They heard nothing," she said quietly. "Static. For ten minutes. And then—" she scrolled, and her thumb hesitated, "—then this. One message. Posted ninety seconds ago."

I leaned in. The message was from a user named **doorkeeper7**, account created that night, no avatar, no history. It read:

**he knows the names. does he know his own?**

The chat refreshed. The account was gone.

Marisol set the phone down face-up between us, like evidence at a trial. Then she did something I'll remember for the rest of my life, however much of it I have left. She picked up a marker, pulled the whiteboard we used for sponsor rotations off the wall, wiped *HOLLOWMERE HARDWARE — REMEMBER US FOR MULCH* off it with her sleeve, and wrote the seven names across the top in her big, slanted, unhesitating hand.

Ruth Calloway. Denny Okafor. Priya Nandakumar. Harlan Voss. Edie Lark. Theo Brandt.

She capped the marker. Looked at the space after Theo Brandt. Looked at me.

"You're not writing my name," I said.

"No," she agreed. "I'm waiting for you to say it out loud. Because I've known you twenty years, Xanthe Amberdale, and I have watched you take calls from the dead and call it anything but that, and I have loved you through every single lie, and I am telling you right now — the lying part of this ends tonight. Whatever your family was. Whatever you are. Say it, or I start calling these seven people myself and telling them a radio host who won't tell the truth needs their help."

The clock said 12:31. Outside, Hollowmere slept its ordinary sleep, and under the square, behind iron, something counted down nine nights with the patience of the buried.

"The Amberdales weren't the help," I said. My voice cracked on the second word and I let it. "I think we were the reason there was a door at all."

Marisol nodded once, picked the marker back up, and wrote an eighth name on the board.

Then she reached for the phone directory.
