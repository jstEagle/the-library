## 10. Marisol's Page

Marisol left her bag on the studio couch again.

She'd been doing it all week — drifting out of the building with her coat half-on and her bag abandoned like an afterthought, like a woman who'd stopped trusting her own pockets. Xanthe noticed because he noticed everything about Marisol: the way she'd started locking the research drawer in the basement, the way she'd gone quiet during the rehearsal debrief when Theo asked how she'd reconstructed the rite so fast, the way she flinched — actually flinched — whenever anyone said the word *Amberdale*.

He hadn't said anything. Twenty years of hosting a show for the dead had taught him the value of letting people keep their silences until they were ready to break them.

But tonight the station was empty, the ON AIR sign dark for the first time in weeks, and the bag sat there on the cracked leather couch like it had been left for him on purpose. Like some part of Marisol wanted him to find what was inside.

He told himself he wasn't going to look.

He looked.

* * *

The bag smelled of coffee and old paper. He went through it gently at first — keys, a granola bar crushed to powder, a paperback with a receipt for a bookmark — telling himself this was nothing, that he'd close it up and feel foolish and never mention it. Then his fingers found the manila folder wedged against the lining, the one with HOLLOWMERE HISTORICAL SOCIETY stamped across the corner in faded blue ink, and his hands stopped being gentle without asking his permission.

Photographs first. The founders, 1893, standing before the iron door in its original setting — not buried under two centuries of square and sidewalk but set into a hillside, framed by raw-cut stone, the grass around it brown as if something under the earth had drunk it dry. Seven figures. He knew their faces now; he'd spent three nights studying them after the rehearsal, matching descendants to ancestors like a man doing genealogy with a gun to his head. Sheriff Ruth Calloway's great-grandmother stood at the center, jaw set like her granddaughter's. The funeral director's forebear held a lantern. And at the edge of the frame, slightly apart from the others, a tall man with hollow cheeks and his hand resting on the shoulder of —

Xanthe sat down.

The child beside the tall man couldn't have been more than eight. White dress. Dark hair braided down the back. And the face — God, the face — was the Amberdale face, the one Xanthe shaved around every morning, the one his mother had worn, the one staring out of the oval frame in the attic where the ledger lived. It was like looking at a photograph of himself run through some terrible age-reversing machine.

He turned the photograph over. Someone had written on the back in pencil, in a hand that shook:

*Elias Amberdale and his daughter Constance, the night before.*

*The night before what?*

He knew. Of course he knew. He'd known since the attic, since the last line of the ledger — *the door must never be opened from either side* — since the moment he'd understood that his family hadn't been bystanders to Hollowmere's founding sin but its architects. But knowing the shape of a thing and seeing its face were different hungers, and the folder still had pages left.

The torn ledger page was the last thing in the folder. Torn along the left edge, ragged, the paper soft as cloth from handling. Marisol's handwriting was all over the margins — translations, cross-references, small desperate arrows connecting one phrase to another. She'd been working on this for weeks. Alone. In secret. In the same building where he slept three nights out of five.

He read it standing up, because sitting felt too much like surrender.

The page was Elias Amberdale's own account, and it did not begin with a door or a ritual or any of the language of protection that filled the rest of the ledger. It began with a debt. Something beneath Hollowmere — Elias never named it, only called it *the patient tenant*, and Xanthe's skin crawled at the word *patient* — had been here before the town, before anything, sleeping in the dark under the hill. The founders hadn't disturbed it. They'd *woken* it. Deliberately. Because it answered questions, and the questions it answered made seven families rich in a single winter. Land that yielded. Wells that never ran dry. Cattle that birthed twins. The founders had fed it small things at first — Xanthe read the list and had to put the page down twice — and then it had asked, in the voice of whatever person stood nearest the door, for something larger.

It asked for a child. Willingly given.

And Elias Amberdale — founder, patriarch, the man whose name Xanthe had carried like a coat his whole life — had walked his eight-year-old daughter to the door on a night in October 1893, and Constance had taken her father's hand, and she had gone inside willingly, because her father told her it was an honor, because she trusted him, because she was eight.

The seal came after. The seal was not protection. The seal was a lid on a pot, a gag, a grave marker laid flat so no one would ever dig. Everything in the other ledger — every prayer, every rite, every generation of Amberdales keeping watch — existed for one purpose: to make sure no one ever learned what the first Amberdale had done. To make sure the town stayed grateful instead of curious. The dead calling his show for twenty years weren't haunting Hollowmere.

They were testifying.

Xanthe became aware, distantly, that he was making a sound. A low continuous note, like feedback, like a microphone left open in an empty room. He pressed the page flat against his chest and breathed until the sound stopped.

Then he heard the studio door open behind him.

* * *

"I forgot my bag." Marisol's voice, casual, already retreating. "I'll grab it tomor—"

She saw him. She saw the folder open on the couch, the photographs fanned out, the torn page in his hands, and Xanthe watched twenty years of friendship reorganize itself in her face — watched her decide, in real time, whether to lie.

"Don't," he said.

"Xanthe."

"*Don't.*" His voice came out wrong, too loud in the dead studio, bouncing off the foam panels. "Don't tell me you were protecting me. Don't tell me you were waiting for the right time. I have heard every version of that sentence, Marisol. I've *been* every version of that sentence. I invented it."

She didn't move toward him or away. She stood in the doorway with her hand still on the handle, and her eyes went wet, and she said, quietly, "How long have you known?"

"Since the attic. Since before the attic, if I'm honest. Since the first call." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Twenty years of callers who were dead, and I explained them away. Grief. Hoaxes. Static. You know why? Because explaining is what Amberdales do. It's literally the family business. We don't keep the door shut, Marisol. We keep the *story* shut. We're not wardens. We're PR."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. A little girl wasn't fair. Constance Amberdale, eight years old, walked to that door by her own father and told it was an honor—" His voice cracked clean through and he let it. Let her hear it. "She went *willingly*. That's what the page says. Willingly. You know what that means? It means they needed her consent. It means the thing behind the door has rules, and our family found the loophole in them, and the loophole was a child who loved her father enough to believe him."

Marisol finally crossed the room. She reached for the page and he pulled it back, an ugly reflexive motion, and something in her expression broke.

"You hid this from me," he said. "Weeks. While we rehearsed the rite together. While Theo mispronounced that word and we laughed about it, we *laughed*, you had this in your bag ten feet away."

"I was trying to understand it first!"

"Why?"

"Because!" Her voice rose to meet his, and for a moment they were just two people shouting in a basement studio, and it was almost a relief. "Because I know what happens to you when you find out the truth about yourself, Xanthe. I watched it with the calls. Every time the show got stranger, you built a bigger explanation, and I let you, because I thought — I *hoped* — that if I could find the whole story first, all of it, every ugly piece, I could hand it to you in one piece instead of watching it tear you apart a scrap at a time." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furious at her own tears. "I wasn't hiding it from you. I was hiding it from *this*. From exactly this."

"And how did that work out?"

"It worked out that you found it in my bag like a thief, so — mixed results."

Despite everything — despite the page against his chest and the child's face burning through the photograph and the sound his own name made in Elias's handwriting — Xanthe felt something crack in him that wasn't anger. He sat down heavily on the arm of the couch.

"She has my face," he said.

Marisol came and stood in front of him. Close. Close enough that he could see she'd been crying longer than tonight — days, maybe weeks, alone with this page in the archive room while he broadcast to the dead.

"I know," she said.

"The first thing the door ever ate was an Amberdale. And now it wants another one. That's what the chorus means when it says my name. It's not calling me. It's calling *back*. It's collecting on a two-hundred-year-old invoice, and my family wrote the terms." He looked up at her. "Did you know? When you took the page — did you already suspect it ended with me?"

"I suspected it ended with your bloodline. That's not the same thing." She knelt so their eyes were level, the way she did when a caller was falling apart on air and she wanted him to stay calm. "Listen to me. Whatever the page says, whoever gave that girl to that door — that was Elias. Not you. You have spent twenty years talking to ghosts because you couldn't stand the idea of anyone being unheard, even the dead. Especially the dead. That's not a debt, Xanthe. That's the opposite of one."

"He used her trust."

"Yes."

"He told her it was an honor."

"Yes."

"And I keep thinking—" Xanthe's voice dropped to almost nothing. "I keep thinking about Theo. Orphaned. Adopted in. Always the outsider, always so grateful for every crumb of belonging. And a voice on his radio promising him he'd finally matter." He met her eyes. "What if we're Elias? What if everything we've built — the station, the group, the *family* — what if it's just a gentler version of walking someone to a door and calling it an honor?"

Marisol was quiet for a long moment. Outside, far off, faint as memory, the dusk hymn rose from the sleepwalking town — the same hymn, the same key, every night, right on schedule.

"Then we stop being Elias," she said. "Right now. Tonight. We take the page downstairs, we lay it next to the ledger, and we tell the others all of it. Ruth. Danny. Priya. All of them. No more secrets between us and the people we're asking to hold the line." She put her hand over his, over the page. "That's the difference. Elias did it in the dark and called it protection. We do it in the light and call it what it is."

Xanthe looked at her hand. At the ink-stained fingers that had rebuilt a rite from fragments, that had poured him coffee through twenty years of 2 a.m. broadcasts, that had, yes, hidden a truth from him for weeks because she loved him more than she feared him.

"I'm still furious at you," he said.

"I know."

"I'm going to be furious at you for a while."

"I've budgeted for it."

A breath shuddered out of him that might have been the beginning of a laugh. He unfolded the torn page carefully, smoothing it flat on his knee, and read the final lines aloud — the ones he'd stopped at, the ones his eyes had refused on the first pass. Below the account of Constance, below the sealing, Elias had written one more passage, cramped and hurried, as if written fast before courage failed:

*The tenant takes what is given freely, but it cannot refuse a gift returned. If the bloodline ever stands at the door and offers back what was taken — not a life for a life, but the debt itself, named and renounced — the bargain may be unwritten. I dare not try. I leave this to whichever of my line is braver than I was, and I am sorry, and I am sorry, and I am sorry.*

Xanthe read it twice. Three times.

"Marisol," he said slowly. "This isn't just a confession. This is instructions."

She leaned in, reading over his arm, and he felt her go still.

"'Offer back what was taken,'" she whispered. "Constance. He means Constance. The debt isn't a life — it's *her*. She was taken, and the seal was built to hide the taking, and if an Amberdale stands at the door and names it—"

The studio lights flickered.

Once. Twice. Then the board lit up, every line at once, all seven phone lines blinking red in the dark room, and from the speakers — unplugged speakers, speakers connected to nothing — came a sound neither of them had heard before. Not the chorus. Not the unison drone of instruction.

Laughter. Soft, delighted, patient.

And underneath it, in a voice like a child reading from a book she'd memorized, something said:

*"Hello, Uncle Xanthe. I've been waiting such a long time for someone brave."*
