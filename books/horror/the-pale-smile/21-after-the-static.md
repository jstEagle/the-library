## 21. After the Static

The town of Hollowmere remembered everything.

That was the part nobody had predicted — not the sheriff's reports, not the therapists who came up from Ashford with their clipboards and their gentle questions, not even the descendants themselves, who had stood in the square on equinox morning expecting to spend the rest of their lives lying to their neighbors. We had rehearsed our story in the station basement like a script: gas leak, mass hysteria, a prank gone too far, a door that was just a door. Ruth had drilled us on it over bad coffee until dawn. And then the sun came up and the town woke mid-hymn, every one of them, and nobody asked for a story at all.

They just knew. The way you know you've dreamed something. The way you know the shape of a room with your eyes closed.

Old Mrs. Calloway, who had spent three weeks setting her table for people dead forty years, told me at the diner that she remembered every meal she'd cooked for them, every wrong word, every hymn sung out of key — and that she'd rather carry it than have it sanded smooth by some explanation. "Explanations are how it got us in the first place," she said, stabbing her fork into a piece of pie like it owed her money. "Xanthe understood that. Twenty years of understanding it."

I hadn't known she listened to the show. Apparently half the town did, had for years, had heard the dead callers and made their own quiet bargains with disbelief. That was the thing about Hollowmere we'd never understood until it was almost too late: the whole town had been holding its breath for a century, and when the door sealed, they finally exhaled, and what came out was the truth, and they swallowed it whole.

They kept Xanthe's chair empty at the diner, too. Nobody asked them to. They just did.

* * *

I took over *The Hollow Hour* eleven days after the equinox, because the alternative was letting the frequency go dark, and I had learned — we had all learned — what lives in dark frequencies.

The first night I sat down at the mixer, my hands were shaking so badly I knocked over his coffee mug. It was still there. He'd left it half-full the night he walked to the square, the way he always did, convinced he'd come back for it. I washed it out. I put it back exactly where it had been. Then I put my own mug next to it, because I am not a sentimental person, whatever anyone tells you, and two mugs is just efficient use of desk space.

"Good evening, Hollowmere," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, and I let it crack, because Xanthe had taught me — without ever meaning to, without ever saying it — that the voice you broadcast should be the voice you actually have. "This is *The Hollow Hour*. I'm Marisol Vega. Tonight we're only taking calls from the living."

That last part became the show's signature. People found it funny at first, then comforting, then necessary. A rule. A border. A line drawn in the static that everyone in town understood without me having to explain it. Callers would open with it sometimes, before I could say anything: *"Still alive, Marisol."* Like checking in at a checkpoint. Like knocking twice on wood.

The calls were different now. Lighter, mostly. People requested songs for their neighbors. A kid called in to report that the civic center foundation had been poured over the old excavation site, and that the concrete crew had poured it in one continuous pour, no seams, no one ordering them to — just seven men working through the night, humming. I played him something with horns in it. The town deserved horns.

But some nights the calls were heavy, and those were the nights I earned my keep. Because the town remembered everything, and remembering everything means remembering at three in the morning, alone, with the ceiling doing that thing ceilings do.

A man named Denny Okafor — no relation to anyone, he always clarified, though no one had ever asked — called in week two to say he couldn't stop seeing his father's face among the pale smiles in the doorway. His father had died in '09. Denny had been at the square that night. He'd seen him offered to him like a gift.

"He looked happy," Denny said. "That's the part. He looked *happy*, Marisol. Am I supposed to live with that?"

"Yeah," I said. "You are. But not alone, and not all at once, and not tonight if tonight's too much. Hang up whenever you need to. The line'll be here tomorrow."

He stayed forty minutes. When he hung up, he sounded like a man who had set down something heavy and discovered his arms still worked.

Afterward I sat in the dead air for a long time, looking at Xanthe's chair. He would have handled that call differently. He would have been funnier, gentler, more oblique — twenty years of practice at talking around the unsayable. I was learning to talk straight through it. Different tool, same carpentry. I think he'd have approved. I think he'd have teased me about it first, which is its own kind of approval.

* * *

The studio changed in small ways. I moved nothing of his. The ON AIR sign still buzzed the way it always had, with that faint electrical complaint he used to pat the wall about. The board still had his handwriting on strips of tape under the faders — *phone hybrid*, *cart machine (don't touch)*, *the good reverb*. I touched the good reverb constantly. It felt like being trusted with something.

Ruth came by most Thursdays. She'd retired from the sheriff's department for real this time; she said she'd spent thirty years keeping order in a town whose founding document was a crime, and she wanted to see what peace felt like from the inside. She sat in the guest chair with her coffee and didn't say much, but she listened the way she'd done everything else in the crisis: completely, without flinching.

"You know what gets me," she said one Thursday, watching the VU needles breathe. "We won. Actually won. Door's shut, town's awake, nobody died. And I still catch myself waiting for the other shoe."

"It's not a shoe," I said. "It's a door. And it's shut from the inside this time. By choice." I turned a fader down a hair. "That's the difference. That's the whole difference. Somebody chose it."

She looked at me for a while. "You talk about him like he's on vacation."

"I talk about him like he's where he chose to be," I said. "Which is the only place I know how to talk about anybody."

She nodded slowly, and drank her coffee, and stayed until sign-off, which was her way of saying the things she didn't say.

Theo's name we didn't speak often, but we didn't avoid it either. That was the agreement, unspoken, reached somewhere in the weeks after: Theo belonged to the story, all of it, the betrayal and the tears and the question he'd asked in the square — *is there any version where I belong without betraying them?* — and the answer we'd given him with our actions, if never clearly enough with our words. There was. There had been. The version where he carried the key was also the version where he warned us, in the end, the only way he could: by walking slow enough for us to reach him. Ruth swore that was true. I choose to believe her. Belief is a thing you maintain, like a seal. Like a show.

There's a plaque now, in the little park they built beside the civic center. Seven names of the founders, and beneath them, smaller: *and for the ones who kept the door, and the ones who opened it, and the ones who closed it again.* Theo's name isn't on it. Neither is Xanthe's. The town decided the plaque should name the dead of 1893 only, because — Mrs. Calloway told me this, with real conviction — "those two aren't done yet."

I didn't ask what she meant. Some sentences you just carry.

* * *

Weeks became a month. The month became autumn proper, leaves coming down over the square like slow amber static. The show found its rhythms. I learned which callers needed music and which needed silence. I learned to read the pause before someone spoke the way Xanthe used to read the pause after — the living have their own hesitations, their own frequencies, and I was getting fluent in them.

And then, on a Tuesday in late October, the phone line lit up at 11:47 p.m., seventeen minutes before sign-off, and the caller ID read the station's own number.

That happens sometimes. Faulty routing, the engineer said once, back when we had an engineer. I stared at it for three full rings. My hand hovered over the fader. The ON AIR light buzzed its small complaint. Somewhere behind my ribs, my heartbeat picked up a rhythm I hadn't felt since a night under the town square, when knocking kept time with it through a pair of headphones.

Rules are rules. Living callers only.

But I answered it. God help me, I answered it, because some part of me had been leaving the porch light on for twenty years and knew exactly whose voice the dark sounded like.

"*The Hollow Hour,*" I said. "Go ahead, caller."

Static first. Soft, oceanic, the sound of a very large room very far away. Then:

"Hey, Sol."

Not his voice exactly. His voice the way a photograph is a face — accurate, complete, and impossible. Warmer than memory, which should have been my first warning, except warmth was also just... him. It was 11:48 on a Tuesday and the voice of my best friend said my nickname like he'd never stopped saying it daily, and every clever thing I had ever planned to say in this moment went out of me like air from a punctured tire.

"Water the plants," I managed. Because that was the first call, the call that started everything, four days after a death, a sister asking a sister for something small and ordinary and impossible. If this was a test, I was going to answer it in the show's own language.

A laugh. Static-wrapped, but a laugh, his laugh, the one that always arrived a half-second before he finished his own jokes because he couldn't wait for himself. "The plants are fine. Everything's fine here. That's — okay, that's not entirely true, but it's true enough for radio."

"Xanthe." His name came out steady. I was proud of that. I would be proud of it later, when my hands finally started shaking, which they did the moment I took them off the board. "Where are you calling from?"

"You know where."

"The number says the studio."

"The number says whatever it needs to say. You know how phones are." A pause, and in the pause I could hear it underneath — not menace, not the pale choir, just space. A big quiet space with streets in it, maybe. With an audience. "Sol. I've got maybe a minute of clean signal, and I didn't spend it on the weather. Put on a song for me."

My eyes were burning. I wiped them with the heel of my hand, professional to the last, and looked at the cart machine, the good reverb, the strips of tape with his handwriting. "What song?"

"Side B, track three. The one I played the night you started here, when you told me the board scared you and I told you the board should be more scared of you." Another laugh, softer. "You remember."

I remembered. Of course I remembered. October, nineteen years ago, a terrified new producer who'd never touched a fader, and a host who talked to ghosts and pretended they weren't real, sliding a record onto the platter and saying, "This one's for the new blood. She thinks she's temporary. Give her six months." Track three, side B. I'd heard it maybe five times since. I could have hummed it in my sleep. I think, some nights, I had.

"Marisol," he said, and the static rose around his voice like water around a stepping stone. "Thank you for keeping the chair warm. Don't ever explain me away. Not to them, not to yourself. Carry it. It's lighter shared."

Then, quick, before the static took him — and I will swear to my grave I heard this, and the tape will swear I didn't, and I believe the tape is lying:

"Tell Ruth the door holds. Tell the girl she sings better than 1893 ever did. And tell yourself—" the signal guttered, surged, held— "tell yourself I'm glad it was you on the other end of the line. Both lines. Every line."

Gone. Dial tone, then the soft click of the hybrid releasing, then nothing, just the room tone of a studio at midnight and the VU needles flat as a pulse drawn on a monitor by someone with very steady hands.

I sat there for I don't know how long. Long enough that the clock rolled past sign-off and I let it, because dead air, I have learned, is not always dead. Sometimes it's just a held breath. Sometimes it's a listener, on both sides of the glass, waiting to see what you do next.

Then I cued the record.

Side B, track three. I set the needle with hands that shook and did not miss. I faded it up slow, the way he taught me — *let it arrive, don't make it crash the party* — and I leaned to the microphone, and my voice, when it came, was the voice I actually have.

"That was a request," I said. "From a caller who's still alive where he's calling from, which is more than most of us can say. This one's for the keeper. You know who you are. You always knew who you were — that was your whole problem, and the town's whole salvation."

The song filled the studio, filled the tower's signal, filled the sleeping town the way hymns once had, and I sat in the glow of the ON AIR light with my hand resting on the edge of his chair, not sitting in it, never sitting in it, just — warming it.

The song ended. I reached to fade it out.

And the request line lit up again.

Caller ID: the station's own number.

I looked at it for a long moment. Then I looked at the clock — 12:04 a.m., well past sign-off, well past any hour that owed me an explanation — and I thought about doors, and choices, and which side of them we stand on, and I reached for the fader.

"*The Hollow Hour*," I said, "going a little long tonight. Go ahead, caller."

The line clicked open. And a voice — not his, not anyone's I knew, young and old at once, a child's voice with a century inside it — said, gently, the way you'd wake someone you loved:

"He can't stay on the line long, Marisol. But he wants you to know the show's going well on this end, too. He's got listeners. He's always had listeners." A pause, and in it, faintly, far away, I heard it: a theme song, a familiar cadence, a voice saying *good evening* to an audience of the lost.

"And once a year," the child's voice said, "on the equinox — he takes requests."
