## 1. The Inventory

Sable Nystrom believed in three things: the metric system, the integrity of a plumb line, and the healing power of a good thermos of coffee. Everything else — ghosts, omens, the vague dread that certain houses exhaled when you unlocked their doors — he filed under *not my department*.

He was, by his own quiet estimation, the best property surveyor in three counties, mostly because he was the only one who still measured everything twice and wrote it down in a ledger with a fountain pen, like a man with something to prove. The bank had given him the Hollowmere job on a Thursday, with the particular reluctance of a teller handing over a coin that might be cursed.

"Hollowmere House," said Mr. Voss, the loan officer, sliding the folder across the desk as though it might bite. "Foreclosure. The heirs haven't paid taxes since — well, since a while. You'll have the place to yourself. Nobody's lived there since 1911."

"Nobody's *lived* there," Sable said, "or nobody's been *in* there?"

Voss smiled the smile of a man who had decided this was not his department either. "The keys are in the folder. Take the whole weekend. It's a big house."

It was, in fact, an enormous house, and it rose out of the fog on Friday morning the way a whale rises out of the sea — slowly, with an air of having been there the whole time and simply deciding, now, to let you see it. Grey stone, black shutters, a roofline like a held breath. The grounds were overgrown but oddly tidy about it, as if the brambles had been instructed to look wild without actually interfering with the walkway.

Sable parked his surveyor's van, drank half his coffee in the manner of a man fortifying a garrison, and got to work.

The front door was oak, banded with iron, and unlocked itself before he finished turning the key. He noted this. He noted it the way he noted everything: *Front door, latch mechanism over-eager. Recommend servicing.* It was a good trick, writing things down. Writing things down made them facts, and facts were friendly. Facts sat still.

Inside, the entrance hall went up and up into a darkness the chandelier didn't so much illuminate as apologize to. Dust lay over everything in a fine grey velvet, undisturbed — and here was the first item Sable filed under *not my department*: there were no footprints in it. Not his own. He looked down at the floor, then at his boots, then at the floor again, and decided the dust must be the sort of dust that didn't take impressions. There were, he was fairly sure, several kinds of dust. He would look it up.

The rooms were numbered.

That was the second thing. Chalk numerals, tall and old-fashioned, written directly on the wallpaper or the plaster at shoulder height: *1* in the entrance hall, *2* in the drawing room, *3* in the library, and so on, marching through the house in a hand that was confident at the start and increasingly hurried as it went. By the time the numbers reached the east wing they were scrawled, smeared, in one case written twice over itself as though the counter had lost track and started again from a guess.

Somebody had once done exactly what Sable was doing. Somebody had walked this house with a ledger and a stubborn heart, counting.

He found the ledger on the library desk, under a leather folder gone stiff as bark. It was the founders' own inventory — Aldous and Verity Hollowmere, according to the flyleaf, though the ink of their names had faded to the color of weak tea — and it was, Sable had to admit, beautiful work. Precise columns. Confident sums. A surveyor after his own heart, seventy or a hundred years dead.

The trouble was the doors.

The ledger counted forty-one doors. Sable had the blueprints in his case — the bank's copies, yellowed but legible — and the blueprints showed thirty-three. He spent a pleasant, focused hour walking the ground floor with his tape measure, ticking off doors like a man counting sheep, and arrived at thirty-three. Then he walked it again, and arrived at thirty-four.

He stood in the corridor outside what the blueprints called a linen closet and looked at the extra door, which was narrow, and painted the same grey as the wall, and gave every indication of having always been there.

"Right," Sable said aloud, because saying things aloud helped, and because the house seemed to be listening and he wanted it to know he was a professional. "Someone's miscounted. Either the dead people or me, and I don't miscount."

The house said nothing, but the silence it said it with had a quality he didn't love. It was the silence of a classroom when the teacher asks a question and one child in the back row knows the answer and is deciding whether to say it.

He measured the corridor. The corridor was four centimeters longer than the blueprints said it should be. He measured it again. Four and a half. He wrote this down — *E. corridor, +4cm, discrepancy under investigation* — and the act of writing it calmed him so thoroughly that he almost missed the fact that the linen-closet door was now very slightly open. A hand's width. Enough to show darkness.

He closed it. It closed with the soft, obliging click of a thing that had only been resting its weight.

*Not my department,* Sable thought, and went to inventory the silver.

* * *

The afternoon went well, in the way that a boat ride goes well right up until the weather. He catalogued two hundred and eleven items of furniture, a portrait gallery of Hollowmere ancestors who all shared the same expression — the look of people listening to a joke they'd paid for — and a drawing room fireplace large enough to roast an ox standing up. He ate a sandwich on the back steps in weak sunlight and felt almost fond of the place. Big houses were just arithmetic with pretensions. He could do arithmetic.

It was while measuring the ballroom that he noticed the light behaving.

Not behaving *badly*, exactly. Behaving *late*. He'd swing his tape case and its shadow would follow a half-beat behind, like a dance partner who'd learned the steps from a description. He stopped. He watched. The shadow caught up and settled, innocent.

"Old glass," he said to the empty ballroom. "Old windows. Refraction."

The ballroom, which had three windows and none of them particularly old, accepted this without comment.

He found the mirrors in the west wing, and he found the chalk there too — but different. The numbers in the west wing weren't inventory numbers. They were small, and low on the walls, at the height of a kneeling person, and they weren't numerals at all but names, dozens of them, chalked and faded and chalked over again, the way you'd write a list you couldn't afford to forget.

The last name on the wall, fresher than the rest, was *Hollowmere*.

Sable wrote in his ledger: *West wing, anomalous markings. Recommend — * and then stopped, because his pen had run dry, and because he could not think of a single professional recommendation that covered it.

He looked up. The west wing corridor ended in a tall mirror, full-length, in a frame of carved oak gone black with age. In it he saw himself: a broad, careful man in a canvas jacket, holding a ledger like a shield, standing in a dead man's hallway at the end of a long day. Perfectly normal. Perfectly ordinary.

Except that his reflection's pen was still moving.

Sable's own pen was capped in his fist. In the glass, the reflected Sable was writing — small, patient strokes, the way you'd write a name — and then it looked up at him, and did not look away when he did not look away, and lifted one hand to point, gently, down.

Toward the cellar stairs.

Sable Nystrom believed in three things, and the third of them had always been that a man could measure his way out of anything. He uncapped his pen. He wrote, in a hand that only shook a little: *Cellar, unassessed. Proceeding.*

And because the house was listening, and because he wanted it to know he wasn't afraid — or rather, that his fear was none of its business — he said, "I'm going to count your doors. All of them. Even the ones you're hiding."

Somewhere below his boots, deep in the dark under the house, something turned a page.
