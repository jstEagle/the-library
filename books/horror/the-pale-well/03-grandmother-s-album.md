## 3. Grandmother's Album

The house on Latch Row had been waiting for her the way old houses wait — patiently, with dust gathered like a held breath.

Zephyrine Sterling let herself in with her grandmother's key, which still turned in the lock as though the door had only just been oiled, though no one had stood on this side of it in eleven months. The smell of the place rose up to meet her: camphor and dried lavender, old paper, the ghost of woodsmoke baked so deep into the plaster that it would outlast the walls themselves. It was the smell of her childhood summers. It was also, she thought now, standing in the dim hall with her suitcase still in hand, the smell of something being kept. Not preserved exactly. Kept. The way one keeps a promise, or a secret, or a vigil.

She had come to Marrow's Bend for the teaching post. That was what she told herself, and what she'd told the school board in her careful letter: that she was returning to her family's home to settle the estate, and that the position at Pale Hollow School was a fortunate coincidence of timing. Both things were true. Neither was the whole truth. The whole truth was that she had dreamed of this house three nights running before she ever saw the listing — dreamed of a kitchen table set for supper, and a chair pulled out from it, empty, facing the window, facing the dark, as though waiting for someone to come home who never would.

In the dream she had not been afraid. She had been setting another plate.

Now, in the honest gray light of an October afternoon, she hung her coat on the hook by the door — the third hook, because the first two belonged to her grandmother and grandfather and were not hers to use, though both of them were years dead — and began the work of sorting through a life.

* * *

Her grandmother, Maren Sterling, had been a woman of exact habits. Every jar in the pantry faced label-out. Every quilt was folded in thirds, never halves. Every photograph, Zephyrine discovered on the second day, lived in albums bound in green cloth gone soft as moleskin at the corners, arranged chronologically on the shelf of the front parlor, and every album — this was the part that made her hands go still on the shelf — had its spine labeled in her grandmother's small, upright hand.

Not *Summers 1962*. Not *Weddings*. The labels read like entries in a ledger:

*GATHERINGS, KEPT.*

*GATHERINGS, KEPT II.*

*GATHERINGS, KEPT III.*

Zephyrine took down the oldest volume and carried it to the window seat, where the light came slantwise through lace curtains the color of weak tea. Outside, the maples along Latch Row were turning the color of embers, and somewhere down the street a dog was barking at nothing in particular, or perhaps at everything, patiently, the way dogs do when they are the only ones who can see what needs barking at.

She opened the cover.

The first photograph showed a harvest supper, dated in pencil on the back: October, some year before she was born. Her grandmother stood young and straight-backed beside a long table dragged out into the yard, groaning under pies and roast fowl and jars of preserves. Around the table sat the family — great-aunts with their hair pinned up, uncles with their sleeves rolled, children squirming on laps. Zephyrine counted them without meaning to; it was a teacher's habit, counting heads. Eleven people.

And one chair.

It stood at the center of the table, not tucked in among the others but placed deliberately, squarely, where everyone could see it. A plain wooden chair, darker than the rest, its back straight as a headstone. No plate upon it. No napkin. Just the chair itself, centered, facing outward toward the camera, toward whoever held it.

Zephyrine frowned. She turned the photograph over. On the back, beneath the date, in that same upright script:

*We set it a place.*

She told herself it was a memorial. Families did such things — kept a chair for the dead at weddings and christenings, a photograph on the mantel, a candle in the window. Plenty of households had their rituals of absence. Her grandmother had been religious in her quiet way; perhaps this was hers.

But there was something in the arrangement that troubled her, some instinct she couldn't yet name. A memorial chair was usually off to the side, or draped in black, or marked somehow — acknowledged as absent. This chair was not marked as absent. It was *centered*. Everything else in the photograph arranged itself around it, the way iron filings arrange around a magnet, the way water arranges itself around a stone dropped in a pool. The family didn't sit near the empty chair. They sat *around* it, carefully, evenly, like people keeping a respectful distance from something sleeping.

She turned the page.

Another gathering. A christening, this time, indoors — her Aunt Verna's living room, she guessed, from the wallpaper of climbing roses. The baby was being passed between arms, everyone laughing. And there again, dead center of the frame, pulled away from the wall and set apart in the middle of the room where no sensible person would put a chair: the same dark wooden chair, its back straight, its seat empty.

*We must not take it away,* said the penciled line beneath.

Zephyrine sat very still. The lace curtains breathed against the sill. Somewhere upstairs, in a house with no one else in it, a floorboard settled with a soft wooden sigh, the way houses do — the way houses do, she repeated to herself firmly, when the temperature changes in the evening.

She turned more pages. The chair was in all of them.

A wedding, 1958: the bride mid-laugh, rice frozen in the air, and behind the celebrants, centered in the churchyard grass as though it had been carried out and planted there, the chair. A birthday party, candles lit, a boy of seven blowing them out while his cousins cheered — and the chair at the heart of the group, close enough that the boy's elbow nearly brushed its armrest. A funeral gathering on a porch, mourners in black, heads bowed, and the chair among them, unadorned, unmourned, simply *present*, the way a creditor is present at a reading of a will.

Always centered. Always empty. Always facing the camera — no. Zephyrine leaned closer, her breath fogging the plastic sleeve. Not always facing the camera. In the earliest photographs it faced whatever direction it pleased, angled toward the yard, toward the road, toward the tree line. But somewhere in the middle of the album — she paged back and forth to find the point where it changed — the chair began, gradually, decade by decade, photograph by photograph, to turn.

Toward the lens.

By the last pages of the first album, it looked directly out at her.

* * *

She made tea she did not drink and carried the second album to the same window seat as the light failed outside. The parlor had grown dim enough that she switched on the lamp with the fringed shade, her grandmother's lamp, and its circle of yellow light made the room feel smaller and warmer and less safe all at once, the way light does in old houses after dark.

The second album covered the years of her own childhood. Here she found herself — a gap-toothed girl of six in a corduroy jumper, sitting on her grandmother's lap at the harvest supper, laughing at something out of frame. She remembered that day. She remembered the taste of cider and the smell of woodsmoke and her grandmother's hand smoothing her hair. She did not remember the chair.

But there it was, centered at the long table, and here was the thing that stopped her breath: in this photograph, and in every photograph that followed, the other chairs had been moved back. Not removed — moved. Pushed a foot farther from the table than comfort allowed, all of them, evenly, leaving a wide clear space around the dark chair at the center, as though the family had agreed, wordlessly, across generations, on the dimensions of a perimeter.

And her grandmother — young Zephyrine's grandmother, Maren, sixty then and straight-backed still — was looking at the camera, yes, smiling her company smile, but her left hand rested on the back of the empty chair. Resting lightly. The way one rests a hand on the shoulder of a child one is keeping an eye on. Or the way one keeps a hand on a lid.

Beneath the photograph, the pencil:

*It grows restless in the cold months. Keep the gatherings. Keep the place set.*

Zephyrine closed the album.

For a long moment she sat in the lamplight listening to the house. The refrigerator hummed in the kitchen. Wind worried the eaves. Down the hill, faintly, she could hear the town of Marrow's Bend going about its evening — a screen door clapping shut, a car passing, the far-off bell of the little white church marking six o'clock. An ordinary town making ordinary sounds. And yet the sounds seemed to arrive at the windows and stop there, unable to quite get in, as if the house on Latch Row sat inside a skin of silence slightly thicker than the rest of the world's.

*Keep the gatherings.* Her grandmother had died in February. There had been no harvest supper since. Zephyrine counted backward in her head, quickly, unwillingly, the way you count the days since a missed dose of medicine. Last October — her first October in Marrow's Bend, her first weeks of teaching — there had been no gathering at this house. No place set. Nothing kept.

And in December, a boy named Elian Marsh had stopped coming to her class.

She pushed the thought away before it could take shape. Correlation was not — she was a teacher, she knew the phrase, she'd written it on her board herself — correlation was not causation. Grief made patterns out of noise. Grandmothers wrote strange things in albums; families kept odd customs; towns had their silences. She was tired. She would make soup, and sleep, and tomorrow she would finish the parlor and move on to the bedrooms, and none of it would seem so —

The lamp flickered.

Only once, briefly, the way lamps do when the wiring is old. But in the instant of its dimming, Zephyrine's eyes went to the album lying closed on her knee, and she saw — or thought she saw, she would tell herself later, she would insist — the green cloth cover shift. Slightly. As though something beneath it had turned over in its sleep.

She opened the third album instead of running, which surprised her. Later she would understand that she opened it because some part of her already knew what she would find, and knowing is its own kind of gravity.

The third album held the recent years. Fewer photographs now — the family scattered, the gatherings smaller. Suppers for four. Suppers for two. And in each one, the chair. Closer to the camera in every image, as though across the decades it had been walking, slowly, patiently, one photograph per step, out of the background toward the foreground. Toward her. In the final picture — dated fourteen months ago, the last autumn, the last harvest — the chair filled half the frame. It stood alone on the lawn of this house, this very window behind it, in the blue hour before dark. Empty.

On the back, her grandmother's handwriting had changed. The upright script wavered now, the letters leaning like tired people:

*I am the last one who remembers why. When I am gone, who will keep the place? Who will count?*

*Forgive us. We set it a place, and we must not take it away, and I have forgotten what we did to owe it.*

Zephyrine sat until the tea went cold, until the moon rose over the ridge and laid pale bars across the floorboards, and the house ticked and settled around her like a ship becalmed. Then, because she could not help it — because her hand moved before her fear could catch it — she turned back to the last photograph, the chair alone on the lawn in the blue hour.

And saw that it was no longer empty.

Resting on the armrest, pale in the failing light, lay a hand. Long-fingered, patient, water-white, its fingers curled over the wood with the ease of long ownership — the hand of someone who has waited so long to be invited that waiting has become its nature.

The photograph had not shown that yesterday. She was almost sure. She was almost, almost sure.

Outside, down the hill, the bell of the little white church rang nine o'clock, and Zephyrine Sterling sat in her grandmother's parlor with an album open on her knees, understanding at last that she had not come home to settle an estate.

She had come home because the house had finished waiting.
