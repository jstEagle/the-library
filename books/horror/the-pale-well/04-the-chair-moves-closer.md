## 4. The Chair Moves Closer

Zephyrine did not sleep so much as hover above sleep, the way a moth hovers above a flame it cannot decide to love or fear.

The house her grandmother had left her sat at the crook of Bend Road where the street gave up its pretense of straightness and surrendered to the valley's will. It was a narrow house, two stories, with wallpaper the color of weak tea and a smell Zephyrine could only describe as *remembered* — lavender and dust and something underneath both, like the ghost of bread baked long before she was born. She had arrived with six boxes and one plant that was already dying, and she had told herself, as she told herself most things, that this was a beginning.

Now, past midnight, she lay in her grandmother's bed — she had not yet had the heart to change the sheets, or perhaps she had not yet had the courage — and listened to the house settle around her like an old dog circling twice before lying down.

The album lay on the pillow beside her. She had meant to put it in a box. She had meant to put it far away from her sleeping body, in the cellar, under other boxes, under the dead weight of things nobody wanted. Instead it was here, breathing its faint paper smell against her ear, and every time she drifted toward actual sleep some instinct older than reason pulled her back up, gasping, certain that if she slept the photographs would do whatever photographs did when no one watched.

She had looked at them eleven times since dark. She knew because she had been counting, the way she counted everything now. Nineteen doors on the walk to school. Eighteen desks in her classroom. Eleven viewings of a family album that should have held nothing stranger than sunburned picnics and stiff holiday portraits.

On the eleventh viewing, she had done what any rational person would have done: she had taken the album into the kitchen, set it on the table beneath the bare bulb, and photographed each page with her phone. Evidence. A baseline. If the images changed again, she would have proof of when and how. This was the kind of thing her students would have called unhinged, and she had agreed with them even as she stood in her dead grandmother's kitchen at one in the morning, photographing photographs, her breath fogging in air that should not have been cold in early September.

Then she had gone back to bed, and lain there, and hovered.

* * *

At some hour without a name — past two, before four — she heard the chair.

Not a sound, exactly. Not wood scraping floorboard. It was more like the memory of such a sound, played back through water, through walls, through the long throat of the house itself: a soft, deliberate drag, as though something heavy were being repositioned by someone very patient who did not wish to wake anyone but did not particularly care if they woke.

Zephyrine sat up in the dark.

The sound came again. Closer. Or deeper. It was difficult to say whether it moved through the room or through the album, and that difficulty was the worst part — the way her mind kept trying to file the sensation somewhere sensible and finding no drawer that fit.

"Grandmother," she said aloud, to no one, the word coming out cracked. Her grandmother had been dead eight months. The house had been empty eight months. Empty houses made sounds; she knew this; she had lived in apartments that groaned and ticked and sighed. But empty houses did not make sounds that answered.

Because that was what happened. She said the name, and after a silence long enough to be contemptuous, the dragging came once more — one slow pull, closer — and stopped, as if satisfied. As if *acknowledged*.

Zephyrine reached for the lamp and her hand shook so badly she knocked it off the nightstand, and in the clatter and dark she sat with her knees drawn up, heart slamming, feeling foolish and terrified in equal measure, which she would learn was the exact recipe for surviving Marrow's Bend: never let either feeling fully win.

She did not look at the album that night. She was proud of that later, and ashamed of it too. She simply turned her back to it, faced the wall with its tea-colored paper, and stared at the faint pattern of vines until the window went gray, refusing — with the whole stubborn weight of her body — to know what was happening three feet behind her head.

Denial, she would think afterward, is just fear wearing its work clothes.

* * *

Morning came up thin and silver, the light of a season turning. Zephyrine made coffee she didn't drink and carried the album to the kitchen table with both hands, the way you carry something that might be sleeping.

She laid it open to the first photograph. The comparison took less than a second, and then she had to sit down, because her legs informed her they were no longer participating.

In last night's phone photos, the empty chair had sat among the family, third from the left in the row of folding chairs at her aunt's graduation picnic, 1987 according to the caption in her grandmother's looping hand. Odd, yes. Wrong, yes — centered oddly, angled toward the camera as no one else's chair was angled. But *among*. Part of the gathering. A guest at the edge of things.

This morning, the chair stood alone.

The picnic was still there — the same blanket, the same relatives frozen mid-laugh, the same deviled eggs going yellow at their edges — but the camera had, impossibly, moved. Or the scene had rearranged itself around the chair. The family clustered now at the far edge of the frame, small and slightly blurred, as though the photograph itself could not quite hold them in focus anymore. And in the foreground, sharp as a knife, close enough that she could see the grain of its wooden seat, sat the chair.

It had been pulled out from the row. Turned. It faced the viewer now, directly, the way a person sits when they intend to stay.

Zephyrine turned the page with one finger, barely touching the paper, as though the photograph might feel her hesitation and take offense.

The Christmas portrait, 1991. Last night: the same composition, family arranged on the sofa, the strange extra chair standing apart near the tree. This morning the chair sat between the sofa and the tree, drawn up to the coffee table as though set for dinner. A place had been cleared for it among the cups. One of her uncle's hands, in the blurred background, seemed to be reaching toward the empty seat — or perhaps away from it. At this size, at this blur, it was impossible to say which, and Zephyrine found she could not stop trying to say which.

Page turn. The wedding, 1974. The chair had been at the back of the reception hall, near the door. Now it stood at the head table.

Page turn. The christening. The funeral. The Fourth of July, 1969, her grandmother young and squinting into the sun. In every image the chair advanced — not jumping, nothing so crude as jumping, but *arriving*, each photograph composed now around its presence the way a sermon is composed around its text. The families shrank. The backgrounds softened. The world of the living receded toward the edges of its own pictures, and the chair came forward into the light, patient as erosion.

By the final page — the last photograph her grandmother had ever taken, a blurry shot of the backyard in October — there was no family in the frame at all. Only the yard, the fence, the burning bush, and the chair, sitting in the middle of the grass where no chair had ever been, facing the house.

Facing the camera.

Facing her.

Zephyrine became aware, distantly, that she was making a sound — a thin continuous note low in her throat, the sound of a kettle deciding whether to boil. She pressed her knuckles against her mouth until the note died.

"It isn't real," she said to the kitchen. "Photographs don't move."

The house said nothing, but the house's saying nothing had a texture now, a listening quality, and she understood suddenly why her grandmother had kept every curtain half-drawn, why she had never let anyone sit in the good chair by the window, why — oh, God, why — she had always, at every meal, in every house, in every decade, insisted on setting one extra place at the table and then pretending she hadn't.

*We set it a place. We must not take it away.*

The handwriting swam in Zephyrine's memory, that careful schoolteacher's script, the same script that had taught her cursive at this very table. Her grandmother had written that beneath a photograph forty years ago and then spent forty years keeping the promise, feeding a place at the table to something that fed on being fed, and the debt had come down the family line like a name, like a nose, like brown eyes, and now it had come to her.

"No," Zephyrine said. It came out steadier than anything she'd said all morning. "No. I didn't invite anything. I'm not part of your — whatever arrangement. I just teach here."

The light through the window dimmed, briefly, as though a cloud had considered passing over and decided instead simply to watch.

* * *

She called the number for the county historical society because it was the only number she could think of that wasn't a person in Marrow's Bend, and the woman who answered was kind and confused and eventually transferred her to someone who transferred her to a recording. Zephyrine hung up halfway through the second verse of the hold music and stood in her kitchen holding a phone that felt suddenly like a toy.

Of course. Of course there was no one to call. Who would she describe it to? *My dead grandmother's photo album is rearranging itself around an empty chair.* They would send someone. There would be forms. There would be a gentle-voiced deputy named something like Dale who would look at the album and see ordinary pictures, because that was how these things worked — she was certain of it the way you are certain of weather — the evidence only showed itself to those already inside the invitation. Everyone else would see a lonely woman new to town, grieving, overtired.

And wouldn't that be easier? Wouldn't it be a mercy, almost, to be wrong in front of witnesses? To be gently taken somewhere with soft lighting and a schedule?

She thought about Cass, the girl in the back row who never took off her jacket, who had whispered on the second day of school — *desk nineteen belongs to the one who waits* — with the flat certainty of someone describing a bus route. Cass hadn't sounded crazy. Cass had sounded like a kid reciting something learned young, the way children learn prayers they don't understand.

Zephyrine looked at the album. The chair looked back out of seven photographs at once, and she felt the full weight of the obvious settle onto her shoulders: the thing in the pictures was not moving toward the camera.

It was moving toward whoever held the album.

Toward her grandmother, all those years. And now —

Her phone buzzed in her hand and she nearly threw it across the room. A text. From Odile Voss, the gravedigger, whom she had met exactly once, buying flowers at the market, who had looked at Zephyrine's cart and said, without preamble, "You're Maren Sterling's granddaughter," in a voice that had not been a question and had not been friendly and had not, Zephyrine realized now, been unfriendly either. It had been the voice of someone checking a list.

The text said:

**Dont go in ur grandmothers cellar tonight. Whatever u hear down there isnt asking to be let out. Its asking to be let IN. Burn the album if u havent. U wont. Nobody ever does. — O.V.**

Zephyrine read it three times. Then she looked at the cellar door at the end of the hall, which she had not opened since arriving, which her grandmother had kept locked, whose key she had found two days ago in an envelope labeled, in that careful teacher's hand, *For Zephyrine, when the chair gets close.*

She had thought it was a figure of speech. Grandmothers said things like that. Grandmothers left riddles, left whimsies, left little jokes for the grandchildren to find.

The chair got close overnight. That was the phrase her own mind supplied, unbidden, in her grandmother's voice, and it fit the photographs perfectly, horribly, the way a key fits a lock it was cut for.

Zephyrine walked to the cellar door and stood before it. Behind her, the album lay open on the kitchen table, and she could feel it the way you feel someone watching from a doorway — not malevolent, not yet, but *attentive*, the politeness of it worse than any snarl. Something in those pictures had waited through sixty years of picnics and christenings, taking its place at the edge of every frame, being fed its empty place at every table, and it had never once rushed. It had never needed to. It had simply, photograph by photograph, year by year, generation by generation, moved closer.

Down the hall, in the silence, the cellar door handle turned — slowly, smoothly, all by itself — and clicked open.

From below, in the dark, came the soft drag of wood across stone. One pull. Closer.

And then, unmistakably, patiently, the sound of a chair being set upright at a table, and waiting.
