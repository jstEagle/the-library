## 5. Odile at the Well

Odile Voss had dug four hundred and eleven graves in her thirty-one years, and she had learned to read the ground the way other people read faces. The earth told her things. It told her when the frost would come early, when the water table was rising under the churchyard, when a coffin needed an extra shovelful of lime. And for as long as she could remember, the ground behind Pale Hollow School had been telling her one thing, over and over, in a voice like a held breath:

*Stay away from the well.*

She was mending the fence along the school's back lot on the last Friday of September when she saw the new teacher standing at its rim.

The woman — Sterling, Zephyrine Sterling, come all the way from the city to teach nineteen children in a town that barely deserved eighteen — stood with her hands braced on the low stone lip, leaning out over the dark like a woman reading a letter addressed to someone else. Her hair had come loose from its clip. The afternoon light fell into the well shaft and did not come back out.

Odile dropped her hammer.

It wasn't a decision. Her body moved before her mind could object, boots crunching across the dry grass, and by the time she reached the schoolhouse wall she was moving fast enough that the teacher turned, startled, one hand still resting on the stones.

"Get away from there."

The words came out harder than Odile intended. Harder than she'd spoken to anyone in years, maybe. She heard them hang in the air between them, ugly and abrupt, and watched the teacher's face do the arithmetic strangers always did — *the gravedigger, the tall quiet woman with the dirt permanently worked into the creases of her knuckles* — deciding whether to be offended or afraid.

"Excuse me?" Ms. Sterling said. "Is this — is it unsafe? I only wanted to look—"

"It's not safe." Odile stopped three paces off. Close enough to grab her if she leaned too far. Far enough to be polite, or what passed for it in Marrow's Bend. "Nothing about it is safe. Please step back."

The teacher looked at her a moment longer, then — to her credit — stepped back. One pace. Two. She didn't leave. Odile noticed that. Most people, once warned off something in this town, left and never asked why. That was practically the town motto: *don't ask why.* But this woman stayed where she was, arms crossing against a wind that hadn't started yet, and studied Odile with eyes the color of tea gone cold.

"You're the gravedigger," she said. "Odile Voss."

"Everyone knows who I am."

"They do, actually. That's strange, isn't it? I've been here two weeks and everyone knows everyone's business except the one piece of business that matters." She tilted her head toward the well without looking at it, the way you'd gesture toward a dog you didn't want to provoke. "Nobody will tell me what happened to the boy who used to sit in desk fourteen. Nobody will tell me why there are nineteen names on my roster and eighteen desks in my room. And nobody — *nobody* — will tell me what this well is for."

"It's not for anything," Odile said. "That's the point of it."

The wind arrived then, right on schedule, coming down off the ridge and bending the grass flat in a long silver wave. It smelled wrong for September. Not like drying corn and woodsmoke but like wet stone, like the inside of a root cellar, like water that had been sitting still too long in the dark. Odile felt it move through her the way it always did, a cold finger drawn up the spine, and she saw the teacher feel it too — saw her glance involuntarily at the well mouth, where the darkness seemed to have thickened since they'd started talking.

"What's your name?" the teacher asked. "Your first name. Everyone says Voss like it's a warning label."

"Odile."

"Zephyrine." A pause. "My grandmother lived here, you know. Before I took this job, I thought I was coming somewhere new. Now I'm not sure there's any such place."

Odile said nothing. The name Sterling sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. She'd seen it before — not often, but she'd seen it. In the ledger. In the photographs in the historical society's back room that no one was allowed to touch. In the margins of her own grandmother's Bible, where the old women of six families had written their covenant in pencil so it could be denied later.

*Sterling.* One of the six.

"I have to finish the fence," Odile said, which was true, and also a door closing.

"Of course." The teacher hesitated. "Thank you. For — whatever that was. I'll stay away from the well."

But she didn't move, and neither did Odile, and the silence stretched between them until Zephyrine Sterling broke it with the question Odile had been dreading for two weeks, the question that hung over every conversation in this town like smoke trapped under a ceiling.

"Why does everyone act like it's going to hear us?"

* * *

Odile walked home the long way, past the churchyard, past the row of Voss plots going back nine generations, each stone worn smooth as river rock. She let herself into the house above the burial ground — the house her family had kept since before the town had its current name — and stood in the kitchen without turning on the light.

Her grandmother's chair still sat by the window. Empty. Of course empty. But Odile had grown up in this house, and she knew better than most what an empty chair could mean, and how quickly it could stop being empty.

She made herself tea she didn't drink. She sat across from the dead woman's chair and had the argument she'd been having with herself since the day the new teacher stepped off the bus with her boxes of books and her city shoes and her grandmother's chin.

*Tell her nothing,* said the part of Odile that had survived thirty-one years in Marrow's Bend by keeping her mouth shut and her shovel sharp.

*Tell her everything,* said the smaller part, the part that had been getting louder every year, the part that sounded uncomfortably like her grandmother on her deathbed, gripping Odile's wrist with grave-cold fingers and whispering, *when the time comes, girl, you tell the truth even if it kills the town.*

The trouble was that both parts were right.

At dusk she went out to the shed and took down the lantern, and told herself she was only checking the fence line, and knew she was lying.

* * *

The well behind the schoolhouse had no official name. On the oldest maps it appeared as a small circle with no label at all, which was its own kind of statement. The children called it the pale well, because of the water — though there hadn't been water in it for decades, not real water. What collected at the bottom, forty feet down, was something else. Something that looked like milk poured into shadow. Something that never rose, never froze, never evaporated, and never reflected the sky.

Odile had looked into it exactly twice in her life. Once at seven, dared by a cousin who'd spent the rest of his childhood unable to sleep with his back to a doorway. Once at nineteen, on the night her father died, when grief made her stupid and she'd gone looking for him in the only hole in Marrow's Bend deep enough to hold what she was feeling.

Both times, the pale surface had shown her her own face — but a half-second late. She'd see herself begin to lean, and then the reflection would follow, like a memory of her rather than a mirror of her. Like something below, patiently learning her movements, practicing being her.

She'd told no one. Who would she tell? Everyone in town over the age of forty already knew, in the wordless way people know things they've agreed not to know. Everyone younger had been carefully, lovingly kept ignorant, the way you keep a child from a hot stove — except the stove in this case was the whole town, and the heat came up out of the ground.

Now there was a teacher asking questions. A teacher with a Sterling jawline and a roster full of names and a way of looking at the well like it owed her an explanation.

Odile found her at the fence line after dark, which surprised her; she'd half hoped the woman would have gone home. Zephyrine Sterling sat on the tailgate of Odile's truck with her coat pulled tight, watching the schoolhouse windows glow yellow against the blue evening. The lantern in Odile's hand threw their shadows long across the grass — two shadows, and then, where the light grazed the well's stone rim, a third that belonged to nothing.

"You followed me," Zephyrine said.

"I work here." Odile set the lantern on the truck bed. "You're sitting in my workplace."

"The schoolyard?"

"The whole bend." She said it flatly, and watched the teacher take it as a joke, and decided to let her. For now. "Ms. Sterling. Why did you come to Marrow's Bend? Really. A woman with your credentials doesn't end up teaching in a town of four hundred people by accident."

"My grandmother left me her house." Zephyrine's voice went careful. "I came to sort it out. The teaching post was — convenient."

"Your grandmother." Odile climbed up to sit on the tailgate too, leaving a careful foot of space between them. "What was her name?"

"Maren. Maren Sterling. She grew up here, I think. She never talked about it."

*Maren.* Odile closed her eyes briefly. There was a photograph, in the historical society, of six families standing in front of the original schoolhouse, harvest moon fat and orange above the roofline. She had counted the faces a hundred times as a girl, trying to understand why none of them were smiling, why the smallest girl in the picture — a child of maybe eight, dark-haired, solemn — was holding a wooden chair that was far too big for her, dragging it toward the center of the group as if setting a place at a table.

Maren Sterling. Eight years old. Holding the chair.

"She died recently," Odile said. It wasn't a question either.

"Last spring." Zephyrine turned to look at her. "Why do you ask about her like that? Like you knew her."

"I knew of her." Odile picked at a splinter on the tailgate's edge, working it loose, giving her hands something honest to do. "Everyone in this town knows of everyone. We're four hundred people in a bowl of hills. We know each other the way teeth know each other. We don't have to like it."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Odile agreed. "It isn't."

The schoolhouse clock began to strike eight. They both listened to it — nine strokes, slow, the old mechanism dragging between each one — and when it finished, the silence afterward felt less like absence than like attention. Odile had felt that silence her whole life. The town's silences weren't empty. They were *held*, the way breath is held, the way a congregation holds its silence during the confession.

"Two weeks ago," Zephyrine said quietly, "my first night here, I counted the doors of the schoolhouse. Nineteen. Someone had written me a note — unsigned — telling me to count them nightly. Nineteen doors, nineteen students, eighteen desks. And today I find the town gravedigger telling me to stay away from a dry well like it's a sleeping animal." She laughed, short and without humor. "In the city, that adds up to a bad landlord and a liability issue. Here it adds up to something else. I can feel it adding up to something else."

Odile looked at her hands. At the dirt in the creases. Four hundred and eleven graves, and not one of them had ever frightened her the way living people did, the way their practiced smiles did, the way the whole town bent around its one missing piece like water bending around a stone.

"If I tell you," she said, "you can't un-tell yourself. You understand that? Whatever I say next, you'll carry it. You'll see it every time you walk past that well, every time you call attendance, every time you count those doors. There's no version of you that hears this and stays easy."

"I haven't been easy since I got here."

"No," Odile said. "I don't suppose you have. That's how it starts."

She stood. Walked to the well. Stopped an arm's length from the rim — closer than she'd let the teacher come, farther than she wanted to be — and put her palm flat on the stone. It was warm. It was always warm, in defiance of every season, as if something below had a pulse and the stone was the skin over it.

"My family helped make this," she said.

Behind her, Zephyrine Sterling went very still.

"Six families. A long time ago — my great-great-grandmother was a girl then, older than the others. The Sterlings were one of the six. So were the Creeds, the Ashfords, the Bellamys, the Hales. And us. The Vosses." She kept her hand on the stone while she talked, the way you keep a hand on a dog that might bolt. "They wanted something the law wouldn't give them. Something had been done in this town — something terrible, done to children — and the man who did it was protected. Respected. Untouchable. So the six families went out to the ridge under the harvest moon and they called for something older than law. Something that keeps accounts."

"And it came," Zephyrine said. Not a question. Her voice had gone thin.

"It came. It took him. And then—" Odile's throat tightened around the shape of the sentence she'd been raised not to say. "And then it stayed. Because an invitation, once given, isn't finished. It has to be *un*-given. Properly. Honestly. And nobody ever did. They just — stopped talking about it. Built the school around it. Raised their children around it. Taught them to smile at newcomers and change the subject." She finally lifted her hand from the stone. Beneath her palm, faintly, the warmth lingered in the shape of her fingers. "Every family in that photograph owes a debt. Mine included. Yours included, Ms. Sterling, whether your grandmother ever told you or not."

The wind came again off the ridge. Below them, deep in the shaft, something shifted — not a sound exactly, more a displacement of silence, the way water displaces air. Odile heard Zephyrine's breath catch.

"The boy," the teacher whispered. "Desk fourteen. Elian Marsh. He stopped coming three weeks before I arrived, and now no one remembers him but me. Is that—"

"That's the interest coming due." Odile turned to face her. In the lantern light, Zephyrine's face was pale as the water at the bottom of the well, and Odile felt something crack open in her chest — something she'd kept boarded shut since she was seven years old and first understood what her family had done. "We're the descendants of people who invited something in and then pretended they hadn't. And it's tired of pretending. It's starting to collect."

"Then we tell people. We go to the town, we—"

"And say what?" Odile's laugh came out bitter. "You've been here two weeks. Have one person — one — given you a straight answer about anything? This town has spent generations perfecting the art of not knowing. You could stand in the church with a megaphone and they'd nod politely and forget you by supper." She shook her head. "No. If it's ever going to end, it ends the way it started. With the six families. With their blood, their names, their confession. Nothing less will un-invite it."

Zephyrine stared at her. "You're saying we do this. You and me. The descendants."

"I'm saying I've dug graves my whole life for a debt I never owed and couldn't pay." Odile picked up the lantern. Its flame bent toward the well, steadily, like a compass needle finding north. "I'm saying somebody has to be the first to speak, and it might as well be the two families with the most to answer for."

For a long moment the teacher said nothing. Then she stepped forward — past the arm's length Odile had kept sacred her entire life — and laid her own hand on the well's warm stone, deliberately, the way you'd lay a hand on a grieving stranger's shoulder.

"We set it a place," she murmured, so softly Odile almost missed it. "We must not take it away."

Odile went cold from the scalp down. "Where did you hear that?"

"In my grandmother's handwriting. Under a photograph." Zephyrine's eyes were wide, fixed on the darkness below. "There's an empty chair in every picture she owned. Centered. Like it was the guest of honor." She looked up. "Odile — I think the chair is here. I think it's been waiting for me."

The lantern flame stood perfectly straight, pointing down into the well, and from forty feet beneath their feet — faint, patient, unmistakably pleased — came the sound of a small wooden chair being dragged across a floor.
