## 13. Cass Is Taken

Cass dreamed of water before the water came.

That was the first wrong thing — the dream itself. They hadn't dreamed of water in years, not since the night they'd run from their uncle's house with a garbage bag of clothes and a bus ticket they'd never used. In those dreams the water was always in their lungs, and their uncle's voice was always saying *you'll thank me when you're older*, and Cass always woke up drowning on dry land, gasping at a ceiling that belonged to someone else.

But this dream was different. This dream was quiet.

In it, Cass stood at the edge of something vast and still, and the surface below them was pale as milk glass, and far down — so far down that distance stopped meaning anything — small shapes moved in slow procession, like children walking to school in orderly lines beneath a frozen lake. And every one of them turned its face upward at once, and every face was patient, and none of them were angry.

*You're late,* said a voice that wasn't a voice, more like the feeling of a hand smoothing down the back of your hair. *We saved you a seat.*

Cass woke in the dark of Zephyrine's spare room with their heart going like a trapped bird and their mouth full of a taste like chalk and pennies. The window above the bed showed a sliver of sky, and the harvest moon hung in it already, swollen and low and the color of a fingernail pressed white against skin. It hadn't been full yet when they'd gone to sleep. Zephyrine had checked. Zephyrine had been checking everything all week — doors, locks, photographs, the roster, the roster again — with the grim diligence of a woman counting stitches in a wound.

The moon was early. That was the second wrong thing.

Cass sat up. The house was silent in the particular way houses are silent when silence is being listened to rather than simply inhabited. No creak of the floorboards under Zephyrine's pacing. No tick of the kitchen clock. Even the wind had stepped outside to wait.

And there was a sound underneath the silence. Faint. Rhythmic. A soft, wet lapping, like a tongue against stone.

Cass knew what it was before they knew how they knew it. Everyone in Marrow's Bend knew, even the ones who pretended otherwise, even the ones who smiled too wide at the new teacher and said *oh, that old thing's been dry for fifty years, honey.* The well behind the schoolhouse. The one Odile Voss had grabbed Cass's wrist about in the grocery store parking lot, her grave-dirt nails biting in, her voice dropped to almost nothing: *You don't go near it. You hear me? Whatever you hear coming out of it, you don't answer.*

Cass got out of bed anyway.

They would ask themselves, later, why. Why not wake Zephyrine, who slept down the hall with her door open because she'd learned that closed doors meant nothing good here. Why not run for Odile, whose cottage sat squat and lamp-lit at the edge of the cemetery like a dog that had decided to guard the graves instead of dig them. Why walk barefoot through a strange dark house, past the photographs on the wall — the ones where the chair kept moving closer no matter how many times Zephyrine turned them face-down — and out the back door into air that smelled of ripe apples and river mud?

Because the voice had said *we saved you a seat.* And some ruined, starved part of Cass — the part that had spent sixteen years being told they were too much, too loud, too wrong, too much trouble to keep — heard that and did not hear a threat.

It heard an invitation.

The grass behind the house was cold and heavy with dew. The town lay silvered under the premature moon, every rooftop beaded with light, and Cass moved through it without deciding to, their feet finding the path the way water finds a channel. Down the hill. Past the shuttered hardware store with its hand-painted HOURS sign. Past the church, where the bell hung motionless in its tower like a tongue that had been cut out. Past the playground where the swings hung dead still, though there was wind now — there was definitely wind, Cass could feel it pushing at their back, gentle as a hand between the shoulder blades, steering them forward.

The schoolhouse rose up ahead of them, pale and patient, and behind it, past the fence with its leaning gate, the well.

It didn't look like much. That was the thing nobody told you. It looked like any old farm well: a circle of mossy fieldstone, a wooden cover gone gray with weather, a rusted pump handle frozen mid-gesture. Except the cover was off. It lay flat in the grass beside the rim like a lid removed from a pot, and from the black mouth of the shaft came the lapping sound, louder now, wetter, and a smell that was not the smell of any water Cass had ever known — cold stone and old paper and something sweet underneath, something like apples left too long in a cellar, something like the inside of a mouth.

Cass stood at the rim and looked down.

There was water at the bottom. There should not have been water at the bottom. Odile had said the well had been dry since before she was born; Zephyrine had said the same, reading from the town's own records, her voice tight with the effort of pretending the records meant anything. But far below, catching the moonlight and throwing it back up broken and trembling, water waited. Pale water. Milk-pale, dream-pale, the exact color of the surface Cass had stood over in the dream.

And rising up along the inside of the stones, pale hands.

They came slowly, without hurry, without grasping — five of them, then eight, then more than Cass could count, emerging from the dark the way roots show through the side of a riverbank. They were slender and long-fingered and bloodless, and they did not reach for Cass. They simply arrived, resting on the inner lip of the well's mouth, palms up, open, the way you hold out your hands to show they're empty. The way you offer to carry something.

*No,* Cass said. Their voice came out small and cracked, a child's voice, and they hated it. *No, I didn't— I'm not—*

But even as they said it, they felt the truth of it settle over them like the dew had settled on the grass. Harlan Creed's cologne. The smell of his car, leather and money. The way he'd found Cass alone outside the diner three nights ago and bought them a hot chocolate they hadn't asked for and talked, gently, reasonably, about how hard it must be, running away, no family, no ties, how a boy like that could just — slip away somewhere, start over, wouldn't that be better, wouldn't everyone understand—

Cass had thought, at the time, that Harlan Creed was being kind. That was the worst part. That was the part that made their eyes burn now, standing barefoot in the frost-wet grass: not the hands, not the well, but the memory of feeling *grateful.*

*I was sold,* Cass said, and the word came out steady this time, and the moment it did, the hands below went very still, all of them, dozens of them, listening. *He sold me. He offered me to you like I was — like I was nothing.*

The well breathed. There was no other word for it. The whole shaft exhaled a long, slow current of cold sweet air, and the water at the bottom shivered, and somewhere in the deep a sound began — not words yet, but the shape of words, the way a melody hummed through a wall is the shape of a song.

Then the hands took hold.

Not cruelly. That was the horror of it, the part Cass would never fully be able to explain to anyone afterward: it did not hurt. The pale fingers closed around their wrists and ankles with the firmness of a nurse, of a parent guiding a sleepy child from the car to the bed, and the stones of the well's mouth passed over Cass's body like the frame of a doorway, and the last thing they saw of the world above was the harvest moon, huge and white and early, watching with the calm of something that had done this many times before.

Then the dark took them, and the water rose to meet them, and Cass went under.

* * *

They should have drowned. They understood that with a distant, academic clarity, the way you understand arithmetic during a fire. Human lungs did not work underwater. Human bodies sank. Human bodies went cold and stayed cold.

Cass sank, and did not drown. The water entered them and it was not like drowning at all — it was like breathing in winter air, sharp and clean and shocking, except the shock faded and the breathing continued, easy, unremarkable, as if lungs had always been optional and Cass had simply never been informed. The cold, too, softened as they descended, until it was less a temperature than a texture, smooth and close, like sliding between clean sheets in a room where someone had left the window open in October.

Light came from below. That was the third wrong thing, and by now Cass had stopped flinching at wrong things; they catalogued them instead, the way Zephyrine counted doors. Light from below, rising up through the pale water, warm-toned and flickering, and as Cass sank toward it the shapes resolved: windows. Rows of tall windows glowing amber, arranged along walls that could not possibly be down here, in a building that could not possibly be standing at the bottom of a flooded shaft behind a schoolhouse in a town that had agreed, politely, for fifty years, to remember nothing.

The water thinned. Cass's feet found a floor — tile, checkered, worn pale at the center of each square by generations of small feet — and the water let them go, sheeting off their clothes, leaving them dry. Dry, and warm, and standing in a hallway that smelled of chalk dust and pencil shavings and floor wax, the smell of every school hallway that has ever existed, distilled to its purest form.

Coat hooks lined one wall, each with a paper name card taped above it, curling with age. Cass walked past them slowly, reading. *Abigail Pruitt. Thomas Merrick. Lucille Gray. Daniel Okafor.* Nineteen hooks. Nineteen names. Some of the cards were so faded the ink had gone the color of weak tea, but every name was still legible, every hook still waiting, and above one empty hook near the end, a fresh card had been taped up in handwriting that was not handwriting at all — letters formed of fine pale lines, like veins seen through skin:

*CASS.*

Their throat tightened. Not fear, exactly. Something adjacent to fear. The feeling of seeing your name on a mailbox for the first time after years of living in places where mail was addressed to whoever happened to be there.

From beyond a set of double doors at the end of the hall came a sound Cass knew in their bones: the murmur of a classroom. A teacher's voice, low and unhurried, rising and falling. The scratch of pencils. A child's laugh, quickly hushed.

Cass pushed the doors open.

The room was their room. Not metaphorically — it was *the* room, the classroom at Pale Hollow School upstairs and fifty years ago, preserved down here like a fly in amber. Eighteen desks in neat rows, and at every desk a child, and the children were writing, heads bent, hair pinned or braided or cowlicked in styles that had gone out of fashion before Cass's grandparents were born. Overalls and pinafores and one boy in a church shirt buttoned to the throat. None of them looked up. At the front of the room stood the teacher.

It was tall. It was pale as the water, pale as the moon, and its face was the face of well-water seen from underneath — shifting, faintly rippling, features suggested rather than fixed, so that looking at it too long felt like looking at your own reflection in moving water and realizing the reflection was blinking at a different speed. It held a piece of chalk, and it was writing on the board in a careful, beautiful hand, and what it wrote was:

*Attendance will be taken at dawn. Please remain seated.*

And then, as if it had felt Cass's eyes on it — as if it had been waiting, patiently, for however many decades it took — it turned.

Its gaze moved over Cass the way lamplight moves over a page. It did not smile; its face was not built for smiling. But something in it *warmed*, the way a room warms when a lamp comes on, and it gestured — one long pale hand opening toward the back of the room, toward the nineteenth desk, the one that had never existed upstairs, the one that had always existed here — and the children finally, finally looked up.

Eighteen faces turned toward Cass. Unaged. Unchanged. Abigail Pruitt with her braids half-unraveled. Daniel Okafor with a gap where his front tooth had been. Children who had been missing for fifty years and were nine years old still, ten years old still, their pencils resting mid-word on yellowed paper, their eyes clear and curious and — this was the part that undid Cass, this was the part they would carry up out of the dark like a coal cupped in both hands — *kind*. Not hungry. Not hollow. Kind, the way children are kind, with the terrible directness of children, eighteen faces brightening at the sight of the new kid the way faces brighten at recess.

The girl nearest the aisle — Lucille Gray, according to the coat hooks, a small solemn girl with a ribbon slipping out of her hair — scooted her desk sideways without a word, clearing a path to the empty seat. Another child patted the desktop of desk nineteen, twice, gently, the universal grammar of *here, sit here, we kept it for you.*

*I don't belong here,* Cass started to say, and the words died, because standing in that warm amber light with eighteen patient faces turned toward them, Cass heard how the sentence sounded, and it sounded like every other place they'd ever been told they didn't fit. Their uncle's kitchen. Three foster homes. The bus station bench where they'd slept the first night on the road, before Marrow's Bend, before Zephyrine, before any of it.

Here, someone had written their name on a card and taped it above a hook.

Here, someone had saved them a seat.

Cass walked down the aisle between the desks. Small hands brushed their sleeves as they passed, not grabbing, just touching, welcoming, the way you touch a new arrival to confirm they're real. They reached the nineteenth desk. They sat. The chair received them with a creak of old wood, and the desk's surface was carved all over with initials and dates and one line, cut deep by some child's jackknife decades ago, that Cass ran their thumb across and read by feel before they read it by sight:

*WE ARE NOT FORGOTTEN. WE ARE ONLY WAITING TO BE COUNTED.*

At the front of the room, the pale teacher turned back to the board and began, softly, to teach — some lesson about seasons, about harvest, about what the earth takes and what it returns — and its voice was the voice from the dream, the hand smoothing down the back of your hair, and around Cass the pencils resumed their scratching, and the amber light held steady, and it would have been easy, so easy, to let the warmth close over their head like the water had, to stay, to stop fighting, to be one more patient face turning toward the next frightened newcomer.

But Cass had run away from worse places than this. Running away was the one thing they'd ever been good at.

So while the teacher's back was turned, Cass raised their hand — high, insistent, the way you do when you actually need to leave the room — and waited, heart hammering, to see whether a thing that had waited fifty years underground would grant a hall pass.

The chalk stopped.

Slowly, ripple by ripple, the well-water face turned toward them, and far above — muffled by fathoms of impossible water, thin as a thread — Cass heard a sound that did not belong to the classroom below.

A woman's voice, calling names into the dark. Calling attendance.

Calling *them*.
