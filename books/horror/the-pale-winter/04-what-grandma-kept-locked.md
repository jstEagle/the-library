## 4. What Grandma Kept Locked

Theo didn't sleep. He told himself it was the coffee, then the drive, then the light left on in the kitchen because he'd forgotten to change the bulb and the old one buzzed at a frequency that got under the skull. None of it was true. What kept him up was the photograph on his laptop, the one from the kitchen, zoomed to four hundred percent, where the fifth chair sat in the corner of the frame with its back to him and its legs planted the way a person plants their feet when they intend to stay.

He catalogued the rest of the job instead. That was the discipline. Forty-one images of the Brightwater farmhouse, timestamped, geotagged, filed under the client code he'd assigned before he'd known whose house it was — before he'd let himself know. BW-1974. He'd typed the number without thinking, and now it sat there in the filename like a splinter.

At 6:14 a.m. he gave up, dressed, and drove back out to Marlow Bend with a thermos of black coffee and the particular nausea of a man returning to a place his instincts had already voted against.

*You're the professional,* he told himself, taking the gravel turnoff with the frost still silvering the fence wire. *You've been in a thousand houses where something was wrong. You document it. You don't have to explain it. Explaining is above your pay grade.*

The farmhouse sat in its field like a held breath. Same sagging porch. Same gutters full of a decade of maple seeds. He'd been here four days ago and the house had felt merely dead. Now it felt *attentive*.

He stood at the bottom of the porch steps a moment longer than he needed to, photographing the front door. Standard practice — establish the exterior condition before entry. The shutter clicked. In the screen of the camera, the door looked the same. He checked anyway. He was checking everything now, and he knew exactly why, and he hated that he knew.

Inside, the smell had changed. Four days ago it had been the standard dead-house smell: dust, mouse urine, the sweet rot of a bag of onions forgotten in a drawer. Now there was something under it. Wet stone. Cold iron. The smell of the well, though the well was forty yards from the house and capped with a slab of concrete his grandfather had poured in — Theo did the math — 1975.

One year after Ivy. He didn't know why that number arrived in his head with such certainty. He'd write it down anyway. Documentation was his prayer, and you don't skip prayers just because you don't understand what you're praying to.

* * *

The droppings had changed.

On his first visit they'd been arranged in rows — neat, deliberate, wrong. Now they were *stacked*. In the pantry doorway — not inside the pantry, he'd get to that, God, he was already avoiding it and he could feel himself avoiding it — in the pantry doorway, the mice had built a cairn of droppings the size of a fist, precise as masonry, and on top of it they'd placed a button.

A small brass button, green with age, with a shank on the back. The kind that came on a child's winter coat in the seventies.

Theo photographed it from six angles. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; that was the one thing about himself he'd never had to work at. He put on a nitrile glove, bagged the button, labeled it. BW-1974-042. Then he stood in the cold kitchen holding the little bag up to the window light and thought: *this came off a coat. This came off a coat that was worn into that field, or worn into that well, and something carried it up forty feet of dry stone and set it on a pile of its own shit like a cat leaving a bird on a doorstep.*

A gift. Or a summons.

"Okay," he said out loud, because the house was the kind of quiet where you either talk or start listening too hard. "Okay. Pantry."

* * *

The padlock was on the *inside*.

He'd noticed the door on the first visit — a standard farmhouse pantry, pine boards, painted the same exhausted green as the kitchen trim — and he'd registered, without registering, that it was closed. Houses that sit empty for years don't have closed doors. Drafts open everything. Mice open everything. A closed door in a dead house is a door someone closed on purpose, and a door someone closed on purpose is a door worth photographing, and he hadn't photographed it, and that failure sat in his stomach now like a swallowed stone.

Because now, up close, in the flat morning light through the kitchen window, he could see what he should have seen four days ago.

The hasp was mounted on the inside of the door. The padlock hung from it — an old brass-bodied thing, corroded green, hanging on the *interior* side of a door that opened *outward*, into the kitchen. Which meant the lock could only be reached from within the pantry. Which meant the last person to lock that door had locked it while standing inside.

And then not come out.

Theo stood in front of it for a long time. The camera hung at his chest, forgotten. He thought about his grandmother — Edith Brightwater, five foot one, hands like tree roots, the woman who'd taught him to gut a fish and to never lie to a man's face about his own house — and he tried to fit her into the image of a person who would padlock herself into a pantry. It wouldn't fit. She'd been strict, yes. Private in the way farm people are private, which is to say she'd have shown you a broken arm before she'd have shown you the inside of her finances. But she was *sane*. She was the sanest person he'd known. Sanity was practically the family brand: Brightwaters documented, Brightwaters measured, Brightwaters did not believe in ghosts, only in failures of maintenance.

So either the lock meant something he wasn't seeing, or his grandmother had not been who he'd spent thirty years believing she was.

He photographed the door. Then the lock. Then the hinges, which were on the outside, which meant the door could be lifted off without touching the lock at all, and he understood that too, suddenly: the lock wasn't there to keep people out. It was there to keep something *in*, and whoever installed it had known that a lock is a symbol and hinges are a fact. The lock was for the thing inside to look at. The hinges were for the family.

"Sorry, Grandma," he said, and got the flat bar out of his truck.

* * *

The pins fought him. Fifty years of paint had welded the hinge knuckles into a single mass, and the third pin sheared, and the door came off with a crack that echoed through the empty house like a shot. Theo stood in the dust drifting out of the dark pantry doorway and felt the house *notice*. There was no other word for it. The temperature in the kitchen dropped — not a draft, drafts move, this was a stillness, a held-breath stillness — and somewhere above him, in the attic he had not yet had the courage to inspect, something shifted its weight.

He turned on his headlamp and went in.

The pantry was six feet by eight, shelved on three walls, empty of everything a pantry should hold. No jars. No cans. No sacks of feed. The shelves were bare and clean — *scrubbed* clean, the wood pale where decades of hands had wiped them down, and wiped them, he understood within seconds, for a different purpose than food storage.

The left wall was tally marks.

They started at shoulder height and ran floor to ceiling, scratched into the paint with something sharp — a knife point, a nail. Groups of four vertical strokes with a fifth struck through, the way prisoners count days, the way shepherds count sheep. They covered the entire wall. Theo swept the headlamp across them and did the math the way he did everything, automatically, helplessly: roughly forty strokes per square foot, call it thirty square feet of wall, call it — he stopped. He didn't want the total. The total was in the thousands. The total was years.

He photographed the wall in overlapping frames, top to bottom, the way he'd document termite galleries, and it was only later, at home, stitching the frames together, that he'd find what the camera caught and his eye had skipped: the tallies weren't uniform. Every seventh group was scratched deeper, gouged, the strokes doubled over each other like a hand pressing too hard. And beside each deep group, in a cramped, slanted hand, a single letter had been cut into the wood.

He couldn't read them yet. He would. Everything got read, eventually. That was the whole point of him.

The floor was where the rest of it was.

In the corner farthest from the door — the corner farthest from *help*, his mind supplied, unhelpfully — someone had arranged a small collection of objects on a square of folded quilt, arranged with the same terrible neatness as the droppings in the kitchen. Theo crouched. The headlamp threw his shadow huge against the tally wall, and he ignored it, and photographed each object where it lay before touching any of it. Procedure. Procedure was the rope he was holding onto.

A child's shoe. Leather, black, a buckle at the strap, worn through at the outer heel the way shoes wear when the child walks on the outside of the foot — a pigeon-toed kid, a kid who'd been measured for this shoe by someone who loved her, size — he turned it over — a size that stopped mattering in 1974. Inside the shoe, curled like a sleeping thing, a ribbon. Hair ribbon, green, the color of the pantry door, the color of the kitchen trim, the color someone in this family had chosen over and over and over. And beneath the shoe, flat and cracked and brown, a photograph lying face-down.

He knew before he turned it over. He knew the way you know a phone is going to ring.

The photograph was of the kitchen. This kitchen. Five chairs at the table — *five*, his mother would say four, his mother would swear on her children four — and at the far end of the table sat a girl. Maybe nine. Dark hair, a green ribbon, hands folded in front of her like a child posing for a school picture. The image was water-damaged, the colors bled to brown and gray, but the face was intact, and the face was *smiling*, and Theo sat down hard on the pantry floor because he had seen that smile before, or its architecture, its blueprint — the same wide-set eyes, the same gap in the front teeth — on his mother. On himself, in enough mirrors.

The girl in the photograph was wearing a winter coat with brass buttons, and the top button was missing.

Theo sat in his dead grandmother's secret room, under forty years of tally marks, holding a photograph of a cousin whose name he did not know, in a house that had been feeding something in the ground for half a century, and he did the only thing his body knew how to do.

He photographed his own hands holding it. Timestamped. Geotagged. Filed.

* * *

He didn't remember driving home. He had the thermos, still half full, and a memory of the well — he'd stopped at it, he must have stopped at it, the truck was parked at an angle that said so — and the concrete cap sitting on the stone throat, and him standing on it in the wind, and the cap *humming*. Not vibrating. Humming, low and patient, like a throat deciding whether to swallow. He had stepped off it and driven away and that was all he had, and it was 9:40 in the morning and his mother answered on the second ring the way she always did, bright and armored.

"Mom." He had the photograph laid flat on his desk, under the good lamp. "When you were a kid. How many chairs were at Grandma's kitchen table?"

A pause with weather in it. "Four. Theo, it's not even ten —"

"Was there ever a fifth?"

The pause got longer. He could hear her deciding. He'd spent his whole life listening to his mother decide, that flicker between the true thing and the safe thing, and he'd always assumed the safe thing won because the safe thing *was* the true thing, in her world, in the world she'd been handed.

"Where are you?" she said. Not *why do you ask*. Not *of course not*. *Where are you.*

"The house, Mom. I was at the house. There's a pantry door that locks from the inside, and there's a wall in there covered in tally marks, and there's a shoe, and there's a photo of a girl at that table, and she has your eyes, and nobody in this family will say her name."

The silence on the line went on so long he checked whether the call had dropped.

Then his mother said, in a voice he had never heard her use, small and old and frightened, a voice from 1974: "Her name was Ivy. And you need to get out of that house, Theo. You need to get out before first frost."

The line went dead.

Theo sat looking at the photograph. The girl at the table. The missing button. The green ribbon. *Ivy.*

He opened the ledger file on his laptop — his grandmother's ledger, the one he'd photographed on his first visit, the one with the payments and that single annotated word, *PAID*, entry after entry after entry — and he scrolled back through the decades of neat, sane, documented columns to the very first page, dated January 1975, and read the opening line in his grandmother's upright, unshakable hand.

*For the winter of '74. Ongoing.*

Below it, the first tally mark.

He was still staring at it when he noticed, in the corner of the photograph on his desk — the pantry photo, the one he'd taken that morning, the last frame before he'd left — that on the tally wall, at shoulder height, at the exact height of a nine-year-old's reaching hand, the final stroke of the final tally hung unfinished.

Mid-scratch. Mid-word. As if she'd been counting up to something.

Or counting down.
