## 6. The Well Is Not Dry

The well sat forty feet behind the farmhouse, past the collapsed clothesline and the blackberry bramble that had gone feral in the two years since Grandma's death. Theo had played near it as a boy. He remembered it as a ring of fieldstone with a rotted wooden cover, dry as a sermon, the kind of hole that swallowed baseballs and nothing else. His grandmother had been strict about one thing only: stay away from the well. Not because of falling. She'd never said why. Just *stay away from the well, Theo*, in a voice she used for nothing else.

He stood at the edge of the bramble now, camera bag on his shoulder, and listened.

Water makes a sound when it moves. Even still water breathes — a faint lap against stone, a settling. The well was making neither sound. It was making a *rising* sound, a low hydraulic hum he felt through his boot soles before he heard it, like standing on the throat of something swallowing upward.

He checked his notes first. Procedure before dread; that was the whole architecture of his life. The Marlow Bend property survey from 1987 listed the well as "dry, capped, non-functional." The county hydrology map showed the water table in this valley at sixty-one feet. A well twenty-two feet deep, if it held water at all, would hold it far below sightline.

Theo walked to the stone rim and looked down.

The water was nine feet below the lip. Clear, black, utterly still, reflecting his face back at him with the flat fidelity of a mirror. He stepped back without deciding to. His heart was doing something arrhythmic and stupid.

"Okay," he said aloud, to nobody, because saying things aloud was how he kept them from getting larger. "Okay. Spring feed. Old farms have them. Water table shifted. There are explanations."

There were explanations for everything. That was the religion of documentation: every strange thing was just an unexplained thing, waiting for its file. He had built a career on it. Rats in a wall were not a haunting; they were a food source and an entry gap. He photographed everything because photographs did not flinch, and because a thing written down stopped being able to grow.

He set up the tripod.

* * *

He measured the well the way he measured everything — twice, then once more to be sure. A fiberglass tape, a plumb weight, a chalk marker on the stone rim so each reading came off the same reference point. 9'2" from rim to surface at 4:17 p.m. He logged it in the field book under the date, under the address, under the single word he'd started using for this job instead of *client*: SUBJECT.

Then he drove home, ate standing up over the sink, and did what he always did after a job. He reviewed the photos.

The well photos were fine. Good, even. The light had been low and gray and the black water had come out looking like poured ink, the fieldstone ring sharp around it. He scrolled through them at his kitchen table, cataloguing, filing, naming files in his flat system — MB_WELL_001 through MB_WELL_014 — and he was on the eleventh frame when he stopped scrolling.

There were faces in the water.

Not reflections. He knew reflections; he'd shot enough glass and chrome to know how they behaved, how they smeared and doubled and obeyed the geometry of whatever stood above them. These faces did not behave. They were arranged across the surface of the water like lily pads, dozens of them, close-packed, all oriented upward, all looking at the camera. Some were large. Some were small. One, near the left edge of the frame, was unmistakably a child's, and it was smiling.

He zoomed in until the pixels broke apart. The smile held.

He went back through the sequence. Frame seven: three faces, indistinct, like people seen through frosted glass. Frame nine: maybe eight. Frame eleven: dozens. Frame fourteen, the last one he'd taken before packing up: the entire surface of the well, edge to edge, crowded with faces pressed together like commuters at a door, and in the center of them, larger than the others, a woman's face with her mouth open — not screaming, he decided, though he couldn't have said how he knew. *Saying.* Saying something to him, or asking him something, patient as a teller at a window while he made up his mind.

Theo put the camera down. Picked it up. Looked again. The faces were still there. Photographs did not flinch. That was the whole point of photographs.

He thought about calling someone. His mother, Ruth, anyone, to say *come look at this, tell me what you see,* and even as he composed the sentence in his head he heard how it sounded, heard the exact tone his mother would use, the careful voice reserved for conversations about Uncle Dale and the year he'd spent medicated. So he didn't call. He opened his field book instead and wrote, in his neatest hand: *Surface anomaly in frames 7–14. Count increasing with exposure time. Possible double-exposure / lens artifact. Re-shoot tomorrow, controlled conditions.*

Possible lens artifact. He read the sentence back and felt the paper accept it the way a court accepts perjury.

* * *

At 6:40 the next morning he was back at the well with fresh batteries, a level, and a second camera as a control. Same tripod position, marked with spray paint on the stones. Same settings. Before shooting, he measured.

8'1" from rim to surface.

A foot overnight. Thirteen inches, if you counted the way he counted, which was exactly. He stood there holding the tape and doing the arithmetic no amount of procedure could make friendly. At a foot a night, the well would reach the lip in eight days. Wells did not rise a foot a night. Wells did not rise at all in October, in a drought year, in a valley where the water table sat at sixty-one feet. This water was not coming from the ground. It was coming from somewhere else, or from some *when*, or it was not coming but *returning*, and he hated himself for the word even as he wrote it down.

He shot the full sequence again. Controlled conditions, both cameras, timestamps logged. Then he made himself do the thing he'd driven out here to do, the thing he'd been circling since yesterday: he dropped something into the well.

It was a practice he half-invented and half-remembered from some documentary about rescue divers — a weighted line, a float, a way to gauge depth and current. He tied a cork bobber four feet above a one-ounce sinker and lowered it over the side. The line paid out smooth and slow. Four feet. Six. Eight. Then the bobber touched the surface and lay there, and Theo crouched at the rim watching it, and the water did not ripple.

The bobber sat on the black surface like a button on a coat. No rings spread from it. No drift. The water held it the way a hand holds something it has decided to keep.

And then, slowly, without any wind he could feel on his face, the bobber began to move. Not sinking. Moving *sideways*, in a slow deliberate arc, tracing the circumference of the well shaft, around and around, like something below taking a measurement of its own. Theo watched it complete three full circuits. On the fourth, it stopped directly beneath him, and stayed there, and he could feel it staying there, the way you can feel a dog staring at the back of your neck.

He reeled the line up. The cork came out dry.

* * *

Things floated up on the third day.

He'd begun keeping vigil now — a folding chair, a thermos, the patience of his trade. Pest work teaches you to wait. You sit with a flashlight on a beam joist for two hours because the rats will not run while you breathe loud, and eventually your breathing becomes part of the house and the truth comes out. So he sat by the well through the afternoon, photographing at intervals, logging the rise: 7'3", 6'10", 6'5". And at dusk, as the light went copper and long, the surface changed texture.

Objects rose in it the way bubbles rise — unhurried, vertical, inevitable. He saw the first one break the surface and hang there and his mind refused it for a full three seconds: a button. Bone-colored, four holes, the kind sewn on a good winter coat. It floated, turned slowly, and sank again. Then another: a brass curtain ring. Then a rectangle of pale cardstock, rising corner-first, and Theo was on his knees at the rim with his sleeve dipped in the water before he'd decided to move, fishing it out.

It was a photograph corner. Literally the corner of a photograph, torn off along a ragged diagonal, showing a wedge of old kitchen wallpaper and the edge of a wooden chair back. The paper was wet but not degraded, not pulpy, as if it had spent fifty years underwater and fifty minutes. On the back, in pencil, in handwriting he knew — he'd seen it on every recipe card and birthday card of his childhood — his grandmother's writing:

*Ivy, age 9, kitchen, June*

Theo sat back on the stones with the scrap in his hand and the cold coming up out of the well through his knees, and he understood that he was not investigating an infestation anymore. Whatever ledger his grandmother had kept, whatever payments those neat rows of droppings had represented, the account was open again. The well was returning collateral. And banks only call in collateral when they mean to take the whole loan back.

Something clacked softly against the stone below him. He looked down. Teeth. A scatter of small teeth, molars mostly, child-sized, riding the surface in a loose constellation, turning gently, catching the last light. Around them, fainter now in the dusk, the faces had come back. All of them looking up. All of them patient. And beneath the teeth, deeper than the camera could reach, something moved that was not a face — a shape the size of the shaft itself, unfolding, unhurried, like a hand opening after a very long time closed.

Theo took the photograph anyway. His hands were steady, which frightened him more than shaking would have. Documentation was his prayer, and you do not stop praying because God turns out to be looking back.

In the frame, later, alone in his apartment with all the lights on: the surface of the well, crowded edge to edge with faces. And every single one of them, he could see now, at full resolution, at pixel-breaking magnification — every face in the well was Ivy's.

Nine years old. Fifty years drowned. Smiling up at him with her mouth open, saying something, asking something, patient as a teller at a window.

He got Ruth's number up on his phone and stared at it for a long time. Then he typed the only question he could form: *Who was Ivy?*

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared again.

Ruth's reply came at 11:52 p.m., a single line, and Theo read it standing at his window with the photo scrap drying on his table behind him:

*Don't say that name near the house. I'll come tomorrow. Bring tea. Don't bring the photos.*
