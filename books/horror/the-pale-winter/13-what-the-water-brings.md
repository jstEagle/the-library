## 13. What the Water Brings

The phone rang at 3:14 in the morning, and I knew before I picked it up that it was her.

Not because the caller ID said anything — it said UNKNOWN, the way it always does when a line has been dead for eleven months. Not because I'm a superstitious man. I photograph everything, I document everything, and documentation is just superstition with better lighting. No. I knew because of the sound underneath the ring. A wet sound. Like something being pulled slowly out of mud.

I sat on the edge of my bed in my apartment forty miles from Marlow Bend and I watched the phone ring six times, seven times, and I told myself: answer it or don't, but either way you'll remember.

I answered.

Static first. Then breathing — slow, unhurried, the breathing of someone who has all night and knows it. Then my grandmother's voice, thin as paper held up to a lamp.

"Theo. The water's coming up faster than I thought it would."

"Grandma." My own voice came out flat, procedural. That's what training does for you. "You died in March."

"I know what I did," she said, and there was something in it I'd never heard in life — not guilt exactly, but the shape guilt leaves behind, like a dent in a mattress after the body's gone. "That's why I'm calling. Listen to me. The ledger was only half of it. The other half is—"

The line filled with water. Not static — actual water, gurgling, close, as if the mouthpiece were submerged. And under the water, faint but unmistakable, a child laughing.

Then dial tone.

I sat there holding the phone until the sun came up. I didn't sleep. I made coffee at five and drank it standing at the window, and I wrote down the time of the call in my case notebook because that's what I do, that's who I am, and my hand only shook a little.

Under the entry I wrote: *She sounded afraid.*

My grandmother had never been afraid of anything in her life. She'd buried two husbands, outlived every one of her siblings, shot a rabid fox off her own porch with a .22 when she was seventy-one. Whatever was rising in that well had scared her enough to come back and say so.

* * *

I drove out to Marlow Bend on Thursday with the truck loaded like an expedition: camera gear, the ledgers, Ruth's photocopied instructions, a pry bar, rope, and — though I didn't let myself think too hard about why — a change of clothes and a sleeping bag. If the well was going to brim, I wasn't going home between shifts anymore. You don't leave a job half-documented. You especially don't leave this one.

The farmhouse looked wrong from the road. Not damaged — *rearranged*. The porch light was on. Nobody had lived there since March, and the porch light was on, burning steady in the gray morning like it had never been off.

Inside, the house had opinions about me.

The kitchen table had moved. It stood now in the exact center of the room, squared to the walls with a precision no farmer ever used, and around it were four chairs. Four. The extra chair — Ivy's chair, the one that haunted every photograph — was gone from its corner by the pantry door.

For one stupid second I felt relief. Then I saw the dust.

There was a clean rectangle on the floor where the chair had stood, sharp-edged, like the chair had been lifted straight up through the ceiling of dust. And on the seat of every remaining chair, arranged dead center: a small pile of mouse droppings. Three each. Neat as communion wafers.

"They're counting down," I said aloud, to nobody, because saying things aloud is how I keep from screaming. Three days. Or three visits. Or three of something.

I photographed the table. I photographed the clean rectangle. In the viewfinder, the rectangle wasn't empty — there was a pale suggestion of wood grain, a chair-shaped absence that the camera insisted on rendering as a chair-shaped presence. I took twelve frames. All twelve showed the same thing. The chair was coming back into focus the way a swimmer comes up through dark water: gradually, and from below.

* * *

The laughter started that afternoon.

I was in the pantry, cataloguing the tally wall behind the broken padlock — I'd brought a proper light rig this time, and the marks went back further than I'd mapped, decades further, some of them tiny and high up near the ceiling, made by a child reaching as far as she could reach — when I heard it through the wall. A girl laughing. Not recorded laughter, not memory-laughter. Live laughter, muffled and delighted, the laugh of a nine-year-old finding something funny in the space between the studs.

I put my palm flat against the plaster. Warm. The wall was warm, in a house with no heat, in October, in a room where my breath fogged.

"Hello?" I said. God help me, I said hello to a wall.

The laughter stopped. Then, very close to my palm, on the other side of half an inch of plaster, something pressed back. Small. A child's hand, maybe. Or a child's hand remembered by a thing that had been underwater for fifty years and was doing its best.

I didn't pull away. I want that on the record. I kept my hand there until the pressure faded, and then I wrote in my notebook: *She's not trying to scare me. She's trying to be heard.* And below that, because grief is honest even when everything else isn't: *Fifty years in the dark and the first thing she does is laugh.*

* * *

Friday morning there was a wet handprint on the bathroom mirror.

Small. Left hand. Fingers slightly spread, the way a child presses a hand to glass to prove she's real. The water that made it hadn't dripped — the print was crisp, deliberate, placed. I checked every mirror in the house. Hall mirror: handprint. The old vanity mirror in what had been Grandma's room: handprint. The little round mirror in the hall closet that hadn't hung on a nail in twenty years and lay face-up in a box: handprint, on the glass, under the dust, undisturbed.

Every reflective surface. Including, when I got back to my apartment Sunday night, my own bathroom mirror, forty miles away. Including the rearview of my truck, which I wiped twice and which reappeared once, small and patient, while I watched.

You can't quarantine against a thing that's already everywhere. I understood that standing in my own bathroom with a paper towel in my hand, understanding finally that this was never going to be a job I finished and filed. This was a debt collection, and the collector had found me at work and at home, and the only question left was whether I'd pay or be paid from.

* * *

Ruth called Saturday, and her voice was wrong in a way I'd learned to hear.

"Theo." A breath. "Carol's at the farm."

"What? Why?"

"She wouldn't tell me. She called me last night asking about the ledger — asking if there was anything in it about *substitutes*. About whether the tending could be done with a different name." Ruth's voice cracked down the middle. "Theo, she's been reading your notes. You left copies. She thinks if the well wants a Brightwater, it'll take whichever Brightwater is offered."

The floor of my stomach dropped away. "Where is Carol now?"

"At the farm. She drove out this morning. She took—" Ruth stopped. Took a breath that sounded like it cost her. "She took the dog's collar. Biscuit's collar. From the box in her attic."

I was already moving. Keys, camera, the ledger, out the door. Forty miles of highway with Ruth's voice still in my ear telling me the last thing she'd said to our sister, which was: *Carol, whatever you're planning, the well doesn't take substitutes, it takes thieves,* and Carol hanging up on her.

* * *

I'll set down what happened at the farm exactly as it happened, because it matters, and because I've decided that lying in this notebook is how houses like this get built in the first place.

Carol's car was in the yard. The well was maybe eight feet below its lip — I could see the water turning in the stone throat, slow, thoughtful, like something rolling over in sleep. And Carol was standing at the edge with Biscuit's red leather collar in both hands, saying words she'd copied out of the second ledger in her own careful handwriting, and the well was listening.

"Biscuit," she was saying. Her voice had the bright, brittle shine of a woman talking herself into something. "He was ours. He was family. Take him instead. Take the dog, take the—"

"Biscuit's been dead thirty years," I said, coming across the yard fast. "Carol. Stop."

She turned and her face stopped me cold. It wasn't crazy. That's the terrible part. It was calm, resolved, the face of a woman who has done arithmetic and gotten an answer she can live with. "It wants a name," she said. "Fine. It can have a name. Any name but yours, Theo. I am not losing anyone else to that hole."

"It's not a hole, it's a *creditor*, and you don't pay a creditor with somebody else's money—"

And that's when the dog came around the side of the barn.

I have documented pest infestations in hospitals, in slaughterhouses, in a grain elevator where a man died in '09 and nobody found him for a week. I have a professional relationship with the grotesque. Nothing in my career prepared me for Biscuit.

He was a border collie, and he had been drowned, and he was walking. His coat hung in wet ropes, streaming water that fell and fell and never seemed to make the ground any wetter. His eyes were the flat silver of the well surface. He moved stiffly, joints full of pond, head low, tail — and this is the detail I will carry to my grave — tail wagging. Slowly. Gladly. He saw Carol and his whole ruined body lit up with joy, because he was a good dog, and good dogs love their people past the end of everything.

Carol made a sound I have never heard a human being make. Not a scream. Something older than screaming.

"Biscuit," she whispered, and the dog came to her, dripping, and pushed his cold wet head against her hands, and she knelt down in the yard and held her childhood dog — drowned fifty years ago in that same well, I realized, drowned the summer before they gave them Ivy, a practice offering, a first payment, a thing the ledger would list under *LIVESTOCK, LOST* — and she held him and sobbed into his sodden neck while he wagged and wagged and leaked well-water onto her knees.

"He came back," she wept. "Theo, he came back, he's here—"

"Yeah," I said quietly. "Everything given comes back. That's the warning, Carol. That's the whole warning. When the well brims, everything they fed it comes home at once." I crouched down level with her, with the dog, with the collar dangling useless from her fist. "You didn't call him up. You called *all of it* up. Every payment. Fifty years of payments. And most of what's down there isn't a dog."

Biscuit turned his silver eyes on me. There was nothing in them but dog. That was somehow worse.

The wind came across the yard and the farmhouse groaned — a long structural complaint, timber flexing, like the house was shifting its weight. Upstairs, in the room that hadn't existed in March, a curtain moved in a closed window.

And from inside the walls, clear as a bell, sweet as anything: a child laughing.

Carol's head came up. She stared at the house. At the well. At the dog dying again by inches in her arms, dissolving even as she held him, his edges going soft, his wag slowing, the water taking him back down into whatever water holds.

"No," she said. "No, no, no—" clutching at a body that was becoming suggestion, becoming memory, becoming the shape of a loss she'd already grieved once and would now grieve forever, twice-used grief, and the well took him back without ceremony, without malice, the way a bank repossesses a car.

The yard was quiet. The collar lay in the grass.

Carol looked at me, and everything resolved in her face had dissolved with the dog, and what was left underneath was nine years old and terrified.

"Theo," she said. "What's coming up?"

I looked at the well. Eight feet from the lip. Five days, maybe less. The water turned in the dark and I swear to God it turned *toward us*, attentive, expectant, a crowd gathering before doors open.

"Everything," I said. "Everything they ever gave it. Starting with a girl named Ivy."

Behind us, in the farmhouse, every mirror in the house fogged over at once — I saw it happen through the kitchen window, glass blooming white, room by room — and in each one, when the fog cleared, a small wet handprint, waiting.

And upstairs, in the room that shouldn't exist, something began, softly, patiently, to hum a lullaby I'd never heard but Carol clearly had, because she went white to the lips and said, in a voice fifty years younger:

"That's the song Mama sang to Ivy."
