## 6. The Choir Roster

My grandmother made tea the way she did everything else: efficiently, and with an air of having done it better in the past.

The kitchen of her house on the edge of Bodmin hadn't changed since I was nine. Same floral wallpaper gone amber with grease, same clock that ticked a half-second behind itself so it always sounded like it was catching up. Edith Ravensworth sat across from me with her hands folded around her cup like a woman holding a small animal still, and she watched me over the rim with eyes that had lost none of their arithmetic.

"You've got his chin," she said. "Your grandfather's. He used to set it like that when he was about to say something stupid."

"I'm not here about chins."

"No," she said. "You're here about a bell."

I'd rehearsed this on the drive down. Three versions, actually — the gentle one, the professional one, and the one where I laid the 1974 police report flat on the table between us and said nothing at all until she filled the silence. In the event I didn't need any of them, because she got there first.

"Clapper," she said. "Not bell. People always say bell. The bell went to the foundry in 'eleven and good riddance. What I took was the clapper out of the reliquary niche. Different thing entirely." She sipped her tea. "Smaller. Worth more."

"You're not going to deny it."

"Why would I? You drove four hours. Denying it would be rude."

There it was — the family trait, the one my mother had spent forty years trying to sand off herself and then off me. A kind of blunt courtesy that treated truth as a form of politeness and everything softer as a waste of time. I'd inherited the chin and apparently the whole jaw.

"The police report doesn't name you," I said. "But the investigating officer was a Ravensworth."

"My brother-in-law. He knew. He chose not to know harder than most men can manage." She set her cup down. "He was a good man in the way that matters least."

* * *

I want to be fair to her, which is not something I expected to feel sitting in that kitchen. Fairness requires context, and context is what she gave me, in pieces, between refills, while the light through the window went from grey to the colour of old dishwater.

She'd been twenty-six in 1974. Widowed at twenty-four — my grandfather, a trawlerman, taken by weather that the inquiry called freak and the harbourmen called routine. She had my mother, aged two, and eleven pounds in a biscuit tin, and a landlord in St Austell who wanted arrears by Michaelmas.

"I wasn't sentimental," she said. "Sentimentality is what people call greed when it's aimed at the wrong objects. I saw a listing — church effects, decommissioned reliquary, bronze, pre-Reformation. Bronze is weight. Weight is money. I went to Hollowmere to look at it."

"And you took it."

"And I took it." No flinch. "The crypt door was unlocked. The niche was behind a grille with a padlock older than the padlock's own iron. I fed us for six years on that clapper and two more jobs after. Your mother ate meat twice a week. I want that understood before anything else gets understood."

"Understood," I said, and meant it, and hated meaning it.

"But," she said, and here her voice changed — not softened, exactly. Leveled. The way water levels. "You said there were prints. In flour, you said on the phone. Small ones."

"Child-sized. Adult proportions, child scale. Very fine ridges. Deliberate placement."

Edith nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed a delivery date.

"Then they're still counting," she said. "Five."

* * *

Here is where I have to slow down, because what she told me next rearranged the furniture of everything I'd driven down there to do, and I have never fully moved it back.

In October of 1974 — five weeks after the clapper left Hollowmere — the choir of St Brannoc's held its Thursday practice. Five boys, ages nine through thirteen. Sons of the verger, the butcher, the schoolmistress, and two farm families whose names I won't write down because Edith wrote them down for me instead, in fountain pen, on the back of a 1989 pawn ticket, and I have that paper now and it feels less like evidence than like a debt instrument.

That evening the vestry flooded. Not the church — the vestry and the undercroft below it, where the boys' coats and boots were kept, and where, when the water came up fast and cold out of the old spring channel, the five of them went to fetch their things because that's what you do, you go and get your coat, and the door at the top of the steps was barred.

From the outside.

"They said it was the spring," Edith said. "An unusual rise. Autumn rains. The coroner recorded misadventure. But the spring channel had been dry since the war — I checked, later, when checking was all I had left to do. And a bar doesn't fall across a door. A bar is placed."

"Who placed it?"

"That," she said, "is the question I paid fifty years not to answer."

I looked at her. She looked back, and for the first time since I'd arrived there was something in her face that wasn't arithmetic. It was older and worse. It was bookkeeping.

"Think it through, girl. I steal a piece of bronze worth three hundred pounds. Within five weeks, five children are dead in the same building. The village buries them quietly, records an accident, and never once — never once in fifty years — comes looking for the thief, though half the parish knew the crypt lock was broken and the niche empty. Why does a village swallow a thing like that?"

"Because—"

"Because grief needs a shape," she said, "and I gave them one. A theft. Something with a name and a face and a price tag. If the clapper was stolen, then the drowning was a consequence, and consequences can be lived with. But if the clapper was only ever the excuse—" She stopped. Her hands were very still around the empty cup. "A village doesn't bar a door over three hundred pounds of bronze, Yara. Whatever else that door was for, it wasn't me."

* * *

I don't remember leaving the table. I remember standing at her window looking out at a garden gone to seed, holding my own elbows, doing the thing I do — the professional thing, the rationalist thing — which is to take a horrifying proposition and reduce it to checkable components.

Component one: five boys drowned in 1974. Checkable. Component two: the flood was engineered or at minimum exploited. Less checkable, but Edith had spent decades assembling documents, and she pushed a folder across the table that contained a surveyor's report on the spring channel (dry, 1968), a letter from the verger to the diocese asking about "the matter of the vestry door" and receiving no reply, and a photograph of the funeral — five small coffins, and a line of adults standing at the graveside whose faces, even in grainy black and white, even at fifty years' distance, wore expressions I recognized.

Relief wears badly on people. It shows around the mouth.

Component three: the fingerprints. This was the one I could test, and testing it was the only thing keeping me upright, so I asked Edith for paper and she gave me the back of another pawn ticket — the woman measured her life in pawn tickets — and I drew the print pattern from memory. Whorl placement, ridge count, the proportions I'd measured twice in the bakery with calipers and photographed with a ruler alongside for scale.

Small hands. Adult architecture. Five sets of minor variations, which I'd noted at the time and filed under *multiple individuals, likely juveniles, unlikely*.

I drew them. Edith looked at them for a long moment.

"Thomas Pellow," she said, touching the first. "He was the youngest. Nine. He had a gap in his teeth you could post a letter through, and he sang the descant like it was being dragged out of him, which I suppose it was." Her finger moved. "The Werry brothers. Then Daniel Hosken — the schoolmistress's boy, the one who could read music before he could read words. And Peter Trenoweth, the verger's son, who should have been watching them and was found nearest the door."

She named them the way you name things you've named many times alone.

And I stood there with my caliper measurements and my ruler photographs and my professional detachment, and I understood that the prints in Mrs. Penhale's flour weren't an infestation. They were a roster. Five children, pressing their hands into whatever soft surface would hold them, week after week, decade after decade, saying the only thing they had left to say:

*We were here. We are here. Count us.*

I am not a woman who cries easily and I didn't cry then. What I did was worse. I felt the whole structure of my skepticism — the load-bearing wall of it, the thing I'd built my life inside — settle half an inch, the way a house settles, with a sound too low to hear and cracks appearing afterward in places you'd sworn were sound.

* * *

"There's more," Edith said, when I came back to the table. There was always more. Horror, I've learned, is not a wave; it's a staircase, and someone polite keeps indicating the next flight down.

"After the funerals, the village changed. Not visibly. Visibly it got politer, which is how these things look from outside. But the vicarage began paying an allowance to each of the five families — discretionary funds, 'parish benevolence,' unrecorded. The butcher's widow got her rent covered for thirty years. The schoolmistress was given early retirement on full pension at fifty-one. Nobody asked why. Everybody knew why. It wasn't compensation." She tapped the folder. "It was retainer."

"A retainer for what?"

"For silence. For the story. For agreeing, every single year, at every harvest festival, to stand in that churchyard and sing the harvest hymn with five voices missing and not one person mentioning it." She leaned forward. "You think those prints started recently? They've been finding them since 1975. In flour, in ash, in the wax on the windowsills. Every few years, some new vicar decides it's rats, or vandals, or a trick of damp, and hires someone, and the someone finds nothing, and leaves, and the village breathes out. You're not the first pest controller, Yara. You're the ninth."

"Ninth," I said.

"The other eight found nothing because they looked for something alive." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it crueler. "You'll find nothing too, unless you stop looking for what's alive and start looking for what's owed."

* * *

I stayed another hour. I don't fully remember what we talked about — small things, my mother, whether the boiler in my van was fixed. Two women clearing a table after a large meal, pretending the meal had been ordinary.

At the door, she stopped me with one hand on my sleeve. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be.

"The clapper," she said. "You'll want to know where it is. Box in the parlour — blue velvet, shaped to fit. Go and look."

I went and looked. The box sat on the parlour mantelpiece between a photograph of my grandfather and a horse brass, and when I opened it, the velvet lining held a clapper-shaped absence, worn shiny at the edges from fifty years of a hand opening and closing it.

"It's not here," I called.

"No," Edith said from the doorway, and there was something almost like apology in it, the first she'd offered. "Sold it in '89, to a dealer in Penzance. Vane, his name was. Aldous Vane." She paused. "Funny thing. The year I sold it — within the month — the prints stopped for eleven years. Longest quiet they've ever had. Then he put it in a glass case somewhere, and they started again, and they've been getting closer together ever since."

"Closer together," I repeated.

"Like footsteps coming downstairs," my grandmother said. "Or a congregation arriving."

I drove back toward Hollowmere in the dark with the pawn tickets in my jacket pocket and the folder on the passenger seat, and somewhere past Lostwithiel I caught myself humming — three notes, rising, a phrase I didn't know and couldn't place, and I shut my mouth so hard I bit my cheek.

When I let myself into the bakery loft after midnight, I didn't turn the light on straight away. When I did, I stood very still in the doorway.

The handprints had climbed in my absence. All night, apparently, patient as tide marks, they had come up off the sacks and along the rafters, dozens of them now, small and fine and deliberate, ascending toward the loft where I slept — and stopping just short of the ladder's top rung, arranged in a neat row, five abreast.

Waiting for me to join them.
