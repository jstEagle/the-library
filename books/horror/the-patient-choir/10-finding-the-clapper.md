## 10. Finding the Clapper

The box was the cruelest thing in Edith Ravensworth's house, and Edith's house contained a taxidermied pike wearing what appeared to be a tiny fez.

I'd driven back from Hollowmere on Thursday morning with the hymnal riding shotgun and a plan so simple it embarrassed me: get the clapper, return the clapper, go home, bill the bakery for the rodent job I hadn't done, never think about flour again. Four days until the harvest moon set on Sunday night. Plenty of time for one errand.

The box sat where she'd said it would, third shelf of the oak cabinet in her front room, behind a row of commemorative plates from towns she'd never visited. It was walnut, maybe eight inches long, hinged, lined inside with wine-dark velvet that had been cut and fitted around something long and heavy and tapered. The velvet held the shape of it perfectly — a negative space worn smooth by fifty years of nothing.

An absence, upholstered.

"Empty," I said, because saying it out loud might make it less true.

Edith didn't look up from her crossword. She was ninety-one and did the Telegraph's cryptic in biro, which told you most of what you needed to know about her. "Course it's empty. You don't keep a thing like that in the house."

"You kept the *box*."

"The box is discreet." She filled in seven across without pausing. "A thing's absence is much easier to explain than a thing."

I sat down across from her. The pike watched us both with its glass eye, fez slightly askew, as if it too had questions about this family. "Where is it, Gran?"

"Sold it." She said it the way you'd say *took the bins out*. "1989. March, I want to say. Cold spring."

My stomach performed a slow, professional drop, the kind I usually only felt when I lifted a floorboard and found something with more legs than the invoice covered. "You sold it. In 1989. Fifteen years after five boys drowned."

"Fifteen years after five boys drowned and nobody came knocking," she corrected. "I'd sat on it fifteen years, Yara. Sat on it through two moves and your grandfather's funeral and that awful winter when the pipes went. A thing under the bed stops being a memorial and starts being clutter. Clutter with a price tag, if you're lucky."

"What did you get?"

She named the figure. It was insultingly small. Less than my average month, less than the van was worth, less than the bakery job. She caught my expression and had the decency to look mildly defensive about it.

"It was 1989 money," she said. "And I wasn't in a position to haggle. Your mother had just — well. There were bills."

*Your mother had just.* The sentence trailed off into the same territory it always did: the territory where my mother's life fell apart and Edith's ledgers got thinner and nobody in this family ever once said the word *shame* out loud where the children could hear it. I fed us. That was her whole defense, delivered four days ago like a closing argument. And the worst part, the part I'd been chewing on all week, was that it was true. Somewhere in 1974 there had been a hungry child — my mother — and a woman with a velvet box and no other options, and the arithmetic of survival doesn't care what it subtracts.

It also doesn't care what it drowns. Five boys had done the subtracting for her.

"Who bought it?" I asked.

"A dealer. Penzance way." She finally looked up, and her eyes were clear and blue and utterly without remorse, which I suppose is its own kind of honesty. "Fellow called Merrick. Had a shop off the top of Chapel Street, antiques and maritime bits. He knew what it was — or thought he did. Paid for reliquary provenance, got church scrap metal. I didn't correct him."

"Gran. I need everything. Dates, receipts, the shop address, anything."

"Why?"

"Because there are fingerprints in flour sacks in Hollowmere shaped like children's hands, and a bell that rings without existing, and on Sunday night they take a voice instead of their clapper back, and everyone in that village has quietly agreed the voice should be mine." I heard my own voice climb and made myself stop, breathe, lower it. "So. Why do you think?"

Edith considered me for a long moment. Then she put down her biro, which from her was the equivalent of standing at attention, and said, "Attic. Green suitcase. Everything I kept, I kept."

* * *

Everything she kept fit in one green suitcase, which is either a testament to tidiness or an indictment, depending on how you feel about a woman who could reduce five deaths to a shoebox of paper. I carried it down myself — she offered to help and I declined with more sharpness than she deserved, maybe, but my hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

Inside: pawn tickets, yellowed and brittle. Bank statements from accounts I'd never known existed. A photograph of St. Brannoc's choir, 1973, six boys in cassocks squinting into the sun, one of them grinning like the sun owed him money. And a folder of correspondence, thin as a communion wafer, that traced the clapper's journey after it left her hands.

Merrick's Antiques, Penzance. Purchase receipt, March 1989, item described as *reliquary bell-clapper, bronze, probable 14th c., Cornish provenance*. Then a gap. Then, 1994, a letter on heavier stock — auction house letterhead, Sotheby's-adjacent, the kind of firm that deals in objects whose value depends on other people's desperation. Merrick had consigned it. It hadn't sold; the provenance was, as Edith had predicted, too thin for the big rooms. It passed to a private collector through a dealer-to-dealer sale, and the trail went quiet.

Except collectors leave footprints. Not in flour — in paperwork. Insurance schedules, exhibition loans, the quiet vanity of men who own things and need other people to know it.

"Merrick's shop," I said. "Still there?"

"Haven't been to Penzance since your grandfather died." She paused. "He liked it there. We honeymooned in Marazion. He said the sea sounded like applause." She said it flatly, but her hand rested on the photograph of the choir boys a moment longer than it needed to, and I decided not to ask, because some doors you don't open with four days left on the clock.

I took the folder. She let me. At the door she said, "Yara," and I turned, braced for a blessing or a warning or whatever passes between us for affection.

"If you give it back," she said, "don't sing anything."

* * *

Penzance took three hours and cost me a tank of diesel and most of my composure. Merrick's Antiques was still there in the sense that a shell is still there — the window now full of mass-produced lighthouse ornaments and fudge tins, a sign in the door reading OPEN MOST DAYS. The man behind the counter was sixty-ish, sun-cured, and introduced himself as Merrick's nephew, running the place since Uncle died.

"Yeah, I know the piece," he said, when I described it. I'd rehearsed a cover story — researcher, church historian — and abandoned it somewhere on the A30, because lying to this man felt like lying to a tide chart. "Uncle sold it on in '94. Consignment fell through, and then a private buyer came sniffing. Cash. Uncle was cash-shaped, God rest him."

"A name?"

"He wouldn't have written it down even if he'd had it." The nephew scratched his jaw. "But the buyer used a solicitor in Truro for the transfer. Old-fashioned type. And there was an insurance valuation done — Uncle kept a copy, thought it proved he'd got a good price. Hang on."

He rummaged in a filing cabinet that predated decimal currency and produced a carbon copy of a valuation sheet. The clapper, photographed against grey felt, looking exactly like what it was: a heavy bronze rod with a mounting collar and a history. Valued at a sum that made Edith's 1989 figure look like pocket lint. And at the bottom, in the field marked *Owner/Insured*, a name.

**A. VANE**

Below it, an address in London, and below that, in the solicitor's careful hand: *per instruction, correspondence via agent, Truro.*

"I've seen him," the nephew said, following my eyes. "Once. Came down years back, buying nautical stuff. Charming as anything. Bought a ship's bell off Uncle — paid over the odds, made a little speech about how bells shouldn't be silent." He laughed. "Odd sort of hobby, bells. But rich men collect stranger."

I thanked him, bought a tin of fudge I didn't want because it seemed rude not to, and sat in the van outside with my phone and the valuation sheet and the specific dread of a woman about to negotiate with someone who has all the leverage.

* * *

Aldous Vane answered on the second ring, which told me either he was expecting trouble or he was the kind of man who treated every call as potentially interesting.

"Vane."

I gave him the truth, or a load-bearing portion of it. My name. The fact that I was calling about a bronze reliquary clapper purchased in 1994 through a Truro solicitor. I heard the pause on the line — small, but a pause — and knew I'd guessed right.

"That's a very specific enquiry, Miss...?"

"Ravensworth. Yara Ravensworth."

Another pause, longer. People who deal in rare objects develop instincts for provenance, and apparently my surname rang some dusty bell of his own. Maybe he'd read the 1974 police report. Maybe he collected stories along with objects. Either way, when he spoke again his voice had acquired the warm, unhurried texture of a man settling into a good chair.

"Miss Ravensworth. How curious. May I ask why you want it?"

"It belongs to a church in Cornwall. I'm arranging its return."

"A gift?"

"A restitution."

"Mm." I could practically hear him smiling. "Restitution. You know, I bought that piece because of what it *wasn't*. Every collector chases the famous things — the crowns, the crosses, the bells themselves. But a clapper, Miss Ravensworth. The part that does the work. The part everyone forgets until the silence starts. I mounted it in my study on a stand of Cornish elm. Guests ask about it constantly. I tell them it's the tongue of a bell that no longer exists, and watch them try to work out whether that makes it more or less valuable." A soft laugh. "They can never decide. Can you?"

"No," I said. "I can tell you what it costs. Five boys drowned waiting for it, and every dusk for a hundred and thirteen years a bell that was melted down in 1911 rings over a village that would rather die politely than talk about it. That's the valuation. What's your price?"

Silence. Long enough that I checked the screen to see if the call had dropped.

Then Vane laughed — genuinely, delightedly, the laugh of a man who has just been handed something better than the object itself. "Oh, you're *wonderful*. Do you know how many people would have invented a charity at that point? A heritage trust, a diocesan fund? You just — said it. Straight out." He exhaled happily. "No, Miss Ravensworth, I won't sell it. Selling implies the transaction ends. I find I'm not ready for it to end."

My hand tightened on the phone hard enough to ache. "Name a number."

"There isn't one. But—" and here he let the word hang like a lure, "—there is an invitation. Bring me the story. The whole of it. The drowning, the theft, the bell that rings. I want to see it returned. I want to *witness* it. Arrange that, and you can walk out of my house with the clapper under your arm and my blessing besides."

"No."

"Then we're at an impasse, and the impasse is on your side of it, I'm afraid. Sunday, is it? Yes — harvest moon. I do keep track. Such a photogenic deadline." His voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was infinitely worse. "You have my sympathy, truly. These village affairs always end with someone paying a debt they didn't run up. Granddaughters, especially. It's practically a folk tradition."

"How do you know about granddaughters?"

Another of those pauses. Longer. When he answered, the charm was still there, but it had gone thin as the velvet in Edith's box, stretched over something I couldn't see the shape of yet.

"Because I've spent thirty years learning everything there is to know about that clapper, Miss Ravensworth. Its weight, its age, its composition — and its history. All of it. Even the parts the county archive hasn't digitised." A beat. "Even the parts the investigating officer chose not to write down."

The van suddenly felt very cold. "What parts?"

"Come and see. Saturday morning. I'll send the address." And then, brightly, before I could speak: "Do drive carefully on the coast road. It's treacherous this time of year. So many distractions."

He hung up.

I sat there in the lay-by with the fudge tin on the passenger seat and the sea doing its applause impression against the seawall, and I understood two things with total clarity. First: I was going to that house, because there was no version of Saturday night where I stood in a flooded crypt empty-handed while five dead boys hummed at me in harmony.

Second: Aldous Vane knew more about my family than I did, and a man like that doesn't learn things by accident. He learns them because he's been waiting.

The address arrived by text before I'd finished turning the key. A house outside Falmouth, gates, grounds, the works. Beneath it, a single further message:

*Bring the hymnal. I've always wanted to hear one sung from.*
