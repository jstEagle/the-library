## 13. The Village Decides

They were waiting for me at the lychgate.

I should have expected it. I'd been in Hollowmere four days, and in that time I'd learned exactly two things about the village: that its people could smile like a locked door, and that they moved as one organism when they thought no one was watching. Saturday evening, the sky the colour of a bruise going yellow at the edges, the harvest moon already a pale promise above the ridge — and there they stood, thirty-odd of them, arranged along the churchyard wall in a way that was too neat to be accidental and too loose to be called a crowd.

Mrs. Trelawney was at the front, of course. She had the air of a woman who had been appointed spokesperson by a committee that had never officially met.

I stopped about ten feet short, the clapper wrapped in my coat and cradled against my chest like something newborn and screaming. Because that's what it was doing now — screaming, in the way that a frequency screams, in the way that a wire under too much tension screams. It had been screaming since Penzance. It had screamed in my passenger seat, screamed in my rearview mirrors, screamed through the crash, and it was screaming now, and I had the sudden, lunatic thought that the villagers hadn't come out because of the moon or the deadline or me. They'd come out because they'd heard it too. The whole village had been listening to my car for the last forty miles.

"Evening, Miss Ravensworth," Trelawney said.

"Evening." I shifted the bundle higher against my chest. My arms were shaking. I'd been telling myself it was adrenaline. It was not entirely adrenaline. "If you're here to stop me, you're about four days too late to have saved yourselves the trouble."

"We're not here to stop you."

"No," I said. "I didn't think you were."

And that was the moment the politeness curdled. I'd seen it coming the way you see a storm front — not the rain itself but the light going wrong underneath it. The smiles stayed exactly where they were, fixed and pleasant, but something drained out from behind them, and what was left looking at me was not hostility. Hostility I could have worked with. Hostility has energy in it. This was relief. Thirty faces wearing the same expression I'd seen on the faces of people watching an ambulance go past for someone else's house.

Better her than any of us.

"You knew," I said. "All of you. You knew what I'd been doing this week, and you knew what tonight was, and none of you — not one — offered to walk in with me."

"Walk in with you?" A man near the back — I think his name was Penrose, the one who ran the garage — laughed, and the laugh had a wet, startled quality, like he hadn't meant to let it out. "It has to be you. That's not — that's not a choice anyone's making, love. It's the rule."

"The rule," I repeated. "Right. The rule written down where? Administered by whom? Because from where I'm standing, the rule looks a lot like thirty people deciding a stranger's grandmother stole something, so a stranger should pay for it."

"It was your grandmother who took it," someone said. A woman. I didn't see who.

"Yes," I said. "It was. And she's ninety-one, and she lives two hundred miles away, and she has never once in fifty years lost a night's sleep over it, which — believe me — I checked. So explain to me the inheritance law under which I'm liable."

Nobody explained. They just looked at me, and the looking was the explanation. The rule didn't need a legal basis. The rule needed only a body that wasn't theirs.

Trelawney came forward a step. Up close, in the last of the light, I could see how old she actually was — older than her voice, older than her posture, old enough to have been a young woman in 1974, old enough to have been there.

"You want me to say we're sorry," she said.

"I want you to say *anything*," I said. "I've been in this village four days. Do you know how many times someone said the boys' names to me? Not once. Five children drowned in your church and the village has spent fifty years not saying their names, and now here you all are, out in the cold, and I'd bet my van not one of you can produce all five names between you."

Silence. Somewhere beyond the wall, inside the churchyard, the humming had stopped. That was worse. The choir had been humming for four days without pause, a sound I'd stopped hearing the way you stop hearing a fridge, and its absence now was enormous — a held breath the size of a graveyard.

"Thomas Curnow," Trelawney said at last. Her voice had gone flat, recitational. "Peter Hosking. The Vercoe brothers, John and Michael. And little William Nance — he was only nine." She said it without looking at anyone, the way you recite a shopping list you've had memorized for decades and never once used. "There. Now you've heard them. Will that make it easier for you?"

"No," I said. "It makes it worse. It was supposed to make it worse. That's what it's *for*."

"Then you understand better than we do," she said, and there it was again — that relief, that terrible lightness in her face, the look of a woman setting down a weight she'd been carrying so long she'd forgotten it wasn't hers. Except it *was* hers. That was the whole obscenity of it. They had carried it, and then in 1974 they had handed it to my grandmother like a parcel at a door — *here, take our sin out of town, we'll pay the postage* — and Edith, hungry and proud and nineteen with a baby to feed, had taken the parcel, and the village had watched her go and had called itself clean ever since.

"We let her carry it," Trelawney said, as if reading my thoughts, or as if she'd rehearsed this. Maybe she had. Maybe they all had, for fifty years, in the way congregations rehearse a liturgy they no longer understand. "That was our sin, and we own it. But the debt—" she spread her hands, "—the debt isn't ours to pay anymore. It was paid forward. To you."

"Paid forward." I tasted the phrase and nearly spat. "That's not how debt works. That's not how *anything* works. You can't just—"

"You can," said Penrose quietly. "When the other party agrees to it. And they agreed, Miss Ravensworth. They've been agreeing all week. Who do you think put the prints in your flour? Who do you think followed you up the tower? They've been *introducing themselves*." He shook his head. "They know your blood. They've been waiting for your blood. We were just — we were the ones who called you, is all."

"The ones who called me." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "You hired a pest controller. You put in the request form that it was *rodents*. You knew — you all knew — and you filed a rodent complaint, because God forbid anyone in this village put anything in writing with an actual word in it."

"It had to be you that came," Trelawney said. "It couldn't be one of us. If one of us went down there, we'd go believing. Every soul in Hollowmere would go down those steps believing, and belief is what they feed on. That's the whole—" she stopped, and for a moment something moved behind her face that wasn't relief at all, something older and more frightened, and then it was packed away again. "Your grandmother's blood doesn't believe. It's the only blood that doesn't. That's not our cruelty, girl. That's the design of the thing."

"The design of the thing," I said. "Designed by whom?"

She didn't answer. None of them did. The moon was clearing the ridge now, one edge of it, white and enormous, and the light it threw down was already the wrong colour — too silver, too sharp, the light of a stage before the curtain.

I looked at them, all thirty of them, and I did something I'm not proud of, which is that I tried one more time.

"My mother is in Truro," I said. "I called her from the archive. I told her everything. You know what she said? She said, *don't go back there, Yara, those people will eat you and call it hospitality.* I told her she was being dramatic." I looked down the line of them. "She wasn't. She grew up here. She got out at nineteen and never came back, and I used to think that was her failure. Some daughter-of-the-village thing, some wound she wouldn't let close. It wasn't a wound. It was an *exit*."

"Then use it," said a voice — a young woman, holding a baby against her shoulder. She wasn't unkind. That was the worst part; she looked at me with genuine, aching kindness, and said: "Drive away. Nobody here will stop you. Nobody *can* stop you. That's the other half of the rule — it has to be willing, or it doesn't count. You could turn around right now and be gone by dark, and in four days they'd take one of us instead, and we'd all know it was because you chose yourself, and we'd live with it, because that's what we do. We live with things." She adjusted the baby. "But you'd live with it too. And you don't live with things. We've watched you all week. You're not built for it."

I hated her. I want to be honest about that — in the moment, with the moon coming up and the clapper screaming against my ribs, I hated her with a purity I haven't managed since, because she was right, and she knew she was right, and she'd done the arithmetic on me the same way the village had done the arithmetic on my grandmother, and the answer came out the same both times: this one will carry it.

"You could come with me," I said to her. "All of you. Thirty of you, five of them. You could walk in there tomorrow and sing the verse yourselves and end it tonight instead of—"

"End it," Penrose said, and now his voice cracked, and I saw for the first time that underneath the relief there was terror, had always been terror, fifty years deep of it. "You think it *ends*? You think the verse gets sung and everyone goes home? We don't know what happens. Nobody knows what happens. Fifty years and nobody's ever gotten as far as you're about to get. The only thing we know is what happens to the village if it *isn't* sung." He looked at the moon. "We know that. We've seen what it takes instead."

"What it took in '74," I said. "I read the file. Five boys, drowned in two feet of water, in a vestry with the door barred from the outside. And the coroner wrote *accidental drowning following flood*, and the village wrote *nothing*, and the only person in the whole parish who wrote down what actually happened was the investigating officer, and his name was Ravensworth, and then he was transferred, and then the file was sealed, and then everyone lived happily ever after for fifty years." I let that sit. "Your constable. My grandfather. He knew. He wrote it down and then he let it be buried, because the alternative was burying the village instead. So don't stand there and tell me the debt was paid forward. The debt was *laundered*. Twice. And now it's come due and you're all standing at the gate holding the door open like ushers."

Nobody spoke. The moon cleared the ridge entirely, and the churchyard went silver, and I could see them all properly now, every face, and I made myself look at each one — not because it helped, but because I wanted them to be seen doing this. Somebody should see it. If I went down those steps and didn't come back, I wanted it on the record, in a witness, that thirty people had watched and opened the gate and felt *glad*.

Trelawney stepped aside. The others stepped aside. The lychgate stood open — it had been open the whole time, I realized; they hadn't needed to hold anything shut, because nothing was holding me here except a screaming lump of bronze and the fact that the young woman with the baby had read me correctly.

"Go on then," Trelawney said softly. "Alone. That's the whole of it. Alone, and before the moon sets, and singing, and not believing. Four conditions. You've met three already just by being who you are."

I walked toward the gate. As I passed her, she put out one hand — not to touch me, just to hover near my sleeve, the ghost of a gesture — and said, so quietly that no one else could have heard it:

"He barred the door from the outside, you know. Your grandfather's report says *unknown party*. It was Garrett Trelawney. My husband. He was sixteen." Her eyes were dry. They had been dry for fifty years; you could see the dryness, the way stone is dry. "So you're not the only one paying for a relative tonight. You're just the only one paying *willingly*."

I went through the gate.

The humming started again the moment my foot crossed the threshold — all five voices, close now, so close, coming up through the ground itself, through the soles of my boots, harmonized and patient and *glad*, and I understood with a clarity that had nothing to do with belief that the choir had heard every word spoken at that wall, and that they had not been waiting for the village's permission, or the moon's, or even mine.

They had been waiting for the gate to open.

Behind me, I heard the lychgate swing shut, and then — softly, one by one, like a congregation settling into pews — the footsteps of thirty people walking away.

* * *

The crypt steps were where I'd left them, behind the vestry, under a hatch I'd unbolted the night before. Water seeped up between the stones, black and moonlit at the same time, and the cold came off it like a held breath. I knelt at the top and unwrapped the clapper, and it stopped screaming.

That frightened me more than the screaming had. It went quiet the way a child goes quiet when it's finally home — a bone-deep, satisfied quiet, the quiet of a thing that has stopped needing to make noise because everything it wanted is now within reach. It lay in my arms, heavy as a sleeping animal, and the bronze was warm.

"You'd better be worth it," I told it. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. Four days ago I'd have said a sentence like that was a joke. Tonight it was a prayer, and I didn't believe in prayers, and I went down the steps anyway.

The water took my knees on the fourth step. On the eighth, it took my waist. And on the tenth, when the diver's lamp found the bottom of the staircase and the shapes waiting there, patient and unhurried, five small silhouettes with their hands already lifted to greet me —

— the choir began to hum my name.
