## 17. Harvest Moon

The water in the crypt came up to my thighs by the time I reached the far stair, and it was the temperature of a held breath. Cold enough to make thought simple. Simple was good. I had a lamp in one hand, a hundred-year-old reliquary clapper wrapped in Edith's old tea towel against my chest like something newborn and furious, and a plan that consisted of the words *up* and *before the moon crests*. I have worked with less.

The belfry stair rose out of the floodwater on my left, stone steps slick with a century of damp, climbing through the dark toward the open sky. Above me, through the gap where the roof had been, the harvest moon hung fat and orange, still low, still climbing. It looked close enough to touch. It looked like an eye deciding whether to blink.

"Right," I said, to no one, because saying things out loud is how I keep from screaming them. "Up we go."

The humming had stopped when I entered the water. That should have been a mercy. It wasn't. For six days the sound had followed me — through the bakery rafters, along the coast road, in the rearview mirror of a car I would not be driving again — and its absence now was the absence of a held note, the silence of an orchestra that has finished tuning and is waiting for the downbeat.

I climbed.

The steps were narrow, worn into shallow bowls by feet that had stopped using them before either of my grandmothers was born. Water sheeted off my legs with each rise, marking my progress in dark drips. The clapper was heavier than it looked — lead and iron and whatever else they'd poured into holy metal in 1890-something — and by the twentieth step my arms were shaking and I was doing the arithmetic of failure: moon's angle, remaining stairs, the strength left in my forearms. The arithmetic kept coming back wrong. So I stopped doing it and just climbed, which is, if I'm honest, the only method that has ever actually worked for me.

Halfway up there was a landing, and on the landing someone had left flowers.

Not fresh ones. Not old ones either, exactly. They were the kind of flowers that exist in a state of permanent between — sea-pinks and thrift, the sort that grow on Cornish cliffs, laid in a careful row of five small bundles. Five. They were dry. Everything else in this tower was sodden, green-black with a hundred years of rain through the open roof, and these five bundles sat in their row dry as paper, as though the water went around them out of respect or fear.

I didn't stop. Stopping felt like an answer to a question I hadn't been asked yet.

* * *

The belfry itself opened around me all at once — a round stone room breathing sky, the walls going up and up to the broken crown of the tower, and hanging in the middle of it, where a bell should be, nothing at all.

That was the thing about St. Brannoc's that no photograph could prepare you for. The headstocks were still there, the great oak beams bolted to the wall, the ironwork brackets, even the rope wheel, green with age. Everything a bell needs to hang from. And beneath it, air. A bell-shaped absence, maybe four feet tall, that my eyes kept trying to fill and kept failing to fill, the way a missing tooth keeps insisting on your tongue.

The rope still hung. Of course it did. Frayed at the end, swinging very slightly in a wind I couldn't feel, wearing its groove into the empty air below the absent bell as it had every dusk for a hundred and thirteen years.

I checked the moon. Lower third of the sky, clearing the headland, coming for the crest. Twenty minutes, maybe twenty-five. Mrs. Trelawney's instructions had been precise in the way of someone who has rehearsed a sentence for fifty years: *the clapper goes home before the moon stands full upon the tower, or it doesn't go home at all.* She hadn't said what happened then. She hadn't needed to. Everyone in Hollowmere had spent four days looking at me with the particular gentleness people reserve for the already-dead.

There was no mounting bracket for a clapper, because clappers don't mount. They hang inside bells, swinging free, striking the sound out of the metal. But the reliquary niche was different — Edith's ledgers had been clear, and the 1911 melting order clearer still: the clapper had hung in the crypt niche as a memorial, suspended on an iron pin above a small stone basin, "there to remain until such time as the bell be recast." The bell never was. The clapper was supposed to wait forever in the dark, holding the bell's voice in trust.

Except the crypt was under six feet of water, and the belfry was where the ringing happened, and I had made a judgment call on the drive back from Penzance with Vane's headlights in my mirror: the voice belonged with the bell, not the grave. If I was wrong, I'd find out shortly, in the traditional manner.

There was a beam. Of course there was a beam — the headstock's crossbeam ran directly over the bell-shaped absence, oak as thick as my torso, and driven into its underside was an iron staple, rusted to lace but present, positioned precisely where a clapper's leather suspension loop would sit if a clapper were hanging there. Which meant that once, before 1911, before the melting, someone had hung a clapper here too. Or meant to. Or —

Or the staple had been waiting. I am a rationalist. I want that on the record. But there are staples and there are staples, and this one had the patient look of furniture arranged for a guest expected any decade now.

I got the tea towel off the clapper. My hands were numb and stupid and the knot fought me, and when the velvet finally fell away the metal caught the moonlight and threw it back red, the way old iron does, and I heard — not heard, *felt*, in the water still clinging to my trousers, in the stone under my bare feet — the whole tower inhale.

"All right," I said. "Don't make a thing of it."

I stood on the parapet ledge with my back against the wall and lifted the clapper over my head, both hands, arms burning, and hooked the loop over the staple. It caught on the first try, which offended me more than if it hadn't. Then I let go and stepped back and watched it hang.

It swung. There was no wind. It swung anyway, a slow pendulum arc, gathering amplitude with each pass like something remembering how.

Below me, faintly, through the floor, through the flooded crypt, through fifty years of patience, the humming started again.

Five voices. I could pick them apart now; I'd had practice. The low one who always held the drone. Two in the middle who wove around each other like eels. The high one, thin and clear, who hummed the melody line the way children do when they don't know the words yet. And the fifth — the fifth didn't hum so much as breathe, in and out, in and out, keeping time for all of them, the way a boy might count seconds in the dark.

The moon touched the crest of the tower.

I know because the light changed — went from orange to white in a single breathless instant, the whole belfry flooding with it, hard-edged shadows snapping out from the pillars — and in that same instant the clapper, swinging, reached the top of its arc and stopped, quivering, pointed straight down at the center of the bell-shaped absence.

And the bell rang.

There was no bell. I will say it as many times as I have to say it: there was no bell, there was only air and a hundred years of absence, and the dead clapper struck the place where the bell used to be and the sound came anyway — solid, bronze, enormous, a note so deep I felt it in my teeth and my sternum and the water standing in the crypt below, a note with weight and grain and a slight flaw in it halfway through, the little crack of impure casting that every bell has and none admits. It rolled out through the open crown of the tower and across the fields and I knew, with absolute certainty, that every soul in Hollowmere was standing at their window hearing it for the first time in a century, and that some of them were on their knees, and that not one of them was surprised.

The clapper swung back. Struck again. The tolling found its rhythm without anyone's help — mine least of all — and with the third stroke the humming below changed key.

It became singing.

Words. Actual words, in Latin, in English, in that older tongue underneath both that the hymnal Mrs. Trelawney gave me had annotated in a hand like spiderlegs. The verse rose up the stairwell on the tide of the bell, and it was beautiful, and that was the worst thing about it. I had braced for horror. Nobody had warned me it would be *lovely* — five boyish voices braided together, pitched in that uncanny space between treble and alto, singing with the unselfconscious precision of children who have practiced one verse for fifty years because it is the only verse they have.

And then my voice joined them.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. I did not open my mouth. I remember that clearly — lips closed, teeth set, jaw locked with an effort that made my temples ache. And the sound came out of me anyway, rising through my chest like water finding a crack, my own voice pulled up and out and shaped into the harmony line as easily as a hand slips into a glove. I felt it leave my throat. I felt it take its place in the chord, slotting between the two middle voices, completing something.

They hadn't asked permission. They had waited fifty years, patient as fingerprints in flour, and now the bell was ringing and the moon was full and they simply — took what the rule said was theirs. One voice to sing the verse with them. Any voice. The nearest voice.

Mine.

* * *

Here is what I understood, hanging onto the parapet while my traitor throat sang: the terror was real, but it wasn't the whole of it, and it maybe wasn't even most of it. Underneath the animal panic there was grief, vast and specific, pouring through me with each stroke of the bell. Not my grief. Theirs. It came up the stairwell with the music the way cold comes off ice, and I couldn't shut it out any more than I could shut out the singing.

The bell tolled. The choir sang. The moon stood full upon the tower, white and total, and the verse went on, and I sang it, whether I consented or not.

Somewhere below, in the flooded dark, five boys were waiting for me to finish.

And somewhere in the middle of the next line, I realized the verse was almost done — and that nobody had told me what happens after.
