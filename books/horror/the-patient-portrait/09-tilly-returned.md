## 9. Tilly, Returned

She was at her desk before the bell.

Isandro noticed it the way one notices a wrong note in a familiar song—not immediately, but as a small cold pressure behind the sternum that would not resolve. He had arrived at 7:40 to prepare the day's arithmetic lesson, and there she sat: Tilly Marsh, third row by the window, hands folded on her workbook, spine straight as a fence post. Her coat hung on its hook by the door where it had hung for eleven days, and he had touched that coat—had felt the cellar-cold of it seeping through his sleeve into the meat of his palm—and now here she was, wearing a clean pinafore, smiling at him with the particular brightness of a child who has been told to smile for company.

"Tilly," he said. His voice came out careful, the way voices do when they are being carried across ice. "You're back."

"Yes, Mr. Pemberton." She said it without inflection, a fact reported rather than a greeting returned. "I'm back."

"Where were you? Your aunt said you'd gone home sick."

"I was home." A pause, so brief it might have been nothing, but Isandro had spent six years learning to read the silences children left between their words, and this one had weight. "I'm better now."

The radiator under the window ticked and clanked in its old way, pushing heat into the room with the laborious effort of a thing past its prime. The classroom was warm. It was always warm; Odile Crane's rules were explicit about the lights and the boiler, and Bram Hale kept both running like a man feeding a fire he could not name. Isandro had been warm every hour of his three weeks at Hollowmere School.

And yet when Tilly exhaled, he saw her breath.

It came out in a thin silver plume, curling once in the air above her desk before dissolving. The room was warm. The radiator was ticking. The other children had not yet arrived, and the morning light through the frosted panes fell in long grey bars across the floor, and Tilly Marsh breathed out cold like a girl standing in a snowfield.

Isandro stood very still with his attendance book in his hand.

"You'll want to mark me present," she said. "I don't want to be marked absent again."

* * *

The others came in at ten to nine, stamping snow off their boots, shaking out their scarves, and the room filled with the ordinary chaos that Isandro had come, against all his expectations, to love—the thump of satchels, the argument over a pencil sharpener, Petey Wren's inevitable late arrival and Odile's inevitable dry acknowledgment of it from the doorway. Eleven students. He counted them twice, because counting had become a habit at Hollowmere, and then a third time because the first two counts had made him afraid to confirm what he already knew.

Tilly made twelve.

The children saw her and went quiet in a way children should not know how to go quiet. It happened all at once, like a held breath passing through the room. Sable Hodge, who sat beside Tilly and had cried for two days when she vanished—Isandro had held the girl's shoulder while she sobbed that it wasn't fair, that Tilly hadn't even said goodbye—looked at her now and moved her chair four inches away. Four inches. Just enough. Then she looked down at her workbook and did not look up again for an hour.

Isandro taught the lesson.

He taught it because teaching was the only liturgy he knew, because the alternative was to stand in front of twelve children—one of whom exhaled winter—and scream, or weep, or run for the door, and none of those things would help anyone. So he wrote fractions on the board. Three-quarters. Two-fifths. Reduce to lowest terms. And Tilly raised her hand for every question, and her hand went up before he finished asking, sometimes before he had turned from the board, and her answers were correct, always correct, delivered in that flat bright voice while her breath bloomed silver in the warm air.

"Seven-twelfths," she said, when he asked what remained if you took five-twelfths from a whole.

"That's right, Tilly."

"I know it's right." She smiled. The smile reached every part of her face except her eyes, which stayed still and dark and attentive, fixed on him with the patience of something that had learned to wait. "I know lots of things now."

A laugh went around the room—the nervous, dutiful laugh children give when an adult is present and something is wrong. It died quickly. Sable's pencil stopped moving. In the second row, Petey Wren leaned toward the boy next to him and whispered something Isandro couldn't hear, and the boy shook his head, very slightly, the way you shake your head at a dog you're afraid will bite.

At recess Isandro watched from the window. The yard was white-rimmed with early frost, the elms bare, the hills beyond the school a study in brown and grey. The children played in their usual knots and clusters—but not one of them went near Tilly. She stood alone at the edge of the yard, perfectly still, facing the fence, and the frost on the ground around her feet, Isandro noticed, had melted. Just there. Just in a circle perhaps two yards wide, the grass showing green and wet in the middle of all that white, as though the ground itself had decided not to be cold near her—or as though she had decided it.

When the bell rang, she turned and walked back inside without waiting for the line to form, and the frost began creeping back over the melted circle before she reached the door.

* * *

He kept her after class.

He told himself it was concern—a teacher's duty, a conversation about where she'd been, whether she was well, whether someone at home was looking after her. He told himself a great many things while the other children filed out, while Sable Hodge paused at the door and looked back at Tilly with an expression Isandro would carry for the rest of his life, whatever length that turned out to be—an expression not of fear exactly, but of grief, the grief of a child watching a friend come back wrong and knowing, with the terrible clarity of the young, that saying so aloud would change nothing.

Then the door closed, and it was just the two of them, and the radiator ticking, and the light going amber and low through the west windows.

"Sit down, Tilly."

She sat. Hands folded. Spine straight. Waiting.

"How are you feeling?" he asked. "Really."

"I feel fine." And then, because she was still somehow Tilly underneath—because some residue of the girl who had argued about the pencil sharpener and lost her mitten in October and laughed with her whole body—she added, quieter, "That's the strange part, Mr. Pemberton. I feel better than fine. I don't feel cold anymore. I don't feel hungry. I don't feel scared of anything." She looked up at him. "Do you know how much time I used to spend being scared of things?"

Isandro pulled a chair around and sat opposite her, close enough that their knees nearly touched. Up close he could see the details he'd missed from the front of the room: the faint blue tracery beneath her skin at the wrists, like veins seen through ice; the way her hair, normally a wild brown riot, lay smooth and orderly as if combed by something with great care; the perfect, unhurried calm of her breathing, which produced, with every exhale, that small silver ghost in the warm air.

"Your aunt says you weren't home," he said gently. "She says no one was home. There's no record of you anywhere, Tilly. Not at the surgery, not at the shop. It's as if—" He stopped himself. *As if you never existed.* "Where did you go?"

Her eyes didn't waver. "Down," she said.

"Down where?"

"You know where." No accusation in it. Just fact. "Everyone knows where, deep down. They just don't say it, because saying it makes it real, and Hollowmere is very good at not making things real. That's the whole town, Mr. Pemberton. One big agreement not to make things real." She tilted her head, considering him with that patient attention. "But you're new. You haven't signed anything. That's why he likes you."

"He."

"The one below." She said it the way she'd say *the headmaster* or *the vicar*—a title, an office, a fact of local geography. "The one the founder talked to. The one who keeps the ledger." Her breath plumed between them, silver, dissolving. "He showed us the ledger. Down there. All the names. Ours were already written in, before we ever went missing—that's the trick of it, see? We were paid before we were taken. The ink was dry before we knew there was a debt."

Isandro's mouth had gone dry. "Tilly—"

"You can stop it." She leaned forward, and for one moment the flatness cracked, and underneath it was a nine-year-old girl, urgent and frightened, speaking fast before the calm closed over her again. "That's why I'm telling you. He let me come back to tell you—he wants you to know, that's the horrible joke of it, he's *polite*, he does everything properly, everything by the terms. You can stop it. The rest of us—the ones still down there, the ones from before, Edmund Voss and the others—they're not gone, they're *kept*, and kept things can be given back, if the debt is discharged right. Properly. With the right payment."

"What payment?" His voice was barely above a whisper. "What does he want?"

Tilly sat back. The calm descended again, smooth as pond ice, and when she spoke it was in that flat bright voice, and yet her eyes—her dark, still, attentive eyes—held his with something that looked almost like pity.

"He wants the rest of us," she said. "Eleven more names before spring. But you can stop it. You have the right name."

The radiator ticked. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed, soft and deliberate, like a period set at the end of a sentence.

"My name," Isandro said slowly, "is just a name. Pemberton. There are thousands of—"

"Not here," Tilly said. "Here there's only been one family of that name, and everyone in this town knows what the founder traded, even if they pretend the ledger is a story. Aldous Pemberton wrote your name in a book fifty years ago, Mr. Pemberton. Or a name very like it. Names are funny that way—down there, they don't distinguish. A debt to the name is a debt to the blood." She unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the desk, palms down, the way a child settles them for prayer or for punishment. "He's been waiting for you to arrive. I think maybe he arranged it. I think maybe that's why the letter came when it came, why the position opened when it opened. Nothing here happens by accident. Everything happens by *terms*."

Isandro stood. His legs felt borrowed, unreliable, and he gripped the edge of the desk. Through the window the light was failing, the hills going purple and dark, and on the glass the frost had begun its evening work—ferns and feathers and long branching lines that almost, if you squinted, almost resolved into letters.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked. "If he sent you—if this is what he wants—why warn me?"

Tilly smiled again, and this time the smile was sad, and this time it reached her eyes, and that was somehow worse than everything that had come before.

"Because he doesn't mind," she said. "Hope is allowed. Hope is part of the terms. He likes it best when you hope first." She slid off her chair, straightened her pinafore, and picked up her satchel with the easy motion of any child heading home to supper. At the door she paused, hand on the frame, breath silvering the amber light.

"He said to tell you: the door is open whenever you're ready. And Mr. Pemberton?" She glanced back, and the last of the daylight caught the blue tracery at her wrists. "Don't keep him waiting too long. He's very patient. But the frost isn't."

She went out. Her footsteps rang down the corridor, even and unhurried, fading toward the coat hooks and the doors and the darkening yard, and Isandro stood alone in the warm room, listening to the radiator tick, watching the frost write its slow letters on the glass—and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the warmth had never been for the children at all.

It had been for keeping something asleep.

And it was waking anyway.
