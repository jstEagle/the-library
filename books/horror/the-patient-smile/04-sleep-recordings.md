## 4. Sleep Recordings

The trouble with losing hours of your life is that nobody warns you how ordinary it feels.

Sebastian had expected blackouts to feel like something — a gap with edges, a sensation of absence, the way a missing tooth announces itself with your tongue. Instead, Tuesday evening simply ended at 11:40 p.m., somewhere between brushing his teeth and deciding whether to reorganize his map drawer again, and Wednesday began at 6:15 a.m. with him standing in his kitchen holding a mug he did not remember filling. The tea was still hot. That was the part that bothered him most, later. Not the missing time itself, but the evidence that someone — some version of him — had spent those hours competently. Kettle boiled. Mug chosen. The blue one, which was correct. Whoever had been driving had known the route.

He stood there for a long moment, steam curling past his face, and said, out loud, to an empty apartment:

"Okay. New rule."

The new rule was the recorder.

---

He bought it Thursday from an electronics shop run by a man named Dev, who had sold Sebastian three headlamps, two battery packs, and one deeply regrettable action camera over the years, and who had long since stopped asking questions.

"Voice-activated or continuous?" Dev asked, setting a slim grey dictaphone on the counter.

"Continuous."

"Continuous eats batteries."

"I have batteries."

Dev looked at him over his glasses with the particular patience of a man who has watched a customer's hobby slowly become something else. "You know, most people who buy these are recording lectures. Or cheating spouses. You're the only one who buys them like he's fortifying a bunker." He rang it up anyway. "Eight-hour card inside. Don't come crying to me if you snore."

"I don't snore," Sebastian said, with the confidence of a man who lived alone and therefore had no witnesses either way.

That night, he set the recorder on his nightstand, angled toward the pillow like a small grey bird listening for worms. He pressed record. The little red light came on, steady and patient, and he lay in the dark watching it glow for longer than he'd admit to anyone.

"Goodnight," he told it, feeling ridiculous.

The light did not answer. He fell asleep almost immediately after that, which was, in retrospect, suspiciously easy.

* * *

Morning arrived grey and unremarkable. Sebastian made coffee, ate toast, read the news, and got all the way to brushing his teeth before he remembered the recorder sitting on the nightstand, faithful as a dog, eight hours of his night presumably captured in its little grey belly.

He carried it to the kitchen table. Plugged in headphones, because whatever was on here felt like it should be private even if it was just him breathing. Pressed play.

The first hour was exactly what you'd expect. The creak of his mattress. His own breathing, slow and even. At 12:22 a.m., a car passed outside, tires hissing on wet asphalt. At 1:03, the refrigerator cycled on with its familiar asthmatic hum. He listened at double speed for a while, the audio chipmunked and absurd, his own sleeping body reduced to weather sounds. It was boring. It was gloriously, blessedly boring.

"See?" he said aloud, to no one. "Perfectly normal. You lose a few hours and suddenly you're convinced your bedroom is haunted. This is what happens when you spend too much time underground, Sterling. You start thinking in tunnels."

He was so pleased with himself that he almost stopped listening.

Almost.

At 3:47 a.m. — he checked the timestamp twice, then a third time, because the first two times he assumed he'd misread it — the quality of the silence changed.

It's hard to describe to someone who hasn't heard it. Silence isn't one thing; it's a fabric, and this was a silence with a shape in it. The refrigerator hum continued. The city murmured on. But underneath it, in the space where his own breathing had been, there was nothing. A held breath. And then, into that held breath, his own voice spoke.

It was flat. That was the word his mind kept returning to, turning it over like a stone. Flat. Not sleepy, not slurred, not the voice of a man talking in his dreams. It was the voice he used for reading coordinates off a page. Measured. Attentive. Awake.

*"What is the count tonight?"*

Sebastian's thumb froze on the wheel.

And then — gently, warmly, in a voice he had never heard before and would know anywhere for the rest of his life — someone answered.

*"Eleven doors, all sealed, all well. You counted them yourself, love. You always count them yourself."*

The second voice was unhurried. That was the other thing. There was no fear in it, no urgency, none of the breathless quality you'd expect from a voice coming out of the dark in a locked apartment at four in the morning. It sounded like someone reading a bedtime story. Like someone who had answered this question many times before and expected to answer it many times more, and found the repetition pleasant.

On the recording, his own flat voice asked: *"Who holds the doors?"*

*"We do. All of us together. Wick on the odd nights, Tallow on the evens, and Ember listens to us both, though she'll never say so."*

A pause. On the tape, the pause stretched out soft as taffy. Then his voice again:

*"Am I one of them?"*

And here the second voice did something terrible. It laughed — quietly, fondly, the way you laugh at a child who asks if they're loved.

*"Oh, sweetheart. You've been ours since before you knew our names. Go back to sleep now. I'll keep the light on."*

The mattress creaked. Breathing resumed — his breathing, deep and slow, dropping back into sleep like a stone dropped into a well. The second voice said nothing more. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere around 4:30 a.m., a siren rose and faded downtown, oblivious.

Sebastian sat at his kitchen table with the headphones on and his coffee going cold, and listened to the remaining hours at normal speed, because skipping felt like disrespect, somehow. Like walking out of a room while someone was still talking to you.

Nothing else happened. At 6:14 a.m., his own voice — his daytime voice, groggy and human — muttered "five more minutes," and the recording ended.

He took the headphones off very carefully, as though sudden movement might break something.

Then he sat there, in his tidy kitchen with his tidy rituals, and let the violation arrive in full.

Because that was the word for it. Not fear, not yet — fear came later, once he'd had time to think about implications. What he felt first, raw and immediate, was violated. Someone had been in his bedroom. Not physically — his door had been locked, his windows sealed, his apartment on the fourth floor of a building with one entrance he could map blindfolded. But his bedroom was the last unmapped room of his life. Everyone had one. The place where you were allowed to be unbearably, embarrassingly yourself — talking to yourself in the shower, singing badly, lying awake composing arguments you'd never deliver. He had recorded eight hours of that private country, expecting to find only himself in it.

Instead he'd found a guest. A guest who called him *love*. A guest who kept the light on.

And underneath the violation — he hated this, hated it the way you hate a splinter you can't reach — underneath it, small and shameful and warm, was the thought: *someone knows my name.*

Not his name-name. Everyone knew Sebastian Sterling; he was in enough planning-committee meeting minutes to paper a wall. But the voice hadn't used his name. It had used the tone people use for names. The tone his mother had used, once, a lifetime ago, calling him down for dinner. The tone nobody used anymore, because nobody had earned it.

He caught himself smiling at the memory of the voice and made himself stop.

"No," he said firmly, to the recorder, to the kitchen, to himself. "No. We are not doing this. Something talked in my room while I slept, and we are going to treat that as a problem, not a *friendship*."

The recorder sat on the table, red light off, innocent as a stone.

He picked it up anyway. Held it. Eight hours of proof fit in his palm.

* * *

He spent the day being normal, aggressively so. He went to work. He attended a meeting about drainage easements and contributed two useful comments and one joke that landed. He ate lunch at his desk. He texted his sister back, finally, after three weeks: *All good here! Busy season.* He deleted the exclamation point, added it back, deleted it again, and sent it with the exclamation point, because a man who was fine used exclamation points.

But all day, under the normalcy, his mind kept returning to the questions. Not the answers — the questions. Because here was the thing he couldn't stop chewing on, the thing that had lodged in him like a fishhook:

His sleeping voice had asked *What is the count tonight?*

Not *what's the meaning of life*, not *who are you*, not any of the questions a dreaming mind might stumble into. He had asked about a count. About doors. Specific, procedural, operational questions — the questions of someone checking on a system they were responsible for. And the voice had answered like a colleague giving a shift report.

Which meant, if you followed the logic down, that somewhere in those lost hours — the hot tea, the competent mornings, the Tuesdays that simply ended — he had been asking these questions for a long time. Long enough for the voice to be fond of him. Fondness takes repetition. You don't get that warm answering a stranger.

He thought about the tunnel beneath the waterworks. Eleven doors, sealed, numbered in his notebook in his own handwriting. He thought about the way the numbers had felt less like discoveries and more like confirmations, lately — as if he'd known them already and the exploring was just a formality.

He went home. He checked the locks twice, which he never did, and hated that he did it.

* * *

That night he set up the recorder again. Same position, same angle, same little red eye. But this time he also did something he'd been turning over since noon: he wrote a note and propped it against the recorder's base, facing the room.

*If you can hear this,* it said, in his neatest hand, *answer honestly: how long have you been visiting?*

He knew it was absurd. Voices on a tape don't read notes propped against the machine. He knew it the way you know lottery tickets don't win. He propped it up anyway, turned off the lamp, and lay in the dark looking at the small pale rectangle until his eyes adjusted and it became just paper again.

"Goodnight," he told the red light.

Sleep took him fast again — that same suspicious, sliding quickness, like a hand on the back of his neck guiding him down — and when he woke, grey morning light on the ceiling, his first thought was not of coffee or work or the meeting he had at nine.

His first thought was: *did it answer?*

He crossed the room in three steps. The note was still propped against the recorder. Below his question, in handwriting that was also his handwriting but slower, rounder, written by someone taking their time —

*Since you were small. Longer than that, really. Ask me properly and I'll tell you everything.*

Sebastian stood in his pajamas in the middle of his bedroom, holding a piece of paper that should not exist, in a room that had been locked all night, and felt the floor tilt gently beneath him — not falling, just tilting, the way a ship leans when something enormous passes underneath.

He looked at the recorder. He looked at the note. He looked at the window, where the city was waking up, buses sighing, someone's radio playing, the whole bright ordinary world going about its business above ground.

Then he sat down on the edge of his bed, put the recorder in his lap, and began, with hands that were almost steady, to make a list of every question he wanted answered — starting with the names.

Wick. Tallow. Ember.

*Ask me properly,* the note had said.

He intended to.
