## 11. Edited Memory

The morning after the thirteenth tap, Sebastian did what he always did when the world stopped making sense: he made coffee, he opened his laptop, and he tried to organize the chaos into folders.

It was a coping mechanism his sister had teased him about for years. *You alphabetize your grief, Seb.* And she was right. When their father died, Sebastian had spent three days scanning forty years of photographs into a directory structure so granular it included a subfolder called "Dad / Hats / Winter." It wasn't that he didn't feel things. It was that filing things made them hold still long enough to be felt.

So: coffee. Laptop. A new folder named TUNNELS_EVIDENCE.

He started with the photographs — the door that photographed as brick, the junction that shouldn't exist, Mother Marrow's smile with its quiet abundance of teeth. Then the audio files. Then, finally, because the folder needed to be complete and completeness was the only prayer he knew, he went looking for his journals.

He kept them in a fireproof box under the bed, which he'd bought after a minor kitchen fire in 2019 and never repurposed. Seven notebooks, dated, indexed in the back like a proper archive. He'd been journaling since university — not diary stuff, mostly, but field notes on his own life. Sleep quality. Routes walked. Hours accounted for. He'd started keeping them partly because of the blackouts, those missing stretches of evening he'd blamed on stress, on cheap wine, on the ordinary erosion of a tired mind.

He carried the box to the kitchen table, set out the notebooks in chronological order, and began at the beginning.

The first three were fine. Normal. His handwriting, his voice, his small obsessions rendered in his small tidy script. *Ran the river path twice. The heron was back on the piling. Bought a better headlamp.*

The fourth notebook was where things went wrong.

* * *

March 14th. Went down past the waterworks again. Found the second junction. Counted the doors — eleven, all sealed, all cold. Wick says I'm getting faster at the count. Tallow made soup. Ember sat next to me the whole meal and put one hand flat on my arm, which is how she laughs.

Home by ten. Lights on. Doors counted.

Sebastian read the entry four times.

Then he held the page up to the window light, the way you'd check a banknote, and looked at the handwriting. It was his. Not similar — identical. The same slight rightward lean. The same way he crossed his sevens, the same cramped ampersand he'd invented in a statistics lecture in 2014 because the professor's ampersands were beautiful and he'd wanted one of his own. Nobody else wrote an ampersand like that. He'd never seen another one like it anywhere.

And yet he had no memory of March 14th. No memory of a second junction. No memory of soup, or of a silent child laughing against his arm with her whole palm.

His first thought — and he was almost proud of how reasonable it was — was that he'd written it during a blackout. That was the whole point of the journals, wasn't it? To catch the hours that slipped? Maybe he'd gone down, come back up, written the entry, and lost the writing along with everything else. Amnesia could do that. He'd read about it. Dissociative gaps, fugue states, the brain quietly shredding its own receipts.

It was a good theory. It was even plausible.

It lasted until he turned the page.

March 15th. Down again. Marrow asked about my mother's kitchen — the yellow tiles, the radio on the windowsill. I don't know how she knows these things. She said, "We keep what walks with us." Wick taught me the candle names tonight. There are more than I expected. I should write them down before I forget.

I won't forget. I said I wouldn't forget.

He checked the date against his calendar app, his bank statements, his email. On March 15th he had worked until six, ordered pad thai, watched a documentary about octopuses, and gone to bed at eleven-forty. He knew this because there was a delivery confirmation, a credit card charge, and — God help him — a note in his own journal from that same night, in the same impossible handwriting, that read:

*Quiet day. Octopus doc was good. They can taste with their skin. Bed early.*

Two entries. Same night. Same hand. One describing a life he remembered, one describing a life he didn't, both filed neatly side by side like a man keeping two sets of books.

Sebastian sat very still and listened to his refrigerator hum, because the refrigerator humming meant the world still had ordinary machinery in it, and right now he needed that very badly.

* * *

He did what any sensible person would do at eleven in the morning on a Tuesday: he laid every journal open on the floor of his apartment in a great spiral of dates, made a second pot of coffee, and began the work of auditing his own life.

It took him most of the day. By late afternoon he had a system — green sticky notes for entries that matched his memory, red for entries that described things he couldn't recall, yellow for the ones that matched too well, that described days with a specificity his normal entries never attempted. His usual style was terse, almost bored. *Long shift. Rain. Good noodles.* But the tunnel entries bloomed. They had texture. They had dialogue.

June 2nd. Marrow told us about the winter she came below. She doesn't say much about before, but tonight she said, "Up there I was someone's second choice. Down here I am the first voice they hear." Ember fell asleep against my knee. I stayed until the candles burned low because moving would have woken her, and I found I didn't want to move anyway.

Sebastian read that one standing up, because sitting felt too much like surrender.

Here was the thing that frightened him most, and he made himself look at it directly, the way he looked at the doors: the entries weren't just records of visits he couldn't remember. They were records of *love*. Warmth, in his own voice, in his own hand, accumulating across years — two years, nearly three — of evenings spent below with people who knew his childhood, who mended his jacket, who fed him soup while a silent child leaned against his knee. And somewhere in the middle of all that warmth, some part of him had apparently decided this was worth doing regularly, worth writing home about, worth remembering.

And then something had taken the memory and left the handwriting, like a burglar who steals the jewelry but politely relocks the door.

He thought about the sleep recordings. Eight hours of silence, then his own flat voice asking questions he didn't remember asking, and the second voice — gentle, unhurried, patient as dripping water — answering. *The family. The count. Doors that must stay shut.* He'd assumed the interviews were recent. Weeks old, maybe months.

They went back years. The family hadn't been interviewing him. They'd been *raising* him.

"No," he said aloud, to the apartment, to nobody. "No, that's — people don't get edited."

But even as he said it, he was already reaching for the evidence, because that was who he was. That was the only self he had left, maybe: the man who counts, who logs, who files. If his memory was a curated collection, then the curation had a shape, and shapes could be mapped.

* * *

He found the pattern around midnight.

The edits weren't random. Every gap — every red sticky note — clustered around the same few subjects. The count itself: how it was performed, what words accompanied it, what happened when a number came up wrong. The thirteenth door: mentioned exactly once, in an entry from last autumn, and the sentence stopped mid-thought.

October 9th. Tonight I followed the sound behind the false wall and I saw

That was it. Full stop. Not even a period — just the end of the line and then, on the next line, in the same ink, in the same hand:

*Good practice tonight. Wick is getting quicker. Home by nine.*

As if the sentence had been interrupted and the interruption papered over with something pleasant. As if whoever — whatever — did the editing understood that a missing entry would look like a hole, but a *replaced* entry would look like a life.

Sebastian laughed. It came out wrong, thin and high, and he pressed his fist against his mouth until it stopped.

Because here was the joke, and it was a genuinely funny joke if you were the kind of person who found horror funny: he had spent his entire adult life building systems to protect himself from forgetting. The journals. The photographs. The labeled boxes, the indexed archives, the backup drives. He had constructed an external memory so thorough that when something reached into his head and rearranged the furniture, it couldn't touch the paper trail. The editors had beaten his memory. They had not beaten his *filing*.

They'd been sloppy, too, or hurried, or simply uninterested — because paper doesn't dream, maybe, and doesn't matter. Whatever the reason, they had left him years of evidence in his own handwriting, and the evidence told a story that his memory had been systematically forbidden to tell:

He had a family. He had loved them. He had been loved back, or something functionally indistinguishable from love, and none of it — not one dinner, not one candlelit lesson, not one sleeping child against his knee — had survived in his actual mind. He was reading his own heart like a stranger reading mail addressed to a previous tenant.

And underneath the fear, shamefully, persistently, there was longing. Because the man in those entries sounded happy. Genuinely, specifically happy, in a way the man who wrote *good noodles* rarely managed. Whoever Sebastian Sterling had been on those nights below, he had been more himself than the version currently sitting on his kitchen floor surrounded by sticky notes.

If his past was editable, what was left to be loyal to?

He turned that question over for a long time. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere above him, a neighbor's television murmured through the ceiling, the muffled laugh-track of a world that still agreed to exist.

Here was what he finally wrote in the margin of the fourth notebook, in fresh ink, in his own hand, deliberately, so that tomorrow-morning-him would find it even if tonight-him didn't survive the night:

*If they can edit what I remember, they cannot edit what I choose now. This note is a choice. I choose the family. I choose the truth. Both. Even if they fight each other. Especially then.*

He underlined it twice. Then, because he was still himself, he added a smaller note beneath it:

*Also: buy more coffee. We are out.*

* * *

He slept with the recorder running and the lights on and the journals stacked beside the bed like sandbags. In the morning, eight hours of silence, then his own voice, flat and calm:

*"Is he still reading?"*

And the second voice, warm as candle wax, unhurried as ever:

*"He reads beautifully. He always has."*

*"Will you take the journals?"*

A pause. Long enough that Sebastian, listening, leaned toward the speaker.

*"No," the voice said, and there was something in it he hadn't heard before — something almost like regret, or like respect. "No. Let him keep his paper. Paper is honest. It was never our enemy."*

Another pause, longer.

*"Besides," the voice added gently, "he'll want to ask Wick his questions soon. And Wick has been waiting years for someone to ask."*

Sebastian sat on the edge of his bed with the recorder trembling in his hand, and realized that somewhere below the city, in a candlelit chamber off the twelfth door, one of the twins already knew what he knew — and had decided, before Sebastian ever found the red sticky notes, that it was time to tell him everything.

He got dressed. He packed the journals. He counted the doors of his apartment — front, balcony, bathroom; three, all shut, all accounted for — and went down to meet the truth.
