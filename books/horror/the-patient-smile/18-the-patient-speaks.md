## 18. The Patient Speaks

The candles went down the tunnel in a procession, and it looked, absurdly, like a birthday party.

Wick carried the first three, cupping his free hand around each flame as though it were a small animal that might startle. Tallow had the lantern and a coil of chalk string over one shoulder, humming something tuneless and old — a counting song, Ember's favorite, the one where every verse ends on a number you're not supposed to say out loud. Marrow walked last with her hands folded, unhurried, the way she always walked, as if the tunnels were a hallway in her own house and the dark were simply a room she hadn't tidied yet.

Sebastian walked in the middle, because they'd arranged him there without ever discussing it, the way families do.

"Eleven," Wick said at the eleventh door, touching its frame with two fingers. "Still eleven. Good."

"Twelve," said Tallow, and then, softer: "Hello, twelve."

The twelfth door was shut now. It had been shut for three days, since the recount began, since Tallow gave up his reflection to hold it closed through the long night. The wood of it — and it was wood here, warm-grained oak where everything else was brick and iron — looked tired. Sebastian thought doors could look tired. He'd thought a lot of strange things lately, and most of them had turned out to be true.

Beyond the twelfth door, the passage narrowed. Beyond that was the false wall. Beyond *that* —

"Thirteen," Sebastian said quietly.

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to. Ember slipped her small cold hand into his and squeezed once, which was how she said *I'm here*, which was how she said almost everything.

Marrow stopped at the mouth of the narrow passage and turned to face them all, candlelight pooling in the too-many creases of her smile.

"Now," she said, "you know what waits past this point speaks in voices. It will use yours. It will use mine. It will say things that are true and things that are almost true, and the almost-true ones are the dangerous ones." Her unblinking eyes found Sebastian's and held them, gentle as a held breath. "Whatever it offers you, love — and it *will* offer — remember that a gift from the patient is only a door opened from the other side."

"I know," Sebastian said.

"You don't," Marrow said, with terrible kindness. "But you will. And knowing afterward is what we're hoping to spare you." She touched his cheek with fingers that were warmer than they should have been. "We'll be right behind you. Not too close. Close enough."

* * *

The false wall had been brick when he'd found it, weeks ago, in another life measured in journal entries. Now it stood open — not broken, not removed, but *open*, swung inward like a door deciding it had waited long enough — and the chamber beyond was vast in a way that made his eyes argue with themselves. His light reached maybe thirty feet and then simply gave up, not fading but refusing, as if the darkness past that line had weight and the beam couldn't be bothered to push it.

And the door.

He'd seen it before, through the seams of the wall. Scarred oak, taller than any door had a right to be, wide as a barn door, banded in iron gone green with age. It breathed. That was the word his mind kept reaching for, though lungs were wrong and tides were also wrong; it swelled and eased, swelled and eased, slow as something sleeping very deeply and dreaming very hard.

The scarring across its surface wasn't random. In the lantern light, Sebastian finally let himself see what he'd been refusing to see: the marks were letters. Hundreds of thousands of letters, carved and burned and scratched by ten thousand hands across however many centuries, names and pleas and tallies, whole biographies worn down to texture. And near the center, at the height of a standing man's chest, one patch of wood was smooth. Unscarred. Waiting.

"That's where the hand goes," Tallow said behind him, very quietly. "When someone becomes the last door. I've seen it done once. I was small." A pause. "I don't remember who it was. That's part of it too."

Sebastian stepped closer. The breathing of the door slowed — he was almost sure it slowed — the way a sleeper's breathing changes when someone enters the room.

And then it spoke.

Not from the door. From everywhere. From the space just behind his right ear, which is where his own voice lives, the place no one else can ever quite reach.

*"Hello, Sebastian."*

His own voice. Perfectly his own voice — the slight rasp on the S, the way he swallowed the end of sentences when he was nervous, all of it. Hearing it come from outside himself was like watching someone wear his face as a mask, fitted so well the edges disappeared.

"No," Sebastian said. His voice shook. "You don't get that one. That one's mine."

*"It was always ours to share,"* the patient said, warm, reasonable, patient above all — a voice built for long conversations in the dark, for talking someone gently off a ledge or gently onto one. *"You've known that since the recordings. Did you really think the second voice was a stranger? You asked the questions, love. I only ever answered."*

"Don't call me love. That's hers."

A pause. When it spoke again, there was something in it that might have been amusement, or might have been hunger wearing amusement's coat.

*"Fair."*

* * *

Behind him, at the edge of the lamplight, the family had arranged themselves in a loose crescent. Marrow's smile was fixed in place, but her eyes moved constantly between Sebastian and the door, reading the air between them like weather. Wick had Ember's hand in both of his. Tallow — reflectionless, hollow-eyed, brave Tallow — had planted himself slightly forward of the others, the way he did during counts, as if his body remembered how to stand between people and doors even when nothing else about him stayed put.

*"Let me show you something,"* the patient said. *"It costs nothing to look. Looking is free. It's the only thing that's ever been free."*

The darkness beyond the lamplight changed.

Not brightened — *resolved*. Like an eye adjusting, except the eye was the room. And Sebastian was standing in his apartment. His actual apartment, third floor, the radiator that clanked in E-flat, the corkboard where his maps hung in careful overlapping layers. Morning light came through the window in the particular slanted gold of a Tuesday in October.

There was a man at the corkboard. Forty-ish, soft around the middle in a way Sebastian had never allowed himself to become, wearing a cardigan with leather buttons. He was pinning up a map of — Sebastian leaned forward despite himself — a map of the storm drains under Calloway Park. Ordinary. Surveyed. Boring. The man pinned it, stepped back, and smiled at it with the mild satisfaction of a hobbyist, and the smile was Sebastian's smile, and the man turned toward the window and the light caught his face and it *was* him, older, unremarkable, content.

On the kitchen table sat a recorder, switched off, gathering dust. There were no tapes beside it. There had never been tapes beside it.

*"No blackouts,"* the patient murmured. *"No lost hours. No family under the city needing you. No count. You explore on weekends. You publish a little zine that eleven people read. You sleep the whole night through, every night, alone, and wake up remembering all of it."*

In the vision, the older Sebastian made coffee. Burned it slightly, the way he always did. Hummed.

*"They never find you, you know,"* the voice said, softer now, coaxing. *"Wick and Tallow and Ember and Marrow. In that life, the family stays down here with no one to walk the count, and the long nights get longer, and eventually—"*

"Stop."

*"—eventually the door opens and there's no one left who knows the numbers, and the city above dreams whatever the patient dreams, and none of them ever surface, none of them ever see morning, because the one who walks chose a cardigan—"*

"*Stop.*"

The vision held. The older Sebastian in the golden light turned from his coffee pot and looked directly out of the vision, directly at him, and smiled the patient smile, the one Marrow wore, the one that had too much of everything in it.

*"Or,"* the voice said, *"there's this: you walk away tonight. Up the stairs, into the real world, and everything stays exactly as it is. Your memories — all of them, the journals, the recordings, Ember's hand in yours, Tallow's humming, Marrow calling you home. All of it stays. The family keeps their warden. The count holds — for now, for years, maybe a generation. And someday, decades from now, some other lost soul finds a tunnel that isn't on any survey, and hears a second voice in the dark, and we begin again with them. Someone else holds the count. Someone else becomes the last door."*

The vision shifted. Now it showed the chamber itself, but warm — candlelit, the family gathered at their long table, passing bread, laughing at something Wick had said. Sebastian was in the picture. Younger, unscarred, seated at the table like he belonged there, because he did belong there. The image lingered on Ember, tapping her spoon against her bowl in sequences of numbers, safe, fed, loved.

*"You keep them,"* the patient whispered in his own voice, tender as a lullaby. *"You keep yourself. You lose nothing but the truth, and honestly, Sebastian — has the truth ever once been kind to you?"*

* * *

The silence after was the loudest thing Sebastian had ever heard.

He became aware, distantly, that he was crying. Not dramatically — just leaking, the way you do when something bypasses all your defenses and goes straight for the plumbing. Because that was the obscenity of it. The offer was *good*. That was the trap inside the trap: it wasn't a monkey's paw, it wasn't a poisoned gift. Every word of it was true. Walk away, and he would keep them. Keep the memories. Keep the warmth. Some other wanderer, years downstream, would pay the bill.

All he had to do was love them slightly less than he loved himself, and dress the difference up as mercy.

"You're using my voice," Sebastian said at last, wiping his face with the heel of his hand, "to tell me it's okay to abandon my family. Don't you see how that sounds? Even *you* should see how that sounds."

*"I see exactly how it sounds,"* the patient said, and for one flickering instant the warmth dropped away and what was underneath was vast and tired and old beyond accounting, a thing that had been dreaming so long it had forgotten it was a dream. *"I have watched wardens stand where you stand for nine hundred years. They all heard the same offer. Do you know what they all had in common, the ones who walked away?"*

"They lived?"

*"They came back."* The words settled over the chamber like ash. *"Every one. Within a year. Within a decade. They surfaced, and they slept, and in their sleep they heard me asking questions, and the questions got louder, and eventually they came back down those stairs carrying candles, and they stood where you're standing, and they took the door. Walking away was never an escape, Sebastian. It was just the long way to the same wall. I am not offering you a way out. I am offering you time. Time is all I have ever offered anyone. It's the cruelest currency there is, and everyone takes it."*

Sebastian looked back at his family.

Ember was staring at him with those enormous pitying eyes, and she raised one small hand — not to beckon, not to warn. She tapped her own chest, twice, over the heart. Then she pointed at him. Then she tapped the floor, once, twice, three times, four — the beginning of a count, the first four numbers, the ones every warden learned first.

*I'm here. You're here. One, two, three, four.*

Tallow had tears running silently down his reflectionless face. Wick's jaw was set the way it got when he was about to confess to something. And Marrow — Marrow was smiling her fixed smile, but her eyes were shining, wet, unblinking, and her lips were moving without sound, and it took Sebastian a moment to read the shape of the words:

*Whatever you choose, you are ours. Whatever you choose.*

That was the moment he understood the trap completely. The bargain was designed to be refused. The patient didn't want him walking away — walking away meant years more of waiting, of whispering through walls, of counting failed counts. What the patient wanted was for him to refuse *angrily*. To refuse in hatred, in bitterness, in the conviction that his family had stolen his life from him. A seal made of resentment cracks. A mind pressed against this door full of fury would dream furious dreams, and furious dreams leak.

The patient needed him to choose the door while hating everyone who brought him to it.

And instead — God help him, instead — Sebastian started to laugh. Wet, cracked, helpless laughter, the kind that comes when the fear and the love hit the same nerve.

"You're good," he told the door. "You're genuinely good. Nine hundred years of practice and it shows. But you made one mistake." He wiped his eyes again and grinned, and it was his grin, the real one, the crooked one, and he felt the whole chamber flinch from it. "You showed me the other life. The cardigan. The burnt coffee. Eleven readers." He shook his head slowly. "I've spent my whole life mapping places nobody's been. And you just showed me a world where nobody needs finding. Where everything's already on the survey." He stepped closer to the door, close enough to feel its breath move his hair. "That's not peace, you idiot. That's *vanishing*. I'd rather be edited than erased."

The breathing stopped.

For three full seconds, the thirteenth door did not breathe, and every candle in the chamber bent sideways as if the air itself had inhaled, and in the ringing stillness Sebastian heard, far beneath everything, something turning over in its sleep and beginning — slowly, with the patience of centuries — to wake.

*"Then come closer,"* said his own voice, no longer warm, no longer anything but hungry and huge and *awake*, *"and let's discuss what you'll give me."*

Behind him, Marrow said, very softly, "Sebastian. Love. It's time."

He turned to look at his family one last time — and found Ember already holding out her hand.
