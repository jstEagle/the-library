## 4. Prior Owner, Prior Loss

The county archive occupied the basement of the old municipal building on Corben Street, a fact that struck Ivo as appropriate — records, like roots, belonged underground. She had called ahead from the parking lot of the Hollowmere gatehouse, her breath fogging in the October morning, and a woman with a voice like dry paper had told her to come before noon because the heating was temperamental and so, frankly, was the staff.

Inside, the air smelled of dust and cold metal and something faintly organic, like old paper digesting itself. Fluorescent tubes hummed overhead in a frequency Ivo could feel behind her eyes. Rows of gray shelving stretched back into shadow, and between them moved a single archivist, small and silver-haired, pushing a cart with the unhurried precision of someone who had spent forty years learning that nothing down here needed to be done quickly.

"Ivo Montrose," she said. "I called about the Vann file."

"Edith Vann." The woman didn't look up. "Hollowmere. You're the third person this year."

Ivo's hand paused on the counter. "Third?"

"Mm." The archivist slid a request slip across the counter without looking at it. "A journalist in the spring. A cousin in June. And now you." She raised her eyes then, pale blue and unbothered. "You're the surveyor, though. That's new. The others wanted gossip. You want paper."

"I want the property history. Chain of title, permits, any surveys on record. It's for an appraisal."

"Everything's for something." The woman wrote Ivo's name on the slip in a spidery hand. "Sit by the radiator. It's the only warm thing in the building and it lies about how warm it is."

* * *

The file arrived twenty minutes later in a gray archival box, tied with cotton tape gone soft as shoelace. Ivo carried it to the reading table beneath the radiator and untied it with the care she'd have given a client's original deed, because in a sense that was what it was: the biography of a house, told in the only language houses spoke — ink, signatures, dates.

She started where she always started, with the structure. Deed transfers first. Hollowmere had changed hands four times since 1911, which was not unusual for a rural parcel, except that each transfer carried the same notation in the margin of the grantor index, a phrase she'd never seen repeated like that: *sold with all appurtenances, named and unnamed.* She ran her thumb over the photocopy. Legal boilerplate drifted toward poetry sometimes; clerks got bored, or superstitious. Still. Four times.

Then the permits, and here the story acquired a body.

Edith Marguerite Vann, purchased Hollowmere House and eleven acres in March, three years ago. The purchase price was high enough to raise an eyebrow — cash, no mortgage, no lender's appraisal, which meant no one in an institutional capacity had ever been required to look closely at what she was buying. Ivo recognized the pattern from her own life in reverse: private money bought privacy, and privacy bought freedom from questions. Edith Vann had been fifty-one years old, per the tax records, recently widowed — no, Ivo corrected herself, reading again, not widowed. Divorced. The file contained a copy of a name-change affidavit, dated two years before the purchase. Before Hollowmere, she had been Edith Marguerite Calloway.

Calloway. Ivo filed the name away without knowing why. It had a weight to it, like a stone dropped into deep water that you listened for and never heard hit bottom.

The renovation permits began in April and read like a woman rebuilding her life one room at a time. New roof on the east wing. Electrical service upgraded. Plumbing — extensive plumbing, more than the scope of work seemed to require, lines rerouted and re-rerouted, all of them converging somewhere near the rear boundary of the lot. Ivo thought of the well on the property line, dry and echoing, and felt the first cold thread of attention move through her, the feeling she got when two survey points refused to reconcile.

In September, three years ago, the permits stopped.

Not concluded. Stopped. The final document in the folder was not a permit at all but a sheriff's report, photocopied twice and annotated in two different hands. *Wellness check requested by neighbor (R. Aldous), 14 Sept.* *Subject last seen 9 Sept.* *Residence secured, no signs of disturbance.* *Vehicle present.* *Case referred to missing persons.*

And then, stapled behind it, the thing that made Ivo stop breathing for a moment without understanding why.

A missing persons intake form, filled out in a deputy's blocky print, listing Edith Vann's description, her last known clothing, her medical information — and beside *medical conditions*, the deputy had written: *per sister-in-law, subject recently diagnosed, terminal, declined treatment. Subject stated she intended to 'get well' at the property.*

Get well. At a derelict house with no functioning plumbing until she'd paid to install it.

Ivo sat back. The radiator ticked. Somewhere in the stacks a cart wheel squeaked its slow circuit, and the fluorescent light buzzed its one note, and she became aware that she had been holding her pen without writing for long enough that the ink had begun to pool into a small black planet on her legal pad. She blotted it and kept going, because this was the job, this was always the job: find the facts, order the facts, let the facts be facts.

But the next item in the box would not stay a fact. It insisted on becoming something else.

* * *

It was a survey report. Bound, stamped, signed — a full boundary and improvement survey of the Hollowmere parcel, commissioned by Edith Vann in May of her first year, performed by a firm out of Harrowgate called Denby & Associates. Ivo knew the name vaguely; they were competent, mid-tier, the kind of outfit that did bank work and estate partitions. She opened the cover expecting nothing. Appraisers read other people's surveys the way doctors skim old charts — professionally, fast, looking only for discrepancies.

The cover sheet listed the parcel, the acreage, the date. Standard.

The second page was the plat. And Ivo's professional speed abandoned her entirely.

She had drawn this map. Not this exact map — but the drawing was hers in every way that mattered. The north arrow tilted at the same idiosyncratic angle she used, twelve degrees off true rather than the conventional zero, a habit her drafting instructor had scolded her for and she'd never broken. The improvements were rendered in the same line weights. The setback dimensions were noted in the same margins, in the same order she noted them: front, side, side, rear. Even the little circled R she used to flag encroachments appeared twice, once at the well — *the well,* there it was, sitting on the lot line exactly where she'd found it, flagged as an encroachment onto the neighboring parcel just as she'd flagged it — and once at a shed foundation she hadn't found yet, on the northeast corner, half-collapsed into a stand of birch.

Her pen hovered over the signature block. The surveyor of record was a man named T. Denby. The date was three years ago. The handwriting in the certification was a stranger's, a cramped masculine script nothing like her own.

But the work was hers. Not similar. Hers. The way a fingerprint is yours.

She turned pages with hands that had gone careful and cold. The interior measurements were included — Denby had measured the house room by room, as she had, and Ivo read his figures with the growing vertigo of someone checking their own math against a ghost. The east parlor: 14'2" × 11'7". Her own measurement, taken nine days ago, had been 14'2" × 11'7". The stair hall: 8'0" × 22'4", with the note *landing irregular, verify.* She had written *verify* beside the same landing. The primary bedroom — Edith's bedroom, the room where Ivo had slept, or tried to sleep, for the past week under the overnight retainer — was recorded at 16'6" × 13'1".

Ivo had measured it at 15'2" × 12'4".

She stared at the discrepancy for a long time. Of everything in the document, this was the only number that differed. Every other figure matched hers to the sixteenth of an inch, across three years, across two different surveyors, in a house whose walls she had personally watched refuse to hold still. And the bedroom — the bedroom where she slept — had shrunk by sixteen inches in one dimension and nine in the other since Edith Vann's surveyor walked it with his tape.

Or grown, she thought. Depending on which direction you assumed the error lived. Maybe Denby was wrong. Old instruments, sloppy field notes. Houses settled; foundations shifted; a man could misread a tape in bad light. There were explanations. There were always explanations, and she had been collecting them all week the way other people collected stamps, pressing each one flat in the notebook of her mind: *settling, moisture, instrument drift, operator error.*

But the explanations had never had to account for this. For a document that read like it had come out of her own head, three years early.

She looked up. The archivist was watching her from the end of the row, cart motionless, face unreadable in the flat light.

"You've gone white," the woman said. Not unkindly. The way you'd note a change in weather.

"The survey in this file," Ivo said. "Who pulled it for the journalist? The one who came in the spring?"

The archivist came closer, slowly, and looked down at the open plat without touching it. Something moved behind her eyes — recognition, maybe, or resignation.

"That one didn't ask for the survey," she said. "Asked for the newspaper clippings. Wanted to know about the search parties." She tapped one knuckle, gently, on the edge of the plat. "Funny thing about that survey. Denby's office has no record of it. Called them myself, years back, when the cousin came through. No job number, no invoice, no field book. Mr. Denby — the elder, he's dead now — swore he never set foot on that property. His son says the same. But there's the stamp, and there's the seal, and there's the signature, and the county accepted it, because paper doesn't lie even when it can't tell the truth."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying I've worked this basement thirty-one years," the archivist said, "and I've learned that some documents arrive in files the way mushrooms arrive on lawns. Nobody plants them. They're just there one morning, and they're always about the same spot." She straightened. "You want the rest of the Vann material? There's a second folder. Correspondence. We don't put it in the main box because it upsets people."

"Upsets them how?"

"It upsets them," the woman said, "in the specific way of things that sound like nonsense right up until they sound like instructions."

* * *

The second folder was thinner and older-feeling, though the paper inside was the same vintage as the rest. Letters, mostly — Edith Vann's correspondence with contractors, with the county planning office, with a lawyer in Harrowgate whose letterhead Ivo recognized from the deed work. And interleaved among them, copies of letters Edith had *received*, which was where the file stopped behaving.

The first was from a title research service, dated five weeks after her purchase. *Dear Ms. Vann: Per your request, we have completed a chain-of-title abstract for the Hollowmere parcel, 1911–present. Please note an anomaly: the original 1911 conveyance contains a redaction affecting the grantee's legal description. Our researcher was unable to locate the unredacted text in any county record. We recommend consulting the original deed book directly.*

The second was from the county recorder's office, responding to Edith's apparent inquiry. *The redaction in Deed Book 44, page 212, was entered pursuant to a court order dated 14 November 1911. The order itself is sealed. We are unable to provide further information regarding the nature or content of the redacted material.*

Sealed. In 1911. Ivo turned the page slowly.

The third letter had no letterhead. It was handwritten, in a strong old-fashioned cursive, undated, and it read:

*Mrs. Vann — You do not know me, and I will not sign this, because my family learned long ago what signing leads to. My grandmother sold you that house, or her estate did, which is the same thing. I am writing because the agent said you intend renovations, and because you asked questions at the recorder's office, and asking questions is how it starts. So I will tell you what my family tells no one, and then you may do with it what everyone does, which is nothing, until it is too late for it to matter.*

*The house was built on Hollowmere village. Not near it. On it. The village drowned in 1908 when the mill dam failed, and the land above the waterline was sold cheap because the dead were never all recovered. Eleven acres. The well on your west line is the village well. It did not drown. Wells draw from below, and below is where they went.*

*My great-grandfather signed something in 1911. I will not say what. Read your deed carefully, Mrs. Vann, and if you find a word struck through, leave it struck. A name is a door, and ours was nailed shut for a reason. If you have already heard it — the voice, the water, the measuring that won't come out even — then it is too late for advice, and I am sorry, and you should know that you were not chosen. You were simply next.*

*Do not lean over the well. Do not answer it. And whatever you do, do not write down what it tells you, because it keeps what is written.*

There was no signature. At the bottom, where a signature would go, someone had drawn a single horizontal line and then, deliberately, scratched it out.

Ivo read the letter twice. Then a third time, her lips moving slightly, her surveyor's mind doing what it always did with impossible site conditions: looking for the error, the datum mistake, the point from which everything else had been mismeasured. Drowned village. Sealed name. A warning unsigned because signing was dangerous. It was nonsense. It was folklore dressed in the grammar of legal caution, the kind of thing that grew on old properties like lichen.

Except.

Except that she had leaned over the well, ten days ago, and it had echoed like it held water, and the neighbor — Aldous, the same name as the wellness-check requester — had warned her not to, in almost exactly those words. Except that her laser level had returned readings from above the roofline. Except that a survey existed in this file that its own firm disowned, drawn in her hand, matching her measurements to the sixteenth of an inch, disagreeing in exactly one room — the room where she slept.

Pattern recognition was supposed to be her gift. It was the whole profession: find the pattern in the terrain, the easement hidden in the metes and bounds, the fraud hidden in the chain of title. She had built a career, and rebuilt a life after Marcus, on the principle that patterns meant something, that the world's irregularities were messages waiting to be correctly read.

She had just found a pattern. Three owners' worth of curiosity converging on the same basement, the same box, the same letters. A surveyor's plat that behaved like a memory. A house that had swallowed a woman who'd gone there to get well.

And underneath all of it, quiet as groundwater, the question she had been refusing to ask since the night the pipes knocked in sequence: *if the file knows what I measured — what else does it know about what comes next?*

The archivist reappeared at her elbow with a date-stamp and a look of professional pity.

"Photocopies?" she asked.

"Yes," Ivo said. "All of it. Including the letter."

The woman nodded, unsurprised, and gathered the pages. As she lifted the correspondence folder, a single sheet slipped loose and fluttered to the table — a memo, internal, county letterhead, dated three weeks ago. Ivo picked it up to hand it back and read the first line before she could stop herself.

*RE: Hollowmere parcel — scheduled action. Pursuant to standing order (1911), the parcel is to be reappraised upon vacancy exceeding three years. Assigned surveyor: I. Montrose. Note: assignment originated from the Vann family trust. Contact for the trust: E. Calloway.*

E. Calloway. Edith's maiden name. Someone with that name had requested *her*, specifically, three weeks ago — before she'd ever seen the listing, before the commission call, before any of it.

Ivo stood very still in the humming cold while the copier whined in the back room, turning her week over in her hands like a document she'd misread from the first page. She had thought she took this job. She had told herself she took this job — for the money, for the distraction, because houses were just assets.

The copier stopped. The archivist returned with a warm stack of pages and a receipt slip, and Ivo signed it, and her signature came out steady, which felt like the most frightening thing that had happened all day.

Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of old pewter. She sat in her car in the municipal lot with the photocopies on the passenger seat and dialed the number on the memo before she could talk herself out of it. It rang four times. Then a young woman's voice, cautious, unfamiliar:

"Calloway residence."

"This is Ivo Montrose," she said. "I'm the surveyor. I think we need to talk about what your family asked me to find."

The silence on the line lasted long enough that Ivo checked whether the call had dropped. When the voice came back, it had dropped its caution entirely, and what remained underneath sounded less like surprise than like dread arriving precisely on schedule.

"She left something for you," the woman said. "In the walls. God help you — she left something in the walls."
