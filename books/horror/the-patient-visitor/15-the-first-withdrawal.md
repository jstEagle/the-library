## 15. The First Withdrawal

Priya woke in the dark with the certainty that something was missing.

Not a sound — the house was silent in the way of houses that had been listening all night and had finally decided to stop pretending otherwise. Not light — the curtains in Edith Vann's guest room hung heavy as wet wool, and no gray came through them yet. It was subtler than that. It was like waking in her own apartment and knowing, before opening her eyes, that one of her plants had died overnight. A subtraction she could feel only by its absence.

She lay still and took inventory, because inventory was what you did when you were frightened. She'd learned it from Ivo, actually — the way their mother used to count the silverware after parties, and Ivo had turned it into a profession, counting rooms instead of forks. Priya counted her own body the way she counted stock at the shop: fingers, ten; the ache in her left knee from the drive up; the bruise on her hip from the door that had swung shut behind her yesterday with the finality of a verdict.

Then she tried to smell the room.

She couldn't describe what was wrong with that sentence, not at first. The room simply had no smell. Not mustiness, though the wallpaper behind the bed was peeling and there should have been decades of damp and old glue and mouse. Not dust. Not the cold mineral breath of an unheated house. Nothing. She breathed in deeply, deliberately, the way you do when you're trying to catch a scent — and it was like inhaling through glass. Air went in. Information did not.

She sat up too fast and pressed her face into the pillow, into the blanket, into the sleeve of the sweater she'd draped over the chair. Wool, she thought. It should smell of wool, of lanolin, of the cedar drawer she kept it in. Her mind supplied the *memory* of those smells with perfect fidelity — cedar, lanolin, a ghost of her own perfume — but the memory arrived without its delivery mechanism. Like reading a menu for a meal she could no longer taste.

"No," she said aloud, and her voice sounded small and stupid in the big dark room.

She got up, found her phone, and turned on the flashlight. In the white circle of light she held the sweater to her nose again, then a bottle of hand lotion from her bag — lavender, aggressively lavender, the kind Ivo always complained smelled like an old lady's funeral. Nothing. She uncapped it and held it under her nose until her eyes watered, and felt nothing but the cold burn of alcohol fumes, and even that was more sensation than scent, a physical sting rather than a smell.

The house had taken her sense of smell sometime between midnight and waking, and it hadn't even left a receipt.

* * *

She found Ivo in the kitchen, sitting at the table with her laptop open and three mugs of tea arranged in front of her like evidence. Only one of them was hers. The other two had gone cold hours ago; Priya could tell by the skin on the surface, or she would have been able to tell, once, by looking — now she just knew it the way you know arithmetic.

"You're up early," Ivo said, without looking up. Then she did look up, and whatever was in Priya's face made her close the laptop slowly, the way you close a door on a room you don't want something to escape from.

"I can't smell anything," Priya said. "Ivo. I can't smell *anything*."

Ivo's face did something complicated — guilt first, quick and naked, then a professional flattening over it, the expression she wore when a client asked about the crack in the foundation and she didn't want them to hear how bad the crack was. "Sit down," Ivo said. "Tell me exactly what happened."

"Nothing happened. That's the point. I went to sleep and I woke up and—" Priya heard her own voice climbing and made herself stop, made herself sit, made herself breathe. Through glass. "It took it. While I was sleeping. Like a—" She almost said *like a thief*, but thieves left windows open, took things you could report, things with serial numbers. This was more like a tax. Something collected quietly, administratively, by an authority that didn't negotiate.

"Did you dream?" Ivo asked.

Priya tried to remember. There had been a dream, or the edge of one — she had a fragment of standing in a long corridor doing something repetitive, something with paper, and a voice asking her questions in a tone that wasn't unkind, exactly. Patient. The patience of a customs officer. "I don't know. Maybe. Why?"

"Because last night I asked it questions," Ivo said. "On paper. Left the recorder running." She turned the laptop around. On the screen was an audio file, waveform flat and gray, and beneath it a transcript she'd clearly been working on since before dawn. Priya read down the list of questions — *What are you? What do you want? What did Edith Vann promise you?* — and the answers, in that second voice Ivo had played for her yesterday, calm and genderless and horribly reasonable.

"What do you want?" Priya read aloud. "'To keep what is mine.'"

"It keeps people alive," Ivo said. "That's the bargain. That's what Edith bought. But it has to feed, and what it feeds on is—" She stopped. Started again. "Everyone who knows the truth gets consumed. And while it's deciding how much it needs, it takes smaller amounts. Advances. It's been taking things from me all week. My mother's ring is gone from my bag. Not lost — gone, and the memory of wearing it is going fuzzy at the edges, like a photograph left in the sun."

"So it's charging me rent," Priya said. "For being in the house."

"I think it's appraising you," Ivo said, and laughed, and the laugh was terrible, hollow, the laugh of someone who has realized her own vocabulary belongs to the monster. "God help me, Priya, I think that's literally what it's doing. Working out what you're worth."

* * *

They got through the morning by agreeing not to talk about it. This was a family skill, honed over decades: after Dad's diagnosis they'd gotten very good at conversations about the garden, about traffic on the M6, about anything that wasn't the thing. Priya made breakfast she couldn't smell — eggs, toast, coffee that might as well have been warm water — and ate mechanically, tasting only texture and temperature, salt and sweet registering as abstract facts rather than pleasures. She caught herself holding the coffee cup under her nose anyway, again and again, like worrying a tooth.

By afternoon she'd moved through fury into bargaining. She stood in the middle of the hall and addressed the house out loud, feeling ridiculous and doing it anyway.

"If you want something, take it and tell me. Don't creep around taking bits. What do you want? Money? I've got maybe four thousand pounds to my name. Take it. Leave my nose alone."

The hallway said nothing, which was somehow worse than an answer. She had the distinct impression of a document being read over, of red ink, of a figure circled and found insufficient.

That evening Ivo spread everything on the dining table — the recordings, the journal pages she'd pulled from behind the wallpaper, her own notebook with its columns of rationalizations now crossed out one by one. Priya read Edith Vann's handwriting by lamplight and felt the shape of the trap close around her understanding: *it keeps one person alive by consuming everyone who learns the truth.* Edith had been dying. Edith had made a deal. And everyone who figured out the terms became currency.

"I should go home," Priya said. "If I leave, if I don't know anything—"

"The door locked behind you," Ivo said quietly. "You felt it."

"But surely I can *leave*. People leave houses. It's a house."

Neither of them said what they were both thinking, which was that neither of them believed it was only a house anymore, and that the well at the property line had echoed like it held water, and that the neighbor's warning — *don't lean over it* — had been delivered in the tone people use for warnings they've given many times, to many people, most of whom hadn't listened.

They slept in the same room that night, beds dragged together, both recorders running. Priya fell asleep listening to her sister's breathing and the house's silence, and told herself that whatever it wanted from her, it would have to get past her first, and she was harder to get past than she looked. Their father used to say that about her. *Your sister looks like a breeze would knock her over,* he'd say, *but she's the one who holds the line.*

She dreamed of him that night, and in the dream she couldn't see his face.

* * *

She woke crying and didn't know why.

This was the cruelest part, she understood immediately, lying in the gray pre-dawn with tears cooling on her cheeks and grief sitting on her chest like a hand: the house hadn't just taken the memory. It had taken the memory *of the memory*. She knew she was grieving. She did not know for what. It was like arriving at a funeral where everyone else mourned a name that had been removed from the order of service.

There had been a man. She knew there had been a man — their father, obviously, she knew the word, she knew the fact of him, the biography of him: David Priyanka-something-Mercer, no — David Mercer, electrician, loved cricket badly and cooking well, died when Priya was nineteen. She knew all of it the way she knew the capital of Peru. Facts. A file. And somewhere behind the file there should have been a face, and there was nothing. Not darkness, not blur — nothing, a smooth blank where a face should be, like a photograph with the emulsion scraped away.

She tried to build it from parts. Brown eyes — was that right? She thought so. Heavy eyebrows. A way of tilting his head when he was laughing at his own jokes before he finished telling them. She assembled these fragments and they wouldn't cohere; they slid off each other like oiled stones. Every time she got close to the whole, it dissolved, and underneath the dissolution was a sensation she had never felt before and hoped never to feel again: the direct experience of a hole. Grief with the object amputated. Mourning a wound that had been surgically, cleanly excised, leaving the scar tissue and none of the injury.

"Ivo," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word. "Ivo, wake up. I can't—"

Ivo woke instantly, the way she always had, already halfway upright. "What is it? What did it take?"

"How do you know it took something?"

"Because you're crying and you don't know why. I've watched myself do it twice this week." Ivo's hand found hers in the dark, gripped hard. "What is it?"

"Dad." The word came out wrecked. "I can't see his face. I know everything about him except — Ivo, I can't *see* him. He's gone and I can't even remember what I'm missing, I just know it's big, I just know I'm bleeding from somewhere I can't find—"

"Stop." Ivo pulled her phone off the nightstand, thumbs moving fast. "Photos. We have photos. Come on."

They huddled over the screen in the blue-gray light, and Ivo scrolled with grim purpose through years of camera rolls, back through family gatherings, back and back, until she found them: photographs of their father. Christmas, maybe fifteen years ago, him in an apron shaped like a chili pepper, flour on his nose. Him at Priya's graduation, arm around her shoulders, mouth open mid-laugh.

Priya stared.

And the photographs were — they were pictures of a man. She could see him, technically, the way you can see a stranger in a crowd. Dark-haired, broad, smiling. But recognition didn't come. It was like being shown a photo of a classmate from a school she'd never attended and being told *this is your best friend, this is the man who taught you to ride a bike, look how much you love him.* The image went in and hit nothing. No warmth. No click. Just data.

"That's him," she said, testing the words. "That's Dad."

"Yes."

"I believe you. Do you understand? I *believe* you, the way you believe a stranger. I'm looking at my own father and I feel nothing, and yesterday I would have felt—" Her voice failed. She handed the phone back as if it burned. "Yesterday I would have felt everything."

Ivo put the phone down and pulled her in, and Priya let herself be held, shaking, furious, weeping without an object, grief pouring out of a hole whose edges she couldn't even locate. Somewhere below them, or around them, or inside the walls — she no longer cared to distinguish — she felt the house settle, satisfied, the way a ledger settles. An entry closed. A balance struck.

*It's pricing me,* she thought into her sister's shoulder. *It's working out what I cost and what I yield, and it's taking payment in installments, and it started with the cheap things — smell, a face — and it will work upward.*

* * *

She spent the day learning to grieve properly, which meant grieving blind.

She sat at the kitchen table with Ivo's laptop and the photographs and made herself look, hour after hour, trying to force recognition back through sheer repetition, saying *this is Dad, this is Dad, this is Dad* like a spell that refused to take. Ivo narrated for her, quietly, when she could bear it: *He used to make pancakes shaped like animals. He cried at the end of Old Yeller every single time and pretended he had something in his eye. He called you Firecracker because you were born during Diwali and screamed the loudest of anyone in the maternity ward.*

"Firecracker," Priya repeated. The word landed somewhere soft and produced no image, only warmth — a residue, a stain of feeling with the picture bleached out. She clung to the warmth. If the house could take faces, apparently it couldn't quite take love itself, not yet, not clean. It left the ash of it.

In the late afternoon she wrote his name on a piece of paper — *David Mercer* — and then, beneath it, everything she could verify: electrician. Cricket. Cooking. Died when I was nineteen. Firecracker. She wrote until the page was full, an inventory of a man, an appraisal of a father, line items where a person used to be. When she finished she understood, with a clarity that felt like a second loss, that she was doing exactly what Ivo did for a living, and that the house had known she would, and that perhaps that was part of the price too — that it was teaching her to see people the way it saw them. As assets. As square footage. As value to be extracted.

That night, the voice came to her directly for the first time.

She was half asleep, drifting in the shallow water between waking and dreams, when she heard it — not through the recorder, not through the wall, but *in*, intimate, courteous, pitched like a hotel receptionist confirming a reservation.

*"You are valued,"* it said. *"Your account is active. Further assessments will follow."*

And then, softer, almost kind, in a cadence she would later realize belonged to a dead woman:

*"Your sister bargains well. But everyone signs eventually."*

Priya lay awake until dawn beside her sister, memorizing the warmth of her hand, the sound of her breathing, the exact weight of her head against her shoulder — cataloguing, hoarding, appraising — because she had finally understood what the house was teaching her, and she intended to learn the lesson better than it did.

Because tomorrow, while Ivo slept, she was going to find the well.
