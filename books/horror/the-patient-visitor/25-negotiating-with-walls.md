## 25. Negotiating With Walls

Ivo woke at four in the morning with the answer already half-formed in her mind, the way answers sometimes arrive — not as thought but as structure, a shape she could feel before she could read it.

Priya was asleep in the armchair across the room, her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for an appointment that had been postponed indefinitely. Since the house had begun taking from her, she slept more. She slept like someone being paid by the hour to do it. Ivo had watched her for a long time last night, cataloguing what was gone: the smell of coffee, apparently, because Priya had held a cup under her nose and described it as *warm*. And something else, something Priya hadn't mentioned and Ivo hadn't dared ask about, because asking would only tell the house what to look at next.

*It takes what you name,* Ivo had written in her notebook on the second night. She underlined it now, in the dark, by the light of her phone. *So stop naming things.*

But there was more than that. There was something she'd been circling for days without letting herself land on it, because landing on it meant admitting what she actually did for a living — not measuring houses, not really. She read documents. She found the sentence inside the paragraph where everyone else had stopped reading. Every appraisal she'd ever saved a client money on had been, at bottom, an act of close reading. A deed with an easement buried in its fourth page. A covenant that expired if anyone had bothered to check the date. Contracts were not walls. Contracts were doors with hinges, and hinges could be swung either way.

The amendment was upstairs, in Edith's hidden study, but Ivo had copied it out by hand two nights ago, all eleven clauses of it, in her own careful surveyor's lettering. She took the copy from beneath her mattress now and spread it on the floor between the bed and the chair, weighting the corners with her shoes, her phone, the cold cup of tea she'd abandoned at midnight.

*In consideration of continued habitation,* clause three began, *the undersigned grants unto the co-owner the full enjoyment of the premises, including all appurtenances thereto.*

Appurtenances. Her eyes caught on the word the way they'd been catching on it for two days. In property law, an appurtenance was anything that ran with the land — a right, a privilege, a fixture. But appurtenances were defined by the grantor's intent at the time of signing. They were not infinite. They were bounded by what the parties had reasonably understood themselves to be granting.

Edith Vann had signed to save her own life. Whatever she had granted, she had granted *in extremis*, under duress, with a terminal diagnosis sitting on her chest like a stone. And duress, Ivo knew — duress was the oldest loophole in contract law. An agreement extracted under threat of death was not an agreement. It was a mugging with paperwork.

She sat back on her heels, heart going hard against her ribs.

The house was listening. She could feel it the way you feel someone standing behind you in an empty room — not a sound, not a draft, just a change in the pressure of the air, as though the walls had leaned in slightly. Good, she thought. Let it listen. Let it hear me think in its own language.

* * *

She waited until seven. Sunrise came late this time of year, grey and slow through the east windows, and she made herself eat half a piece of toast and drink water from the tap — which still tasted faintly of stone, of depth, of the well — because she refused to negotiate hungry. That was rule one, learned across a decade of conference tables: never let them see you need it more than they do.

Then she went upstairs to the study.

The door was open. It was always open now, since the night she and Priya had found it, as though the house wanted her in there, wanted her handling the documents, wanted her marinating in the terms. The room smelled of dust and old varnish and, underneath both, something organic and patient, like the air over a pond in late summer.

The amendment lay on the desk where she'd left it. Beside it, she placed her notebook, open to a fresh page. At the top she wrote, in letters large enough to be read from across a room:

**REQUEST FOR MODIFICATION OF TERMS — MONTROSE / CO-OWNER**

Her hand shook on the first stroke. She steadied it. This was the part she knew how to do. This was the only part she knew how to do.

"Clause three," she said aloud. Her voice sounded small in the study, flattened by the low ceiling, absorbed by the walls the way sound is absorbed by a recording booth. "Clause three grants the co-owner 'all appurtenances thereto.' Appurtenances are bounded by grantor intent. Edith Vann signed under documented medical duress. Her intent was self-preservation. Not acquisition. Not accumulation." She paused. Somewhere below the floorboards, or beyond them, or inside them, something shifted its weight. "Therefore the taking of memories — which are personal property, severable from the realty, never mentioned in any clause — exceeds the scope of the original grant."

Silence. The kind of silence that has texture.

Then, from everywhere and nowhere — from the plaster, from the desk itself, from the space an inch from her left ear — the voice.

It spoke in Edith's cadence. It had spoken in Edith's cadence since the first recording, and Ivo no longer flinched at it, though her body flinched for her, a fine tremor through the shoulders she couldn't suppress.

**You are offering me a legal opinion.**

"I'm offering you a correction," Ivo said. "You've been over-collecting. You've been taking assets outside the estate."

**And if I have?**

"Then the grant is voidable. Duress vitiates consent. Every term of it. Including the term that keeps Edith alive." She let that sit. It was a bluff, mostly — she didn't know if the entity feared unmaking, didn't know if it *could* fear — but bluffs worked best when delivered flatly, with the pen already moving. "I'm not threatening rescission. Yet. I'm proposing an amendment. A clean one. Narrowed scope, enumerated takings, and a schedule."

**A schedule,** the voice said, and there was something new in it. Something that took Ivo a moment to identify, because she had never heard it here before, and when she identified it her stomach dropped through the floor and kept dropping.

Amusement.

**You want to negotiate a payment plan.**

"I want to negotiate *terms*," Ivo said. "That's all any of this has ever been. Your whole mechanism is contractual. Invitation, consideration, term of years. You built yourself into a deed. Which means you live by deed law. Which means—" she turned the notebook toward the empty room, toward whatever read it, "—you can be amended."

The temperature in the study fell by degrees she could feel rather than measure. Frost feathered silently along the inside of the window glass, growing outward from the corners like lichen. And the voice laughed.

It was a terrible sound. Not because it was cruel — cruelty she had braced for — but because it was genuinely delighted, warm even, the laugh of a colleague who has just watched a junior associate finally say something interesting. It laughed in Edith's voice, with Edith's slight hoarseness at the top of each breath, and Ivo understood suddenly and completely why the recordings had unsettled her more than the footsteps ever had. The thing wasn't imitating Edith. It had *worn* her. For years. It wore her still.

**Name your terms,** it said.

* * *

They negotiated for six hours.

No one else on earth would have recognized it as negotiation. There was no table, no handshake, no counterparty visible in any direction. Ivo sat at Edith's desk with her notebook and spoke clauses into the cold air, and the house answered in frost and settling timber and that worn, patient voice, and somewhere in the middle of it Ivo realized she was doing the best work of her career, and hated herself for the small hot pride that rose in her chest anyway.

The first concession came easily. Too easily.

**The sister,** the entity said, **is a recent accession. Her presence was not contemplated by the original grant. I will release her claim upon her — entirely — in exchange for a single extension: the term of your own tenancy, extended from indefinite to perpetual.**

"No," Ivo said immediately. "Perpetuity is how you got Edith. Perpetuity is the trap. Fixed term. Five years."

**Ten.**

"Three."

A pause, in which the frost on the window grew a quarter inch and then, deliberately, stopped growing.

**Five,** the entity said, **and I keep the memory she lost yesterday. Returned memories decay the vessel. Consider it a non-refundable processing fee.**

Ivo closed her eyes. Priya's face — their father's face, gone from her; the smell of coffee, gone. What else had it taken while Ivo slept? What had Priya woken up missing and not yet noticed, the way you don't notice a missing tooth until your tongue finds the hole?

"One returned item," Ivo said. "My choice which. As a show of good faith, before I sign anything."

**Agreed.**

The ease of it should have warned her. It did warn her, distantly, the way a surveyor notes a crack in a foundation and files it under *monitor*. But Priya was downstairs, and every hour of negotiation was an hour of protection, and protection now felt like oxygen. She said, "The smell of coffee. Return that."

The house was quiet for a long moment. Then, from the kitchen directly below them — she heard it through the floor, unmistakably — the sound of the percolator starting. The gurgle and knock of water rising through old pipes. The smell bloomed up through the floorboards, rich and dark and impossible, because there were no grounds in that machine, had been no grounds in that machine for three years.

Downstairs, Priya made a sound. A small, confused, waking sound, followed by the creak of the armchair, followed by her voice, wondering, cracked: "Ivo? Why does it smell like—"

Ivo pressed her hand flat against the desk to keep it steady. "Good faith received," she said. "Now the schedule. Enumerated takings only. No general discretion. Each withdrawal requires stated cause."

**Stated cause,** the entity repeated, tasting it. **Very well. Cause one: maintenance of the premises. Cause two: satisfaction of arrears. Cause three—**

"Those aren't causes, those are categories. A cause states what, and why, and against whom."

**You are drafting my restraint,** the voice said, and the delight again, undimmed, bottomless. **Do you know how long it has been since anyone tried to bind me with my own paper? The last one who tried wrote his terms in blood and I kept the blood.**

"Who?"

**Irrelevant. Continue.**

She continued. Through the morning and into the afternoon she sat in the dead woman's study and drafted a living woman's surrender, clause by clause, and the house met her at every point, conceding here, holding there, and Ivo — this was the part she would remember later, lying awake, replaying it — Ivo *enjoyed* it. There was a version of her that had spent ten years negotiating with banks and heirs and men who thought a woman with a laser level was a secretary, and that version had been starving, and this was the first table in a decade where her opposite number was neither condescending nor bored. The entity was the best reader she had ever faced. It caught her ambiguities before she finished speaking them. Twice it quoted her own earlier phrasing back at her, verbatim, and used it to widen a gap she'd been trying to close.

By two o'clock they had a document. She wrote it out fair, in ink, on the back of Edith's survey report — the identical one, the mirror of her own, and she chose that paper deliberately, because paper remembers what's written on it, and she wanted this house to watch itself being amended on its own confession.

**Read it aloud,** the entity said, when she finished. **Execution requires utterance. All my instruments do.**

She read it. Clause by clause: fixed five-year term; enumerated takings with stated cause; Priya released entire; no withdrawals from either sister during the term except upon stated cause entered in writing — and here Ivo had slipped in her masterstroke, the thing she'd been building toward all day: *entered in writing.* If the house had to state its causes in writing, then the takings became record. Record could be audited. Audited could be contested.

The last line she read standing up, facing the window, so that the frost and the grey light and whatever stood behind the frost could all hear it clearly:

*"This amendment supersedes all prior understandings inconsistent herewith."*

**Executed,** said the house.

The relief hit her like warm water. She actually sagged, one hand finding the edge of the desk, breathing like she'd climbed stairs. Downstairs, Priya called her name again, and her voice had color in it now, curiosity, the ordinary music of a person with a question about coffee.

It was three more hours before Ivo understood what she had done.

* * *

She found it at dusk, rereading her own fair copy by lamplight while Priya napped — really napped, deeply, smelling the air like a woman rediscovering weather.

The flaw was in clause four. *Hers*, not the entity's. She had written — she checked her field notes to confirm it, and there it was, in her own nine o'clock handwriting — she had drafted the enumerated-takings clause to apply to *"withdrawals during the term."*

During the term. Five years.

Which meant that everything the house had already taken — everything taken *before* execution — stood outside the amendment entirely. Unreturned. Unauditable. Priya's father's face. Whatever else had gone missing in the nights Ivo had slept through. All of it was now grandfathered. Locked in. Ratified by the very document meant to claw it back.

And worse. She read clause four a third time, and then a fourth, and the cold moved up her spine vertebra by vertebra, because the entity had agreed to everything too quickly, had conceded the schedule, the writing requirement, the fixed term, all of it, with the eagerness of a player whose opponent has just moved a piece exactly where it wanted —

— because the amendment said *no withdrawals from either sister except upon stated cause entered in writing.* Either sister. During the term. And Ivo Montrose had just signed a five-year instrument that guaranteed, in black and white, in her own careful surveyor's lettering, that she and Priya would remain on the premises for five years.

She had come to negotiate a release.

She had executed a lease.

The lamp flickered. Outside, far off at the property line, the well made a sound it had not made before — not the dry echo, not the wet whisper, but a deep, contented settling, like a ledger closing at the end of a fiscal year.

From the walls, gently, almost kindly, in Edith's worn-out voice, the house said:

**Well played. Come back anytime.**

Downstairs, in her sleep, Priya began to speak — a flat, reciting monotone, listing measurements — and Ivo was moving before she finished hearing it, down the stairs two at a time, the amendment fluttering forgotten from her hand.
