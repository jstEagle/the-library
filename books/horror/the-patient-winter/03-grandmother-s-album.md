## 3. Grandmother's Album

The house smelled the way it always had—cedar and lavender and something underneath, something older, like the inside of a drawer that had never been opened. Theo stood in the doorway of Edith Dravenmoor's bedroom with a box of trash bags under one arm and felt, absurdly, like he should knock.

She'd been gone eight months. The house had been waiting for him ever since, patient as winter, holding everything exactly where she'd left it. Her glasses folded on the nightstand. A library book face-down on the arm of her chair, spine cracked at page 212. A half-finished crossword on the kitchen table, filled out in her small, precise hand: *seventeen across, "longing," four letters*—*ache*. She'd gotten it right. She'd gotten everything right, his grandmother, and now he was here to bag it all up and carry it to the dump.

He'd taken the Hollowmere job partly because of this house. That was the honest version, anyway—the version he gave the superintendent, who'd raised an eyebrow when Theo said he had local family. *Had*, he'd corrected himself too late. The other version, the one he didn't say out loud even to himself, was that after Claire left he needed somewhere small enough to disappear into, and Hollowmere had looked on the map like a place where a man could be forgotten professionally. A town with one school, one road in, one road out. Eleven months of snow.

He started with the bedroom because it seemed kindest to start anywhere but the kitchen.

Edith had been a secretary at Blackpine School for thirty-one years. He knew this the way you know things about grandparents—not as facts learned but as weather, constant and unexamined. There were plaques. There was a coffee mug with a cartoon owl wearing reading glasses that said WORLD'S BEST SECRETARY, chipped at the handle, still sitting by the sink as if she might come back for it. And there were photographs. Photographs everywhere, but especially in the front room, arranged on the mantel and the sideboard and two dedicated shelves, a museum of a life spent mostly in one building with children moving through it like water.

Theo worked through the bedroom slowly, folding sweaters he couldn't bring himself to bag. In the closet he found shoeboxes of photographs, unsorted, hundreds of them, and he sat down on the floor among them like a kid and started going through them one by one. Edith young, squinting into sun. Edith holding a baby—his mother, or maybe his uncle, the faces too similar to tell. Edith at a lake, laughing at someone outside the frame, and Theo found himself staring at that one a long time, trying to imagine what could make her laugh like that, trying to hear it.

It was nearly dark when he found the staff photo.

It was larger than the others, eight by ten, mounted on cardstock with a gold border gone brown at the edges. *BLACKPINE SCHOOL FACULTY & STAFF — WINTER TERM, 1974.* They stood on the school's front steps in two rows, back row standing, front row seated on the steps, everyone in wool coats and the stiff, patient expressions of people posing in cold. Theo counted them without meaning to. Fourteen adults. He recognized none of them except, in the front row, second from the left, a young woman with dark hair pinned up and a smile she was clearly holding in check for the camera.

His grandmother. Thirty years old, maybe less. He'd never seen her young. It was like meeting a stranger who knew all his secrets.

He was smiling at her—at the girl she'd been—when he noticed the scratch.

Back row, far right. A man in a dark coat, tall, standing slightly apart from the others, and where his face should have been there was nothing. Not faded, not damaged. *Scratched.* Deliberately, deeply, with something sharp—a pin, a knife tip—gouged down to the white of the paper, over and over, until the features were obliterated. The scratches hadn't torn the photo; they'd worried it, worked at it, the way you'd worry a stain. Someone had spent time on this. Someone had wanted that face gone and had made sure.

Theo turned the photograph over. On the back, in Edith's hand, the same small precise letters:

*We keep what we keep.*

No date. No name. Just that, and beneath it, almost too faint to read, pressed so lightly the pen had barely kissed the paper: *I'm sorry.*

He sat on the closet floor until his legs went numb, turning the photo over and over, looking for anything else—a mark, a clue, some way to know which of these fourteen people had been erased and why. The scratched-out man's body told him nothing. Dark coat, hands in pockets, head tilted slightly toward the woman beside him as if listening to something she was saying. Everyone else faced the camera. Only he leaned toward her.

Only he had no face.

Outside, the light went blue and then gray and then gone. Theo didn't remember deciding to stay the night; the house decided for him, the way it must have decided things for Edith for fifty years. He made tea in her kitchen, sat in her chair, put the staff photo on the table beside the unfinished crossword, and told himself the cold feeling in his stomach was just the house being empty. Houses did that. Empty houses got loud.

* * *

He woke at some black hour—he never learned exactly when—to the sound of the album falling.

Not the shoebox photos. The album: the big green one on the living room shelf, the family album, the one he'd flipped through as a boy while Edith narrated every picture. It had hit the hardwood with a slap, and Theo lay in the guest bed listening to the silence afterward, telling himself it was a shelf bracket, settling wood, the ordinary physics of an old house in February cold.

He got up anyway. He told himself it was because he wanted to make sure the album wasn't damaged.

The living room was moonlit, snow-light, blue and silver. The album lay open on the floor, pages splayed, and Theo crouched and gathered it up—and stopped, because it had fallen open to a page he hadn't turned to yet. He'd only gotten partway through the shoeboxes. This page was from later, from the seventies by the clothes, and it held three photographs: Edith in front of the school, Edith with a group of children on the playground, and a wide shot of the schoolyard in winter, kids scattered across the snow like punctuation marks.

He knew the third photograph. He'd known it since he was six years old. Edith used to hold him on her lap and point at it: *That's the school, that's the big pine, that's me by the door.* He could have drawn it from memory. The yard, the treeline beyond the fence, the children, the snow. No chair. There had never been a chair in that photograph. He would have remembered a chair; he had, in fact, built entire childhood games around the contents of this exact picture.

In the middle of the schoolyard, dead center, plain as a nail driven into clean wood, stood a wooden chair.

Simple. Straight-backed. Unpainted. Facing the camera—or facing *him*, it was impossible not to feel it that way—empty, casting a long thin shadow across the snow toward the treeline. It belonged to the photograph the way a splinter belongs to a finger. Everything around it had continued undisturbed: the children played, the snow sparkled, Edith smiled by the door. None of them looked at the chair. None of them seemed to see it at all.

Theo carried the album to the kitchen table, turned on every light in the house, and set the staff photo beside it. The scratched-out man. The empty chair. *We keep what we keep. I'm sorry.*

He went back through the album, page by page, fast now, his breath loud in the quiet house. Nothing. Nothing. Nothing. Then, near the end—the most recent pictures, Edith old and gray at a retirement party—he stopped.

In the background of the last photograph, behind the cake and the balloons, in the corner of the school cafeteria, stood a wooden chair. Plain. Straight-backed. Unpainted. Empty.

It hadn't been there when he was a child. He was sure of it the way you're sure of your own name. He had studied this album the way other kids studied comic books. There had been no chair. There had never been any chair.

And yet here it was, in two photographs fifty years apart, in the same impossible grain of wood, the same stubborn shadow—as if it had always been there and he'd simply never been allowed to notice, or as if it had just arrived, walking backward through the decades to take its place.

He touched the page. The paper was cold. Colder than the room, colder than the window glass beside it, cold the way metal is cold, the way a doorknob is cold at three in the morning when something has just come through the house.

Theo sat down in his grandmother's chair and looked at the empty one in the photograph and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the photograph was not showing him the past.

It was showing him an invitation.

And somewhere in the treeline past the edge of the picture, whatever the chair was for was already counting.

* * *

He didn't sleep again. When the sky finally went the color of dishwater, he showered, dressed, and drove to Blackpine School through streets so quiet his tires sounded like a confession. He arrived early—earlier than any teacher needed to be—and let himself into the building with the keys they'd given him, and walked the halls alone, past the framed class photographs that lined the corridor between the office and the gym.

Class of '73. Class of '75. Class of '72, '76, '71, '77. He walked it twice before he admitted what he was looking for, and what wasn't there.

There was no photograph for the winter term of 1974. Between '73 and '75 there was a gap in the wall exactly the size of a frame—a rectangle of unfaded paint, sharp-edged, clean, like a tooth pulled from an otherwise perfect jaw.

Someone had taken it down. Recently. The paint around it had never yellowed because the frame had hung there for fifty years and been removed within the last few months—since, say, the death of the school's longtime secretary, since the house on Maple Street had gone empty and quiet, since its keys had been mailed to a grandson in another state.

Theo stood in the hallway with his breath fogging, looking at the gap, and thought about a young woman in a staff photo, smiling with her mouth closed, and a man beside her with no face, and a chair standing in the snow.

Then, from the end of the hall, where the janitor was working his way toward him with a mop bucket, Theo heard it—low, unhurried, almost musical, the way a man talks when he isn't talking to anyone at all.

"Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen."

A pause. The squeak of the mop.

"Seventeen."

Theo counted the doors between himself and the sound, and there were eighteen, and the janitor's voice stopped exactly at seventeen, and did not go on.
