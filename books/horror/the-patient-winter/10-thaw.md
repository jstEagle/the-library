## 10. Thaw

The root cellar door had been buried under fifty years of snowfall and fifty years of pretending, but the storm had stripped the hillside bare, and Theo went down into it on his hands and knees, the way you enter any grave.

Inside, the cold was different. Not the wind's cold, not the storm's cold—this was a held breath. A patience. The dark down there had texture, like wool pressed against his face, and it smelled of earth and apples long rotted and something underneath both that he could only call *waiting*.

He had Edith's ledger page in his coat, folded against his chest where his heartbeat could reach it. He had her hymnal in his bag, open to the page she'd marked—the torn corner fitting the ledger scrap like a wound to its knife. And he had the name.

He'd carried it for three days without saying it aloud. Not in the truck. Not when the elders stood in his classroom with their faces like closed doors and offered him Edith's place, her chair at the table, her share of the quiet. Not even alone, mouthing it silently, because some part of him understood that this word was not shaped for silence. It had been kept too long. Names are meant to be spoken the way seeds are meant to be planted, and Wren Halloway had been sitting in the dark ground of Hollowmere's collective throat for half a century.

The cellar floor was dirt. In the beam of his flashlight he could see the old shelves, the crocks, the ladder to the collapsed hatch above. And on the far wall, at the height of a child's shoulder, small marks scratched into the stone. Tally marks. Hundreds of them. Some deep, some faint, some so worn they were only shadows of counting.

She had counted too. All these years, whatever was left of her down here had counted, because that was what the winter taught everyone in Hollowmere eventually: count the doors, count the days, count the chairs. Counting was how you held the shape of yourself when everything else was being taken.

Theo knelt in the dirt. His knees sank through the frost crust into soil that was not frozen, though everything above it was. The ground here had never thawed and never fully froze. It was the seam. The hinge. The place where the town had opened its hand and let the winter take what it asked for.

"Wren," he said.

The dark did not move. The dark *listened*. He felt it lean toward him the way a room leans toward an open window.

"Halloway."

The name came out of him whole, and saying it was like pulling a splinter from a wound so old the flesh had grown around it. His mouth tasted of iron and pine sap. Somewhere above him, the storm stopped—not faded, not lulled, *stopped*, mid-gust, as if the whole sky had been caught holding its breath.

He said it again, because once did not feel like enough. Once was a whisper; this needed to be a bell.

"Wren Halloway. Wren Halloway. You were eight years old. You liked to draw birds on the corners of your worksheets. Your grandmother made you mittens with red thread through them so you couldn't lose them." He was crying now, and he let himself, because Edith's journal had told him that much and more, and grief was the only currency this transaction accepted. "You were real. You were here. They took your name and left you nothing to be, and I'm giving it back. Do you hear me? I'm giving it back."

The tally marks on the wall began to glow. Faintly, like embers under ash—one, then ten, then all of them, hundreds of small scratches filling with light the color of dawn through ice.

And then the cellar exhaled.

It was not wind. It was warmer than wind. It moved through him and out through the collapsed hatch above, up through the snow, out across the hills, and Theo understood—with the certainty you feel in dreams—that it was going everywhere at once. Into Blackpine School, where thirteen chairs sat facing the windows. Into the church basement where the elders kept their minutes and their silences. Into every house in Hollowmere, under every bed, behind every careful gap in every photograph on every wall.

The light in the tally marks went out all at once, and in its place there was nothing on the wall at all. No scratches. No marks. Smooth stone, as if no one had ever counted anything there—as if waiting had finally been allowed to end.

Theo climbed out of the cellar into a night that was not night anymore.

* * *

The storm had broken apart like a fever breaking. Stars stood over the treeline, hard and clean, and the snow on the hills glowed blue-white, and the air smelled—Theo stood very still to confirm it—smelled of *thaw*. Of mud under ice. Of the particular green rot of spring coming early and uninvited.

At the treeline, they were walking out of the trees.

Eleven of them. Small. Unhurried. Dressed in the clothes of 1974—wool coats, corduroy pants, one girl in a yellow rain slicker far too bright for winter. They came out of the pines the way children come out of a game of hide-and-seek when they've decided to stop hiding: casually, as if no time had passed at all, as if the person calling them home had simply taken a while to say the name right.

They had not aged a day. That was the thing that broke something loose in Theo's chest—not horror, not anymore, but a grief so large it had passed clean through terror and come out the other side as something almost like tenderness. Fifty years. Fifty winters. And they walked out of the treeline aged eight years old, blinking at the stars, one of them rubbing his eyes like he'd just woken from a nap on the bus.

The girl in the yellow slicker stopped at the edge of the field. She looked toward the school, toward the lights Theo had left burning in every window, and then she looked at the ground, and her small face did something complicated—confusion folding into recognition folding into a question she didn't have words for yet.

Behind her, the others stopped too. Eleven children standing in the blue snow, and between them, where a twelfth should have been, there was nothing—and the nothing was different now. Not an absence. Just an empty space, the way any space is empty, the way air is empty, ordinary and harmless.

Wren Halloway was not among them. But neither was she gone. She was *named* now, which is a different thing than either, and Theo felt the truth of it settle into him like a stone into a riverbed: some debts can't be repaid, only acknowledged. He had given back what was taken. What the taking had cost—that was beyond anyone's power to return, and always would be.

The children began to walk toward the school. Toward the lights. Toward the doors that no longer needed counting.

* * *

By morning, Hollowmere was unrecognizable in the way that a face is unrecognizable after crying.

The chairs were gone. Every one of them—the thirteen from his classroom, the ones in the photographs, the one that had appeared in his own apartment with his name carved into its seat. Mara found the photograph albums in Edith's house wiped clean of them, the backgrounds suddenly, impossibly ordinary: hallways and classrooms and staff photos with no extra furniture anywhere, no careful gaps, no accumulating arithmetic of dread. Only the scratched-out face remained, and even that seemed different in daylight—less like erasure, more like a scar. Proof that something had happened here. Proof that someone had tried to hide it and failed.

The town did not celebrate. Theo had expected—what? Relief? Gratitude? Instead he watched the survivors of 1974 stand in the school parking lot in the strange warm morning, watching eleven children eat pancakes in the cafeteria through the window, and their faces were not joyful. They were the faces of people whose armor has been removed. For fifty years the compact had protected them—had protected their children and their children's children—from whatever else lived in the long winters, whatever else the famine-bargain had held at bay. Now the protection was dissolved. Now they were claimable again, all of them, every soul in Hollowmere, and they knew it, and they had chosen to let Theo speak anyway—or rather, they had failed to stop him, which in the arithmetic of loyalty amounts to the same confession.

Old Aldous Pike found him by his truck. The elder's hands were shaking, and he did not try to hide it.

"Fifty years," Pike said. "Fifty years we kept it, and it held. You understand that, don't you? It *held*. Nobody else was taken. Not one person, not one winter, all that time." His voice cracked on the last word. "And now?"

"Now it's honest," Theo said. "That's all I have. It's not safety. It's just true."

Pike looked at him for a long moment, and Theo saw the war behind the old man's eyes—the loyalty that had kept a town alive grinding against the knowledge that its price had been a child, compounded yearly, paid in silence. Then Pike nodded, once, slowly, like a man signing something, and walked away across the melting lot, leaving wet footprints that steamed in the sun.

Spring came early that year. Earlier than anyone could remember. The snow went soft and gray in a week, then vanished, and the hills showed green in February, and the old-timers said it was wrong, it was too fast, the land doesn't do that—but the land did it, as if released from a long-held pose, as if the whole valley had been clenching since 1974 and had finally, finally let go.

* * *

Mara Voss found him packing his truck on the last morning.

She stood at the end of the driveway with her hands in her pockets, twelve years old and older than that, the way kids get older in a single season when they learn what their town is made of. She'd stopped playing the counting game. All the children had. But she still recited names sometimes—he'd heard her doing it on the playground, quietly, to herself: the eleven from the treeline, learning them, keeping them, saying each one out loud the way you'd feed a fire.

"You're really leaving," she said. Not a question.

"I'm really leaving." Theo set the last box in the truck bed. Edith's hymnal was in it, and the ledger, and the photograph of the 1974 staff with its single ruined face. He'd thought about burning them. He'd decided the town would spend the next fifty years trying to forget, and someone ought to keep the evidence somewhere the forgetting couldn't reach.

"Are they okay?" Mara asked. "The kids. The little ones. They're staying with families and stuff, but—" She kicked at the mud. "They wake up at night. Asking for people who died a long time ago. One of them keeps asking for a girl named Wren. She says Wren promised to show her how to draw birds."

Theo crouched down to her level. The morning was warm enough that his breath didn't fog, and that still felt like a miracle, like a door left open in a house that had been sealed for half a century.

"They're okay," he said. "They're going to have bad nights for a long time. So will everyone here. That's what happens when you stop hiding from a thing—you have to actually live with it." He held her gaze. "You're going to be the one who remembers properly. I can tell. The others will want to forget, and you won't let them. Don't let them."

"I won't," Mara said, and he believed her.

She helped him carry the cooler to the truck, and when it was loaded she stepped back and looked up at him with an expression he'd seen before on the faces of people who had survived something and were deciding what story to tell about it.

"Mr. Dravenmoor? When you write it down someday—the book, or whatever you're going to do with all this—" She hesitated. "Say her name in it. All of it. Spelled right. Wren Halloway. So it's somewhere permanent. So nobody can ever take it again."

"It'll be the first line," Theo said. "I promise."

Mara nodded, satisfied, and turned to go. Then she stopped, halfway down the driveway, and looked back over her shoulder at him, and her face in the early spring light was solemn and bright and terribly young.

"Mr. Dravenmoor? Last night, one of the little girls said something weird. About the treeline." Mara's voice dropped. "She said that when they were walking out, she counted. Just habit, she said. From the game." Mara swallowed. "There were twelve of them when they started walking. Eleven when they got to the lights. And the one who left—she says it wasn't Wren. She says Wren stayed behind on purpose. To hold the door open for the rest of them." Mara's eyes were wide and steady. "She says Wren told her: *tell the teacher the counting isn't finished. Tell him some things wait longer than fifty years.*"

The morning breeze moved through the budding trees, warm and green and utterly wrong for February, carrying the smell of thawed earth from the direction of the hills.

From the treeline, faint and clear, came the sound of a child humming a counting rhyme—and stopping, deliberately, one name short.
