## 4. First Night Recording

Isandro Fairweather had slept in worse places than a derelict house. He had slept in a grain silo in Saskatchewan with the wind trying to peel the roof off, in a condemned tenement in Newark with the floor breathing under his cot, in a lighthouse keeper's cottage on a Scottish headland where the fog came through the stone as if the stone were only a rumor of a wall. He told himself this, unpacking the monitoring kit in the east bedroom, and the telling worked the way a hand pressed over a wound works: it stopped nothing, but it gave him something to hold down.

The temperature anomalies were real. He had verified them twice, then a third time because the third time was the one that let him sleep at night, metaphorically, since the actual sleeping was about to happen here. The house held cold in patterns that made no thermodynamic sense. The northwest corner of the second floor ran eleven degrees cooler than the room beside it, though they shared a wall and the same failing plaster. The cold moved. On Tuesday it had pooled in the stairwell like water finding a low place; by Thursday it had migrated to the parlor, sitting over the floorboards in a shape that his thermal camera rendered as a human silhouette, standing, arms at its sides, patient as furniture.

He had logged it. *Anomalous thermal mass, humanoid profile, non-responsive to acoustic stimulus.* He had written *non-responsive* the way a man writes a wish and calls it an observation.

The kit was his own design, assembled over years of appraising buildings that had opinions about being measured: four thermocouples on calibrated leads, a humidity logger, an omnidirectional microphone running to a handheld recorder with a fresh card, and a battery bank that would carry him through the night. He placed the thermocouples at the room's corners, taped the leads flat against the baseboard, and set the recorder on the folding table beside his cot, its red standby light a small patient eye.

The cot he positioned in the center of the room, equidistant from all four walls, because a surveyor's first instinct is always geometry. If something in this house wanted to approach him, it would have to cross a measured distance, and measured distances could be trusted. This was the profession's oldest comfort and its oldest lie, and he wrapped it around himself like a blanket.

He spoke a calibration line into the recorder before lying down. "Test. Isandro Fairweather, licensed property surveyor, license number 4471-C, conducting overnight thermal monitoring at Cold Spring House, east bedroom, second floor." His voice sounded smaller than usual in the room, the way voices do in places that swallow echoes. "Time is 11:47 p.m. All systems nominal."

The house said nothing. The house had been saying nothing for two weeks, in a register of nothing that was beginning to feel like attention.

* * *

Sleep came for him the way sleep comes in strange houses: sideways, in pieces, each piece convinced it was the whole. He dreamed of measurements. He dreamed of the tape measure unspooling itself down a corridor that grew longer the more he measured it, the numbers on the blade sliding past like the teeth of some silver comb, and behind him, always, the sound of a pen writing in notation he almost recognized.

He woke once at what his watch said was 3:12 a.m., and lay listening to the house settle. Old houses settle. They creak and tick as the temperature drops, timber shrinking against nail, stone releasing the day's heat. But Cold Spring House did not settle the way other houses settled. Other houses made sounds at random. This house made sounds in sequences. Three ticks, pause, two ticks, pause, one tick. Then silence long enough to be deliberate. Then the sequence again, slightly faster, like someone working through a problem and getting closer.

He lay in the dark and counted with it, because counting was what he had, and after the fourth cycle he said, quietly, into the room:

"What are you counting?"

The house did not answer. The ticks stopped, though, all at once, mid-sequence, the way a person stops talking when they realize they've been overheard. The silence afterward had a texture. It had a lean to it. Isandro lay with his heart going hard and steady, surveyor's heart, a heart that took its own measurements, and told himself: *thermal expansion. Timber contraction. A coincidence of cadence.*

He was a good liar in the dark. Most professionals are.

He slept again, and this time the dreams had a woman in them, standing at the foot of the cot with her arms at her sides in the exact posture of the thermal silhouette, and she was speaking, and in the dream he could not hear her, and this frustrated him so profoundly that he woke himself trying to lean closer.

* * *

Morning came gray through the boarded windows, light in strips, the room striped like a specimen tray. Isandro sat up with the particular headache of a man who has slept badly in a place that noticed, drank half a bottle of water, and reached for the recorder with hands that were, he observed, perfectly steady. He noted the steadiness. He noted that he had noted it. The habit of documentation was a spine in him; even fear had to file its reports.

He pressed playback.

His own voice first, the calibration line, tinny and professional. Then a long stretch of room-tone, the recorder's gain turned high enough that the silence had a grain to it, a static like distant surf. He listened at 1x speed for a while, then pushed to 4x, watching the waveform scroll, a seismograph of nothing.

At 1:33 a.m., the waveform moved.

He backed up. Played it at 1x. His own voice, thick with sleep, barely words: *"...how old... the foundation..."*

And then, in the gap after, a second voice.

It was a woman's voice. It was quiet—not whispered, but *economical*, the way people speak in sickrooms, each word placed like a coin on a counter. And it said:

*"Seventeen-ninety-one. The stones came from the river bend. Aldous chose them himself, and the river has wanted them back ever since."*

Isandro sat on the edge of the cot with the recorder held in both hands, and the morning light lay in stripes across his knees, and he did not move for a long time.

He played it again. And again. He got up, found his laptop, and copied the file to three locations before he let himself listen a fourth time, because the first law of his profession was *preserve the record before you interpret it*, and the second law, learned in a decade of disputed boundaries and contested easements, was *nobody believes a record that exists in only one place*.

The voice was real. It was on the card, on the drive, in the cloud. Three copies. It had a timbre, a slight dryness at the ends of words, a habit of dropping the final consonant of *back* so it became *bah*. It was a voice with a history in it, a voice that had been speaking a long time.

He scrubbed forward. At 2:04 a.m., his sleep-thick voice again: *"...who built... the rooms..."*

*"Aldous built the shell. Verity built the meaning. You are standing inside her sentence, surveyor."*

He wrote the names down. His handwriting, he noticed, was very neat. Neater than usual, the way a person's handwriting goes neat when the alternative is shaking. *Aldous. Verity.* He underlined them once, and the underline came out perfectly straight, and he stared at that straight line for a while because a perfectly straight line drawn by a frightened hand is its own kind of anomaly, and he had no thermocouple for that.

At 2:51 a.m.: *"...why... the eleven feet..."*

And here the voice did something new. It paused. He could hear it in the waveform, a flat line where a voice should be, a held breath rendered in silence. Then:

*"The house keeps what it is owed. You will understand the accounting soon enough. Sleep, surveyor. You are being read."*

He stopped the playback. The room was very quiet. Outside, a bird was doing something idiotic and cheerful in the hedge, and the ordinariness of it was almost obscene.

*You are being read.*

He got up and walked the room, corner to corner, tape measure in hand, because his hands needed work the way other men's hands needed drink. Forty-one feet, four and a quarter inches, wall to wall. He knew this number. He had measured it six times over two weeks and it had been forty-one feet, four and a quarter inches every time, and this morning, for the first time, he hated the number for being reliable. Somewhere in this house was footage that didn't reconcile, and somewhere in this house was a voice that knew the foundation stones had come from a river bend, and the two facts were the same fact, and he could feel the shape of that fact the way you feel weather coming in a healed fracture.

He sat back down. He opened his field notebook to a fresh page and wrote the date at the top, and beneath it, in the notation system every surveyor learns and none of them expects to use for this:

*Overnight audio: unexplained voice, female, adult, calm. Responds to sleep-vocalized queries with historical specifics. Source unknown. No persons on premises. Verified: 3 copies, timestamps intact, chain of custody maintained.*

He looked at the words *chain of custody* and almost laughed. Chain of custody was for evidence. Evidence was for courts. Courts were for a world where things happened to other people.

* * *

He should have left. He knew that with the flat clarity of a man reading his own obituary in advance: a professional who documents an unexplained voice on a property responds by leaving, by reporting, by washing his hands in triplicate. That was the protocol. That was what the sensible surveyor in Saskatchewan and Newark and Scotland would have done, the man he had been before the eleven feet, before the ledger in the wall cavity written in his own notation, before a dead woman's voice had told him to sleep.

Instead, he spent the day preparing the next night.

He told himself it was methodology. He told himself a single sample was anecdote; he needed replication; he needed controlled conditions; he needed to vary the variables and hold the variables and do all the things a careful man does when he is building a case. And all of that was true. But underneath the methodology, in the place where the methodology grows, there was something else, and he made himself look at it while he taped down the thermocouple leads, because a surveyor who lies in his own field notes is finished as a professional and possibly as a man.

He wanted to hear her again.

Not wanted the way you want a voice in a dark house, which is to say not at all. He wanted the way an appraiser wants a complete set of records. The voice was *data*. The voice was the first thing in this house that had answered him, that had engaged with his questions instead of simply exceeding them, and Isandro Fairweather had spent his whole life in buildings that kept their secrets in plaster and lathe, and here, finally, impossibly, was a building that talked back. Terror was in it somewhere—he could locate it, a cold node behind the sternum, eleven degrees cooler than the rest of him, a thermal anomaly of his own—but terror had always been a fuel he ran well on. It sharpened him. It made his handwriting neat.

He tested the microphone. He tested the batteries. He laid out the cot in the same position, equidistant, geometry as amulet. And then, because the day was ending and the light through the boarded windows had gone the color of old paper, he did the thing that the protocol-bound part of him had been arguing against since noon.

He leaned toward the recorder and spoke aloud, awake, in a clear voice, to an empty room.

"Good evening. Isandro Fairweather. I'll be asking some questions tonight. I'd be grateful for accurate answers."

The silence that followed was not empty. He had spent two weeks in this house and had learned its silences the way you learn a language by immersion, and this one was *attentive*. It had the quality of a room where someone has just set down a pen.

He lay down. He did not sleep easily, but he slept, and the last thing he registered was the red standby light of the recorder, a single point of red in the dark, like a pilot flame, like a votive.

* * *

He woke at dawn with the dream already fading, the woman at the foot of the cot, the pen writing behind him. His first act—before water, before the headache, before thought—was the recorder. He was aware, distantly, of what this ritual was becoming. He was aware and he proceeded, the way a man proceeds into a church he doesn't believe in because the kneeling itself has started to feel like the belief.

Playback.

1:12 a.m. His sleeping voice: *"...the ledger... whose writing..."*

*"Mine. I kept the accounts. I keep them still. Verity Crane keeps the books of Cold Spring House, and they have never once failed to balance."*

He wrote the name a second time, and this time he added a surname, and the surname on the page looked like a door opening.

2:38 a.m.: *"...am I... in danger..."*

A pause, the flat-line pause he was learning to read like a facial expression.

*"Everyone in this house is in the ledger, surveyor. The question is only which column."*

3:15 a.m., and this one he had to play four times, because his sleeping voice was almost inaudible, slurred at the edges, and the answer came so softly the gain had to be pushed until the room-tone hissed like a tide:

*"...what do you... want from me..."*

And the voice said:

*"Accuracy."*

One word. He sat with it while the striped light crawled across the floor. *Accuracy.* Not his soul, not his blood, not his name spoken backward. Accuracy. It was the most professional demand he had ever heard from the impossible, and that was the most frightening thing about it, because it meant the house understood him. It knew what he was for. It had been waiting, all these years of dereliction, for a man whose religion was exact measurement, and now it had one, asleep in its east bedroom, asking questions into the dark.

He closed the notebook. He looked at the two entries—*Verity Crane* and *Accuracy*—and understood that he had crossed a line that did not appear on any survey. On one side was a surveyor documenting anomalies. On the other side was something else: a man in correspondence with a house, keeping his side of a conversation, asking his questions each night with increasing care, refining his diction, choosing his words the way you choose offerings.

He knew what that was. He had seen it in the founder's ledger, in the careful, patient notation, the same system he used, the same abbreviations, the same habit of triple-verification noted in the margins. Whoever had written that ledger had also begun with a recorder and a question. Whoever had written that ledger had also found that the fear, given somewhere to go, becomes devotion.

He opened a fresh page. At the top he wrote: *Questions for the house.* And beneath it, in his neatest hand, he began a list.

Outside, the bird was singing again. The thermocouples ticked and logged. And somewhere in the walls, in the eleven feet of nowhere, he was almost certain he could hear—though he would never claim it in the field notes, though no instrument would ever corroborate it—the sound of a page being turned.
