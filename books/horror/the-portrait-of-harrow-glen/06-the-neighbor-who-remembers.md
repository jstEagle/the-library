## 6. The Neighbor Who Remembers

Casper Yarrow did not sleep well in her van. She had done it often enough over fifteen years — stakeouts for squirrels in attics, for raccoons in crawlspaces, for the occasional fox that had decided someone's garden shed was a maternity ward — and she had a system: seat reclined to the second notch, sleeping bag rated to minus ten, radio off, phone face-down. The system was supposed to make the van feel like a small, defensible country with borders she controlled.

It didn't work at Harrow Glen.

The house watched her. That was the only way she could put it, and she hated the way it sounded even inside her own skull, because watching was not something houses did, and she was a woman who dealt in things houses actually did: settle, rot, leak, host. But the windows had a quality of attention to them, the way a portrait has, the way a dog does when it's deciding. She had parked facing away from it, which helped not at all, because the rearview mirror caught the porch light and held it a beat too long, the way everything in that house held everything a beat too long.

At 6:40 in the morning she gave up, made coffee on the camp stove, and sat in the driver's seat watching steam curl off the cup. In the rearview, the steam curled a half-second after the actual steam. She turned the mirror away from herself and drank her coffee looking at the road instead.

The job, technically, was unfinished. She had bait stations to check, a report to write, an invoice to leave. The invoice was going to be a problem. *Evidence of rodent activity: none. Evidence of other activity: pending.* She'd have to invent droppings just to have something professional to say. Fifteen years of rational explanations, and here she was, contemplating fraud to avoid writing the truth, which was: *your walls have handprints on the inside, ma'am, and they are greasy, and they are human, and they are not yours.*

The house's owner — a property management company in the city, a voice named Doreen who sounded like she smoked through a kazoo — had given her one more piece of useful information before the line went quiet: the house wasn't owned by the management company at all. They just handled the calls. The property was, as far as anyone could establish, unclaimed. "Nobody's paid taxes on it since before I started here," Doreen had said, "and nobody's ever come to look at it. You'd think somebody would come look at it."

Casper had thought about that a long time in the dark.

So: records office when it opened at nine. But first — coffee, and the neighbor.

She had noticed the house three doors down on her first visit. It was the only one on the lane that looked loved. The others were the usual rural decay — vinyl siding gone chalky, a trampoline rusting into a field like a shipwreck — but this one had painted shutters, a swept porch, and a garden that, even in the gray tail of the season, was cut back and mulched and deliberate. Someone lived there on purpose. Someone who noticed things.

Casper parked at the curb — not in the drive, she wasn't a monster — and walked up with her cap in her hand and her most disarming, I'm-not-selling-anything expression already in place. Fifteen years of door-knocking taught you that expression the way a soldier learns to clean a rifle: automatically, in the dark, without thinking.

The woman who answered was small and straight-backed, somewhere north of eighty, with white hair pinned in a way that had gone out of fashion twice and come back once. She looked at Casper the way people look at weather: assessing, unhurried, already deciding.

"Morning," Casper said. "Sorry to bother you. I'm doing work up at the old Harrow place, and I wanted to ask —"

She didn't get to finish, because the woman's eyes had dropped to the van.

To the lettering on the van. *YARROW PEST SOLUTIONS — SERVING THE COUNTY SINCE 2009.* Her own name in vinyl, in a friendly blue font she'd chosen at a print shop eleven years ago because it looked trustworthy.

The woman went pale.

Not the polite pallor of surprise. Casper had seen people faint, seen people get bad news on their doorsteps, seen a man open his front door onto a dead opossum in his hallway and go the color of candle wax. This was that color. The blood didn't drain from Edith Tolliver's face so much as it was *recalled*, like a debt being called in, and her hand came up and gripped the doorframe with a knuckle-whitening certainty, and for one long moment the only sound on the lane was wind moving through the cut-back garden.

"You're a Yarrow," the woman said.

"That's right. Casper Yarrow. Is something —"

"Yarrow Pest Solutions." She read it like scripture, like a line she'd been waiting her whole life to hear read aloud. Then she did something Casper had never seen an eighty-year-old woman do on her own front step: she looked over her shoulder, into her own house, the way you look behind you when you've heard your name called in an empty room.

"Come inside," she said. "Not because I want you to. Because I'm not saying this on the street."

* * *

The house inside was warm and smelled of bergamot and old paper. A cat regarded Casper from the back of a sofa with the flat contempt of its kind. The woman — Edith Tolliver, she said, shaking hands with a grip like a fence post — poured tea without asking, in cups that had clearly never met a dishwasher, and sat down across from Casper at a kitchen table worn smooth along its edges by fifty years of elbows.

"You work at Harrow Glen," Edith said. It wasn't a question.

"Job came through a management company. Scratching in the walls."

"And what did you find in the walls, Casper Yarrow?"

Casper set down her cup. She considered lying, and was interested to discover she didn't want to. Something about this kitchen — the bergamot, the cat, the woman's terrible steady eyes — made lying feel like tracking mud across a clean floor.

"Handprints," she said. "On the inside of a sealed cavity. Grease, like — like the residue off skin. Five fingers. Right size for a grown person. And no way in or out that I could find, and I looked for two days." She paused. "And the mirrors lag. Everything reflective in that house is running about a second behind."

Edith Tolliver nodded. Not the nod of someone hearing something strange. The nod of someone confirming a delivery.

"My sister Ruth kept books," Edith said. "Forty years, books for half the county. Farmers, the feed store, the church fund. And for a while there, she kept the books for a woman named Alma Yarrow." She let the name sit on the table between them like a stone. "Your grandmother."

"I never met her." Casper heard her own voice go careful. "My mother didn't talk about this side of the family. We were from — I thought we were from upstate. My mother said we were from upstate."

"You were from here." Edith said it without drama, the way you'd tell someone the road out front floods every spring. "Number eleven, the Harrow lane. The house with the green door, gone now, they put up those duplexes. Your mother was born in that house, Casper. I held her at a church picnic when she was a baby and she screamed the whole time, and your grandmother laughed and said, *she doesn't like being held by strangers, she'll grow out of it.*" Edith's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "She never grew out of it, I'd wager. Did she."

Casper's mother had not, in fact, ever liked being held, or surprised, or rooms she couldn't see the exits of, or the sound of a house settling at night, which she had once, drunk on Christmas sherry, described as *the walls counting us.* Casper had been eleven and had laughed. She was not laughing now, sitting in a warm kitchen three doors from a house with handprints in its walls, feeling her whole life quietly reorganize itself around a new fact.

"The Yarrows left in 1961," Edith said. "Packed up overnight. Sold to a family called Merrick — quick sale, low price, no inspection, which Ruth always said should have been the first clue. Nobody sells a good house cheap unless the house has done something to them." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "And then in '74 the Merricks went too. And that house has stood empty ever since, and nobody has ever come for it, and nobody has ever torn it down, and the county has never once — not once in fifty years — moved to seize it for back taxes. You tell me how that happens, Casper. You're the one with fifteen years of rational explanations. Tell me how a house just *stays*."

"I was going to the records office this morning," Casper said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"You'll find what I found, which is nothing, which is worse than something." Edith leaned forward. The cat left the sofa, unhurried, as if it had an appointment elsewhere. "I'm going to tell you the thing I should have told someone fifty years ago, and you're going to sit there and drink your tea while I do, because my mother told me never to say it aloud and I have watched what happens to people who owe that house, and I am tired, Casper. I am eighty-three and I am tired of being quiet."

* * *

What Edith Tolliver told her, in a voice like dry leaves, was this:

Her mother, Ruth's and hers, had done sewing for the Merrick women. alterations, curtains, a christening gown for the youngest. She was in the house twice a week through the spring of '74, and she came home from those visits a little quieter each time, and by summer she had stopped going, and she never said why, not once, not in the thirty years she lived afterward. But she started sleeping with the radio on. Low. All night. And when Edith asked her why, once, her mother had said: *so I can't hear the walls.*

"Then in September of '74, the Merrick boy — Daniel, he was maybe nineteen, a nice boy, awkward, polite — came to our door at night. Pale as you please. He asked my mother to keep something for him. He said his father had dug it up out of the cellar floor and it had to go somewhere the house couldn't reach, and he said, and I have never forgotten the words, he said, *it's warm, Mrs. Tolliver, it's been warm the whole way here and I don't know how to make it stop.*" Edith's hands were flat on the table now, pressing down, as if the table might otherwise drift. "My mother took it. She took it into the house and she hid it, and she never told me where, and she never told Ruth, and she kept it hidden for the rest of her life. And the Merricks were gone inside a week. Not moved. *Gone.* Their car in the drive, their supper dishes — Ruth heard this from the milkman — still on the table."

"And the boy," Casper said. "Daniel. He carried it out of the house and the house still took them anyway?"

Edith looked at her for a long moment, and what was in her face was pity, which was worse than fear.

"You think it's about the box," she said. "Everyone thinks it's about the thing they took. It's not. It was never about the thing. It's about the *owing*." She said the word the way you'd name a disease. "That house lends itself, Casper. Warm rooms in a cold winter. Quiet nights. Walls that hold the sound in so your neighbors can't hear you weep. It lends and lends and it never names the price, and you go on living in it, comfortable, comfortable, and one day you realize you've been living on credit for years and the house has come to collect." Her voice dropped. "It doesn't take people. Say it back to me."

"It — doesn't take people."

"It takes people who *owe.*" She sat back. "The Merricks owed. God knows what the previous owners did, the ones before, the ones in '61 — your people, the Yarrows, they sold it to the Merricks, and I used to wonder if they sold it cheap because they knew what was coming due. And now you've been in that house two days, and you've put your hands on its walls, and you've looked in its mirrors, and you've slept in its yard." She reached across and turned Casper's wrist over, gentle as a nurse, and looked at the underside of her forearm as if reading it. "What did you touch in there?"

Casper thought of the cavity. The greasy prints. The way she'd pressed her palm flat against the plaster — she couldn't even say why, some professional instinct, checking for vibration, checking for heat — and the plaster had been *warm*. Warm like skin. Warm like a hand held against yours from the other side.

"Nothing," she said. "I checked a wall. That's all. I checked a wall."

Edith released her wrist and looked at her with an expression Casper had seen before, on the faces of clients who knew the raccoon was still in the attic no matter what she told them.

"Here's what you do," Edith said. "You go to your records office. You find your nothing. And then you drive home, wherever home is, and you don't take another call from that management company, and if anything follows you — anything, a sound, a lag, a dream — you come back here and you tell me, because my mother left instructions, in the end. She left instructions and I have spent fifty years hoping to God I'd never need them." She stood, and the standing cost her something visible, and Casper understood that the straightness of her back was not constitution but discipline, paid for daily. "And Casper. One more thing."

Casper stood too. Her tea was untouched and cold.

"Your grandmother, when she sold that house — Ruth processed the paperwork. And your grandmother signed her name, and under it she wrote a line, and Ruth copied it into her own notebook because it frightened her, and Ruth's notebook is in a box in my back room, and I could show it to you, but you'll wish I hadn't." Edith walked her to the door, slowly, the cat reappearing to supervise. "She wrote: *We were only the latest. Ask the house who it belonged to before us, and before them, and before them. The debt is older than the deed.*"

The door opened onto gray morning light and the lane and, three houses down, Harrow Glen with its dead windows.

"It doesn't take people who are curious," Edith said from her doorway. "It doesn't take people who are brave. It takes people who *owe*. So the only question that matters, Casper Yarrow — and you think about it before you go back inside that house, because you will go back, they always go back — is this." Her voice was almost gentle now, which was the worst thing yet. "What did your family leave unpaid?"

Casper walked down the path. At the gate she turned, and Edith was still in the doorway, and behind Edith, in the dark of the hall, the hall mirror showed the doorway, and Edith standing in it — and showed her a full half-second after she was already there.

Casper stood very still.

The lag had followed her out of the house. Or — she made herself consider it, made herself be the professional, the woman of fifteen years of rational explanations — the lag had never been in Harrow Glen at all. Maybe the house wasn't where the mirrors were wrong.

Maybe the mirrors were wrong wherever *she* was.

Behind her, Edith Tolliver said, quietly, to herself or to the house or to the dead: "Oh, Ruth. Oh, you poor girl. It's the daughter."

Casper got in her van. Her hands were steady on the wheel, the way they'd been steady for fifteen years, and she drove toward the county records office, and in the rearview mirror — which she had turned away and now, helplessly, turned back — the lane behind her unspooled a half-second late, and at its end, small and patient, Harrow Glen's front door stood open.

She had locked it. She had definitely locked it. She had heard the lock go.

The door stood open, and the house was waiting for her to come back, and the terrible thing — the thing she would not let herself think about until the records office, until the dark, until the dream she didn't yet know was coming — was that some small, starved, unaccounted-for part of her had already decided to.
