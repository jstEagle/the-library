## 11. Digging

Casper woke in the van with dirt under her fingernails.

Not grime — not the honest, greasy soil of a workday — but fine, dry earth, pale as ash, packed deep into the crescents beneath every nail. She held her hands up to the gray pre-dawn light coming through the windshield and turned them over slowly, the way you'd turn over a stranger's hands, looking for something familiar. Her knuckles ached. Her shoulders carried the deep, specific soreness of someone who had spent hours digging, and her forearms were scored with faint red lines where something — she refused to finish the thought — had gripped her while she worked.

She had not been digging. She had been asleep. She had been asleep in the front seat of her van on the shoulder of Tolliver Road with the engine off and the windows fogged, and she had dreamed a dream so complete, so furnished with detail, that she could still smell the pot roast and the cigarette smoke and the beeswax polish of a table she had never touched, in a house she had only ever known as a job site.

The Merricks. Thomas and Ruth and the boy, Daniel, seventeen, all elbows and laughter. She knew their faces now the way you know faces from old photographs — slightly too saturated, slightly too still. She knew the way Thomas Merrick had smiled at his wife across the dinner table while his foot tapped a private, nervous rhythm against the floor. She knew the way Ruth's hands had trembled when she passed the gravy boat, and how she'd covered it by tucking her hair behind her ear, twice, three times, a gesture worn smooth by repetition. She knew the boy had been the one who found the box, and she knew — with a certainty that lived in her body rather than her mind — the sound the cellar floor made when the first flagstone came up: a wet, reluctant kiss of old mortar letting go.

And she knew where the floor was broken. She knew the exact stone, the fourth from the hearth wall, the one with the hairline crack running through it like a river seen from very high up.

Casper sat in the fogging van and breathed until her hands stopped shaking, which took longer than she would have liked. Then she did what she had done for fifteen years, what she did with every job that went sideways, every crawl space that turned up something wrong: she made a plan. Rational. Sequential. Bounded.

One: drive to the hardware store in Millbrook when it opened at seven. Buy a sledgehammer, a cold chisel, a pry bar, a bag of quick-set concrete, and — because the dream had left her with a thirst she couldn't explain — a jug of water.

Two: go back into the cellar.

Three: break the floor.

Four: find out what was buried there.

Five: bury it again, or remove it, or call someone — a priest, a historian, the county, anyone — and let the problem become someone else's.

It was a good plan. It was the kind of plan that had gotten her through fifteen years of attics full of starlings and basements full of rats and one unforgettable crawlspace in Ashford where a raccoon family had nested inside a wall for three generations. She repeated it to herself as she drove, and the repetition worked the way it always worked, sanding down the edges of the fear until it was just a shape she could carry.

She was on the porch of Harrow Glen by 7:40, tools in her arms, and the front door was unlocked.

She had locked it. She was nearly certain she had locked it — she had a ritual, deadbolt then handle, handle then deadbolt, a little superstition she'd never examined. But the door swung inward at her touch without a sound, without the swollen-stickiness she'd have expected from a house that had stood empty for fifty years, and the hallway received her the way a throat receives a swallow.

The mirrors were lagging. She didn't look at them. She had learned, over the past week, to walk the corridor with her eyes fixed on the wallpaper — faded roses, the color of old meat — because the reflections had started showing her things, and the woman in the glass had started mouthing words whose shapes Casper was beginning, horribly, to recognize. *Help us. Help us. Help us,* or possibly *leave, leave, leave,* or possibly her own name, spoken by a mouth that was hers and was not hers, one second late, then two, then four.

The cellar door stood at the end of the kitchen, and it was open.

She had left it closed. She knew that with the same bodily certainty she knew her own hands. And yet here it stood, ajar by exactly the width of a hand, breathing out cold air that smelled of wet stone and, beneath that, of turned earth — fresh-turned earth, the smell of a garden bed the morning after planting.

Casper stood at the top of the cellar stairs for a long time, the sledgehammer heavy across her shoulder, and told herself the truth she'd been avoiding since the dream: the plan had a flaw in it. The plan assumed she was the one doing the digging. The plan assumed the house was a place and not a participant. The plan assumed that whatever was under the floor wanted to stay there.

She went down anyway. That was the thing about her, the thing her mother had complained about and her ex had complained about and every ex-employer had noted in her file in one phrasing or another: Casper Yarrow went down anyway. Curiosity had always been less a trait of hers than a compulsion, a hunger with teeth, and it had never once in her life asked her permission before it fed.

* * *

The cellar was bigger than the dream had shown her.

In the dream it had been a low room, close and warm, lit by a single bulb, the Merrick family's evening life pressed into every corner — the boy's bicycle leaning by the stairs, Ruth's preserves in ranks on the shelves, the smell of coal and onions. Now it was a cavern. The walls she remembered had retreated or had never been there; her flashlight beam traveled twenty feet and found nothing, then thirty, then gave out against a darkness that didn't reflect so much as absorb. The ceiling was lost somewhere above her, higher than the floor of the kitchen could possibly allow, and the geometry of it made her teeth ache, the way a wrong chord does. The house's footprint was finite. She had measured it. She had the survey in her van. This room did not fit inside that footprint, and yet here it was, breathing its cold breath over her, patient as a held note.

The floor, though. The floor was exactly as the dream had promised: flagstones, old and wide, laid in a herringbone that had gone out of fashion before the war, each one worn into a shallow dish by a century of feet. And fourth from the hearth wall — the hearth itself bricked over, she noticed now, the bricks newer than the rest, mortared with a hand that had been hurried or frightened — the fourth stone bore its hairline crack, running through it like a river seen from very high up.

Casper set down her tools. She knelt. She put her palm flat on the stone, the way you'd check a fever.

It was warm.

Not sun-warm. Not pipe-warm. Warm the way a body is warm — a living, circulating warmth that rose to meet her hand and then, unmistakably, withdrew from it, a flinch, a contraction, the stone pulling its heat inward like a snail into its shell. She snatched her hand back. Her palm tingled. The tingling spread up her wrist in a slow wave, and under her skin, in the small bones of her hand, she felt a rhythm that was not her pulse. Slower. Deeper. Patient as geology.

"Oh," she said, out loud, to the empty dark. Her voice came back to her wrong — flattened, close, as if the room were holding it near. "Oh, you're alive."

The stone did not answer. But the warmth came back to her palm, gently, the way a frightened animal will risk a second approach, and Casper knelt on the cold floor of a house that did not fit inside itself and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she was not about to dig something up.

She was about to perform an excavation. And excavations, she knew from a documentary she'd half-watched at 2 a.m. some insomniac winter, were not recoveries. They were interruptions. You did not find what was buried; you disturbed its rest, you let in the air and the light and the present tense, and whatever the ground had been doing with its dead — preserving, transforming, digesting — stopped, forever, at the moment your shovel broke the surface.

She thought about driving away. She gave the thought its full weight, honestly, the way she tried to give all thoughts their full weight now. She thought about Edith Tolliver's face when Casper's name on the van had turned it the color of tallow. *It doesn't take people. It takes people who owe.* She thought about her mother, dead six years, and the Yarrow name in the plaster, in the tally marks, in a wall she had walked through like a corridor wider than the house could hold. She thought about the woman in the mirrors, mouthing.

Then she picked up the sledgehammer, set the cold chisel against the mortar at the cracked stone's edge, and swung.

* * *

The mortar fought her for an hour.

It was old, but it was good — laid by someone who'd wanted the stone never to move again, and Casper worked the chisel around the flagstone's perimeter in a slow, ringing circuit, each blow of the sledgehammer traveling up her arms and down through her spine into the floor, and the floor took the blows without complaint, without echo. That was the strangest part, the part her body kept flagging while her mind worked: the sound. A cellar should ring. Stone on stone, in an enclosed space, should bounce and multiply and come back at you doubled. This cellar swallowed every strike. Each blow landed with a sound like a fist into a mattress — a dense, dead *whump* — and then silence, total, as if the darkness around her were leaning in to listen and disapproving.

Sweat stung her eyes. Her shoulders burned. She broke for water, sitting on the stairs with the jug, and in the pause she heard it: above her, faint, through the floorboards, a sound like a house settling — but rhythmic, deliberate. *Tap. Tap-tap.* A pause. *Tap. Tap-tap.*

Three knocks. Then three back. The same call and answer from the stakeout, from the night the wall had bulged like skin under pressure, and her hand was on the chisel before she'd decided to move, and she was rapping the flagstone — three, pause, three — before the part of her that still maintained a rational worldview could stop it.

Silence.

Then, from everywhere in the dark at once, from the walls and the floor and the air: three knocks, gentle, almost fond. And beneath them, so low she felt it in her sternum rather than heard it, a long exhalation, like a sigh, like relief, like a held breath finally released after fifty years.

*You came back,* the house said, in the only language it had left.

Casper's hands were steady. That surprised her most. She returned to the stone with the calm of a woman in a dream — and the thought arrived and passed through her without stopping, that maybe she was, that maybe she'd never woken from the van, that the dirt under her fingernails had been real and the waking a courtesy — and drove the chisel into the crack.

The flagstone moved.

Not all at once. It moved the way a tooth moves on the third day of loosening: a grudging millimeter, then a grating half-inch, then a sudden, sickening give as the mortar beneath it — hollow beneath it, she realized, hollowed out, the cavity pre-made, waiting — surrendered. She put down the sledgehammer and used the pry bar, working it into the gap, and the stone came up with a sound she recognized from the dream, the wet, reluctant kiss of old mortar letting go, and she set it aside and knelt at the edge of the hole with her flashlight.

The cavity was shaped exactly like a box.

Not a grave, not a cache, not a rough hole. A mold. Rectangular, crisp-cornered, about the size of a bread box, sunk a foot into the earth, its walls packed hard and smooth as fired clay. The earth had taken the impression of the thing that had lain there and kept it, faithful as a fossil — and the thing that had lain there had been an iron box, because the inner faces of the cavity were lined with rust, a skin of orange-brown bloom transferred stone-slow from metal to earth over fifty years.

And inside the rust, scored deep into the clay walls, were claw marks.

They ran in every direction. They ran upward, mostly — hundreds of them, overlapping, frantic, gouged into the packed earth from the inside, and every one of them pointed the same way: up, toward the surface, toward the air, toward the light that had never come. Some were deep enough that Casper could see where the earth had squeezed between whatever fingers had made them. Some were shallow, fading, the scratches of something that had grown weak. They were layered like tree rings. The oldest were at the bottom. The newest were at the top, and the newest were the deepest, and the newest had been made recently — the clay at their edges still sharp, still bright, still dark with a moisture that had no business existing in a cavity sealed under a floor for half a century.

Casper's flashlight hand was shaking now. The beam trembled across the claw marks and she made herself look, made herself count, made herself do the arithmetic her mind kept sliding away from. The box had been buried. The box had been buried by Thomas Merrick while his wife wept at the dinner table, and the box had been buried *full*, and the box had clawed its way out — or the box had been opened from outside, gently, the earth un-packed with care by hands that knew what they were doing — and either way, the cavity was empty, had been empty, and the warmth in the floor, the flinch, the pulse, the sigh —

The house hadn't been haunted by something that got in.

The house had been haunted by something that got *out*. Something that had been buried alive in iron and earth, by a family who had stolen it — or rescued it — and hidden it badly, and it had dug, and scratched, and waited in the dark with its patience like geology, and it had gotten out, and it had never left, because where would it go? It had been born in this ground. It had been *made* here, or kept here, or broken here, and the house was the only body it had.

And it had been watching her since the day she walked in with her toolbag and her rational explanations, and it had cleaned her tools and arranged them on the porch, because it was courteous, because it was *grateful*, because no one had come for it in fifty years and here, finally, was a woman who dug.

Casper sat back on her heels in the cold and the dark, above the empty mold lined with the scratches of something that had once been buried alive, and she began, quietly, to laugh — a small, cracked sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a woman whose last rational wall has just come down and who finds, behind it, not a room but a corridor.

That was when every door in the house opened at once.

* * *

She heard it as a single sound, though it must have been many: a great soft concussion of hinges, a house-wide intake of breath. Above her, through the swallowed silence of the cellar, doors swung open — the front door, the kitchen door, the doors of rooms she'd never entered, cupboards, cabinets, the little doors of things she didn't want to think about — every latch and lock in Harrow Glen releasing in the same instant, and the air pressure in the cellar changed, a slow outward flow, as if the house had opened its mouth.

Then, from the floor of the kitchen above, through the boards: footsteps. Slow. Unhurried. Crossing the room toward the cellar stairs, each step landing with the particular weight of someone who has all the time in the world and knows the way.

Casper got to her feet. She took up the pry bar — not because she believed it would help, but because her hands wanted a shape to hold — and she stood at the bottom of the stairs with her flashlight beam climbing the steps ahead of her, and she watched the doorway at the top, and the darkness in it thickened, and gathered, and began, politely, to come down.

The last thing she saw, before the light found it, was the fourth flagstone behind her, in the beam's spill: the hairline crack through its center, and pressed into the dust on its underside, fresh and five-fingered and greasy, a single handprint — pointing down.
