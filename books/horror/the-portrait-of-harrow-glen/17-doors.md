## 17. Doors

The corridor did not end. That was the first thing Casper understood about it, standing with the iron box against her chest and the walls breathing their slow, plaster-warm breath around her: she had walked into a throat, and throats are not built for escape.

She had counted her steps at the beginning — an old habit from stakeouts, from long nights in vans counting the seconds between scratching sounds to keep herself sane. She'd reached four hundred and eleven when she stopped being able to trust the number. The corridor was lined with doors on both sides, close-set as teeth, and every door was different. Painted wood. Stained glass. A screen door with a torn mesh that buzzed faintly, though there was no wind in here, no outside at all. Brass knobs worn bright by hands. Iron handles furred with cold. And behind each one — behind *every* one — a sound.

Not silence. Never silence. Harrow Glen had taught her that much: the house abhorred a quiet room the way nature abhorred a vacuum, filling every hollow with something living or something pretending to be.

Behind the nearest door, a radio played. Big band music, tinny and warm, the kind of sound that came out of a kitchen radio set on the counter while someone chopped onions. Behind the next, laughter — a woman's laugh, quick and unguarded, followed by a man's lower chuckle, and then a child's shriek of delight that made Casper's chest tighten before she could stop it. Behind the third, nothing she could name, only a feeling, like standing outside a lit window on a cold night: the specific ache of warmth you were not part of.

*It knows what I'm hungry for,* she thought, and the thought itself felt like a violation, like the house had reached into her and read the label off the thing inside.

The box hummed against her sternum. Cold iron, colder than the air, humming the way a struck bell hums after the sound has technically ended. She shifted her grip. Her palms had gone slick, and the ledger inside — she could feel its weight shifting, pages settling like something turning over in sleep — pressed against the metal from within.

"Keep walking," she said aloud. Her voice came out flat and small and immediately got swallowed. "Just keep walking."

But the doors kept offering.

That was the horror of it, she realized somewhere around the two-hundredth step — not that the corridor trapped her, but that it *courted* her. Each door stood slightly ajar as she passed, just enough to breathe its life into the hallway, just enough to make her slow. None of them swung wide. None of them demanded. They simply *were*, the way a lit kitchen window is, the way a table set for two is, and they let her own want do all the work.

A door of pale yellow paint, chipped at the bottom where a dog had scratched it years ago. Behind it: the smell of coffee. Actual coffee, real enough that her mouth watered, bitter and burnt the way her mother had always made it, left too long on the burner because she forgot about everything when she was reading.

Casper's feet slowed.

*Don't.* She said it to herself the way she'd say *down* to a dog straining at a leash. *You know what this is.*

She knew. She'd known since the tally marks, since YARROW carved into plaster in a hand that wasn't hers but might have been her grandmother's. The house didn't take people who owed it money. It took people who owed it *something else* — people with holes in them shaped exactly like a home. She had spent fifteen years crawling through other people's houses, other people's attics and crawlspaces, fixing what lived in their walls and driving home to an apartment she never bothered to decorate because decorating implied staying. She had starved herself so gradually she'd mistaken the hunger for a personality.

And now here was a corridor built entirely out of meals.

* * *

The seventh door was blue.

Not painted blue — stained, deep and even, the color of a lake at dusk, with a brass handle polished to a soft gold shine. Casper might have walked past it. She meant to walk past it. But the sounds coming from behind it stopped her the way a hand on the shoulder stops you.

A kettle, clicking off. The clink of a spoon against ceramic. Then a voice, low and unhurried, half-talking to itself the way people do when they're alone in a kitchen and have given up pretending otherwise:

"Casper? Is that you, sweetheart? You're late."

Her whole body went cold and hot at once, a fever arriving in a single second.

She knew that voice. Fifteen years dead and she knew it the way you know a scar — not by looking, but by the way weather changes in it. Her mother's voice, roughened by cigarettes and morning, softened by the particular gentleness she'd saved only for Casper's name.

"She's been asking about you," the voice said, to someone else now, someone unseen. "Every week. Every single week she asks. I keep telling her, she'll come round, she's just busy, she's got her work—"

Casper's hand was on the handle.

She didn't remember moving. That frightened her more than anything the house had done yet — more than the bulging wall, more than the bleeding light, more than the figure assembled from five missing people wearing a father's smile wrong. Because those things had been *outside* her. This was her own arm, her own fingers, closing around warm brass with the sureness of a woman coming home.

The handle was warm. Body-warm. The house had gotten that right, too.

Through the keyhole — old-fashioned, heavy, the kind of lock you turned with an actual key — she could see a slice of kitchen. Yellow light. Wallpaper with little climbing roses, faded to ghosts of pink. A table covered in oilcloth printed with lemons. And at the table, seen from behind, a woman with gray-streaked hair pinned up carelessly, one slipper dangling off her toe, a crossword folded in front of her and a mug steaming at her elbow.

"Mum," Casper said. The word came out of her like a splinter leaving skin — relief and pain in the same motion, indistinguishable.

The woman didn't turn. Of course she didn't turn. But she spoke again, easy as Sunday, easy as fifty thousand mornings that had never happened:

"There's tea in the pot. Sit down before it goes bitter. You know how she gets about the tea."

*She.* Casper frowned. *Who's she?*

And then, gently, the handle turned under her hand — no, she turned it, she was still turning it, her traitor body carrying out the oldest instruction it knew — and the door began to open, and the brass face of the knob swung toward her, catching the yellow kitchen light, becoming a mirror.

Small and curved and perfect, the way only old brass can be.

In it, Casper saw the kitchen whole.

The woman at the table was there. The tea was there. The crossword, the lemons, the climbing roses — all there, all rendered in miniature on the curved metal, warm and golden and complete.

Except the chair across from the woman was empty.

Not recently empty. Not waiting empty. Empty the way a chair is empty when it has always been empty — no cushion flattened by sitting, no mug at its place, no second spoon beside the first. The oilcloth beneath it lay smooth and unwrinkled, factory-fresh, as if no one had ever pulled that chair out in the entire history of kitchens.

And the woman — her mother, her mother's hair, her mother's hunched careful shoulders — was talking to it anyway. Talking to the empty chair, pouring nothing into nothing, smiling at nobody, saying *she'll come round, she'll come round* to a seat that had never held anyone at all.

Because that was the truth, wasn't it. That was the truth the house had finally, in its terrible courtesy, decided to show her.

Her mother had waited her whole life for a daughter who sat down at her table. Had set the place, had kept the kettle warm, had told the neighbors *she's busy, she's got her work* — and Casper had come, hadn't she, sometimes, holidays, funerals, the obligatory circuits — but she had never *sat*. Not really. Not the way sitting means. She'd perched on the edge of her childhood like a bird on a wire, ready to lift off, one eye always on the road out. And her mother had died with the chair across from her still smooth and unwrinkled, still waiting, and the last thing she'd said — Casper remembered it now with the force of a physical blow, she'd kept it buried for fifteen years and the house dug it up in one breath — the last thing she'd said was:

*You never stay, Casper. Not anywhere. Not even here.*

The door stood open. The kitchen breathed its rose-and-tea warmth over her. The woman at the table went on talking to her empty chair, and the sound of it was worse than screaming, worse than the scratching, worse than anything with fingerprints, because it was true.

"No," Casper said.

Her voice cracked the vision like a stone through a window.

"No," she said again, louder, and pried her own fingers off the handle one at a time, watching them uncurl like something dying. "That's not her. She's dead. She's *dead*, and you don't get to — you don't get to use her—"

The woman at the table paused. Mid-sentence. Mid-word.

Then, slowly — with a motion no living person makes, a motion like film run backward, like a puppet remembering which strings are whose — she began to turn her head toward the door.

Casper slammed it shut.

The brass knob rang under her palm like a bell. Behind the door, silence — total, instant, absolute, the kitchen snuffed out like a candle between finger and thumb. Then, very softly, through three inches of blue-stained wood, her mother's voice said:

"You never stay."

And then, kinder, which was infinitely worse:

"But you will."

* * *

Casper ran.

Not far — there was nowhere to run *to*, the corridor stretched ahead and behind in equal impenetrable dark, and the box thumped against her ribs like a second heart insisting on its own rhythm. But she ran until her lungs burned, past doors that called out to her in voices she almost recognized, past a screen door that whispered her name in Edith Tolliver's papery rasp, past a child's bedroom door behind which someone was crying the way Casper had cried at nine years old, alone, in a house where the walls had already started listening.

She stopped when she couldn't breathe. Bent double, box hugged to her chest, sweat stinging her eyes.

The corridor waited. Patient as plaster.

*Think,* she told herself. *Fifteen years, Yarrow. Think like it's a job.*

Okay. The doors weren't random. That was the first fact. The house had curated them — her mother's kitchen, Edith's voice, a crying child she had no memory of but recognized anyway, the way you recognize your own handwriting. These weren't generic lures. This was a ledger too, a ledger of wants, and someone had read hers cover to cover.

Which meant the house was spending its ammunition. Which meant it was *worried*.

The second fact: the Merricks. Somewhere behind these doors, behind one of these doors, 1974 was playing on loop — dinner, laughter, a father burying something beneath the cellar floor while his wife wept. Chapter sixteen of her own nightmare. If the house kept its prisoners behind doors, then the doors weren't just bait.

They were cells.

And if they were cells, then somewhere ahead — at the end, at the hearth, wherever the corridor finally gave up its pretense of infinite — the entity was waiting. The thing with fingerprints. The polite horror that asked if she'd brought it home.

It wanted the box. It wanted her to put it down, walk away, let the Merricks rot comfortable forever. Comfort as damnation; she'd understood that the moment it offered. So every door between here and there was a question the house was asking her, over and over, in a voice sweet as her mother's:

*How badly do you want to belong? Badly enough to stop?*

Her answer was the same one she'd been giving her whole life, the answer that had emptied her mother's chair and filled her with this particular species of grief: she didn't stay. She had never stayed. It was the cruelest thing about her and tonight, God help her, it was going to save her life.

Casper straightened up. Wiped her face with her sleeve. Shifted the box to her hip like a carpenter carrying tools, because muscle memory was a kind of armor, and started walking.

The doors noticed. She felt it — a ripple running down the corridor ahead of her, door after door going quiet as she approached, the way birdsong stops when a hawk crosses the yard. The yellow-painted door with the dog scratches fell silent. The big band radio wound down mid-phrase, the singer's voice stretching and slowing like a record losing power. Even the screen door stopped buzzing.

They were regrouping. Choosing something better.

She should have known. She should have known there was one door left, one card the house had been holding the whole time, patient as only a building can be patient.

It stood alone at a place where the corridor widened slightly — a landing, almost, a pocket of shadow where the walls drew back as if in deference. The door was ordinary. That was what undid her. After the stained glass and the lemon oilcloth and the blue lacquer, it was just a door: white paint gone gray at the edges, a cheap brass knob, a mail slot. The door of a hundred houses she'd worked in. The door of every job she'd ever finished, walking back to her van alone in the dark.

No sound came from behind it. That was the trick of it. After miles of kitchens and laughter, the silence behind this door sounded like peace.

Taped to it, at eye level, was an envelope. Her name on the front — *CASPER* — in her mother's handwriting, the loopy, impatient script she'd know anywhere, the script from grocery lists and birthday cards and the note propped against the coffee maker on the last morning, the one that said *gone to the shop, back soon*, and she hadn't come back, she'd died in the parking lot of the shop with the note still folded in her coat pocket—

Casper took the envelope with shaking hands. Inside: a single sheet, and on it, in that same looping hand, only four words.

*Come in and stay.*

Below the words, the paper was smudged. Damp. As if the letter had been written by someone weeping.

Casper stood there a long time. Long enough that the corridor grew bold again and the doors behind her began, one by one, very quietly, to reopen — the kettle clicking, the band striking up, the child sobbing, the whole orchestra of her starvation tuning itself around her.

She looked at the brass knob. Just looked. She'd learned that much.

In its curved face, small and distorted, she saw the room beyond the white door reflected: not a kitchen, not a living room, nothing furnished at all. Just a small bare space, and in it a single wooden chair, facing the door. Facing *her*. Waiting.

Empty.

Always empty. It would always be empty. That was the thing about the dead — no door opened backward, no matter how many keys the house forged. Whatever sat waiting behind any door in this corridor, it would be a chair with no one in it, and the house would pour her mother's voice into the emptiness like tea into a cup with no bottom, and Casper would kneel there forever, pouring and pouring, and never once be filled.

"No," she said quietly. And then, folding the letter closed with a gentleness that surprised her — because whatever else it was, the handwriting had been her mother's, and some things you don't crumple — "I'm sorry, Mum. I'm working."

She tucked the letter into her jacket, over her heart, where the box's cold couldn't reach it quite so well.

And she walked past the white door without touching it, and the corridor screamed — actually screamed, a sound like nails drawn down the inside of the world — and every door along both walls burst open at once, spilling their warm yellow light and their impossible smells and their voices calling her name, her mother's name, Edith's name, names from the ledger, dozens of names, hundreds, all of them crying out *stay, stay, stay* —

—and ahead, at the end of the corridor, where the darkness had thickened all night into something almost solid, a shape detached itself from the wall.

It wore the father's smile. It was holding out its hands.

"You brought it home," it said, and its voice was five voices agreeing. "Now let's talk about what happens next."
