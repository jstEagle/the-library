## 22. The New Call-Out

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt right. Debts, Casper had learned, kept business hours.

She found it wedged in the screen door of Harrow Glen's side entrance, though the porch had been swept clean the night before and there had been nothing there when she checked at midnight — a habit now, checking, the way other people checked locks. The envelope was heavy cream stock, the kind that cost more per sheet than she used to charge for a full inspection. No stamp. No postmark. Just her name in a hand that was elegant and slightly wrong, the letters leaning as if written by someone standing at an angle to the page, or by several someones taking turns.

*Casper Yarrow, Keeper of Harrow Glen.*

She hadn't put that title anywhere. She'd never said it aloud. But the house had heard it anyway, the way it heard everything, the way it had heard her mother's name whispered down its corridors until Casper stopped flinching at the sound.

Inside, a single page.

*Dear Ms. Yarrow,*

*We are writing on behalf of the family at Wrenfield House, out past Kettle Creek. There is scratching in our walls. Our daughter says it answers her when she knocks. My wife has found prints on the inside of the pantry wall — five-fingered, greasy, human-sized. We called three pest services. Two never returned. The third sent a man who stood in our hallway for four minutes, watching the mirror, and then got back in his truck and drove away without a word.*

*You were recommended.*

*There was no signature to say by whom, but we understood. Please come. We will pay whatever is asked.*

*Yours in hope and considerable fear,*
*The Aldous Family*

Casper read it twice, standing in the doorway with the morning cold coming through the screen and the house warm at her back like a body standing close. Then she went inside, set the letter on the kitchen table beneath the window where the light was honestest, and made coffee she didn't drink.

The house had opinions about mornings. It liked the radiators to tick themselves awake before she rose; it liked the mirrors in the upstairs hall to be wiped with vinegar water on Sundays. In return it kept the drafts gentle and the cellar door from sticking. Fifteen years of pest control had taught her that every arrangement between two living things was a negotiation, and Harrow Glen was the only client she'd ever had whose terms she could live inside of.

Literally inside of. That was the part she'd stopped telling herself was strange.

* * *

The ledger lived in the cellar, on the shelf beside the jars of nails her predecessor — the house's previous heart, the thing with fingerprints, the five grieving people she'd named apart — had left behind. She still didn't have a good word for what it had been. *Entity* sounded clinical. *Ghost* sounded finished, and nothing about it had ever felt finished. She thought of it mostly as *the tenant*, because that was what it had acted like: something renting space in the walls, collecting on debts older than anyone alive, keeping the rooms comfortable for the souls it held because comfort was easier than freedom, for everyone involved, until someone came along willing to read names aloud.

She carried the ledger up to the kitchen table and set it beside the letter.

It was a heavy book, iron-cornered, its cover cold enough to fog in warm air no matter the season. Inside were pages of names in many hands across many decades — every soul the house had ever held, Edith had told her, every debt ever carried through its rooms. Since the night Casper had read them aloud in the long corridor, giving each trapped person their name back until the tenant unraveled into five separate mourners, the filled pages had gone quiet. Settled. Ink dried into history.

Except.

She opened to the first blank page — the one after her own name, added in ink she still couldn't account for, in handwriting too much like her mother's to be coincidence and too steady to be grief. The page was no longer blank.

Three lines had appeared near the top, in a brown-black ink that looked wet and wasn't:

*Eleanor Aldous.*
*Thomas Aldous.*
*June Aldous, aged nine.*

Beneath the third name, the ink was still moving. A fourth line was forming itself letter by letter, slow as frost climbing glass: *M* — *a* — *r* —

Casper sat down hard on the kitchen chair and watched it write.

She hadn't provided this ink. That mattered. She'd learned the ledger's grammar over the months — it recorded, it did not compose. When a name appeared, it meant a soul was already held, already folded somewhere into plaster and lath, already listening to its family knock on the wrong side of the wall. The ledger was a record of the taken, not a prophecy.

But this ink was fresh. And the address on the letter was forty miles away, past Kettle Creek, in a house she'd never entered.

Which meant either the ledger had learned to reach beyond Harrow Glen's walls — or the walls had grown.

She thought about the corridor. About how, carrying the box toward the hearth, the house had stretched itself around her like a throat deciding whether to swallow. About how, since the Merricks walked free and the tally marks gained her name, the house had felt — larger. Not in square footage. In appetite. A dog that has worked once does not forget the shape of the work.

"Nobody asked you," she told the ledger.

The ink finished the *g* in *Margaret* and waited, patient as a held breath.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing the things you do before a call-out, because ritual is just fear wearing work clothes. She serviced the van. She restocked the traps she no longer believed in but still carried, because clients needed to see tools, needed the theater of competence, needed someone to stand in their kitchen and say *I've handled worse* with a straight face. She wrote a note for the bulletin board in case anyone came looking — nobody would, but the note made the leaving feel temporary, and temporary was a fiction she maintained the way other people maintained gardens.

Before she packed, she walked the house.

This was new, this walk. For the first weeks she'd moved through Harrow Glen like a guest, apologizing to doorframes. Now she knew its moods the way she'd once known the moods of every rental property in the county: which stair complained in dry weather, which room held heat like a grudge, which window in the east bedroom showed the orchard at exactly the hour the light made it look like something worth saving. The house had been a ruin when she arrived, all water damage and sagging plaster and mirrors running a second behind the world. Now the plaster was smooth. The mirrors kept time. The warmth in the walls was real warmth, furnace-warmth, and if sometimes at dusk she felt the whole structure breathe around her — a slow expansion, ribs of joist and beam drawing air — she had decided, in the way you decide things that keep you sane, that old houses settle.

Old houses settle. New houses shift. Houses that eat families learn to want.

In the front hall she paused at the big mirror, the one that had lagged four seconds, then ten, then shown her a future she'd walked into willingly. It showed her now: a woman of thirty-eight in canvas work clothes, gray coming in early at the temples, a face that had gotten quieter each year and had recently stopped getting quieter and started getting *settled*, which was different, and which she tried not to examine.

Her reflection looked tired in a way she didn't feel yet. Filed that away. The cellar mirror showed tomorrow; maybe the hall mirror had started showing next week.

"Don't," she told it.

The reflection almost smiled. Almost. The difference between almost and actually was the width of everything, and she turned away before she could learn which side of it they were standing on.

* * *

Edith's grave was on her route home from anywhere, which is to say she detoured past it most days, and today she parked at the county cemetery gate and walked the rows with her hands in her pockets while the light went long and amber through the bare oaks.

*EDITH TOLLIVER, 1931–20—*. Below the dates, at Casper's request and expense, the stonecutter had added a line Edith herself had dictated in her last lucid week: *SHE LISTENED TO THE WALLS SO NO ONE ELSE HAD TO.*

"I got a letter," Casper told the grass. "Wrenfield House. Family named Aldous. Kid's nine."

The wind moved through the oaks with its usual indifference. Edith had never been an indifferent woman — she'd carried guilt for fifty years like inherited furniture, hauling it from house to house of her life, never once setting it down — but graves simplify everyone. What Edith would have said, if she were standing here with her cardigan buttoned wrong and her eyes sharp as tacks, was probably what she'd said the day she handed over the iron box: *You don't get to choose whether it's yours, girl. You only get to choose what you do with it being so.*

"It's writing names I haven't earned yet," Casper said. "That's new. That feels like it shouldn't be allowed."

The wind again. Somewhere a crow said one flat syllable and thought better of a second.

She crouched, pulled a weed from the base of the stone, and let herself say the true thing, the thing she'd say to no living soul: "Part of me wants to go. You know that, right? Part of me has wanted this since the corridor." Since the doors. Since the door with her mother waiting at a kitchen table, steam rising off a cup she'd never poured, a chair that had always been empty and always would be. Casper hadn't opened that door. But she'd stood before it with her hand raised, and the wanting had been so large and so simple that it had frightened her more than the tenant ever had — because the tenant only wanted her body, and the wanting wanted everything else.

The house had known. The house had counted on it. Houses that take people who owe — they can smell an owed thing from a long way off, and what Casper owed her mother was a conversation fifty years overdue, and no door in any corridor was going to provide it.

So she'd stayed instead. Chosen the trade. Become the keeper. And discovered, terrifyingly, that the cage fit like a coat she'd been measured for.

"Okay," she told Edith. "Okay."

She got up. Drove home through the blue hour with the headlights carving tunnels out of the dark, and the dark closing behind her like water, and somewhere under the engine noise — she was almost sure, almost — a rhythm in the roadbed answering the rhythm of her tires. Three beats. Three beats back.

* * *

Packing took twenty minutes. It had taken fifteen the first time, back when her toolbag held only the ordinary arsenal: flashlight, borax, snap traps, the borescope camera that had shown her a wall bulging like skin under pressure. Now the bag held those things plus a box of chalk, a flask of salt, a printed copy of the Aldous letter with certain phrases circled — *answers her when she knocks*, *prints on the inside* — and the ledger itself, wrapped in oilcloth, riding low against the bag's spine like a second vertebra.

She hesitated over that. Taking it felt like bringing fire to a forest. Leaving it felt like leaving a child's name unregistered.

The ledger solved the question itself. When she lifted the bag, the oilcloth warmed against her palm, faintly, unmistakably, the way the iron box used to hum. It wanted to come. Whatever the ledger was now — record, compass, lure — it considered itself part of the equipment.

"Fine," she said. "But you don't write anything unless it's already true. We clear?"

The oilcloth cooled. Agreement, or patience. With houses and their paperwork, the two were often indistinguishable.

Last, the cellar.

She went down the stairs with the flashlight off, because she didn't need it anymore — the cellar knew her steps and lit itself in increments, small warm pools blooming ahead of her feet like the house clearing its throat. At the bottom, past the shelf of nails, past the bricked-over hearth that was no longer bricked, that was open now and swept clean and held a small eternal fire she fed on Sundays and never otherwise — past all of it, to the last corner, where the one mirror hung that did not keep time.

It was a modest thing, a shaving mirror really, foxed at the edges, mounted on a nail by whoever had built this place and needed to see behind themselves while they worked. Every other mirror in Harrow Glen showed the present, punctual to the second. This one showed tomorrow.

Not dramatically. Not with thunder. Tomorrow arrived in it the way weather arrives: a grayness in the glass a shade deeper than the room deserved, a suggestion of events queuing up behind the visible ones. Most days it showed her only the cellar, a few hours advanced — the fire burned lower, her coffee cup emptied, small proofs that time was moving even here, especially here.

Tonight, when she stepped in front of it, the mirror showed her standing in front of it.

And then it showed her nodding.

She hadn't nodded. She stood absolutely still, arms at her sides, watching her reflection watch her — and the reflection gave a single slow dip of the chin, deliberate, the way you nod to someone across a room to say *yes, I saw you, yes, we're agreed.* Then it straightened, and became merely her mirror-image again, and the grayness in the glass thinned back to ordinary silver.

Her heart was doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional. She made herself breathe. Made herself count — a trick from the corridor, from the nights she'd read two hundred names aloud while the tenant came apart around her: numbers were a floor you could stand on when meaning got deep.

One. Two. Three.

A nod. Before hers. Her reflection had agreed to something she hadn't been asked.

Or — she made herself consider it, because the whole terrible education of Harrow Glen had been learning to consider things — the reflection hadn't agreed to anything. It had acknowledged. The way you acknowledge the driver of an oncoming car at an intersection. *I see you. Go ahead. I'll meet you there.*

Meet her where. Wrenfield House, presumably. Tomorrow, presumably. In a hallway with a lagging mirror and a nine-year-old girl knocking on walls that knocked back.

"Right," Casper said aloud, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Right."

She nodded — now, deliberately, on her own schedule, reclaiming the gesture — and the reflection nodded back a half-second late, obediently, the way reflections are supposed to.

For now, they were synchronized. She intended to keep it that way as long as the keeping lasted.

* * *

Dawn was still an hour off when she loaded the van. Frost furred the orchard grass; her breath hung in clouds; the house stood behind her with every window dark except one — the east bedroom, where the light was always on at this hour, though she'd never wired a bulb to any switch that controlled it. She'd stopped investigating that. Some courtesies you accept without auditing.

She set the letter on the passenger seat, weighted the ledger on top of it, and stood a moment in the gravel with her keys in her fist, looking back at the house that owned her.

It looked, in the blue pre-light, like what it had become: not a ruin, not a trap, but a lighthouse with the sea on every side. Something you tended. Something that burned for strangers. Something that required, as the price of its burning, that somebody stay.

"I'll be back tonight," she told it. "If the job runs long, tomorrow. Don't wait up."

The east-bedroom light dimmed, fractionally, and brightened again. Acknowledgment. Or amusement. She'd given up distinguishing.

She drove out the lane with the windows cracked despite the cold, because the house liked to hear the van leave and she'd learned to indulge it, and at the gate she stopped — one last habit, the check — and looked in the rearview.

Harrow Glen receded, patient as a held breath. And in the rearview's small warped oval, for just the length of the gate, she saw the house again — but furnished. Warm-lit. A table set for dinner, chairs occupied by shapes she refused to count, a woman at the window with her hand raised, not waving, not beckoning. *Watching.* Keeping her own kind of vigil, in a room that existed one second behind the world, or perhaps one second ahead.

Then the mirror held only road, and frost, and the graying sky, and Casper Yarrow alone at the wheel with forty miles and a family's borrowed fear in the seat beside her.

She drove toward Kettle Creek as the sun came up red and slow, and behind her, wrapped in oilcloth, the ledger lay open to its newest page, where a fourth name had finished writing itself in ink she hadn't provided — and beneath it, in smaller script, in a hand that was almost hers, a fifth line had begun:

*Delivered by:*
