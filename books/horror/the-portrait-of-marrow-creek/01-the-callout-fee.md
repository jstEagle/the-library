## 1. The Callout Fee

Ivo Lockridge counted doors the way other people counted sheep. It wasn't superstition, exactly — or it was, but a working kind, the kind you could bill for. Eleven doors between the van and any given job site meant an ordinary day. Twelve meant something had shifted, and Ivo paid attention to shifts.

The Vess farmhouse in Marrow Creek had fourteen doors visible from the gravel drive, and one of them stood open at the end of a porch that hadn't been painted since before Ivo was born. That made fifteen, and fifteen felt like a number with an opinion.

Ivo sat in the van a moment longer, engine ticking down, and reviewed the file on the phone screen. Callout fee: four thousand dollars. Follow-up work billed separately, terms generous, payment upfront via wire transfer already cleared. The client name was A. Vess, and the intake form — filled out by hand, scanned, uploaded — said only: *Something in the walls. It leaves prints.*

Four thousand dollars for a first callout. Ivo's standard rate was two hundred and fifty plus mileage, and most of Marrow Creek haggled it down to one-fifty and a thermos of coffee. Nobody paid four grand unless they were rich, or desperate, or both. The Vess place didn't look rich. Paint gone gray as old bone, a barn listing like a drunk leaning on a fence, fields gone to goldenrod and thistle. Which left desperate, and desperation paid well but always wanted something back.

Ivo got out, took the kit from the passenger seat — flashlight, borescope, moisture meter, gloves, the good headlamp — and counted steps up the porch. Seven. Odd number. Fine. Everything was fine until it wasn't.

The door opened before the knock landed.

"You're the pest man." The voice belonged to a man in his seventies, maybe older, propped against the doorframe like furniture someone had leaned there and forgotten to move. He was tall once; now he was mostly angles and papery skin, and his eyes were the pale washed-out blue of skim milk. "Aldous Vess. Come in. Mind the third floorboard."

"Mr. Vess. Ivo Lockridge." Ivo stepped in, counted doors out of habit — six inside, which was wrong for a house this size, meaning there were rooms behind rooms, or doors that led nowhere anymore. "Your form says something in the walls. Can you describe what you've seen? Droppings, grease marks, gnawing damage?"

"Prints," Aldous said. He closed the door behind Ivo, and Ivo heard the latch catch with a sound like a knuckle cracking. "Small ones. In the plaster. My granddaughter found them first. She counts things too, I think. You'll get along."

"I'd like to see them."

"In time." Aldous turned and moved down the hall without checking whether Ivo followed, which was either trust or indifference, and Ivo couldn't decide which was worse. The house smelled of dust and camphor and, underneath it, something mineral and cold, like the air over a well. "First, terms. Four thousand covers today. You find what's making the prints, you tell me what it is. You don't touch it. You don't remove it. You identify it. That's all I'm buying."

Most clients wanted removal. Exclusion work, sealing, baiting, the whole program. This one wanted a diagnosis and nothing else, and he'd priced it like a specialist consult rather than an extermination. Ivo had been doing this eleven years and had learned that when a client constrains the work, the client already knows the answer and doesn't want it confirmed by anyone who might act on it.

"That's unusual," Ivo said carefully. "If I identify an active infestation, my professional recommendation would normally include—"

"Your recommendation is yours. My money is mine." Aldous stopped at a doorway — the seventh interior door, so now the count was thirteen, and Ivo's spine prickled at the symmetry — and looked back with those milk-pale eyes. "You seem like a careful person, Mr. Lockridge. Careful people do well here. Reckless ones don't finish the job."

*Here.* Not *in this house.* Here, in Marrow Creek, or here in some larger territory Aldous considered himself the landlord of.

"I'll need access to the walls," Ivo said. "Attic, cellar, exterior inspection. And I'll want to know how many people live in the house."

"Three. Myself, and my grandchildren. Wren and Tam." Something moved behind Aldous's face, quick as a fish under ice. "They're good children. They'll show you where the prints are. I'd rather not look at them myself."

He said it flatly, the way a man says *I'd rather not drive at night,* and then he was moving again, leaving Ivo to follow through a house that kept rearranging its own arithmetic.

* * *

The grandchildren were waiting in the kitchen, and they were not what Ivo expected from the word *grandchildren.* Wren was maybe nineteen, sharp-faced, dark-haired, sitting very straight at the table with a notebook in front of her that she closed the moment Ivo entered — closed it like you close a door on a room you don't want company seeing. Tam was younger, twelve or thirteen, all elbows and restless hands, and he was standing on a chair pressing his ear flat against the wall.

"It stopped," Tam reported, disappointed, dropping down. "It was scratching. Like, writing. But it stopped when the truck came."

"Trucks don't scare things in walls," Ivo said. "Engines vibrate. Sometimes it makes them go quiet."

"This isn't scared-quiet," Tam said. "This is polite-quiet."

Wren gave her brother a look that had clearly been deployed many times before. "Ignore him. He narrates everything. I'm Wren. You're the pest controller. Grandfather said you only identify, not remove."

"He mentioned that."

"Do you always do what clients say?" she asked, and her tone wasn't hostile — it was diagnostic, the same way Ivo asked questions, and Ivo recognized a fellow counter when she looked at one.

"I do what the contract says," Ivo said. "Within reason. If whatever's in your walls turns out to be a hazard — structural, sanitary, a danger to anyone in this house — identification stops being enough, and I'll say so. In writing if necessary."

Something loosened around Wren's mouth. Not quite a smile. Approval, maybe, filed under restraint. "Okay," she said. "Come on. I'll show you the first ones. They start upstairs."

She led Ivo up a staircase that creaked in a pattern — three creaks, pause, two creaks — and Ivo counted the treads out of reflex. Thirteen again. The house was full of thirteens, and every one of them felt less like coincidence than like punctuation.

The first print was in the hallway outside what Wren called the blue room, though the room was green. It was pressed into the plaster at knee height, small — child-sized, maybe smaller — five fingers splayed, palm flat, as if something had braced itself there mid-climb. Plaster doesn't take prints easily. It crumbles, it resists. This print was crisp. Every whorl defined. Ivo put on a glove, leaned close with the flashlight raked low across the surface, and felt the professional vocabulary begin to fail.

Raccoons leave hand-like prints, but raccoons have claws, and these had none. Possums have thumbs, but possums don't press palms flat against vertical plaster inside sealed wall cavities. And no animal leaves dermal ridges — actual fingerprint whorls, arches and loops, the kind you match in a lab.

"These are human," Ivo said. "Or — they're human-type. Ridge patterns. Child-scale."

"We know," Wren said.

"They can't be human. There's no entry point, no exit point. I'll need to open the wall—"

"No," Wren said quickly, and then, more controlled: "Grandfather's rule. Identify, don't disturb. Please. He gets — upset. When things are disturbed."

Ivo straightened up and looked at the print again. Then at the wall beside it, where the flashlight caught a second print, fainter, higher. And above that, a third, ghost-faint, near the ceiling line. A descending line, coming from somewhere above. Or an ascending line, going toward it. Direction depended on what you thought was climbing.

"How many have you found?" Ivo asked.

Wren opened her notebook — the one she'd closed — and turned it around. Pages of sketches. Dates. Locations. Heights off the floor. She'd been cataloguing them the way Ivo catalogued everything, and the notebook went back eight months.

"Hundred and forty-one so far," she said. "They come in pairs. Mostly. Two appear, and later two disappear — not fade, *disappear*, the plaster goes smooth again like nothing was ever there. Grandfather says it's damp. Damp doesn't make fingerprints, Mr. Lockridge."

"No," Ivo agreed quietly. "Damp doesn't make fingerprints."

Tam appeared on the stairs behind them, silent for a kid his size, and pointed past Ivo at the descending line of prints. "There's more in the cellar," he said. "But we're not supposed to go down there after dark, and you came late, so." He shrugged, elaborately casual, and failed completely at casual. "You could stay. For the count. We've got a spare room. Grandfather already made it up."

Ivo hadn't quoted an overnight rate. Hadn't offered to stay. The wire transfer had cleared that morning for exactly the callout fee, and yet the spare room was made up, sheets turned down, as if the house had budgeted for Ivo's presence before Ivo knew about it.

"I should check my equipment," Ivo said slowly. "And the exterior. Daylight work first."

"Sure," Tam said. "But the prints move at night. Wren marked them, right? You'll want to see where they go."

*Where they go.* Prints didn't go anywhere. Prints were records, not travelers. But Ivo looked at the descending line — knee, shoulder, ceiling — and thought about the smell under the house's dust, that cold mineral breath like air off a well, and thought about a client who wanted identification and nothing else, priced like a secret.

"I'll stay," Ivo heard themself say, and watched relief cross both grandchildren's faces at once, identical, synchronized, like two halves of one expression.

* * *

The exterior told Ivo almost nothing, which was itself information. No droppings anywhere. No tracks in the soft earth along the foundation, though the ground held every other mark — deer, dog, Wren's sneakers, Tam's smaller ones circling the yard in patterns that looked like games and might not have been. No gnawing at soffits or vents. Whatever used the walls used them without entering or exiting, which violated the basic physics of having a body.

At dusk Ivo came back inside and counted the doors again. Sixteen now. Ivo stood in the hall with the clipboard and did not write the number down, because writing it down would make it data, and data would demand explanation, and Ivo wasn't ready to explain why a house gained doors.

Wren found Ivo in the kitchen, where Ivo had set up the borescope and was feeding the camera into a gap behind the baseboard. On the little screen: plaster lath, dust, darkness — and a curve of something pale, smooth, rounded, gone before the scope could focus.

"What was that?" Wren whispered.

"Couldn't tell you. Could be a pipe fitting. Could be—" Ivo stopped. Professional detachment. Wary professionalism. That was the contract with oneself, if not with Aldous Vess. "Could be anything. I'll run the scope through every cavity tomorrow. Systematic. Room by room."

"Room by room," Wren repeated, and there was something in her voice — not fear exactly. Hunger. The hunger of someone who has been alone with a mystery for eight months and just watched reinforcements arrive. "Nobody's ever been systematic before. Grandfather won't look. Tam looks, but Tam's nine—"

"Twelve," Tam called from the doorway, wounded.

"—and Grandfather says identify, don't disturb, and nobody will tell me *why* those are the rules." She set her notebook on the table, open to the last page, where she'd drawn something that wasn't a sketch of prints. It was a tally. Rows and rows of tally marks, grouped in fives, and beneath them a single line of handwriting: *What is it counting?*

Ivo looked at the tally for a long moment. Outside, the light died off the windows, and somewhere in the walls — faint, patient, unhurried — something began to scratch. Not randomly. Rhythmically. In groups.

Five. Pause. Five. Pause. Five.

Then a pause longer than the others, and one more scratch, solitary, waiting to be joined.

"Counting," Tam said softly, from the doorway, watching their faces. "Told you it writes."

Ivo reached over and switched on every light in the kitchen — overhead, counter, the old fluorescent tube that buzzed like a trapped fly — because eleven years in attics and crawlspaces had taught one lesson above all others: keep the lights burning, keep the numbers known, and never let the dark be the only one keeping books.

"Tomorrow," Ivo said, "you're going to show me the cellar."

Wren's eyes flicked to the ceiling, where the scratching had paused — listening, Ivo thought, the way you listen when your name comes up in another room.

"After dark," she said, "it shows you the cellar whether you ask or not."
