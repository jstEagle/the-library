## 2. Small Hands, Old Plaster

I counted the doors on my way in. Eleven. Twelve if you counted the cellar hatch, and I did, because a door is a door whether it lies flat or stands up, and a man who doesn't count his exits is a man who's already decided he won't need one.

The Vess farmhouse had eleven doors and forty-one windows, of which nine were painted shut. I logged all of it in the little green notebook I keep in my breast pocket — doors, windows, egress points, water sources, the location of every electrical panel. People think pest control is about poison. It isn't. It's about arithmetic. You count what comes in, you count what goes out, and when the numbers stop matching, that's where the work begins.

Aldous Vess watched me do it from a wheelchair by the kitchen window, hands folded on a wool blanket, face like a folded letter nobody wanted to open.

"You'll want to start upstairs," he said. "That's where they say."

"Where who says?"

"The children." His eyes didn't move from the yard. "They hear it at night. Behind the plaster. Like someone knocking with the flat of a hand instead of knuckles."

I wrote *flat of hand* in the notebook, underlined it once, and hated myself for underlining it. Underlining is for things that matter, and nothing mattered yet. Nothing ever matters until it does.

* * *

The plaster told me before anyone did.

I'd been in the house three hours when I found the first handprint. Not on the plaster — behind it. The wall in the upstairs hallway had a bulge the size of a dinner plate, soft when I pressed it, and when I cut a survey square with my utility knife and peeled the lath back like the lid off something, there it was: a print pressed into the back of the plaster itself, dusted gray, five fingers, small. Child-small. Maybe eight years old, maybe smaller.

Except no child had been inside this wall, because children don't fit between lath and brick, and even if one did, it wouldn't have pressed its palm into wet plaster from the *back*. Plaster goes on wet and dries hard. To leave a print in it, you have to be there while it's setting. This house was built in 1891.

The print was fresh. That was the part I kept circling back to while I ate a sandwich in my truck and tried to talk myself out of what I already knew. The edges were crisp. No dust had settled into the whorls. Whatever had made that print had made it recently, against a surface that had been dry for a hundred and thirty years.

I went back in and opened more walls. That's the job — you don't guess, you open. Behind the hallway wall: two prints, side by side, left and right, like a child standing with its palms flat against the inside of the house. Behind the linen closet: four. Behind the wall at the top of the back stairs: six, arranged in a descending line, each one lower than the last, each one closer to the floor, as though whatever made them had been walking down a staircase only it could see, trailing its hands along the inside of the walls.

The line continued. Back stairs, landing, kitchen wall, pantry. Every print a little lower than the last. Every print pointing down.

They ended at the cellar hatch. The twelfth door. The last print sat in the plaster directly beside it, small palm, small fingers, pressed so deep the plaster had cracked around it like ice around a boot.

I stood there a long time with my knife still open and my notebook still shut, because some findings don't fit in notebooks. Then I heard footsteps on the stairs above me, light ones, two sets, and I closed the knife and put on the face I wear for clients — the competent, slightly bored face that says *it's probably mice.*

It wasn't mice. But the face costs nothing to wear.

* * *

"You opened my grandmother's wall," said the girl.

She was maybe sixteen, dark-haired, arms crossed, standing at the bottom of the back stairs with a boy of ten or eleven half-hidden behind her. Wren and Tam, according to the intake form Aldous had signed. Grandchildren. Caretakers, by the look of them — the girl had caretaker written all over her, in the way she stood between me and her brother, in the way her eyes went first to the opened wall and then to me, assessing which was the bigger threat.

"I opened a section of lath to inspect for activity," I said. "Standard practice. I'll patch it before I leave."

"There's something behind there." Tam said it from behind his sister's elbow, matter-of-fact, the way kids report weather. "We hear it walking."

"Walking where?"

Tam looked at the floor. "Down."

Wren shot him a look that said *we agreed not to lead with that*, and something in the exchange — the silent shorthand of two people who've been keeping watch together too long — snagged on something in me I didn't have a name for yet. I'd worked alone nine years. Nine years of trucks and motels and clients who lied about what they'd seen. I'd forgotten what it looked like, people covering for each other.

"It's likely raccoons or squirrels in the wall voids," I said. "Weight on old lath makes sounds travel strange. Sounds like steps. Sounds like—" I almost said *hands* and stopped myself.

"Like hands?" Wren said.

I didn't answer, and my not-answering answered, and she knew it, and I could see her deciding what to do with that knowledge. She decided, apparently, to feed me. That's how it started, if you want the honest version. Not with trust. With soup.

* * *

Aldous Vess deflected the way old men deflect — by answering a different question entirely, slowly, with authority, until you forgot what you'd asked.

I showed him photographs of the prints on my phone. He looked at each one for exactly as long as politeness required and handed the phone back.

"Old house," he said. "Settling does queer things to plaster."

"These are handprints, Mr. Vess. Pressed from inside the wall cavity."

"Children's games, perhaps. When the house was young." He adjusted the blanket over his knees with great care. "You'll find the family Bible has gaps in it. Houses do too. Gaps aren't evidence of anything except that people were tired of writing."

"What's in the cellar?"

His eyes came up from the blanket for the first time, and for one unguarded second I saw something underneath the folded-letter face — not fear exactly. Scheduling. The look of a man checking a clock only he can read.

"Nothing," he said. "Dry well. Sealed since before my father. Keep your business above the floorboards, Mr. Lockridge, and we'll have no trouble concluding our arrangement."

Our arrangement. Three thousand dollars, half up front, wired from an account with a bank name I didn't recognize. I thought about that wire transfer a lot over the next few days. Nobody pays three thousand dollars for raccoons. You pay three thousand dollars when you already know what it is and you want someone else to touch it.

I said, "I'll need access to the cellar eventually. Pest pressure maps from below."

"You won't." And he smiled, and the smile was the worst thing in the house, worse than the prints, because it was patient. "But thank you for asking."

* * *

The children found me the next morning in the pantry, where I was running a borescope through a gap in the wainscoting, and instead of shooing them off I let them watch, and that was the second mistake, or the second right thing — I've never settled which.

Tam held the flashlight. He held it well, steady, tracking where I pointed without being asked twice. Wren asked questions that weren't really questions — *how long have you done this, what's the strangest thing you've found, do you work alone* — questions shaped like curiosity but weighted like reconnaissance. I answered honestly, which surprised me. I'm not a dishonest man but I'm a private one, and there was something about these two, their careful tiredness, that made privacy feel like a luxury I could afford to spend.

"Strangest thing," I said, feeding the scope deeper into the wall. "Bat colony in a church attic, once. Thousands of them. They'd arranged themselves on the rafters in pairs. Always pairs. If one died, another would move to fill its place within a day. Even number, every time I checked. Two hundred and six bats, every inspection, for a year."

"That's not strange," Tam said. "That's just math."

"Math is strange, kid, if you stare at it long enough."

The scope screen glowed green. I was looking at the inside of the exterior wall, sixty years of dust and mouse droppings and one dead starling, ordinary as bread — and then the image shifted as I angled the lens downward, and there, on the inner face of the brick, low near the sill, was another print. Small. Fresh. Fingers spread, pressing toward the cellar.

And beside it, close enough to be touching, a second print. Larger. Adult-sized. As if something small had been holding something bigger by the hand, and both of them had pressed their palms to the inside of the house.

I hadn't seen that one yesterday. I'd scoped this exact stretch of wall yesterday. I checked my notes. Same panel, same height, same angle. Yesterday: clean brick.

Wren was watching my face, not the screen. She saw whatever moved across it.

"They're not always alone," she said quietly. "Are they."

"No," I said. "Apparently not."

Tam turned off the flashlight, and the three of us stood in the dark pantry for a moment, and it should have been frightening and it partly was, but mostly it was the first time in nine years I'd stood in a dark room with other people and not minded the dark. That scared me more than the prints did, if I'm honest. The prints belonged to the house. The other feeling belonged to me.

* * *

That night I ran thermal cameras through every room, per protocol, and per protocol I logged the readings, and per protocol I noted the anomaly: a consistent cold column in the kitchen floor, roughly circular, roughly three feet across, running colder than ambient by eleven degrees. Directly beneath the kitchen. Directly beneath the last handprint in the descending line.

Directly beneath the place where Aldous Vess had told me, smiling, there was nothing.

I packed up my gear at ten and told the family I'd be back tomorrow with extraction equipment, and Aldous thanked me from his wheelchair without moving his eyes from the window, and Wren walked me out to the truck past the eleven doors, and at the bottom step she stopped.

"He's counting us," she said.

I waited.

"Aldous. Every night at supper he looks around the table and counts us under his breath. Him, me, Tam. Three. And then he counts again, slower, like he's waiting for the number to change. Like he needs it to come out different." She hugged her elbows against the cold. "I thought it was the illness. The doctors say it's in his mind, the counting. But today you looked at that wall the same way he looks at the table."

I got in the truck. I sat with the engine off for a long time, watching the farmhouse in the rearview, every window lit because I'd told them to keep every light burning — a standard anti-wildlife measure, I'd called it, and they'd obeyed like people glad to be told what to do.

Eleven doors. Forty-one windows. A line of small hands walking down through the walls toward a sealed well. A dying man counting to three and wanting four.

In the green notebook, under *flat of hand*, I added a new line, and underlined it, and this time I didn't hate myself:

*The house isn't haunted. The house is accounting.*
