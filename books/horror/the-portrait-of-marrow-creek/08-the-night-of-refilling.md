## 8. The Night of Refilling

I woke because the house had gone quiet in the wrong way.

Not the quiet of sleep — sleep has texture to it. Breathing through walls, a fridge cycling, Tam's television murmuring down the hall at two a.m. when he thought nobody knew. This was the quiet of a held breath. The kind of quiet a room makes when someone is standing in it.

I lay still on the cot in the back office where Aldous had insisted I stay — *you'll want to be close to your work, Mr. Lockridge* — and counted doors by memory. Front door. Kitchen door. Cellar door. Back porch. Four ways out, and I'd checked every latch at sundown because that was who I was, that was what twenty years of crawling under houses taught me: know your exits before you know anything else.

The lamp on my nightstand was off.

I had left it burning. I always leave it burning. Lights on, count even, that's the whole religion of my trade — you don't give the dark an unattended doorway into your head. And now the lamp was off, and I could not remember turning it off, which meant either I hadn't or something in this house had opinions about light.

I clicked it on. The bulb glowed amber and steady, and the small comfort of it let me think.

That's when I tasted the tea.

* * *

Wren had brought me chamomile at nine. She'd stood in the doorway with two mugs, one for me, one for herself, and she'd said, "Grandfather says we should all sleep well tonight," and there had been something in her voice like a person reading a note they weren't supposed to have found. I'd drunk half the mug while talking her through tomorrow's plan — the parish records, Mercy Vess, the name spoken downward. She'd drunk hers too.

Now my mouth was dry as chalk and my limbs felt poured from wet cement, and I understood, with the slow horrible clarity of a man watching his own arithmetic fail, that the tea had been dosed. Both mugs. Wren hadn't known. Aldous had made sure she wouldn't.

I got up. The floor tilted and agreed to hold me. I put my shoulder against the wall and walked.

The hallway stretched longer than it should. Every door along it stood open except the ones I needed open, and I counted them as I passed — one, two, three — because counting kept the cement from setting. Under my feet the floorboards were warm. Not warm like a heated house. Warm like skin.

"Aldous," I said aloud, and my voice came out flattened, as if the hallway were eating the edges of it. "Whatever you signed, you don't have to do it tonight."

The hallway did not answer. But somewhere below — beneath the kitchen, beneath the cellar, beneath the dry well that was no longer entirely dry — something answered anyway. A sound like water being poured very slowly into a very deep place, except there was no water in Marrow Creek that ran without asking permission first.

* * *

I found the kitchen empty. Two chairs pushed back from the table. Three mugs — Wren's, Tam's, and Aldous's own, still steaming, untouched. He hadn't needed the drug himself. Whatever he was about to do, he wanted to be awake for it.

The cellar door stood open at the end of the kitchen, and the dark coming up out of it was not the ordinary dark of a stairwell. It moved. Slowly, patiently, the way a tide moves, and it smelled of iron and old rain and something sweeter underneath, something like flowers laid on a grave.

"Wren?" I called down it. "Tam?"

Nothing. Then, faintly, from deep below: footsteps. Small ones. Descending.

I went to the cellar door and put my hand on the frame, and that was when the front door slammed.

Not opened. Not blown. *Slammed*, hard enough to shake dust from the ceiling, and then I heard the bolt throw itself, a brass clack like a knuckle cracking. I turned. The kitchen door — the one to the mudroom — swung shut with the same deliberate weight. Clack. The porch door followed. One, two, three, four, the house closing its exits in sequence, unhurried, exact, a hand ticking off fingers.

It was counting itself shut.

"Okay," I said to the house, because I talk to buildings, I always have, and because my voice steadied me more than any prayer would. "Okay. You want to play it that way."

I went for the windows. There were four in the kitchen and I'd oiled every latch myself last week, professional habit, and now every sash came down against my hands like a jaw snapping closed. I put my knee into one and shoved until my thigh burned. The window didn't so much as rattle. Behind the glass, the night looked normal — stars, the black line of the tree line, the dead fields — and its normality was the most obscene thing I'd seen all week.

The lights flickered.

Every bulb in the kitchen dimmed together, once, twice, like the house blinking, and I understood what it was doing. It wasn't trying to scare me. It was testing how much fight I had left in a drugged body, and it was doing it on a schedule, because inheritances keep schedules, and somewhere below us the well was filling and Aldous Vess was standing at its rim reading words off a page older than the parish, and the only thing between his grandchildren and that dark was a pest controller with concrete legs and half a cup of chamomile in his blood.

I grabbed the kettle off the stove and filled it, and while it heated I took inventory the way I take inventory of an infestation: what's here, how many, where does it travel. One patriarch, downstairs, committed. One boy, wrong-eyed since Tuesday, possibly already walking. One girl — Wren — who drank the tea but whose mind had been sharpening itself against this house for months, and who had read the deed, and who knew the name.

Mercy Vess. Say it downward. Empty the well.

All I had to do was get to the bottom of the stairs before the house finished closing.

* * *

The stairwell dark rose to meet me like water rising in a lock, and halfway down I heard Tam's voice.

"Ivo." Sweet and flat, a recording of a child rather than a child. "Come see. Grandfather says come see."

He was three steps above the well chamber, holding a candle, and his eyes caught the flame and gave nothing back. Wren was beside him, swaying, her face slack with the drug, and Tam had her wrist in his small hand and was drawing her forward one gentle inch at a time, patient as tide, patient as arithmetic.

"Tam." I made my voice the voice I use on panicked animals, low and level. "Let go of your sister."

"He never left," Tam said, smiling. "Did you know that? He never left. He just got counted wrong. And Grandfather's fixing the count, Ivo. Grandfather's making it even."

"The count doesn't need fixing," I said, and started down the stairs, and the house felt me coming.

The door at the top slammed behind me. The candle flames bent sideways, all of them, toward the well, as if the whole room were inhaling. Below, the pouring sound had become a rushing, and up out of the well throat came the smell of iron and grave-flowers so thick I could taste it, and threaded through it — thin, cracked, unmistakably human — a voice. Not the second voice from the recordings. An old man's voice, reciting, rhythmic, liturgical.

Aldous. Reading the refill into the world.

Wren's knees buckled. Tam pulled her another step, and her heels dragged two lines through the dust, and I saw the rim of the well ahead of them, and I saw the dark standing up out of it like a column, like a thing being poured upward instead of down, and I stopped thinking like a professional and started moving like family.

I hit Tam from the side.

We went down together, the candle rolling away in a comet of sparks, and I wrapped both arms around the boy and held him because he was strong, impossibly strong, the strength of the house in him, the strength of the count, and he twisted in my grip and said in a voice that was not entirely his: "*One body short,*" and drove his elbow into my ribs where the cement had set hardest.

"TAM." Wren. On her knees, fighting the drug, fighting her own eyelids, her hand finding the back of her brother's collar. "Tam, look at me. Look at *me*. You're Wren-and-Tam. You're a pair. You're *even.*"

Something in him shuddered.

And Aldous's voice below faltered — one syllable, half a beat — because the count had wobbled, because a pair had refused to split, and in that half-beat of silence I heard the well make a sound I will hear until I die: a sound like a great held breath finally beginning to exhale after a hundred years.

Then Aldous's voice resumed, faster now, urgent, and the dark in the well surged, and the last candle died, and in the blackness I heard the old man say, clear and close and terrible:

"*Bring me the girl.*"

I got my feet under me. I got Wren's arm over my shoulders. I got Tam's collar in my fist, and I turned us all toward the stairs, toward the door that had counted itself shut, and I said the only prayer my trade ever taught me:

"Lights on. Count even. We walk out."

Behind us, at the rim of the well, something began to climb.
