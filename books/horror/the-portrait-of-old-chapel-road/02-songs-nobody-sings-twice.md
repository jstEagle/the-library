## 2. Songs Nobody Sings Twice

The Lamb and Lantern had the particular smell of pubs that had stopped trying: old ale soaked into floorboards, woodsmoke that never quite left the stone, and beneath it all something faintly mineral, like water standing too long in a cellar. Ivo Yarrow noticed smells before she noticed people. It was a folklorist's habit—scent was memory's first archive—and so she stood in the doorway of Hollowmere's only public house for a full three seconds, cataloguing, before anyone looked up.

When they did look up, they did it in a wave, faces turning toward her one after another like a wind moving through barley, and then turning away again just as deliberately. No hostility in it. Something worse: a practiced courtesy of omission. She might have been weather.

The landlady was a broad woman with forearms like a blacksmith's and a name Ivo would later learn was Mrs. Hobb. She pulled a pint without being asked and set it on the bar with a small nod that managed to be both welcoming and final.

"You'll be the one at the Hollis cottage," she said. Not a question. "Folklorist."

"That's right. Ivo Yarrow." She offered her hand; Mrs. Hobb looked at it for a moment as though it were an unusual object, then shook it briefly, her grip dry and strong. "I'm collecting songs. Local songs. The old ones especially."

"Are you," said Mrs. Hobb, and turned to serve a farmer who hadn't actually asked for anything.

Ivo took her pint to the corner table by the window—the one seat from which she could see the whole room without seeming to watch it—and set her recorder bag on the bench beside her like a sleeping dog. Through the glass, Old Chapel Road ran downhill into the dark, and somewhere along it the chapel itself stood with its dry well, its boarded windows, its refusal to be anything but what it was. She'd photographed it that morning. In every frame, no matter the angle, the wellhead sat slightly off-center, as if the building were leaning away from it.

The pub filled slowly. There were perhaps twenty souls by half past seven, and Ivo noted, without meaning to, that they arranged themselves the way water arranges itself around a stone: leaving gaps, soft spaces, places where no one sat. One such gap surrounded a table near the fire where an old man sat alone with a pint of bitter and a face like a plowed field.

Tam Weller. She knew him already from the parish newsletter's ancient photograph and from Maren Oduya's brief description—"the last one who remembers them properly"—though Maren had said it looking out the window, not at Ivo, and had changed the subject immediately after.

Ivo nursed her drink and waited. Waiting was most of the work. People sang when they forgot you were listening, and forgetting required time, and comfort, and usually a second pint.

It took forty minutes and the arrival of a younger man—Weller's son-in-law, by his manner—who clapped the old man on the shoulder and said something Ivo couldn't catch. Tam laughed, a dry rustle. And then, because the fire was low and the evening had gone soft and the young man said, louder, "Go on then, Dad. Give her the harvest one. She's come all this way"—and here he tipped his head toward Ivo's corner, and Ivo felt the whole room flinch, a single animal motion—

Tam Weller looked at her.

His eyes were pale blue and very clear, and what was in them was not reluctance. It was recognition. He looked at her the way a man looks at a bill he has been expecting.

"Well," he said. "All this way."

He drank half his pint in one long pull, wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, and began to sing.

* * *

Later—Ivo would think about this many times in the weeks to come, turning it over like a stone with something underneath it—later she would try to reconstruct exactly how the song went, and find that she could not. Not the words, not the tune, not even its shape. Her recorder held it. Her own ears, in the moment, held it. But the moment it ended, it slid out of her memory like water through cupped hands, and only the fact of it remained: that an old man in a dying village had opened his mouth and given her four verses of the oldest thing she had ever heard.

She had the presence of mind to start the recorder two lines in. She remembered fumbling the zipper, the small mechanical click, the red light. She remembered thinking, absurdly, *this is why we're here, this is the whole reason any of us do this*—the sense of holding something alive and vanishing in the palm of your hand.

The song was a lament, though the word felt inadequate. Harvest laments existed across the whole island, whole archipelago, whole world—songs sung when the fields were cut down, mourning and thanking in the same breath, death and gratitude braided together because the reaping is both. She knew hundreds of variants. This was none of them.

This one moved downward. That was the only way she could describe it to herself even as she heard it: each verse descended, musically, emotionally, like a stair going under. The first verse was about the wheat, or something like wheat—it named the crop in words she didn't recognize, older words, the kind that survive in songs after they've died everywhere else. The second verse named the singers' grandmothers. The third named the ground. And the fourth—

The fourth verse began, "And what we gave the well we give it still—"

And Tam Weller stopped.

Not trailed off. Stopped. Mid-word, mid-breath, his mouth still open around a syllable that never arrived, and the silence that followed was so total that Ivo heard the fire settle and the clock above the bar tick twice.

The color left his face the way water leaves a sink—all at once, and then in reluctant dregs. His hands, which had been keeping time on the table's edge, went flat and still. He stared at the middle distance with the expression of a man who has walked into a room and forgotten, catastrophically, why.

"Dad?" The son-in-law's voice, uncertain now.

"Sing the rest, Da." A woman had risen from a table near the door—young, dark-haired, with Tam's same field-worn face softened by thirty years. His daughter. "You always sing the rest. You've sung it every harvest since I was small, you never stop at—Da, finish it."

"I can't." His voice came out sanded flat. "I've said too much already."

"It's four verses. It's always been four verses."

"It's five." Tam Weller said it quietly, to the table, to his own hands. "It's always been five. We don't sing the fifth."

"Then sing the fifth," his daughter said, laughing a little, frightened by his face. "Da. Everyone's looking."

"I know they're looking." He pushed back his chair. It shrieked against the flagstones and every person in the room winced as one body. "That's rather the point of it, girl."

He stood there a moment, swaying slightly, an old tree deciding whether to fall. Then he took his coat off the chairback, put money under his glass, and walked out into the dark without looking at anyone, without looking at Ivo most carefully of all.

His daughter followed him, calling his name into the night. The door swung shut behind them both, and the pub exhaled, conversation resuming in a low, careful murmur, everyone suddenly very interested in their drinks.

Ivo sat with her recorder's red light burning and her heart going hard against her ribs.

* * *

She played it back before bed. Of course she did. She was a scholar; the tape was the truth; whatever her ears had done with the song, the machine kept it faithfully.

Except that it didn't, quite.

The recording was good—better than good, the little condenser mic catching the room's warmth, the fire, the creak of the bench. Tam's voice came through cracked and true. Verses one through three were everything she'd hoped: a variant unlike any in the archives, possibly unique, possibly the last living thread of a tradition older than the parish records. She made notes in the margin of her notebook with a hand that wasn't entirely steady. *Descending melodic contour. Archaic lexicon—cf. no known cognates. Performance context: communal, but—*

But.

Between the third verse and the fourth, between the line naming the ground and the line beginning *what we gave the well*, there was a gap. On the tape it lasted eleven seconds. Eleven seconds of room tone: fire, breath, the tick of the clock. She had no memory of an eleven-second gap. In her memory the song had flowed unbroken until it broke.

She listened again. The gap was there, patient as a held door. And underneath the room tone—not beneath it in volume, but beneath it in some other register, the way a fish is beneath the surface of a pond—there was something else. Not a voice. Not yet a voice. A quality in the silence, the way a room where someone has just died has a quality.

She told herself: tape hiss. Room resonance. The microphone picking up the chimney draft.

She told herself this while she played the fourth verse six more times, and each time, at the word *well*, she felt the hair rise on her forearms, and each time she wrote nothing down about it, which was itself a kind of data, if anyone had been there to collect it.

* * *

The landlady found her at closing.

Ivo had stayed past the last of the locals, nursing a second pint she hadn't wanted, writing up her field notes in a hand that grew more cramped as the hour wore on. Now Mrs. Hobb collected glasses around her with the efficient deafness of publicans everywhere, and Ivo assumed she was invisible again, and was surprised when the woman stopped at her table and set something down beside her notebook.

Coins. The exact price of her first pint.

"You've overpaid me," Ivo said. "I haven't finished the second."

"That's for the first." Mrs. Hobb stood with her tray against her hip, and in the empty pub her face had lost its professional blankness and become something older and wearier. "Your drink tonight. It's paid."

"I don't understand."

"No," said Mrs. Hobb. "You don't. Best keep it that way as long as you can." She glanced at the recorder bag on the bench, and her mouth thinned. "You got what you came for, I expect. Take my advice and let that be the end of it. Some songs are sung once so they don't have to be sung twice."

"He stopped," Ivo said. "Mr. Weller. Why would a man stop singing his own song? His daughter said he sings it every year."

Mrs. Hobb looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the wind had come up, moving down Old Chapel Road from the direction of the chapel, and it made a sound against the window that Ivo's ear—trained, professional, treacherous—insisted on parsing as almost-words.

"Because he remembers the fifth verse," Mrs. Hobb said. "And because remembering isn't the same as owing. You sing a thing, you owe a thing. That's how it works here. Has worked here since before my grandmother." She straightened, and the blankness came back over her face like a shutter. "Drink's paid. Go home to your cottage, Miss Yarrow. Count your doors if you like. We all do."

"Count my—"

But Mrs. Hobb was already stacking glasses, already elsewhere, and the word hung in the smoky air with nowhere to land.

* * *

Ivo walked home through streets that had emptied early. Nine o'clock, and every window on Old Chapel Road was dark behind its curtain except one—a lamp burning in a downstairs room three doors from hers, and she thought it might be Maren Oduya's house, the beekeeper's, though she couldn't have said why she thought so. The lamp glowed amber and steady, and Ivo found herself walking faster to get past it, and then slower, wanting to look, and then faster again.

At her own gate she stopped and did something she hadn't done since childhood: she counted the doors of her cottage. Front door, back door, the cupboard under the stairs that had a door, the little hatch to the cellar that had a door. Four. She counted them twice, because the first count had felt insufficient, and could not have told you, if asked, what she was checking for.

Inside, she lit the lamp, set the recorder on the kitchen table, and looked at it for a long time.

The scholar in her—the self she had built out of libraries and patience, the self that believed every mystery was a footnote waiting to be written—wanted to play the tape again. All of it. The eleven seconds. The fourth verse. The place where the fifth should have been, the shape of its absence, which was itself a kind of music if you knew how to listen.

The rest of her, the part that had counted the doors, wanted to put the machine in a drawer and leave it there until morning.

She compromised, as scholars do. She played the fourth verse once more, pen ready, determined to transcribe it properly this time, to pin it down in notation before it could slide away again.

"And what we gave the well we give it still," Tam Weller sang, in the little speaker, in the lamplit kitchen, in the house that agreed she should be invisible. "What we gave the well we give it still—"

And then, in the eleven seconds that followed, in the space where the fifth verse refused to live, Ivo heard—clearly, unmistakably, in a register below hearing and above imagination—someone else breathing.

Slowly. Patiently. As if it had all the time in the world, and all the world besides.

She stopped the tape. The lamp flickered once, though the windows were shut.

Outside, far down the road, a dog began to bark, and then thought better of it.
