## 3. Field Notes, Night One

The cottage had a name once. Ivo found it scratched into the lintel above the kitchen door in a hand that had pressed hard enough to split the wood grain: *Wren's Rest*. Below it, someone had chiseled the date 1847, and below that, in a different century's handwriting, someone else had added *and be quiet in it*.

She appreciated the honesty of that. Most houses lied about what they wanted from you.

By nine o'clock the village had gone dark around her—not gradually, not like towns usually dimmed, with porch lights lingering and televisions flickering blue behind curtains, but all at once, as if a hand had passed over Hollowmere and closed its eyes. Ivo stood at her window with the lamp off and watched the last lamp on the road—old Tam Weller's, she guessed, by its position—shutter itself behind glass, and then there was nothing but the moon doing indifferent work over the rooftops, and her own window, the only lit square on Old Chapel Road.

*They can see me*, she thought, and then corrected herself: *They can see my light.* There was a difference, and the difference was the whole architecture of her life. People had never looked at Ivo Yarrow; they had looked at the space where she wasn't, at the chair she'd angled away from the group, at the silence she left in conversations like a place setting removed before the meal. She had made an art of it. Unremarkable on purpose. A woman built to be skimmed past.

And yet Hollowmere counted its doors when she walked by. She'd heard it tonight—Mrs. Aldous at the corner house, murmuring numbers under her breath as Ivo passed: *four, five, six*—not counting her own doors, Ivo was almost sure. Counting *hers*. As if Ivo were a door too, something that might swing open in the night if nobody kept track of it.

She turned from the window and got to work, because work was the one room in her life where she had never felt like a guest.

* * *

The equipment went up on the kitchen table: the field recorder with its worn leather strap, the shotgun microphone, the headphones whose earcups had been reupholstered twice with foam and gaffer tape until they fit her skull like a reliquary. She'd recorded ambient sound in forty-one villages across three countries—bells, looms, the particular creak of a specific bridge in Norfolk that no one under seventy could hear anymore—and she had a ritual for it, honed and private as prayer. Set the levels. Let the machine breathe. Listen back the same night, while the day's sounds still clung to the inside of your ears like burrs.

Tonight's capture was thin pickings. She'd run the recorder from dusk onward, microphone propped in the open bedroom window facing the lane, gathering whatever Hollowmere gave up after dark. The first hour was wind—that dry, papery wind that came down off the ridge and combed through the hedges, making them whisper like an audience settling. Under it, the tick of the cottage cooling. Once, distantly, a dog that stopped barking mid-note, which happened with dogs and meant nothing.

Ivo made her notes in pencil, in shorthand only she could read. *23:14 — wind NE, steady. No song. Noted absence.*

That last phrase again. She'd written it three times today already. *Noted absence.* It was becoming the unofficial title of the whole expedition. A folklorist came to collect songs the way a beekeeper came for honey, and Hollowmere had sealed every comb. Nobody sang past dark. Nobody sang twice. The archive she'd crossed half the country for was guarded not by indifference but by fear, and fear, professionally speaking, was the most interesting material there was. She should have been thrilled.

She was thrilled. That was the problem. Thrill and dread lived so close together in her chest that she'd long ago stopped being able to tell which one was knocking.

She poured the last of the tea—cold now, tannic, faintly bitter—and put the headphones on, and scrubbed back through the file to the deepest hour, between two and three, when even insomniac villages bottom out into true silence. This was where the good stuff lived, if there was good stuff. The sound underneath the sound.

There was wind. There was the hedge-whisper. There was—

Ivo's hand stopped on the wheel.

Between her own breaths.

That was the thing she noticed first, later, when she tried to reconstruct it: the rhythm of it. She had been asleep by then—she could hear herself in the recording, the slow tide of her breathing through the open window, the small unconscious sounds a body makes when it has surrendered the day. And threaded into the gaps between those breaths, fitted into them with a precision that made her scalp tighten, was another voice.

Low. Cracked, like a riverbed in August. Patient—the way stone is patient, the way rot is patient.

It was answering questions.

She hadn't asked any questions. She knew that absolutely, the way you know your own signature. She had come home, made tea, written notes, gone to bed. But the voice spoke in the cadence of answers—in the pauses where questions would go, filling them, completing them—as though a conversation had been happening all along and she had simply failed to attend her side of it.

She caught fragments. The wind chewed most of the words, but some came through whole, and they were ordinary words, which was somehow worse than strange ones would have been:

*"—the third door, yes—"*

*"—she counts better than he did—"*

*"—ask her again when the water—"*

Then wind, long and blank, and her own breath, and then the voice again, closer to the microphone or simply more awake:

*"—not yet. Let her listen first."*

Ivo took the headphones off.

The kitchen was very quiet. The refrigerator hummed its one note. Outside, nothing moved, and the darkness beyond the window had the specific density of a thing pressing its face to the glass—not close, exactly, but attentive. She sat with her hands flat on the table, feeling the wood's cool grain, and performed the discipline she had spent thirty years perfecting: she explained it.

Radio bleed. Of course. The recorder's input gain was set high—she remembered adjusting it at dusk, chasing the hedge-whisper—and cheap receivers picked up stray transmissions, truckers' chatter, shortwave numbers stations, the ghost-traffic of a crowded spectrum. She'd captured bleed before. In Suffolk she'd gotten four minutes of a shipping forecast laid over a christening hymn, and it had sounded, played back at midnight, like the sea itself learning to pray. Voices out of context were just noise wearing meaning the way a coat rack wears a coat. Put the coat on yourself and you'd swear it moved.

That was the explanation. It was a good explanation. It had physics in it.

She put the headphones back on and played it again.

*"—the third door, yes—"*

Third door. Her cottage was the third from the corner. Mrs. Aldous had counted six doors this evening, walking the lane, and Ivo's had been among them—had it been the third? She tried to remember and couldn't, and the not-being-able-to sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

*Ask her again when the water—*

When the water what. Rose? Came? Was asked about? The sentence broke off where the wind took it, and the incompleteness had teeth. She wrote in her notebook, pencil pressing hard: *Possible radio bleed. Check freqs tomorrow. Note: phrasing conversational. Note: "she."*

Note: *she.*

* * *

On the fourth listening, she stopped pretending to take notes.

She had the passage isolated now—ninety seconds, timestamped 02:47 to 02:49—and she'd learned its shape the way you learn a piece of music: where the wind swelled, where the voice surfaced, where her own sleeping breath rose and fell like a slow bellows feeding a fire she couldn't see. And each pass revealed another layer, the way staring at a dark window eventually shows you your own face.

The voice wasn't random. It was *responsive*. On the fifth listening she caught it clearly: her sleeping body sighed—a long exhale, the kind that carries a dream with it—and in the exact pocket of that exhale, the voice said, softly, *"No. Slower."* As if correcting her breathing. As if teaching it.

On the sixth listening, she heard it say her name.

Or nearly. The wind took the consonants, but the shape beneath was unmistakable—two syllables, rising slightly on the second, the way people said *Ivo* who weren't from anywhere near here, who'd only ever read it aloud to themselves. *Eye-vo.* The founders' pronunciation, maybe. Or the well's.

She paused the file. Sat in the dark kitchen with the headphones around her neck like a stethoscope, listening to her own heart, which was doing something unprofessional.

Here was the scholar's truth, the one she'd built her whole career inside: recordings didn't lie. Tape was the honest witness folklore had always lacked. Every legend she'd ever collected dissolved a little under a microphone—became weather, became memory, became a story a frightened person told another frightened person until the fright wore smooth as a river stone. She had spent her life being the person who arrived afterward with the machine that proved the monster was a barn owl.

So the machine was telling her there was a voice in her bedroom window at 2:47 in the morning, answering questions she hadn't asked, in a village where nobody sang after dark because something had taught them not to.

She thought about Tam Weller going grey mid-verse, his mouth closing like a drawer. She thought about the landlady refunding her drink without a word, the coins placed gently, the way you'd settle a debt with something you didn't want to touch. She thought about *and be quiet in it*, carved into her own lintel a hundred and seventy years ago by hands that had known why.

"Radio bleed," she said aloud, to the kitchen, to prove her voice still worked in it.

The refrigerator hummed. Nothing answered. But the silence had a texture now, a held-breath quality, and she understood suddenly why the villagers shuttered their lamps at nine—not to keep the dark out, but to stop giving it anything to look at.

* * *

She played it eleven times.

She would count them later, in the margin of her notebook, the tally standing there in her own neat hand like evidence against her: eleven passes through ninety seconds of tape, each one a small act of self-harm dressed as scholarship. Because that was the other truth, the one she kept in the locked drawer of herself: the thrill was real. Whatever the voice was, it was *hers*—her find, her recording, her name in its cracked mouth—and some cold bright core of her, the part that had spent decades being overlooked and had turned the overlooking into armor, felt seen by it. Terribly, precisely seen. *Let her listen first.* First before what? Before whom? The voice had plans for her, and the shameful, sleepless fact was that being planned-for felt, for one vertiginous moment, like being wanted.

Then the moment passed, and what remained was dread with a scholar's face on it.

On the eleventh listening, she heard the last thing. It came at the very end of the ninety seconds, after the voice had sunk back beneath the wind, in the final breath-gap before her sleeping self stirred toward waking. Four words, so quiet she had to raise the gain until the tape hissed like rain:

*"Goodnight, then. Tomorrow."*

Not a question. An appointment.

Ivo sat back. The kitchen window had gone grey-black with the hour, and somewhere down the lane a cockerel cleared its throat and thought better of it. She looked at her notebook—at *possible radio bleed*, at the eleven tallies, at the word *she* circled twice—and she did what she had always done with things that frightened her: she filed them. Closed the notebook. Capped the pencil. Made the morning tea ritual motions with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be.

But when she finally lay down, as the sky over the ridge went the color of dishwater, she left the recorder running. Just in case. Just to have the witness.

And in the pause before sleep took her, in the gap between one breath and the next, she found herself listening—for an answer.

Down the lane, unseen in the last dark, the old chapel well stood dry and sealed behind its iron grate, as it had since 1911.

For now.
