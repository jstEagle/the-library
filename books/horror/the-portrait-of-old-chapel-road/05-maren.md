## 5. Maren

The knock came at half past ten in the morning, which was itself a small miracle. In Hollowmere, Ivo had learned, visitors did not knock before noon or after three, as though the hours between were somehow unclean, and they certainly did not knock on her door at all. She had been in the cottage nine days. Nine days of nods across the lane, of the postman leaving her parcels on the wall rather than handing them to her, of Mrs. Aldous at the shop counting her change into her palm without letting their fingers touch.

She stood very still, listening, the way she had learned to stand still and listen since the recordings began. The knocking came again—three raps, unhurried, the knuckles of someone who expected to be answered but would not be wounded if she wasn't.

Through the frosted pane of the front door she could see a shape: broad-shouldered, wearing something yellow. Not the grey wool and oilskin that seemed to be the village uniform. Yellow, like a struck match held up against all that damp.

Ivo opened the door.

The woman on her step was perhaps fifty, with skin the deep brown of turned earth and hair going silver at the temples, cropped close and practical. She wore a beekeeper's veil pushed back off her face like a hood, and she was holding two jars of honey against her chest the way other people held infants. Her eyes were dark and quick and, most startlingly of all, they looked directly at Ivo—not past her, not through her, not with that careful deflection the village had perfected, but *at* her, the way you look at a person you have decided actually exists.

"You're the folklorist," the woman said. It wasn't a question. "Ivo Yarrow."

"I am." Ivo heard how rusty her own voice sounded, used these days only for murmured notes and one-sided conversations with equipment. "And you are?"

"Maren Oduya. I keep bees on the south slope, past the Hendersons' fallow field." She lifted the jars slightly. "Limeflower and heather. The lime is this year's; the heather's from two seasons back, but honey doesn't spoil, it only changes its mind about what it wants to be." A pause, and then, with the directness Ivo was already beginning to suspect was characteristic: "Can I come in? Your step's cold and my knees are fifty-one years old and honest about it."

Ivo hesitated. It was not hesitation about Maren herself—or not only that. It was the accumulated instinct of nine days, the sense that every kindness in Hollowmere arrived wrapped in conditions she hadn't read yet. But the woman's gaze held steady, and there was no ledger visible anywhere in her face, and Ivo realized with a small, humiliating jolt how long it had been since anyone had stood on her threshold wanting nothing more complicated than to be let inside.

"Of course," she said, and stood back.

* * *

Maren entered the cottage the way water enters a dry basin—completely, without apology, filling every corner of the small front room within moments. She admired the shelves of recording equipment without touching any of it, which Ivo noted with approval; she commented on the view from the window ("You can see the chapel roof from here. Most people would call that a selling point"), and she set the jars of honey down on the kitchen table with a soft glassy clink that sounded, in the quiet house, like a small bell being rung.

"Tea?" Ivo asked, because her mother had raised her to ask, and because the alternative was standing in her own kitchen feeling like a guest.

"Please. Milk if you've got it, sugar if you haven't." Maren settled into the chair by the window—the good chair, though Ivo suspected she'd have taken whatever chair was offered—and looked out at the lane with an expression Ivo couldn't quite parse. Something between affection and appraisal. The way you look at a house you grew up in after learning what was in its walls.

"You'll have noticed," Maren said, while the kettle began its low gathering hum, "that nobody talks to you."

Ivo kept her back turned, measuring tea into the pot. "People talk to me. They tell me where the shop is. They nod."

"They *nod*," Maren agreed. "Hollowmere could win prizes for nodding. My husband used to say—" She stopped. Just stopped, mid-clause, the way you stop when you've stepped on something in the dark and need a moment to work out whether it moved. Then she continued, quieter: "My family came here in 1974. From Kampala, by way of Manchester. We were the first new blood this village had accepted in living memory, and even then it took them eleven years to stop calling us 'the Oduyas' like we were weather."

"And now?"

"Now they call me Maren, when they call me anything." She turned from the window. "But I'm telling you this so you understand what I'm about to say isn't sour grapes. I'm not bitter about the nodding. I understood the nodding. What I couldn't work out, for years, was why they nodded at *me* and wouldn't even look at *you*. And then I worked it out, and I didn't like the answer, and I've been sitting on it for nine days deciding whether to bring you honey or mind my own business." She spread her hands. "As you can see, I brought the honey."

The kettle climbed toward its whistle. Ivo poured the water over the leaves and watched the tea steep, buying herself thirty seconds of ordinary domestic ritual before she had to answer. When she finally turned around, carrying two cups, she said: "Tell me the answer."

Maren took the cup but didn't drink. She held it in both hands, warming her palms, and studied Ivo over the rim with those direct dark eyes.

"They're kind to you because you're temporary," she said.

The words landed in the room and stayed there. Outside, a crow called once from the chapel roof and fell silent, as if it too were waiting.

"Temporary," Ivo repeated.

"That's how it works here. That's how it's always worked, long before my family came. There are people who belong to Hollowmere and people who pass through it, and the ones who pass through get a different treatment. Politer, in some ways. Kinder, in others. Nobody asks them to help with the harvest. Nobody invites them to things, but nobody shuts a door in their face either. They're given the last habitable cottage and left alone, and everyone is very, very careful never to give them a reason to stay longer than they meant to." Maren finally drank, set the cup down. "Or shorter."

"Shorter," Ivo said. Her mouth had gone dry despite the tea.

"There was a birdwatcher, maybe six years back. Stayed a fortnight at the B&B above the shop. Lovely man, obsessed with nightjars. On his last morning Mrs. Aldous gave him a free breakfast and the vicar walked him personally to the bus stop, and everyone waved him off like a nephew." Maren's voice was level, but Ivo heard the thing underneath it, the way you hear stone under shallow water. "And the whole time he was here, do you know what they did? Every evening, they counted their doors. Out loud. You'll have heard it—I know you have, don't look surprised, the walls here are thin and the people are old and they count loud enough for God to hear them. One door, two doors, three doors. They weren't counting their own doors, love. They were counting yours. Making sure you were still just passing through. Still just one person, in one house, for one while."

Ivo sat down slowly across from her. Through the window, the light had begun to go strange and coppery, the way it did here in the late afternoon, as though the sun were being slowly lowered into something.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you've got that look." Maren pointed at her with one blunt finger. "That look you get when you've been alone so long that the first warm thing that happens feels like fate. I've seen it. I wore it myself, my first winter here, when Mrs. Aldous gave me a jar of damson jam and I nearly cried over it at my own kitchen table. Loneliness makes you grateful for crumbs, and grateful people don't ask what the crumbs cost." She leaned forward. "So I'm asking for you. What does the kindness cost, Ivo? Why does a village that won't speak your name make sure your rent is fair and your chimney draws properly? Why did the landlady at the pub refund your drink the night Tam Weller stopped singing—and don't think I didn't hear about that, the whole village heard about that—and why, out of every empty house on this road, were you given the one closest to the chapel?"

The questions stacked up like stones. Ivo found she had no answer for any of them, only the cold spreading feeling that she had noticed each of these things herself, individually, and filed them away as curiosities rather than adding them together.

"I came for the songs," she said at last, and heard how thin it sounded. "The harvest laments. The wassails. This village has musical traditions that exist nowhere else in the county. That's all. That's genuinely all."

"I believe you." Maren said it simply, without irony. "I believe that's all *you* came for. But folklorists aren't the first outsiders to arrive here thinking they knew what they'd come for. There was a surveyor in 1953. A photographer in 1968. A young priest doing research on parish boundaries in 1981." She ticked them off on her fingers, and her voice dropped lower with each name. "They all stayed at the end of Old Chapel Road. They were all treated gently. And they all left early, except the priest, and nobody will tell me what happened to the priest."

The fire in the grate had burned down to embers without Ivo noticing. She got up to feed it, mostly to have something to do with her hands, and when she spoke again her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You said your family came in 1974. After the founding. What founding?"

Maren was quiet for a moment. When Ivo glanced back, the woman was looking at the window again—at the chapel roof, just visible above the yew hedge, its slate gone green with age.

"The founding of the custom," she said. "Whatever it is they do here. Whatever it is that makes grown men stop singing mid-verse and go grey as ash. Whatever it is that makes them count doors and shutter lamps at nine and keep their children indoors when the well—" She caught herself. Stopped. Picked up her tea and drank, and Ivo watched her decide, visibly, deliberately, not to finish the sentence.

"When the well what?" Ivo asked.

"It's dry," Maren said. "It's been dry since 1911. That's all I was going to say. It's dry, and everyone is very glad it's dry, and you should be glad too, and that's the last I'll say about wells." She set the cup down with a firmness that ended the subject, then rose, briskly, brushing off her skirt as though the conversation were a chore she'd completed. "I've taken enough of your morning. The bees want checking before the light goes."

"You could stay," Ivo said, and heard the nakedness in it too late. "For tea. For—longer. I don't—"

She stopped. Maren waited, patient, giving her room to finish or not.

"I don't know anyone here," Ivo finished quietly. "Nine days, and you're the first person who's looked at me. Properly looked. I know how that sounds."

"It doesn't sound like anything I haven't thought myself," Maren said. "Fifteen years a widow, and the village counts me as theirs now, but theirs-at-a-distance. Bees don't gossip much." She smiled then, and it transformed her face, made her suddenly beautiful in a worn and weathered way, and Ivo felt the warmth of it land somewhere under her ribs like a coal. "Come up to the apiary sometime. Thursday, if the weather holds. I'll show you the hives, and if you're lucky the swarm will behave, and if you're luckier still I'll tell you what I know—which is more than the villagers think I know and less than you'll want to hear."

"I'd like that."

"I know," Maren said, not unkindly. "That's what worries both of us."

At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at Ivo with an expression that had gone serious again, stripped of the smile.

"One more thing, and then I'll go. The honey—the lime, especially—eat it before bed. A spoonful. Don't ask why, just do it. And if you ever hear counting in the night that isn't coming from a neighbor's house—if it's coming from under the ground, or under the road, or from inside your own chimney—you put the lamp out and you don't answer it. No matter what it counts. No matter whose voice it counts in." Her eyes held Ivo's. "Especially not if it counts in a voice you know."

Then she was gone, down the path and along the lane, the yellow of her veil shrinking between the hedgerows until the village swallowed it, and Ivo stood in her doorway holding two cold cups of tea and watching the place where she'd been.

* * *

That evening she opened the limeflower honey.

She told herself it was for the taste. She was a folklorist; foodways were part of her remit; sampling local produce was practically methodology. She spooned some onto bread and ate it standing at the counter, and it was very good—pale gold, faintly resinous, tasting of high summer in a way that made the wet grey lane outside seem like a rumor.

But after the third bite she noticed something, and stood very still with the spoon in her hand.

The cottage was quiet. It was always quiet—that was part of what she'd loved about it, in the beginning, before the quiet started answering back. But tonight the quiet had a texture. Beneath the wind in the chimney, beneath the settling of old timber, there was a rhythm. Faint. Regular. Almost like breathing.

Almost like counting.

She fetched her recorder from the front room with hands that were steadier than she had any right to expect, set it on the counter, and let it run for ten minutes while she washed the dishes, dried them, put them away. Then she played it back with the gain pushed high, headphones on, the world shut out.

Wind. Timber. The tick of the cooling stove.

And underneath, so far down in the mix that she might have imagined it, might have invented it out of Maren's warning and her own rattled nerves—a voice. Not the cracked, patient voice from her first night's recordings. Something older in the sound of it, or younger, or both. It wasn't speaking words, exactly. It was keeping time. One. Two. Three. Four. Rising and falling like breath, like waves, like footsteps circling a house.

Like someone walking the perimeter of her life, counting the ways in.

Ivo pulled the headphones off. The cottage was just a cottage. Wind, timber, stove. She screwed the lid back onto the honey jar, and her reflection looked back at her from the glass, pale and doubled in the amber.

*A spoonful before bed,* Maren had said. *Don't ask why.*

She ate another spoonful standing right there at the counter, watching her own eyes in the honey-colored glass, and went to bed with the lamp burning past nine for the first time since she'd arrived.

Outside, far down Old Chapel Road, something counted to forty and started over.
