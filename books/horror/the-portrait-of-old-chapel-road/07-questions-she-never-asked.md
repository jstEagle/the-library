## 7. Questions She Never Asked

The voice came back on the third night, and this time it did not wait for the wind to cover it.

Ivo had learned its rhythm by then—the way it arrived in the pauses, filling the space between her breaths like water finding the low places in a stone. She had stopped telling herself it was radio bleed. Radio bleed did not pause politely. Radio bleed did not *wait*.

She sat at the kitchen table with her headphones on and her recorder running, the cottage around her shuttered and dark except for the green eye of the equipment light. Outside, Hollowmere had gone through its nightly liturgy: doors counted aloud at dusk—she'd heard old Mrs. Petchy through her window, murmuring *one, two* as she touched her own doorframe and then, absurdly, Ivo's from across the lane—lamps shuttered at nine exactly, and then nothing. A village holding its breath. She had thought, when she first arrived, that the silence was neglect. Now she understood it was discipline. The silence was a wall they all maintained together, brick by brick, every night, and she was the only one who kept making noise inside it.

*Ivo Yarrow.*

Her name. Not spoken so much as remembered aloud, the way you'd recall a word you'd read years ago and never used. Dry. Patient. It came through the left earpiece only, though both channels were live, though the microphone sat on the table six feet from her and no one else was in the room.

She did not answer. That was the rule she'd set herself, the small dam she maintained against the flood: she would listen, because listening was her profession and her compulsion and possibly her illness, but she would not speak to it. Speaking was an invitation. Every folklorist knew that. You didn't answer the thing that called your name out of a well; you wrote down what it said and you told yourself the writing made it safe.

*You were seven,* the voice said. *The summer of the culvert.*

Ivo's hand went still on the notebook.

*There was water in it that year. Not much. Enough. Your brother dared you and you went in anyway, though you were afraid of it—you were always afraid of water, even then, even before you had reason—and you found a bicycle bell in the mud. Silver. Cracked down one side so it rang wrong, a flat note, a note that fell instead of rose. You kept it in your coat pocket for two winters until the coat was thrown away and you cried about the coat but not the bell, because by then you'd already learned which losses were acceptable to grieve.*

The recorder turned. Ivo could hear it turning, the faint mechanical whisper of tape, capturing everything—the voice, the silence after it, and underneath, unmistakable now, the quickening percussion of her own heart picked up by the sensitive condenser mic she'd placed too close to her chest.

*Your mother sang while she cooked,* the voice went on. *Not well. She sang the way people hum, absent, half a melody with words missing, and you used to stand in the doorway and try to catch where the words should go. You invented lyrics for her. You never told her. When she died you tried to remember the tune and found you could only remember your inventions, and you have hated yourself for that for eleven years—not because you lost the song, but because you replaced it. Because even at fourteen you were already an archivist of the wrong things. Already more devoted to what you made than what was given.*

"Stop." It came out of her before she could stop it, a whisper, barely voiced, and she heard it land on the tape and wanted to tear the tape out and burn it. She had answered. After three nights of discipline, four syllables had broken the dam.

The voice paused. When it resumed there was something in it that might have been satisfaction, if satisfaction could be dry as chalk dust, as old paper, as a mouth full of well-water.

*You said stop. But you leaned closer.*

She had. She realized it with a lurch of vertigo—she had pulled the headphone tighter against her ear, had bent toward the recorder like a plant toward light, her body committing an act of hunger her mind hadn't authorized. That was the violation under the violation: not just that it knew these things, but that knowing them, it had found the exact shape of her wanting. Everyone alive who knew about the culvert and the bell and her mother's half-songs had earned that knowledge across years of proximity, years of being let in. This thing had simply reached into her and read her like a ledger, and worse—it knew which entries she treasured. It knew the ones she'd have paid anything to keep private, and it laid them out gently, almost tenderly, the way a thief lays out stolen jewelry on velvet to show you he appreciates the craftsmanship.

*No one living knows those things,* it said. *You checked. You've spent your whole life making sure no one living knows them. You are unremarkable on purpose. You wear grey. You rent rooms at the edge of towns. You introduce yourself with your surname only, as if Yarrow were armor. And yet here, in this village that does not look at you, you feel—for the first time since the culvert—*

"Don't." Her whisper cracked.

*—seen.*

The word hung in the dark kitchen. Ivo pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw red constellations. It was true, and the truth of it was the cruelest instrument in the whole performance. She had come to Hollowmere because Hollowmere ignored people. She had chosen it off a map the way you choose a blindfold. Half-abandoned, the listing said. Dying oral tradition, urgent preservation needed. No one would ask her questions. No one would want anything from her. She had built her entire adult life into a shape designed to pass through rooms unnoticed, and this village—with its counted doors and shuttered lamps and its refusal to sing past dark—had seemed like the one place on earth that agreed with her architecture.

And now something in a dry well was telling her, in a voice like a page turning, that it had watched her arrive and recognized the design. That her invisibility wasn't camouflage here. It was a costume the village had been waiting for someone to put on.

*Ask me something,* the voice said.

She shook her head, then felt foolish—head-shaking meant nothing to a microphone, or to whatever listened past the microphone.

*Everyone asks eventually. Tam Weller asked whether his daughter would live. Ellery Finch will ask why him, though not for some weeks yet. Ask me something, Ivo Yarrow. I have been under this village since before it had a name, and I am very good at answers. Answers are most of what I have left.*

"I don't have questions," she whispered, and hated how thin it sounded, how much it sounded like a girl in a doorway inventing lyrics for a song she couldn't keep.

*You have eleven questions,* the voice said mildly. *You have had them since the night you played my first words back. Shall I count them? One: what is it. Two: why me. Three: why now. Four: what happened in 1911. Five: what do the tallies on the doorframes mean—I know you've seen them, you photograph everything, you photographed the well today and noticed the water higher in each frame, clever girl, careful girl. Six: why does the vicar burn juniper at the lip of the stone. Seven: what did Tam Weller stop singing. Eight: what happens if the song finishes. Nine: what happens if it doesn't. Ten: why did they let you stay when they send everyone else away. Eleven—*

"Stop."

*Eleven:* the voice continued, unhurried, patient as sediment, *you wonder if anyone has ever stayed for you. Not for your recordings, not for your archive, not for what your hands can do—but for you. You wonder it constantly and you have never once said it aloud, and you are wondering right now whether I will say it back to you, and whether hearing it from a dead thing under a chapel would still count.*

The kitchen was very cold. Ivo became aware of it gradually, the way you become aware of a headache—as something that had apparently been building for some time without your permission. Her breath fogged above the table. The window glass wore a skin of frost despite the mild September night, frost growing inward from the corners in fern-patterns, reaching toward the center of the pane like something pressing its palm against the other side.

She looked at the recorder. The tape reels turned. The level needle trembled with her heartbeat, faster now, much faster, a rabbit's rhythm rendered in ink.

*Will you sing the seventh?*

There it was. Four nights of circling and it had finally landed on the question itself, plain as a stone dropped in still water. She didn't know what the seventh verse was. She wouldn't learn until later, in the vestry dust, in the founders' ledger with its six blood-brown signatures and its waiting blank line. But the question needed no context. It arrived complete, the way dread arrives complete, and she understood it the way you understand a hand extended in the dark: something wanted her voice. Something had wanted it since before she was born, perhaps, since the founders signed their pact and left the last line empty—a socket shaped like a throat, waiting decades for the right instrument to walk down Old Chapel Road carrying microphones.

"No," she said.

The frost on the window stopped growing.

*No,* the voice repeated, tasting it. *You said stop twice tonight and meant neither time. You said no once and meant it entirely. Do you know how rare that is? In forty years, no one has meant it. They mean yes slowly, over months, the way fruit softens. Or they mean yes quickly, in terror, which spoils the flavor. But you—" it paused, and the pause had texture, had weight, like something leaning close to a candle flame to study it without burning, "—you mean it and you're still sitting there. Still listening. Still recording. You are the strangest thing to come down this road since the founders themselves, Ivo Yarrow, and I have eaten strangers older than their language.*

"You haven't eaten me," she said. Her voice was steadier than she felt, which surprised her. There was a strange clarity in extremity, she was discovering—a scholar's calm that descended when the material got truly impossible, as if some deep stratum of her personality had been waiting her whole life for evidence this bad so it could finally do its work.

*No,* the voice agreed. *Not yet. Eating is for afterward. First there is the singing. There is always the singing. Tell me—when you played my voice back, the first night, eleven times—what did you hear in it?*

Ivo stared at the recorder. The reels had stopped. She hadn't noticed when.

"A man," she said slowly. "An old man. Tired. Like Tam Weller, but older. Like a recording of Tam Weller's grandfather."

*A man,* the voice repeated, and something moved beneath the word, some vast adjustment of posture in a space far too small for it. *Interesting. To the founders I sounded like the river. To their children, like their mothers. To the last singer, forty years ago, I sounded like her own voice, returned. You hear an old man. I wonder what that says about what you're missing.*

"What am I missing?"

But the frost was receding from the window, drawing back to the corners like a tide going out, and the cold lifted from the kitchen, and the voice—if it answered—answered below the threshold of hearing, in a register she felt in her sternum rather than her ears, one long descending note like a bell rung underwater, like a silver bell cracked down one side so it rang wrong, a note that fell instead of rose.

Then nothing. Wind. The tick of the cooling stove. Her own breathing, ragged, loud, embarrassingly human.

Ivo sat for a long time with the headphones around her neck. Then, because she was who she was—because the illness and the profession were the same thing—she rewound the tape and played it back.

It was all there. Every word. Her own whispers, thin and frightened. The heartbeat rising under everything like a drum under a hymn, accelerating through the whole exchange, and she could hear the exact moment the rhythm changed character—from fear to something else, something with a different tempo, a different intent. Anyone analyzing the tape would hear it. Any listener would hear it. The heartbeat didn't sound like terror.

It sounded like recognition. Like a woman hearing a song she'd forgotten she knew, her pulse picking up the beat before her mind consented to it. Like an answer delivered by the one part of her that had never learned to lie.

She played it again. On the eleventh playback, near dawn, she caught something she'd missed every time before: beneath the voice, beneath her heartbeat, at the very floor of the recording where the noise lived—there was singing. Distant, layered, many-voiced, wordless. Six voices, she counted, though counting them was like counting ripples. Six voices singing a melody that circled and circled and never resolved, holding itself open at the end, leaving a gap in the phrase the exact size and shape of a seventh line.

The sun came up grey over Old Chapel Road. Ivo took off the headphones, went to the window, and looked out at the lane where Mrs. Petchy stood in her garden in the early light, touching her doorframe, lips moving. Counting.

Across the lane, on Ivo's own doorframe, white and fresh in the dawn, were new chalk marks. She hadn't put them there.

There were seven of them.
