## 20. An Eighth Verse, Unwritten

**IVO**

The water was singing in six voices when Maren came through the collapsing floor.

Ivo heard her before she saw her—the scrape of boots on wet stone, the ragged pull of breath—and then the dark above the crypt split open and Maren Oduya dropped through it, landing hard on the founders' bones, one hand pressed to her side where blood seeped between her fingers like something spilled from a jar. Behind her, at the broken lip of the chapel floor, Ellery Finch stood swaying, his face white as candle tallow, a lantern in one hand and a bell in the other. The bell was cracked down its throat. It made no sound at all.

"Maren—" Ivo started forward, but the water stopped her. It had risen past their ankles now, black and warm as bathwater, and it moved against the current of their movement, curling toward them with an attention that felt like being read.

"I'm fine." Maren's voice came out ground flat. "It's not mine. Mostly not mine." She looked up, and in the light of Ivo's dying torch her eyes were fierce and terrified and absolutely certain. "The vicar's men tried to carry you down here ahead of us. We disagreed."

"Ellery," Ivo said, because Ellery had begun to sway again, and because she did not know what else to say to a boy who had given his voice to a song he'd learned from her recorder. "Ellery, are you—"

He shook his head. Then he held up the note he had written her, the one she still carried folded against her ribs, and tapped it twice with one finger: *It doesn't want your song. It wants your consent.* His other hand found the bell and gripped it like a weapon that had failed him once already and might fail him again.

The well convulsed.

It was not a sound so much as a swallowing—a vast muscular contraction beneath the floor, and the black water leapt a foot into the air all around them and came down singing. Six voices now, braided and patient, and beneath them a seventh that was no voice at all but the pressure of being known, the feeling of a great dry mouth opening somewhere under the world.

*Will you sing the seventh?*

It asked without words. It asked the way the tide asks the shore.

And then, from the far end of the crypt, where the stair descended in broken steps from what remained of the vestry, came light. Torchlight, steady and unhurried, and Vicar Ansel Crane picking his way down through the ruin with his cassock hitched up out of the water and his face lit with something Ivo could only call joy.

"There you are," he said warmly, as if they had all arrived at the same garden party. "There you all are. Right on time."

* * *

**MAREN**

Maren had watched Ansel Crane bury two parishioners and marry three couples and christen every child born in Hollowmere for nineteen years, and she had never once seen him look happy until this moment. That was the thing about shepherds, her grandmother used to say, back before her grandmother stopped saying anything at all. They love the flock the way a man loves his supper.

"You're bleeding," Crane observed, nodding at her side without concern. "You should have stayed home, Mrs. Oduya. This is holy work."

"It's murder work," Maren said. "With better singing."

"It's *mercy*." He waded toward the well's rim, unhurried, the water parting for him or him for the water, Maren couldn't tell which. "Forty years, and do you know what we've lost? The orchards went first. Then the bees—" he glanced at her, apologetic, almost tender, "—then the children's teeth, then the milk, then the will to stay. Hollowmere has been dying by inches since old Josiah Oduya died before he could sign. Your husband's family, Mrs. Oduya. A whole line meant to hold this place up, and it fell asleep at its post." He smiled at Ivo. "But God provides. God always provides. He sent us a folklorist."

"He sent you nobody," Ivo said. Her voice shook, and Maren heard what lived underneath the shaking—not fear, or not only fear. Fury. "You let me be invisible. You counted my nights on my own doorframe. You never once asked me to dinner. That wasn't protection. That was *fattening*."

"Yes," Crane agreed pleasantly. "And now the hour is come."

He turned to the well and began to sing.

Maren knew the sixth verse the way everyone in Hollowmere knew it—in the body, not the mind, absorbed through walls and winters, hummed by mothers who didn't know they were humming it. She had never heard it sung aloud on purpose. It was worse aloud. It was a rope being thrown down into deep water, and the water rising up the rope to meet the hand.

The thing beneath them rose to meet it.

She felt it pass through her like cold weather through a wall. The six dead voices sharpened; the black water climbed the well's inner stones in a spiral, faster, faster, and the pressure in the crypt changed the way pressure changes before a storm breaks—everything leaning toward one point, everything waiting for the seventh verse, for the outsider's voice, for Ivo Yarrow to open her mouth and be emptied forever.

"Don't," Maren said, and didn't know who she was begging.

Ivo looked at her across the flooded crypt. Blood-warm water to her knees, torch guttering, hair plastered to her face, and her eyes—her eyes were clear. Clearer than Maren had ever seen them. All those months of careful distance, of being unremarkable on purpose, of doors counted and lamps shuttered, and here at the bottom of everything she looked like a woman who had finally been given a question worth answering.

Then Ivo opened her mouth—

—and sang the wrong song.

* * *

**ELLERY**

Ellery couldn't sing. That was the whole of his ruin, the whole of his mornings spent weeping without sound into his pillow while his mother stroked his hair and his father stared at the wall. But he could hear. And what he heard, standing hip-deep in the dead founders' water while the vicar's verse pulled the thing below up hand over hand—Ivo's voice, thin and cracked and deliberately, gloriously *off*, wandering through a melody that belonged to no ledger and no founder and no grave—

What he heard was freedom.

Because the seventh verse wanted surrender. He understood that now, in the wordless way he understood most things since the road took his voice. The seventh verse was a door that only opened from the inside, and everything—the kindness with a ledger, the tallies on the door, the vicar's reasonable mercy—was designed to make her open it herself and call it grace.

But an eighth verse. An eighth verse was a wall built in front of the door. And walls could be built by anyone.

He raised the cracked bell and struck it against the well's stone rim.

No sound came out of it. There never would again. But the *motion* rang, somehow, in the water, in the bones under the water, in the six dead throats that had been singing the founders' song for a hundred years—and they faltered. All six faltered at once, like a choir losing its place, and into that gap Ivo poured her broken melody, and Maren stepped forward through the flood to stand at her shoulder, bleeding and swearing and ready.

Ellery put down the bell. He could not give a note. But he could give the rest of it.

He waded to Ivo's other side, took her hand, and squeezed twice—the signal they'd agreed on upstairs, in the terrible ordinary kitchen, over tea neither of them drank. *We mean it. We're here. Begin.*

* * *

**IVO**

Later—Ivo would think about this later, in the years after, whenever anyone asked her what it was like, and people did ask, though fewer as time went on—later she would say that the eighth verse was not composed. It was *recognized*. It had been waiting in the marginalia of the founders' ledger, unwritten, in the space where ink refused to go, and all they had done was walk into that space and stand there together.

But in the moment there was no later. In the moment there was only this:

Ivo sang, and she sang badly, and she meant it.

The words came from nowhere and everywhere—from Tam Weller's harvest lament, from the field recordings, from the names of the dead read out in the vestry, from Maren's honey-scented kitchen and Ellery's silent weeping and her own long unremarkable life of doors she'd closed on purpose. She sang about staying. Not being kept—staying. Choosing. She sang *we count our own doors* and *we leave the lamp lit* and *no one sings alone past dark*, and none of it rhymed, and half of it didn't scan, and the thing beneath the well recoiled from it the way a tongue recoils from a burn.

Beside her, Maren's voice joined hers—low, rich, off-key in an entirely different direction, so that the two melodies fought each other like siblings and refused, absolutely refused, to resolve. Maren sang her husband's name into it. She sang her grandmother's. She sang *you do not get to keep what was freely given* and *the bees are ours and the winter is ours and the grief is ours*, and the water around their knees shuddered.

And Ellery—

Ellery could not make a sound. So instead he gave them the rhythm. He beat the water with his palms, slow and human and arrhythmic in the way living hearts are arrhythmic, and where his hands struck, the six dead voices could not follow. The founders' song needed its grid, its terrible perfect meter, and Ellery broke the meter with every slap of his hands against the flood, grinning through his tears, mouthing along to words he would never speak again.

Three voices. One of them ruined, one of them borrowed, one of them bleeding. Not one note in tune.

The well screamed.

* * *

**MAREN**

It was not a sound a body should contain. Maren felt it in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the wound at her side which tore wider and did not bleed so much as *leak*, as if the water had opinions about what was inside her. The six dead voices rose to a shriek and the shriek became words and the words were the founders' names, all of them, offered like coins, like bribes, like the last argument of a drowning merchant: *take the harvest, take the bounty, take forty more years, only finish the song, only finish the song, only—*

Ansel Crane was still singing.

That was the horror of it. The vicar had reached the end of the sixth verse and simply begun it again, louder, forcing the rope down into the dark, trying to drag the seventh verse into existence by sheer clerical will. And the thing below, wounded and starving and furious, turned on him the way a betrayed animal turns on its keeper.

Maren saw it happen and would spend the rest of her life failing to describe it.

The water stood up. All of it, the whole flooded crypt, in one column, and in the column was a shape—not a body, nothing so kind as a body, but the *idea* of a mouth, vast and dry and ringed with the memory of teeth. It bent over Ansel Crane the way a parent bends over a child showing off a recital.

And it listened to him sing.

The false verse went into it like a hook. Because that was the flaw the founders had left, the flaw in the margin, the mercy hidden inside the cruelty: the ritual could not abide a lie. Every verse for a hundred years had been sung true—sung in fear, sung in duty, but true. Crane sang for control. Crane sang to *own*. And the thing beneath Old Chapel Road, which had dined on true voices for a century, found itself fed a song that meant nothing, and it could not digest it, and what a thing cannot digest, it returns.

The column of water collapsed onto him.

Maren turned away—but not fast enough, and she would see it anyway, forever: the vicar's mouth still moving, shaping verses, as the water filled it, as the six dead voices poured themselves into him and out of him and through him until there was no Ansel Crane left, only a shepherd-shaped emptiness kneeling in the flood, singing a song that had already ended.

Then the well convulsed a final time—and *broke*.

* * *

**IVO**

Not shattered. Broke the way a fever breaks. The pressure that had leaned on the crypt for a hundred years released all at once, and the black water dropped away from them as if the earth had opened a second mouth beneath the first and swallowed downward, down past the founders' bones, down past the sealed lip of 1911, down into whatever dark had been waiting behind the dark.

The six voices went with it. Ivo heard them go—one by one, not dragged but *released*, each voice peeling free of the chorus like a hand letting go of a rope, and the last one, the youngest, lingered a moment at the edge of hearing and sang three notes of the harvest lament, gently, correctly, the way it was meant to be sung, before it too went down into the quiet.

Then silence. Real silence, the kind Hollowmere had forgotten how to make.

Ivo stood in ankle-deep clear water among the founders' bones, holding Maren's hand with one of hers and Ellery's with the other, and discovered she was laughing. Ragged, gasping, ugly laughter, the kind that lives next door to sobbing. Maren was laughing too, and crying, and pressing her bleeding side, and Ellery was shaking with soundless laughter that was somehow louder than anything in the room.

"We sang terribly," Ivo managed.

"Appallingly," Maren agreed.

"The worst music ever performed in this county."

"And it wouldn't take it." Maren wiped her eyes with her free hand, smearing blood and water across her cheek. "A hundred years of perfect verses, and it starves to death on three people singing out of tune because they *chose* each other." She looked at Ivo, and her expression shifted into something quieter. "It couldn't digest us."

Above them, through the broken floor, dawn was coming up grey and ordinary over Old Chapel Road. Somewhere up there, Tam Weller was probably drunk. Somewhere up there, doors were opening for the first time in a hundred years without anyone counting them shut again.

Ivo opened her mouth to say something—she didn't know what, something about going home, something about breakfast, something small and human—and no sound came out.

She tried again. Nothing. Not pain, not loss, exactly. Just a door, closed, from the inside. Spent.

Maren saw her face and understood, and tightened her grip on Ivo's hand, and did not offer pity, because pity would have been a lie between them. Ellery touched her arm, once, gently—*one of us now*—and smiled.

The water in the well settled into stillness. Clean. Quiet. Done.

And far overhead, faint through the broken chapel floor, Ivo heard the first sound of the new Hollowmere: a child's voice, high and uncertain, humming the harvest lament—all the way through, and then, wonder of wonders, beginning it again.
